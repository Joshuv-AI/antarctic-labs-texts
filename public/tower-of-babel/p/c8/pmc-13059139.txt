Surgical resection of pleural lipoma after 9 years of serial imaging
Authors: Chadi Nahal, Chetna Purohit, Andrew Toma, Peter Seirafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPleural lipomas are rare benign intrathoracic mesenchymal neoplasms. Although typically asymptomatic, they can enlarge and cause symptoms secondary to compression of surrounding intrathoracic structures. There is minimal reporting of pleural lipoma observed with computed tomography throughout prolonged periods of surveillance; therefore, differentiating well-differentiated malignant liposarcoma from imaging alone remains a diagnostic challenge. We present the case of a 74 year-old male with tobacco use and asbestos exposure who underwent surveillance of a pleural neoplasm over 9 years. Continued enlargement and morphological changes concerning for malignancy prompted video-assisted thoracoscopic surgical resection. The pleural mass was diagnosed as a pleural lipoma and the patient recovered well. Pleural lipomas, especially in patients with risk factors for malignancy, should be resected for accurate diagnosis and prevention of complications associated with compression of adjacent structures.Keywords: Computed tomography, lipoma, liposarcoma, pleura, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery

Introduction

Lipomas are benign mesenchymal neoplasms originating from adipose tissue. They are rarely found in the thorax, with reported sites including fat containing regions of the lung parenchyma, mediastinum, diaphragm, and tracheobronchial tree [1]. It is particularly rare for lipomas to originate in the pleural tissue. We present the case of a 74 male with prior tobacco use and asbestos exposure who had an enlarging pleural mass monitored over 9 years that developed concerning imaging features and diagnosed as pleural lipoma following resection.

Case report

A 74 year old male had a left upper extrapulmonary pleural based mass that exhibited gradual enlargement over 9 years to a size of 4.6 by 4.7 by 3.5 centimeters on computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings 5 years prior to surgery showed a homogenous-well demarcated mass. Positron emission tomography imaging 1 month prior to surgery did not reveal significant radiotracer uptake.

Interval CT surveillance imaging showed enlargement of the pleural mass over 9 years prior to resection. Measurements in the axial and coronal views respectively were (A) 4.3 cm by (B) 4.92 cm at 2 months, (C) 1.89 cm by (D) 1.15 cm at 9 years prior to surgery

Medical history was notable for hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Oncological history included intracranial meningioma status post resection, prostate cancer status post radiotherapy, and bladder cancer status post endoscopic resection. Notable social history included 30 pack-year smoking history and asbestos exposure during childhood.

Given the newly appearing calcifications on the most recent CT imaging, continued growth of the mass, and history of tobacco use and asbestos exposure, there was a higher suspicion of malignancy and we recommended surgical resection. He underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgical (VATS) excision. Intraoperatively, the mass was observed beneath the parietal pleural in the left superolateral chest (Fig. 2A). Using blunt and sharp circumferential dissection the mass was removed in en bloc. There was no extension into the ribs. At 1 month follow-up, patient continued to have adequate respiratory function and physical activity.

Grossly tan-yellow fatty lesion was identified intra-operatively (A). Microscopic examination of the lesion at 10x magnification revealed predominantly adipose tissue with osseous and cartilaginous differentiation (B)

Pathological analysis revealed a grossly tan-yellow fatty lesion with bony appearing areas. Microscopically, there was predominantly adipose tissue admixed with skeletal muscle tissue, bone tissue with trilineage hematopoietic elements and cartilage tissue (Fig. 2B). The case was sent for consultation to Joint Pathology Center and findings were consistent with an intramuscular lipoma variant with cartilaginous and osseous components. Fluorescence in situ hybridization was negative for MDM2 amplification, indicating low likelihood for liposarcoma.

Discussion

We present a pleural mass diagnosed as lipoma in a 74 year-old male with known risk factors for pleural malignancy, particularly asbestos exposure. The diagnostic uncertainty stemmed from oncologic history, chemical exposure, tobacco use, and radiographic findings. Pleural lipoma is rare, and there is limited documentation of surveillance of this lesion over an extended period of time.

Our case is unique because the lipoma exhibited gradual growth over 9 years and developed heterogenous calcifications, which prompted evaluation for malignant transformation. Prior to the most recent CT study, our patient’s pleural mass had benign features consistent with lipoma, particularly homogeneous fat attenuation [2]. Given the patient’s age, comorbidities, and preferences, initial management consisted of scheduled radiographic monitoring; however, CT cannot reliably differentiate pleural lipoma from well-differentiated liposarcoma [2]. Calcifications have been reported on CT for pleural lipoma [3], but this is uncommon amongst the few reported pleural lipomas, and interval development of calcifications over a year warrants further evaluation. MRI is more sensitive for identifying malignant features including nodularity and septations, which were not present in our patient’s MRI taken 5 years before the most recent CT [4]. There is limited evidence to support MRI in reliably differentiating between benign lipomas and well-differentiated liposarcoma. Positron emission tomography has been reported to show increased uptake values in liposarcoma [5], but given the longitudinal changes in our patient’s surveillance imaging that raised suspicion for malignancy, positron emission tomography would not have affected our decision for resection.

Although lipomas are characteristically slow-growing, pleural lipomas can cause symptoms secondary to compression of surrounding structures. One case involved a 25 cm mass occupying the majority of the left hemithorax and causing shortness of breath [6]. Pleural lipomas are recommend for resection prior to such progression, preventing symptoms from occurring and avoiding potential surgical complications associated with larger intrathoracic masses [2]. Recurrence occurs in less than 5% of cases following resection [1]. Resection is diagnostic and preferred over biopsy, which is limited by intertumoral hemorrhage, necrosis, and inflammation [4]. Heterogenous features of a pleural mass can prevent adequate tissue sampling to rule out liposarcoma. Therefore, we opted for surgical resection with negative gross margins for diagnostic and therapeutic benefit to avoid the procedural risks of pre-operative biopsy which may have outweighed any diagnostic benefit. Given low suspicion of metastasis or localized spread on pre-operative imaging, intra-operative pathological analysis was not deemed necessary.

Our patient had significant asbestos exposure as a child, a known risk factor for pleural malignancy. Given continued growth of his pleural mass over 9 years and development of calcifications previously unseen on prior CT studies, minimally invasive VATS surgical resection was effective for accurate diagnosis and prevention of complications associated with progressive enlargement. The illustrated morphological changes from longitudinal surveillance imaging shows how lipoma can have characteristics similar to liposarcoma. This can guide evaluation of similar imaging and contribute to informed decision making when deciding between resection versus surveillance in future cases.
