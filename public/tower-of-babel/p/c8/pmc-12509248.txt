Tibia‐Fibula Relative Motion During Gait Cycle by 2D‐3D Registration
Authors: Lichao Zhang, Peng Su, Junlin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInjuries to the distal tibiofibular joint are often associated with ankle fractures, sports‐related injuries, or instability, whereas proximal tibiofibular joint injuries are more commonly present with lateral‐ or posterolateral‐compartment lesions of the knee. These conditions may be related to the relative motion between the tibia and fibula; however, precise movement patterns have yet to be fully elucidated. This study analyzes the relative motion of the tibia and fibula in 16 healthy adults (32 bones; 8 males and 8 females) throughout a normal gait cycle. CT scan data were initially acquired to construct 3D models of the tibia and fibula. X‐ray images were taken at seven points during the gait cycle for 3D‐2D and 3D‐3D model registration, facilitating the calculation of relative degrees of freedom (DOF) movements between these bones. Our results demonstrate that from the heel strike to the single‐legged stance phase, the fibula transitions from plantarflexion to dorsiflexion, reaching up to 0.41°. It shifts from inversion to eversion, increasing to 0.06°, while internal rotation decreases to 0.5°. From the single‐legged stance to the toe‐off phase, changes in plantarflexion/dorsiflexion are minimal, eversion decreases to 0.02°, and internal rotation increases to 0.63°. The proximal fibula exhibits anterior‐posterior, medial‐lateral, and inferior‐superior movements, with similar patterns observed in the distal fibula. During a standard gait cycle, internal/external rotational movements, along with anterior‐posterior and medial‐lateral shifts, are predominant. Introduction

The connection between the tibia and fibula involves the proximal and distal tibiofibular joints, along with the tibiofibular syndesmotic and interosseous ligaments. These structures form a robust linkage designed to resist the separation of the bones [1]. The three‐dimensional (3D) movement of the tibia and fibula is intricately linked with the mechanics of the knee and ankle joints. Injuries to the distal tibiofibular joint are often associated with ankle fractures, sports‐related injuries, or instability. In contrast, proximal tibiofibular joint injuries are more commonly present with the knee's lateral‐ or posterolateral‐compartment lesions. These conditions may be intricately connected to the relative motion between the tibia and fibula.

Accurately understanding the 3D movements of the tibia and fibula is crucial for diagnosing, treating, and prognosticating related disorders. However, compared to other major joints, studies on the relative motion of the tibia and fibula are scarce. Most research has focused on the distal tibiofibular joint, with limited attention on the proximal joint. Observing these joints in isolation fails to capture the complexity of the integrated system they form. There is a notable lack of research on the in vivo 3D movement of the human tibia and fibula.

Previous studies have employed fresh‐frozen cadavers to examine the motion of the tibia and fibula under non‐weight‐bearing conditions [2, 3]. These ex vivo methods face inherent limitations, as non‐weight‐bearing conditions and passive joint motions do not fully replicate actual gait or reflect physiological conditions. Some researchers have used in vivo techniques, such as surface or bone‐mounted markers, but these approaches present challenges. Surface markers cannot prevent movement between skin and bone, while bone‐mounted markers pose risks of invasive trauma, restricted movement, and significant ethical concerns [4, 5].

In recent years, 2D‐3D image registration has proven effective for measuring bone and joint kinematics. Its application in joint motion research has increased, demonstrating enhanced accuracy in analyzing structures like the ulna and radius in cases of elbow instability [6]. Similar techniques have examined the distal tibiofibular joint's motion during walking and shoulder joint movement. Despite its advantages, 2D‐3D image registration faces challenges like registration errors. Studies have shown that biplane registration offers greater accuracy over uniplane methods. However, biplanar registration is challenging to apply in continuous gait cycles. Encouragingly, Relevant studies show that in 2D‐3D single‐plane registration, the displacement error is within 0.5 mm, and the rotation error is within 1 degree [7]. Lawrence RL et al. demonstrate that single‐plane fluoroscopy and 2D/3D shape‐matching can accurately quantify whole shoulder complex kinematics in static positions [8]. The research by Zhu et al. shows that the in‐plane error during translational motion on a single plane is less than 0.5 mm, and the in‐plane error during rotational motion is less than 1° [9]. Additionally, roentgen stereophotogrammetric analysis using implanted opaque markers has been validated for accuracy in shoulder joint X‐rays and offers a benchmark for verifying 2D‐3D registration methods [10].

This study aims to apply 3D/2D registration technology to investigate the six degrees of freedom of 3D motion of the tibia and fibula in vivo during the normal gait cycle. The findings of this study could significantly impact clinical practice, providing a scientific foundation for diagnosing tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries, including high ankle sprains and inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis separations. In addition, it seeks to inform decisions regarding surgical fixation methods, such as screw fixation versus elastic fixation, as well as guide the development of effective rehabilitation plans.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This study included 16 healthy adult participants, 8 males and 8 females, with an average age of 38.75 years (±16.18). The participants had an average height of 1.68 meters (±0.10), an average weight of 68.81 kilograms (±12.10), and a BMI of 24.35 (±4.41).

Inclusion Criteria

Participants were required to be healthy adults, male or nonpregnant, non‐lactating females, who exhibited normal physical development, mobility, and walking gait. They needed normal lower limb development and alignment, no excessive ligament laxity, normal foot arch development, and no history of chronic lower limb pain, fractures, or surgeries. Mental and intellectual status had to be normal, with good compliance and cooperation with study procedures. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Exclusion Criteria

Exclusion factors included abnormal lower limb development or alignment, excessive ligament laxity, abnormal foot arches (either high arches or flat feet), a history of chronic lower limb pain, fractures, or surgeries, and inability to comply with study procedures.

Radiation Safety Measures

Due to the use of X‐ray and CT scans, strict radiation safety protocols were implemented. Participants were protected with lead aprons, data collection intervals were controlled, and radiation doses were monitored to ensure safety. Informed consent procedures were thoroughly explained, and consent was documented. Approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Shijingshan Hospital, Beijing (Ethics Statement Number: sjsky‐202101).

CT Scanning

CT scans (Discovery CT750HD, GE, USA) included the full length of the tibia and fibula. Scanning parameters were set at 0.625 mm thickness, 120 kV voltage, 200 mA current, and an image matrix of 512 × 512 pixels. Bone contours were identified on CT images based on density differences between bone and soft tissue. 3D models were reconstructed using Mimics software (Mimics Research, Mimics Innovation Suite 21, Materialize NV, Leuven, Belgium), followed by image segmentation to establish anatomical coordinate systems within the bones. The anatomical coordinate systems were constructed in both bones following the Yamaguchi et al. [11] method and the ISB recommendation [12]. The directions of the XYZ axes and the movements’ positive and negative values followed the right‐hand rule.

X‐Ray Imaging and Gait Cycle Analysis

The tibia and fibula were captured using a digital multifunctional X‐ray fluoroscopy system (SHIMADZU, SONIALVISION G4, Japan). The gait cycle was analyzed from heel contact to the same heel's next contact, incorporating typical postural changes. Seven key phases were identified on the left side. For the left side, these include: (1) left heel preparing to contact the ground, right heel lifting off; (2) left heel contacting the ground, right heel lifting off; (3) left foot mid‐stance, right toes lifting off; (4) left foot mid‐stance, right foot in swing phase; (5) left heel lifting off, right heel preparing for contact; (6) right heel contacting the ground; (7) left toes lifting off, right foot mid‐stance (Figure 1).

Segmentation and Creation of 3D Tibial and Fibular Geometries

The 3D geometric figures of the tibia and fibula were segmented by CT scans using Mimics® (Mimics Research, Mimics Innovation Suite 21.0, Materialize, Leuven, Belgium) software to obtain the tibia and fibula. Two independent investigators (I1, I2) conducted the segmentation process to evaluate the accuracy. The results of the two groups were compared for segmentation using the Dice similarity Coefficient.

(DSC = 2 × | X ∩ Y | )/ | X | + | Y | , where X and Y are the sets for comparison. The range of DSC can be between 0 and 1. The higher the value, the more overlap there is between the sets. Generate surface triangular meshes based on the generated masks and perform uniform meshing and smoothing processing.

Image Registration

Mimics software created a 3D virtual environment comprising 3D models, X‐rays, and a virtual radiation light source. Following segmentation, the tibia and fibula were independently movable and rotatable at six degrees of freedom (Figure 2). The 3D bone model was projected onto the 2D X‐ray lateral image in the X‐ray module. A five‐step registration method was employed: (1) manual X‐ray registration, (2) manual registration of the tibia and fibula, (3) contour selection of the tibia and fibula, (4) automatic 2D/3D registration based on contour features, and (5) automatic 3D/3D registration of the tibia, followed by the fibula's registration (Figures 3 and 4).

Determining the six degrees of freedom (DOF), the spatial position and orientation of the tibia or fibula were analyzed using 3‐matic 13.0 software (Materialize NV, Leuven, Belgium). The fibula model in the resting position served as the reference point for calculating bone‐to‐bone relationships by decomposing the XYZ coordinates relative to this position. The X‐axis reflects lateral/medial movements, the Y‐axis anterior/posterior movements, and the Z‐axis is superior/inferior. Angles were categorized as α: plantarflexion/dorsiflexion, β: inversion/eversion, and γ: external/internal rotation (Figure 5). For each DOF, the average range of motion (ROM) was calculated as the sum of individual ROM measurements divided by 32, and the average joint position for the seven postures was computed similarly.

X‐axis: Lateral (+)/Medial (−), Y‐axis: Anterior (+)/Posterior (−), Z‐axis: Superior (+)/Inferior (−), α: Plantarflexion (−)/Dorsiflexion (+), β: Inversion (−)/Eversion (+), γ: External Rotation (−)/Internal Rotation (+).

Statistical Methods

The measurement data were first subjected to the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test to determine that the data conformed to a normal distribution. Continuous data were summarized as mean and standard deviation.

Discussion

The tibia and fibula are integrally connected to the ankle and knee joints, underscoring their importance in knee and ankle disorders. The clinical significance of this study lies in analyzing the relative motion of these bones in a normal lower limb, providing valuable insights that can guide the treatment of conditions such as lateral compartment knee lesions, anterior lateral ankle impingement syndrome, and lateral ankle instability.

Using a 2D/3D registration technique, we non‐invasively examined the in vivo kinematics of the tibia and fibula during the gait cycle. The capability of the X‐ray module in Materialize Mimics® software was harnessed to rigidly map 3D geometries onto X‐ray images captured at different phases of the gait cycle. Previous studies, such as those by Pieroh et al. and Hajnal B., have effectively utilized similar techniques for assessing sacroiliac screw loosening and registering lumbar CT to standing X‐rays, respectively, achieving significant accuracy and segmentation indexes [13, 14].

While much research has examined fibular movement, it often focuses separately on the proximal or distal tibiofibular joint. For example, Murat Bozkurt's study demonstrated that with ankle movement from plantarflexion to dorsiflexion, the fibula transitions from a posterior to an anterior position, increasing its rotational angle [2]. Similarly, Weinert CR demonstrated that under load‐bearing conditions, the fibula tends to move inferiorly and laterally [15]. Alves’ research highlighted the stabilizing roles of the anterior and posterior superior tibiofibular ligaments and the interosseous membrane in maintaining rotational stability [16].

Our study revealed that the fibula's rotational motion during plantarflexion/dorsiflexion (α), inversion/eversion (β), and internal/external rotation (γ) was 0.67° ± 0.27°, 0.18° ± 0.10°, and 1.18° ± 0.62°, respectively. These findings show relatively small rotational movement compared to other joints, aligning with prior studies by Alves and Soavi R., which demonstrated rotational angles below 2° during similar conditions [16, 17].

Displacement measurements showed the proximal fibula moved medially/laterally by 1.79 mm ± 0.64 mm, anteriorly/posteriorly by 2.84 mm ± 1.13 mm, and superiorly/inferiorly by 1.89 mm ± 1.21 mm. The distal fibula had slightly larger displacements, affirming Chen Wang's observations of a 2 to 4 mm range during gait [7]. These variations might be influenced by weight‐bearing, loading, and muscle contractions, which differ significantly from passive cadaver studies [17].

The study's kinematic findings offer critical insights, particularly in contexts like debating elastic fixation against traditional screw fixation for syndesmotic ligament injuries. Elastic fixation accommodates primary fibular movements, potentially allowing for natural healing, whereas rigid fixation may hinder this flexibility.

Limitations of this study include the reliance on manual registration, which is time‐intensive, and the use of single‐plane X‐ray imaging, not being an accurate kinematic analysis with dynamic biplane fluoroscopy, as larger slice thickness potentially introduces error in small translational values, impacting registration precision. Employing biplanar orthogonal X‐rays could improve accuracy and reduce errors.

In conclusion, understanding the physiological movements of the tibia and fibula during the gait cycle is foundational for further exploration of complex lower limb dynamics. This study's six degrees of freedom kinematic data enhance the in vivo database with quantitative insights, supporting future clinical applications in diagnosing and treating tibiofibular syndesmotic‐related diseases.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that internal/external rotation, medial/lateral movement, and anterior/posterior movement of the fibula play significant roles during the gait cycle. From the preparation for heel contact to mid‐stance, the fibula exhibits a rotational movement trend characterized by dorsiflexion, eversion, and external rotation. The proximal fibula tends to displace laterally, posteriorly, and superiorly, while the distal fibula tends to move laterally, anteriorly, and superiorly. Conversely, from mid‐stance to toe‐off, the fibula shows a rotation trend of plantarflexion, inversion, and internal rotation. During this phase, the proximal fibula tends to displace medially, posteriorly, and inferiorly, whereas the distal fibula tends toward medial, posterior, and inferior displacement.

To address the potential out‐of‐plane errors that may occur during the registration process, we conducted a verification test on the lower limb model (Yinuo Medical Technology Co. Ltd., Rizhao, Shandong Province) to measure the accuracy and error conditions of the single‐plane 3D/2D registration technique applied to the tibia and fibula. Using the three‐dimensional stereometric analysis (RSA) method as the gold standard, we compared the spatial movements of the tibia and fibula, measured via 3D/2D registration, against the RSA results under six degrees of freedom.

For this purpose, eight 2.5 mm titanium beads were implanted at the proximal and distal ends of the tibia and fibula, allowing simulation of seven gait pose movements. This outcome confirms that single‐plane registration can achieve satisfactory accuracy. The maximum error was found in detecting an internal and external rotation error of 0.43°, which does impact our kinematic data marginally in this rotation direction. However, since the translational movements of the tibia and fibula were significantly greater than the error, these did not affect our conclusions.

Overall, single‐plane 3D/2D registration can accurately reproduce the in vivo kinematics of the tibia and fibula. Due to its practical simplicity, we recommend a broader application of this technology to solve diagnostic, therapeutic, and surgical evaluation issues related to tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries.
