Durable Cancer Control and Extended Long‐Term Survival Following Salvage Surgery for Squamous Cell Carcinoma of the Anal Canal: A Cohort Study
Authors: Paul Savage, David P. Cyr, Dan E. Schiller, Bernard J. Cummings, Alexandra M. Easson, Andrew J. Smith, Carol J. Swallow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimSalvage surgery is the standard‐of‐care for patients with squamous cell carcinoma of the anal canal (SCCa) who experience isolated locoregional failure after upfront chemoradiotherapy. While perioperative morbidity and short‐term survival outcomes have been described, long‐term outcomes of surgical salvage are unknown. This study examines recurrence patterns and survival in a cohort of patients with extended follow‐up.MethodA retrospective chart review was performed on a cohort of consecutive patients who underwent salvage surgery at two specialized cancer centers between 1987 and 2006. Overall, disease‐specific and re‐recurrence‐free survival (OS, DSS, RRFS) were estimated using the Kaplan‐Meier method. Prognostic variables were evaluated using univariate and multivariable Cox models.ResultsForty patients were included (29 female, 11 male; median age 56). Median follow‐up was 16.5 years (IQR 15.0−20.5) for patients alive at last follow‐up. Re‐recurrence developed in 52% of patients, 95% of which occurred within 3 years of salvage surgery; re‐recurrence was associated with a 91% risk of subsequent SCCa‐specific mortality. DSS was 45% (95% CI 30−61) at 5 years, after which point conditional 5‐year DSS was 100%.ConclusionApproximately half of patients who undergo salvage surgery for persistent/recurrent SCCa experience durable cancer control with extended long‐term survival. Emerging treatment options such as immunotherapy should be evaluated relative to this benchmark.

Introduction

Squamous cell carcinoma of the anal canal (SCCa) is the most common histological subtype of anal cancer, and while it is considered a rare malignancy, the incidence has been steadily increasing worldwide [1]. The standard of care for patients who present with localized primary SCCa is upfront definitive combined chemoradiotherapy (CRT), where modern‐day regimens result in initial complete clinical response rates of 90%, sparing the majority of patients from a permanent colostomy.

Unfortunately, 15%−30% of patients experience persistent or recurrent disease following definitive CRT, the predominant pattern being isolated locoregional (LR) disease without distant metastasis [2, 3]. Risk factors for LR failure include higher T and N stages, treatment interruptions or non‐completion, and male sex [4, 5, 6]. Currently, salvage surgery is the only treatment option that has been shown to effectively control disease in patients who have failed upfront CRT [7]. This type of surgery often requires multivisceral resection (MVR) to attain a negative microscopic margin and can be technically demanding given the anatomical relationships of the anal canal with pelvic structures such as the prostate, urethra, and vagina. Involvement of adjacent structures can be challenging to delineate in the densely fibrotic previously irradiated field. As such, the decision to proceed with salvage surgery is complex and requires careful weighing of patient, tumor, and technical factors. The most consistently identified negative prognostic factor following surgical salvage is involvement of the resection margin by cancer, while male sex, high comorbidity index, large tumor size, nodal involvement, and lymphovascular invasion (LVI) have also been associated with adverse outcomes [7, 8]. Previous studies from our own center and other groups have demonstrated that perioperative mortality is low (< 5%), but serious morbidity is significant (up to 70%), and indicate a 5‐year overall survival (OS) of approximately 40% [7, 8, 9]. However, most of these studies are limited by relatively short follow‐up, and the incidence of late re‐recurrences and SCCa‐attributable mortality remains unknown.

To address this knowledge gap, we investigated long‐term outcomes by extending the follow‐up for a cohort of patients who underwent surgical salvage for persistent or recurrent SCCa following CRT [7]. By analyzing patterns of re‐recurrence, as well as long‐term DSS and OS, we provide an improved understanding of the durability of response to salvage surgery for this challenging disease entity.

Methods

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

A retrospective review was performed on a cohort of consecutive SCCa patients who underwent salvage resection following definitive CRT between September 1987 and April 2006 at Mount Sinai Hospital or Sunnybrook Health Sciences Center, Toronto [7]. Inclusion criteria were as previously described; patients with persistent or recurrent disease were included. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Boards at Sinai Health System, Princess Margaret Cancer Center/University Health Network, and Sunnybrook Health Sciences Center. The data cut‐off was 05/31/21.

Treatment and Follow‐Up

Patients had been treated upfront with a multiphase CRT approach, as described [7]. The primary tumor and any clinically positive inguinal lymph nodes were treated with 4500−6300 cGy in 30−35 fractions over 8‐11 weeks. Clinically negative inguinal node basins were treated with 2700−2800 cGy in 14−15 fractions. 5‐FU was administered as a continuous infusion at 1000 mg/m2 per 24 h over a 96 h period, given two to three times over the RT course. Mitomycin C was administered at 10 mg/m2 by bolus IV injection one to two times during RT. Following CRT, patients were examined by the treating radiation oncologist every 2 months for 2 years, then every 6 months for 3 years, then annually. Imaging and biopsy were pursued only if there was suspicion of persistent or recurrent disease on clinical exam. Patients with persistent or recurrent disease were reviewed at a multidisciplinary cancer conference and considered for salvage surgery with curative intent. Additional chemotherapy or RT was not administered prior to salvage surgery. Surgery included en bloc resection of adjacent structures or viscera that appeared involved with the goal of attaining a microscopically negative margin. Following salvage surgery, patients were followed up within 2−4 weeks of discharge from the hospital. Surveillance then included a physical examination and CT abdomen/pelvis every 4−6 months for 5 years, then annually. Re‐recurrence after salvage was defined by findings on physical examination, imaging and/or biopsy.

Definitions

Patient comorbidities were scored using the Charlson comorbidity index [10]. Stage is reported according to the contemporaneous American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) edition. Persistent disease was defined as failure to reach a complete clinical response to CRT, based on physical examination and/or imaging. Recurrent disease was defined based on physical exam and/or imaging following a complete clinical response of at least 1 month duration after completion of CRT. Time‐to‐event analyses were performed according to the definitions below. OS events included death from any cause. Disease‐specific survival (DSS) events included death from/related to treatment of SCCa (perioperative or after re‐recurrence). Secondary recurrence after salvage surgery was referred to as re‐recurrence. Re‐recurrence‐free survival (RRFS) events included any re‐recurrence (LR or distant metastasis, DM). LR re‐recurrence included any pelvic or inguinal disease, while DM included any distant failure following salvage surgery, where nodal stations were classified per AJCC. All survival endpoints were measured from the date of salvage surgery, unless otherwise indicated. Patients who did not have an event were censored at the date of last follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Cumulative survival probabilities were estimated with the Kaplan−Meier method. Univariate and multivariable analyses (UVA, MVA) were performed using Cox proportional‐hazards regression to model the effects of clinicopathologic factors on OS, DSS, and RRFS. Effect estimates derived from these models are reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two‐tailed). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM) and Prism 9.1.0 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Patient Clinicopathologic Features and Treatment

A total of 40 consecutive patients who underwent curative‐intent surgical salvage were included; clinicopathologic and treatment characteristics have been described [7], and are summarized in Table 1. There were 11 (28%) males and 29 (72%) females. At the time of salvage surgery, the median age was 56 (IQR 47.8−64), and the median CCI was 3.5 (IQR 3−5). Initial therapy had comprised combined CRT for 38 (95%) patients, and radiation (RT) only for two (5%) patients. CRT was incomplete in one patient who proceeded to surgery due to a symptomatic anovaginal fistula. Nineteen patients underwent surgery for persistent cancer, with a median time from completion of CRT of 6.5 months (IQR 3.5−7.8). Twenty‐one had surgery for recurrent disease, with median time to first recurrence post‐CRT of 12.4 months (IQR 7.1−19.6), and time from first recurrence to surgical salvage of 1.8 months (IQR 1.0−3.0). The salvage procedure was APR alone in 14 (35%) patients, MVR in 24 (60%) patients, and local excision in two (5%) patients. No patient had grossly incomplete resection; margins were microscopically positive (R1) in seven (17.5%) patients.

Extended Follow‐Up

For the 19 patients who were still alive at the time of our initial report on this cohort, a median of 9.5 years (IQR 2.3–14.8) of additional follow‐up was obtained for the present study. For the 11 patients who were alive at last follow‐up for the present report, median total follow‐up was 16.5 years (IQR 15.0–20.5). For the original cohort of 40 patients, median follow‐up after salvage surgery was 3.1 years (IQR 1.0–12.5). Two patients were lost to follow‐up (at 1.6 and 6.6 years following surgery); neither had developed recurrence at that time. At last follow‐up, 11 (28%) patients were alive with no evidence of disease, while 29 (72%) patients had died, 21 (52%) from SCCa and 8 (20%) from other causes (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

Overall and DSS

For the entire patient cohort, 5‐, 10‐ and 15‐ year OS were 42% (95% CI 26–56), 34% (95% CI 20–48) and 28% (95% CI 15–43), respectively; median OS was 3.4 years (Figure 1A). The 5‐year DSS was 45% (95% CI 30–61), and there were no further deaths attributable to SCCa after 5 years (Figure 1B). Median DSS was 4.2 years. For the 21 patients who died as a result of SCCa, the median time from surgical salvage to death was 13.9 months (IQR 7.0−26.6) (Figure 1B, Supporting Information S1: Table S1). This includes two (5%) patients who died perioperatively, one on post‐operative Day 2 from cardiac arrest, and another at 4.1 months from sepsis and a cerebrovascular accident. These patients had CCIs of nine and eight, respectively, the two highest CCIs of the cohort.

While these survival statistics are relevant at the time of salvage surgery, they do not reflect the dynamic probabilities that become more salient to the individual patient as long‐term follow‐up continues. We therefore calculated 5‐year conditional DSS (cDSS) at 1, 3, and 5 years following surgery. Patients who had already survived 1 year had a 5‐year DSS (5‐year cDSS1) of 58% (95% CI 38−74); 5‐yr cDSS3 was 80% (95% CI 55−92), and 5‐year cDSS5 was 100% (Figure 1C).

Re‐Recurrence Patterns, Management and Survival

Twenty‐one patients (52%) re‐recurred after salvage surgery. The majority of re‐recurrences occurred early, with a median time‐to‐re‐recurrence from salvage surgery of 3.9 months (IQR 3.1−10.5). 95% of re‐recurrences were observed within 3 years of resection, and all re‐recurrences had in fact been captured at the time of our initial report (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). RRFS was 43% (95% CI 27−58) at 5 years, and remained stable through 15 years (Figure 2A); median RRFS was 2.3 years. At the time of salvage surgery, the cumulative risk of re‐recurrence by 3 years was 54% (95% CI 38−68), whereas the conditional 3‐year risk of re‐recurrence given that the patient was alive and disease‐free after one (cRR1) or 3 years (cRR3) was 26% (95% CI 4−57) and 7% (95% CI 0−68), respectively (Figure 2B). The only patient who re‐recurred after 3 years developed pelvic and inguinal re‐recurrences at 37 months, lung metastases at 44 months, and died of SCCa at 51 months following salvage surgery.

Of the 21 patients with re‐recurrence, the first site was isolated LR in 13 (62%) patients, isolated distant metastases (DM) in two (10%), and both LR and DM in six (29%) (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). When accounting for all re‐recurrences (not just the first event), 18 (86%) patients developed pelvic and six (29%) inguinal re‐recurrences during their disease course (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). The median LRFS was 3.5 years, and LRFS remained at 48% (95% CI 31−63) at years 5 through 15 (Figure 2C). Sites of distant metastases included distant lymph nodes in six (15%), liver in six (15%), and lung in five (13%) patients (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). DMFS was 63% (95% CI 43−77) at years 5 through 15 (Figure 2D), and median DMFS could not be analyzed.

Re‐recurrences were managed with surgery, chemotherapy, or radiation in four patients each, surgery and chemoradiation in one, and best supportive care in two. Only two patients had long‐term survival post‐re‐recurrence. One had an isolated pelvic re‐recurrence 13.6 months following a transanal excision, then underwent redo salvage with an APR and died 20 years later from a primary lung cancer. The other patient had a complete response to CRT for an upper abdominal para‐aortic lymph node metastasis and survived another 4.5 years, then was lost to follow‐up with no evidence of disease. Ultimately, 19 (91%) of the patients who re‐recurred died of SCCa, with a median DSS of 1.1 years and median time‐to‐death after re‐recurrence of 8.0 months (IQR 3.5−17.9) (Figure 2E, Supporting Information S1: Table S1). Moreover, among the 19 SCCa‐associated deaths that occurred beyond the perioperative period, 18 (95%) occurred in patients who had re‐recurred by 3 years post‐surgical salvage. This indicates that cure had been achieved in patients alive and disease‐free at 5 years, and that non‐SCCa deaths are the main contributor to OS events after 5 years.

The relationships between various clinicopathologic factors and long‐term oncologic outcomes are summarized in Table 2. LVI was strongly associated with oncologic outcomes upon UVA, including DSS (HR 3.4, 95% CI 1.4−8.1; p = 0.008), RRFS (HR 2.5, 95% CI 1.1−6.0; p = 0.04), and OS (HR 2.4, 95% CI 1.1−5.2; p = 0.02) (Figure 3B). LVI was also independently prognostic for both DSS and OS. Male sex was significantly associated with worse DSS and OS on MVA. The indication for surgery (recurrent vs. persistent disease), which has been debated as a prognostic factor in the literature, was not associated with long‐term oncologic outcome in this cohort.

Discussion

Definitive CRT has dramatically improved outcomes in localized SCCa, yet 15%−30% of patients develop isolated LR relapse, which represents a major therapeutic challenge [2, 3]. Salvage surgery has been proposed as the only curative‐intent treatment option in patients with recurrent or persistent disease who could tolerate an extirpative operation and whose tumors are technically resectable, but the durability of response to salvage surgery had not been previously evaluated beyond 5 years. Here, we show long‐term disease control based on extended follow‐up, with a median of 16.5 years in those alive at last follow‐up, comparing favorably to the median follow‐up of 2.0 years (IQR 1.5−3.9) in previous series [9]. We identify survival milestones that accurately predict for future disease control, including 3‐year RRFS and 5‐year DSS, after which the risk of re‐recurrence and SCCa‐specific mortality was exceedingly low. With detailed and complete long‐term follow‐up, we show that fewer than 5% of re‐recurrences occur later than 3 years following salvage surgery, and patients who are alive and disease‐free at this milestone have an excellent prognosis. Indeed, there were no SCCa‐specific deaths after 5 years, indicating that response to salvage surgery is extremely durable and patients can be cured. While OS continues to decrease in the long‐term, non‐SCCa‐specific mortality was the main contributor to late death, consistent with the prognostic capacity of CCI for OS but not DSS.

Our analysis of re‐recurrence patterns in conjunction with long‐term survival outcomes has allowed us to delineate important prognostic factors. We show that the prognostic value of resection margin involvement and LVI is maintained with long‐term follow‐up [7]. While margin status has been established as a robust predictor of re‐recurrence, the prognostic value of LVI has been less consistent [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. In our series, all patients with a positive margin who survived the postoperative period re‐recurred locoregionally and died of SCCa. While we can often anticipate close or involved margins based on preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings, neither intraoperative RT nor adjuvant systemic therapy has been shown to provide a survival benefit in this setting [16, 18]. More stringent patient selection could potentially increase the 80% R0 resection rate reported across most modern series [8], but criteria to achieve this have not been defined. In a population‐based study of patients who underwent salvage surgery, Lee and co‐authors found that an interval of more than 3 months between CRT and resection was associated with a higher R1 rate [19]. While heightened vigilance might reduce R1 resections, the unintended consequence could be unwarranted surgery in patients who take longer to reach complete clinical response.

In our cohort, median time from salvage surgery to re‐recurrence was only 3.9 months, with a tight interquartile range. A recent systematic review of previous series described median time from salvage operation to re‐recurrence ranging from 4 to 30 months [9]. This discrepancy might reflect the intensity of follow‐up exams and imaging studies that prevailed at our center. Re‐recurrence following salvage surgery is particularly challenging to effectively address. Here, we found a median DSS of 13 months after re‐recurrence, consistent with previous series [9]. New systemic therapy regimens, including carboplatin‐paclitaxel and nivolumab, have been approved for treatment of patients with advanced SCCa, but the durability of response and survival benefit from these regimens appear to be limited [20, 21]. At the recent ESMO Congress (2024), Rao and colleagues presented early outcomes of InterAACT2, a phase 3 RCT testing the addition of anti‐PD‐1 agent retifanlimab to carboplatin‐paclitaxel in patients with previously untreated locally recurrent or metastatic anal SCCa. While there was a Progression Free Survival (PFS) benefit observed for the former (9.3 vs. 7.4 months), 2‐year PFS in the +retifanlimab group was ≈30% [22]. The re‐recurrence free survival we show here at 5 years post‐salvage surgery (43%) compares favorably. Nevertheless, the potential for combining standard‐of‐care chemotherapy and immunotherapy with salvage surgery is an important area for future research.

Given the information bias inherent to the retrospective nature of our study, we chose to examine only objective survival outcomes in long‐term survivors. While delayed perineal wound healing was frequent (49%), recent data suggest that the majority of wounds heal within 1 year [7, 23]. In terms of quality‐of‐life, although overall functional scores are generally high among disease‐free survivors at a median of 4 years after salvage surgery, half of patients experience urinary dysfunction, the majority of men report impotence, and half of women suffer from dyspareunia [24]. How these issues evolve in long‐term survivors remains unclear. We did not study the rate of second primary cancers in this cohort, but the elevated risk in patients who have had anal SCCa necessitates ongoing screening and surveillance in long‐term survivors [25].

In conclusion, the long‐term follow‐up data we present here strongly support salvage surgery as a curative‐intent treatment modality for recurrent/persistent anal canal SCCa when the tumor appears technically resectable with a negative margin and the patient can tolerate the extirpative procedure required. While recent guidelines suggest that immune checkpoint blockade can be considered in this scenario in an attempt to avoid surgery [26], there are currently no published data that document the outcomes of a non‐operative approach in patients who are eligible for curative intent management. The survival milestones described here should serve as the benchmark by which non‐surgical salvage therapies are evaluated. Furthermore, the long‐term results we share in this report improve our ability to appropriately counsel patients regarding relapse and mortality risks over the course of their cancer journey.

Synopsis

By tracking long‐term outcomes in a well‐defined cohort of patients who underwent curative‐intent salvage surgery for recurrent squamous cell carcinoma of the anal canal, this study demonstrates durable control in approximately half of patients. No deaths from anal cancer were observed after 5 years of follow‐up. This result serves as a benchmark against which newer treatment modalities, such as immune check point blockade, should be evaluated.
