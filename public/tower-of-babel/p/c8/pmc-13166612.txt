Introduction

Vulvar cancer represents 3% of all gynecologic cancers[1]. The most common type of vulvar cancer is squamous cell carcinoma, and represents 90% of all cases[2].

The management of early-stage vulvar squamous cell carcinoma includes radical local excision of the primary tumor. Optimal margins of excision are still undefined and unclear, but it is acceptable to desire limiting radicality, in order to preserve all midline structures such as the anus, urethra, and clitoris[3].

Before the introduction of sentinel lymph node dissection, the standard procedure was inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy (IFL), which showed low recurrence rate. Nowadays, evaluation of sentinel nodes for metastases is used for diagnostic and prognostic purposes, as well as determining treatment options[4] being as efficient and safe as IFL, with less complications[5].

We present a case of a female patient with a history of radical vulvectomy and lymph node dissection with negative results, found to have unusual recurrence of cancer 10 cm above the pubic bone.

The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria[6].

Presentation of case

We present the case of a 62-year-old patient, with a medical history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and most significantly radical vulvectomy with inguinal lymphadenectomy due to vulvar cancer 7 months prior to presentation, with no allergies, non-smoker, gravida 0 para 0. The patient presented to the emergency department for syncope and somnolence of same day duration. The patient has experienced fatigue, dizziness, and fever of 38°C with an altered mental status of 2 days duration. No symptoms were associated with the respiratory, urinary, gastrointestinal, or other systems. Her blood pressure upon presentation was 148/71 mmHg, her pulse 103 beats per minute, and temperature 37.2°C. Physical examination showed an open inguinal incision wound with severe pus, with the presence of new suprapubic lesions – 10 cm above the pubic bone (Fig. 1). Discussion

Survival prognosis is shown to be good in patients with early-stage cancer. Patients with negative sentinel lymph nodes have a 5-year disease-specific survival of 93.5% and 90.8% for 10 years, whereas patients with metastasis have a 5- and 10-year disease-specific survival of 75.5% and 64.5%, respectively[7]. More recent population-based and prospective data have confirmed comparable oncologic outcomes between sentinel lymph node biopsy and inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy in carefully selected early-stage disease, supporting the long-term safety of sentinel lymph node biopsy[8,9].

Several mechanisms of metastasis can be hypothesized, such as hematogenous spread, lymphatic skip metastases or skip metastases to non-sentinel lymph nodes, dermal lymphatic dissemination, or occult micrometastases missed by initial staining techniques. Hematogenous spread in vulvar squamous cell carcinoma is rare in the absence of nodal involvement but has been described in advanced disease, typically involving lung, liver, or bone[10]. Dermal lymphatic spread and aberrant lymphatic drainage patterns may also explain atypical recurrence sites outside the expected inguinofemoral basins[11]. Additionally, although uncommon, iatrogenic tumor implantation following surgical manipulation has been described in multiple solid malignancies and represents a theoretical mechanism for non-regional recurrence[12].

Metastasis is classified into three major categories: macrometastasis, micrometastasis (≤2 mm), or isolated tumor cells[13]. Adjuvant therapy after sentinel lymph node biopsy in vulvar cancer is not fully standardized. Data from the GROINSS-V II study demonstrated that patients with isolated tumor cells or micrometastases had a low rate of groin recurrence, particularly when adjuvant radiotherapy was administered, although favorable outcomes were also reported in select patients managed without further treatment[14,15].

In a study by Boran et al, out of 21 patients, two had false-negative sentinel lymph nodes with non-sentinel lymph node metastasis. Approximately 10% of the patients had skip metastases to other regional lymph nodes. More recent analyses confirm that skip metastases and non-sentinel lymph node involvement can occur despite negative sentinel lymph nodes, particularly in larger or multifocal tumors[16].

Micrometastases are detected by immunohistochemistry (IHC) techniques and have been implicated as a causative factor for recurrence of cancer in node-negative patients[17]. Juretzka et al noted that two out of four patients with micrometastases had recurrence of cervical cancer, despite previous histological negative lymph nodes[18]. Tamussino et al reported a case of vulvar cancer recurrence after sentinel lymph node dissection where analysis confirmed it was negative with no micrometastases[19]. Narayansingh et al reported a 42% detection rate of micrometastases in previously node-negative patients[20]. More recent studies emphasize that ultrastaging protocols, including serial sectioning and sensitive IHC antibodies, significantly improve detection of low-volume nodal disease and may influence recurrence risk[21,22].

Klapdor et al concluded that false-negative sentinel lymph nodes can carry a high risk of groin recurrences[23]. Contemporary series report isolated groin recurrence rates of approximately 2–5% following negative sentinel lymph node biopsy, underscoring the small but clinically relevant risk of false-negative results[3].

Local recurrences are managed by radical local excision of the recurrent tumor, accompanied by unilateral or bilateral inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy if it was not done at initial treatment[24]. In the setting of negative sentinel lymph nodes, no additional treatment is typically required.

Our case is unusual due to the recurrence of cancer approximately 10 cm above the pubic bone rather than at the vulvar or groin level, despite negative margins and sentinel lymph nodes at the time of radical vulvectomy seven months earlier. This atypical recurrence location raises the possibility of non-nodal metastatic mechanisms, including hematogenous or dermal lymphatic spread, and, less likely, iatrogenic implantation related to prior surgical intervention.

Conclusion

Early-stage vulvar cancer is mainly treated by radical vulvectomy, and sentinel lymph node dissection is a diagnostic factor used to determine further management. If lymph nodes are negative, no additional treatment is necessary. In our case, the patient did not need adjuvant therapy due to all-negative lymph nodes. In spite of the negative results, the patient presented for the recurrence 10 cm above the pubic bone, rather than locally or in the groin area. This raises questions about the mechanism of recurrence and encourages further investigations as well as preventative measures to be taken in order to prevent recurrence and complications even in low-risk patients.
