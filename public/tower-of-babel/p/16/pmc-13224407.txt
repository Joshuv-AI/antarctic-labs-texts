Wrist external fixator-assisted percutaneous reduction for intra-articular calcaneal fractures: clinical and radiographic outcomes
Authors: Jin-Chang Wang, Jun-Jun Tang, Hai-Bo Lu, Yong-Jing Shi, Jiang-Hua Li, Jia-Yu Lu, Jian Zhang, Hua Chen, Ke-Jian Wu, Pei-Fu Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous reduction is a minimally invasive procedure for treating intra-articular calcaneal fractures that helps preserve the integrity of the soft tissues around the subtalar joint and reduces surgical complications. Auxiliary traction and temporary fixation are critical components in the process of percutaneous reduction. To overcome the limitations of current techniques for the percutaneous reduction of intra-articular calcaneal fractures, we introduced a technique in which wrist external fixators are used for assistance. This study aims to assess the effectiveness and safety of this approach through a retrospective case series.MethodsFrom March 2015 to September 2018, patients with intra-articular calcaneal fractures who met the inclusion criteria underwent wrist external fixator-assisted percutaneous reduction at our hospital. Postoperatively, X-rays and CT scans were used to evaluate the degree of fracture reduction. The American Orthopedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle–Hindfoot score, along with the range of motion of the ankle and subtalar joints, was measured at the final follow-up.ResultsAmong the 31 patients who completed the follow-up, 10 had Sanders type II fractures, and 21 had Sanders type III fractures. Postoperative imaging revealed significant improvements in calcaneal alignment, including the Böhler and Gissane angles. Excellent or good restoration of the subtalar joint surface was achieved in 83.8% of the patients, as confirmed by postoperative CT scans. The average AOFAS Ankle–Hindfoot score at the final follow-up was 93.84 ± 7.92. The mean range of motion for ankle flexion–extension was 64.35 ± 1.38 degrees, and for the internal–external motion of the subtalar joint, it was 29.27 ± 1.63 degrees, with no significant differences compared with the unaffected side. Only two patients developed subtalar osteoarthritis, and no cases of wound infection, skin necrosis, or sural nerve complications were reported.ConclusionThe use of a wrist external fixator to assist in the percutaneous reduction of intra-articular calcaneal fractures appears to be an effective technique for restoring the calcaneal structure. This approach enhances postoperative subtalar joint mobility and overall ankle‒hindfoot function while significantly reducing surgery-related complications.Keywords: Closed reduction, Complication, Intra-articular calcaneal fracture, Minimally invasive surgery

Background

Calcaneal fractures are the most common fractures of the tarsal bones, accounting for approximately 2% of all fractures [1, 2]. These fractures typically result from axial compression injuries and are often intra-articular. Effective management of Sanders type II fractures or above intra-articular calcaneal fractures relies on the restoration of both the subtalar joint and the calcaneal anatomy [3, 4]. However, the complex anatomy of this region, coupled with the often compromised condition of the surrounding soft tissue, presents significant surgical challenges. The complication rates associated with open reduction and internal fixation—such as wound infection, tissue necrosis, nerve damage, and joint stiffness—are notably high, ranging from 16.7 to 62.5% [5–9]. While the sinus tarsi approach may reduce some of these risks, it does not effectively address issues such as joint stiffness [10–12].

As an alternative, percutaneous reduction aims to preserve the integrity of the soft tissue around the subtalar joint, helping to maintain joint mobility and improve foot function postoperatively. However, percutaneous reduction often lacks effective auxiliary traction and temporary fixation, making it difficult to achieve anatomical realignment of the calcaneus and subtalar articulation without additional supportive techniques [8, 13–19]. The wrist external fixation device, which includes a central ball joint and side fixator, provides reverse traction and provisional fixation at specific angles. While primarily used for fractures and dislocations in the wrist region [20, 21], this device was introduced as a novel technique to assist in the reduction of intra-articular calcaneal fractures.

We hypothesized that the use of a wrist external fixator could aid in the morphological and articular restoration of these fractures. To evaluate the safety and efficacy of this technique, we conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with intra-articular calcaneal fractures treated at our hospital. This analysis focused on tracking complications, functional recovery, and postoperative imaging outcomes to assess the effectiveness of this approach.

Methods

Patients and study design

This retrospective study was approved by our institutional ethics board (2021KY034-KS001), with informed consent obtained from all participants. Between March 2015 and September 2018, patients with intra-articular calcaneal fractures who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged between 18 and 60 years; (2) had unilateral Sanders type II–III fractures; and (3) received treatment involving external fixator-assisted reduction and percutaneous screw fixation. Patients with multiple traumas, follow-up periods shorter than five years, incomplete clinical or radiographic data, or those who did not provide informed consent were excluded.

Surgical equipment

The equipment used in the study included a wrist external fixator (Fig. 1), cortical self-tapping screws, and headless cannulated compression screws (Johnson (Shanghai) Medical Devices Co., Ltd., USA).

Wrist external fixator: A The device features a central ball joint, with fixators on either side, allowing for multidirectional traction and temporary stabilization at the desired angles; B bilateral external fixators are in place during surgery

Operative procedure

Under general or spinal anesthesia, the patient was positioned laterally to facilitate fluoroscopic guidance (Fig. 2A-C). Two 3.5 mm diameter K-wires were inserted parallelly through the body and the calcaneal tubercles of the talus. Subsequently, bilateral wrist external fixators were applied via these K-wires as anchors.

Morphological Restoration of the Calcaneus: Reverse traction was applied via the wrist external fixator to restore the original length and height of the calcaneus. Adjustments to the Böhler and Gissane angles were made via the ball joint of the fixator, which was then secured in place. Lateral adjustments were performed to correct any varus or valgus deformity (Fig. 2D).

Lateral adjustments were performed to correct any varus or valgus deformity (Fig. 2D).

Reconstruction of the Subtalar Articular Surface: Through a lateral incision approximately 2 cm in length located along the fracture line of the lateral wall of the calcaneus (Fig. 2E), a periosteal elevator was employed to elevate the impacted subtalar articular surface. The realignment of the subtalar joint’s articular facets was guided by Brodén view radiographic imaging [22], with temporary stabilization achieved via K-wires.

Narrowing of the calcaneus and percutaneous screw fixation: Manual compression was applied to reduce the width of the calcaneus. Cannulated screws were inserted from the calcaneal tuberosity to the calcaneocuboid joint, with additional screws securing the restored length and height. Two cortical screws placed beneath the subtalar articular surface stabilized the joint fracture (Fig. 2F). The wrist external fixator was then removed, and the incision was closed.

Rehabilitation

Postoperatively, no plaster or brace was used for fixation. Patients began immediate range-of-motion exercises, including ankle flexion and extension as well as internal and external subtalar joint movements. Weight-bearing was restricted for the first six weeks after surgery. Partial weight-bearing was allowed starting at six weeks, whereas full weight-bearing was typically initiated approximately three months postsurgery, depending on the progress of fracture healing.

Clinical evaluation

The data collected included the time from injury to surgery, length of hospital stay, operative time, and blood loss. Postoperative evaluations focused on wound integrity, infection, and nerve injury. At the final follow-up, the American Orthopedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle–Hindfoot score [23] was assessed, along with the flexion‒extension range of motion of the ankle and the internal‒external range of motion of the subtalar joint.

Radiological examination

Bilateral lateral and axial radiographs of the calcaneus were used to evaluate the restoration of calcaneal dimensions, including length, height, width, Böhler angle, and Gissane angle. Computed tomography (CT) imaging was employed to assess the reconstruction of the subtalar joint surface. We followed the criteria established by Kurozumi et al. [24] to categorize the quality of reduction. Specifically, the absence of step-off, defects, or angulation in the joint was classified as an “excellent” reduction. Reductions with a step less than 1 mm, a defect less than 5 mm, or angulation less than 5° were considered “good.” A “fair” reduction was defined as a step of 1–3 mm, a defect of 5–10 mm, or angulation of 5–15°. Conversely, a reduction was deemed “poor” if it involved a step of 3 mm or more, a defect of 10 mm or more, or angulation exceeding 15°. In addition, CT scans were used to assess the presence of osteoarthritis in the subtalar joint. Traumatic arthritis was identified on the basis of criteria such as joint space narrowing, periarticular bone hypertrophy, or the presence of subchondral bone cysts [11].

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 26.0 was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize patient demographics, time from injury to surgery, length of hospital stay, operative time, blood loss, and AOFAS Ankle–Hindfoot scores. Independent t tests were used to compare the mean ranges of motion in the ankle and subtalar joints between the injured and noninjured sides. Variance analysis was used to assess the calcaneal dimensions, with the least significant difference test applied for intertemporal comparisons. Tamhane’s T2 test was used for datasets that did not meet the homogeneity of variances assumption. The chi-square test was applied to compare categorical variables. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Clinical outcomes

Among the 47 qualifying patients, 31 completed the study, resulting in a follow-up rate of 65.96% over a median duration of 72 months (range: 62–98 months), as shown in Table 1. The average time from injury to surgery was 3.2 ± 1.9 days. The mean length of hospital stay was 7.3 ± 3.1 days, the mean operative duration was 87.74 ± 30.30 min, and the average estimated blood loss was 41.45 ± 34.16 ml. The mean AOFAS Ankle–Hindfoot score was 93.84 ± 7.92, with 24 patients (77.4%) rated as excellent, six (19.4%) as good, and one (2.7%) as acceptable. The mean flexion–extension range of motion in the ankle joint was 64.80 ± 1.30° (range: 48.4–81.3°) on the noninjured side and 64.35 ± 1.38° (range: 46.4–80.2°) on the injured side, with no significant difference (p =.811). The average range of motion for the healthy subtalar joint was 35.05 ± 1.27° (range: 18.9°−47.4°), whereas it was 29.26 ± 1.63° for the injured joint (range: 11.5°−43.1°), with no significant difference detected (p =.051).

Complications

Postoperative CT revealed one case in which an extruding screw head affected the calcaneocuboid joint surface, which required screw revision six weeks prior to initiating partial weight-bearing. Two patients developed traumatic arthritis: one presented severe arthritis due to inadequate subtalar reduction at 35 months postsurgery (Fig. 4), and the other presented mild arthritis at 24 months, with subtalar surface reduction classified as fair. There were no reported incidences of wound infection, necrosis, or sural nerve injury.

Discussion

This study introduced the use of a wrist external fixator as an innovative technique to aid in the percutaneous reduction of intra-articular calcaneal fractures. Our findings revealed significant improvements in calcaneal alignment, including the Böhler angle and Gissane angle. In addition, CT scans revealed that over 83.8% of the subtalar articular surfaces achieved excellent or good reduction.

Percutaneous reduction can be performed through various approaches, including percutaneous balloon dilatation [25, 26], arthroscopy-assisted reduction [14, 15], and external fixator-assisted reduction [7–9, 27]. Among these methods, wrist external fixators offer a distinct advantage by enabling controlled reverse traction at specific angles, which helps restore the length, width, height, Böhler angle, and Gissane angle of the calcaneus. This technique allows temporary fixation of the fracture until successful reduction of the subtalar articular surface is accomplished. Our findings indicate that over 83.8% of subtalar articular surfaces achieved excellent and good reduction on the basis of CT scans, surpassing outcomes associated with open reduction (65.1%) [26].

The reduction in calcaneal morphology is crucial for achieving favorable clinical outcomes, with the Böhler and Gissane angles serving as important indicators for assessing the degree of calcaneal height loss and posterior talocalcaneal joint surface collapse [3]. A decrease in the Böhler angle or an increase in the Gissane angle suggests more severe posterior collapse of the joint. The importance of maintaining subtalar articular congruity is highlighted by evidence indicating that even a 1 mm loss in subtalar articular reduction can lead to long-term traumatic arthritis [14, 28]. While external fixators offer an advantage in correcting calcaneal deformities by enabling targeted traction adjustments and maintaining temporary fixation [7–9], their inability to provide direct visualization may result in suboptimal subtalar surface reductions [29]. In our study, 16.2% of patients failed to meet anatomical reduction standards, and two patients developed traumatic arthritis over a 5-year follow-up. With prolonged follow-up, the symptoms of foot pain in these patients may worsen, and tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis may be necessary in the future [30].

Percutaneous screw fixation, while effective, is associated with an inherent risk of reduction loss [15, 17]. Grün et al. reported a significant decrease in the Böhler angle from 27.7° immediately postsurgery to 20.3° at the final follow-up following percutaneous reduction for intra-articular calcaneal fractures [15]. To mitigate the risk of reduction loss, our approach involves securing the major fracture fragment, including the subtalar articular surface, with at least two screws. In addition, we emphasize the importance of initiating both active and assisted exercises for the ankle and subtalar joints postoperatively. A well-structured weight-bearing schedule, carefully aligned with the stages of fracture healing, is essential to avoid fracture reduction loss that could result from inadequate rehabilitation strategies. Importantly, our study revealed no instances of reduction loss, as confirmed by radiographic and CT evaluations during the postoperative follow-up period.

Perry et al. emphasized the critical role of the subtalar joint in counterbalancing forces during ankle movement and providing cushioning for hindfoot motion. As such, maintaining subtalar joint mobility is essential for overall foot function [31]. A study examining the open reduction of intra-articular calcaneal fractures via the sinus tarsi approach reported that 25% of patients experienced moderate to significant restrictions in subtalar joint mobility postoperatively [11]. This limitation is often attributed to joint stiffness, which may result from damage to adjacent subtalar soft tissues [11, 32]. In contrast, the technique of percutaneous poking reduction minimizes damage to surrounding tissues, effectively preserving joint mobility [33]. Our study demonstrated that the range of motion in injured ankles was comparable to that in healthy ankles, with the injured subtalar joint maintaining 88.6% of the motion observed on the unaffected side. Furthermore, patients reported high scores on the AOFAS Ankle‒Hindfoot scale, with an excellent/good rating of 96.8%, surpassing the outcomes typically observed with traditional open reduction and internal fixation methods [23].

Percutaneous reduction is particularly suitable for fresh Sanders type II–III calcaneal fractures but is not recommended for Sanders type IV fractures or older injuries [14, 18, 19, 27]. In cases involving crushed calcaneal nodules or severe osteoporosis, traction reduction may not be effective. In our study, two patients initially scheduled for poking reduction had their surgical plans altered because of crushed calcaneal nodules and an old calcaneal fracture that was 26 days postinjury. As a result, their treatment was switched from poking reduction to open reduction and internal fixation.

Compared with open surgery, closed reduction with percutaneous screw fixation offers several advantages. It reduces the time to surgery, shortens the length of hospital stay, and minimizes the need for tourniquets. Close reduction also contributes to less blood loss and a lower incidence of surgery-related complications [5, 7–9, 17]. In our study, we did not encounter any cases of infection, necrosis, or sural nerve injury. The only exception was one patient who required a screw revision due to issues with the length of the screw, which was performed six weeks after the surgery.

This study is a retrospective observational analysis. Notably, the research did not include controlled studies for comparisons with outcomes from the extensile lateral approach or the tarsal sinus approach. Moreover, the number of patients followed up in this study was limited. While the minimum follow-up period was five years, longer-term follow-up studies are needed. Such studies are crucial for a more comprehensive evaluation of the safety and efficacy of this technique, particularly in diagnosing subtalar arthritis.

In conclusion, the use of a wrist external fixator-assisted poking reduction technique has proven effective in restoring the morphology of the calcaneus. This technique has significant potential for improving postoperative subtalar joint mobility and overall ankle-hindfoot function while also reducing the incidence of surgery-related complications.
