Trends of Adjuvant Radiation After TORS in Clinically Node Negative Oropharyngeal Cancer
Authors: Najm S Khan, Craig A Bollig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveFrequency of adjuvant treatment for clinically node negative (cN0) oropharyngeal cancer (OPC) patients, candidates for single modality treatment, is not well established. We aimed to determine the rate and clinical predictors of adjuvant radiation therapy (aRT) among cT1–T2 N0 patients undergoing transoral robotic surgery (TORS).MethodsThe National Cancer Database was retrospectively reviewed for cT1–T2 N0 OPC patients who received TORS as definitive treatment between 2010 and 2017. Demographic, oncologic data, and the proportion of patients receiving aRT were collected. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to determine clinical predictors of aRT. Adjusted Odds Ratios (aOR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (95% CI) were calculated.Results1173 cN0 OPC patients underwent TORS (mean age 62 ± 9 years). 95% of patients were pT1–T2 and 59% were pN0. Overall, 29% of patients received aRT, including 21% of cT1 patients and 37% of cT2 patients. 32% of patients with tonsil/lateral pharyngeal wall and 29% with base of tongue tumors received aRT. Among those with negative margins, 25% received aRT. Positive margins (aOR 4.0, 95% CI 2.7–5.8), base of tongue subsite (aOR 2.7, 95% CI 1.5–4.7), and salivary gland malignancies (aOR 2.9, 95% CI 1.6–5.3) had an increased likelihood of adjuvant RT. Compared to pT1 patients, pT2 was associated with receiving adjuvant RT (aOR 1.9, 95% CI 1.4–2.6). HPV status was not a significant predictor of aRT.ConclusionNationally, most cT1–T2 N0 OPC patients undergoing TORS ultimately do not receive adjuvant radiation therapy, thus representing excellent candidates for single‐modality treatment in most cases.Level of EvidenceN/A.Keywords: adjuvant radiation therapy, node negative cancer, oropharyngeal cancer, transoral robotic surgery

Introduction

Head and neck cancers account for 4% of all cancers within the United States, with incidence rates increasing by 15.5% since 2003 [1, 2]. As disease pathology has concomitantly shifted to increases in human papillomavirus (HPV)‐related cancer, the oropharynx has now become the most common subsite [3]. Treatment pathways for oropharyngeal cancer (OPC) include primary surgery with risk‐directed adjuvant treatment versus primary radiation +/− chemotherapy [4]. Adjuvant therapy is generally indicated for patients with locally advanced primary tumors (pT3 or pT4), positive surgical margins, perineural invasion (PNI) / lymphovascular (LVI) invasion, multiple positive lymph nodes, large lymph nodes (3 cm), or lymph nodes with extranodal extension (ENE) [4]. Meanwhile, the elucidation of improved survival with HPV‐associated disease [5, 6] has garnered concentration on deintensification strategies to address early‐stage primary oropharyngeal squamous cell cancers (OPSCC).

Since obtaining Food and Drug Administration approval in 2009, transoral robotic surgery (TORS) has been increasingly utilized as a primary surgical treatment for early stage OPC [7, 8, 9]. It has revolutionized OPC management by demonstrating significantly improved functional outcomes over traditional surgical approaches to the oropharynx, with most patients returning to regular diets over time [8, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Comparable functional and oncologic results have been described with TORS with risk‐directed adjuvant treatment versus primary non‐surgical treatment in HPV‐associated OPC, and some national data indicate improved oncologic outcomes with TORS in HPV‐negative OPC [14, 15]. Several retrospective and prospective studies have demonstrated superior functional outcomes following TORS in patients treated with surgery alone versus those receiving adjuvant treatment [16, 17, 18, 19]. Therefore, there is considerable interest in identifying appropriate patients eligible for single modality treatment. The national frequency of adjuvant radiation therapy (aRT) among early stage OPC patients without clinically evident nodal disease (cT1–T2 N0) is not well established. This study aimed to determine the rate and clinical variables associated with aRT among patients with cT1–T2 N0 OPC undergoing transoral robotic surgery (TORS).

Materials and Methods

Data Source and Patient Selection

After the study was determined to be exempt from institutional review board review by Rutgers Robert Wood Johnson University Hospital in New Brunswick, we obtained the 2017 participant user file from the NCDB to perform a retrospective review of patients ≥ 18 years old diagnosed with OPC undergoing TORS. The NCDB is a national hospital‐based registry maintained as a joint project of the American Cancer Society and the Commission on Cancer of the American College of Surgeons. The NCDB currently captures > 70% of newly diagnosed cancers from > 1500 facilities in the United States. There are established criteria to certify the quality of the submitted data, as well as an application process to obtain the de‐identified data. After distribution of the data, the Commission on Cancer of the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society is not responsible for the statistical analysis or the conclusions presented by the investigators.

The NCDB was queried for all patients ≥ 18 years old that were diagnosed with an OPC between 2010 and 2017. Records were identified using morphologic (histologic) and topographic codes from the International Classification of Disease for Oncology, 3rd Edition. Morphologic codes included squamous cell carcinoma including variants (8070–8076, 8083) and salivary gland malignancies, which were composed of: Adenocarcinoma NOS (8140), Adenoid Cystic Carcinoma (8200), Polymorphous Low‐Grade Adenocarcinoma [PLGA] (8525), Mucoepidermoid Carcinoma (8430), and other rare types (8550, 8562, 8310, 8147, 8440, 8480, 8290, 8500, 8980, 8940). The topographical codes that were used included base of tongue [C01.9, C02.4], tonsil/lateral pharyngeal wall [C09.0, C09.1, C09.8, C09.9, C10.2], and other (soft palate [C05.1, C05.2], vallecula [C10.0], posterior pharyngeal wall [C10.3], and overlapping lesion/not otherwise specified [C10.8, C10.9]). The robotic surgical approach code was used to identify the subset of patients that underwent TORS. The subset of patients with cT1–T2 N0 M0 was included. Patients were excluded if they had distant metastatic disease (M1), grossly positive margins, or missing data. Because treating facility type is suppressed for patients < 40 years of age, these patients were excluded from the analysis as well.

Patient Variables and Statistical Analysis

Baseline patient characteristics included age, sex, race, insurance status, Charlson Deyo Comorbidity Class (CDCC), facility type, facility volume, tumor subsite, clinical histologic type (HPV‐positive squamous cell carcinoma, HPV‐negative squamous cell carcinoma, unknown HPV squamous cell carcinoma, and salivary gland carcinomas), clinical T classification, pathologic T/N classification, and surgical margin status. For this analysis, patients were considered HPV‐positive if they had positive testing for high‐risk HPV type 16 or 18. They were considered HPV‐negative if they were tested for HPV but negative for high‐risk HPV types 16 and 18. If it was not known if the patient had HPV testing, they were grouped as unknown HPV squamous cell carcinoma. High‐volume facilities were defined as the facilities with the top 5% of annual treatment volume, and the remainder was designated as low‐volume facilities. A Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) flow diagram is reported describing the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure 1).

Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) flow chart describing inclusion and exclusion criteria for patients.

The main outcome of interest, the proportion of patients receiving aRT, was then calculated overall as well as in clinically relevant subgroups. Variables associated with receipt of aRT were then identified using logistic regression. A multivariable model was created to identify variables independently associated with our aRT. Clinically relevant factors that were statistically significant (p < 0.05) on univariable testing were then included in the initial multivariable logistic regression model. A backward elimination procedure was used to obtain a model containing only predictor variables whose coefficients in the model were significant at the 0.05 level. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and associated 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for each model. For all analyses, the threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. SPSS v29 software was used for data analysis (SPSS Inc., an IBM Company, Chicago, Illinois).

Data and Code Availability

The data analyzed in this study is from a de‐identified participant user file of the NCDB. This data is not publicly available and qualified investigators associated with the Commission on Cancer may request the user file through an application process. The script and analytic code utilized to complete this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

A total of 1173 OPC patients underwent TORS between 2010 and 2017 (Table 1). The mean age was 62.3 ± 9.2 years, the majority were males (n = 875, 74.6%), white (n = 1041, 88.7%), and had either private insurance or Medicare (n = 1033, 88.1%). Most were treated at academic centers (n = 946, 80.6%) and had a Charlson‐Deyo comorbidity score of 0 (n = 871, 74.3%). Overall, 369 (31.5%) of patients were treated at high‐volume facilities. The most common tumor subsites involved were tonsil/lateral pharyngeal wall (n = 612, 52.2%) and base of tongue (BOT) (n = 423, 36.1%). Overall, 31.4% (n = 368) were HPV‐positive, 34.0% (n = 399) were HPV‐negative, and 28.1% (n = 330) had unknown HPV status. Following surgical resection, 93.5% (n = 1097) of patients were classified with pT1–T2 disease and 69.4% (n = 814) with pN0–N1 disease.

In total, 343 (29.2%) TORS patients received aRT, including 24.8% (n = 244) of patients with negative surgical margins. In terms of primary sites, 30.8% (n = 319) of patients with tonsil/lateral pharyngeal wall and BOT tumors underwent aRT (Figure 2). Examining tumor characteristics, 32.6% (n = 120) of patients with HPV positive and 28.6% (n = 114) of HPV negative tumors received aRT. 33.2% (n = 79) of T1–T2 tonsil and 31.3% (n = 36) T1–T2 BOT HPV positive tumors received aRT, while 31.5% (n = 65) of T1–T2 tonsil and 26.5% (n = 35) T1–T2 BOT HPV negative tumors received aRT. Regarding staging, 27.3% (n = 299) of patients with pT1–T2 and 24.6% (n = 200) of patients with pN0–N1 disease underwent aRT.

Proportion of patients receiving adjuvant radiation therapy in various populations.

Clinical variables associated with aRT are reported in Table 2. Factors associated with an increased likelihood of aRT included surgical positive margins (OR 4.0, 95% CI 2.7–5.8), BOT subsite (OR 2.7, 95% CI 1.5–4.7), salivary gland malignancies (OR 2.9, 95% CI 1.6–5.3), and pathologic T/N classification. In terms of staging, pT2 (OR 1.9, 95% CI 1.4–2.6), pT3 (OR 3.8, 95% CI 2.0–7.3), and pT4 (OR 5.3, 95% CI 1.7–16.1) were more likely to receive aRT. Increased pN staging (pN1 OR 4.7 95% CI 3.1–7.2; pN2/3 OR 18.6 95% CI 11.0–31.6) was also associated with a higher likelihood of aRT. A patient's HPV status did not significantly differentiate their predilection for aRT. This model demonstrated good discriminatory ability with a c‐index of 0.807. There were no concerns for collinearity, with tolerance values ranging from 0.916–0.956 and variance inflation factors from 1.046 to 1.092.

Discussion

TORS has been shown to have an integral role in the management of both HPV‐associated and HPV‐negative OPC. Although, with the epidemiologic shifts over the last few decades to increasing incidence of HPV‐associated OPC, the debate for the optimal treatment in this population between surgical‐based treatment versus radiation therapy‐based treatment remains ongoing. Given the improved prognosis of HPV‐associated OPC, there has been significant emphasis on treatment de‐intensification in low‐intermediate‐risk patients to reduce the risk of long‐term toxicities and the associated decreased quality of life. Although HPV‐negative OPC is associated with comparatively more aggressive disease, those with early‐stage, low‐risk disease are also candidates for single‐modality treatment. From a national perspective, the prevalence of aRT administration in clinically node‐negative OPC is not well described; therefore, in this NCDB study, we investigated the prevalence of aRT administration in this population.

We found that the majority of cN0 patients with both HPV‐negative and HPV‐associated OPC who underwent definitive treatment with TORS ultimately received surgery alone as their treatment. Numerous studies have generally shown similar quality of life outcomes between surgical‐based treatment and radiation therapy‐based treatment, with slightly different treatment toxicity patterns, most notably the ORATOR trial [20]. However, these results have largely been driven by the proportion of patients that receive adjuvant treatment (71% in the ORATOR trial) [17, 20].

Functional outcomes after TORS alone compare very favorably with primary RT, with some studies reporting superior results with TORS [16, 17, 18, 19]. In a retrospective cohort study, Xu et al. administered five different QOL questionnaires to 76 patients with T1–T2, N0–N1 HPV‐associated OPC, undergoing TORS alone, TORS with adjuvant radiation ± chemotherapy, and nonsurgical treatment [16]. TORS with adjuvant therapy and primary nonsurgical treatment were associated with similar QOL scores, while those treated with TORS alone were associated with significantly higher QOL scores. Similar results have been shown with objective assessments of modified barium swallow studies graded per Dynamic Imaging Grade of Swallowing Toxicity (DIGEST). In an analysis of a prospectively collected registry of 257 patients with low‐intermediate risk HPV‐associated OPC, Hutcheson et al. reported similar rates of moderate to severe dysphagia at 3–6 months between patients treated with TORS with adjuvant treatment (13%) versus radiation/chemoradiation therapy alone (16%) [19]. However, notably, the prevalence of severe dysphagia at 3–6 months in the population of patients treated with TORS alone was significantly lower (0%).

Appropriate patient selection reduces the risk of patients receiving triple‐modality therapy in HPV‐associated disease and the associated long‐term toxicities. We also investigated clinical features associated with aRT. These results came back as anticipated, as they are all traditional indications for post‐operative adjuvant therapy according to NCCN guidelines [4]. Patients with positive surgical margins, advanced pathologic T classification (T3/T4), or pathologic N classification > N1 were all more likely to receive aRT. Interestingly, patients with base of tongue tumors were more likely to receive aRT, independent of T/N classification or surgical margins. It is unclear what this may be attributed to. Notably, no significant difference was seen between patients with HPV‐associated versus HPV‐negative disease.

The present work should be interpreted with limitations in mind. As with any large database, the NCDB may contain coding errors, incomplete data, and selection bias. Additionally, the NCDB does not contain information on PNI, LVI, or extranodal extension, characteristics that may be factors in recommending aRT. Nonetheless, the model implemented in this study had excellent discriminatory ability (AUC 0.807). Lastly, this analysis only considers the proportion of patients that ultimately receive aRT and would not capture patients who do not have an appropriate functional status to tolerate treatment or those who refuse treatment.

Conclusion

In this large national analysis, most cT1–T2 N0 OPC patients undergoing TORS ultimately do not receive adjuvant treatment; thus, this population represents excellent candidates for single modality treatment in most cases.
