Association between Daily Physical Activities and Clinical Knee Osteoarthritis: A Community-Based Study
Authors: Rudy Hidayat, Fara Fauzia, Faisal Parlindungan, Suryo Anggoro Kusumo Wibowo, Anna Ariane, Johanda Damanik, Abirianty Priandani Araminta, Jessica Audrey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Osteoarthritis (OA) is a leading cause of disability, especially in the knee. The relationship between daily physical activities and knee OA remains unclear. This study aimed to examine the association between daily physical activities and knee OA in an Indonesian population.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the Community-Oriented Program for Control of Rheumatic Diseases (COPCORD) survey in Jakarta. Information on age, sex, body mass index, daily physical activities, occupational types, and sports participation was collected through interviews. Knee OA was identified using the American College of Rheumatology clinical classification criteria. Bivariate and multivariate analyses were performed to assess the associations between these factors and knee OA.Results:A total of 534 participants were included, with a median age of 47 years (range: 18–82). Among them, 71 (13.3%) met the criteria for knee OA. Female sex (prevalence ratio [PR] 3.05, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.63–5.71), obesity (PR 2.51, 95% CI 1.45–4.36), and age ≥50 years (PR 6.16, 95% CI 3.42–11.12) were significantly associated with knee OA in multivariate analysis. While laborious and housekeeping jobs were linked to OA in bivariate analysis, this association was not significant in the multivariate model. No significant associations were found between knee OA and daily physical activities, including standing, walking, squatting, climbing, knee-bending, cycling, and carrying.Conclusion:Daily physical activities were not significantly associated with knee OA. However, established risk factors such as female sex, obesity, and age ≥50 years were confirmed to be significant predictors of knee OA in this study.

INTRODUCTION

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disease that primarily affects the cartilage along with its surrounding tissues.[1] The resulting joint pain and stiffness cause a great burden to the population, ranking it as the 11th highest contributor to global disability.[23] The knee joint is the most commonly affected joint, accounting for approximately 6% of the adult population. As the risk of developing OA increases with age, its prevalence is expected to rise alongside the growing aging population.[4] In addition to its associated physical problems, OA also carries substantial psychological, social, and economic burdens.[3] Considering its disabling nature and lack of a cure, understanding its preventable risk factors is of great importance.

The etiology of OA is considered to be multifactorial, involving a combination of genetic, environmental, and biomechanical factors. Established risk factors for knee OA include older age, female gender, obesity, genetics, and a history of knee injury.[5] However, the role of physical activities in OA development has yielded inconsistent findings. Excessive mechanical loading on chondrocytes disrupts the balance between anabolic and catabolic processes, favoring catabolic processes that lead to cartilage defects, thinning, and OA development.[6] As physical activities may increase the risk of excessive joint loading and use, they may be a possible contributor to OA development. Nevertheless, physical activities also strengthen muscles and support joints, which is beneficial to reduce pain and improve physical function.[78]

Daily physical activities include sports, occupational activities, as well as leisure or non-occupational activities. Several studies have shown that frequent and prolonged squatting or kneeling increases the risk of OA development and progression.[910] However, another population-based study found no significant association between daily occupational and non-occupational activities and knee OA.[8] These varied findings suggest the complex role of daily physical activities in relation to knee OA. Nevertheless, it is important to identify the specific type, duration, and intensity of daily physical activities that may contribute to OA development. This knowledge is essential for implementing appropriate preventive measures and avoiding joint overuse.

The World Health Organization (WHO) together with the International League Against Rheumatism (ILAR) have initiated the Community-Oriented Program for Control of Rheumatic Diseases (COPCORD). In Indonesia, COPCORD was a community-based study which aimed to screen for musculoskeletal symptoms and associated disability, including OA. Using this COPCORD data, the present study aims to evaluate the association between daily physical activities and clinical knee OA within an urban community setting. Additionally, we also aim to investigate the risk factors associated with knee OA in an Indonesian urban population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study utilizes data from the COPCORD home-to-home survey conducted in 2023 across five administrative cities in Jakarta. A multistage stratified random sampling method was employed, where a random district was selected from each city, followed by the selection of two subdistricts from each district. This calculation resulted in a minimum required sample size of 384.14 (rounded to 385) participants. To ensure adequate representation, we decided to collect 100 samples from each subdistrict, yielding a total minimum of 500 subjects from the five administrative cities.

Participants eligible for this study were adults aged 18 years and above. Those who consented to participate were interviewed by trained physicians using the WHO-ILAR COPCORD questionnaire, which assesses musculoskeletal symptoms and related disabilities. This questionnaire had been translated and validated in the Indonesian language.[1314] Participants reporting knee symptoms were further evaluated by a rheumatologist according to the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria for clinical knee OA.[15]

Assessments

Subjects were classified as having OA if they met at least three out of six clinical criteria for knee OA: age >50 years, morning stiffness lasting less than 30 minutes, crepitus, bony tenderness, bony enlargement, and no palpable warmth.[15] During the interview, age, sex, and body weight and height were recorded. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (in kilograms) by height (in meters) squared and classified into obese (BMI ≥25 kg/m2) and non-obese (BMI <25 kg/m2) according to the WHO Asia-Pacific BMI classification.[16]

Daily activities performed for at least one year were obtained from interviews and were recorded as the average number of minutes per day each participant spent standing, walking on flat ground, walking uphill or downhill, sitting on the floor or a chair, cycling, performing knee-bending activities, squatting, carrying loads, and climbing stairs. For this study, knee-bending activities were defined as movements involving repeated flexion and extension of the knee without assuming a full squatting posture. Examples include bending the knees while cleaning, gardening, lifting objects from the floor in a semi-flexed position, and other household or work-related tasks that require repetitive knee flexion. In contrast, squatting referred specifically to maintaining a full squat position, with the hips and buttocks lowered toward the heels, below knee level.

Participation in sports over the last six months was also recorded, specifying the type (e.g., running/jogging, fitness/gym/bodybuilding, soccer, volleyball, or others) and duration in minutes per week. Occupation details were collected during the interview, including the type of job, perceived level of physical activity involved (classified as low, moderate, or high), and the duration in years the participant had been engaged in that job. Occupational activities were categorized into sedentary (jobs involving a low level of physical activity, such as office work, freelancing, or remote work), laborious (jobs involving moderate to high levels of physical activity, such as farming, construction work, factory work, and other forms of manual labor), and housekeeping/housewives (reflecting a large proportion of the female population engaged in non-formal work as housewives).

Statistical analysis

Univariate analyses were performed to summarize numerical variables as mean and standard deviation for normally distributed data, and median and interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions. Bivariate analyses, using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, were conducted to explore the associations between age, sex, BMI, daily living activities, sports participation, occupational activities, and OA. Variables with a bivariate P value < 0.25 were entered into the multivariate logistic regression model. A threshold of 0.25 was used to avoid excluding potential confounders that may exhibit weak associations in unadjusted analyses but become important predictors after adjustment for other variables. Multivariate results were presented as adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) with 95% confidence interval (CI). All analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.

RESULTS

A total of 534 subjects participated in the study, and 71 (13.3%) had knee OA. Most subjects were under 50 years (60.3%), with a median age of 47 years. Females constituted 55.8% of all subjects, and 69.1% were non-obese. A majority of them did not participate in sports (69.9%) and performed laborious jobs (44.4%). DISCUSSION

This cross-sectional, community-based study provides additional insights into the association between daily activities and OA, particularly within the Indonesian population. Our findings indicate that daily activities—including standing, walking uphill or downhill, walking on flat ground, sitting on the floor or chair, cycling, knee-bending activities, carrying loads, and climbing stairs—do not appear to be associated with an increased risk of knee OA.

Previous research on the relationship between daily or physical activities and OA has produced conflicting results. For instance, the Framingham cohort study found no association between walking, engaging in vigorous activities, or higher activity levels and OA.[17] Similarly, Hootman et al.[18] reported that increased joint-associated physical activities did not correlate with a higher risk of OA. The second phase of the WHO-COPCORD study conducted in Tehran also found no links between standing, walking, squatting, kneeling, climbing, lifting, and carrying loads with OA.[8] In contrast, Dahaghin et al.[10] identified prolonged squatting and cycling as risk factors for knee OA, whereas prolonged standing, walking uphill or downhill, and sitting were not.

Conversely, other studies have reported opposite findings. A case-control study in Germany identified kneeling or squatting activities as predictors of OA, and daily lifting and carrying loads as increasing the risk of OA in women.[19] A population-based study in Thailand found that certain knee-bending positions, such as side knee-bending and lotus position, as well as habitual lifetime squatting, elevated OA risk.[20] Climbing stairs and lifting heavy weights were also linked to an increased risk of knee OA in both men and women.[21] These discrepancies may stem from variations in definitions, cutoff points, and categorization of physical activities across different studies.

In our population, the absence of a significant association between daily activities and knee OA may reflect several factors. First, many of the activities recorded were likely of mild to moderate intensity and may not have imposed sufficient repetitive mechanical load to the knee joint. We recorded only the time spent performing each activity, without objective measurement of joint load or intensity, and substantial variability likely existed in how participants performed these activities. Stratifying activities by intensity, for example, by load carried, distance walked, or elevation change, might have revealed a closer relationship between daily activities and knee OA. Second, the duration of potentially high-load activities in our cohort may not have reached the cumulative thresholds associated with OA risk in previous studies, particularly those estimating lifetime exposure of these activities.[2223] Third, measurement‐related factors may have contributed. Previous studies have shown that self‐reported physical activity frequently overestimates moderate to vigorous activity and underestimates light activity and sedentary time when compared with accelerometer‐based measurements.[2425] Finally, the broad classification of “laborious jobs” may have combined occupations with widely varying biomechanical demands. This grouping could have diluted the effects of specific high-load occupations, such as agricultural, construction, or industrial work, which have been more consistently associated with elevated knee OA risk in earlier studies.[2326] Collectively, these factors may have attenuated associations in our analysis.

Known risk factors for OA include female sex, obesity, and age, and our study’s results align with these established factors. A meta-analysis indicated that a BMI of ≥24 kg/m2, female gender, and age ≥40 years are significant risk factors for OA.[27] This finding is supported by another systematic review, which reported an increased risk of OA associated with obesity.[28] Additionally, research indicated that the interaction between high BMI and kneeling/squatting had a multiplicative effect, further increasing OA risk.[29] Traditionally, the link between obesity and OA has been attributed to the mechanical “wear-and-tear” effect, but recent studies highlight that obesity also contributes to systemic inflammation, which promotes cartilage matrix destruction through increased degradative enzymes and cytokines.[6]

A key strength of this study is its population-based design and the use of a multistage stratified random sampling method, which limits selection bias and enhances the generalizability of the findings to the Indonesian population. In addition, interviewers were specifically trained to ensure consistency in data collection. However, there are several limitations. First, data on daily physical activity were self-reported by interviewer-administered questionnaires, which may be prone to recall or reporting bias. Objective measures such as accelerometers or observational assessments could provide more accurate estimates of activity patterns. Furthermore, information on the intensity of each activity that may affect joint loading was not obtained. Second, although the ACR clinical criteria are widely used, classifying knee OA without radiographic confirmation may lead to misclassification bias, particularly underdiagnosing radiographic OA or misclassifying early or minimally symptomatic cases. Third, despite the use of multistage random sampling, individuals who were more likely to be at home during survey hours, such as housewives or those not working outside the home, may be overrepresented, while manual laborers working outside may be underrepresented, introducing potential selection bias. Fourth, several exposure categories (e.g., cycling ≥30 minutes/day) included small numbers of participants, which may limit the ability to detect associations for less common activities. Finally, the study’s cross-sectional design limits the ability to confirm whether the exposure precedes the outcome (OA). Longitudinal studies are needed to verify the associations between daily physical activities and OA.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study found no significant association between self-reported, duration-based daily physical activities and knee OA. However, we identified female sex, obesity, and age ≥50 years as significant risk factors for OA. These findings highlight the importance of targeting modifiable factors such as weight management and early detection in high-risk groups, particularly older and obese women. Further studies incorporating objective measures of physical activity are recommended to better elucidate the relationship between daily activities and knee OA.
