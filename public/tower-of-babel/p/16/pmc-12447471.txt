Astragaloside IV reduces diabetic kidney injury by inhibiting the PKC/NOX4/MAPK pathway
Authors: Wenhan Bai, Yuan Yang, Limin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic nephropathy (DN) is a major complication of diabetes and a leading cause of end-stage renal disease. This study investigated the renoprotective effects and underlying mechanisms of Astragaloside IV (AS) in a rat model of type 2 diabetes induced by a high-fat diet and low-dose streptozotocin. The rats received AS treatment (20, 40, or 80 mg/kg) for 13 weeks. AS significantly improved blood glucose and lipid profiles, enhanced the glomerular filtration rate, and reduced kidney injury without inducing hepatic toxicity. Histological staining, including hematoxylin and eosin, Masson’s trichrome, and periodic acid–Schiff staining, revealed attenuation of glomerular hypertrophy, mesangial expansion, and interstitial fibrosis. These improvements were corroborated by molecular analyses showing the downregulation of kidney injury molecule-1 and fibronectin, along with the restoration of nephrin expression at both the mRNA and protein levels. AS also attenuated high glucose-induced oxidative stress both in vivo and in vitro, as indicated by reduced reactive oxygen species and malondialdehyde levels and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, including superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase in diabetic renal tissues and high glucose-stimulated HBZY-1 cells. Mechanistically, AS inhibited protein pinase C (PKC) beta expression and downstream NADPH Oxidase 4 activation, leading to suppression of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling (ERK, p38, JNK) and NF-κB phosphorylation, thereby reducing inflammatory cytokine production. siRNA-based knockdown experiments further validated the PKC-β/NOX4 axis in mediating these protective effects. Collectively, AS alleviates diabetic renal injury by modulating the PKC-β/NOX4/MAPK pathway to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, supporting its potential as a therapeutic candidate for DN.

Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN), a prevalent and severe complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), is a leading cause of end-stage renal disease [1]. The incidence of DN has been rising yearly in both developed and developing countries, and approximately 25–50% of patients with DM are at risk of developing DN [2,3]. The main clinical manifestations of DN are proteinuria and specific changes in kidney morphology and function [4,5]. Its pathological characteristics include the accumulation of glomerular mesangial cells and extracellular matrix, diffuse thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, and ultimately nodular glomerulosclerosis [6]. DN is associated with glucose and lipid metabolism disorders and oxidative stress response [3,7], and this process involves multiple signal transduction pathways and the corresponding cytokines.

Astragaloside IV (AS) is the primary bioactive component of Astragalus, exhibiting the highest concentration and broadest pharmacological activity [8]. AS exhibits multiple pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, anti-oxidative stress, anti-diabetic, and cardioprotective effects, which can be triggered by the regulation of different signaling pathways [9]. Reportedly, under ultraviolet irradiation, AS delays or inhibits keratinocyte oxidation by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) or acts as an exogenous antioxidant to maintain or reestablish redox homeostasis [10]. The increased expression of pro-inflammatory factors disrupts homeostasis of the inflammatory microenvironment, and AS regulates NF-κB signaling, the core of the inflammatory response, to reduce neuroinflammation [11]. NF-κB cooperates with numerous signaling pathways and can crosstalk through different enzymes, such as GSK-3β, MAPK p38, which govern NF-κB transcriptional activity or impact upstream signals [12–14]. AS is also considered a potential natural drug for the treatment of systemic inflammation and cardiovascular diseases because it inhibits protein kinase C (PKC) activation in umbilical vein endothelial cells [15].

PKC is a family of enzymes that can be activated by 1,2-diacylglycerol (DAG) and other lipids [16,17]. Multiple isoforms have been identified in different cell types. Hyperglycemia can activate PKC, which increases the expression of TGF-β1, leading to increased production of glomerular extracellular matrix [18]. Studies have suggested that glucose concentration-induced PKC activation mediates the inhibitory effects of glucose on insulin-stimulated chemokines [19,20]. PKC activation also induces ROS synthesis, promotes the expression of cell adhesion factors in mesangial cells, leads to the aggregation of leukocytes in the glomeruli, and accelerates glomerular damage [21]. Therefore, AS may reduce DN-related damage by inhibiting PKC expression.

Renal mesangial expansion and inflammatory response under a hyperglycemic environment partially cause abnormal expansion of the renal mesangial area, and abnormal mesangial cells can trigger early lesions in DN [22,23]. In this study, DM rats and high glucose (HG)-treated mesangial cells were used to explore the role of AS in renal injury and its regulatory pathways. These findings will enhance our understanding of the pathological mechanisms underlying DN and promote the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

DM Rat model establishment

Sprague Dawley rats (male, 180 ± 20 g) purchased from the Zhejiang Vital River Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (animal production license no: SCXK (Zhe) 2019-0001) were randomly assigned as control and DM rats. The control rats received an AIN93G standard diet, whereas the other rats received a high-sugar and high-fat diet (HFD) for 4 weeks, followed by a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 30 mg/kg). This combined HFD + low-dose STZ protocol has been widely used to induce a type 2 diabetes-like phenotype with insulin resistance and partial β-cell dysfunction.

The rats exhibited persistent hyperglycemia, with fasting blood glucose levels >16.7 mmol/L for three consecutive days, confirming the successful establishment of the DM model prior to treatment initiation. Furthermore, the DM model rats were randomly divided into the following groups: DM, positive control (PC; losartan), and AS low, medium, and high administration groups (AS-L/M/H). The animal sample size (n = 6 per group) was determined based on prior studies and our preliminary data, ensuring a statistical power of over 80% to detect medium-to-large effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≥ 0.8) with a significance level of 0.05.

Rats in the treatment group were administered losartan (20 mg/kg) or AS (20, 40, and 80 mg/kg) daily by gavage, body weight was recorded, and urine was collected. Blood lipid triglyceride (TG), cholesterol (CHO), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels were measured using an RA-1000 automatic blood analyzer. After 9 weeks, the rats were euthanized using sodium pentobarbital (180 mg/kg; i.p.) and kidney tissues were collected. The selected dosages (20, 40, and 80 mg/kg) were based on previous studies of AS in kidney injury [24]. This study was approved by the Tongxiang First People’s Hospital. All methods were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and other relevant guidelines and regulations.

Cell culture and handling

Rat glomerular mesangial cells HBZY-1 (CL-0092, Procell) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Invitrogen) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. HBZY-1 cells were grown under normal glucose (NG; 5 mM glucose), osmotic control (MN; 5 mM glucose + 25 mM mannitol), and HG (30 mM glucose) conditions. The inclusion of mannitol as an osmotic control ensured that the observed cellular responses were due to hyperglycemia rather than osmotic stress. Cells in each group were treated with DMSO or AS-L (50 μM) and AS-H (100 μM).

To determine the optimal concentrations of AS for in vitro studies, HBZY-1 cells were exposed to HG and treated with increasing concentrations of AS (5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500 μM) for 24 h. Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay. Based on the results, 50 and 100 μM were selected for subsequent experiments.

Additionally, for mechanistic investigation, HBZY-1 cells cultured under HG conditions were transfected with siRNAs targeting PKC-β or NOX4, using jetPRIME® reagent (Polyplus) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 h, the cells were harvested for protein expression and ROS level analyses. Mannitol was not included in these experiments, as we specifically aimed to validate pathway relationships under HG conditions.

Glucose tolerance test (GTT)

The rats were subjected to GTT before euthanasia (sodium pentobarbital, 180 mg/kg; i.p.). The rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg, i.p.) throughout the experiment. Following an overnight fasting, a glucose solution (2 g/kg) was administered by oral gavage. Blood samples were collected from the lateral tail vein 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after glucose administration. Blood glucose concentrations were measured using a glucometer, and the area under the GTT curve was computed.

Histological analysis

Parts of the rat kidneys were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then routinely embedded in paraffin. The tissues were sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), and Masson stains. Histopathological features were examined using an optical microscope (Leica). H&E staining reflected the pathological morphological changes in the kidneys. ImageJ software was used to analyze the glomerular cross-sectional area and mesangial expansion coefficient based on PAS staining. Masson’s trichrome staining was used to evaluate the renal interstitial collagen deposition.

Biochemical index detection

The biochemical parameters were determined using commercial kits. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN; QC15190, Qcheng Bio), urine and serum creatinine (RC-E100229A, RUICHUANG Biology), and urine protein (BY-MK1287, BAIYI Biology) levels were used to evaluate renal function. The malondialdehyde (MDA; BLL-S7761G, BAILILAI), superoxide dismutase (SOD; im-E30267, Immunoclone), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; CSB-E12146r, Cusabio) levels were measured to indicate oxidative stress. The experimental operations were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was indirectly estimated by calculating the creatinine clearance rate (Ccr) using the following formula: Ccr (mL/min) = (urine creatinine × V)/(serum creatinine × T). V is urine volume (mL), SCr is serum creatinine concentration (μmol/L), and T is collection time (min, typically 1440 min for 24 h).

Liver toxicity assessment

To evaluate potential hepatic toxicity associated with long-term AS treatment, liver tissues were collected at the end of the 13-week experiment. Histological changes were examined by H&E staining. Additionally, serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured using ELISA kits (ALT, ab234579, Abcam; AST, YS08230B, YaJi Biological), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

ROS level

A portion of fresh kidney tissue was used to obtain a single cell suspension using the RWD single cell suspension preparation instrument, and the cells were incubated with 50 µM DCFH-DA probe (BC005, ELK Biotechnology) for 60 min. The cells were collected after centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min, and the fluorescent signal was detected using flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson). For HBZY-1 cells, 10 µM DCFH-DA probes were incubated with cells for 30 min to assess cellular ROS. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica).

RT-qPCR

RNA was extracted from tissue homogenates using RNAzol® RT RNA Isolation reagent (GeneCopoeia). Reverse transcription was performed with the Evo M-MLV reverse transcription kit (AG11728, Agbio). qPCR was conducted using BlazeTaq™ SYBR Green qPCR Mix (QP032, GeneCopoeia) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, and 60 °C for 30 s. PKC expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH. The primers used for qPCR are detailed in Table 1.

Proteins from tissue homogenates or HBZY-1 cells were quantified using NanoDrop, resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes (M-chemical). Following blocking for 1 h with 5% skim milk, the membranes were soaked in primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The details of the antibodies used are listed in Table 2. The blots were developed using a chemiluminescence kit (NCM Biotech) and quantitatively analyzed using the ImageJ software.

ELISA

TNF-α (CSB-E11987r, Cusabio), IL-1β (PI303, Beyotime), and IL-6 (ER0042, FineTest) levels were measured using ELISA kits. Tissue homogenates were centrifuged at 5000 × g, and HBZY-1 cell lysates were centrifuged at 500 × g, both for 5 min. The supernatant was collected immediately for analysis. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BMG LabTech).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For parametric data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated for the representative pairwise comparisons., Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 27.0. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

AS reduces hyperglycemia in DM rats

Rats in each group were treated as shown in Figure 1A. Body weights and blood glucose levels were monitored for 13 consecutive weeks. In the first four weeks, body weight was similar across all groups. However, in the following nine weeks, rats in the DM group exhibited significantly lower body weights than those in the control (p = 0.0011, 95% CI: −189.4 to −59.66 g, η2 = 0.5696), while those in the PC and AS treatment groups showed higher weights than those in the DM group (PC, p = 0.0002, 95% CI: 51.06 to 127.3 g, η2 = 0.6628; AS-H, p = 0.0004, 95% CI: 39.09 to 104.1 g, η2 = 0.6351) (Figure 1B). Blood glucose levels in the DM group continued to increase over time, in contrast to the lower levels observed in the PC and AS treatment groups; the PC group demonstrated a more pronounced glucose-lowering effect compared with the AS-H group (Figure 1C). During the GTT, blood glucose levels in the DM group were significantly elevated relative to those in the control group (AUC: p < 0.0001, 95% CI: 2272 to 2696, η2 = 0.9855). The PC and AS treatment groups exhibited reduced blood glucose levels compared with the DM group; however, only the PC and AS-H treatment groups showed significantly reduced blood glucose levels (AUC: PC, p < 0.0001, 95% CI: −1652 to −951.4, η2 = 0.8726; AS-H, p = 0.0001, 95% CI: −1137 to −526.3, η2 = 0.7864) (Figure 1D and E). Analysis of the four blood lipid indicators revealed that the TG, CHO, and LDL levels were markedly higher in the DM group than in the control group. These three indicators were significantly lower in the PC and AS treatment groups, and the reduction effect was more obvious with increasing AS concentrations. No significant differences in HDL levels were observed between groups (Figure 1F). These findings indicate that AS-H can reduce aberrant glucose or lipid metabolism in rats with DM.

AS ameliorates renal damage in DM rats

Renal tissue pathology was comprehensively evaluated using Masson’s trichrome, PAS, and H&E staining. In the control group, glomeruli exhibited an intact architecture with no mesangial proliferation. Conversely, the DM group exhibited glomerular hypertrophy, mesangial expansion, glycogen accumulation, and fibrosis. These lesions were alleviated by drug intervention, with the AS-H group exhibiting an improvement comparable to that of the PC group (Figure 2A). Quantitative analysis of the glomerular area (Figure 2B) and mesangial expansion coefficient (Figure 2C) confirmed pathological enlargement and matrix proliferation in DM rats, which were reversed by AS treatment in a dose-dependent manner. Renal function was further assessed by measuring BUN, urine creatinine, and urine protein levels. DM rats exhibited elevated BUN and urine protein levels, along with reduced urine creatinine levels, indicating renal impairment. Treatment with AS ameliorated these changes in a dose-dependent manner, with the AS-H group achieving effects comparable to those of the PC (Figure 2D–F). To evaluate glomerular filtration directly, creatinine clearance (Ccr) was calculated as a surrogate for GFR. DM rats exhibited a significant decline in GFR relative to the controls (p < 0.0001, 95% CI: −3.605 to −2.268 mL/min, η2 = 0.9055), which was significantly improved by AS treatment in a dose-dependent manner (F (1, 20) = 63.84, p < 0.0001, 95% CI: 0.4991 to 0.8519, η2 = 0.9055) (Figure 2G). Furthermore, to evaluate renal injury and fibrosis, we quantified the expression of key markers using qPCR and western blotting. The DM group exhibited significant upregulation of KIM-1, Nephrin, and Fibronectin at the mRNA and protein levels, indicating tubular damage, podocyte dysfunction, and extracellular matrix accumulation, respectively. Treatment with AS markedly attenuated the expression of these markers in a dose-dependent manner, with the AS-H group showing effects comparable to those in the PC (Figure S1A and B). These results provide molecular confirmation of the histological findings and substantiate the protective role of AS against renal structural injury in DN.

To assess the safety of AS, we performed H&E staining of liver sections and measured serum ALT and AST levels. Histological evaluation revealed no signs of hepatocyte injury or structural abnormalities in the AS-treated rats. Additionally, serum ALT and AST levels remained within physiological ranges, showing no significant elevation compared to the control animals (Figure S2). These results suggest that AS does not induce overt hepatic toxicity during the 13-week treatment period.

AS reduces oxidative stress in renal tissue and HBZY-1 cells

Oxidative stress has also been implicated in DN development. ROS content in the renal tissue was quantified by flow cytometry, which revealed that the DM group had significantly higher ROS content than the control group (p < 0.0001, 95% CI: 20.49 to 25.04, η2 = 0.9802). Both PC and AS significantly reduced the ROS content (PC, p < 0.0001, 95% CI: −19.48 to −14.18, η2 = 0.9525; AS-H, p < 0.0001, 95% CI: −17.97 to −13.19, η2 = 0.9547) (Figure 3A). Additionally, the MDA content (Figure 3B), SOD activity (Figure 3C), and GSH-PX activity (Figure 3D) were detected. The DM group showed a marked increase in MDA content and a marked decrease in SOD and GSH-Px activities, indicating that oxidative stress was activated in the renal tissue. The MDA content in the PC and AS treatment groups decreased, accompanied by increased SOD and GSH-PX activities relative to the DM group. This indicates that they inhibit oxidative stress.

In vitro, AS dose-dependently improved cell viability under HG stimulation, with the protective effect plateauing at concentrations above 100 μM (Figure S3). AS significantly attenuated HG-induced increases in ROS (Figure 3E) and MDA (Figure 3F), while restoring SOD (Figure 3G) and GSH-PX (Figure 3H) activity. Notably, cells in the MN group did not exhibit significant changes in oxidative stress markers compared to those in the NG group (Figure 3E–H), suggesting that the observed oxidative stress under high-glucose conditions was not due to osmotic effects.

PKC-β/NOX4 axis regulates oxidative stress

To explore the regulatory mechanism underlying the AS-mediated inhibition of oxidative stress, we first assessed the mRNA expression of PKC isoforms in renal tissues. RT-qPCR showed that PKC-α, PKC-β, and PKC-γ levels were significantly increased in DM rats compared to controls. While losartan suppressed all three isoforms, AS selectively reduced PKC-β expression (Figure 4A), suggesting that PKC-β may play a pivotal role in the renoprotective effects of AS. Then, the content of PKC-β protein and its downstream NOX4 in tissues and cells was evaluated. WB analysis confirmed elevated protein levels of PKC-β and NOX4 in both diabetic kidney tissues and HG-treated HBZY-1 cells. AS treatment significantly reduced their expression, mirroring the effects of the positive controls (Figure 4B and C).

To further validate the role of PKC-β, siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments were conducted. Among the two siRNA sequences tested, siRNA#2 exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency (Figure 5A) and was used in subsequent assays. Under HG conditions, knockdown of PKC-β significantly reduced ROS levels and NOX4 expression, while AS treatment failed to provide additional benefit (Figure 5B and C), indicating that AS acts upstream of NOX4 through PKC-β inhibition. Consistent with this finding, NOX4 silencing abolished the ROS-lowering effect of AS (Figure 5D and E). Furthermore, PKC-β expression was also decreased by AS treatment following NOX4 silencing, confirming a reciprocal regulatory loop between these two proteins (Figure 5F).

AS inhibits the MAPK pathway and inflammation

Increasing evidence supports the regulatory role of PKC in the MAPK pathway in DN. Therefore, in this study, we determined the levels of MAPK pathway-related proteins in renal tissues by WB. Phosphorylation of ERK, p38, and JNK was activated in the DM group. PC significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of these three proteins, whereas AS had the most significant inhibitory effect on ERK phosphorylation, followed by JNK phosphorylation. Only AS-H could inhibit p38 phosphorylation, while AS-L and AS-M could not (Figure 6A). Additionally, NF-kB phosphorylation was activated in the DM group, and PC, AS-M, and AS-H treatments could inhibit NF-kB phosphorylation (Figure 6B). Inflammatory factors in renal tissues, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, were markedly elevated in the DM group compared to the control (TNF-α: p < 0.0001, 95% CI: 422.9 to 518.7 pg/mg, η2 = 0.9796; IL-1β: p < 0.0001, 95% CI: 329.9 to 387.3 pg/mg, η2 = 0.9873; IL-6: p < 0.0001, 95% CI: 38.62 to 48.64 pg/mg, η2 = 0.9741). AS treatment effectively reduced the levels of inflammatory markers in a concentration-dependent manner (TNF-α: p < 0.0001, F (1, 20) = 299.8, 95% CI: −108.7 to −138.5 pg/mg, η2 = 0.9318; IL-1β: p < 0.0001, F (1, 20) = 297.2, 95% CI: −77.28 to −98.56 pg/mg, η2 = 0.9341; IL-6: p < 0.0001, F (1, 20) = 103.8, 95% CI: −8.015 to −12.14 pg/mg, η2 = 0.7975). The inhibitory effect of AS-H on inflammatory factors was comparable to that observed in the PC group (Figure 6C–E).

NOX4 knockdown abolishes the effect of AS on the MAPK pathway and inflammation

To evaluate the involvement of NOX4 in the regulatory function of AS, cells with NOX4 knockdown were used to assess the activated signaling pathways. In HBZY-1 cells, AS-H reduced HG-induced ERK, P38, and JNK phosphorylation, whereas phosphorylation activation disappeared in cells with NOX4 knockdown, even in the presence of AS-H (Figure 7A). NF-kB phosphorylation was also activated by HG treatment, and both AS-H and NOX4 knockdown could inhibit NF-kB phosphorylation (Figure 7B). HG treatment generally increased the levels of inflammatory factors in cells. AS-H treatment was beneficial in reducing inflammatory factors in a HG environment, and NOX4 knockdown assisted in the inhibition of inflammation by AS-H (Figure 7C–E). Taken together, NOX4 knockdown contributes to inhibition of the MAPK pathway and inflammation, and AS may act in DM by downregulating NOX4.

Discussion

DM is characterized by hyperglycemia and is accompanied by various complications, among which DN is one of the most common and serious [1]. Although the STZ dose of 30 mg/kg may appear lower than the typical range (50–65 mg/kg), we employed a combined model involving HFD feeding and low-dose STZ injection, which has been validated in several published studies to induce T2DM features with moderate β-cell destruction and insulin resistance. In this study, the DM rats maintained stable hyperglycemia (>20 mmol/L) throughout the 13-week observation period, supporting the successful establishment of a stable diabetic phenotype. Pathological staining revealed an obvious enlargement of the glomerular volume in the models, accompanied by diffuse proliferation of glomerular mesangial cells, increased extracellular matrix, and glomerular fibrosis. Additionally, BUN and urine protein levels in the rat models were markedly elevated, confirming that the diabetic rats used in this study showed pathological characteristics of early-stage DN. Furthermore, to validate the histological findings, we assessed key molecular markers of renal injury. AS treatment significantly reversed the upregulation of KIM-1 and Fibronectin and restored nephrin expression at both the mRNA and protein levels. These markers reflect tubular injury, fibrosis, and podocyte integrity. Their modulation provides quantitative molecular evidence supporting the protective effects of AS. This helps overcome the potential subjectivity of conventional histological staining and enhances the reliability of our findings.

In addition to abnormal glucose metabolism, diabetic patients often exhibit lipid metabolism disorders [25]. In the initial period of DN, TG increases. During the proteinuria period, LDL, TG, and CHO increase while HDL decreases; these metabolic disorders may become further aggravated [26]. Excessive fat deposition in the kidneys increases the blood pressure of the glomerular capillaries and aggravates glomerular sclerosis. Our findings show that AS ameliorates lipid metabolism disorders in rats. A similar finding was reported in a previous study on atherosclerosis, where AS was found to reduce pathological changes and inflammation in the aorta of rat models [27].

Recently, oxidative stress has been shown to be associated with DN [28]. High glucose levels stimulate the body to produce excessive ROS, which exceed the clearance capacity of the antioxidant system, thereby damaging kidney tissue [29]. In the present study, the model group exhibited increased MDA levels and decreased SOD and GSH-Px activities, indicating that DN induces oxidative stress. Administration of AS reduced MDA content and enhanced SOD and GSH-Px activities, suggesting that AS can inhibit oxidative stress by improving antioxidant capacity, thereby protecting rat renal tissue. It has also been shown to mitigate oxidative stress in other diseases. For example, in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, AS can correct ferroptosis-related lipid peroxidation and is considered a potential treatment for cardiovascular disease [30], including diabetic cardiomyopathy [31]. Additionally, under high glucose stimulation, AS can enhance the antioxidant capacity of pigment epithelial cells and inhibit disease progression in retinopathy [32]. These findings demonstrate that AS is effective against various diabetic complications.

This study also explored the action pathway of AS. AS mainly regulates ROS by inhibiting the PKC-β/NOX4 pathway. A previous study reported that podocyte-specific NOX4 deficiency in diabetic mice reduces albuminuria [33]. Calcium causes oxidative stress in renal tubular epithelial cells through PKC-induced NOX4-triggered ROS, and the MAPK signaling pathway is abnormally activated, inducing BMP2 pathway activation and kidney stones [34]. During the initial period of DN, the secretion of inflammatory markers is dysregulated, with elevated IL-6 and TNF-α levels. Inflammation is a major factor that promotes glomerular cell damage and proteinuria in DN [35]. This study identified increased levels of inflammatory markers in HBZY-1 cells cultured under HG conditions and in a mouse model of DN. Importantly, AS can inhibit the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, thereby mitigating inflammation.

In addition to the PKC/NOX4/MAPK pathway, several other signaling pathways have been implicated in the pathogenesis of diabetic nephropathy. The TGF-β/Smad axis is known to mediate renal fibrosis and mesangial matrix expansion [36], while the PI3K/Akt and AMPK pathways regulate insulin signaling, cellular energy metabolism, and oxidative stress responses [37]. Although AS primarily targets the PKC-β/NOX4 axis in our study, previous reports have shown that AS may also exert regulatory effects on PI3K/Akt and AMPK in other disease contexts, such as cardiac and hepatic injury [38,39].

Furthermore, several natural compounds, including berberine, resveratrol, and curcumin, have demonstrated renoprotective effects in diabetic models, partly by modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling [40–42]. Compared to these agents, AS shows a distinct mechanism by selectively inhibiting PKC-β and its downstream ROS production via NOX4, which we confirmed through genetic silencing. This unique signaling route positions AS as a promising and mechanistically novel candidate for DN intervention. Notably, although AS reduced hyperglycemia in vivo, its renoprotective effects were evident in vitro under stable high-glucose conditions. In these settings, AS mitigated oxidative stress and inflammation, even in the absence of systemic glucose changes. These findings suggest that AS confers direct cytoprotective benefits to renal cells, thereby reinforcing its therapeutic potential beyond glycemic regulation.

Although this study was conducted using preclinical models, the findings provide mechanistic insights into the therapeutic potential of AS for DN. AS attenuates oxidative stress and inflammation without inducing hepatic toxicity, thereby supporting its clinical application. Notably, current first-line therapies for DN, including ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and sodium-glucose transport protein 2 inhibitors, primarily exert protective effects via modulation of the renin-angiotensin system and glucose reabsorption, with additional anti-inflammatory or anti-fibrotic benefits. In contrast, AS acts through a unique pathway by selectively inhibiting PKC-β and its downstream NOX4/MAPK signaling cascade. Unlike conventional agents, AS directly mitigates oxidative stress and inflammation at the cellular level, independent of systemic glucose reduction. A comparison of mechanisms and features among AS and standard therapies is provided in Supplementary Table 1 to better contextualize its translational relevance. Additionally, our 13-week treatment data indicate that AS does not induce hepatic toxicity, suggesting a favorable safety profile. While previous studies have shown that AS can attenuate myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury and improve endothelial function by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation [43,44], these outcomes may be dose- and context-dependent. Given its vasoactive properties, further studies are warranted to evaluate the long-term safety and potential off-target toxicity in other organs under varied dosing regimens, to better support its clinical application.

Given these promising results, further efforts are needed to bridge the gap between experimental findings and clinical translation. Although rodent models and rat-derived mesangial cells have provided valuable mechanistic insights, they do not fully recapitulate the complexity of human DN. To address this, future studies employing human renal cell lines or clinical biopsy samples are essential to validate whether the PKC-β/NOX4/MAPK axis is similarly dysregulated in patients and whether AS exerts comparable effects in human systems. Such investigations are critical for establishing the translational potential of AS in DN therapy.

Overall, AS alleviates DN by inhibiting the PKC/NOX4/MAPK pathway, which is involved in reducing oxidative stress and inflammation. However, one limitation of our study is that we did not assess the involvement of the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway, which is known to contribute to renal fibrosis and mesangial matrix accumulation in DN. Although our findings strongly support the specificity of AS in modulating the PKC-β/NOX4/MAPK axis, further studies are warranted to determine whether AS may exert additional regulatory effects through the TGF-β1/Smad pathway. This pathway provides a new approach for DN prevention and control. AS, an emerging natural product, is a favorable choice for the treatment of DN.
