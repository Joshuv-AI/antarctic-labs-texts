Does the management of anastomotic leakage after rectal cancer resection affect long-term oncological survival? A retrospective propensity score-adjusted cohort study
Authors: Y Ammann, L Marti, R Warschkow, S Bischofberger, C Reißfelder, B Schmied, W Brunner, I Tarantino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe International Study Group of Rectal Cancer (ISREC) provides a classification of anastomotic leakage (AL). This classification categorises the severity of AL according to its clinical management. The aim of this study was to determine whether AL and its management influence survival.MethodsPatients who underwent elective mesorectal excision (PME or TME) for primary rectal cancer stages I–III and who underwent anastomosis were included. A retrospective analysis of clinical data retrieved from a prospectively conducted database was performed. The primary endpoint was cancer-specific survival (CSS). Risk factors were adjusted by propensity score matching (PSM). The secondary endpoints were overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and local recurrence-free rate (RFR).ResultsA total of 942 patients underwent surgery between 1991 and 2020 and were followed for a mean of 71.9 (SD 48.5) months. AL occurred in 141 patients (15.0%). Twenty-three patients had grade A AL (16.3%), 48 patients had grade B AL (34.0%), and 70 patients had grade C AL (49.6%). AL had no significant negative influence on the 5-year propensity score-adjusted survival rate for CSS (no AL 92.2%, AL 87.9%, p = 0.161), but did so on OS (no AL 78.6%, AL 66.9%, p = 0.005), DFS (no AL 72.9%, AL 60.9%, p = 0.011), and RFR (no AL 94.2%, AL 88.5%, p = 0.047). The severity of AL did not have a significant influence on CSS (p = 0.642).ConclusionsAL had a negative influence on OS, DFS, and RFR. Whether aggressive surgical clinical management of AL has any influence on CSS remains unclear.Clinical trial registrationThe study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06059924).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10151-026-03289-6.Keywords: Rectal cancer, Rectal resection, Anastomotic leakage, ISREC classification, Cancer-specific survival, Propensity score

Introduction

Anastomotic leakage (AL) is one of the most concerning complications of rectal cancer resection. It influences postoperative morbidity, postoperative mortality, and functional outcome [1–6]. Known risk factors for AL are low anastomosis, large tumour mass, male sex, smoking status, perioperative bleeding, and preoperative radiochemotherapy [7–9]. Protective ileo- or colostomy formation and transanal tube placement may decrease the risk of AL and reduce the rate of reoperation due to AL [10, 11]. The International Study Group of Rectal Cancer (ISREC) provides a classification of AL according to its clinical management [12], where a grade A AL results in no change in patient management, a grade B AL requires active therapeutic intervention without the necessity for relaparotomy, and a grade C AL requires relaparotomy. This easy and reproducible classification allows good stratification of AL regarding postoperative therapeutical management [13–16]. However, although AL influences other morbidity and mortality, the ISREC classification does not stratify for all other postoperative morbidity, nor for short-term mortality [17, 18]. The association between the ISREC classification of AL and the long-term oncological outcome of rectal cancer treatment is still unclear [19–21]. The aim of this study was to determine whether the AL and its subclasses according to the ISREC classification influence long-term oncological outcomes after rectal cancer resection.

Materials and methods

Patients

Patients who underwent elective total (TME) or partial mesorectal excision (PME) for primary rectal cancer between February 1991 and December 2020 at the Cantonal Hospital of St. Gallen were enrolled in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: a diagnosis other than rectal cancer, underwent discontinuity resection (no anastomosis), underwent emergency operation, showed R1 resection, had incomplete staging, had metastatic disease, died within 30 days postoperatively, declined participation in the retrospective data analysis, experienced secondary malignancy, and under 18 years of age. Diagnoses other than rectal cancer were any kind of benign or malignant lesions in the rectum such as polyps and adenomas, neuroendocrine tumours, gastrointestinal stroma tumours, or metastases.

The patient characteristics included age, sex, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) classification, body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy (chemotherapy, radiotherapy), tumour height from the anal verge (tumour height), year of operation, anastomosis technique (stapler, suture), and stoma formation. The pathological outcome parameters included the pT category, pN category, number of lymph nodes resected, Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) stage, and tumour grade.

AL was defined as a defect of the intestinal wall at the anastomotic site leading to communication between the intra- and extraluminal compartments [12]. AL was diagnosed according to the criteria proposed by the ISREC, these include abscesses close to the anastomosis [12]. The patients were divided into two cohorts: the leak group (with AL) and the no-leak group (no AL). The leak group was further subdivided into three subgroups according to the ISREC classification of AL (grades A, B, and C) [12]. Endoscopic or endoluminal vacuum therapy/sponges, transabdominal drainage, transanal drainage or suturing were classified as ISREC grade B. Relaparoscopy and relaparotomy were classified as ISREC grade C. Furthermore, we wanted to evaluate the impact of a conservative therapy of AL on long-term survival.

All patients underwent regular follow-up according to the guidelines of the Swiss Society of Gastroenterology [22].

Operative procedure and neoadjuvant therapy

Experienced colorectal surgeons performed or instructed all operations. Dissection of the mesorectal plane was performed as described by Heald [23]. For TME, complete dissection was performed down to the pelvic floor [24]. For PME, dissection was performed 5 cm below the tumour for middle (6–12 cm) and high (> 12 cm) tumours, according to the exact location of the tumour [25, 26]. The decision on whether a PME or a TME was performed was made intraoperatively. After complete mobilization of the rectum, the resection margin was determined 5 cm below the inferior border of the tumour using a rigid rectoscope. If parts of the mesorectum remained underneath, it was called a PME, otherwise it was a TME [27]. When intersphincteric resection was necessary, a hand-sewn coloanal anastomosis was performed. All anastomoses were checked for bleeding or AL with a rectoscope. A protective enterostomy was placed through the musculus rectus abdominis at a preoperatively marked position. This procedure was routinely performed for patients with middle and low rectal cancer who underwent surgery with TME.

The first laparoscopic TME procedure was conducted on April 4, 2012, and the first transanal TME procedure was conducted on October 30, 2013, as described in a previous article involving a different patient cohort from our hospital [28].

Neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy were administered as recommended by the interdisciplinary team meeting and included long-course radiochemotherapy, short-course radiotherapy, and/or chemotherapy.

Analysed outcome measures, data collection, and statistical analysis

The primary outcome was cancer-specific survival (CSS; time from surgery to death caused by rectal cancer; deaths due to other causes were censored). The secondary outcomes were overall survival (OS; time from operation to death), disease-free survival (DFS; time from operation to local and/or systemic recurrence or death), and the local recurrence-free rate (RFR; time from operation to radiological and/or pathological evidence of recurrent disease in the small pelvis; patients who died without local recurrence were censored).

Patient data were collected from the electronic medical records and entered prospectively in a scientific, relational database. Missing data were sought after contacting the patients, their general practitioners, and gastroenterologists.

Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (www.r-project.org). A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Proportions were compared by chi-square tests, and continuous variables were compared by t tests and Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Risk factors for the occurrence of AL were assessed by univariate and multivariate logistic regression, and risk factors for subclasses of AL according to ISREC classification were assessed by cumulative link models. Time-to-event data were assessed by Cox regression. The p values for logistic, Cox and cumulative link regression analyses were estimated by likelihood ratio tests, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained by the Wald method [29, 30].

To further address potential bias, a full bipartite matching and weighting propensity score analysis (full matching) was performed using the Matching R package based on the confounding variable set [31–33]. The variables adjusted for were age, sex, BMI, ASA group, tumour height, year of operation, stoma formation, UICC stage, type of mesorectal excision, and neoadjuvant therapy. The impact of AL on OS, CCS, DFS, and RFR was finally assessed via stratified Cox regression analysis by applying the subclasses and weights obtained via propensity score analysis.

Sample size calculation

Before the start of the analysis, a sample size calculation was performed to assess the feasibility of the analysis and to obtain ethical approval. In accordance with the literature, the rate of AL was set at 10% [14]. The clinically reasonable treatment effect was set to a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.30 for unfavourable outcomes. The level of confidence was set at 95%. A total of 1114 patients were needed to reach a statistical power of 0.80 [34]. On the basis of these assumptions, the study was approved by the local ethics committee.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1118 patients underwent elective PME or TME for primary rectal cancer between February 1991 and December 2020. A total of 942 of these patients were enrolled in this study. Seventy-nine patients were excluded because of metastatic cancer, 36 patients were excluded because of discontinuity resection, 10 patients were excluded because of R1 resection, and 51 patients were excluded because of death in the first 30 days after the operation (Fig. 1). Two of these 51 patients died as a result of ALs. The AL grades were A and C. Both patients initially underwent TME with protective ileostomy.

Patient selection

The mean follow-up time was similar between the leak group and the no-leak group (69.9 ± 47.9 months vs. 72.3 ± 48.6 months, p = 0.642).

AL occurred in 141 patients (15.0%). Most of these patients underwent TME (n = 118, 83.7%) with stoma formation (n = 99, 70.2%). According to the ISREC classification [12], 23 patients had grade A AL (16.3%), 48 patients had grade B AL (34.0%), and 70 patients had grade C AL (49.6%).

Patient baseline characteristics and pathological outcome parameters are summarized in Table 1. The leak group had significantly more obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, n = 32 (22.7%) vs. n = 97 (12.1%), p = 0.003) and underweight patients (BMI < 18 kg/m2, n = 4 (2.8%) vs. n = 16 (2.0%), p = 0.003) than the no-leak group. Laparoscopic (n = 49 (34.8%) vs. n = 228 (28.5%%), p = 0.036) and sutured coloanal anastomoses (n = 16 (11.3%) vs. n = 31 (3.9%), p = 0.001) were more common in the leak group than in the no-leak group.

Cancer-specific survival (CSS)

No-leak vs. leak

Univariate analysis revealed similar 5-year CSS rates in the leak group (90.7%; 95% CI 84.9–96.9%) and the no-leak group (92.6%, 95% CI 90.5–94.8%) (HR for leakage = 1.34 (95% CI 0.72–2.50), p = 0.371) (Fig. 2a).

Univariate Kaplan–Meier cancer-specific survival curves of Cox regression of all patients (n = 942, not yet propensity score adjusted) with (HR hazard ratio, CI confidence interval). a No-leak (blue line) and leak (red dashed line). b No-leak (blue line), grade A leak (green dashed line), grade B leak (olive line), and grade C leak (red dashed line)

The risk factors were evenly distributed after propensity score matching in the two groups. A well-matched sample of both groups (leak, n = 141; no-leak, n = 795; not matched and excluded n = 6 (all from the no-leak group); total n = 935) was generated by propensity score matching (leak 0.151 ± 0.054 vs. no-leak 0.151 ± 0.053, p = 0.958). There was no significant difference in the 5-year CSS between the leak group (87.9% [95% CI 80.8–95.7]) and the no-leak group (92.2% [95% CI 90.0–94.6]) after propensity score matching (HR 1.59 [95% CI 0.79–3.20], p = 0.161) (Fig. 3).

Severity of AL according to the ISREC classification

Univariate analysis revealed similar 5-year CSS rate (p = 0.642) for patients with no-leak (92.6 [95% CI 90.5–94.8%]), grade A AL (94.7% [95% CI 85.2–100.0%]), grade B AL (84.4% [95% CI 73.5–97.0%]), or grade C AL (94.2% [95% CI 86.3–100.0%]) (Fig. 2b).

According to the multivariate Cox regression analyses, the severity of AL did not exert a significant effect on CSS (p = 0.854). Older age, lower BMI, ASA classification III/IV, later year of operation (2011–2020), and higher UICC stage had a significant negative impact on CSS (Table 3).

Secondary outcomes

No-leak vs. leak

AL had a significant negative influence on 5-year OS (leak 71.4% (95% CI 63.6–80.1) vs. no-leak 78.5% (95% CI 75.4–81.8); HR 1.53 (95% CI 1.11–2.10), p = 0.013) and DFS (leak 63.2% (95% CI 55.0–72.5) vs. no-leak 72.8% (95% CI 69.5–76.3); HR 1.53 (95% CI 1.13–2.06), p = 0.008) but not on RFR (leak 91.9% (95% CI 86.9–97.2) vs. no-leak 94.3% (95% CI 92.4–96.1); HR 1.54 (95% CI 0.74–3.19), p = 0.266).

After propensity score matching and analysis of survival as described above, AL had a significant negative influence on OS (HR 1.67 (95% CI 1.17–2.39), p = 0.005), DFS (HR 1.57 (95% CI 1.12–2.20), p = 0.011), and RFR (HR 2.03 (95% CI 0.99–4.17), p = 0.047).

Severity of AL according to the ISREC classification

Univariate analysis revealed a significant effect of severity of AL according to the ISREC classification on 5-year OS (overall p = 0.017) and DFS (overall p = 0.014) but not on RFR (overall p = 0.656) (Fig. 4).

Multivariate Cox regression analyses revealed a significant effect on DFS (overall p = 0.039), but no significance for OS (overall p = 0.075) and RFR (overall p = 0.655). Older age, lower BMI, ASA classification III/IV, higher UICC stage, later year of operation (2011–2020), and lower tumour height had significant negative impacts on OS, DFS, and/or RFR (Table SM1, Table SM2 and Table SM3 in supplementary material).

Discussion

AL had a negative influence on long-term OS, DFS, and RFR. The influence of the different AL subclasses according to the ISREC classification on long-term survival after elective mesorectal excision for primary rectal cancer was inconsistent in our results.

The division of AL according to the ISREC classification had a significant influence only on OS and DFS in this study. Comparable data on this topic are rare, and the differences between patient groups, surgical techniques, and survival analyses in publications on this topic are relevant. Allaix et al. [20] described grade B AL as an independent risk factor for poorer OS and DFS in a study with 532 patients with a median follow-up of 80 months. AL occurred in 42 patients (7.9%), 15 with grade B (2.8%) and 27 with grade C (5.2%). The non-occurrence of AL grade A could indicate a bias as asymptomatic patients were not routinely screened.

Takahashi et al. [19] reported significantly worse CSS and DFS for patients with grade C AL than for patients with conservatively treated AL (grades A and B) or no AL. The study included 659 patients with a median follow-up of 65 months. AL occurred in 44 patients (6.7%), seven had grade A (1.1%), 21 had grade B (3.2%), and 16 had grade C (2.4%).

Nachiappan et al. [21] presented a study with 1048 patients and a significantly shorter OS in patients with grade C AL than in patients with conservatively treated AL (grades A and B) or no AL. Patients with grade C AL had a better DFS than patients with conservatively treated AL (grades A and B), but the difference was not significant (p = 0.058). The median follow-up was 40 months. AL occurred in 99 patients (9.4%), 43 (4.1%) were managed conservatively, and 56 (5.3%) were managed operatively. However, the study included all kinds of colonic anastomosis (ileocolic, colocolic, colorectal, colorectal, ileo-pouch-anal, and ileorectal).

AL leads to local inflammation in the pelvis and a systemic inflammatory response. The released proinflammatory mediators support cell proliferation, cell adherence, avoidance of apoptosis, and resistance to drugs and chemotherapy [19, 35, 36]. Allaix et al. reported significantly greater CRP levels as a sign of elevated systemic inflammation in patients with AL grade B than in patients with AL grade C [20, 37]. Early surgical approaches drain inflammatory collections faster and more efficiently than less invasive procedures, such as antibiotic agent use, drainage, or endoscopic vacuum therapy [20, 38]. Prolonged postoperative rehabilitation in patients with non-radical treated AL could delay the start of beneficial adjuvant chemotherapy, which should be applied in the first 8 weeks after the operation [39]. This may increase the growth of residual tumour cells and worsen survival through the progression of local recurrence and metastasis [40–43]. More aggressive management of AL with an early revisional operative strategy and up to Hartmann’s procedure could have a beneficial influence on long-term survival. This could explain the nearly 10% better 5-year CSS rate of grade C AL (94.2%) vs. grade B AL (84.4%) although these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.642). The morbidity of an approach involving laparotomy and Hartmann’s procedure resulting in a potential permanent colostomy and worse quality of life must be considered. Between 2012 and 2020, seven patients required Hartmann’s procedure due to AL. Colostomy reversal was possible in only one of these patients (14.3%).

Cumulative link model ordinal regression identified a higher BMI as a risk factor for AL in this study. The increase in subcutaneous and visceral fat complicates the operation. This results in more laborious procedures, a smaller intraabdominal space, poorer viewing conditions, and more difficult dissection along the embryological plane. These difficulties can result in a higher rate of postoperative surgery-associated complications, especially AL [9]. However, a higher BMI was associated with significantly better CSS, OS, DFS, and RFR. As cancer is a complex systemic and consuming illness, patients with a higher BMI can benefit from their larger energy reserves [44, 45]. This might allow these patients to better cope with the stress of the operation, possible complications, and adjuvant therapy [46].

In the multivariate analysis a worse CSS for patients operated on between 2011 and 2020 was found. Although the exact reason for this result is not clear, there are several possible explanations. The advanced, and more consequently applied neoadjuvant treatment may have resulted in pathologically decreased post-treatment UICC stages in high-risk patients, which suffered from more advanced tumour stages before the application of neoadjuvant treatment. Unfortunately, also a worse surgical quality might have led to this result. Impairment of surgical quality might have resulted from a more difficult operative environment in the small pelvis as a consequence of neoadjuvant therapy [47]. Furthermore, minimally invasive approaches like laparoscopy and transanal endoscopic operations were implemented after 2011 in our hospital. This could have resulted in a possible learning curve for these patients [28, 48]. Other reasons for worse CCS in more recent patients could have been that growing expertise in rectal cancer surgery led to bolder indications for operations in more complex cases. Additionally, the progress in medical treatment makes surgical interventions possible in older patients suffering from severe other illnesses, previously considered inoperable [49].

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its thorough and long follow-up period. The main limitation of this study is its retrospective design. The differences in baseline parameters, resulting from the absence of randomization, were corrected by propensity score matching, although possible unknown parameters might still have influenced the results. The duration of AL and the time to clinical recovery were not addressed in this study. This is a major limitation, as prolonged pelvic sepsis or delays in adjuvant therapy may influence long-term outcomes. The cohort analysed here included a greater proportion of patients with mid and low rectal cancer (91.8%) and, consecutively, a greater proportion of patients with rectal cancer treated with TME (83.3%) compared to the usual distribution of rectal cancer in the population of Switzerland. This results from governmental regulations demanding that such cases be centralized in specialized centres [50]. Another limitation is that some of the data are very old and were recorded more than 30 years ago. It is possible that minor AL went undetected and unreported in the earlier years of the study as a result of a lack of high-resolution radiologic imaging, and the lack of a standardized follow-up care. As consequence of the long recruitment period, major changes of the therapy for rectal cancer were introduced during the study. Therefore, the evolution of minimally invasive approaches (laparoscopy, robotics, transanal resection), technology (endoluminal vacuum therapy, sealing devices, stapler), and the oncologic therapy (neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatment, radiation) as well as intensive care might be possible confounders [51]. The calculated sample size of 1114 patients was not reached in this study (n = 942). The sample size was calculated according to the rate of AL. The subdivision of AL reduces the statistical power to determine whether the severity of AL has an influence on survival. Therefore, the study is slightly underpowered and the statistical power of it must be assumed to be only moderate. It cannot be excluded that the differences, not reaching significance in this analysis, would reach a significant result in a larger cohort.

Conclusion

AL has a negative influence on long-term overall and disease-free survival as well as the local recurrence rate after rectal resection for patients with primary rectal cancer. Further efforts to prevent AL are therefore needed. Whether aggressive surgical management of AL has any influence on long-term oncological survival remains unclear.
