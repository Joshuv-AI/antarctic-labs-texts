Machine learning-based prediction and identification of determinants of teenage pregnancy in ten East African countries
Authors: Nebebe Demis Baykemagn, Ashebir Mamay Gebiru, Mihret Getnet, Berihun Agegn Mengistie, Amare Belete Getahun, Desale Bihonegn Asmamaw, Worku Chekol Tassew, Mikias Mered Tilahun, Yosef Belay Bizuneh, Desalegn Anmut Bitew, Habtu Kifle Negash, Mihret Melese

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbout 21 million teenagers became pregnant annually throughout the globe. Teen pregnancy is a serious issue in Sub-Saharan Africa, with East Africa reporting the highest rates. In the field of public health, machine learning has become an invaluable tool due to its ability to process large, complex datasets and identify trends. This study uses machine learning to predict and identify key determinants of teenage pregnancy in East Africa, utilizing DHS. A supervised machine learning approach, specifically the Random Forest algorithm, was applied to analyze relationships between predictors and teenage pregnancy outcomes. Data preprocessing included handling missing values, feature scaling, and addressing class imbalance using Tomek Links and SMOTE Model performance was evaluated using metrics such as accuracy, confusion matrix, and ROC AUC. The final model was validated on a separate test set to ensure generalizability and predictive accuracy. Random Forest demonstrated superior performance, with an AUC of 94.6, an accuracy of 89.1%, an F1 score of 89%, a recall of 88%, and a precision of 90%. Kenya had the highest rate of teenage pregnancies at 19.1%, with a 95% confidence interval of [18.12%, 20.08%]. Key predictors of teenage pregnancy in East Africa include maternal education, marital status, age at first sexual intercourse, wealth status, place of residence, distance to health facilities, and social media usage. These findings suggest that expanding reproductive health services in rural areas, with strengthened youth-friendly services; promoting education about teenage pregnancy through social media; and integrating reproductive health education into school curricula may decrease teenage pregnancy in East Africa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43004-x.Keywords: Machine learning, Teenage pregnancy, Africa, Digital health, Data scienceSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Background

According to the WHO report in 2024, About 21 million teenagers became pregnant annually throughout the globe1. Twelve million of them lived in middle- and low-income countries2.

Teen pregnancy is a serious issue for reproductive health in Sub-Saharan Africa, with East Africa reporting the highest rates3, health and economic challenge nowadays where limited access to reproductive health services, restricted educational opportunities, and early marriage contribute to the problem; notably, around 20 countries in sub-Saharan Africa account for 82% of adolescent maternal mortality4–8. Also, nearly 16 million teenagers aged 15–19 and two million girls under 15 give birth annually9.

The global prevalence of teenage pregnancy was 14%, with rates of 19.3% in Sub-Saharan Africa10. Among them 95% of occur in low- and middle-income countries, In Sub-Saharan Africa, the rate is 19.3%10. East Africa has the highest rates compared to other regions of the world, at 21.5 to 54.6%11,12 there is also high variation in East Africa. particularly in Ethiopia, Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania, with rates of 16.5%, 25%, 15.3%, 18.8%, and 15.3 respectively, according to the 2016 DHS13. Some African countries, like Uganda, have developed guidelines for preventing and managing teenage pregnancy in schools. However, teenage pregnancy remains a significant issue, particularly in East Africa3, which highlights unresolved challenges across the continent. To address this issue and generate strong evidence for practical, actionable solutions, a representative, machine-based study is essential14.

Teenage pregnancy leads to a range of negative consequences of include poor child development, social stigma, and poverty, dropping out of school, higher maternal mortality, anemia, nutritional deficiencies, pregnancy-induced hypertension, preterm birth, inadequate weight gain, obstructed labor, fistula, and sepsis, and psychosocial stress which are among the most common15–17. According to the WHO, unsafe abortions, which account for approximately 13% of maternal mortality globally, have become prevalent among young girls18.

Evidence indicates that teenage pregnancy is influenced by unprotected sex, low socioeconomic status, inadequate contraceptive awareness, limited access to contraceptives, cultural norms, family dynamics, peer pressure, media influence, low self-esteem, mental health issues, and a history of abuse19–22. Additionally, according to traditional approach factors like rural residency, early marriage, lack of contraceptive use prior to pregnancy, and poor sexual and reproductive health education also contribute to the risk23–26.

Traditional statistical models frequently fail to account for complicated and non-linear relationships between several risk factors when predicting adolescent pregnancy in East Africa.

We employed machine learning (ML) due to its ability to uncover complex, non-linear patterns in large health datasets that traditional statistical models may overlook. Additionally, there is limited use of predictive ML models in understanding risk factors for teenage pregnancy in low-resource settings using nationally representative datasets.

This ML technique was applied to the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) because of their standardization, large sample size, comprehensive health indicators, and structured format.

The aim of this study is to compare the performance of multiple ML models in predicting teenage pregnancy and to identify key predictive features from the DHS data.

Methods and materials

Study setting

The study was conducted across ten East African countries with the latest DHS data, namely Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, Madagascar, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania, and Zambia.

Population

For this study, our study population consisted of teenage girls aged 15–19, with a total of 32,188 participants included in the sample.

Study variables and operational definitions

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was teenage pregnancy. From the age group of 15–49 years, individuals aged 15–19 years were extracted (using v013 = 1, which represents the 15–19 age group). The variable was then dichotomized into two categories: Yes’ for those who had experienced pregnancy (recoded as 1), and ‘No’ for those who had not (recoded as 0)17.

In dependent variable

The independent features were selected based on previous studies27–29, clinical factors, and expert opinions. These included marital status, sexual exposure, maternal educational status, wealth status, knowledge of family planning methods, household size, maternal residence, distance to health facilities, access to social media (including radio, television, and newspapers), and the intention to use family planning.

Regarding the sample size, the data was collected by the Ministry of Health of each East African country. We initially began the process after extracting the collected data and then started preprocessing.

Data preprocess and management

After a detailed review of previously published studies and DHS guidelines, we selected country datasets based on the availability of the outcome variable. We then examined the independent variables to ensure their comparability across surveys. After confirming similarities, we appended the selected country datasets using STATA. Finally, we recoded variables to align with established definitions from the literature and DHS standards.

Data preprocessing is a key challenge in machine learning studies, as it transforms raw data into a clean, usable format. noisy data can disrupt statistical analyses, making effective preprocessing is essential. It helps eliminate duplicate records, handle outliers and missing value, enhance model performance, and ensures that data can be used for policy formulation and decision-making.

For this study, missing data were managed by applying KNN Imputation to continuous variables due to its reduced sensitivity to skewed data compared to mean and median imputation, thereby ensuring statistical consistency and overall dataset integrity. Mode Imputation was applied to categorical features to maintain the original distribution and avoid distortion.

According to previous study evidence, One-Hot Encoding was applied to categorical features such as Residency, where the values 1 and 2 do not represent an ordinal relationship. This transformation converted the feature into binary vectors (1, 0) and (0, 1), preventing the model from interpreting them as ordinal data30.

In order to ensure all features are treated equally and to improve model performance, we employed normalization (Min-Max scaling) code. After imputation, outliers were found using the Interquartile Range (IQR) approach, and IQR-based capping was used to address them.

Also, Feature engineering involves a set of techniques that allow us to generate new features by combining existing ones. In this study, we generated the Social_Media_Access feature by combining data from TV, radio, and newspaper access.

Machine learning study indicates that feature selection is an effective approach for selecting the most significant features variables31. Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was applied to systematically eliminate irrelevant features, reducing dimensionality while preserving the most predictive variables—thus improving model efficiency and generalizability by removing the least important ones based on performance rankings from a tree-based model. It was used to maintain predictive accuracy while reducing model complexity and overfitting.

A systematic study showed that unbalanced data in machine learning models might result in bias against the majority class, increasing false positives (FP) and decreasing true positives (TP)32. SMOTE + Random Under sampling was employed in this investigation to minimize data loss and achieve fair balance. To ensure balance, random under sampling was done on the majority class after SMOTE was used to create synthetic samples for the minority class.

As demonstrated by prior machine learning study, model training is essential for effective machine learning performance, and the effectiveness of this process depends on the quality of the training data and the procedure used33. In this study, we used an 80 − 20 train-test split strategy, with Random Forest, Decision Tree, XGBoost, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Logistic Regression classifiers. See the procedure in Fig. 1.

Model selection

Those five supervised models namely Random Forest, Decision Tree, XGBoost, KNN, and Logistic Regression were selected for this study based on our outcome variable, which is classification; they were also chosen for their high accuracy, robustness to overfitting, ease of interpretation, capacity to handle non-linearity, and effective handling of huge datasets.

Model evaluation

According to evidence, the use of machine learning (ML) models in healthcare data analysis can increase the speed and accuracy of decision-making, and improve treatment planning and patient care34. This is realized when using a validated model. In this study we employed accuracy, precision, recall, f1_score, ROC, and K-fold cross-validation (k = 10), confusion matrix and domain experts’ opinion methods.

Data balancing

To enhance both data quality and class balance, in this study, the combination of Tomek Links with SMOTE. This approach helps reduce noise, enhance decision boundaries, minimize synthetic data, and lessens over fitting. To address class imbalance, Random Over-Sampling combined with Tomek Links is used, as it eliminates only borderline or ambiguous instances instead of randomly removing majority class samples. This approach helps prevent the creation of unrealistic synthetic data, reduces the likelihood of overfitting due to duplicated entries, and maintain discard many potentially relevant features. In this study, we used common supervised machine learning metrics, including, Evaluation Metrics table, evaluation Metrics Graph, confusion matrix, ROC curve, and expert opinion.

Accuracy is defined as the ratio of the number of correct predictions to the total number of predictions. It is the most fundamental metric used to evaluate a model’s performance. In the context of evaluating teenage pregnancy prediction in East Africa, accuracy measures how well the model correctly predicts instances of teenage pregnancy compared to the total predictions.

Random Forest performed best, not only based on the evaluation metrics results but also due to its lower sensitivity to feature scaling, its ability to reduce overfitting by aggregating multiple decision trees, and its capacity to handle complex non-linear data. See in Table 4, and Fig. 4.

Discussion

Machine learning is a novel approach nowadays, especially in the healthcare field, to generate strong evidence for patient management, interventions, and policy formulation to enhance healthcare service quality at all levels. It offers superior predictive capabilities, flexibility, and accuracy in handling complex, real-world data, making it a more powerful choice for predicting teenage pregnancy compared to traditional statistical models. The aim of this study is to compare the performance of multiple ML models in predicting teenage pregnancy and to identify key predictive features from the DHS data.

Among machine learning approaches, supervised ML is suitable for binary outcome variables like teenage pregnancy (yes or no) and can easily identify its predictors on unseen data after model performance measurement. In this study, Random Forest is the best-performing model with an accuracy of 94.6%, supported by earlier studies36–40.

According to this machine learning study, marital status (unmarried), age at first sex, low maternal education, poor household wealth status, living in rural areas, infrequent use of social media, and difficulty accessing health facilities due to distance were identified as predictors associated with an increased risk of teenage pregnancy. However, the use of modern family planning methods was associated with a decreased risk of teenage pregnancy in East Africa.

SHAP analysis from this study revealed that being unmarried increased the risk of teenage pregnancy, this is supported by previous studies in some part of Africa, Rwanda, Zambia, Ethiopia, Western Greece, Uganda, and sub-Saharan Africa29,41–47.

The possible explanation for how being unmarried increases the chances of teenage pregnancy might be a lack of access to contraception, limited knowledge, cultural stigma, financial insecurity, peer pressure, and a lack of awareness about the negative impact of teenage pregnancy on their future life48.

SHAP analysis indicated that an earlier age at first sexual activity increases the risk of teenage pregnancy in the eastern Africa. This is supported by previous studies conducted in Zambia, Cameroon, Ethiopia, and Uganda49–52.

The findings of this machine learning study showed that use modern family planning decreasing risk of teenage pregnancy this supported by studies conducted in Ethiopia, Rwanda, Pakistan, and Cameroon28,53–55.

The SHAP analysis indicated that poor household wealth status increased the risk of teenage pregnancy. This is in line with previous studies conducted in Uganda, Ethiopia, Malawi, and Pakistan27,53,56,57. A possible justification, particularly in rural areas, is the presence of economic vulnerability, early marriage practices, poverty-driven early sexual relationships, limited access to education, and inadequate access to modern family planning services.

This study also indicated that living in rural areas increased the risk of teenage pregnancy in East Africa. This is supported by previous studies conducted in Ethiopia, Uganda, South Africa, and Brazil58–61. The possible justification could be that living in rural areas leads to limited access to education, reproductive health services, cultural norms favoring early marriage, and poverty, which may drive young girls into early relationships62.

This ML study indicated that women who do not use social media have an increased risk of teenage pregnancy, as supported by previous studies conducted in Malawi, South Africa, Uganda53,63–65, . The possible justification is that those who do not use social media may have limited exposure to digital health education, including content related to reproductive health, contraception, and the consequences of early pregnancy, making them more vulnerable to misinformation, early sexual activity, and unprotected sex66.

This study showed that, for women who faced significant problems with distance to health facilities increased the risk of teenage pregnancy. this inline studies conducted in Ethiopia, Bhopal, and Rwanda47,55,67. The possible justification could be that those women can easily access reproductive health services, including modern family planning, and reduce travel costs to reach health facilities.

This evidence also showed that a low educational level among women increased the risk of teenage pregnancy, which is supported by previous studies conducted in Pakistan, Malaysia, Colombia, and Ethiopian53,68–70.

Conclusions

Random Forest demonstrated superior performance, with an AUC of 94.6, an accuracy of 89.1%, an F1 score of 89%, a recall of 88%, and a precision of 90%. Kenya had the highest rate of teenage pregnancies at 19.1%, with a 95% confidence interval of [18.12%, 20.08%].

According to this machine learning study, marital status (unmarried), age at first sex, low maternal education, poor household wealth status, living in rural areas, infrequent use of social media, and difficulty accessing health facilities due to distance were identified as predictors associated with an increased risk of teenage pregnancy. However, the use of modern family planning methods was associated with a decreased risk of teenage pregnancy in East Africa.

These findings suggest that expanding reproductive health services in rural areas, with strengthened youth-friendly services; promoting education about teenage pregnancy through social media; and integrating reproductive health education into school curricula may decrease teenage pregnancy in East Africa.

Strengths and limitations of this study

The use of DHS data provides a large, representative sample from East Africa, ensuring robust and generalizable results. Applying supervised machine learning, particularly the Random Forest algorithm, helps identify complex, non-linear relationships between predictors and teenage pregnancy, improving prediction accuracy. However, the cross-sectional nature of the DHS data limits causal conclusions, and the study’s scope is limited by the exclusion of factors like cultural beliefs and peer pressure. The study is focused at the regional level of East Africa; therefore, we could not conduct individual country-level analyses. Moreover, machine learning models do not establish causality.

Policy implication of the study

The research results may promote the optimal distribution of resources, providing that outreach, education, and health initiatives target the most vulnerable populations. For instance, governments might give priority to policies that encourage girls’ education, prevent early marriage, and increase access to reproductive health services in remote area.

Additionally, the design, implementation, and monitoring of adolescent health initiatives can be improved as well as real-time decision-making through the integration of machine learning techniques into national health information systems. For studies that serve as a standard.
