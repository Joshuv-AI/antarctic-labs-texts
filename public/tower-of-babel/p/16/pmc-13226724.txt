Sintilimab plus concurrent chemoradiotherapy for treatment of locally advanced small cell lung cancer (SINCE-01): a phase II clinical trial
Authors: Qian Zhao, Yintao Li, Hongjiao Zhang, Dan Han, Yanni Kan, Hongfu Sun, Chengxin Liu, Linlin Wang, Xiao Han, Ying Hua, Xinran Wang, Chengrui Fu, Zhongtang Wang, Xuexiang Du, Jiye Liu, Baosheng Li, Wei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients diagnosed with limited-stage small cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC) face dismal long-term outcomes, as relapse occurs frequently even after standard concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Integrating immune checkpoint blockade into chemoradiotherapy regimens may enhance sustained disease control, yet prospective evidence for this strategy in LS-SCLC remains sparse. In this single-arm phase II study, we investigated sintilimab in combination with concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) among LS-SCLC patients who had favorable functional status. Twenty-two histologically verified LS-SCLC patients were treated with four 3-weekly cycles of chemotherapy alongside sintilimab, with concurrent thoracic radiation (45 Gy delivered in 30 fractions). The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS). After a median follow-up of 44.7 months, the median PFS reached 29.6 months, with 12- and 24-month PFS rates of 72.7% and 54.5%, respectively. Median overall survival (OS) stood at 40.6 months, and the corresponding 12- and 24-month OS rates were 95.5% and 72.7%, respectively. An objective response rate (ORR) of 95.5% was observed. Most grade 3–4 adverse events were hematologic in nature, particularly neutropenia and thrombocytopenia. Three patients developed pneumonitis, one of whom had grade 2 or higher. No deaths attributable to treatment occurred. Elevated human leukocyte antigen class I (HLA-I+) tumor cell expression correlated with longer PFS, suggesting potential value as a predictive biomarker. These findings suggest that concurrent administration of sintilimab with CCRT demonstrates encouraging antitumor efficacy and a manageable safety profile in LS-SCLC. (Trial registration number: ChiCTR2100043184).

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), a neuroendocrine malignancy of high histologic grade, represents roughly 13% of all newly diagnosed lung cancers and is defined by rapid cellular proliferation, early metastatic spread, and marked initial chemosensitivity.1,2 Limited-stage SCLC (LS-SCLC) is identified in approximately one-third of patients at diagnosis; for these individuals, concurrent administration of platinum-etoposide chemotherapy with thoracic irradiation has remained the therapeutic backbone for more than thirty years.3–6 Pooled analyzes and randomized comparisons have consistently shown that initiating thoracic irradiation early and concurrently with chemotherapy yields superior locoregional disease control and longer survival relative to sequential delivery.3–5,7 Despite these benefits, lasting remissions are uncommon: most patients initially respond but subsequently develop recurrent disease, with distant failure being the prevailing pattern.5,7 In landmark phase III studies, specifically CONVERT and CALGB 30610, median progression-free survival (PFS) ranged from 14 to 16 months, and 2-year OS did not exceed 60%, even with optimized concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) protocols.8–12 Failure-pattern analyzes from these pivotal investigations revealed that distant recurrence was the principal cause of treatment breakdown, underscoring the limited gains obtainable from escalating locoregional therapy alone and the critical demand for stronger systemic agents. Collectively, these data confirm that LS-SCLC behaves as a disseminated disease at the time of presentation and that meaningful outcome improvement hinges on eradication of occult distant deposits. Notwithstanding substantial efforts to intensify chemotherapy dosing, modify radiation fractionation, and escalate thoracic doses, no clinically significant advance has materialized in LS-SCLC over several decades.13–15 New treatment approaches capable of targeting both locoregional and distant disease compartments without incurring excessive toxicity are therefore imperative.

The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has significantly transformed cancer treatment paradigms. By modulating key co-inhibitory signaling axes, including cytotoxic T lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)—these agents have produced lasting responses in numerous tumor types.16–18 Within the SCLC landscape, the pivotal IMpower133 and CASPIAN phase III studies showed that adding anti-PD-L1 therapy to frontline platinum–etoposide in the extensive-stage setting resulted in statistically meaningful gains in both PFS and overall survival (OS) over chemotherapy alone.17,18 These results overturned the prevailing assumption that SCLC is inherently resistant to immunomodulation, indicating that immune-based approaches can translate into clinically relevant improvements. From a biological standpoint, SCLC harbors one of the highest tumor mutational burdens among solid cancers, coupled with pervasive genomic instability, characteristics that may enlarge the repertoire of tumor-associated neoantigens and thereby create a substrate for checkpoint inhibitor efficacy, even in the context of generally sparse baseline immune infiltration. Concurrently, thoracic radiation has gained recognition for its immunomodulatory properties, including enhanced release of tumor-associated antigens, upregulation of major histocompatibility complex expression on malignant cells, and recruitment of cytotoxic effector cells into the irradiated field.19,20 Beyond local effects, radiation-triggered abscopal phenomena may potentiate systemic immune responses against lesions outside the treatment field, strengthening the rationale for combining radiotherapy with immune checkpoint blockade. This interplay between radiation-mediated immune activation and ICI-driven T-cell reinvigoration has spurred considerable interest in embedding checkpoint inhibitors within curative chemoradiotherapy regimens for LS-SCLC. Early-phase studies exploring pembrolizumab or durvalumab delivered alongside CCRT have reported favorable survival signals with tolerable toxicity, and a recently reported phase III randomized study demonstrated an OS advantage with consolidation immunotherapy following CCRT completion.21–23

Despite these encouraging signals, prospective data on concurrent ICI-CCRT integration in LS-SCLC remain scarce, and the ideal timing, sequence, and mode of immunotherapy incorporation relative to cytotoxic treatment and radiation have not been established.22,23 A key unresolved area is whether delivering immunotherapy concurrently with chemoradiotherapy, rather than reserving it for the consolidation phase, can initiate immune priming earlier and produce more durable tumor suppression. Furthermore, treatment responses to immunotherapy-containing regimens are heterogeneous; a substantial proportion of patients progress early despite intensive multimodality therapy. Conventional biomarkers, notably PD-L1 expression, have demonstrated inconsistent predictive performance in SCLC,18,21 emphasizing the need to identify alternative markers of treatment benefit. Importantly, SCLC is often marked by impaired antigen presentation machinery, particularly diminished or absent expression of human leukocyte antigen class I (HLA-I) molecules, which can facilitate immune escape.24 When HLA-I expression is insufficient, tumor cells fail to present antigenic peptides to cytotoxic T lymphocytes effectively, constituting a central mechanism of immune evasion. Data from diverse solid tumor types indicate that preserved HLA-I expression is essential for robust anti-tumor T-cell activity and may modulate responsiveness to checkpoint blockade.25,26 Beyond simply cataloguing immune cell abundance, the spatial organization of immune and tumor cells within the microenvironment may carry distinctive prognostic and predictive significance, as intercellular proximity and neighborhood architecture often provide richer biological insight than aggregate cell counts. Such spatially resolved immune neighborhoods could govern treatment sensitivity or resistance; however, spatial profiling in LS-SCLC remains exceedingly rare, especially in the setting of combined immunochemoradiotherapy.

We initiated the SINCE-01 phase II trial to evaluate the efficacy and tolerability of concurrent PD-1 inhibition with sintilimab alongside chemoradiotherapy in LS-SCLC. A feature of this study is the simultaneous integration of PD-1 blockade with curative-intent chemoradiotherapy. Alongside the clinical investigation, we conducted in-depth translational analyzes employing high-dimensional, spatially resolved immune profiling to delineate tumor immune crosstalk that extends beyond conventional single-marker assessments. Our findings reveal, for the first time, that spatially organized niches of HLA-I–positive tumor cells correlate with lasting clinical benefit, identifying antigen presentation capacity as a key determinant of therapeutic response.

Patient characteristics

Between December 2020 and April 2021, 25 patients were evaluated for eligibility (Fig. 1a). Three were ineligible: one had stage IV disease, one had a performance status of 3, and one declined radiation therapy. The remaining 22 patients were enrolled and commenced thoracic radiotherapy. Concurrent immunochemotherapy was delayed in three patients because of neutropenia. Twenty patients transitioned to maintenance immunotherapy; however, two discontinued early—one due to patient refusal and one due to pneumonia. All 22 enrolled patients were included in the analyzes for efficacy and safety. Baseline demographics and disease characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The median age was 63.5 years (range: 43.0–71.0). Patient characteristics

Every patient completed all four planned chemotherapy cycles; 19 received at least one induction immunochemotherapy cycle. No patient required interruption of CCRT due to treatment-related toxicity. The median gross tumor volume (GTV) and planning target volume (PTV) were 36.4 cc and 179.6 cc, respectively. The mean total lung V5 was 35%, the mean ipsilateral V20 was 26%, the mean cardiac dose was 8.32 Gy, and the mean lung dose was 13.26 Gy. Prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI) was administered to 17 patients (77.3%). The mean number of maintenance immunotherapy cycles was 10 (range: 0–28).

Efficacy results

As of the data cutoff of October 28, 2024, the median follow-up duration was 44.7 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 42.9–46.4). Among all 22 patients, the objective response rate (ORR) was 95.5% (95% CI, 77.2%–99.9%), comprising five complete responses and 16 partial responses (PR). One patient had stable disease, yielding a disease control rate of 100% (95% CI, 84.6%–100%) (Fig. 2a). Safety results

All 22 patients were assessable for safety. Each patient (100%) experienced at least one treatment-related adverse event (TRAE) of any grade (Table 3). Grade 3 or 4 TRAEs were observed in seven patients (31.8%), primarily leukopenia (31.8%) and thrombocytopenia (9.1%). Febrile neutropenia was not reported in any case. Three patients required dose reductions or treatment delays owing to grade 4 hematologic toxicity. No TRAE resulted in death. One patient died of a cerebrovascular infarction 2 years after thoracic radiotherapy initiation; this event was judged unrelated to study treatment. Spatial microenvironment of LS-SCLC patients

Patients were stratified into two prognostic subgroups: a short PFS (SPFS) group, defined by disease progression within 24 months, and a long PFS (LPFS) group, defined by absence of progression for at least 36 months. Each subgroup comprised 10 patients; the SPFS group had a median PFS of 11.0 months (range: 5–20) and the LPFS group a median PFS of 42.6 months (range: 39.5–45). Archived formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens obtained before treatment initiation from SINCE-01 trial participants were used for translational analyzes. IMC combines laser ablation with time-of-flight mass spectrometry to detect metal-conjugated antibodies, enabling quantification of protein expression at subcellular resolution. We employed this technology to characterize the TME architecture and key tumor cell phenotypes in detail. A panel of 38 antibodies was designed to identify epithelial, endothelial, stromal, and diverse immune cell populations (Fig. 3b). Clone-specific antibody information is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Unsupervised clustering classified a total of 126,290 cells into major lineages, including tumor cells, endothelial cells, myofibroblasts, and immune cells (Fig. 3c). After tumor cells, fibroblasts constituted the most abundant population, while immune cells represented a comparatively minor fraction (Fig. 3c).

Pan-cytokeratin (Pan-CK)+ tumor cells, CD31+ endothelial cells, and podoplanin (PDPN)+ endothelial cells were readily identifiable in the IMC images and showed spatial distributions concordant with matched hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections (Fig. 3d). Among the immune populations detected were B cells (CD20+), T cells (CD3+), macrophages (CD68+), and tissue-resident macrophages (CD163+CD68+), all of which were reliably resolved by IMC (Fig. 3d). In LPFS patients, immune cells including T cells (CD3+), B cells (CD20+), and macrophages (CD68+) localized predominantly within tumor cell aggregates, whereas in SPFS patients they were concentrated along the tumor periphery. Comparable distribution patterns were noted across additional patients.

Specific profiling of immunocytes

Following connectivity-aware segmentation, marker expression for each cell was quantified and organized into an expression matrix. A correlation heatmap of all staining markers is displayed in Fig. 3e. To dissect the immune landscape underlying different PFS outcomes, we performed focused re-clustering of CD45+ immune cells using unsupervised dimensionality reduction. Sixteen immune-restricted markers guided this analysis. Eight distinct immune cell subsets emerged; each manually annotated according to defining marker positivity (Fig. 3f). Subset identities were confirmed by overlaying marker expression onto a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization.

The proportions of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and neutrophils relative to total cell counts did not differ significantly between the SPFS and LPFS groups (Supplementary Fig. 3a). CD14+/CD16+ intermediate monocytes were marginally more prevalent among total immune cells in the SPFS group (Supplementary Fig. 3a), hinting at an expanded functionally undefined immune compartment in patients with shorter PFS, though this difference did not reach statistical significance. Neutrophils were the most abundant immune subset, followed by M1 macrophages, helper T cells, cytotoxic T cells, and intermediate monocytes. M1 macrophages and neutrophils tended to predominate in the LPFS group, while helper T cells were more prevalent in the SPFS group; however, none of these differences achieved statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Specific profiling of substantial cells

Unsupervised dimensionality reduction of CD45− cells identified seven substantial cell subsets (Fig. 4a, b). Tumor cells were further subdivided into three phenotypic categories: Ki67+ tumor cells, HLA-I+ tumor cells, and HLA-I–Ki67– tumor cells. The HLA-I+ tumor cell population was significantly larger in the LPFS group than in the SPFS group (Fig. 4c). Cellular neighborhood (CN) within the HLA-I cells

The cellular neighborhood (CN) functional unit was defined as a single HLA-I+ tumor cell together with its 20 spatially nearest neighbor cells. Each CN was annotated according to its predominant cellular cluster (Supplementary Fig. 4b), revealing five distinct CN categories (Fig. 4d). CN1—comprising clusters of HLA-I+ tumor cells juxtaposed with cytotoxic T (Tc) cells and M1 macrophages—was found almost exclusively among LPFS group (Fig. 4d, and Supplementary Fig. 4c). Its virtual absence in the SPFS group precluded formal statistical comparison (Fig. 4e). Conversely, CN5—consisting of HLA-I– and Ki-67– tumor cells enveloped by fibroblasts and neutrophils—were relatively more abundant in the SPFS group (Fig. 4e). The CN-coded topological map closely recapitulated the corresponding IMC image, as shown in Fig. 4f.

Cell interactions at spatial and molecular levels

To evaluate how intercellular interactions relate to survival, we conducted a spatial interaction analysis. Contacts between HLA-I+ tumor cells and both M1 macrophages and intermediate monocytes were significantly more prevalent in the LPFS group than in the SPFS group (Fig. 5a). HLA-I+ tumor cells were also significantly more numerous in the LPFS group, and intermediate monocytes, M1 macrophages, and M2 macrophages formed denser clusters in the vicinity of these HLA-I+ tumor cells (Fig. 5b). By contrast, the SPFS group exhibited fewer HLA-I+ tumor cells and reduced macrophage/monocyte interactions, with spatial avoidance patterns that likely contributed to diminished immunotherapy responsiveness (Fig. 5b). For independent validation, we assembled a separate cohort of 10 SCLC patients who had also received sintilimab and chemoradiotherapy. Pre-treatment biopsy specimens were stained with multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) targeting CD14, CD68, CD163, HLA-I, and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to assess HLA-I expression across patients with different PFS durations. Patients with prolonged PFS exhibited enrichment of HLA-I+ tumor cells accompanied by abundant peritumoral M1 macrophage and intermediate monocyte clusters compared with those who had SPFS (<12 months; Fig. 5c), and this spatial distribution pattern was independently confirmed.

Patch of immune cells detection

We next examined local accumulations of immune cells by comparing the cellular composition within immune patches (Supplementary Fig. 4d). In pre-treatment specimens, the LPFS group demonstrated significantly higher M1 macrophage densities within immune patches relative to the SPFS group (Fig. 5d). Outside the patch boundaries, no significant differences in immune cell proportions were detected. Additionally, surface expression of β2-microglobulin (β2m) on Tc cells was significantly elevated in the LPFS group (Fig. 5e). Because β2m is an indispensable structural component of the HLA-I complex, this observation aligns with our cellular neighborhood findings. These data suggest that the association of HLA-I+ tumor cells with Tc cells through β2m within spatially organized immune patches may constitute a functional unit that bolsters antitumor immunity and supports sustained PFS.

Association between HLA-I and PD-L1 expression

PD-L1 and PD-1 expression levels did not differ between the SPFS and LPFS groups in IMC analyzes (Fig. 5f). However, when PD-L1 expression was specifically assessed on HLA-I+ tumor cells, markedly higher levels were found in the LPFS group (Fig. 5g). Neighborhood analysis further revealed that cells in spatial proximity to HLA-I+ tumor cells exhibited elevated PD-L1 expression (Fig. 5h). To corroborate the HLA-I–PD-L1 association, immunohistochemistry was performed on an independent set of 15 SCLC patients treated with chemoimmunotherapy (concurrent or sequential radiotherapy). This analysis confirmed a statistically significant positive correlation between HLA-I and PD-L1 expression (p = 0.0049, HR = 0.6845, Fig. 5i).

We then investigated whether forced HLA-I expression modulates tumor cell apoptosis when co-cultured with immune effectors (Fig. 6a). Flow cytometric assessment of early apoptosis showed that HLA-I–overexpressing (OE) cells had significantly heightened sensitivity to both peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and anti-PD-1 treatment, as evidenced by substantially increased overall apoptosis compared with negative control (NC) cells (Fig. 6g). Quantitative data are presented in Fig. 6h, i. In the absence of PBMCs, apoptosis rates were comparable between NC and OE groups. In the presence of PBMCs, however, the early apoptosis fraction was significantly higher in OE cells, an effect observed in both the PBMC-only and PBMC plus anti-PD-1 conditions (p < 0.05). Together with the spatial IMC data, these results indicate that augmented HLA-I expression promotes tumor cell apoptosis and amplifies PBMC-mediated killing.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that the addition of sintilimab to CCRT is both feasible and clinically active in LS-SCLC patients with preserved functional status. A median PFS of 29.6 months represents a substantial advance relative to historical CCRT survival benchmarks.22,23 When placed in the context of standard CCRT for LS-SCLC, the landmark CONVERT and CALGB 30610 (Alliance)/ RTOG 0538 studies yielded median OS values between 25 and 30 months, with 2-year OS rates in the range of 50–56%.8–12 These results represent the performance ceiling of conventional cytotoxic therapy combined with early thoracic irradiation. Although the single-arm nature of our study precludes a formal head-to-head comparison, the outcomes observed in our cohort with a median PFS of 29.6 months and a 2-year OS of 72.7% appear to surpass these historical benchmarks by a margin of potential clinical significance, suggesting that concurrent immunotherapy delivery alongside chemoradiotherapy may overcome the longstanding efficacy plateau in LS-SCLC.

Prospective experience with ICI–CCRT combinations in LS-SCLC has thus far been limited,22,23 and our data extend these observations to a previously underrepresented East Asian population. Prior studies established that adding pembrolizumab or durvalumab to CCRT is a viable strategy in LS-SCLC,22,23 producing 2-year OS rates of 65.8% and 67.8%, which exceeded the 56% and 58% observed in the CONVERT and CALGB 30610 control arms.8,9 Those trials documented median PFS values of 19.7 and 14.4 months, respectively, both numerically superior to the 15.4- and 14.2-month figures from CONVERT and CALGB 30610. In our study, median PFS and the 2-year OS rate reached 29.6 months and 72.7%, respectively, a numerically noteworthy improvement. Cross-trial comparisons warrant caution given differences in baseline characteristics (early-stage [I–II] disease: 9.1% vs. 12.5%), follow-up duration (46.4 vs. 60.2 months), and sample size (22 vs. 264); nonetheless, the 2-year OS in our cohort (72.7%; 95% CI, 56.3–93.9) was broadly aligned with the ADRIATIC trial result (68%; 95% CI, 61.9–73.3).16 Taken as a whole, these findings lend support to the hypothesis that concurrent immunotherapy and CCRT deliver meaningful clinical benefit in LS-SCLC through synergistic mechanisms of immune activation and radiation-mediated tumor elimination.

To the best of our knowledge, the SINCE-01 phase II trial has produced the longest median PFS yet reported for any ICI–CCRT combination in LS-SCLC.8,9,22,23,27 At the last follow-up, 10 patients remained free of disease progression. We attribute this prolonged PFS to several factors. First, every patient who achieved PFS of 30 months or longer had received at least one cycle of induction immunochemotherapy before radiotherapy initiation. This induction phase yielded meaningful tumor volume reduction: the mean maximum tumor diameter was 3.85 cm among non-progressors versus 3.18 cm in those who progressed, and mean GTV was markedly smaller in the non-progression subgroup (22.1 cc vs. 53.9 cc). Second, concurrent delivery of ICIs with radiotherapy likely augmented immune activation while mitigating early lymph node injury. Third, our study employed residual field irradiation (RFI), targeting only post-chemotherapy residual tumor and pathologically confirmed nodal disease prior to thoracic radiotherapy. Published data suggest that limiting the radiation target volume and omitting elective nodal coverage reduces normal-tissue injury and may improve OS in LS-SCLC,28–31 and this approach may partly account for the favorable PFS observed. Available evidence indicates that RFI does not adversely affect failure patterns or survival in LS-SCLC.28–31 In the current immunotherapy era, RFI combined with selective lymph node treatment may therefore offer a promising approach that maintains therapeutic efficacy while lowering the risk of radiation pneumonitis through reduced exposure of uninvolved lung parenchyma.

The safety profile in this study was broadly consistent with prior reports,20,22,23 particularly regarding anemia, nausea, vomiting, and fatigue. Of note, the rate of grade 3–4 neutropenia was lower than previously published figures.20,22,23 Although neutropenia was the most frequently observed adverse event, no patient developed febrile neutropenia. Hypothyroidism was the most common immune-related adverse event, but did not necessitate treatment interruption or cessation in any case. No grade 5 toxicities occurred. The overall incidence of pneumonitis was in line with historical CCRT data,8,22,23,27 and no case of grade 3 or higher pneumonitis was documented. The rate of severe pneumonitis was consistent with published safety profiles for concurrent ICI–CCRT in thoracic cancers and appeared numerically lower than rates reported with sequential ICI administration after completion of radiotherapy.32–34 In the durvalumab–CCRT study, for instance, severe pneumonitis (grade 3–5) affected only 1 of 62 patients (1.6%).35 Likewise, all-grade pneumonitis rates were comparable between the CCRT-only and immunotherapy-added cohorts in PACIFIC-II36 and KEYNOTE-799.37 A further noteworthy observation is the sustained disease control achieved in a subset of patients, reflected by a plateau in the tail of the PFS curve. As of October 2024, 10 patients remained progression-free. A stark separation in outcomes was evident between the LPFS group, with a mean PFS of 42.6 months (range: 39.5–45), and the SPFS group, whose mean PFS was only 11.0 months (range: 5–20). The pronounced benefit experienced by the LPFS group with sintilimab–CCRT underscores the value of investigating the biological mechanisms underlying this divergence.

Among the translational findings, a particularly notable result was the statistically significant association between baseline HLA-I expression—measured via IMC—and extended PFS and OS in LS-SCLC patients receiving ICI-based therapy. Surface HLA-I molecules on tumor cells constitute the principal apparatus for displaying tumor-derived peptides to cytotoxic T lymphocytes, initiating adaptive immune responses,24–26 and PD-L1 levels were elevated in the HLA-I+ subset, consistent with an immunologically active microenvironment. Spatial analyzes further revealed that patients with prolonged PFS harbored distinct aggregates of HLA-I+ tumor cells flanked by M1-polarized macrophages and cytotoxic T (Tc/CD8+) cells—a pattern indicative of an immunologically activated baseline microenvironment poised for effective surveillance and therapeutic engagement. In contrast, patients with abbreviated PFS exhibited reduced HLA-I expression and fragmented M1–Tc peritumoral co-localization, suggesting immune escape via deficient antigen presentation and baseline checkpoint resistance. Collectively, these data indicate that both the abundance of baseline HLA-I+ tumor cells and their spatial proximity to macrophage populations correlate positively with treatment outcomes and may function as predictive biomarkers of PFS in LS-SCLC. While published data on HLA-I immunostaining in non-SCLC are limited,38,39 elevated HLA-I expression has been associated with improved ICI outcomes in non-SCLC; ours is the first study to demonstrate an analogous relationship in LS-SCLC, underscoring the central importance of HLA-I–mediated antigen presentation in shaping ICI responsiveness.

Several limitations of this work merit consideration. The modest sample size and absence of a randomized control arm constrain the generalizability of our conclusions, and independent validation in larger, randomized studies is indispensable. Additional mechanistic investigations are also warranted to elucidate how HLA-I–dependent antigen presentation and its spatial coordination with myeloid cell populations underpin sustained disease control. In conclusion, the SINCE-01 phase II trial demonstrates that concurrent delivery of sintilimab with chemoradiotherapy achieves prolonged PFS and a manageable safety profile in LS-SCLC. These results provide a rationale for designing adequately powered, randomized investigations to validate this multimodality treatment strategy.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This prospective, single-arm, open-label phase II investigation was conducted at Shandong Cancer Hospital, China (registration: ChiCTR2100043184). The study protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice standards. Ethics approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital (Approval No. SDTHEC2020-177-02), and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. Patients were eligible if they had histologically or cytologically confirmed LS-SCLC staged per the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) 8th edition system, a projected life expectancy of more than 3 months, and an ECOG performance status of 0 or 1. At least one measurable lesion as defined by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 and adequate pulmonary reserve (forced expiratory volume in one second [FEV1] > 1 L) were also required (Fig. 1b). Only treatment-naïve patients with no prior systemic anticancer therapy for lung malignancies were enrolled. Key exclusion criteria comprised concurrent participation in another interventional trial, a histologic diagnosis of combined SCLC/non-SCLC, and any previous exposure to agents directed against PD-1, PD-L1, PD-L2, CTLA-4, OX-40, CD137, or other co-inhibitory or co-stimulatory checkpoint receptors.

Interventions

Treatment consisted of four 21-day cycles of combined immunochemotherapy. Each cycle comprised sintilimab (200 mg by intravenous infusion on day 1), etoposide (80–100 mg/m² on days 1–3), and carboplatin (area under the curve 5 mg·min/mL on day 1). Thoracic radiotherapy was initiated no later than the start of the second chemotherapy cycle. Dose adjustments were dictated by hematological status: chemotherapy was withheld for clinically meaningful cytopenias and restarted at 75% or 50% of the initial dose following count recovery. Permanent treatment discontinuation was mandated if toxicity persisted for more than 6 weeks after the most recent cycle. Upon CCRT completion, sintilimab was maintained on a 3-weekly schedule until radiographic progression per RECIST 1.1, unacceptable toxicity, completion of a 24-month course, or physician decision, whichever came first.

Thoracic radiation was delivered as 45 Gy in 30 fractions (1.5 Gy per fraction, five fractions weekly). Organ-at-risk dose constraints included: mean lung dose ≤20 Gy, lung V20 < 35%, lung V5 < 50%, heart V50 < 25%, and mean esophageal dose ≤34 Gy. RFI encompassed the post-chemotherapy residual tumor and pathologically confirmed lymph nodes, delivered using intensity-modulated radiation therapy. Weekly cone-beam computed tomography was performed to monitor anatomical changes. Target volumes were delineated on computed tomography (CT) simulation: the GTV comprised all macroscopically visible tumor together with lymph nodes measuring ≥1.0 cm in short axis or demonstrating positron emission tomography (PET) avidity. Primary tumors were outlined using lung window settings; mediastinal structures were contoured on soft-tissue windows. In cases of atelectasis, co-registered PET/CT data-informed GTV delineation. PTV was generated by adding 5–10 mm margins to account for respiratory motion and setup uncertainty.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoint was PFS, defined as the interval from treatment initiation to the earliest documented radiologic disease progression or death from any cause. Secondary endpoints included OS (time from treatment start to death or last follow-up contact), ORR, and safety. Response evaluation followed RECIST 1.1. Baseline staging comprised thoracic and abdominal computed tomography, bone scintigraphy, and brain imaging (magnetic resonance imaging or PET/CT). Radiological assessments were repeated every 9 weeks (±7 days) until RECIST 1.1–defined progression.

Patients who discontinued study treatment in the absence of documented radiologic progression continued protocol-specified imaging until initiation of alternative anticancer therapy, confirmed disease progression, withdrawal of consent, or death. Adverse events were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) version 5.0.

Immunohistochemical staining

PD-L1 status was determined using the 22C3 pharmDx immunohistochemistry assay (Agilent Technologies) on the Dako Autostainer Link 48 platform. The tumor proportion score was calculated as the percentage of PD-L1–positive tumor cells among all viable tumor cells.

For HLA-I staining, tissue sections were heated at 63 °C for 60 min, followed by deparaffinization and stepwise rehydration through graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer (pH 9.0) using the PT Link system (Dako). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary anti-HLA-I antibody (1:20,000 in Dako diluent). Signal detection employed the EnVision FLEX+ system (Dako); 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen was developed on the Autostainer Link 48. Counterstaining with Mayer’s hematoxylin preceded dehydration and coverslipping.

The staining procedure commenced with antigen retrieval of deparaffinized and rehydrated FFPE sections in AR6 buffer (Akoya Biosciences) via microwave, prior to peroxidase inactivation with 3% H2O2. Multiplex detection was performed through successive staining cycles. Each cycle consisted of blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin, application of primary and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, and tyramide-based signal development using an Opal fluorophore (Akoya Biosciences, 1:100) for covalent epitope labeling. Antibodies were removed between cycles to enable sequential staining. The sequence of primary antibody and Opal fluorophore was anti-CD14 (ab181470, 1:200, Abcam), anti-HLA-I (15240-1-AP, 1:800, Proteintech), anti-CD68 (GM087629, 1:200, Gene Tech), anti-CK (ZM-0069, 1:400, ZSGB-BIO), and anti-CD163 (Ab182422, 1:400, Abcam). Sections were counterstained with spectral DAPI (Akoya Biosciences), mounted (ab104135, Abcam), and scanned at ×200 magnification using a PANNORAMIC SCAN II Imaging System.

Imaging mass cytometry acquisition

FFPE sections (5 μm) were heated at 68 °C for 1 h, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. After three phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) rinses (5 min each), heat-induced antigen retrieval was conducted in citric acid buffer at 100 °C for 40 min, followed by passive cooling to room temperature (30 min).

Sections were rinsed sequentially in double-distilled water and PBS (10 min each), partially air-dried, and circumscribed with a hydrophobic barrier pen. Non-specific binding was quenched with SuperBlock reagent (37515, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min. Metal-labeled antibody cocktails were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by three PBS washes. Nuclear labeling used Intercalator-Ir solution (201192B, Fluidigm) at 1:400 dilution in PBS for 30 min. After final PBS and water rinses, tissue sections were acquired on the Hyperion imaging mass cytometer (Fluidigm).

IMC image preprocessing and downstream analysis

A board-certified pathologist identified regions of interest on corresponding H&E-stained sections. Multiplexed images spanning 1000 × 1000 μm² were acquired using the Hyperion Imaging System (Fluidigm). Raw acquisition data were processed with spillover compensation following published protocols, subjected to median filtering to reduce noise, and underwent linear intensity normalization to separate signal from background.40 Individual cell boundaries were delineated via connectivity-aware segmentation, and discrete cellular objects were identified using the MATLAB regionprops function.41 Membrane-associated signals located more than 15 pixels from the nearest nuclear centroid were excluded to reduce segmentation error.

Marker intensities per channel were normalized to the 99th percentile. Batch effects across samples were corrected with Harmony (v0.1.0), and cell phenotyping was performed through Rphenograph (v7) clustering. Cluster-level expression centroids were displayed as heatmaps to guide manual annotation. Spatial interaction analyzes between cell populations within defined neighborhoods were conducted using the imcRtools package (v1.0.2).

Identification of cellular neighborhoods

For each HLA-I–positive tumor cell, the 20 nearest neighboring cells were identified on the basis of Euclidean distance between centroid X/Y coordinates. The resulting neighborhood composition vectors were clustered by k-means (k = 15), assigning each cell to its respective cellular neighborhood. Neighborhood assignments were verified by superimposing Voronoi tessellations onto the original IMC images.

Patch and milieu detection

Spatially clustered immune cell aggregates were detected using the patch detection module of imcRtools. The analysis encompassed T lymphocytes, B cells, monocyte subsets, macrophages, and neutrophils. Cells were classified as belonging to a patch if they lay within 20 μm of one another, with a minimum of 10 cells required per patch. Patch outlines were generated using concave hull algorithms. The surrounding milieu was defined as the zone extending 20 μm beyond each patch border.

Cell culture

Five SCLC cell lines (DMS273, DMS53, NCI-H196, SW1271, DMS114) were obtained from Cobioer Biosciences (Nanjing, China); identity was authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling. SW1271 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (C11995500BT, Gibco), and the remaining lines in RPMI-1640 (C11875500BT, Gibco). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (A5256701, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (BL505A, Biosharp). Cells were grown at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ in a humidified incubator.

Cell transfection

HLA-I overexpression and corresponding empty-vector control constructs were produced by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). DMS273 cells in logarithmic growth phase were dissociated with 0.25% trypsin and seeded into 6-well plates at 20–30% confluence 1 day prior to transfection. Growth medium was replaced with serum-free medium immediately before transfection. Plasmid delivery was accomplished with TurboFect Reagent (R0531, Thermo Scientific). After 4–6 h of incubation, serum-free medium was exchanged for complete growth medium. Effective HLA-I upregulation was confirmed by Western blotting and real-time qPCR; empty-vector–transfected cells served as negative controls.

Protein extraction and Western blotting

Cells were harvested with trypsin, washed twice in PBS, and lysed on ice for 30 min in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA buffer) (P0013B, Beyotime) containing protease inhibitors. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (1200 × g, 15 min, 4 °C). Supernatants were mixed with loading buffer and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). Membranes were blocked, then probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting HLA-I (17260-1-APP, Proteintech) and HSP70 (66183-1-Ig, Proteintech). Bound antibodies were detected with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (HY-K1005, MCE).

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the SPARKeasy Cell RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (AC0205-B, Sparkjade). Reverse transcription was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol with ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (FSQ-201, TOYOBO). Gene expression was quantified by real-time PCR using 2×Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (G3326-15, Servicebio) on a LightCycler Pro system (Roche). Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was quantified using the Annexin V-FITC/Propidium Iodide Dead Cell Apoptosis Kit (V13242, Invitrogen). Transfected DMS273 cells (negative control and HLA-I overexpression groups) were allocated to experimental arms: tumor cells cultured alone, tumor cells co-cultured with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs; PB100C/PB100C-W, MILECELL BIO), or tumor cells co-cultured with PBMCs and anti-PD-1 antibody (Fig. 6a). Untransfected DMS273 cells served as baseline controls. Co-culture was conducted for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂.

After co-culture, cells were harvested from 6-well plates, dissociated enzymatically, and washed twice. Cell pellets were washed in ice-cold PBS, resuspended in 100 μL binding buffer, and stained with 5 μL Annexin V-FITC, 1 μL propidium iodide (PI) working solution, and 5 μL FcZero-rAb APC Anti-Human CD45 Antibody (APC-FcA98117, Proteintech). Following a 30-min incubation in the dark at room temperature, 400 μL binding buffer was added and samples were immediately acquired on a BD Fortessa flow cytometer. Single-color controls were used for compensation; FlowJo v10.8.1 was used to classify cells as viable (Annexin V−/PI−), early apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI−), late apoptotic/necrotic (Annexin V+/PI+), or necrotic (Annexin V−/PI+).

Statistical analysis

The anticipated improvement in median PFS with sintilimab maintenance therapy was from 9 to 16 months. A one-sided α of 0.1 was chosen for hypothesis testing, in accordance with accepted practice for exploratory single-arm phase II studies whose primary aim is to assess whether the treatment effect surpasses a pre-specified historical benchmark. Under these parameters, with a 12-month accrual period and 24-month follow-up (total study duration 36 months), a sample of 20 patients was required to provide 80% statistical power. After accounting for 10% anticipated dropout, the minimum enrollment target was set at 22 patients.

OS and PFS were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method, and inter-group differences were compared using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed from multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression; covariates were selected by forward stepwise entry based on the Wald statistic. Given the exploratory intent and small sample size, no correction for multiplicity was applied. Pairwise comparisons were performed with two-tailed Student’s t-tests; analyzes involving three or more groups used two-way analysis of variance. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. All analyzes were performed in R (version 3.3.4).
