Prenatal heat stress and predicted postpartum cardiovascular disease risk: a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Noelle Pardo, Xiaoran Yang, Yuhong Hu, Yan Xu, Roxana Khalili, Aviva Wolf-Jacobs, Sandrah P Eckel, Zhongzheng Niu, Tingyu Yang, Xinci Chen, Mario Vigil, Brendan H Grubbs, Laila Al-Marayati, Claudia M Toledo-Corral, Jill Johnston, Genevieve F Dunton, Carrie Breton, Theresa M Bastain, Rima Habre, Shohreh F Farzan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat stress has been implicated as a risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD). Wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) is considered a more physiologically relevant measure of heat stress than temperature alone, accounting for direct sunlight, relative humidity, solar radiation, and wind speed. Pregnant individuals may be particularly vulnerable to heat stress due to physiological changes in thermoregulation. Pregnancy may serve as a sensitive period influencing future health, but it is unknown if prenatal heat stress impacts postpartum cardiovascular health. We examined associations between prenatal, residential WBGT exposure and postpartum Pathobiologic Determinants of Atherosclerosis in Youth (PDAY) risk scores, which predict future atherosclerosis. Among 193 participants from the Maternal and Developmental Risks from Environmental and Social Stressors pregnancy cohort, annual postpartum PDAY scores were calculated across 6 years postpartum, using age, non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, hypertension, obesity, and hyperglycemia from in-person questionnaires and biomarker samples. We used hierarchical generalized additive models (GAMs) to examine associations between pregnancy average and trimester-specific WBGT and postpartum PDAY scores. Effect modification by prenatal psychosocial stressors, urban heat island index (UHII), and climate vulnerability index (CVI) was assessed through stratification. Distributed lag non-linear models (DLNM) with mixed effects assessed weekly associations. Models were adjusted for maternal age at consent, pre-pregnancy body mass index, household income, maternal education, race/ethnicity, and conception season. Model fit indices indicated a nonlinear relationship between average prenatal WBGT and postpartum PDAY scores, with a positive association emerging at temperatures above 19 °C, though confidence intervals were wide. We were unable to observe evidence of associations with trimester-specific WBGT, sensitive windows of exposure, or effect modification by psychosocial stressors, UHII, or CVI. Our data suggested an association at pregnancy average WBGT greater than 19 °C with postpartum cardiometabolic health risk scores, however sparsity at temperature extremes limited our conclusions. The relatively limited exposure variability in our study region and small sample size may have limited our ability to observe more robust associations. Background

Globally, populations are experiencing widespread increases in the intensity and frequency of extreme weather events, natural disasters, and record-high temperatures due to climate change1. Climate-related environmental stressors can influence health, with extreme heat implicated as an important risk factor contributing to cardiovascular disease (CVD) morbidity and mortality2,3. While all populations are at risk, certain populations are more vulnerable to physiological impacts of heat4. For example, normal physiological adaptations during pregnancy can affect the body’s ability to thermoregulate, which may make it harder for pregnant individuals to dissipate heat and increase vulnerability to the impacts of heat stress4. Given projected increases in climate-related heat events and their influence on CVD, it is critical to understand how these environmental stressors may impact long-term cardiometabolic health in at-risk populations, particularly pregnant individuals.

Most studies in pregnant populations have observed a non-linear relationship between temperature and cardiometabolic health outcomes. For example, a systematic review and meta-analysis of fifteen studies across multiple geographic regions with varied climates found that heat exposure in early pregnancy increased the risk of preeclampsia, but in mid- to late pregnancy, results indicated both cold and heat exposure may increase risk5. Similarly, a review of eight studies found higher temperatures during pregnancy were generally associated with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and two studies, based in China and Southern California, showed cold temperatures below − 0.8 °C and 11.3 °C in each study, respectively, were also associated with GDM6–8. Dehydration is a potential mechanism hypothesized to trigger a cascade of events, including sympathetic nervous system activation, endothelial cell activation, and inflammation, leading to excessive cardiac strain9,10. Animal studies have also suggested that oxidative stress, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and brown adipose tissue may also be possible pathways10–12. While there is evidence linking extreme heat to acute CVD-related pregnancy complications, the long-term effects and mechanisms remain unclear9.

Although there are multiple methods to measure heat stress, wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) is proposed as an international standard and considered a more sensitive measure of how the body responds to heat stress and its ability to dissipate metabolic heat compared to temperature alone, by incorporating temperature in direct sunlight, relative humidity, solar radiation, and wind speed13,14. A systematic review of 913 international studies published between December 1957 and July 2023 found that of the 567 studies examining outdoor WBGT, only three included pregnant individuals, highlighting a critical gap in heat research for this vulnerable population15. Since this review, there have been additional studies using WBGT assessing pregnancy outcomes. For example, a prospective cohort study in Taiwan found that extreme WBGT exposure (≥ 95th percentile, approximately 27 °C) during late pregnancy (26 weeks to birth) was associated with reduced birth weight and increased risk of low birth weight, while extreme WBGT across the entire pregnancy (~ 24 °C) was associated with increased risk of preterm birth (OR = 3.35) and low birth weight16. At the global level, a recent ecological study across 204 countries found a significant nonlinear, U- or J-shaped association between WBGT and the disability burden of maternal hypertensive disorders, with the lowest burden at ~ 11.1 °C and risk increasing beyond 24.0 °C, though this association was less apparent among high socioeconomic countries17.

Notably, the potential long-term impact of prenatal heat stress on maternal cardiometabolic health remains understudied. To our knowledge, there is no research on the long-term impact of prenatal heat stress on maternal health beyond the pregnancy period. While it is known that prenatal heat stress can adversely affect maternal cardiometabolic health and that exposures during this pregnancy can lead to increased risk of CVD, there is a gap in studies directly examining pregnancy as a sensitive window for heat stress on postpartum cardiovascular health18–20. Addressing this gap could provide valuable insights into the long-term health trajectories of mothers following pregnancy.

Vulnerability to heat stress may differ based on contextual factors, including social, environmental, economic, and health access conditions, that influence a community’s capacity to respond to extreme weather events21,22. The Climate Vulnerability Index (CVI) captures United States census-tract level factors that influence a community’s vulnerability to climate change, while the Urban Heat Island Index (UHII), developed by CalEPA, quantifies the degree to which neighborhoods are warmer than surrounding rural areas due to impervious surfaces and reduced vegetation21–23. At the individual level, perceived stress and depression may heighten vulnerability to environmental stressors, while social cohesion may bolster resilience; these psychosocial factors are independently associated with CVD risk and are thought to operate through similar biological pathways as environmental stressors, including inflammatory and oxidative stress mechanisms20,26–28. Whether these contextual or psychosocial factors modify the long-term association between prenatal heat stress and postpartum cardiometabolic health remains unknown.

Our study aimed to evaluate the association between prenatal heat stress, using WBGT assessed based on daily residential timelines, and postpartum cardiometabolic health, measured by the Pathobiological Determinants of Atherosclerosis in Youth (PDAY) risk score, among a primarily low-income, Hispanic pregnancy cohort. The PDAY risk score predicts atherosclerotic CVD risk in young adults under 40 by incorporating multiple cardiometabolic risk factors, such as hypertension, cholesterol, and diabetes24,25. We also examined whether exposures during critical gestational windows may drive these potential associations. Secondary analyses included assessing effect modification by UHII, CVI, season of conception, and psychosocial factors. Understanding critical windows for exposure and effect modification could help identify specific periods of pregnancy for possible intervention and uncover novel vulnerabilities.

Methods

Study population

The Maternal and Developmental Risks from Environmental and Social Stressors (MADRES) study is an ongoing prospective pregnancy cohort, which recruited participants from November 2015 through May 2023 from three prenatal clinical sites that serve low-income communities in Los Angeles, California. Details of the study population and protocol have been previously published26. To be eligible, pregnant individuals had to be 18 years or older, less than 30 weeks of gestation, and fluent in English or Spanish. Participants were excluded if they were HIV positive, currently incarcerated, or had a disability preventing participation or informed consent.

Participants were prospectively followed at each trimester during pregnancy and annually after birth. A subset of the MADRES participants (N = 246) contributed postpartum serum, plasma, or whole blood samples across six years postpartum. Of this subset, 240 participants had complete postpartum cardiometabolic health information, defined as having all biomarkers used in the PDAY score equations available. Among these, 228 participants had prenatal WBGT data assessed at the primary residence. We excluded participants with chronic diabetes (N = 10), chronic hypertension (N = 9), and those who were currently pregnant during the postpartum measurement (N = 8, Number of observations (Nobs) = 13). The final analytic sample included 193 participants (Nobs = 241), with 145 participants with one measure and 48 with two repeated measures. The consort diagram is presented in Figure S1.

Prenatal exposure assessment

Daily geocoded residential histories were assembled for each participant using a prenatal residential history questionnaire administered in the third trimester, encompassing the period of 1 year before conception through the third trimester and capturing residential mobility, which study staff subsequently reviewed with participants to ensure data accuracy. Afterward, residential addresses and mobility were prospectively collected at each follow-up visit. Daily exposure to outdoor temperature (T), relative humidity (RH), precipitation, wind speed and direction, and downward shortwave radiation at the participant’s residential location were assigned to participants using the Abatzoglou gridded 4 × 4 km gridMET model, a widely used ecological model that provides complete coverage of our study area with high spatiotemporal resolution27. WBGT (oC) was derived from these meteorological data, taking into account daily average temperature, daily average relative humidity, shortwave radiation, and wind speed with previously described equations27–29.

Postpartum predicted cardiometabolic health

The Pathobiologic Determinants of Atherosclerosis in Youth (PDAY) risk score was developed to predict atherosclerotic CVD events using cardiometabolic risk factors measured before age 4024,25. PDAY scores are calculated using a weighted combination of age, sex, non-HDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, sex-specific BMI, blood pressure, and hyperglycemia24. The point value assigned to each risk factor is shown in Table S1. We generated a modified PDAY score, following the original scoring method with two exceptions. First, we extended the age categories to include two additional bins for 40–44 and 45–50 years of age, following the same scoring convention. There were few participants older than 40 in the analysis (N = 13, Nobs = 14). Secondly, smoking was included in the original scoring system, with 0 or 1 points; however, due to limited data availability on postpartum smoking and very low rates of prenatal smoking in our population (1.5%, N = 3), we excluded it from our scoring metric.

Cardiometabolic health data used for the modified PDAY scores were collected from participants during annual in-person visits conducted within an 8-week window around their child’s birthday. Trained staff took three to five readings of systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) after a five-minute rest period using an automated oscillometric instrument (GE Carescape Dinamap V100), which were averaged. Study phlebotomists collected up to 50 ml of blood using standard venipuncture protocols at each annual visit. Serum from maternal blood samples was analyzed by ELISA for circulating lipids, including high density lipoprotein (HDL) and total cholesterol. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured using a DCA Vantage Analyzer (Siemens Medical) in whole blood. Postpartum BMI was calculated using study-measured weight and height using a commercial stadiometer. Participants additionally answered questionnaires on medical diagnosis and medication use for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. A total of 193 participants had all components for the PDAY score equations across six years postpartum. Among these, 145 participants contributed one measurement, while 48 participants had two repeated measures. Over the course of six years postpartum, the number of participants with PDAY scores was as follows: 105 at one year, 25 at two years, 55 at three years, 42 at four years, 13 at five years, and 3 at six years.

Covariates

Covariates to be included in multivariate models were identified a priori based on a review of the literature and visualized through directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) (Figure S2). Covariate information was collected via interviewer-administered questionnaires at study entry and prospectively across follow-up. Pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated using self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and study-measured height using a commercial stadiometer during pregnancy. The season of conception was coded as warm (May to October) and cool (November to April). Participants were asked about air conditioning use, time spent outside, physical activity, and psychosocial factors during each prenatal visit in the first, second, and third trimesters. Air conditioning use was defined as use during any trimester. Time spent outdoors was assessed by the question, “Thinking back to a typical weekday in this past week, approximately how many hours (out of 24 hours in total) did you spend outdoors?” Physical activity was measured using the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ)30. Time spent outside during pregnancy and physical activity scores were averaged across trimesters. Personal behavior variables that may influence outdoor heat exposure, including air conditioning use, time spent outdoors, and physical activity, were evaluated as potential confounders; as these did not meaningfully alter associations, they were not retained in the final models. Maternal demographic covariates assessed included age at the consent date, race and ethnicity, education level, annual household income, parity, pre-pregnancy BMI, and season of conception.

Effect modifiers included the Urban Heat Island Index (UHII), Climate Vulnerability Index (CVI), prenatal perceived stress, depressive symptoms, and neighborhood social cohesion. The UHII was assigned at the residence and measures the difference in temperature between an urban area and a rural reference point outside of the city in Celsius-hours per day23. The mean UHII during pregnancy appropriately weights the time spent at each location if the participant lived at more than one residence. The CVI was developed by the Environmental Defense Fund to estimate vulnerabilities nationwide that increase climate change risk and reduce resilience, using 184 indicators based on public health, socio-economic, environmental, and climate data in the United States at the census-tract level21. To assess effect modification, we created binary variables based on the 75th percentile of each index. Psychosocial factors were measured by the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) and Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CES-D) Scale, which were administered during prenatal visits and averaged across pregnancy to assess perceived stress and depressive symptoms, respectively31,32. The perceived Neighborhood Social Cohesion (NSC) scale was administered once during the second trimester, representing the extent to which neighbors/communities share values, support, and trust each other33. All scales have been validated and used among diverse populations31,33–37. Pregnancy average PSS and NSC scales were dichotomized based on the median scores in the sample. The pregnancy average CES-D scale was divided using a cutoff score of 16, which is used to identify clinically significant depression symptoms32.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed and summarized in Table 1 using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables. To investigate the association between pregnancy-averaged and trimester-specific WBGT and PDAY scores across six years postpartum, we first assessed linearity using hierarchical GAMs, with a penalized spline term. Given the significance of the non-linear term, we fitted hierarchical GAMs with random intercepts adjusted for covariates. We also fit linear mixed-effect models to evaluate WBGT as a linear variable. Given the distribution of WBGT in our sample, we also performed a sensitivity analysis excluding influential values at lower WBGT, defined as below the 5th percentile (Pregnancy WBGT < 15.4 °C (N excluded = 10), first trimester WBGT < 12.1 °C (N excluded = 9), second trimester WBGT < 12.6 °C (N excluded = 9), third trimester WBGT < 12.1 °C (N excluded = 13)). Since postpartum smoking was not included in the scoring system and given the small number of participants who reported smoking during pregnancy (N = 3, N missing = 1), we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding these participants. Due to differences in temperature ranges and potential acclimatization or adaptations to heat during warmer seasons, we conducted a sensitivity analysis stratified by season of conception38. We performed an inverse probability weighted sensitivity analysis to account for and assess potential selection bias39. Briefly, inverse probability weighting accounts for selection bias by upweighting participants more likely to be missing based on observed characteristics; weights were derived from logistic regression models predicting data availability at each postpartum year. Additional detail is available in the Supplemental Methods. We additionally conducted secondary stratified analyses to assess effect modification by psychosocial factors (PSS, CES-D, and NSC), CVI, and UHII.

Lastly, we fit distributed lag nonlinear models (DLNMs) with mixed effects, including a random intercept for each participant to account for repeated measures of PDAY scores across six postpartum years, to estimate potential critical windows of weekly prenatal heat stress exposure. The DLNM concurrently included exposures of each weekly heat stress measure across 40 gestation weeks using a “cross-basis,” specifying a lag-response relationship and exposure-response function to constrain correlated weekly exposures. We evaluated both linear and non-linear lag-response relationships and assessed non-linearity for the exposure-response relationship40. Model selection was determined by the quasi-information criterion (QIC)40. We conducted several sensitivity analyses for the DLNM to address differences in gestational lengths, as DLNMs cannot accommodate missing lag exposures within the model framework. Initially, for women with varying gestational lengths, we set their weekly WBGT to 0 °C. Additionally, we carried out three sensitivity analyses: (1) We fit the same DLNM model for up to 40 gestational weeks and set their weekly WBGT to the median value of 17.3 °C. (2) We fit the DLNM model for up to 25 gestational weeks, including only those with complete data, and truncated the exposure windows to include all mothers who delivered preterm (N = 193). (3) We fit the DLNM model for up to 39 weeks, including mothers with full-term births and excluding those with missing lags (N = 135). R version 4.3.1 was used. GAMs were performed using the mgcv package, while DLNMs were performed using the lme4 and dlnm packages40–42.

Results

Participant characteristics are described in Table 1. Participants were, on average, 29.0 (SD: 5.7) years old at consent with a pre-pregnancy BMI of 27.9 kg/m2 (SD: 5.5). Most participants were Hispanic (82.4%), had an annual family income of less than $30,000 (45.6%), and conceived during the cool season in Southern California (57.7%). Half had a high school education or less (49.3%). The average WBGT was 17.3 °C (SD: 1.4; Range: 7.9–20.6) during pregnancy, 16.9 °C (SD: 3.8; Range: −2.8–22.6) in the first trimester, 17.7 °C (SD: 3.4; Range: 4.0–22.7) in the second trimester, and 17.1 °C (SD: 3.6; Range: 11.1–23.4) in the third trimester. Across 40 gestation weeks, the weekly average WBGT was 17.3 °C. (SD: 4.2; Range: −10.5–26.5). PDAY scores ranged from 2 to 38 across six years and are shown in Table S2 along with the score components. Further participant characteristics comparing this subset to the full MADRES cohort are shown in Table S3.

Figure 1 presents partial effect plots illustrating the association between pregnancy and trimester average WBGT and PDAY scores across six years, adjusted for covariates, using hierarchical GAM models. Fit indices indicated a nonlinear relationship between average pregnancy WBGT and postpartum PDAY scores (estimated degrees of freedom = 4.10, p = 0.010). Below 19 °C WBGT, 95% confidence limits of the partial effect crossed zero, suggesting no significant association with PDAY scores. Above 19 °C, the curve suggests a significant positive association between higher WGBT and higher PDAY scores; however, these data at the upper end of the curve were sparse, with wide confidence intervals (Fig. 1A). Trimester-specific associations varied but overall confidence intervals crossed zero throughout, suggesting no statistically significant association between prenatal WBGT and postpartum PDAY scores. The first trimester showed a nonlinear pattern with negative associations at lower and higher temperature extremes, the second trimester demonstrated a relatively flat association, and the third trimester showed a modest positive association at mid-range temperatures that declined at higher temperatures (Fig. 1B–D).

Additional sensitivity analyses, excluding WBGT values below the 5th percentile and those who smoked during pregnancy, and inverse probability-weighted analyses revealed patterns consistent with the main analyses (Figures S3-S5). Further, sensitivity analyses for DLNMs collectively indicated no significant associations, similar to the main analysis (Figures S6-S8). Secondary analyses did not find evidence of effect modification by depressive symptoms, perceived stress, or neighborhood social cohesion (Figures S9-S11). Stratified models revealed patterns that were consistently close to zero and shapes that were similar across levels. Additionally, stratification based on the UHII and the CVI did not indicate heterogeneity between low and high groups (Figures S12 and S13). Fit indices indicated that non-linear relationships provided the best fit; however, linear mixed effects model results are also included in Table S4 and showed non-significant positive relationships between pregnancy average, first- and second-trimester WBGT, and non-significant negative associations with third-trimester average WBGT.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate how prenatal heat stress may affect postpartum cardiometabolic risk over six years after giving birth. Findings from pregnancy-average heat-stress analyses suggested potential associations at the highest exposure levels in our sample population, beyond the 95th percentile of WBGT (18.9 °C), but caution is advised in interpreting this finding, given sparse data above the 95th percentile. DLNM analyses suggested no significant association between prenatal weekly WBGT and postpartum PDAY scores, which may be a more reasonable measurement than pregnancy-averaged temperature, as it does not average out temperature extremes and retains the greatest temperature variability. After stratifying by conception season in pregnancy and trimester-specific analyses, we observed different shapes of association for participants who conceived in warm and cool seasons; however, given the small sample size and sparse data at the low and high ends of WBGT, these stratified findings should be interpreted with caution. There may be multiple reasons why we were unable to observe an association in our population.

First, heat stress appears to be more strongly linked to acute cardiometabolic risks, while evidence for long-term effects remains limited and not well-understood. A review of 282 articles and a meta-analysis of 266 studies have established consistent associations between heat stress and CVD-related mortality2. Evidence among pregnant populations is mounting, with a meta-analysis of fifteen articles on HDPs and a review of eight studies on GDM, showing shorter-term relationships of heat stress and cardiometabolic health5–8. In contrast, only four studies have focused on the long-term impacts of temperature on CVD mortality among non-pregnant populations9. Findings from these studies were inconsistent, not significant, or indicated an association with cold temperature, but not heat9.

Second, we may be limited by the low variability of heat stress within the study region. Participants within this pregnancy cohort lived in urban Southern California with an average WBGT ranging from − 2.81 °C to 23.4 °C during pregnancy. Currently, no other studies have used WBGT as an exposure measure in Southern California in relation to cardiometabolic health outcomes. However, the Southern California Kaiser Permanente cohort observed a relationship between extreme temperature and GDM risk in a similar urban population and reported a notably wider range of temperature exposures across pregnancy7. Their ability to detect associations may reflect both their substantially larger sample size and greater temperature variability compared to our study.

Our sample’s range of WBGT typically falls within low-risk categories among institutions that use WBGT to define heat hazard risks. However, since there are no pregnancy-specific guidelines, these indices may underestimate the risk of heat stress on more vulnerable groups. For example, in Southern California, heat safety guidelines for acclimatized individuals from the American College of Sports Medicine, allow normal activity at a WBGT of 6.7 °C to 24.5 °C43. When the WBGT exceeds 24.6 °C, intense or prolonged exercise is recommended to be approached with caution, and at over 29 °C, exercise should be canceled43. For unacclimatized workers on the job for under two weeks, OSHA guidelines state that a WBGT below 21 °C has a low risk of heat-related illness44. A WBGT of 21 °C to 25 °C is potentially unsafe for strenuous work, and above 25 °C is higher risk44. The military also has heat hazard categories for work, with 26 °C to 29 °C WBGT classified as low risk for personnel45. Although these indices are not directly comparable since they were developed for specific groups and may need to be assessed in the context of pregnancy, they provide a helpful reference point, indicating the participants in this study had relatively low variability in WBGT.

Third, outdoor WBGT exposure is modeled based on residential locations over time, which accurately captures moving primary residences, but is not a direct, personal exposure measure of heat stress. WBGT was derived from a gridded climate model at a 4 × 4 km resolution, which may not fully capture neighborhood-level microclimatic variability within densely urbanized areas such as Los Angeles, potentially underestimating heterogeneity in heat exposure across participants. Participants’ actual experience of heat stress in their day-to-day lives may differ from outdoor, residential WBGT estimates due to time-activity and behavioral factors, such as AC use, time spent outdoors or at non-home locations, and physical activity. We attempted to account for these differences by adjusting for time-activity variables to the best of our ability; however, questionnaire data availability varied across time periods and required harmonization. Additionally, third trimester average WBGT estimates should be interpreted with caution, as averaging over this period may be influenced by variation in the length of the third trimester, particularly among individuals with preterm births who had shorter third trimester periods. Most importantly, our sample is a smaller subset of the MADRES cohort who provided biospecimens postpartum; thus, our findings and ability to conduct stratified analyses are significantly limited by sample size.

Nevertheless, our study had several strengths. We employed novel methods, including the use of DLNMs, and leveraged the study’s prospective design, comprehensive residential history data, and high spatiotemporal resolution of prenatal heat exposure. We utilized PDAY scores to represent a comprehensive overview of cardiometabolic health, which incorporates multiple risk factors for CVD. PDAY scores were developed to be highly predictive of atherosclerotic CVD events; for example, a one-unit change in PDAY scores in early adulthood was associated with an OR of 1.31 (1.27–1.35) for coronary artery calcium scores assessed 25 years later24. Additionally, we were able to evaluate non-linear relationships between prenatal WBGT and PDAY scores among participants who comprised an understudied population. Overall, we were unable to find evidence of an association between prenatal heat stress and postpartum cardiometabolic health.

Conclusions

We were unable to observe an association between prenatal heat stress and cardiometabolic health across six years postpartum. These findings represent an initial step toward understanding potential long-term climate change impacts among pregnant individuals. This study is the first to explore how heat stress during pregnancy might affect future maternal cardiometabolic health. Future studies with larger sample sizes, greater exposure variability, and detailed information on personal and neighborhood adaptation and vulnerability factors may better elucidate the complex, non-linear, and longer-term impacts of prenatal heat stress on cardiometabolic health.
