The activity‐regulated cytoskeleton‐associated protein (Arc) functions in a cell type‐ and sex‐specific manner in the adult nucleus accumbens to regulate non‐contingent cocaine behaviors
Authors: Daniel J Wood, Jessica L Huebschman, Dalia Martinez, Evgeny Tsvetkov, Kirsten Snyder, Raymond Tjhia, Jaswinder Kumar, Brandon W Hughes, Makoto Taniguchi, Laura N Smith, Christopher W Cowan, Rachel D Penrod
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRepeated cocaine use produces adaptations in brain function that contribute to long‐lasting behaviors associated with cocaine use disorder (CUD). In rodents, the activity‐regulated cytoskeleton‐associated protein (Arc) can regulate glutamatergic synaptic transmission, and cocaine regulates Arc expression and subcellular localization in multiple brain regions, including the nucleus accumbens (NAc)—a brain region linked to CUD‐related behavior. We show here that repeated, non‐contingent cocaine administration in global Arc KO male mice produced a dramatic hypersensitization of cocaine locomotor responses and drug experience‐dependent sensitization of conditioned place preference (CPP). In contrast to the global Arc KO mice, viral‐mediated reduction of Arc in the adult male, but not female, NAc (shArcNAc) reduced both CPP and cocaine‐induced locomotor activity, but without altering basal miniature or evoked glutamatergic synaptic transmission. Interestingly, cell type‐specific knockdown of Arc in D1 dopamine receptor‐expressing NAc neurons reduced cocaine‐induced locomotor sensitization, but not cocaine CPP; whereas, Arc knockdown in D2 dopamine receptor‐expressing NAc neurons reduced cocaine CPP, but not cocaine‐induced locomotion. Taken together, our findings reveal that global, developmental loss of Arc produces hypersensitized cocaine responses; however, these effects cannot be explained by Arc's function in the adult mouse NAc since Arc is required in a cell type‐ and sex‐specific manner to support cocaine‐context associations and locomotor responses.Keywords: AMPA, Arc/Arg3.1, cocaine, conditioned place preference, knockout mouse, medium spiny neurons, NMDA, nucleus accumbens, sensitization, sex differences

INTRODUCTION

The activity‐regulated cytoskeleton‐associated protein (Arc or Arg3.1) is an immediate early gene (IEG) induced by neuronal activity following experiences like exposure to novel environments, learning during various behavioral paradigms, and exposure to psychostimulants or other drugs of abuse. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 The rapid induction of Arc expression following neuronal activity leads to its accumulation in both the nucleus, where it has been observed at sites of transcriptional regulation and colocalizing with chromatin remodelers, 5 , 6 , 7 and neuronal dendrites and synapses, where it is involved in aspects of synaptic plasticity like amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid receptor (AMPAR) endocytosis, 8 long‐term potentiation (LTP), 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 but see also Kyrke‐Smith et al., 13 and long‐term depression (LTD). 11 , 14 , 15 , 16 Arc is important for numerous rodent behaviors, including anxiety‐ and depressive‐like behavior, contextual and cued fear conditioning, novelty discrimination, and impaired long‐term memory consolidation and maintenance. 9 , 11 , 17 , 18 Several rodent studies indicate that Arc also negatively regulates various drug‐related behaviors, including locomotor sensitization, 19 , 20 drug conditioned place preference (CPP), 20 , 21 and dose–response behavior during cocaine intravenous self‐administration, 22 emphasizing that Arc is a critical gene involved in CUD‐like behavioral adaptations.

The ventral striatum, or nucleus accumbens (NAc), has been implicated in both the early reinforcing effects of drugs of abuse 23 and subsequent drug‐seeking behavior, 24 with a critical role in both psychostimulant‐induced locomotor sensitization and drug CPP in rodent models. 25 D1 and D2 dopamine receptor‐expressing medium spiny neurons (D1‐ and D2‐MSNs), the primary neuronal cell‐types within the NAc, work in opposition to regulate basal ganglia output, with activity in D1‐MSNs typically promoting, and activity in D2‐MSNs typically opposing, drug‐related behavior. 26 , 27 , 28 Arc is expressed in numerous brain regions, including the NAc, but a gap in knowledge remains regarding when, where and how Arc functions in the developing and/or mature brain to influence cocaine‐induced behaviors.

Here, we report that global, developmental loss of Arc (Arc KO) in male mice results in hypersensitization of cocaine‐induced locomotion and drug experience‐dependent sensitization of cocaine conditioned place preference (CPP). However, using a viral‐mediated shRNA approach in adult mice, we find that knockdown of NAc Arc in male, but not female, C57BL/6J mice reduces these cocaine‐related behaviors, but with no effects on basal glutamatergic synaptic transmission in NAc MSNs. Finally, we show that NAc Arc is required in D1‐MSNs to mediate normal cocaine‐induced locomotor sensitization, but it is required in D2‐MSNs for normal cocaine CPP, revealing distinct cell type‐specific roles for Arc in these cocaine‐induced behaviors in male mice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

For all mouse behavioral, surgical and electrophysiology experiments, young adult males were used. For the studies in WT (C57Bl/6J) mice with AAV2‐shArc, both males and female mice were tested. Since the shArc effects on cocaine behavior were only observed in males, subsequent experiments using D1‐Cre and D2‐Cre mice with Cre‐dependent shArc were performed only in males. All mice were group‐housed with same sex littermates on either a standard (Arc KO studies) or reverse (AAV2‐shArc studies) light cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water throughout the duration of the study. Mice received stereotaxic infusion of viral vectors, where relevant, at 7–9 weeks of age and began behavioral examinations at 9–12 weeks of age. Arc‐GFP knock‐in mice (Wang et al. 29 ; The Jackson Laboratory stock no. 007662), where the Arc protein coding region is replaced with the GFP coding sequence and referred to here as Arc knockout (KO) mice, were backcrossed to congenicity on the C57Bl/6N strain (Charles River), and homozygous Arc KO and wild‐type littermates were generated from Arc‐GFP heterozygous crosses. AAV2‐shArc studies were conducted with C57Bl/6J (Jackson Labs) mice. For cell type‐specific studies, heterozygous bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) transgenic mice in which Cre recombinase (Cre) was expressed under the control of the Drd1a promoter (Drd1a [D1]‐Cre, line FK150) or the D2R promoter (Drd2 [D2]‐Cre, line ER44) were used. 30 , 31 These mice were originally obtained by J. Heinsbroek & P. Kalivas (MUSC) from N. Heintz & P. Greengard (Rockefeller University), C. Gerfen (National Institute of Mental Health) and NINDS/GENSAT (www.gensat.org). All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at UT Southwestern Medical Center, McLean Hospital and/or Medical University of South Carolina.

Drugs

Cocaine‐HCl (NIDA Drug Supply Program) was dissolved in sterile 0.9% (w/v) saline at 3.0, 2.0, 1.5, 0.75 and 0.5 mg/mL for mouse cocaine behavioral experiments and injected at 0.1 mL per 10 g body weight. Ketamine, xylazine, cefazolin and ketoprofen (all from Patterson Veterinary Supply) were diluted or resuspended in sterile 0.9% (w/v) saline.

Surgical procedures

Stereotactic delivery of viral vectors in mice were conducted as previously described. 32 Briefly, mice were anesthetized using inhaled isoflurane (1%–4% v/v) in oxygen and received ketoprofen (5 mg/kg; s.c.) perioperatively. High‐titer (1013) AAV2 virus (150–350 nL, not varied within experiment; University of South Carolina Viral Vector Core) was infused bilaterally into the NAc (10° angle; from Bregma +1.6 A/P, ±1.5 M/L, −4.4 D/V) using Hamilton syringes (Fisher, #84851) or Nanojectors (Drummond, #3‐000‐204) at a rate of ~50 nL per 30 s.

Viral vectors

Constitutive shRNA viruses (AAV2‐shArc and AAV2‐shLuciferase—“shControl”) were previously validated. 18 Briefly, these constructs drive eGFP expression under the control of a CMV promoter and shRNAs are driven by the U6 promoter. Target sequences were as follows:

shArc–AATCTGCAGGATCACATTGGGCTTCCTGTCACCCAATGTGATCCTGCAGATTTTTTT;

shArc–AATCTGCAGGATCACATTGGGCTTCCTGTCACCCAATGTGATCCTGCAGATTTTTTT;

shLuciferase—CTTACGCTGAGTACTTCGATTCAAGAGATCGAAGTACTCAGCGTAAGTTTTT.

shLuciferase—CTTACGCTGAGTACTTCGATTCAAGAGATCGAAGTACTCAGCGTAAGTTTTT.

Cre‐dependent viruses were created using the AAV‐SICO‐shRNA 33 with some modifications and using the same target sequences as above. The construct constitutively expresses mCherry under the control of an EF1 promoter. Downstream, a U6 promoter containing a TATA‐loxP site drives the expression eGFP containing a second TATA‐loxP site further downstream. When Cre is present, eGFP is excised, bringing the shRNA under control of the U6 promoter. Therefore, cells that do not express the shRNA express both mCherry and eGFP, while cells expressing Cre and the shRNA only express mCherry. All viruses were manufactured at the University of South Carolina Viral Vector Core at high titer (≥1013 viral genomes/μL).

Locomotor assays

Locomotor sensitization assays were conducted as previously described. 34 Briefly, following 1 h of habituation to the test chamber, mice received an injection of either saline or cocaine and were returned to the test chamber for an additional hour during which locomotor activity was recorded via infrared beam break. Arc KO mice received habituating injections to saline (4 days; 0.9% w/v, i.p.) followed by seven daily injections of cocaine (15 mg/kg; i.p.) and in some cases, challenge injections were given after indicated abstinence periods (Figure 1B). In the abbreviated protocol (Figure 3C), Arc shRNA mice received habituating injections to saline (3 days; 0.9% w/v), then one injection of cocaine (20 mg/kg; i.p.) and a second injection 5–7 days later. Data are reported as the sum of beam breaks in the first 15 or 30 min of each session.

Stereotypy analysis

Stereotypy was assessed in the cocaine‐treatment groups shown in the Figure 1 locomotor experiment, as previously described. 34 Briefly, behavior during the indicated locomotor testing trials was video recorded for later analysis. Observations took place in 10 s bins at 3‐min intervals over 30 min following injection, starting at the beginning of the 2nd minute. The predominant behavior during each bin was scored using a published stereotypy scale 35 ; modified so that 6 = intense sniffing or orofacial stereotypy in one location. Median scores for each mouse across bins were used to calculate group averages. Additional time‐sampling was performed for multiple individual behaviors during these trials, including inactivity, grooming, locomotion, rearing, head‐up and head‐down sniffing and orofacial stereotypies (taffy‐pulling and paws‐to‐mouth). For these analyses, mice were observed in 1‐min bins at the same intervals as above and received a score of 0 or 1 per behavior per bin, depending on the behavior's absence/presence. “Inactivity,” “head‐up sniffing,” “head‐down sniffing” and “paws‐to‐mouth” had to be observed continuously for at least 5 s to be scored as present. “Locomotion” was defined as crossing the lengthwise midline of the cage. Scores for each individual behavior were summed over bins per mouse and used to calculate group averages.

Cocaine conditioned place preference (CPP)

CPP was performed as previously described 36 using two‐chambered (shArc studies) and three‐chambered (Arc KO studies) CPP apparatuses (Med‐Associates, St. Albans, VT), in an unbiased experimental design. Mice that exhibited a pre‐conditioning (pre‐test) preference of ≥1000 s for either of the conditioning chambers were excluded from data analysis. Following pre‐test, mice received alternating days of cocaine (5 mg/kg) and saline (0.9% [w/v] NaCl) injections and were confined to alternating sides of the conditioning chamber for 30 min. Following four pairing sessions (2 cocaine, 2 saline), mice were placed in the apparatus with all chambers accessible, and time spent in each chamber was measured for 20 min. Cocaine CPP score is calculated as the difference in time spent on the drug‐paired side post‐conditioning compared with the same chamber pre‐conditioning (post minus pre).

Tissue collection and processing

Mice were decapitated and brains were fixed for 24 h in 4% (w/v) PFA before transfer to 30% (w/v) sucrose for cryoprotection. Following cryoprotection, brains were sectioned (40–50 μm) on a freezing microtome and viral expression was confirmed by eGFP expression (AAV2‐shArc) or eGFP and mCherry expression (AAV2‐SICO‐shArc). Animals with excessive viral diffusion, uneven expression between hemispheres, or low viral expression were noted under blinded conditions and excluded from the study.

Fluorescent in situ hybridization

D2‐Cre mice expressing Cre‐dependent shArc or shControl viruses were decapitated and brains were rapidly extracted and flash frozen in isopentane solution on dry ice. Brains were sliced at 16 μm in a cryostat (−18°C), and sections were air‐dried to the slides at −18°C and stored at −80°C. For the detection of Cre recombinase, Arc, or mCherry mRNA, the RNAscope Fluorescent Multiplex kit (320293; Advanced Cell Diagnostics) was used with slight modification of manufacturer instructions and using commercially available probes (Cre [C1, #474001], mCherry [C2, #431201‐C2], Arc [C3, #316911‐C3]) as described previously. 37 Briefly, sections were fixed for 20 min in neutral‐buffered 10% (w/v) formalin, followed by ethanol dehydration series on 50% (v/v), 70%, 95% and 100%, 5 min each. Before hybridization, sections were treated with protease IV (322340, Advanced Cell Diagnostics) for 15 min to reduce viral protein fluorescence. Sections were hybridized for 2 h at 40°C, and probes were amplified using TSA fluorescent detection kits as directed by manufacture (Akoya Bioscience). The C1 (Cre) probe was conjugated to Alexa 488, C2 (mCherry) to Cy3 and C3 (Arc) to Cy5. Sections were then counterstained with DAPI and mounted using ProLong Gold mounting medium (Thermo Fisher) and allowed to dry. Composite z‐stack images at 63× magnification were generated using confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 880) and images were analyzed using ImageJ. Briefly, z‐stack images were max‐projected to a single plane image for each fluorescent channel and the free‐hand selection tool was used to encircle DAPI stained nuclei. The same selection area was used to quantify the integrated density of the Arc signal. Cells were identified as infected Cre+ (co‐expression of Cre and mCherry puncta), uninfected Cre+ (Cre puncta in the absence of mCherry puncta), and infected Cre− (mCherry puncta in the absence of mCherry puncta). Arc signal intensity was compared between infected Cre+ cells from either shArc or shControl conditions as well as Arc intensity in “incomplete combinations” (Cre+/mCherry negative; mCherry+/Cre negative).

Electrophysiology

Electrophysiological measurements were similar to previously described. 34 Briefly, shRNA‐expressing mice were decapitated by guillotine, and acute coronal slices containing the NAc were prepared using a vibratome (Leica VT1200s). Slices were recovered for 30–60 min in aCSF solution saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 containing (in mM) 127.0 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 1.2 MgCl2, 2.4 CaCl2, 24.0 NaHCO3 and 11.0 glucose, pH 7.4 (310–315 mOsm). Cells were visualized using infrared‐differential contrast (IR‐DIC) microscopy, and MSNs were identified by their morphology and typical hyperpolarized resting potential (−70 to −80 mV). MSNs were voltage‐clamped at −70 mV using borosilicate pipettes (3–6 MΩ; Sutter Instruments) prepared using a micropipette puller (NARISHIGE, PG10) and filled with internal solution, the composition of which was dependent on type of experiments.

Excitatory miniature postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) of NAc MSNs were recorded in the normal ACSF with the presence of picrotoxin (100 μM, Sigma Aldrich) and 1 μM tetrodotoxin (TTX, Fisher Scientific) to block GABAARs and prevent action potentials. The pipette solution contained (in mM): 140 K‐gluconate, 5 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 0.2 EGTA, 11 HEPES, 2 NaATP, 0.2 Na2GTP and 0.1 CaCl2, pH 7.2 (290–295 mOsm). Data were recorded in a series of 10 sweeps as 10 s each. At the beginning of each sweep, a depolarizing step (4 mV for 30 ms) was generated to monitor series (10–40 MΩ) and input resistance (>400 MΩ). Synaptic events were detected via custom parameters in MiniAnalysis software (Synaptosoft, Decatur, GA) and subsequently confirmed by the observer. For each event, amplitude and frequency was measured and used to determine mean. Data were analyzed until >700 events were collected. Outliers were assessed by ROUT test in GraphPad Prism software and excluded from analysis.

Evoked postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) of MSNs in the NAc shell and core were elicited bilaterally by field stimulation of excitatory afferents in area of distance about 300–400 mm from recording cell at a frequency of 0.05 Hz (0.05 c−1). The low‐intensity pulses of stimulated current (25–100 μA, 50–100 μs duration) were applied through a fine‐tipped (~2–3 μm) bipolar stimulating electrode made from borosilicate theta glass capillary tubing (Warner Instruments). Recording pipettes (3–6 MΩ) were made on a NARISHIGE puller (NARISHIGE, PG10) from borosilicate tubing (Sutter Instruments) and filled by solutions containing (in mM): 140 Cs‐methanesulfonate, 5 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 0.2 EGTA, 11 HEPES, 2 NaATP, 0.2 Na2GTP and 0.1 CaCl2, pH 7.2–7.4 (290–295 mOsm). Recordings were made in the presence of picrotoxin (100 μM, Sigma Aldrich) to block GABAARs. AMPA and NMDA receptor‐mediated excitatory postsynaptic currents (AMPA‐ and NMDA‐responses) were recorded at −70 and +50 mV, respectively. The amplitude of AMPA responses was calculated at the maximum current value, and the amplitude of the NMDA response was calculated from the value at 50 ms post‐stimulation.

Statistics

RNAscope validation of Cre‐dependent knockdown via viral delivery of shArc was analyzed by Brown‐Forsythe and Welch One‐Way ANOVA with Dunnett's T3 multiple comparisons test, where individual variance was computed for each comparison. Locomotor sensitization was analyzed by two‐way repeated measures (RM) analyses of variance (ANOVAs) for each experiment. Within‐subjects variable was day and between‐subjects variables were genotype/virus alone or genotype × treatment. Locomotor challenge time points were analyzed by multi‐variate (MV) ANOVAs. CPP scores were analyzed by two‐way ANOVA, or, if only two groups were compared, were assessed for a normal distribution with a D'Agostino & Pearson test. If normally distributed, the data were compared using a two‐sided unpaired t‐test. If normality was not indicated, the data were compared with a Mann–Whitney test. Interactions were followed by additional ANOVAS (i.e., one‐ and/or two‐way) and/or Tukey's or Sidak's post‐hoc analyses, as appropriate, to determine simple main effects. CPP planned comparisons were analyzed by a two‐sided t‐test. ROUT outlier tests (Q = 1%) were conducted where appropriate and outliers removed from analysis. RESULTS

Global Arc KO mice display hypersensitized locomotor responses to repeated cocaine administration

To examine a possible role for Arc in cocaine‐induced behavioral responses and plasticity, we first habituated all mice to the locomotor assay with four daily saline injections. Following equivalent habituation, we injected global Arc KO mice or littermate controls daily with saline or cocaine (15 mg/kg, i.p.) and examined locomotor responses (Figure 1A). While Arc KO mice initially showed hypoactivity in the first two saline habituation sessions (Figure 1B; two‐way RM ANOVA, genotype × session interaction F (14,308) = 5.680, p < 0.0001, Tukey's post‐hoc, saline day 1 p = 0.0181, saline day 2 p = 0.006), they habituated normally by the third saline session. After habituating injections of saline, mice were split into repeated saline (SAL) or cocaine (COC) injection groups. Repeated cocaine injections produced hypersensitized locomotor responses in Arc KO mice (Tukey's post‐hoc, cocaine day 3 p = 0.02, cocaine day 4 p = 0.0098, cocaine day 5 p = 0.0009, cocaine day 6 p = 0.001, cocaine day 7 p = 0.0133). The enhanced locomotor sensitization in the Arc KO mice persisted after 1 week of drug abstinence and was observed at multiple “challenge” doses of cocaine, but not saline (Figure 1B; Tukey's post‐hoc, challenge 15 mg/kg p = 0.0051, challenge 7.5 mg/kg p = 0.0070). Levels of locomotor sensitization can be influenced by the threshold for induction of competing stereotypy behavior 34 ; however, video analysis of Arc KO and WT mice before and after acute and repeated cocaine exposure during locomotor sensitization showed no group differences in stereotypical behavior at any time point (Figure 1C; Supplemental data Figure S1A).

Arc KO mice display sensitized cocaine conditioned place preference following prior cocaine experience

Our previous findings revealed no significant differences in cocaine CPP in drug‐naïve Arc KO mice at the 5 mg/kg (i.p.) dose. 22 Since Arc limits cocaine locomotor sensitization (Figure 1B), we tested whether Arc might also limit prior drug experience‐dependent changes in cocaine reward‐context learning and memory in the CPP test. WT and Arc KO mice received daily injections of either saline or cocaine in the locomotor sensitization regimen (Figure 2B) and after a 7‐day abstinence period they were tested for cocaine CPP using a near‐threshold dose (Figure 2A, 5 mg/kg; i.p.). While the sensitizing regimen of cocaine had no effects on cocaine CPP in WT male mice (Figure 2C), prior cocaine administration in the male Arc KO mice produced a significant increase in cocaine CPP (Figure 2C, right, 2‐way ANOVA genotype × pretreatment interaction F (1,36) = 3.436, p = 0.072; planned comparison Mann–Whitney test p = 0.0216 WT cocaine vs. Arc KO cocaine), suggesting that Arc antagonizes cocaine “reward sensitization” as measured in the CPP test. Together, these data suggest that Arc functions in the developing and/or mature mouse to limit sensitized behavior responses produced by repeated cocaine exposure.

Arc KO mice show drug experience‐dependent enhancement of cocaine reward. (A) Experimental timeline for cocaine sensitization and subsequent conditioned place preference. Mice received 4 days of habituating saline injections before a week of daily cocaine injections (15 mg/kg). After 7 days of home‐cage abstinence, mice were re‐exposed to the locomotor chamber in the drug‐free state (black arrow) and then underwent cocaine CPP (C = cocaine, 5 mg/kg; S = saline). (B) Arc KO mice show enhanced locomotor responses to cocaine across the sensitizing regimen, with no differences in locomotor activity in repeated‐saline exposed mice (n = 17–19/condition). (C) Cocaine‐sensitized Arc KO mice show increased cocaine conditioned place preference to low dose cocaine (5 mg/kg; i.p.) compared with saline‐treated Arc KO mice. CPP score is post‐pre (n = 9–11/condition). Data shown are mean ± SEM. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Arc in adult male, but not female, NAc neurons is required for cocaine CPP and locomotor responses

To determine if Arc functions within the NAc to limit these non‐contingent cocaine behaviors, we utilized a previously validated AAV2‐shArc virus to reduce endogenous Arc expression bilaterally in the adult NAc of WT mice (shArcNAc 18 ) and we examined cocaine CPP and locomotor sensitization behavior (Figure 3A). In contrast to the saline pre‐treated or drug‐naïve global Arc KO mice (Figure 2C and Penrod et al. 22 ), male shArcNAc mice displayed significantly reduced cocaine CPP compared with their shControlNAc littermates (Figure 3B; 5 mg/kg, i.p.; unpaired t‐test p = 0.0299). After a week‐long period of home‐cage abstinence, we then performed an abbreviated, two‐exposure procedure for locomotor sensitization. Similar to cocaine CPP, the shArcNAc male mice showed a significant reduction in the locomotor‐activating effects of cocaine (Figure 3C, two‐way RM ANOVA session × virus interaction F (4,84) = 2.767, p < 0.0325; Sidak's post‐hoc, cocaine day 1 p = 0.0134, cocaine day 2 p = 0.0112), which was in stark contrast to the potentiated locomotor sensitization observed in the global Arc KO mice (Figures 1B and 2B). Interestingly, these effects were only observed in male mice, as females showed no significant effects of shArcNAc on either CPP or locomotor sensitization (Figure S1B,C).

Reduction of Arc in NAc neurons does not alter basal glutamatergic synaptic transmission

Since Arc is reported to mediate glutamatergic synaptic transmission in hippocampal pyramidal neurons, we next tested whether viral‐mediated knockdown of Arc in adult male NAc neurons alters miniature or electrically evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs). However, using ex vivo acute slices preparations from male mice, we found that shArc had no significant effects on mEPSC amplitude or frequency (Figure 3D). Similarly, viral‐mediated Arc knockdown had no detectable effects on evoked AMPAR‐ or NMDAR‐mediated EPSCs (Figure 3E), or the AMPA/NMDA ratio (Figure S1D), suggesting that the reduction in cocaine CPP and acute cocaine locomotor response in shArcNAc mice (Figure 3B,C) is not caused by changes in basal NAc glutamatergic synaptic transmission.

Arc functions in a cell type‐specific manner in NAc MSNs to regulate cocaine CPP and locomotor sensitization

To test whether the cocaine behavior phenotypes observed in the shArcNAc male mice are attributable to Arc's function in dopamine D1 and/or D2 receptor‐expressing MSNs in the NAc, we infused a Cre‐dependent shArc virus (AAV2‐SICO‐shArc) bilaterally in D1‐Cre (shArcD1) or D2‐Cre (shArcD2) male transgenic mice and validated cell type‐specific knockdown of Arc by RNAScope‐based in situ hybridization (Figure S1E,F). We then examined effects of Arc knockdown in D1‐ or D2‐expressing cells in cocaine CPP and locomotor sensitization (Figure 4A). While shArcD1 mice exhibited no differences in CPP compared with control mice (Figure 4B), they showed reduced cocaine locomotor sensitization (Figure 4C, two‐way RM ANOVA virus × session interaction, F (4,56) = 3.388, p = 0.0150; Tukey's post‐hoc, cocaine day 2 p = 0.0066), suggesting that Arc functions in D1‐MSNs to support the processes underlying cocaine locomotor sensitization. In contrast, knockdown of NAc Arc in D2‐MSNs (shArcD2) significantly decreased cocaine CPP (Figure 4D, Mann–Whitney p = 0.0413), while having no effects on cocaine locomotor responses or sensitization (Figure 4E). Together, these data reveal that Arc has NAc cell type‐specific contributions to cocaine CPP and locomotor responses.

(C) shArcD1 mice show reduced cocaine locomotor activity compared with shCntrlD1 mice (n = 8/condition). DISCUSSION

Arc expression is induced in multiple brain regions following exposure to drugs of abuse, such as cocaine, and there is growing evidence for its involvement in regulating various cocaine‐related behaviors. Despite this, there remain major questions regarding when, where and how Arc mediates its effects on complex drug behaviors. We report here that while global, developmental loss of Arc in male mice leads to enhanced locomotor sensitization and drug experience‐dependent sensitization of cocaine CPP, local reduction of Arc in the adult NAc of male, but not female, C57Bl/6J mice suppressed both cocaine CPP and locomotor responses to cocaine. Despite its reported role in regulating excitatory synaptic transmission in pyramidal neurons, viral‐mediated reduction of Arc in NAc neurons did not alter basal excitatory synaptic transmission in NAc MSNs, NAc GluA1 protein levels, or cocaine‐induced phosphorylation of NAc GluA1. Notably, our results also suggest a sex‐dependent role for Arc in the NAc, as behavioral differences were observed in male, but not female, mice, which is similar to male‐specific influences of NAc Arc that we previously observed for anxiety‐like behavior. 18 Finally, we showed that Arc has a cell type‐specific function in NAc D1‐positive neurons to influence cocaine locomotor responses and in NAc D2‐positive neurons to support cocaine CPP.

Enhanced response to non‐contingent psychostimulant exposure is a previously described phenotype in the Arc KO mouse. 19 , 20 , 21 Drug‐naïve Arc KO mice are reported to have enhanced sensitivity to cocaine CPP at subthreshold cocaine doses (2.5 mg/kg; Salery et al. 20 ), but this effect is not observed at cocaine doses that produce cocaine CPP in WT mice (e.g., 5 mg/kg; Penrod et al. 22 ), suggesting a subtle increase in cocaine reward sensitivity in drug‐naïve Arc KO mice. Our new findings here reveal that repeated, non‐contingent cocaine exposure (15 mg/kg; i.p.) in WT mice does not alter cocaine CPP after 1‐week in the home cage, despite the development of strong locomotor sensitization, but Arc KO mice develop a sensitized cocaine CPP, suggesting that Arc functions to suppress sensitization of cocaine reward‐context associations. While the NAc is a logical place for Arc to limit sensitized cocaine behaviors, our findings here suggest that Arc functions elsewhere in the brain and/or during NAc development to limit cocaine sensitization. While NAc‐specific Arc knockdown in adult mice does not recapitulate the Arc KO cocaine phenotypes, we cannot rule out the possibility that reducing NAc Arc levels by shRNA produces a different NAc cellular effect than complete loss of Arc (global Arc KO mice), but that explanation of the behavioral findings seems less likely. Future studies will be important to identify how and why Arc KO mice show hypersensitized cocaine responses, but since Arc is strongly expressed in numerous upstream NAc projecting brain regions, such as prefrontal cortex, ventral hippocampus and amygdala, a role for Arc to limit cocaine sensitization in one or more of these NAc‐projecting populations is possible. It is worth noting the strain differences present between our Arc KO (C57Bl/6N) and AAV2‐shArc models (C57Bl/6J). C57Bl/6N mice exhibit decreased cocaine locomotor sensitization compared with C57Bl/6J mice. 36 However, while these strain differences in our experiments limit our ability to compare across the different Arc manipulations, we would not expect these strain differences to account for the complete reversal of the phenotypes that we observed in the global Arc KO and NAc‐infused AAV2‐shArc studies. An additional limitation of our study is the difference in light–dark cycle behavioral testing between Arc KO and AAV2‐shArc models. Due to institutional differences in light–dark schedule over the course of this study, the Arc KO mice were tested during the light cycle while the AAV2‐shArc mice were tested during the dark cycle. Again, although this inhibits us from directly comparing the global Arc KO versus NAc‐targeted AAV2‐shArc, we do not expect the light–dark cycle differences to fully account for the opposite effects in cocaine behaviors that we tested. It is also worth noting that while Arc is induced in both NAc core and shell following intravenous cocaine self‐administration in rats, viral‐mediated Arc reduction in the NAc shell, but not core, augments reinstatement of cocaine seeking. 38 This highlights how differences in the studied species (rat vs. mouse), behavioral assay (CPP/Locomotor sensitivity vs. self‐administration), and brain subregion (NAc core vs. shell) may result in distinct, and often contrasting, interpretations of Arc function in drug‐related behaviors.

Our findings here revealed that reduction of Arc in NAc neurons in male mice did not alter mEPSCs nor AMPAR or NMDAR currents. Future studies examining different timepoints after acute or chronic cocaine exposure will be important to examine if Arc regulates cocaine‐induced short‐ or long‐term plasticity in NAc neurons. Given our behavioral findings and the well‐known role for Arc in the regulation of LTP, LTD and AMPA receptor trafficking (reviewed in References 39, 40), it remains possible that Arc's role is related to modulation of ionotropic glutamate receptors, but it is also possible that Arc supports CPP and locomotor responses through distinct cellular mechanisms such as gene expression 6 or heterosynaptic plasticity. 41

As mentioned, there is a potential developmental role for Arc that has not been studied in the context of cocaine behavior. Arc expression is detected in multiple regions of the brain at P14 (Allen Brain Atlas) and might be transient during development since many of these brain regions show low or no detectible Arc expression at later developmental timepoints (e.g., P28) or adulthood (Allen Brain Atlas). Arc can mediate activity‐dependent synapse elimination in the cerebellum during development, 42 implicating Arc's function in synaptic pruning and refinement events that may perturb wide‐spread circuitry. Indeed, aberrant expression of Arc has been associated with a number of neurodevelopmental disorders, many of which are also characterized by circuit disorganization or dysfunction. 15 , 43 Given this, it seems plausible that genetic loss of Arc expression during development could alter the maturation and/or function of brain circuits that regulate cocaine‐induced behavioral plasticity.

It is interesting that reducing Arc levels in the adult NAc affected cocaine behavior only in male mice. In hippocampal neurons, Arc is regulated by sex hormones in a sex‐dependent manner, where estradiol increases Arc expression in female‐derived neurons, and dihydrotestosterone increases Arc expression in male‐derived neurons. 44 Moreover, Arc is highly expressed in mouse testis following puberty, further implicating male sex hormones in the regulation of Arc expression. 45 We previously observed that reduction of NAc Arc reduced anxiety‐like behavior in the elevated plus maze (EPM) in male, but not female, C57Bl/6J mice. 18 It is unclear whether NAc Arc's influence on male‐specific threat vigilance in the elevated plus maze might influence, or interact with, male‐specific deficits in cocaine CPP and cocaine‐induced locomotor responses, but our findings continue to reveal strong sex differences in NAc Arc function as it relates to mood and drug behaviors.

D1‐MSNs and D2‐MSNs are thought to have opposing roles in drug‐related behavior, with activation of D1‐MSNs promoting it and D2‐MSNs typically inhibiting it, 26 , 27 , 28 however see Zachry et al. 46 Interestingly, we find that while NAc Arc functions in D1‐MSNs to support cocaine locomotor sensitization, but not CPP, and it also functions in D2‐positive NAc neurons (presumably MSNs) to support cocaine CPP, but not locomotor sensitization. Although this phenotypic dissociation was unexpected, it is in line with previous findings indicating that potentiation of excitatory input onto D1‐MSNs is necessary for cocaine locomotor sensitization, 47 , 48 that depotentiation of D2‐MSNs is required for CPP 49 and that D1‐ and D2‐MSNs are both capable of promoting associative learning. 46 , 50 Arc has established roles in both LTD and the stabilization of LTP, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 14 , 15 , 16 and it is possible that Arc preferentially promotes LTP stabilization in D1‐MSNs, but it promotes LTD in D2‐MSNs. It is also interesting to note that while constitutive shArc in NAc reduced both acute and sensitized locomotor responses in males, D1‐MSN specific knockdown only affected the sensitized response. This discrepancy suggests that Arc might support acute locomotor responses in neurons that do not express either D1 or D2 dopamine receptors, including local interneurons. In this study, we validated the Cre‐dependent reduction of Arc mRNA with NAc‐infused AAV2‐shArc in the Drd2‐Cre mice. While the shRNA construct should operate similarly in Drd1‐Cre mice as we published for a similar Cre‐dependent shNpas4 vector, 49 we did not directly confirm D1‐MSN‐specific knockdown of Arc in this study. In general, our findings emphasize the importance of considering Arc's function in a cell type‐specific manner and warrant future electrophysiological studies aimed at disentangling the differences in Arc's contribution to potential sex, cell‐type and input‐specific forms of plasticity that mediate cocaine‐related behaviors.

Overall, our findings indicate that Arc functions in the adult NAc in a cell‐type specific manner to regulate multiple non‐contingent cocaine‐related behaviors in male, but not female, mice. We observe a significant decrease in cocaine CPP and acute and sensitized locomotor responses following constitutive knockdown of Arc in the NAc, a sharp contrast to the increases we and others have reported in the global Arc KO mouse. Further, we find distinct cell type‐specific contributions to these behaviors, with D1‐MSN‐specific reduction of Arc reducing locomotor sensitization and D2‐MSN‐specific reduction of Arc reducing CPP. Importantly, this work also reveals a novel sex‐related difference in Arc's function as it relates to drug‐related behavior, which suggests that distinct molecular mechanisms contribute to the development or expression of these drug‐related behaviors in males and females.
