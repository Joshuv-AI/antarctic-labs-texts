A joint model for a longitudinal outcome and a progressive multistate model under a mixed observation scheme
Authors: Leif Erik Lovblom, Laurent Briollais, Bruce A Perkins, George Tomlinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractJoint models for longitudinal and event-time data can be used to understand the co-development of multiple correlated non-fatal outcomes, such as those encountered when studying diabetic microvascular complications. However, such models were not developed for event-times characterized by a multistate process where the entry time into certain states is interval-censored, which is an important feature of interval cohort study designs. Our aim was to develop a joint model for this setting. Specifically, we formulated a shared random effects joint model with linear mixed-effects and proportional intensities progressive three-state Markov submodels, with interval-censoring of entry into the transient state and exact observation of entry into the absorbing state. Maximum likelihood estimation was used, with exploration of the functional forms and association structures. Bias and confidence interval coverage of the model parameters were assessed using simulation, and performance was compared to existing approaches that assume exact observation of all entry times, which were found to sometimes overestimate the magnitude of association between the longitudinal and multistate outcomes. In an application of the model, the trajectory of a routinely measured diabetic complication (retinopathy) was associated with progression through states of a more difficult to measure complication (neuropathy), suggesting potential efficiencies in screening and monitoring.Keywords: Longitudinal data, multistate model, joint model, interval-censoring, intermittent observation, type 1 diabetes, correlated data, biostatistics

Introduction

In diabetes, there is a gap in our understanding of how multiple correlated non-fatal microvascular complications co-develop over time and this gap has important implications for clinical care. For example, standard diabetes practice calls for an annual dilated retinal examination to determine presence and stage of diabetic retinopathy (eye disease), annual blood and urine tests for kidney function and presence of albuminuria (kidney disease), and a detailed foot examination by a diabetes care professional for presence of peripheral sensation and foot abnormalities (nerve complications). However, screening for each of these three complications occurs independently of the others and it would be valuable if results from screening for one complication could rule in or rule out the immediate need to screen for the others. From a methodological perspective, it is not obvious how to analyze this complex multivariate process, and recently the relative advantages of multistate models and joint models for this setting were discussed. 1 It was found that while both types of models could address a wide range of specific questions regarding the natural history of chronic complications, neither was sufficient on its own and it was concluded that it would be useful to combine elements of multistate and joint models into a single framework.

A feasible way to create such a framework would be to define a joint multistate model, specifically via the introduction of random effects shared by different submodels for the different complications, or outcomes. However, existing approaches are currently under-developed. In particular, they assume that the multistate transition times are exactly observed,2–4 thus making them unsuitable for interval cohort studies, which are one of the most popular study designs for questions surrounding the natural history of complications. In interval cohort studies, participants are studied at regular intervals: they are intermittently observed and subject to a mixed observation scheme where the times of certain events are interval-censored and the times of other events are exactly observed.3,5 Therefore, to allow for a greater range of applications and to provide greater insight into the natural history of a full complications process, the specific aim of this paper is to propose a shared random effects joint model for a longitudinal outcome and a multistate process under a mixed observation scheme. For the longitudinal outcome, a linear mixed-effects submodel is used and for the multistate process, a progressive three-state Markov submodel is used. A second aim is to demonstrate how the proposed model could introduce efficiencies into microvascular complications screening in type 1 diabetes. We use data from a long-term study of microvascular complications of diabetes, the Diabetes Control and Complications Trials (DCCT) and Epidemiology of Diabetes Interventions and Complications (EDIC) study for this purpose.6–8

Before introducing the structure of this paper, we proceed to review the few existing methods that could be used to simultaneously model a multistate submodel under a mixed observation scheme and a longitudinal submodel. First, some existing general approaches could be used. For example, when the longitudinal outcome can be treated as an exogenous time-dependent covariate, a joint model might not be necessary and a traditional survival or multistate model valid for interval censoring can be used.3,9,10 Among these multistate models, some can allow for the explicit co-modeling of a longitudinal outcome via expansion of the original multistate process into a larger one.3,11–14 In this larger state space, the states now represent combinations of the original states and categories of the longitudinal outcome. However, the state space of these expanded models can increase rapidly, and the resulting multistate model can become overly complex when different features of the longitudinal outcome are of interest or when the longitudinal outcome must be divided into many categories. 1 On the other hand, when the longitudinal outcome is endogenous to the survival process, joint models for longitudinal and event-time data can be used. 15 General joint model extensions have been proposed to accommodate either single interval-censored event times16–19 or multiple exactly-observed event times,2–4 but none of these extensions were designed for multistate processes under mixed observation schemes. Second, four specialized models have been developed and could be used (bottom half of Table 1): Dantan et al. developed a joint progressive illness-death model under a mixed observation scheme where entry into the illness state was interval-censored. 20 The model accommodated interval-censoring by including a latent unobserved state and by assuming that the risk of death was independent of the current state. Rouanet et al. defined a similar joint progressive illness-death model under a mixed observation scheme; their approach differed from Dantan et al.’s in that they used latent classes rather than shared random effects to account for the underlying relationship between the multistate and longitudinal submodels. 21 Van den Hout et al. proposed a joint progressive illness-death model under a mixed observation scheme where the longitudinal outcome data were modeled via a latent growth curve. In this model the parameterization of the association between the multistate submodel and the longitudinal submodel was restricted in that it included only the random effects of the latent growth curve; furthermore, a Bayesian estimation procedure was used instead of a frequentist one, which requires expertise to implement in general practice and can be computationally intensive. 22 Separate from these illness-death models, we note that a joint model with a two-state reversible multistate outcome with interval-censoring was proposed; however, unlike for standard joint models, this model made the likelihood tractable via a two-stage estimation procedure. 23 To overcome the limitations of both the general and specialized approaches, we therefore seek a solution that i) takes a frequentist and computationally efficient form and ii) directly maximizes the joint likelihood function and avoids the use of latent states.

This paper is structured as follows: in Section 2, we define our joint multistate model for a mixed observation scheme. In Section 3 we give the likelihood function, the modeling assumptions, and the estimation procedure. Section 4 summarizes a simulation study that assessed the proposed model and compared it to two existing approaches. In Section 5, we illustrate the interest of the model through an application in the DCCT/EDIC study. The paper concludes with a discussion in Section 6. Additional details and computer code for the methods, simulation, and application are contained in the supplemental material.

Definition of the joint multistate model

Assume that we have a sample of N individuals who are followed over the course of an interval cohort study and that we aim to determine how two non-fatal disease complications co-develop over time; one complication is represented by a three-state two-transition progressive multistate process and the other is represented by a longitudinal outcome (Figure 1). The representation as this type of multistate process is appropriate for a diabetic complication that is progressive and from which a patient is unlikely to fully-recover once he or she enters its early subclinical stages, like neuropathy. The representation as a longitudinal outcome is appropriate for a diabetic complication that may fluctuate over time and can be consistently measured, like retinopathy. Following standard assumptions, 15 and following a similar form to the models described by Ferrer et al., 2 Cook & Lawless (Chapter 8), 3 and Rizopoulos et al., 4 we proceed to define a joint model that stipulates that the transition intensity functions and the linear mixed-effects model are linked via a set of unobserved shared random effects. Permitted parametric and functional forms

As is the case for existing joint models for one longitudinal outcome and one survival or event-time outcome,15,24 our proposed model can accommodate any general parametric or functional form for the linear mixed-effects submodel, the baseline transition intensity functions, and the association functions. The form of the transition-specific association function fs1s2(⋅) depends on the hypothesized relationship between the longitudinal and multistate processes. For example, α12⊤f12(Mi(t);bi)=α12mi(t) states that the risk of a 1→2 transition at time t depends on the true value of the longitudinal outcome at time t , and exp(α12) measures the change in the intensity for a 1-unit higher current value of the true longitudinal outcome. This is known as the “current value” association. Other examples include the “current slope” association αs1s2mi′(t) and the “current value and current slope” association αs1s21mi(t)+αs1s22mi′(t) .

Likelihood when the multistate process is under a mixed observation scheme

Though λi12(⋅) and λi23(⋅) represent the true underlying transition intensity functions in continuous time, we need to accommodate the fact that the multistate process is observed intermittently. For the multistate process, participant i is observed at times ai1<ai2<⋯<aili . The total number of times li can vary per participant, and the set of assessment times {air,r=1,…,li} can be fixed or random. Denote a realization of the process at visit time air as eir . We note that even though a participant must pass through state 2 before entering state 3, due to interval censoring it is possible to observe state 1 at the time ai,li−1 and state 3 at the time aili . Furthermore, it is possible that a participant can be right-censored at time aili , and was only known to be in state 1 or state 2 at the time ai,li−1 ; in this case, we will define eili=0 . The observed multistate data then consists of the set Ei={air,eir;r=1,…,li,eir∈{0,1,2,3}} . For the longitudinal process, we observe the measurement values yi(t) intermittently at times ti1<ti2<⋯<tini . The two different sets of observation times do not have to coincide, but it is necessary that tini≤aili . The observed covariate data for the two submodels consist of the sets Xi={xi(tij),j=1,…,ni} , Zi={zi(tij),j=1,…,ni} , and Wi={wi12,wi23} .

In addition to the conditional independence assumption, we make the following assumptions: first, we assume that the visit process is conditionally-independent of the multistate and longitudinal processes. Here we mean that, conditional on the observed multistate, longitudinal, and covariate history up to a visit time aj−1 , the values of these features occurring after aj−1 do not affect the time aj of the next visit; 3 this assumption implies that the visiting process generating {air} and {tij} does not have to be modeled. Third, we assume that any right censoring, if present, is random, is conditionally independent of the multistate and longitudinal processes, and is non-informative. Lastly, we make a conditional Markov assumption for the multistate process, the definition of which will be given below.

Simulation studies

Overview

We used the ADEMP (aims, data-generating processes, estimands, methods, and performance measures) format to guide our simulation studies.25,26 Aims: first, we aimed to show the proof-of-concept that the estimates derived from the proposed model had suitable statistical properties like low bias, acceptable variance, and high coverage probability. Second, we aimed to determine if the proposed model offered any advantages over existing methods that, while not specifically designed for our setting, are well-established and might still be used in this context. Data-generating processes: five were used and are described in the next subsection. Estimands: the main estimands of interest were the joint model current-value association parameters α12 and α23 , which represented the true population-level values of the association between the longitudinal outcome and the transition intensities for 1→2 and 2→3 , respectively. We also evaluated all other parameters of the joint model. Methods: the first comparator method was based on the joint multistate model for multistate processes under exact observation, while the second comparator method was a multistate model for interval-censored panel data and time-dependent covariates; additional details are given in the next subsection. Performance measures: we calculated bias, empirical standard error, model-based standard error, mean squared error, and coverage. For the second aim above, these measures were used as the evaluation criteria to compare our proposed method to the competing models. The definitions and estimates of these measures, as well as other details of the simulation design, are given in Supplemental Section S2.1; Monte Carlo standard errors were also calculated. Using a sample size calculation for bias, we set the number of repeated simulations nsim=200 .

Data-generating process and methods

We used parametric data generating processes to simulate joint multistate and longitudinal data. Specifically, to generate plausible data, we used previously described processes to generate joint longitudinal data and exact multistate event times.2,4,27 We then extended this process to allow for mixed observation schemes and interval-censoring by applying techniques described by Cook & Lawless (Appendix B) 3 and van den Hout (Chapter 3). 5 These techniques impose a study visit schedule over the exactly known simulated state transition times. The data generating processes were informed by preliminary results from the 28-year data of the DCCT/EDIC study.

For a given data generating process (DGP), we simulated data in six broad steps (see the flowchart in Supplemental Figure 1). First, we generated longitudinal data for a simulated study participant according to a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept and slope, independent error terms, and a baseline covariate. The times of assessment for the longitudinal outcome began at year 0, which represented the start a study observation period common to all simulated participants. Subsequent times occurred approximately annually, with successive times drawn from a uniform distribution to introduce noise. Second, conditional on this longitudinal data, we defined the two transition intensity functions based on baseline intensity parameters, covariates, and the current value of the longitudinal data. where the independent error terms εi(t) were drawn from a N(0,σ2) distribution; the random effects were drawn from a bivariate normal distribution with mean zero and variance-covariance matrix elements d11 , d21 , and d22 ; xi1 was a continuous covariate drawn from a N(9.1,1.6) distribution where 1.6 was the standard deviation; and xi2 was a dichotomous covariate taking the value of 1 with probability of 0.5. Though we considered them to be nuisance parameters, for the baseline transition intensity functions we chose a piecewise exponential form with two knots; this was to allow their values to vary over the long-term. Third, we simulated true event times Ti12* and Ti23* - representing the known transition times for entry into state 2 and state 3, respectively - using Brent’s univariate root-finding algorithm.2,4,27 Fourth, we generated an independent assessment schedule for the multistate process by assuming that each simulated participant had a multistate assessment at time 0 and where the times in-between subsequent assessments were drawn from an exponential distribution. The random assessment times stopped at a pre-determined administrative censoring time CA , and the vector of multistate assessment times thus had the form ai=(ai1=0,ai2,…,aili)⊤ , where the number of assessments per participant could vary. In some data generating processes, random censoring times Ci were drawn from a uniform distribution; in the end, the final observation time aili was either the time of entry into state 3, CA , or the random censoring time Ci . Fifth, the multistate assessment times, the true event times, the administrative censoring time, and the random censoring times were all overlaid to create a vector of multistate observations and assessment times. Sixth, and finally, longitudinal observations occurring after censoring or entry into state 3 were discarded.

For the first data generating process (DGP), we deliberately chose a simplified setting with administrative censoring at year 25, without random censoring, with frequent assessment of the multistate process (approximately annual), and where all simulated participants began their multistate process in state 1. The linear mixed-effects model parameters were chosen to specify gradually increasing longitudinal trajectories over time with relatively high variability in random intercepts. Transition intensity function parameters were chosen to reflect a prevalence about 60% of a simulated population transitioning from 1→2 , and about 12% transitioning into state 3. Knots for the piecewise constant intensities were placed at years 4.8 and 17.4. The sample size was 1440 simulated participants. This scenario was meant to serve as the reference point for subsequent ones.

The four additional data generating processes were defined as follows (see Supplemental Figure S2 and Supplemental Table S3 for overviews of how the processes differed). DGP 2 had less-frequent multistate assessments (approximately biennial or every 2 years instead of every year). DGP 3 specified higher event rates. DGP 4 specified random censoring between years 5 and 25 so that about 12% of the simulated population would be censored. Finally, DGP 5 allowed for left-truncation. Additional details are given in Supplemental Section S2.2. To demonstrate how the five data-generating processes were generated, and to show the parameter values, we give the overall simulation workflow in Supplemental Algorithm 1.

Regarding the simulation methods, our proposed joint model estimated all parameters of the simulation model in Equation 10; we refer to this method as the JMStateMixed model. The first comparator method was based on the joint multistate model for multistate processes under exact observation.2–4 We refer to this method as the JMStateExact model; it was implemented using the JMStateModel function in R, which combines elements of the mstate and JM packages and is freely available. 2 JMStateExact does not accommodate interval-censoring, and is therefore misspecified for our simulations. The second comparator method was a multistate model for interval-censored panel data and exogenous time-dependent covariates. We refer to this method as the MSM model and it was estimated using the msm package in R. 10 Because the longitudinal process could yield data at times different from the assessment times for the multistate process, the MSM model assumed exogeneity and that the last observation was carried forward; it did not account for random variation nor did it estimate any of the longitudinal submodel parameters. The models estimated by JMStateExact and MSM differ slightly from Equation 10. Despite this, estimates of α12 and α23 for JMStateMixed, JMStateExact, and MSM have similar interpretations and are comparable. Additional details are given in Supplemental Section S2.3.

Results

The proposed joint model JMStateMixed had good or excellent performance for all five data generating processes. For α12 , there was no or minimal bias, the empirical SE and model-based SE agreed, the mean squared error was low, and the point-estimates of coverage ranged from 0.935 to 0.965. These values are presented graphically in Figure 2 in the top row of each panel. For α23 , the point-estimates of bias ranged from − 0.004 to − 0.015 (or − 7% to − 17%), the empirical SE and model SE agreed, the mean squared error was low (0.001 or lower), and the point-estimates of coverage ranged from 0.89 to 0.955. The highest absolute value of the bias and the lowest coverage were apparent for DGP 3, which had higher 2→3 event rates by design. These values are presented graphically in Figure 3.

We proceed to discuss the results for the other model parameters for JMStateMixed: for the remaining 1→2 and 2→3 transition intensity parameters, including those for the baseline functions and for the covariates, JMStateMixed showed excellent performance. For the linear mixed-effects model and the random effects parameters, JMStateMixed also showed excellent performance. We next discuss performance measures of the comparator methods, which are displayed alongside those of JMStateMixed in Figures 2 and 3. For α12 , JMStateExact and MSM showed slight underestimation across all data generating processes, with bias ranging from − 0.003 to − 0.007, or − 2.5% to − 6% (Figure 2). For JMStateExact within DGP 2, this underestimation was slightly greater at − 10%. The empirical SE and model SE generally agreed for both methods. The empirical SE for both methods was smaller than that of JMStateMixed, suggesting better efficiency, though MSE was higher or similar and coverage lower or similar. For α23 , while MSM showed similar if slightly worse performance than JMStateMixed, problems were apparent for JMStateExact (the method that ignored interval-censoring). In particular, JMStateExact showed meaningful and consistent overestimation (Figure 3). For the 1→2 and 2→3 baseline transition intensity parameters, performance of MSM for ξ121 and ξ231 was similar to that of JMStateMixed, while MSM slightly overestimated ξ122 , ξ123 , ξ232 , and ξ233 , and showed poor coverage. JMStateExact did not estimated these piecewise exponential function parameters, but for comparison we include estimates of the B-spline parameters in Supplemental Tables S4 through S8. For the covariate parameters for both types of transitions, performance was similar across all methods. For the linear mixed-effects model and random effects parameters, performance of JMStateExact was comparable to that of JMStateMixed. In particular, the mismatched estimates for β0 , d11 , and d21 for DGP 5 were present. We note that MSM did not estimate any of these linear mixed-effects model or random effects parameters. Full results of all simulations are shown in Supplemental Tables S4 through S8. One convergence issue was noted for MSM in one simulation for DGP 3; this was solved by changing the default reltol value; no others were apparent.

Owing to the misspecification within DGP 5 of the linear mixed-model intercept and two of the random effects variance-covariance parameters, we re-fit the JMStateMixed model with the retrospective likelihood correction discussed in Section 3.5. This correction was possible in our simulated data since we had access to the simulated retrospective data prior to the left-truncation times and since we could assume that the model for mi(t) was valid from time 0 (i.e. no extrapolation was required). The correction successfully recovered the parameters β0 , d11 , and d21 , as illustrated in the top row of the swarm plot in Supplemental Figure S3. For comparison, this plot includes the original JMStateMixed results (bottom row, labeled “original” and showing bias), as well as an attempt at a simple correction (middle row, labeled “subset” and also showing bias). This simple correction involved removing observations with study entry-time greater than 5 years from time 0, and was thus meant to limit the analysis to patients in the earliest stages of disease. Finally, we note that the retrospective approach generally yielded increased efficiency when compared to the naive and subset approaches; this occurred for most parameters, and we display the standard errors for α12 and α23 in Supplemental Figure S4. Full results for the three approaches are shown in Supplemental Table S9.

Summary

In all simulation scenarios, the proposed model showed good to excellent performance. It successfully recovered the association parameters α12 and α23 , which were the main parameters of interest, and it recovered most of the other model parameters. In contrast, the comparator method using a joint modeling approach that did not account for mixed observation schemes with interval-censoring showed a greater magnitude of bias. An exception to the strong performance of our model was encountered for certain longitudinal submodel parameters in the setting of left-truncation and delayed entry, but the problem was fixed by correcting the likelihood to account for the left-truncation.

Application in the DCCT/EDIC study

Overview

The DCCT was a practice-changing transformative clinical trial initiated in the early 1980s that demonstrated efficacy of an intensive diabetes therapy regimen on the reduction of the incidence and progression of microvascular complications in type 1 diabetes, and the EDIC study is an ongoing observational interval cohort study assessing the durability of the initial intensive therapy of the DCCT and other objectives related to advanced clinical outcomes;6,7 additional details are given in Supplemental Section S3.1. The overall aim of our application was to discover features of how the natural history of different microvascular complications co-develop in people with type 1 diabetes. From a clinical perspective, because peripheral nerve damage is difficult for clinicians to measure and to predict, we aimed to make use of the eye complication’s longitudinal data to determine if these more systematic and feasible measures could be used for prediction of nerve damage. For the multistate nerve outcome, state 1 was defined as the absence of peripheral nerve damage, state 2 was defined as having early-stage nerve damage (via clinical tests and exams), and state 3 was defined as having late-stage nerve damage (e.g. serious foot ulcers and amputations). State 2 is interval-censored and state 3 is exactly observed; further details of these definitions are given in Section S3.1. We assume that state 3 is absorbing and that the multistate process terminates upon entry; that is once a person has an ulcer or amputation, they carry that state permanently. The observed state frequencies at certain times of the study are shown in Supplemental Figure S5; note the increase in the prevalence of state 2 (the green bar) at year 5, which coincided with a scheduled NCS exam. For the longitudinal eye outcome, we used results of the 23-point ETDRS scale (developed by the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study Research Group). This scale is determined from examination of the retina (back part) of the eye, and it is a well-established, feasible, and standardized clinical measure. The DCCT investigators defined 3-point progression in the ETDRS scale as their primary outcome for retinopathy; instead, for our modeling purposes, we treat the ETDRS scale as continuous longitudinal data. Higher values of the ETDRS scale represent more severe forms of eye damage. The observed participant-level ETDRS trajectories are shown in Supplemental Figure S6; these trajectories are displayed according to the corresponding observed multistate history.

Joint model specification

Time since randomization was the chosen time-scale and we explored linear mixed-effects models for the ETDRS longitudinal outcome. Randomization arm (intensive vs. conventional therapy) and intervention group (primary prevention vs. secondary intervention group) were included as covariates, represented by xi1 and xi2 , respectively; we included only the randomization group-by-time interaction and not the main effect of randomization. Random intercept and slope models were fitted, but they did not adequately model some of the long-term non-linear trajectories; instead a random-effects B-spline model was used. Regarding the transition intensity function estimates from Model JM1 (shown in Table 2), for the 1→2 transition from no nerve damage to early-stage nerve damage, we observed that a 1-point higher current value of ETDRS, controlling for other covariates including the current slope of ETDRS, was associated with a hazard rate ratio of exp(0.11)=1.12 . We explored the model diagnostic procedures outlined in Supplemental Section S3.2. The diagnostic plots for the joint model JM1 are shown in Supplemental Figure S7, and they suggest generally good model fit. However, there was an apparent underestimation for the marginal survival predictions of the Kaplan-Meier survival probability estimates of S^23(t|b^i;θ^) , and a slight overestimation of the Kaplan-Meier estimate of S^13(t|b^i;θ^) . Assuming that the Kaplan-Meier curves represent unbiased estimates of the true population-level survival curves, this suggests that eye complications dynamics and baseline HbA1c may not fully-explain the risk of late-stage nerve damage.

Lastly, we undertook two sensitivity analyses. The first consisted of comparing results from the proposed model to those of the existing JMStateExact method described in the simulation studies. For JMStateExact - the method that assumed the 1→2 transition times were exactly observed rather than interval-censored - point-estimates for α121 and α122 agreed with Model JM1 (0.12 and 0.52 vs. 0.11 and 0.57, respectively) . However, the point-estimate for α23 was higher (0.12 vs. 0.07, or 71% higher). Estimates for other parameters generally agreed. Additional details are given in Supplemental Section S3.3; there, results derived from the MSM method are also discussed. The second sensitivity analysis consisted of re-running Model JM1 using disease duration as the time-scale rather than time since randomization, and thus required correction for left-truncation. Overall, we observed similar dynamics between the longitudinal and multistate outcomes for the models using disease duration as the time-scale; however, model estimates did differ and the current slope of ETDRS could be more impactful on the risk of a 1→2 transition when studying the duration time-scale. Full details of this second sensitivity analysis are given in Supplemental Section S3.3.

Discussion

We proposed a joint multistate model for an intermittently-observed multistate process and a longitudinal outcome, we assessed its performance in simulations, and we applied it to a real-world study of diabetes complications. The proposed model improved upon existing shared random effects approaches that treat the multistate model as exactly-observed (rather than intermittently observed) and that in simulation showed potential to over-estimate the magnitude of the relationship between longitudinal and multistate outcomes; it is thus a valuable option for studies with interval censoring, such as those using the commonly-encountered interval cohort design. In the application, we showed that the proposed model could help discover how multiple non-fatal disease complications co-develop over time and thus provide much-needed data to clinicians by using knowledge of the longitudinal process of a clinically well-established and feasible measure (eye damage) to help predict the progression of a clinically less-established and less-feasible measure (nerve damage). Applications are not limited to diabetes, and for practitioners interested in using this model in their own research, example computer code used to fit the proposed model and to run aspects of the simulation studies is included in the supplemental material.

Some limitations of the proposed model were apparent. First, though there are no theoretical restrictions on the permitted structure of the multistate process, we studied only one type of multistate submodel (a progressive three-state one). Extensions for progressive submodels with more than 3 states seem feasible, as is the inclusion of external time-dependent covariates, but more research is required for illness-death models and for other general multistate processes. We note that mortality, competing risks, and informative dropout were not thought to be a serious concerns in the DCCT/EDIC study because follow-up was extensive. Second, we did not explore semi-Markov or non-Markov models, and we did not fully-explore how left-truncation and other state-dependent selection schemes could impact this model. We were able to incorporate a likelihood correction making restrictive assumptions, but this might not be possible in many settings. Other approaches to truncation in multistate3,5,29–32 and joint models33,34 have been discussed; we note that approaches involving integration over a conditional random effects distribution would be challenging in our high-dimensional B-spline models. Third, though the SAS NLMIXED procedure allowed for efficient computation of our proposed model, we did not explore other estimation procedures. Candidates include an expectation maximization algorithm combined with Monte Carlo integration previously used for a frailty model, 5 as expectation maximization algorithm combined with pseudo-adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature used for joint multistate models with exact transition times,2,15 and Bayesian techniques using Stan or R-INLA.35,36

Though our results from the DCCT/EDIC study should be considered as preliminary and treated with caution, the model implies that prevention of neuropathy could benefit from the direct knowledge of prior frequent and clinically well-established annual eye examination procedures, rather than solely on clinical examination for nerve injury which is not clinically well-established or adhered to in practice.37,38 For example, if a patient is observed during an upwards trajectory of ETDRS measurements, immediate intensive therapy could be emphasized by clinicians and care-teams to slow down both the eye-damage and nerve-damage processes. Taking this idea further, ultimately it would be useful if such results could be incorporated into a living electronic medical record where complications trajectories could be extracted from regularly collected clinical information, and where the trajectories would automatically inform risk of microvascular complications and other important diabetes complications including macrovascular outcomes (such as cardiovascular disease, stroke, and death) as well as acute complications (such as diabetic ketoacidosis and severe hypoglycemia). Future work will seek to extend the proposed joint multistate model and thus enhance its ability to accomplish this. Three particular extensions stand out: first, it would be useful to include multivariate longitudinal outcomes and other time-to-event outcomes. This would allow for the modeling of more than two complications and would thus make the model more clinically relevant for the determination of risk of complications other than neuropathy in type 1 diabetes. Second, because dynamic predictions from standard joint models have shown potential to help support personalized treatment plans,39–42 it would be useful to develop a dynamic prediction framework for joint multistate models. Finally, though we modeled longitudinal and multistate outcomes simultaneously, more focus was placed on the multistate outcome in our application. A model allowing for the longitudinal outcome’s trajectory to be affected by the occurrence of the interval-censored intermediate event could yield better understanding of the total bivariate process. 20
