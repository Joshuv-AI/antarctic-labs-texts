Exploring Factors Related to Dysphagia Among Community-Dwelling Older Adults: A Scoping Review
Authors: Kristamuliana Kristamuliana, Deborah Siregar, Huan-Fang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dysphagia, or difficulty swallowing, is common but often underdiagnosed among community-dwelling older adults, particularly those without neurological, neuromuscular, or structural disorders. This scoping review aimed to map the existing evidence on factors related to dysphagia in this population. The review followed Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework and the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Embase, Ovid-MEDLINE, and CINAHL were searched up to June 2025, with search strategies developed in consultation with a professional librarian. Eligibility criteria were predefined by the research team based on the review objective and applied independently by two reviewers. Studies were included if they involved community-dwelling older adults aged ≥ 60 years and reported multivariate analyses of factors related to dysphagia. Dysphagia was identified using self-reported instruments, interviews, or swallowing tests. Of 1,043 records identified, 14 studies met the inclusion criteria. Advanced age, comorbidities (including diabetes and cardiovascular diseases), poor oral health (e.g., tooth loss, reduced mastication ability, and oral dryness), sarcopenia and frailty, pain, and poor nutritional status were significantly related to dysphagia. The identified factors were grouped into five categories: demographic factors, health conditions, oral health and function, physical and functional status, and psychosocial factors. These findings indicate that dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults is multifactorial. Future research should employ longitudinal designs and standardized assessments to clarify causal relationships and inform targeted prevention strategies aimed at improving quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00455-026-10950-7.

Introduction

The population of older adults is increasing rapidly as life expectancy rises and birth rates decline. World life expectancy has increased by 8.4 years since 1995 and for 2025 was 73.49, a 0.23% increase from 2024. This demographic transition is particularly apparent among individuals aged 60 and over. The number of older adults aged 60 and over is expected to increase from 1.1 billion in 2023 to 1.4 billion in 2030 [1, 2]. Increasing life expectancy has led to an increasing number of older adults living with various problems, such as chronic conditions, functional limitations, and geriatric syndromes [3–5]. One of the geriatric syndromes commonly found in the aging group is dysphagia [6–8].

Swallowing changes in older adults can occur due to age-related physiological changes, commonly referred to as presbyphagia. These changes can affect all phases of swallowing. During the oral phase, changes in dentitions, oral hygiene, salivary flow, and food texture perception, along with decreased tongue strength and pressure, can delay bolus preparation and oral transit [9]. In the pharyngeal phase, delayed initiation of the swallowing reflex, reduced laryngeal excursion, and diminished airway protection are commonly observed. These changes may result in bolus pooling in the valleculae and increase the risk of penetration and aspiration, particularly when physiological reserves are compromised by acute illness [10]. In the esophageal phase, prolonged bolus clearance times and reduced upper esophageal sphincter flexibility may contribute to upper gastrointestinal dysfunction such as reflux or achalasia [11].

The prevalence of dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults is high. A previous study estimated that approximately 46% of people aged 60 years and older undergo dysphagia or are at risk of swallowing difficulty [12]. Despite its high prevalence, dysphagia often remains unrecognized and untreated, particularly in older adults without identifiable neurological, neuromuscular, or structural causes [13, 14]. A study found that one in nine healthy older adults living in the community experiences symptoms similar to dysphagia, which is often underrecognized and underreported [15].

Considering the high prevalence of dysphagia among this population, it is important to explore related factors, especially those without neurological and neuromuscular issues. Early studies have identified those factors, including older age [16–19], chronic diseases such as diabetes [16] and cardiovascular conditions [20], oral health problems (e.g., tooth loss, reduced mastication ability, and oral dryness) [17, 21, 22], sarcopenia and frailty [20, 23, 24], poor nutritional status [25], and cognitive or psychological impairment [26]. However, it has used diverse study designs, populations, and assessment methods. To address this, a scoping review is needed to explore the existing evidence, clarify how dysphagia-related factors are studied in community-dwelling older adults, and identify research gaps to guide future research and preventive efforts, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. Therefore, this scoping review aims to map the existing evidence on factors related to dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults.

Method

The protocol for this scoping review was prospectively registered on Figshare (DOI: https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.29301107). The five steps of this scoping review were: (1) Defining the research question; (2) Identifying relevant studies; (3) Study selection; (4) Charting the data; (5) Collating and summarizing the results [27], and in line with the PRISMA-ScR [28].

Defining The Research Question

This scoping review answered the following questions: “What is the existing evidence on factors related to dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults?” The P (Population): older adults; C (Concept): factors related to dysphagia; C (Context): community-dwelling.

Identifying Relevant Studies

The studies in this review were from three databases: Embase, Ovid-Medline, and CINAHL, following a research strategy that was consulted with a professional librarian. The terms included ‘aged,’ ‘dysphagia,’ and ‘community’ along with their synonyms. The search syntax for Embase includes:

(aged OR (aged NEAR/2 (patient OR people OR person OR subject)) OR elderly OR (elderly NEAR/2 (patient OR people OR person OR subject)) OR “senior citizen” OR senium): ti, ab, kw, de OR “aged”/exp AND (dysphagia* OR aphagopraxia OR ((deglutition OR swallow*) NEAR/2 (difficult* OR disorder*)) OR oropharyngeal NEAR/2 dysphagia): ti, ab, kw, de OR “dysphagia”/exp AND (community NEAR/2 (individual* OR participant* OR people OR person* OR subject*) OR (community NEAR/2 dwelling) OR (independent NEAR/2 living) OR “aging in place” OR healthy NEAR/2 (elder* OR older)): ti, ab, kw, de OR “community-dwelling person”/exp OR “healthy older adults”/exp.

Eligibility criteria were defined a priori using the Population–Concept–Context framework. This review included studies of community-dwelling adults aged 60 years and older. Studies involving older adults with conditions that directly cause dysphagia (e.g., stroke, Parkinson’s disease, dementia, or head and neck cancer) were excluded to focus on non-disease-specific factors since dysphagia in these conditions is primarily disease-related. Studies conducted in nursing homes or long-term care facilities were also excluded due to their higher rates of frailty, comorbidity, and care dependency, which differ from the characteristics of community-dwelling older adults. These criteria were applied to ensure that the review specifically identified factors related to dysphagia among relatively independent, community-dwelling older adults. The concept of interest was factors related to dysphagia. Only studies using multivariate analyses were included to ensure that the identified factors were independently related to dysphagia, rather than relying on unadjusted or exploratory associations. Quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies were considered, whereas review articles, conference abstracts, editorials, and commentaries were excluded to ensure inclusion of original empirical research. There were no date restrictions, and databases were searched from inception to June 2025. Only English-language publications were included due to feasibility constraints.

Study Selection

The studies were evaluated by two independent reviewers (KK and DS) using reference management software (EndNote 21). Titles and abstracts were first screened. The remaining full-text studies were extracted and screened for inclusion. A third reviewer (H-FL) resolved any disagreements. In total, 1,043 studies were identified from 3 databases. After removing 420 duplicate records, 623 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 44 studies were considered potentially relevant and were assessed for full-text eligibility. Five studies could not be retrieved in full text, leaving 39 studies for full-text review. Following full-text assessment, 25 studies were excluded for not meeting the predefined eligibility criteria (e.g., 15 studies included stroke, Parkinson’s disease, or dementia, 3 studies did not focus on older adults, and 7 studies did not perform multivariate analysis). To minimize potential selection bias, predefined eligibility criteria were applied consistently across all stages of the screening process. Data Charting

Two reviewers independently extracted data, including author(s), year of publication, study location, study design, participants’ characteristics, and factors related to dysphagia (KK and DS). Reviewer discrepancies were discussed among the research team until a consensus was reached and verified by a third reviewer (H-FL).

Critical Appraisal

In accordance with established methodological guidance for scoping reviews, a formal risk-of-bias or methodological quality assessment was not conducted [29]. The primary aim of this review was to map and categorize the existing evidence on factors related to dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults, rather than to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions or estimate pooled effect sizes. Therefore, critical appraisal was considered beyond the scope of this review.

Collating and Summarizing Results

In this section, the data were divided into two parts. The first part covered study characteristics, such as authors, year, study location, study aims, participant characteristics such as age, gender, sample size, instruments, and key findings. The second part focused on factors related to dysphagia obtained from the included studies, including demographic factors, health conditions, oral health and function, physical and functional status, and psychosocial factors.

This review synthesized evidence from 14 studies conducted in Japan (n = 4) [16, 17, 30, 31], South Korea (n = 4) [18, 21, 23, 25], China (n = 3) [22, 32, 33], Thailand (n = 2) [19, 34], and Taiwan (n = 1) [24]. These studies were conducted between 2012 and 2025, with sample sizes ranging from 62 to 4462 community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and above. Most study designs were cross-sectional studies (n = 13). The instruments to assess dysphagia are various; however, the most used were the Eating Assessment Tool 10 (EAT-10) and the 30-mL water swallowing test. Factors Related to Dysphagia among Community-dwelling Older Adults

Age was the most consistently examined demographic variable and was positively related to dysphagia across studies [16–19]. Chronic health conditions, including diabetes mellitus and other underlying diseases, were related to dysphagia; however, these findings were reported in relatively few studies [16, 19]. Oral health–related variables were among the most frequently identified relationships. Tooth loss, reduced mastication ability, oral dryness or hyposalivation, and decreased saliva flow were consistently related to dysphagia [16, 17, 21, 22, 24, 25, 30, 34]. Measures of oral muscular strength and alterations in oral microbiota were reported in individual studies, indicating emerging areas of investigation.

Declines in physical and functional status, including sarcopenia, reduced skeletal muscle strength, impaired balance, falls, frailty, and poor nutritional status, were consistently related to dysphagia [18, 23, 25, 31, 32, 34], demonstrating a strong relationship between systemic functional decline and swallowing impairment. Psychosocial factors were rarely examined, although limited evidence suggested an association between lower health literacy and dysphagia [33]. Overall, the evidence supports a multidimensional pattern of relationships, with the most consistent findings observed in oral and physical-functional categories.

Discussion

This scoping review identified a wide range of factors related to dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults, which were categorized into five categories. Those categories consist of demographic factors, health conditions, oral health and function, physical and functional status, and psychosocial factors. These findings highlight the multifactorial nature of dysphagia in the aging population, particularly in non-institutionalized community settings.

Several scoping reviews on dysphagia in older adults have been conducted previously; however, most focused on interventions or management strategies [35, 36] and on dysphagia screening tools [37–39]. In addition, many reviews included heterogeneous populations and settings, such as hospitalized patients, long-term care residents [36], or individuals with neurological and neuromuscular conditions, including dementia, Parkinson’s disease, and stroke [36, 39]. In contrast, the present scoping review focused on community-dwelling older adults without neurological, neuromuscular, or structural diseases. By narrowing the population and setting, this scoping review offers a distinct contribution by mapping the various factors related to dysphagia in relatively healthy older adults. This review extends previous studies by providing evidence-based insights highly relevant for early identification, risk stratification, and prevention of dysphagia in older adults living in community settings.

For demographic factors, increasing age was the most consistently reported determinant of dysphagia. This finding confirmed that dysphagia, particularly oropharyngeal dysphagia, is a geriatric syndrome [6]. Age-related physiological changes, including delayed oral transit, lowered pharyngeal clearance, reduced sensory perception, and decreased saliva production, are known to impair swallowing efficiency [7].

Health conditions are found as the second category related to dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults. Diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and other underlying diseases are frequent. Chronic diseases may influence the neuromuscular coordination of swallowing, resulting in swallowing difficulties [40, 41]. Other comorbidities, such as chronic inflammation, were also linked to impaired swallowing mechanisms [42]. These findings reinforce the importance of integrating dysphagia screening into chronic disease management programs in community-based care.

Oral health and function is the third category and is the largest group of factors. Tooth loss, reduced mastication ability, and oral dryness were the most consistently reported. Previous studies found that the number of functional teeth has a direct impact on chewing efficiency and bolus preparation [43, 44]. Another study stated that oral dryness can influence bolus formation and transit, leading to an increased risk of residue and aspiration [45]. On the other hand, weakness of the oral muscles, including the buccinator muscle and tongue, can lead to swallowing difficulty. Furthermore, the existence of oral microbiota, such as Prevotella spp can also lead to swallowing difficulties [30]. Impaired chewing ability, poor oral moisture, and reduced salivary function can interfere with the preparatory phase and the oral phase of swallowing [46, 47]. Because the oral cavity is the point of entry for food before swallowing, maintaining oral health is an important strategy for preventing dysphagia.

Physical and functional status was the fourth, and another important category. Declines in muscle strength, mobility, and overall physical performance were related to dysphagia. Sarcopenia and frailty, in particular, may reduce the strength and coordination of muscles involved in swallowing [23]. Other related factors included falls, mobility impairment, and poor nutritional status [25, 34]. Malnutrition and reduced appetite can accelerate muscle wasting in the oral cavity, pharynx, and esophagus, further compromising swallowing efficiency [48, 49]. These conditions may result in slower bolus transit and an increased risk of aspiration, highlighting the close interrelationship between physical decline, nutrition, and swallowing function.

Psychosocial factors were the least frequently examined but contributed to dysphagia. This domain only examined low health literacy. Older adults with dysphagia generally have lower levels of health literacy, particularly in their ability to seek medical advice, understand health information, and access social support resources [33]. These findings suggest that psychosocial assessment, including health literacy assessment, should be considered as part of comprehensive dysphagia screening in community settings.

Although numerous factors have been reported to be related to dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults, inconsistencies across studies should be interpreted considering substantial variation in the assessment methods used. This scoping review identified a wide range of dysphagia measurement approaches, including interviews, single questions, self-report questionnaires, and objective assessments. Most studies use self-reporting instruments, the most common being the Eating Assessment Tool (EAT-10). Objective measures, such as the 30 mL water swallow test, have only been used in a few studies. The heterogeneity of these contributions likely contributes to wide variations in the reporting of related factors. Differences in instrument use can reduce comparability among studies.

Strengths and Limitations

The strength of this review is that it provides a comprehensive map of factors related to dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults. The review mapped the evidence across demographic, health, oral, functional, and psychosocial domains. This study also included only studies that used multivariate analysis, which allows for the identification of factors independently related to dysphagia. This scoping review also has several limitations. First, most studies used cross-sectional designs, thus preventing causal inference. Second, all studies were conducted in Asian countries and therefore cannot be generalized to countries outside the region. Third, only studies published in English were included, potentially missing studies published in other languages. Finally, dysphagia was assessed using various instruments, with most studies relying on self-reported questionnaires (e.g., EAT-10), and only a few incorporating objective assessments (e.g., the 30 mL water swallow test) or combining both methods; notably, none employed instrumental gold-standard assessments (e.g., videofluoroscopic swallowing study or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing). Self-reported tools are suitable and widely used in community-based research because they are practical and non-invasive; however, differences in assessment tools, scoring methods, and cut-off points may introduce measurement bias and lead to inconsistencies in reported prevalence and related factors.

Implications

The findings of this review have implications for research, clinical practice, and policy. From a research perspective, this review identified factors related to dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults and identified gaps in the current evidence, including the use of multiple instruments to assess dysphagia and the use of cross-sectional designs by most studies. Future research should expand beyond community-dwelling older adults to examine dysphagia across a broader range of care contexts. Many of the factors identified are needed to explore how these factors interact across different care settings. Longitudinal designs are essential to clarify temporal and causal relationships. Standardization of assessment instruments is also recommended, including the consistent use of validated screening tools and simple objective measures such as swallowing tests. Furthermore, intervention studies targeting modifiable risk factors are needed to support the development of evidence-based prevention and management strategies. In clinical practice, these findings underscore that dysphagia in older adults can be related to multiple factors. Healthcare professionals should use standardized screening tools and, where possible, objective assessments. Routine screening and early detection should be integrated into community health programs to identify individuals at risk and prevent complications. From a policy perspective, this evidence supports the development of standardized guidelines for dysphagia screening and management in community settings. Integrating dysphagia assessment into community health programs can improve early identification and enable timely intervention, ultimately improving health outcomes for older adults.

Conclusion

This review identified various factors related to dysphagia among community-dwelling older adults, including demographic factors, health conditions, oral health and function, physical and functional status, and psychosocial factors. The findings show that not only neurological, neuromuscular, or structural problems related to dysphagia. Recognizing this complexity is essential for promoting comprehensive screening and preventive strategies in community settings, thereby supporting early identification and more holistic management of swallowing difficulties among older adults.
