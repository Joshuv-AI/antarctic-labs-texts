Prevalence of cognitive impairment in older medical inpatients: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Azasma Tanvir, Minji Jinny Kim, Glen Katsnelson, Felix Hu, Ellene Yan, Aparna Saripella, Marina Englesakis, Frances Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive impairment is common among older adults and is associated with adverse outcomes such as prolonged hospital stays and increased mortality. Many of the medical inpatients subsequently undergo surgical procedures, where cognitive impairment is known to influence perioperative risk, anesthetic management, and postoperative outcomes. No meta-analysis has comprehensively synthesized global data specific to cognitive impairment among medically hospitalized patients. We aimed to determine the global prevalence of cognitive impairment in older medical inpatients and explore variations by assessment method and region.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase, Cochrane Central, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and PsycINFO for relevant articles. We included patients ≥ 60 years old admitted to hospitals and studies examining prevalence of cognitive impairment. Excluded were elective surgical patients and non-hospital settings. Cognition was assessed by validated cognitive assessment tools, diagnostic instruments, or prior diagnosis. The primary outcome was the pooled prevalence of cognitive impairment in older patients admitted to hospitals. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on cognitive assessment tools, geographic regions, study type, and sample size.ResultsFifty-six studies (n = 62,853) were included. Mean age was 79.8 ± 7.7 years with 56% female. Meta-analysis revealed substantial variability across studies, with pooled prevalence estimates demonstrating high heterogeneity. The prevalence of newly suspected cognitive impairment/dementia using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders and/or validated screening tools was 38% (95% CI: 33%–42%). The prevalence of pre-diagnosed cognitive impairment/dementia documented from medical records was 24% (95% CI: 19%–29%). The prevalence of dementia was higher in Western countries than Eastern countries [41% (95% CI: 32%–50%) vs 27% (95% CI: 21%–33%)].ConclusionsCognitive impairment is prevalent among older medical inpatients. The variability in prevalence was dependent on assessment method and geographic region. Given the substantial heterogeneity and low to very low certainty of the evidence available, these prevalence estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is prevalent among older adults and poses significant challenges to healthcare systems. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated the global prevalence of dementia in 2021 to be 57 million, with 10 million new cases emerging annually.1 Cognitive impairment burden is substantial, resulting in increased hospitalizations, healthcare costs, caregiver stress, and institutionalization.2, 3 This growing burden is particularly concerning in hospitalized older adults, where cognitive impairment remains under-recognized and undertreated.4

Hospitalized patients ≥ 60 years old are especially vulnerable to cognitive impairment due to factors such as decreased physiological reserve, acute illness, polypharmacy, and the hospital environment itself.5 Cognitive impairment in this setting is associated with increased risks of delirium, longer hospital stays, higher readmission rates, and elevated mortality.6, 7 Moreover, unrecognized cognitive impairment can lead to suboptimal management and poorer health outcomes. Understanding the prevalence of cognitive impairment in this population encourages cognitive screening, enables risk mitigation, and optimizes management during the hospital stay.

Several professional societies, such as the American Geriatrics Society and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence recommend screening and assessment for cognitive impairment in older adults, especially those hospitalized for illnesses.8, 9 To date, there is currently no universally accepted guideline for screening in all hospitalized older inpatients.

Prior reviews have examined the prevalence of preoperative cognitive impairment in older surgical patients and among residents in nursing homes.10, 11 A separate review quantified the global prevalence of delirium in inpatients.12 While some narrative reviews have described the cognitive burden in hospitalized older adults,13, 14 no systematic review and meta-analysis has comprehensively synthesized global data specific to cognitive impairment in older medical inpatients.

Additionally, a substantial proportion of these inpatients may need surgical procedures, where cognitive impairment is known to influence perioperative risk, anesthetic management, and postoperative outcomes. Understanding the prevalence of cognitive impairment in inpatients is essential, as it informs both inpatient care and perioperative planning for those who proceed to surgery. Identifying these conditions early during hospital admissions provides opportunities to optimize management, mitigate complications, and improve overall outcomes.

To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis with the aim of quantifying the global prevalence of cognitive impairment among hospitalized medical inpatients ≥ 60 years old and examining variation by geographic region. By synthesizing prevalence data across diverse healthcare settings, we aim to inform clinical guidelines, promote early detection, and improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD#42024573361) and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines.15

Literature search methodology

A comprehensive literature search strategy was developed by an information specialist (ME). The following databases were searched from their inception dates to 26 May 2024: MEDLINE, MEDLINE ePub Ahead of Print and In-process and Other Non-indexed citations, Embase Classic+Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and APA PsycINFO.

The search process followed the Cochrane Handbook16 and the Cochrane Methodological Expectations of Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR)17 for conducting the search, and the PRISMA 202015 for reporting. The search strategy was peer-reviewed in accordance with the PRESS 2015 Guideline18 Evidence-Based Checklist to mitigate potential search errors. Results were limited to participants aged 60 years or older, with publications limited to the English language. Conference and non-journal materials were excluded when possible. Supplemental searching was conducted via PubMed. Continued literature surveillance was conducted until 10 April 2025. The complete search strategy is in Supplementary material.

Study selection and data extraction

Following duplicate removal, four independent reviewers (AT, GK, MK, FH), divided into two groups, performed title and abstract screening using the Covidence platform.19 Conflicts were resolved by discussion and consensus between two pairs of reviewers or by a third reviewer (EY). Full-text screening and data extraction were independently performed by each pair.

The primary outcome was the prevalence of cognitive impairment among older medical inpatients. Cognitive impairment was categorized as unspecified cognitive impairment/dementia and documented cognitive impairment/dementia, as defined in the individual study. Included were: (1) hospitalized patients ≥ 60 years old, with a length of stay of ≥ 1 day; (2) cognition was assessed using a validated cognitive screening tool, a diagnostic instrument, or a previous diagnosis before admission; (3) prospective cohort (PC) or cross-sectional (CS) studies; (4) reported cognitive impairment number or prevalence; and (5) sample size of ≥ 100.

Excluded were: (1) elective surgical patients; (2) non-hospital settings or not admitted to the hospital/ward (e.g., nursing homes, retirement homes, long-term care facilities, clinics, rehabilitation or neurorehabilitation centres, emergency department, geriatric day hospital, psychiatric ward, long stay unit, palliative care); (3) brain injury, stroke (e.g., infarctions, hemorrhages, cerebral small vessel disease, hydrocephalus), or minor medical conditions; (4) reporting only on delirium; (5) retrospective studies, randomized control trials (RCTs), case-controls, or case series; and (6) Non-English language.

Data were extracted by two independent reviewers using a standardized Excel data collection sheet. The following information was extracted: author, country, publication year, study design, total number of patients, age, sex, cognitive assessment tools, and prevalence of cognitive impairment either by Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)20 screening tools, or previously documented in medical records.

Assessment of methodological quality and risk of bias

Quality assessments of included studies were independently performed by two reviewers (GK, MK) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS)21 and the Meta-Analyses of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE)22 with discrepancies resolved through consensus or by a third reviewer (EY). The NOS was utilized to assess the selection and comparability of cohorts as well as the outcomes from PC and CS studies. A range from seven to nine indicated moderate to high quality. The MOOSE checklist was utilized to assess the study design, statistical analysis, study population identification, definition of outcome and outcome assessment, selective patient loss during follow-up, and identification of confounding and prognostic variables.

Statistical methods

Meta-analysis was completed for the pooled prevalence of newly recognized cognitive impairment/dementia using different validated cognitive assessment tools (DSM,20 Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE),23 Mini-Cog,24 Abbreviated Mental Test Score (AMTS),25 and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)26). Meta-analysis was also completed for the pooled prevalence of documented cognitive impairment on hospital admission. Subgroup analysis explored cognitive impairment and dementia prevalence based on continents, study type and sample size. We used the Cochrane Review Manager Version 5.4 to complete the meta-analysis when there were 2 or more studies. The pooled prevalence and its corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using random effect model. Inverse variance method was used, and a P-value of 0.05 or less was considered statistically significant.

We performed sensitivity analysis for all significant outcomes and checked the effect of individual study on heterogeneity by leave one-study-out meta-analysis. Egger’s test using linear regression test for asymmetry and visual examination of funnel plot were applied to evaluate publication bias.27 We conducted meta-regression analysis using Open Meta Analyst software and R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to discover the effect of covariates (age, sex, sample size and study type) on cognitive impairment/dementia prevalence diagnosed by the DSM and screening tools and by prior diagnosis.28 Publication bias and meta-regression were completed when there were ≥ 10 studies.

Prevalence of dementia stratified by geographic locations

A subgroup analysis was conducted based on geographic location, stratified by Europe and Asia (Table S1). Dementia prevalence among European countries was 41% (95% CI: 32%–50%, I2 = 98% P < 0.001). The high heterogeneity suggested significant variability in study populations, assessment methods, or clinical settings. In Asia, the prevalence was significantly lower at 27% (95% CI: 21%–33%, I2 = 50%, P < 0.001). The test for subgroup differences was significant (P = 0.01) and I2 was 85.1%.

Prevalence of dementia stratified by study design

We stratified results by study design, comparing prospective cohorts to cross-sectional studies (Table S1). DSM diagnosed dementia prevalence among prospective cohort studies was 44% (95% CI: 36%–51%, I2 = 97%), and 22% for cross-sectional studies (95% CI: 13%–32%, I2 = 92%). The test for subgroup differences was significant (P = 0.0007) and I2 was 91.4%.

Prevalence of dementia stratified by sample size

We compared DSM diagnosed dementia prevalence based on sample size. We found that the prevalence was similar in studies with ≥ 500 participants vs. < 500 participants (41%; 95% CI: 29%–53% vs 38%; 95% CI: 27%–50%) (Table S1). The test for subgroup differences was not significant (P = 0.79) and I2 was recorded as 0%.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a sensitivity analysis for cognitive impairment/dementia prevalence diagnosed by neuropsychological batteries, screening tools (MMSE, Mini-Cog, AMTS, and MoCA) and prior diagnosis. Heterogeneity was re-calculated after removing each study one at a time for every outcome. Funnel plot asymmetry was observed, and Egger’s linear regression test indicated a statistically significant result (P = 0.02) for newly identified cognitive impairment and dementia using the neurological battery test or screening tools. No asymmetry was visually detected in funnel plots, and Egger’s test showed no statistically significant result (P = 0.82) was not significant for documented cognitive impairment/dementia by pre-existing diagnosis (Fig. S2).

Meta-regression

We performed meta-regression analysis to determine the effect of covariates (age, female sex, sample size and study design) on the prevalence of cognitive impairment/dementia diagnosed by the DSM/screening tools and by prior diagnosis. We observed a positive association between newly identified cognitive impairment/dementia and age (P = 0.001). There were no significant association between any other covariate and newly identified cognitive impairment/dementia. The relationship between previously documented cognitive impairment/dementia and covariates of age, female sex, sample size and study type was not statistically significant. (Table S1).

Grading of recommendations assessment, development, and evaluation (GRADE)

We evaluated the certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework approach, which considers risk of bias, directness, precision, consistency, and publication bias. GRADE Classification identified the evidence of seven results as ‘low’, five results as ‘very low’, and one result as ‘moderate’ (Table S5).

Quality assessment

The NOS scores (Table S2) for the included PC studies ranged from 7 to 9, indicating moderate to high quality. All studies demonstrated appropriate representativeness of the exposed cohort, clear ascertainment of exposure, and verification that the outcome of interest was not present at baseline. Additionally, all studies reported adequate follow-up durations to capture the relevant outcomes, and selective loss of patients during follow-up was minimal. Most studies scored well in outcome assessment; however, there was some variation in the level of confounder adjustment. For example, while a few studies accounted for age, sex, Charlson index, and comorbidities,55, 65 one study included fewer confounders in its analysis.83 This variability in confounder adjustment may contribute to heterogeneity in prevalence estimates.

The MOOSE checklist (Table S3) confirmed that all studies clearly identified their study populations, provided explicit outcome definitions, and applied appropriate statistical analyses such as logistic regression, ANOVA, and Chi-square tests. Some heterogeneity was observed in the assessment methods used to measure cognitive impairment, which may impact comparability across studies. Overall, the included studies adhered to rigorous methodological standards, ensuring the reliability of their findings.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 56 studies (n = 62,853), we evaluated the prevalence of cognitive impairment/dementia among older medical inpatients. Our analysis distinguishes between two forms of cognitive impairment/dementia: (1) newly suspected cognitive impairment/dementia identified during hospital admission using neuropsychological batteries or screening tools and (2) cognitive impairment/dementia based on documented pre-existing diagnosis, identified by medical records.

We found that the prevalence of newly suspected cognitive impairment/dementia was 38% and documented pre-existing cognitive impairment/dementia to be 28%. The reliance of pre-existing diagnoses alone may underestimate the burden of cognitive impairment in the inpatient populations. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to high heterogeneity and low certainty of evidence. Further high-quality, standardized studies are needed to better define the role and impact of cognitive assessment in older inpatients.

The prevalence rates reported in our meta-analysis align with previous research identifying cognitive impairment as a widespread yet under-diagnosed condition.85, 86 Previous systematic reviews have focused on the prevalence of cognitive impairment in community-dwelling older adults or long-term care settings, with limited emphasis on hospitalized medical inpatients.86, 87, 88, 89, 90, 91, 92 To date, no systematic review and meta-analysis has comprehensively quantified the global prevalence of cognitive impairment/dementia in medical inpatients.

Our findings are consistent with the growing evidence in the literature that supports cognitive assessment in hospitalized older adults. Several professional organizations recognize the importance of cognitive screening in older adults. Specifically, the American Geriatrics Society9 and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence8 recommend cognitive assessments for at-risk older adults, particularly those hospitalized for medical conditions. There is no universally adopted guideline for screening in all hospitalized older patients. The recommended screening tools vary but commonly include the MMSE, MoCA, and the AMTS.93

Our meta-analysis revealed significant heterogeneity in the prevalence of cognitive impairment/dementia, likely driven by difference in geographic regions, study populations, hospital settings, and assessment tools. Geographically, the prevalence of cognitive impairment /dementia was higher in European studies than Asian studies, potentially due to differences in diagnostic criteria, healthcare accessibility, and cultural attitudes toward cognitive screening.94 Additionally, differences in life expectancy, comorbidities, and healthcare accessibility could contribute to these regional disparities. Socioeconomic factors may also play a role, as underreporting in Asian countries could result from limited healthcare access, stigma surrounding dementia, or differences in hospital admission criteria for older adults with cognitive impairment.95

In some regions, particularly in parts of Asia, families may be more likely to care for cognitively impaired individuals at home, potentially delaying or avoiding hospital admission unless acute medical needs arise. Lower thresholds for hospital admission, greater availability of institutional care, or differing patterns of referral in Western healthcare systems may increase the likelihood of hospitalization among patients with cognitive impairment. These contextual factors may influence observed prevalence estimates when interpreting geographical differences.

Differences in study design also contributed to variability. Prospective cohort studies reported higher cognitive impairment prevalence than cross-sectional studies, likely due to differences in data collection methods and follow-up assessments. The observed variability highlights the need for standardized cognitive assessment protocols to ensure consistency across studies and facilitate accurate identification of cognitive impairment in hospitalized patients.

Our meta-regression analysis explored whether study-level characteristics influenced dementia prevalence diagnosed by the DSM or by prior diagnosis. Among the covariates examined, only study design demonstrated a significant association. Specifically, cross-sectional studies were associated with a significantly lower reported dementia prevalence diagnosed by the DSM than prospective cohort studies. This may reflect methodological differences, such as the potential for under-detection in cross-sectional designs due to limited follow-up or reliance on single time-point evaluations. The absence of significant associations with age, sex, or sample size suggests that these factors did not meaningfully contribute to the heterogeneity in our pooled estimates for cognitive impairment/dementia diagnosed by the DSM or screening tools and by pre-existing diagnosis.

Clinical implications

The high prevalence of newly suspected cognitive impairment among older medical inpatients highlights the burden of under-diagnosis in hospitalized populations. Cognitive impairment is associated with adverse outcomes such as prolonged hospital stays, increased readmission rates, higher mortality, and greater post-discharge care needs.7 Cognitive screening at hospital admission could facilitate timely interventions, including enhanced discharge planning, involvement of geriatric specialists, and initiation of cognitive support strategies.

Moreover, many hospitalized inpatients may require surgical procedures. The presence of cognitive impairment in these individuals can significantly influence perioperative management, anesthetic risk, and postoperative outcomes such as delirium, functional decline, and prolonged recovery. Identification of cognitive impairment during hospital admission may be relevant when surgery is anticipated.

For patients who screen positive for cognitive impairment, multidisciplinary care approaches may be considered, including cognitive rehabilitation, medication optimization, and caregiver education. Non-pharmacological interventions such as reorienting patients, avoiding polypharmacy, and ensuring adequate sensory input (e.g., providing glasses and hearing aids) have been shown to reduce the risk of cognitive decline.96 Additionally, preventing complications such as delirium, which often exacerbates cognitive impairment, through targeted interventions like early mobilization, hydration, and minimizing unnecessary sedation may prove beneficial.97

Strength and limitations

A key strength of our review is the rigorous methodology employed in study selection and data synthesis, following PRISMA guidelines, which enhances the reliability of our pooled estimates. We also included a substantial dataset from diverse geographic regions, which provides a global assessment and improves the generalizability of our findings.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the high heterogeneity substantially constrains the interpretability and generalizability of our pooled prevalence estimates. These estimates aggregate data from studies that differ markedly in cognitive assessment tools, diagnostic thresholds, study design, inpatient populations, hospital settings, and geographic locations. Thus, the pooled prevalence values should be interpreted with caution. Secondly, reliance on screening tools rather than comprehensive clinical evaluations in many included studies may have led to over- or underestimation of the true prevalence. Thirdly, only English language publications were included, introducing language bias.

Additionally, the certainty of evidence for most outcomes was rated as low or very low according to the GRADE framework, reflecting substantial heterogeneity, methodological variability, and imprecision across studies. The low certainty of evidence emphasizes the need for cautious interpretation and highlights the importance of further research.

RCTs were excluded from this review as they are not designed to estimate the prevalence of population. Inclusion of data from RCT would introduce methodological heterogeneity and limit the interpretability of pooled prevalence estimates. Another limitation is the exclusion of elective surgical inpatients. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence of cognitive impairment in surgical patients had been done.11

Conclusions

In older medical inpatients, we found the prevalence of newly suspected cognitive impairment/dementia to be 38% on hospital admission, while that of pre-existing cognitive impairment/dementia was 24%. Given the substantial heterogeneity and low to very low certainty of evidence, these prevalence estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Further well-designed studies using standardized assessment approaches are required to improve precision and comparability of estimates.
