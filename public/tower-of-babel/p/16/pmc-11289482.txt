Magnon gap excitations in van der Waals antiferromagnet MnPSe3
Authors: Dipankar Jana, D. Vaclavkova, I. Mohelsky, P. Kapuscinski, C. W. Cho, I. Breslavetz, M. Białek, J.-Ph. Ansermet, B. A. Piot, M. Orlita, C. Faugeras, M. Potemski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Magneto-spectroscopy methods have been employed to study the zero-wavevector magnon excitations in MnPSe3. Experiments carried out as a function of temperature and the applied magnetic field show that two low-energy magnon branches of MnPSe3 in its antiferromagnetic phase are gapped. The observation of two low-energy magnon gaps (at 1.70 ± 0.05 meV and 0.09 ± 0.01 meV) implies that MnPSe3 is a biaxial antiferromagnet. A relatively strong out-of-plane anisotropy imposes the spin alignment to be in-plane whereas the spin directionality within the plane is governed by a factor of 2.5 × 10−3 weaker in-plane anisotropy.

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) materials beyond graphene constitute an exceptional platform for the exploration of the unique electronic, optical, and magnetic properties of low-dimensional systems1–5. Among currently studied materials are layered, van der Waals magnets, which include a family of metal phosphorus trichalcogenides (MPX\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3, with M = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni and X = S, Se)6–11. MPX\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 compounds are semiconducting antiferromagnets with diverse spin ordering configurations of the transition metal ions12. Since MPX\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 layers are held together by weak van der Waals forces, typical distances between the metal ions within the layers are shorter than those between ions in different layers, and the magnetic order in MPX\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 compounds is governed mainly by spin-spin coupling within the layers. Such quasi-2D or even strictly 2D (in monolayers) magnetic ordering persists in MPX\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 materials since they are not necessarily perfect Heisenberg antiferromagnets, but, in addition to the exchange terms, are also characterized by non-zero magnetic anisotropies. A non-negligible spin-orbit interaction within the M-ions is a primary cause of the so-called single-ion magnetic anisotropy. At first sight, the spin-orbit interaction is expected to be rather weak for Mn\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document}2+ ions in MnPX\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3, though the nature seems to be against the Mermin–Vagner theorem13 (no magnetic order in the 2D limit for Heisenberg antiferromagnets) and MnPX\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 compounds do display antiferromagnetic ordering14,15. Interestingly enough, the change of the chalcogen atom from sulfur (S) to selenium (Se) imposes the change in the type of magnetic ordering16,17. A single-ion anisotropy may still be relevant for ordering spins in MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}315,16, whereas the competing anisotropy due to magnetic dipolar interaction is likely imposing the character of spin-ordering in MnPS\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}314. From another perspective, the single ion anisotropy in MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 is considered to be small while interlayer exchange integrals are significantly large18. Despite these ambiguities, it is known that spins are aligned in the direction nearly perpendicular to the layers’ plane in the case of broadly studied MnPS\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}319. Instead, the less studied MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 is an easy-plane antiferromagnet, as deduced from neutron scattering18,20 and magnetization15,16 measurements as well as theoretical calculations7. The open questions addressed in the present work are in regards to the character of low energy magnon excitations in MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3. One of the calculated magnon branches7 has been concluded to be gapless (being dispersed linearly with the k-vector) and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=0$$\end{document}k=0 gap of the second magnon branch is estimated to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}∼14 cm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-1}$$\end{document}-1. Neutron scattering data18 confirm the upper 14 cm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-1}$$\end{document}-1 gap but lack the resolution to trace the low energy magnon branch in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=0$$\end{document}k=0 limit. On the other hand, the magnetization measurements16 indicate a spin-flop field at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}∼0.7 T, pointing out a non-zero onset (at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=0$$\end{document}k=0) of the lower magnon branch.

In the present work, we demonstrate that magnon gap excitations in MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 antiferromagnet can be effectively probed using magneto-spectroscopy methods (Raman scattering, far-infrared, and microwave absorption techniques). While neutron diffraction is a common experimental tool for investigating magnon dispersion in antiferromagnetic materials10,18, the magneto-optical techniques employed in this work represent useful alternatives that offer additional insights8,9,21,22. Mostly, these methods address the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=0$$\end{document}k=0 excitations only, but they allow one to explore the magnon modes in a broader energy range and with a greater energy resolution. With these experiments, we identify MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 as a de facto biaxial antiferromagnet, by revealing the energies of two fundamental magnon gaps, both of which are non-zero (14 and 0.7 cm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-1}$$\end{document}-1). This implies that the antiferromagnetic order in MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 is governed by two anisotropy terms: one previously identified and directed across the layers’ plane (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{\perp } \approx 0.3$$\end{document}D⊥≈0.3 cm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-1}$$\end{document}-1)16, which imposes the in-plane spin alignment, and another one, weak but non-negligible \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{\parallel }$$\end{document}D‖ term, estimated here to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 7.5 \times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}≈7.5×10-4 cm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-1}$$\end{document}-1, which imposes the spins’ alignment along a certain, yet unidentified, direction within the plane.

Concluding remarks

We have employed Raman scattering, far-infrared transmission, and microwave absorption measurements, carried as a function of temperature and of the magnetic field, to identify magnon gap excitations in MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 van der Waals antiferromagnet. The observation of two distinct magnon gap excitations supports the identification of MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 as a biaxial antiferromagnet with non-negligible in-plane anisotropy. Relevant parameters (fundamental magnon gap energies, g-factor, spin-flop field, anisotropy energies) are also estimated.

Methods

We utilized commercially available thick and large area (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 25$$\end{document}≈25 mm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document}2 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}× 500 μm) MnPSe\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}3 crystals for FIR-transmission and Raman scattering measurements and relatively thin and small size crystals for GHz absorption measurements. Magneto-optical experiments were performed in both the Faraday and Voigt configurations with the magnetic field (from a superconducting solenoid magnet) applied perpendicular and parallel to the layers’ plane respectively. In the case of Raman scattering measurements, the sample was excited by a semiconductor-based laser (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}λ = 515 nm) at a tilt of 20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}∘ with respect to the surface normal?. The laser beam was focused on the sample using a microscope objective with a numerical aperture of 0.83. The scattered signal was collected using the same objective and was analyzed by a 0.7m grating spectrometer equipped with a liquid nitrogen-cooled charge-coupled device (CCD). A set of reflection-based Bragg filters was employed in both the excitation and collection paths to effectively eliminate any unwanted laser line and to reject backscattered laser. For far infrared magneto-transmission measurements21, the radiation emitted from a mercury lamp was modulated using a Bruker Vertex 80v Fourier-transform spectrometer. The modulated radiation was delivered to the sample through light-pipe optics and subsequently detected by a composite bolometer placed directly behind the sample. Magneto-absorption experiments in the GHz frequency range were performed utilizing the modulation technique22. The radiation emitted by an Agilent gigahertz source was transmitted to the sample through a coaxial cable. The sample was positioned in the proximity of a resistive thermometer, which served as a bolometer. When the sample effectively absorbs the incident radiation, it results in a reduction of the radiation reaching the resistor. This causes a change in the resistor’s temperature, which can be detected by monitoring the variation in its resistance. The alteration in resistivity is detected through changes in modulation response amplitude, while the delay in the resistivity response, owing to the finite thermalization time, is observed through the modulation response phase.
