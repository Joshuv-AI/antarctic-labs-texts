Early radiotherapy delays acquired gefitinib resistance in EGFR-mutant NSCLC by suppressing c-FOS-associated survival signaling
Authors: Lin Peng, Juejun Gong, Yichen Li, Siyou Deng, Jinjie Li, Yujie Zhang, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) have been widely used in the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) harboring EGFR-sensitive mutations. However, acquired resistance remains a major clinical challenge. In a previous phase II clinical trial conducted by our group, early radiotherapy significantly delayed acquired resistance to EGFR-TKIs. Nevertheless, the optimal timing of radiotherapy and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsWe compared gefitinib alone with gefitinib plus radiotherapy in EGFR-mutant NSCLC xenograft models and evaluated early versus late radiotherapy intervention. RNA sequencing of xenograft tumors was performed to identify timing-associated mediators of radiotherapy-delayed gefitinib resistance. Based on these findings, we used gefitinib-resistant NSCLC cell lines, radiotherapy, c-FOS perturbation, and pathway inhibition assays to validate the underlying mechanisms and assess treatment efficacy in vitro.ResultsGefitinib combined with radiotherapy showed enhanced antitumor activity in the xenograft model. Compared with gefitinib alone, early radiotherapy reduced tumor volume by approximately 70%–75% at the experimental endpoint, whereas late radiotherapy reduced tumor volume by approximately 50%. Early radiotherapy was associated with improved survival during the observation period compared with late radiotherapy; notably, median survival was not reached in the early radiotherapy group during follow-up. RNA-seq identified c-FOS as a timing-associated differentially expressed gene in xenograft tumors. In vitro, radiotherapy enhanced the sensitivity of gefitinib-resistant cells and reduced c-FOS expression. Mechanistically, c-FOS promoted survival and proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis in resistant cells, in association with increased PI3K/AKT and JNK/p38 signaling activity, thereby contributing to gefitinib resistance.ConclusionsThis study indicates that early radiotherapy delays acquired gefitinib resistance, at least in part, by suppressing c-FOS in the tested model system. c-FOS may serve as a candidate biomarker for radiotherapy timing and as a hypothesis-generating therapeutic target for combination strategies, although further validation in other EGFR-mutant subtypes and with later-generation EGFR-TKIs is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03810-3.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor, Radiotherapy, Acquired resistance, c-FOS

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most prevalent malignant tumor worldwide and is associated with persistently high mortality, posing a serious public health burden [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for more than 80% of all lung cancer cases, and more than half of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [2, 3]. According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, targeted molecular therapy is the treatment of choice for advanced NSCLC with driver mutations. Epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) provide significant clinical benefit in advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC and are the preferred first-line option for tumors harboring EGFR-sensitive mutations [4–8], improving progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and objective response rate (ORR) compared with chemotherapy alone [9–11]. However, most patients ultimately relapse due to acquired resistance.

Acquired resistance to EGFR-TKIs is heterogeneous, evolves under therapy, and complicates subsequent management, underscoring the need for biomarkers linked to resistance evolution and strategies that delay or overcome resistance [12, 13]. Third-generation EGFR-TKIs, particularly osimertinib, are now the standard first-line treatment; nevertheless, acquired resistance still inevitably develops through diverse mechanisms, including C797S mutation, MET amplification, HER2 alteration, and phenotypic transformation [7–9]. Exploring strategies to delay resistance beyond the development of new agents remains a pressing clinical priority. First-generation EGFR-TKIs, although no longer the first-line option for most newly diagnosed patients, offer a well-characterized and tractable model of acquired resistance evolution established over decades. This mechanistic framework may provide a basis for future hypothesis testing in later-generation EGFR-TKI settings; however, direct extrapolation to those settings will require independent validation.

Radiotherapy is currently a main clinical modality for patients with local progression after EGFR-TKI resistance [14, 15], providing an immediately translatable backbone for EGFR-TKI-based combinatorial approaches. Although the molecular basis for timing-dependent radiotherapy benefit remains unclear, RNA sequencing of tumors from control, gefitinib monotherapy, and time-staggered combination radiotherapy groups revealed a prominent timing-associated transcriptional signal dominated by the FOS family, nominating c-FOS as a candidate mediator. Mechanistically, c-FOS encodes a component of the AP-1 transcription factor complex (via dimerization with JUN proteins) and regulates gene programs involved in proliferation, stress responses, and apoptosis [16, 17]. Across disease contexts, c-FOS has been linked to tumor progression and therapy resistance, including regulation of SLC7A11 and ferroptosis in pancreatic cancer [18], ERK1/2/c-FOS-dependent cellular programs in hepatic fibrosis [19], and malignant phenotypes in osteosarcoma [20], as well as drug-resistance pathways involving NANOG-driven stemness, P-glycoprotein, and gefitinib resistance-related signaling [21–23]; importantly, clinical studies in lung cancer associate c-FOS expression with patient outcomes [24, 25]. These observations support c-FOS as a plausible node connecting radiotherapy-responsive transcriptional reprogramming with resistance evolution in NSCLC.

Radiotherapy is a cornerstone of malignant tumor management and can reshape oncogenic signaling and transcriptional programs [26, 27], suggesting potential intersection with adaptive gene expression changes that drive therapeutic resistance. In a prior phase II clinical trial conducted by our group (NCT03595644), early combination radiotherapy significantly delayed the emergence of acquired resistance to EGFR-TKI and was accompanied by improved survival outcomes [28]. Importantly, our previous phase II trial used gefitinib as the EGFR-TKI backbone; therefore, the present study was designed as a direct mechanistic extension of that clinical observation. Using gefitinib therefore allows us to test the timing‑dependent hypothesis in a system where the clinical benefit has already been documented and where the resistance landscape is well mapped. Additional studies also support that combining radiotherapy with osimertinib enhances clinical outcomes in lung cancer patients who develop oligoprogression after osimertinib treatment [29]. However, the present study was conducted in a gefitinib-based model, and the relevance of the identified mechanism to osimertinib or other later-generation EGFR-TKIs remains to be established. Moreover, the optimal timing for integrating radiotherapy with EGFR-TKIs and the molecular mediators underlying this benefit remain poorly defined. To address this gap, we modeled early versus late radiotherapy under gefitinib pressure in vivo, performed RNA-seq of xenograft tumors to nominate timing-associated candidates, and functionally validated c-FOS in gefitinib-sensitive and gefitinib-resistant NSCLC models in vitro.

Together, these studies aim to define a mechanistic basis for timing-guided radiotherapy integration with EGFR-TKIs to delay acquired resistance in EGFR-mutant NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

Human NSCLC cell lines PC9, A549, HCC827 and H1975 were maintained in the Oncology Laboratory of Tongji Hospital. Cells were cultured using complete medium, comprising RPMI 1640 (Hyclone, USA), which contained 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Hyclone, USA). All cell lines were confirmed to be mycoplasma-negative by PCR-based testing within 1 week before the experiments and were used at passages 5–15 after thawing. Routine mycoplasma testing was performed every 2–3 months. The short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) specific to c-FOS (sh-c-FOS#1/2/3) were synthesized and cloned into the lentiviral vector by Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The target sequences are as follows: sh-F#1 (AAAGGAGAATCCGAAGGGAAA), sh-F#2 (CAGCATGGAGCTGAAGACCGA), and sh-F#3 (TGCCAACCTGCTGAAGGAGAA). In addition, lentiviruses containing full-length c-FOS cDNA were generated and used to transduce gefitinib-sensitive cells to establish c-FOS-overexpressing (oe-FOS) cell lines. Cell transfection with the virus was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Establishment of the acquired gefitinib-resistant cell line

The gefitinib-resistant PC9/GR cell line was established by long-term exposure of parental PC9 cells to gradually increasing concentrations of gefitinib-containing medium. Briefly, PC9 cells were initially exposed to 10 nmol/L gefitinib (Selleck Chemicals, S1025) in medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, and the drug concentration was increased stepwise over time. After 8 months, cells that were able to proliferate in the presence of 1 µmol/L gefitinib were obtained. The resistant phenotype remained stable even after more than 4 months of culture in the absence of gefitinib. A resistance index (RI) greater than 10 was considered indicative of successful establishment of the PC9/GR cell line.

Animal procedures

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Female BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. A subcutaneous xenograft model was established by injecting 5 × 10^6 PC9 cells into the right flank of each mouse. Mice were randomly assigned, using a random number table, to four groups (n = 10 per group): control, gefitinib alone, gefitinib plus early radiotherapy, and gefitinib plus late radiotherapy. When tumor volumes reached 100–150 mm^3, gefitinib (75 mg/kg/day; MedChemExpress, USA) was administered orally. Mice in the radiotherapy groups received local tumor irradiation with a single dose of 8 Gy using 6 MV X-rays delivered by a linear accelerator (Clinac 2100EX, Varian Medical Systems) at a dose rate of 200 cGy/min. Tumor volumes and body weights were measured three times per week by an independent investigator blinded to group assignment. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = 0.5 × length × width^2. At day 36, mice were euthanized, and tumors were harvested and weighed for further analyses.

In vitro irradiation

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 6-well or 96-well plates and allowed to reach approximately 70% confluence before irradiation. Irradiation was performed using the same linear accelerator as in the in vivo experiments. During irradiation, culture plates were placed on a 1.5-cm bolus to ensure full backscatter and covered with a 0.5-cm layer of culture medium to achieve consistent dose build-up. Cells received a single dose of 8 Gy delivered as 6 MV X-rays at a dose rate of 200 cGy/min. Control cells were handled identically but were not exposed to X-rays. The same irradiation setup, including the linear accelerator, beam energy, dose rate, plate positioning, bolus arrangement, and build-up conditions, was used consistently across all in vitro irradiation experiments unless otherwise specified.

H&E staining and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining according to standard procedures and to immunohistochemical staining with an anti-Ki67 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #9129). Ki67 expression was evaluated using a semiquantitative manual scoring method rather than automated digital pathology software. Three non-overlapping fields per section were randomly selected at 200× magnification. Two independent observers, blinded to the treatment groups, assessed the percentage of positive tumor cells and the staining intensity (0, no staining; 1, weak; 2, moderate; 3, strong). The final score for each field was calculated as the percentage of positive cells multiplied by the staining intensity score. The mean value across the three fields and the two observers was used as the final IHC score for each sample.

RNA extraction and RT‑PCR

Total RNA was obtained from the cells using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan) and the concentration of each RNA sample was measured using NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA for 1 µg of RNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (RR036A; Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primer sequences are as follows: c-FOS (forward: CCGGGGATAGCCTCTCTTACT; reverse: CCAGGTCCGTGCAGAAGTC), and GAPDH (forward: GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT; reverse: GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG).

Western blot analysis

Western blotting was performed according to standardized protocols described previously [30]. The following primary antibodies were used in this study: rabbit anti-c-FOS antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #2250); rabbit anti-GAPDH antibody (ABclonal, #A19056); rabbit anti-AKT antibody (Proteintech, #10176-2-AP); rabbit anti-phospho-AKT(Ser473) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #4060); rabbit anti-JNK antibody (Proteintech, #24164-1-AP); rabbit anti-phospho-JNK (Thr183/Tyr185) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #4668); rabbit anti-p38 antibody (Proteintech, #14064-1-AP); rabbit anti-phospho-p38 (Thr180/Tyr182) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #4511); rabbit anti-ERK1/2 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #4695); rabbit anti-phospho-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #4370); rabbit anti-BAX antibody (Proteintech, #50599-2-Ig); rabbit anti-Bcl-2 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #3498); rabbit anti-Caspase-9 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #9502); rabbit anti-Caspase-3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #9662); rabbit anti-Cleaved Caspase-3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #9661); rabbit anti-β-Actin antibody (ABclonal, #AC026).

Cell counting Kit-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (MedChemExpress, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1,000 cells per well were seeded into 96-well plates. At the indicated time points, the medium was replaced with 100 µL fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent, and cells were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Gefitinib sensitivity and IC50 values for PC9 and PC9/GR cells were determined by CCK-8 assay (Fig. S1). For IC50 determination, cells were exposed to a series of gefitinib concentrations for 72 h, and dose-response curves were fitted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1).

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in triplicate in 6-well plates at 1,000 cells per well and cultured for 14 days. The culture medium was replaced every 2 days to maintain adequate nutrient supply. Colonies were then fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 20 min. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted under a microscope.

Cell apoptosis assay

Cells were collected and washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V-APC/PI staining according to the manufacturer’s instructions (BD Biosciences, USA). Samples were analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h after staining. The gating strategy was as follows: debris and doublets were excluded based on FSC-A/FSC-H and SSC-A/SSC-H, and quadrants were defined using unstained and single-stained controls to identify viable (Annexin V^-/PI^-), early apoptotic (Annexin V^+/PI^-), late apoptotic (Annexin V^+/PI^+), and necrotic (Annexin V^-/PI^+) cells. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software. Total apoptosis was defined as the sum of early and late apoptotic cells.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was extracted from xenograft tumor tissues from the control (Ctrl), gefitinib monotherapy, gefitinib plus early radiotherapy (ERT), and gefitinib plus late radiotherapy (LRT) (n = 3 biological replicates per group). The gefitinib monotherapy tumors collected at the endpoint represented a resistance-associated regrowth state. The concentration of each RNA sample was measured with the NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific, USA). The quality of the samples was evaluated using an Agilent 2200 system (Agilent, USA). The preparation of the sequencing library for each RNA sample was conducted in accordance with the protocol stipulated by the manufacturer (Life Technologies, USA). The Ion Proton Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 was used for this purpose. RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis were performed by BGI Biotech (Shenzhen, China). Detailed RNA-seq quality-control metrics and sample clustering analysis are provided in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 20.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For data that met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test when appropriate. For data that did not meet these assumptions, nonparametric tests were used as appropriate, including the Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired data. Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated three times. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. All P values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001).

Results

Early radiotherapy enhances the antitumor efficacy of gefitinib in EGFR-mutant NSCLC xenograft models

To validate our previous clinical observations and explore the optimal timing of radiotherapy in vivo, we established PC9 xenograft models in nude mice. PC9 cells were subcutaneously injected into BALB/c nude mice (5 × 10^6 cells per mouse, n = 10 per group). Mice were randomized into four groups: control, gefitinib alone, gefitinib plus early radiotherapy, and gefitinib plus late radiotherapy. Early radiotherapy was delivered on day 7 after gefitinib initiation, when gefitinib-mediated tumor suppression was already evident, whereas late radiotherapy was delivered on day 14, when tumor growth inhibition had begun to diminish, consistent with resistance-associated regrowth (Fig. 1A). Gefitinib was administered at 75 mg/kg/day, and radiotherapy was delivered as a single 8 Gy dose.

B-D PC9 cells were subcutaneously injected into BALB/c nude mice, followed by randomization and the indicated treatments to establish tumors (B). Quantitative analysis of the volume (C) and weight (D) of subcutaneous tumors in each group. E Survival curves during the observation period; median survival was not reached in the early radiotherapy group. F Representative images of H&E and IHC staining for each group, as well as the relative Ki67 IHC score in tumor tissues from mice (n = 6). Ki67 scores were independently assessed by two blinded observers and averaged. Statistical analyses were performed as described in the Methods. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001 vs. control; #P < 0.05 versus gefitinib plus late radiotherapy. N = 10/group

Compared with the control group, gefitinib treatment significantly reduced both tumor growth rate and tumor weight, indicating that gefitinib effectively inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 1B-D). Both combination therapy groups exhibited lower tumor volume and tumor weight than the gefitinib monotherapy group, supporting an additional antitumor benefit of combining gefitinib with radiotherapy. At the experimental endpoint, early radiotherapy reduced tumor volume by approximately 70%-75% relative to gefitinib alone, whereas late radiotherapy reduced tumor volume by approximately 50% (Fig. 1C). Notably, the tumor-suppressive effect of the early combination group was stronger than that of the late combination group, indicating that earlier radiotherapy intervention conferred greater antitumor benefit (Fig. 1B-D). Moreover, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that mice receiving gefitinib plus early radiotherapy had improved overall survival during the observation period compared with those in the late combination group (log-rank P = 0.0301; Fig. 1E). Notably, the median survival time was not reached in the early radiotherapy group during the observation period.

Consistent with these findings, Ki67 expression was markedly lower in tumors from the early combination group than in those from the late combination group (Fig. 1F), indicating stronger suppression of tumor proliferative activity. Together, these findings indicate that radiotherapy timing is a critical determinant of combination efficacy and that earlier radiotherapy integration provides greater benefit in this model.

RNA-seq identifies c-FOS as a timing-associated candidate gene in gefitinib-resistant NSCLC tumors

To investigate the molecular basis of the timing-dependent benefit of radiotherapy, we performed RNA-seq on tumor samples collected at the experimental endpoint from the control (Ctrl), gefitinib monotherapy (Gef), gefitinib plus early radiotherapy (ERT), and gefitinib plus late radiotherapy (LRT) groups. The gefitinib monotherapy tumors collected at the endpoint represented a resistance-associated regrowth state (GR). Each group contained three biological replicates to identify differentially expressed genes and signaling pathways. Differential expression analysis between ERT and LRT identified timing-associated genes (Fig. 2A), hierarchical clustering demonstrated distinct expression patterns across groups (Fig. 2B), and the volcano plot visualized the distribution of differentially expressed genes (Fig. 2C). Principal component analysis showed clear clustering of the four experimental groups, supporting the overall quality and biological separation of the RNA-seq data (Fig. S2A). Consistently, the sample-to-sample correlation heatmap showed high overall correlation across all samples and strong consistency among biological replicates within each group (Fig. S2B). GO and KEGG analyses suggested that these genes were enriched in processes related to transcriptional regulation, biological regulation, and pathways implicated in tumorigenesis (Fig. 2D-E).

Among the differentially expressed genes, c-FOS showed a distinct expression pattern across treatment groups (Fig. 2F). RT-PCR and western blotting validated that c-FOS was increased in the gefitinib-resistant group compared with the control group and was reduced in the early radiotherapy group compared with the late radiotherapy group (Fig. 2G-H).

We next evaluated c-FOS expression in NSCLC cell models. Basal c-FOS expression, as assessed by western blotting and RT-PCR, was relatively low in gefitinib-sensitive PC9 and HCC827 cells but higher in acquired gefitinib-resistant PC9/GR cells and intrinsically resistant H1975 cells (Fig. 2I-J). These observations support c-FOS as a resistance-associated gene that is also modulated by radiotherapy timing in vivo.

Effects of c-FOS on proliferation and apoptosis in NSCLC cells

To test whether c-FOS plays a functional role in resistance-associated phenotypes, we depleted c-FOS in acquired gefitinib-resistant PC9/GR cells and intrinsically resistant H1975 cells using three independent shRNAs, and we overexpressed c-FOS in gefitinib-sensitive PC9 and HCC827 cells. RT-PCR and western blotting confirmed efficient c-FOS knockdown and overexpression (Figs. 3A-B and 4A-B).

In PC9/GR and H1975 cells, c-FOS depletion markedly inhibited proliferation (CCK-8) and reduced colony formation (Fig. 3C-D). Flow cytometry showed that apoptosis increased after c-FOS knockdown (Fig. 3E). Consistently, western blotting demonstrated increased cleaved caspase-3, increased cleaved caspase-9, and increased BAX, with reduced Bcl-2 (Fig. 3F), indicating activation of apoptotic programs.

In contrast, c-FOS overexpression in PC9 and HCC827 cells enhanced proliferation and clonogenic growth and reduced apoptosis (Fig. 4C-E). Western blotting supported these findings by showing reduced cleaved caspase-3/9 and BAX and increased Bcl-2 in c-FOS-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4F). Together, these results indicate that c-FOS promotes proliferation and suppresses apoptosis in NSCLC cell models.

Radiotherapy suppresses proliferation and promotes apoptosis in NSCLC cells in association with c-FOS inhibition

We further assessed whether radiotherapy regulates c-FOS and whether this regulation is associated with changes in proliferation and apoptosis. In acquired gefitinib-resistant PC9/GR cells and intrinsically gefitinib-resistant H1975 cells, we established the following treatment conditions: negative control, stable c-FOS knockdown, radiotherapy (RT), and c-FOS knockdown plus RT. Because sh-F#1 showed the most robust and stable knockdown efficiency while producing phenotypic effects consistent with the other validated shRNAs, it was selected for the subsequent combination experiments. Western blotting and RT-PCR showed that c-FOS knockdown significantly reduced c-FOS mRNA and protein levels; RT alone also decreased c-FOS, and the combination of c-FOS knockdown with RT produced the lowest c-FOS expression (Fig. 5A-B). Functionally, CCK-8 and colony formation assays demonstrated that RT inhibited proliferation and clonogenic growth, with the most pronounced suppression observed in the c-FOS knockdown plus RT group (Fig. 5C-D). Flow cytometry revealed that RT increased apoptosis, which was further enhanced when combined with c-FOS knockdown (Fig. 5E). Consistent with these phenotypes, western blotting showed that RT, especially when combined with c-FOS knockdown, increased cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-9, and BAX while decreasing full-length caspase-3, caspase-9, and Bcl-2 (Fig. 5F), indicating further activation of apoptotic programs.

To further validate the functional role of c-FOS in the response to RT, we generated stable c-FOS overexpression models in gefitinib-sensitive PC9 and HCC827 cells with corresponding negative controls and treated them with RT. Western blotting and RT-PCR confirmed that c-FOS overexpression markedly increased c-FOS mRNA and protein levels, whereas RT effectively reduced c-FOS expression even in the overexpression background (Fig. 6A-B). Functional assays showed that c-FOS overexpression promoted proliferation and colony formation and suppressed apoptosis, while RT significantly attenuated these pro-proliferative and anti-apoptotic effects; the lowest proliferative capacity was observed in the empty vector control plus RT group (Fig. 6C-E). Consistently, western blotting indicated that c-FOS overexpression decreased cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-9, and BAX and increased full-length caspase-3, caspase-9, and Bcl-2, whereas RT reversed these trends (Fig. 6F).

Radiotherapy attenuates gefitinib resistance by suppressing c-FOS-associated PI3K/AKT and JNK/p38 signaling

To investigate downstream signaling linked to c-FOS in acquired resistance, we examined the PI3K/AKT and JNK/p38 pathways. In gefitinib-resistant PC9/GR cells, radiotherapy reduced phosphorylation of AKT, JNK, and p38; c-FOS knockdown produced similar effects, and the combination of radiotherapy with c-FOS knockdown further reduced phosphorylation levels (Fig. 7A, C). Similar trends were observed in H1975 cells (Fig. S3A, C). In gefitinib-sensitive PC9 and HCC827 cells, c-FOS overexpression increased phosphorylation of these pathway components, while radiotherapy reversed this increase (Fig. 7B, D; Fig. S3B, D).

To test functional relevance, we treated PC9/GR cells with pathway inhibitors and measured gefitinib IC50 values using CCK‑8 assays (Fig. 7E). Co-treatment with a PI3K inhibitor (LY294002), a JNK inhibitor (SP600125), or a p38 MAPK inhibitor (SB203580) reduced the gefitinib IC50 compared with gefitinib alone, indicating that inhibition of these pathways can partially restore gefitinib sensitivity in resistant cells.

Collectively, these results suggest that c-FOS elevation in resistant cells is associated with activation of PI3K/AKT and JNK/p38 pro-survival signaling and that radiotherapy may attenuate resistance, in part, by suppressing c-FOS and downstream pathway activation.

ERK inhibition enhances the response of gefitinib-resistant cells to radiotherapy and further suppresses c-FOS

Because ERK signaling is a known upstream regulator of c-FOS induction, we examined whether ERK inhibition further suppresses c-FOS in resistant cells. Acquired gefitinib-resistant PC9/GR cells and intrinsically gefitinib-resistant H1975 cells were treated with an ERK inhibitor (SCH772984), radiotherapy, or the combination. Either ERK inhibition or radiotherapy reduced phosphorylated ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2) levels and c-FOS expression. Combined treatment further suppressed p-ERK1/2 and c-FOS (Fig. 8A-D), suggesting that radiotherapy and ERK inhibition are associated with suppression of ERK/c-FOS signaling in resistant cells.

Discussion

EGFR-TKI therapy is a cornerstone of treatment for EGFR-mutant NSCLC; however, acquired resistance remains a major obstacle to durable clinical benefit [31, 32]. In our previous phase II trial (NCT03595644), early integration of radiotherapy delayed acquired resistance to EGFR-TKI [28]. In the present study, we modeled radiotherapy timing under gefitinib treatment, identified c-FOS as a timing-associated candidate gene, and functionally linked c-FOS to resistance-associated survival signaling.

In the xenograft model, radiotherapy added to gefitinib improved tumor control compared with gefitinib monotherapy, and early radiotherapy conferred a stronger and more durable benefit than late radiotherapy. RNA-seq of xenograft tumors highlighted c-FOS as a timing-associated differentially expressed gene, and RT-PCR/western blotting validated c-FOS elevation in the resistant group and reduction in the early radiotherapy group relative to the late radiotherapy group. These findings support c-FOS as a candidate mediator linking radiotherapy timing to resistance evolution.

The proto-oncogene c-FOS functions as a transcriptional regulator and plays a pivotal role in tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Previous studies have reported that c-FOS contributes to the development of drug-resistant phenotypes in various cancers [33, 34], and its overexpression can lead to intrinsic resistance to TKIs, thereby elevating the therapeutic threshold [35]. However, the specific biological function of c-FOS in radiotherapy-mediated regulation of gefitinib resistance remains unclear. To further elucidate the role of c-FOS in EGFR-TKI resistance, we complemented the animal-model findings with additional functional experiments in vitro. By knocking down c-FOS in gefitinib-resistant cells and overexpressing it in sensitive cells, we compared the effects of its expression modulation on cellular behavior. The results revealed that radiotherapy reduced c-FOS expression and counteracted c-FOS-driven phenotypes, suggesting that c-FOS represents a mechanistically relevant node through which radiotherapy may influence tumor cell survival during EGFR-TKI treatment.

Downstream analyses suggested that c-FOS elevation is associated with activation of PI3K/AKT and JNK/p38 signaling. Pharmacologic inhibition of PI3K, JNK, or p38 reduced the gefitinib IC50 in resistant cells, supporting the functional relevance of these pathways in maintaining resistance. In addition, prior studies have shown that the ERK/c-FOS pathway can be activated to promote tumor progression and stimulate malignant biological behaviors [36–38]. Drug resistance is often associated with reactivation of the ERK signaling pathway [39–42]. Therefore, we treated gefitinib-resistant cells with an ERK inhibitor combined with radiotherapy and observed that ERK inhibition combined with radiotherapy was associated with further suppression of c-FOS expression, supporting the possibility that ERK/c-FOS signaling participates in this regulatory network.

Based on the integrated research findings, we propose the following mechanistic model (Fig. 9): Under the selective pressure of gefitinib treatment, tumor cells exhibit significant upregulation of c-FOS expression, which is associated with increased PI3K/AKT and JNK/p38 signaling activity and may contribute to the resistant phenotype. This effect may be further enhanced by ERK inhibition, which appeared to cooperate with radiotherapy to suppress c-FOS expression and increase the susceptibility of resistant cells. These findings suggest that the ERK/c-FOS axis may participate in the regulatory network through which radiotherapy modulates resistance-associated survival signaling.

Proposed schematic model of c-FOS-associated signaling in radiotherapy-related delay of acquired gefitinib resistance. High c-FOS expression in gefitinib-resistant cells is associated with sustained activation of PI3K/AKT and JNK/p38 signaling, which may promote tumor cell survival and proliferation. Radiotherapy is associated with reduced c-FOS expression and suppression of downstream survival signaling, and ERK inhibition may further enhance this effect

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the subcutaneous xenograft model, although convenient for serial tumor measurement, does not fully recapitulate the native thoracic microenvironment of lung cancer; therefore, the observed radiobiological effects should therefore be extrapolated with caution. Second, the present study compared radiotherapy delivered on day 7 versus day 14 only, which supports a timing effect but does not define the precise biological window of maximal benefit. Third, although our data support a functional role for c‑FOS, we did not perform ChIP assays to confirm direct binding of c‑FOS to specific downstream target promoters; future ChIP studies could further identify the direct transcriptional targets of c‑FOS, complementing the current mechanistic model derived from transcriptomic and functional analyses. Fourth, we did not conduct time‑series transcriptomic profiling after radiotherapy; thus, dynamic changes in c‑FOS and its downstream network during acute radiation stress and chronic resistance remodeling remain unresolved. Fifth, we did not examine canonical DNA damage response and repair pathways, such as γH2AX, ATM/ATR, CHK1/2, or RAD51, and therefore cannot determine whether adaptive reactivation of DNA repair contributes to the observed effects. Sixth, the extent of c‑FOS suppression by radiotherapy differed between in vivo and in vitro settings. This discrepancy is biologically plausible and may reflect the influence of stromal components, vascular supply, hypoxia, inflammatory signals, and more complex spatiotemporal heterogeneity in vivo, all of which are absent in monolayer cell culture. Seventh, because PI3K/AKT and JNK/p38 are components of broadly conserved stress‑response networks, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that part of the radiotherapy effect reflects a non-specific cellular stress response rather than exclusively c‑FOS‑specific regulation. Eighth, the lack of PDX models, clinical tissue validation, and comprehensive in vivo toxicity assessment limits the immediate translational generalizability of our findings.

From a translational perspective, our findings suggest that c-FOS may represent a candidate biomarker linked to resistance evolution and radiotherapy timing during gefitinib treatment. However, clinical validation using patient specimens will be necessary before c-FOS can be considered for treatment stratification or therapeutic targeting in practice.

Conclusions

In EGFR-sensitive NSCLC with exon 19 deletion treated with the first-generation EGFR-TKI gefitinib, our findings identify c-FOS as a timing-associated candidate mediator of radiotherapy benefit. Early radiotherapy was associated with reduced c-FOS expression and suppression of PI3K/AKT and JNK/p38 survival signaling, accompanied by delayed resistance emergence in our model system. These data support c-FOS as a candidate biomarker for radiotherapy timing and as a hypothesis-generating therapeutic target in EGFR-mutant NSCLC treated with first-generation EGFR-TKIs, although further validation is required. However, validation in additional EGFR-mutant subtypes, later-generation EGFR-TKI settings, and more clinically relevant models is required before any broader translational inference can be made.
