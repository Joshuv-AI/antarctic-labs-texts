Utility of Duplex Ultrasound in the Diagnosis and Treatment of Functional Popliteal Artery Entrapment Syndrome
Authors: Naoya Kuriyama, Shinsuke Kikuchi, Yuki Hashimoto, Tsutomu Doita, Keisuke Kamada, Nobuyoshi Azuma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 19-year-old female presented with intermittent claudication without anatomical abnormality in the popliteal fossa on magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography. However, duplex ultrasound (DUS) showed compression of the popliteal artery (PA) and vein during plantarflexion and dorsiflexion. She was diagnosed with functional PA entrapment syndrome (PAES) and underwent resection of the plantaris and gastrocnemius muscles using DUS with stress maneuvers, which relieved the symptoms. In physically active adults, functional PAES can develop without anatomical abnormality. Thus, in the field of vascular medicine, it is important to consider this underrecognized pathophysiology among young people with lower leg pain.

Introduction

Popliteal artery entrapment syndrome (PAES) is a relatively rare condition affecting young people that involves compression of the popliteal artery (PA) and is often caused by anomalous myotendinous structures in the popliteal fossa.1) Although PAES has no specific characteristic symptoms, the most common symptom is intermittent claudication. Sometimes, a decrease in the ankle-brachial index (ABI) may be noted, but it is not specific.1,2) PAES is diagnosed by the presence of abnormalities in the PA and gastrocnemius muscle (GM) on duplex ultrasound (DUS), contrast computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and/or angiography, but a definite diagnosis is often difficult.2) Here, we report a case of type VI PAES, also known as functional PAES (FPAES), which is a rare subtype that does not involve any anatomical abnormalities.2) Here, we report a case of type VI PAES, also known as FPAES.1)

Case Report

The patient was a 19-year-old woman who had experienced bilateral calf pain during exercise beginning at 9 years of age. She had consulted a local physician at 17 years of age due to worsening intermittent claudication. She was unable to pursue further education or employment because claudication symptoms appeared after walking for about 5 minutes. The patient had been performing ballet. Enhanced CT and MRI were performed at the previous hospital (Figs. 1A–1D). However, no obvious vascular lesions or anomalous muscle bundles were detected. The patient had been prescribed a prostacyclin derivative and a calcium channel blocker, but her symptoms did not improve. Therefore, the patient was referred to our department for further evaluation.

High-pitched sounds were heard when a Doppler stethoscope was placed at the bilateral popliteal fossa. Upon reviewing the previous MRI, we observed that both plantaris muscles (PMs) were located higher than expected and that the medial head of the right GM was hypertrophied compared to the left (Fig. 1D). Compression of the bilateral PAs and popliteal veins (PVs) was observed on DUS during dorsiflexion and plantarflexion of the ankle (Figs. 1E–1H). During plantarflexion, the peak systolic velocity ratio (PSVR) was 4.3 on the right and 2.5 on the left. Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with bilateral type VI PAES.

We performed surgery on the left leg. We made an S-shaped skin incision to expose the PA and PV through a posterior approach. Compression of the PA and PV by the PM during plantarflexion was confirmed, and the PM was severed (Fig. 2A). Postoperatively, rehabilitation was conducted, but claudication remained after walking for 5 minutes. Postoperative DUS revealed an increase in PSV during plantarflexion (Fig. 2B), which led us to perform the secondary surgery. Preoperative DUS showed that the PA and PV were being compressed by the remaining PM and the lateral head of the GM (Figs. 2C and 2D). Thus, we decided to resect these muscles. In the second surgery, which was performed 7 months after the first surgery, we determined the extent of resection for the lateral head of the GM while performing intraoperative DUS.

Specifically, we first identified the compression sites of the PA and PV using preoperative DUS and reduced the muscle volume of GM. Subsequently, we utilized intraoperative ultrasound to reconfirm the compression sites of the vessels during dorsiflexion and plantarflexion. If compression remained, we performed additional volume reduction and concluded the surgery when there was no residual compression during dorsiflexion and plantarflexion (Fig. 2E). After this second surgery, the symptoms completely disappeared; postoperative DUS showed no compression of the PA during plantarflexion (Fig. 2F).

Two months after the first left leg surgery, the right leg surgery was performed. The PA was found to be compressed by the PM during dorsiflexion. Although the PM was severed, the claudication and compression of popliteal vessels were not relieved. In a second surgery, the remaining PM and tissue around the PA and PV were completely removed because the postoperative DUS after the first surgery showed that these tissues still compressed the popliteal vessels during dorsiflexion and plantarflexion. Unfortunately, the patient’s symptoms persisted. DUS revealed that the PA and PV were compressed between the medial head of the GM and the femur during hyperextension of the knee and dorsiflexion or plantarflexion (Fig. 3A). Retrospectively, although the medial head of the right GM was anatomically normal, it was more well developed than the left (Fig. 1D). In a third surgery, we reduced the volume of the medial head of the right GM at the compression site while using intraoperative DUS to evaluate whether or not our surgical procedures were releasing the compression of the popliteal vessels (Figs. 3A and 3B). Finally, using intraoperative DUS, we confirmed that the compression was relieved during dorsiflexion and plantarflexion. Postoperative DUS showed that compression of the PA had disappeared (Figs. 3C and 3D). The patient’s symptoms of intermittent claudication have completely improved, and she has been able to carry out daily activities without any issues.

Discussion

PAES is classified into six types (I–VI) based on the relationship between the GM and the PA.1,2) Types I–V are associated with anatomical abnormalities in the popliteal fossa. By contrast, type VI is a functional type of entrapment with no anatomical abnormalities; thus, it is also called FPAES.1) FPAES has been reported to be more common in women who are highly active in sports and often occurs bilaterally.3–5) A Japanese study reported no cases of type VI PAES.1) However, Sinha et al. reported that approximately 23% of PAES cases were FPAES.2) The prevalence in Japan may be higher than previously recognized. Although an ethnic difference in muscular mass may exist, Fujimura et al. reported that when Japanese institutes were asked about FPAES, more than half of them stated that they did not understand it.1) This suggests that FPAES is underrecognized.

Diagnosis of FPAES is challenging because the vessel compression may not be apparent in a resting position, and the symptoms may be nonspecific or intermittent.6) Therefore, it is important to perform dynamic imaging tests, such as DUS or Doppler stethoscope, to detect compression during active or passive movements of the knee and ankle.1,6) Furthermore, previous reports suggested that FPAES is caused by a hypertrophied GM and an aberrant PM,7,8) which is located higher and more medially. Thus, it could be important to confirm their presence through an MRI.

Myotomy is the most common treatment for FPAES. Among patients who underwent myotomy for FPAES, 76%–86% reported symptom improvement, and 3%–8% had symptom recurrence.4) Surgical treatment of PAES typically involves exposure of the popliteal fossa and release of the neurovascular bundle through resection of the hypertrophied gastrocnemius, soleus, and PMs.9) However, there is no consensus on surgical treatment of FPAES. A previous report suggested that the use of intraoperative DUS to determine the amount of resection from the medial head of the GM was useful, as was performed in the present case.9) Thus, we believe it is desirable to perform muscle resection and determine the extent of resection according to the following steps: First, identify the sites of vascular compression and the responsible muscles preoperatively. Then, perform muscle resection and volume reduction around the affected area, and finally confirm that there is no residual compression. In the treatment of FPAES, detailed patient information, including physical activity and the use of pre-, intra-, and post-operative DUS with stress maneuvers, could improve outcomes.

Conclusion

We surgically treated bilateral FPAES, which is a rare but likely underdiagnosed condition, in Japan. Myotomy of the plantaris, soleus, and GM has achieved favorable outcomes. Confirming residual popliteal stenosis with DUS during dorsiflexion and plantarflexion is vital before ending surgery.
