Clinical characteristics of acute lower respiratory tract infection caused by Omicron, respiratory syncytial virus, and influenza virus a in children in Chengdu: a retrospective study
Authors: Mingjia Chen, Lei Zhang, Hao Dong, Shuai Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the differences in clinical features of acute lower respiratory tract infection in children caused by suspected Omicron, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and influenza virus A (Flu A).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 241 hospitalized children with lower respiratory tract infections due to suspected Omicron, RSV, or Flu A at Chengdu Women and Children’s Central Hospital from December 9, 2022 to August 1, 2023. Differences in age, sex, clinical characteristics, laboratory results, imaging findings, and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO) results between the three viral infections were analyzed.ResultsPatients infected with suspected Omicron were predominantly infants and toddlers; in contrast, those infected with Flu A were mostly preschool children. Fever was most common in Flu A-infected patients, followed by RSV-infected and Omicron-infected patients. Inspiratory crackles in the lungs were most frequently observed in suspected Omicron-infected patients, whereas wheezing was more common in RSV-infected patients. No wheezing was observed in the lungs of any suspected Omicron-infected patients. Patients with both suspected Omicron and RSV infections had longer hospital stays. Notably, Flu A infection significantly decreased white blood cell count and increased neutrophil percentage (Neu%) compared with the other two infections. Furthermore, patients with suspected Omicron infection exhibited a pronounced reduction in Neu%; however, the decrease in lymphocyte percentage (Lym%) was considerable in all groups, with the Flu A-infected group showing the greatest decline. C-reactive protein levels were markedly higher in the RSV- and Flu A-infected groups. Liver function was significantly affected in suspected Omicron-infected patients, who were also more likely to have Mycoplasma pneumoniae or bacterial infections. Pulmonary consolidation was more common in suspected Omicron-infected patients, and they had the highest positive rate of FENO.ConclusionsThe clinical manifestations of suspected Omicron infection in patients with acute lower respiratory tract infections are similar to those of Flu A and RSV infections. However, these infections exhibit more severe liver function damage, lung consolidation, and airway inflammation. Paying attention to respiratory tract infections caused by the suspected Omicron virus in children is imperative to ensure timely and appropriate diagnosis and treatment. Promoting child vaccination remains crucial for safeguarding susceptible populations and protecting children’s health.Keywords: Acute lower respiratory tract infection, Omicron, Influenza virus, Respiratory syncytial virus

Background

Acute lower respiratory tract infection (ALRTI) is a major cause of death in children under 5 years of age worldwide, especially in developing countries. Approximately two million children die from ALRTI yearly [1], and respiratory viruses are the main microorganisms in children with lower respiratory tract infections. Influenza virus (Flu) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) are the major causes of ALRTI, which cause significant morbidity and mortality [2, 3]. In 2019, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) emerged worldwide and continues circulating. Since the beginning of the epidemic, SARS-CoV-2 has evolved and mutated continuously from Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron strains with different virulence and transmissibility [4]. SARS-CoV-2 will most likely coexist with humans for a long time. Therefore, the impact of SARS-CoV-2 on ALRTI in children cannot be ignored in the future. On December 13, 2022, Chengdu officially launched epidemic control according to China’s policy. Omicron was the main epidemic strain, with remarkable characteristics of strong transmissibility, fast transmission speed, and immune escape [5]. Thus, it has caused widespread infection among children in the Chengdu area [6]. Previous pandemic measures to slow the spread of SARS-CoV-2 have indirectly affected seasonal respiratory pathogen infections in children, most notably the RSV and Flu [7], excluding rhinoviruses (Avs) [8].

The initial symptoms of lower respiratory tract infections caused by Omicron, RSV, and Flu A are similar. Only a few studies have compared the clinical characteristics of the three viruses. Considering the unknown and fear of SARS-CoV-2, as well as the long-term and co-existence of the fight against SARS-CoV-2, it is necessary to analyze the characteristics of lower respiratory tract infections in children caused by the three viruses in the region; comparative analysis is expected to provide more reference for medical staff and provide a basis for formulating prevention and treatment strategies and reasonable treatment.

Methods

Study design and setting

Children with ALRTI treated in the inpatient department of Chengdu Women and Children’s Central Hospital from December 13, 2022 to August 1, 2023, were selected as the research participants. Exclusion criteria: (1) neonates; (2) patients with kidney disease, hematologic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, immunodeficiency, or malignancies; (3) patients who had received antiviral medications within the preceding week; and (4) patients with incomplete clinical data. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Women and Children’s Central Hospital (approval 2023[113]), and informed consent was obtained from the minors’ legal guardians before the study’s commencement. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and kept all patient data confidential. The diagnostic criteria for ALRTIs were acute respiratory tract infections caused by infectious pathogens, such as bacteria and viruses, that caused acute inflammation in any part of the respiratory system. Anatomically, they were divided into upper and lower respiratory tract infections [9].

All children collected nasopharyngeal swab samples for pathogen detection within 24 h after admission. SARS-CoV-2, RSV, Flu A, Flu Bwere detected by the polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-fluorescent probe method. For other potential pathogens (such as mycoplasma pneumoniae, adenovirus, etc.), the corresponding detection methods were selected according to the specific situation. And the coinfection Mycoplasma, coinfection bacterium within three groups were prsented with n %.

Chest X-ray examinations including the location, distribution, morphology of the lesions were conducted using a Toshiba and Philips Healthcare 64-CT scanner (scanning tube voltage:110 kv, reference tube current: 100 mAs, layer thickness: 2.5 mm, maximum scanning layer thickness: 10 mm, interval: 2.5 mm, scanning range: from the thorax entrance to the lung bottom), assessed by two senior radiologists. For children < 4 years old, they were determined at the sedation state to reduce artifacts when applying radiation protection.

And the first peripheral blood routine was performed, and the reference values were set according to different age groups: reference intervals of blood cell analysis for children (WS/T 779–2021) [10].

Data on admission and discharge time, age (Infants and toddlers, < 3 years; preschool children, ≥ 3–5 years; and school children, ≥ 5–7 years), sex, clinical signs and symptoms, previous eczema, allergy history (yes, no), respiratory virus in throat swabs, peripheral blood white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO), chest radiography, and other examination results were collected from the electronic information system.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). The measurement data per normal distribution were expressed as xˉ±s, and the comparison between groups was analyzed using t-test and analysis of variance. Non-normal distribution measurement data were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)]. Kruskal–Wallis H rank sum test was used to compare multiple groups, and Dunn’s multiple comparison method was used for pairwise comparison between groups. Count data were expressed as frequencies, rates, or constituent ratios (%). The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method was used for comparisons between groups, and the Bonferroni correction test was used for pairwise comparisons between groups. All statistical tests were two-sided, and results associated with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 241 cases were retrospectively analyzed. Based on the pathogen detection results, the patients were divided into three groups: Suspected Omicron (61 cases), RSV (90 cases), and Flu A (90 cases) (no patient was infected with three or two viruses simultaneously). The study included 151 males (62.66%) and 90 females (37.34%). The youngest patient was 1-month old, and the oldest was 7-years-old. There were 114 infants and toddlers (47.3%), 81 preschool children (33.60%), and 46 school-age children (19.10%). There was no sex differences between the patients infected with any of the three viruses (P > 0.05). The suspected Omicron group had the highest number of infants and toddlers; in contrast, the Flu A group had the highest number of preschool children. Age differences among the three groups were significant (P < 0.05, Table 1). Cough and/or fever were the main clinical manifestations in all patients; cough accounted for 100% (241 cases), and fever accounted for 63.07% (152 cases). There were 89 (36.93%) patients with inspiratory crackles and 13 (5.39%) with wheezing. Patients with Flu A infection had the highest fever rate, followed by those with RSV infection; in contrast, those with suspected Omicron infection had the lowest number of fevers. The differences between the three groups were significant (P < 0.001). Patients with suspected Omicron infection had the most inspiratory crackles, and the difference among the three groups was significant (P < 0.05); there was no wheezing in the lungs of the suspected Omicron infection group (P < 0.05). The hospital stay was from 3 to 13 days, with an average of 6.06 ± 1.49 days in all patients. The hospital stay in the patients with suspected Omicron infection was 3–10 days, with a median of 7 days. The hospital stay in the patients with RSV was 3–13 days, and the median was 6 days. In the Flu A group, the shortest and longest hospital stay were 4 and 11 days, respectively, with a median of 5 days. There were significant differences among the three groups (P < 0.05). However, there was no significant difference in the hospital stay between the suspected Omicron infection and RSV groups (H = 16.225, P = 0.44, Table 2).

Discussion

A high incidence of Omicron infection was observed in infants and toddlers in Chengdu area, a big city in western China, in accordance with the literature [11]. Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) vaccination has been reported to effectively reduce the infection rate of Omicron [12–14]. We performed an analysis for surveillance of respiratory viruses at our hospital before and after preventing and controlling COVID-19. Apart from AV, adenovirus (ADV), and influenza B virus (Flu B), the number of RSV and influenza A (Flu A) co-infected patients increased sharply after the prevention and control of the epidemic was fully implemented (Fig. 1) consistent with the literature reports [15]; however, it was different from previous epidemic seasons [16, 17]. Based on respiratory virus surveillance data, this study focused exclusively on tracking the epidemiological trends of single pathogens, without systematic screening for co-infections (e.g., limited application of multiplex PCR testing). This approach differs from certain large cohort studies that reported high co-infection rates (e.g., 18.8% of cases involving multiple pathogens) [18, 19]. However, the grouping criteria adopted in this study ensured analytical consistency in evaluating changes in individual viruses, allowing for dynamic assessment of common respiratory viruses (e.g., RSV and influenza A) before and after epidemic periods.

Analysis of respiratory virus monitoring before and after epidemic prevention and control

The high incidence of Omicron infection may be related to children under 3 years of age not being vaccinated against COVID-19. Infants and toddlers should be considered the key population for preventing and controlling pediatric Omicron strain infections, and the protective measures should be strengthened. Simultaneous administration of two doses of the vaccine in children older than 3 years is necessary to prevent Omicron infection. In the past, RSV was more likely to infect children under 2 years; however, this study showed that the infection rate of RSV in preschool children was not different from that in infants and toddlers. It has been reported in the literature that the genetic diversity of RSV was greatly reduced after the emergence of COVID-19. The number of cases of ALRTIs in children has surged [20]. Whether SARS-CoV-2 infection affects the host selection of RSV or other respiratory viruses needs further investigation. The Omicron strain was found to replicate more rapidly in the human bronchus but more slowly in lung tissue than earlier variants [21]. Literature has shown that SARS-CoV-2 requires the help of transmembrane protease serine 2 (TMPRSS2) and cathepsin L to enter host cells. Previous virus strains mainly rely on the TMPRSS2-mediated membrane fusion pathway; in contrast, Omicron strains prefer the cathepsin L-mediated endocytosis pathway to invade host cells, and it is difficult to induce syncytium formation. At the same time, TMPRSS2 is highly expressed in the lower respiratory and gastrointestinal tract and is relatively low in the upper respiratory tract; therefore, the utilization of TMPRSS2 by the Omicron strain is low, which reduces its pulmonary infection ability and pathogenicity [22]. In line with the results of our study, the suspected Omicron infection was characterized by fever and cough; however, the clinical manifestations were relatively mild, and the incidence of fever was the lowest compared with RSV and Flu A infections. Inspiratory crackles in the lungs are the most common lower respiratory tract infections caused by Omicron, which may be related to the finding in this study that patients with Omicron infections are more likely to have bacterial infections. There was no wheezing in the lungs of patients with Omicron infection, consistent with the literature report that wheezing was rare in patients with Omicron infection [23–25]. In conclusion, the symptoms are not severe in suspected Omicron infection, the course of the disease is short, and the length of hospital stay is similar to that in RSV infection.

This study used the normal reference ranges of different ages to determine abnormal blood cells. Leukopenia was most obvious after Flu A infection, and only one patient with suspected Omicron infection presented with leukopenia. Neu% increased, and Lym% decreased most frequently in Flu A-infected patients, followed by RSV-infected and suspected Omicron-infected patients. Some studies have found that the increased proportion of neutrophils and decreased proportion of lymphocytes are significantly positively correlated with the severity and death caused by COVID-19 [26, 27]. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio is an essential predictor of the severity of COVID-19. The decrease or depletion of lymphocytes in patients with severe COVID-19 may be due to viral attack and destruction of target cells, which activate immune cells, cause systemic inflammation, stimulate the production of neutrophils, and accelerate the apoptosis of lymphocytes [28]. However, this study suggested that the abnormality of the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio in suspected Omicron-infected patients was much less than that in Flu A-infected patients, suggesting that the immune damage caused by suspected Omicron may be lower than that caused by Flu A and RSV. This further proved that the virulence of the Omicron strain was lower than that of the former variant strain.

The abnormal increase in PCT and CRP levels may be related to coinfection with underlying bacteria or superinfection [29]. The abnormal rate of CRP in the suspected Omicron infection group was lower than those in the Flu A and RSV infection groups, and there was no significant difference in PCT among the three groups. However, patients with suspected Omicron infections were more likely to be coinfected with bacteria and Mycoplasma pneumoniae. Considering the early detection of blood indicators, there may be a window of response, which suggests that the possibility of combined infection with other microorganisms should not be ignored because of the normal levels of PCT and CRP in patients with suspected Omicroninfection. At the same time, hepatic function damage in patients with suspected Omicron infection was higher than that in the other two groups, and the treatment for organ protection should be strengthened.

On pulmonary imaging, speckle-floc shadows were common among all groups; however, pulmonary consolidation was more prevalent in the suspected Omicron infection group. This suggests the potential utility of X-ray examination for distinguishing between infections caused by these three viruses. FENO has been widely accepted as a noninvasive biomarker of pulmonary inflammation and oxidative stress [30]. In Flu A-infected patients, FENO decreased at the onset of symptoms but increased after 8 days [31]. FENO levels decreased in RSV-infected patients but increased after 3 months. These results suggest that RSV inhibits nitric oxide (NO) production in the acute phase and that eosinophils dominate the recovery phase [32]. There was no difference in FENO between the COVID-19-infected population and healthy individuals during the non-epidemic period of Omicron [33]. In contrast, our study found that atopy was low in suspected Omicron-infected patients; however, FENO abnormalities were significantly higher in RSV-infected patients than in Flu A-infected patients, consistent with previous reports [34]. SARS-CoV-2 promotes neutrophil activation and interferon γproduction [35]. Both these factors may alter NO production during disease progression. It is suspected that the suspected Omicronstrain mainly infects upper respiratory epithelial cells (especially nasal epithelial cells) compared to other variants and thus significantly affects FENO. However, whether FENO can be used as a clinical indicator to distinguish Omicron infections from RSV and Flu A infections requires further study with a larger sample size.

This study had some limitations. First, we classified the patients infected with the suspected Omicron strain based on epidemiological data only and did not test the virus strain directly; however, this analysis method is relatively reliable given the country’s careful monitoring of the transmission of SARS-CoV-2. Second, children with ALRTIs who were not included in the outpatient clinic were subject to a selection bias. Third, this was a single-center retrospective study with a small sample siIn the future, larger research samples from multiple centers will be required to improve the external validity of the findings. Forth, the FeNO level is indeed influenced by age. However, in the present study, the limited sample size (n = 166 children) may have restricted the feasibility of conducting age-stratified analyses or regression adjustments, as smaller sample sizes are prone to introducing bias in stratified analyses or compromising the reliability of negative results (e.g., failure to detect significant interactions or generalize findings). Moreover, high rates of co-infection in the suspected Omicron group may affect the results. We currently conducting a prospective study with a larger sample size and longer follow-up duration to obtain more precise conclusions.

The lack of stratified analysis regarding the high co-infection rates in the Omicron-suspected cohort (e.g., 39.3% Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection rate, 32.8% bacterial infection rate) may affect the causal interpretation of our results. Existing literature has clearly demonstrated that the Omicron variant itself significantly increases the risk of co-infections: multiple studies have reported a co-infection rate of 7.9% (737/9,274), which is markedly higher than that of other variants such as Delta (3.2%) (risk ratio 1.88, 95% CI 1.53–2.32) [36]. This phenomenon may stem from the immune evasion properties of Omicron, leading to increased host susceptibility to co-pathogens [37]. Furthermore, co-infections substantially exacerbate clinical severity—patients with Omicron co-infections exhibit an ICU admission rate as high as 15.38%, significantly higher than those with single infections (1.64%) [38]. Therefore, the observed differences in clinical indicators (e.g., decreased lymphocyte percentage, abnormal liver function) are more likely to reflect the synergistic effects of Omicron infection and co-infections rather than a singular etiological effect.

Conclusions

This study provides crucial insights into the clinical characteristics of children’s lower respiratory tract infections caused by suspected Omicron, RSV, and influenza virus A. Understanding these differences is essential for healthcare professionals to ensure accurate diagnoses and effective prevention and treatment strategies. Moreover, FENO may be a potential diagnostic tool for distinguishing viral infections. Finally, the importance of child vaccination in safeguarding health cannot be underestimated.
