Reagentless Real‐Time ATP Monitoring with New DNA Aptamers
Authors: Adara Bacon, Obtin Alkhamis, Caleb Byrd, Jacob Perry, Juan Canoura, Yi Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdenosine triphosphate (ATP) is essential to numerous biological processes, and there is considerable interest in methods for measuring this molecule in real time. Current ATP sensors have fundamental shortcomings that limit their utility, such as poor specificity, the need for exogenous reagents, or assay complexity. Aptamer‐based biosensors have shown great promise for ATP detection, but existing aptamers have modest affinity and poor specificity that limit their practical utility. Here, the systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) process is used to discover new DNA aptamers that bind ATP with unprecedentedly high affinity and specificity. The best‐performance aptamer, ATP18‐13, displays at least tenfold greater affinity for ATP relative to previously reported aptamers, with far superior capacity to discriminate against analogs including adenosine diphosphate (ADP), adenosine monophosphate (AMP), and adenosine. ATP18‐13 is used to develop a fluorescent beacon sensor that achieves a limit of detection of 125 nm ATP in buffer and 1 µm ATP in cell culture medium. The capability of this sensor is demonstrated to achieve real‐time, seconds‐scale monitoring of deoxyATP consumption during DNA polymerase‐mediated template extension. The aptamers and sensors developed here can thus prove useful for monitoring ATP in a variety of biological contexts.

Introduction

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is critical for life, playing roles in energy regulation and metabolism,[ 1 ] neurotransmission,[ 2 ] cell signaling,[ 3 ] enzymatic reactions,[ 4 ] and molecular transport.[ 5 ] Accordingly, quantifying ATP concentrations is useful for applications ranging from environmental monitoring[ 6 , 7 ] to diagnostics[ 8 , 9 ] and biomedical research.[ 10 ] The concentration of ATP constantly changes in biological systems, with a dynamic range spanning from nanomolar to millimolar concentrations depending on the location and context.[ 3 , 11 , 12 ] Several methods have been developed for monitoring ATP levels in real time. For example, ATP can be electrochemically detected with sub‐second temporal resolution based on its oxidation at high voltages.[ 13 ] Others have used synthetic small‐molecule receptors to optically sense ATP in a reagentless manner. For example, Xu et al. designed a benzene‐bridged imidazolium probe containing a pyrene excimer that detects ATP through binding‐induced disruption of the excimer, which results in the recovery of monomer fluorescence.[ 14 ] These methods are simple, but face difficulty in differentiating ATP from analogs such as adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and other substances. More specific bioreceptor‐based methods have also been described. For example, the enzyme luciferase catalyzes the ATP‐mediated conversion of the substrate luciferin into oxyluciferin, yielding a bioluminescent readout that is dependent on ATP concentration. Luciferase assays are highly sensitive, but saturate at nanomolar ATP concentrations, which makes them unsuitable for use in many biological contexts. Additionally, when ATP sensing is performed in vivo, the need to deliver exogenous reagents introduces complications including heterogeneous substrate distribution and cell toxicity.[ 15 , 16 ] Genetically‐encoded biosensors offer another means for the specific monitoring of ATP in real‐time, wherein cells are genetically modified to express artificial fluorescent protein biosensors that contain an ATP‐binding domain and a signaling domain, which typically consists of a green fluorescent protein (GFP) derivative.[ 16 ] The binding of ATP to the protein biosensor triggers a conformational change that either increases GFP fluorescence or produces a readout via Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET).[ 17 ] A variety of such biosensors systems have been developed for ATP, where the highest‐affinity biosensor reported to date has a K D of 4 µm for ATP with tenfold lower sensitivity to ADP.[ 18 ] However, the genetic modification process can be laborious and costly, and may also require extensive optimization to achieve reliable protein expression.

Aptamers represent a promising alternative bioreceptor for real‐time ATP monitoring. These are short oligonucleotides that bind to specific molecules with high affinity, which are discovered from random libraries through in vitro methods such as systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX).[ 19 , 20 ] Aptamers can be selected or engineered to undergo a reversible target‐binding‐induced conformational change, which makes them valuable biorecognition elements for continuous, reagentless real‐time sensors. For instance, prior work has demonstrated reversible electrochemical and optical aptamer‐based sensors that enable continuous monitoring of specific molecular targets in complex matrices,[ 21 ] and even in vivo.[ 22 ] However, the development of continuous ATP sensors has been impeded by the lack of aptamers with sufficiently high affinity and specificity for ATP. Numerous selections have been performed to isolate aptamers for ATP in the past three decades. The first RNA and DNA aptamers for ATP were both discovered by the Szostak group nearly thirty years ago.[ 23 , 24 ] These aptamers are short (<30 bases) and feature simple binding motifs that primarily engage ATP via interactions with the adenine nucleobase.[ 25 ] However, both aptamers bind ATP with relatively low (micromolar) affinity and bind to related analogs with similar affinity including ADP, adenosine monophosphate (AMP), and adenosine. Various groups have subsequently performed SELEX experiments to discover new ATP aptamers with improved binding properties, but most of these led to the rediscovery of the same aptamers first reported by the Szostak group,[ 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] or resulted in aptamers with similar or poorer affinity and specificity.[ 30 , 31 , 32 ] Notably, the Szostak[ 33 ] and Zheng[ 34 ] groups have both discovered ATP aptamers with greatly improved specificity against ADP, AMP, and adenosine, but the ATP‐binding affinity of these aptamers remains low (K D = 11 and 26 µm, respectively). More recently, the Liu group performed SELEX to isolate aptamers for ATP, but these preferentially bind adenosine over ATP and slightly cross‐react to ADP.[ 35 ]

Here, we address the need for high‐quality ATP aptamers and reagentless real‐time ATP sensors by performing library‐immobilized SELEX to isolate new DNA aptamers with unprecedentedly high affinity and specificity for this target. Our selections here were guided and optimized based on previous failures to isolate aptamers for ATP by both our group and others. Using high‐stringency selection protocols, we identified aptamers with nanomolar affinity for ATP in our first trial of selection. However, these aptamers had undesirable cross‐reactivity toward analogs such as ADP, AMP, and adenosine. In a second SELEX trial, we performed more stringent counter‐selection against ADP to select for more specific aptamers. The best of these aptamers had a K D of 700 nm for ATP, with similar affinity to deoxy ATP (dATP) and one to two orders of magnitude lower affinity for adenosine analogs. To demonstrate the utility of our aptamer, we employed it in a molecular beacon‐based sensor format to monitor dynamic real‐time changes in dATP concentration that occur during an oligonucleotide extension reaction by the Klenow fragment DNA polymerase. This sensor enabled highly sensitive and specific monitoring of dATP depletion and replenishment at second‐scale resolution, even in a complex biological environment. Thus, the high‐quality aptamers reported here will prove valuable for ATP monitoring in a variety of applications.

Results and Discussion

The First Selection Trial

Aptamers for ATP have been discovered from RNA, DNA, and modified nucleotide libraries using both target‐ and library‐immobilized SELEX approaches. We opted to use DNA aptamers due to their convenience, and library‐immobilized SELEX because it allows us to present the target in its free, unmodified form, permitting maximal interaction between aptamer and target. We used a 73‐nucleotide (nt) DNA library containing an 8‐base‐pair (bp) stem, 30‐nt randomized loop serving as the putative binding domain, and flanking PCR primer sites (Figure 1A). We chose this library design since it has successfully yielded high‐performance aptamers for a variety of small‐molecule targets.[ 36 , 37 ] For SELEX, the library was first hybridized with a complementary DNA (cDNA) containing a 5′ biotin tag, which enables the immobilization of the library‐cDNA duplex onto streptavidin‐coated agarose resin. Aptamers capable of binding the target dissociate from the cDNA and are collected, amplified by PCR, and subjected to another round of selection (Figure 1B). To select for aptamers that can be used in biological systems, we buffered the solution to physiological pH with 20 mm Tris (pH 7.4) and used physiologically relevant levels of electrolytes (140 mm NaCl, 4 mm KCl, 2 mm MgCl2). Also keeping biological applications in mind, we performed counter‐SELEX to remove binders to endogenous molecules that co‐exist with ATP (norepinephrine, L‐glutamate, acetylcholine, γ‐aminobutyric acid, dopamine, serotonin, L‐phenylalanine, histamine, L‐tryptophan, L‐glutamine), nucleotides/nucleosides and purines due to their structural similarity to ATP (cytidine, cytidine 5′‐triphosphate (CTP), uridine, uridine‐5′‐triphosphate (UTP), hypoxanthine, caffeine, inosine, inosine‐5′‐triphosphate (ITP), guanosine‐5′‐triphosphate (GTP), and deoxyguanosine 5′‐triphosphate (dGTP)), as well as molecules that bind to three‐way junction‐structured oligonucleotides, which are known to have poor specificity (using benzocaine, diphenhydramine, procaine, and lidocaine) (Figure S1, Supporting Information). We began counter‐SELEX starting in Round 2 with the endogenous molecules and three‐way junction ligands, and then incorporated the nucleotides and nucleosides in later selection rounds once the pool was more enriched.

Previous selections for ATP aptamers have generally used relatively high target concentrations (i.e., in the millimolar range),[ 23 , 24 , 29 ] and we hypothesized that this led to the isolation of aptamers with low to moderate ATP affinity. Recently, we have demonstrated that beginning SELEX with relatively high target concentrations (e.g., 1 mm) can lead to the enrichment, dominance, and persistence of low‐affinity aptamers (e.g., K D > 10 µm).[ 38 ] By using lower target concentrations in the initial rounds of SELEX, the enrichment of such low‐affinity sequences can be largely prevented, and the enrichment of high‐affinity aptamers (K D < 1 µm) becomes much more feasible. We therefore performed the first round of SELEX using 200 µm ATP. In Rounds 1–4, we did not observe any meaningful difference in the quantity of library eluted by each wash with ATP relative to the last of several buffer washes performed prior to target addition. In Round 5, however, we noted a slight increase in the target‐eluted pool (≈0.21%) compared to the last wash with buffer (0.17%). Despite halving the concentration of ATP to 100 µm in Round 6, pool elution by ATP rose to 1.7%, or about five fold greater than pool elution by buffer alone, indicating that aptamers for ATP were being enriched. In Rounds 7 and 8, using 50 µm ATP, pool elution rose to ≈2.5%, which was ≈5‐fold above pool elution by buffer alone. Remarkably, we did not see meaningful elution by the counter‐selection molecules. In Round 9, when we reduced ATP to 25 µm, pool elution decreased slightly to 1.6%, still well above background buffer elution levels. However, we also observed a sudden surge in pool elution by the counter‐selection molecule caffeine, with 6% elution. In Rounds 10 and 11, we obtained relatively low pool elution (0.6%) with 15 µm ATP, which was still above buffer elution levels, but also observed 35% pool elution by caffeine after increasing the number of caffeine washes from three to ten and its concentration from 250 to 500 µm. Apprehensive that the pool was being depleted of ATP binders, in Rounds 12–16 we relaxed the counter‐selection against caffeine by returning to three washes and continuing with 15 µm ATP. Although pool elution by caffeine remained at ≈4% and all other counter‐targets exhibited pool elution < 1% during these rounds, ATP‐induced pool elution continuously rose from 1.4% in Round 12 to 4.3% in Round 15, decreasing slightly to 3.0% in Round 16. The target concentration and pool elution over all 16 rounds are shown in Figure 1C. To assess whether aptamers for ATP were being enriched, we performed a gel‐elution assay in which we challenged the Round 16 pool with different concentrations of ATP and determined the proportion of the pool eluted by the target at each concentration. We obtained a K D of 11 µm for ATP with maximal pool elution at 6%, relative to ≈1% background elution with buffer alone (Figure 1D). The specificity of the pool was poor, however, as it responded to adenosine, AMP, ADP, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), and caffeine with > 50% cross‐reactivity (Figure S2, Supporting Information). At this point, we concluded the selection.

High‐Throughput Sequencing (HTS) Analysis of Trial 1 and Aptamer Characterization

To identify ATP aptamers, we subjected the Round 8, 10, 12, 14, and 16 SELEX pools to Illumina‐based HTS. We found that as the selection progressed, the proportion of unique sequences in the pool decreased from 21.1% in Round 8 to just 2.1% in Round 16 (Figure S3, Supporting Information). To facilitate the selection of candidates for further affinity characterization, we plotted the abundance of every sequence in Round 16 against the enrichment fold of these sequences between Round 16 and Round 8 (Figure 1E). The most abundant sequence, ATP‐1, represented 20% of the pool, with the next most abundant sequences (ATP‐2 and ATP‐3) each making up 14% of the pool. For aptamer characterization, we synthesized ten sequences that had a population abundance of ≥ 1% and an enrichment fold > 10. As a control, we also synthesized ATP‐8, an aptamer with relatively high abundance (1%), but with a lower enrichment fold (3). We performed isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) to determine the binding affinity of these aptamers to ATP in our selection buffer (Table S4, Supporting Information). Eight of the ten aptamers had K D < 1 µm, ranging from 550 to 790 nm. The ITC data also confirmed that the binding stoichiometry of these new aptamers is ≈1. This differs from the ATP‐binding DNA aptamer reported by the Szostak group, which binds two molecules of ATP with negative cooperativity.[ 39 ] However, the control aptamer ATP‐8 apparently binds to two ATP molecules with K D1 of 3 µm and K D2 of 19.7 µm. Closer examination of the sequence of this aptamer revealed it contains the motif GGGGGA…GG(A/G)GGA also present in the Szostak ATP‐binding DNA aptamer,[ 23 ] which explains its binding behavior. Thus, as with previous aptamer selections against ATP, we too enriched the original ATP aptamer motif, however, such aptamers did not dominate our pool in abundance primarily because of our more stringent selection conditions.

We next determined the specificity of our ATP aptamers using a previously reported exonuclease‐based fluorescence assay.[ 39 , 40 ] Here, aptamers are digested by a mixture of single‐ and double‐stranded DNA exonucleases in the absence and presence of a ligand. Unbound aptamers are readily digested into mononucleotides, while ligand‐bound aptamers resist digestion in proportion to their ligand‐binding affinity. Aptamer digestion is monitored over time using SYBR Gold, which produces a fluorescence signal in the presence of oligonucleotides but not mononucleotides. Aptamer‐ligand binding strength is computed in terms of the “resistance value,” which describes the degree to which the aptamer resists nuclease digestion in the presence of ligand versus without ligand over the course of the digestion (see [ 38 ] for calculation).[ 40 ] We assessed the binding of aptamers to 50 and 250 µm ATP and 250 µm of the ATP analogs dATP, ADP, AMP, and adenosine, as well as 250 µm of structurally‐similar ligands including NAD, caffeine, hypoxanthine, ITP, GTP, and CTP. Most aptamers were cross‐reactive to ATP, dATP, ADP, AMP, adenosine, NAD, and caffeine, but not hypoxanthine, GTP, CTP, and ITP, which implied that these aptamers had specificity profiles that were generally similar to the original Szostak DNA ATP‐binding aptamer[ 23 ] (Figure 1F). However, there were a few aptamers with markedly improved specificity. For example, ATP‐15 cross‐reacted to ATP, dATP, ADP, AMP, and NAD but had a minimal response to adenosine. Aptamers ATP‐2 and ATP‐5 had low cross‐reactivity to AMP, but still bound dATP, ADP, adenosine, and NAD. The most specific aptamer was ATP‐7, which bound only ATP and dATP, weakly cross‐reacted to ADP, and did not respond to AMP, adenosine, NAD, caffeine, GTP, CTP, or ITP (Figure 1F). These data suggest that in contrast to the Szostak ATP aptamer, which seems to only recognize the nucleobase moiety of ATP, ATP‐7 recognizes the entirety of the ATP molecule, including both the phosphates and the nucleobase. Thus, our first trial was somewhat successful in that we were able to identify ATP‐7, an aptamer that binds with sub‐micromolar affinity to ATP (K D = 750 nm) and slight cross‐reactivity to ADP, while entirely rejecting AMP, adenosine, NAD, and other nucleotide triphosphates.

The Second Selection Trial

Although we identified aptamers with improved affinity and specificity for ATP in Trial 1, even the best of these aptamers had some cross‐reactivity to ADP. We therefore performed a second trial of selection with the goal of discovering ATP aptamers that more strongly discriminate against all other analogs—especially ADP. To begin, we returned to Round 7 of Trial 1 and applied three changes to the selection protocol. First, we used lower ATP concentrations to enrich aptamers with higher affinity (Figure 2A). Second, we increased the concentration of caffeine during counter‐SELEX to deplete the enriched pool of the low‐specificity sequences we identified in the first trial (Figure 2B). Third, we began implementing counter‐selection against ADP to remove any aptamer that recognized this analog (Figure 2C). In Round 7, when we performed counter‐selection with 250 µm caffeine and 100 µm ADP, 2.2% and 6.4% of the pool were eluted, respectively, while 50 µm ATP only eluted 1.3% of the pool. In this round and in subsequent rounds, no other counter‐selection molecule eluted meaningful proportions of the pool. When we increased the concentration of ADP in Round 8 to 250 µm, roughly 18% of the pool was eluted, compared to 1.2% with caffeine and 0.9% with ATP, indicating that most of the oligonucleotides in the pool were being removed by ADP. In Round 9, we increased counter‐selection stringency by increasing the number of washes with caffeine to six and ADP to nine, and respectively observed 11% and 21% elution by these molecules compared to 0.5% with 50 µm ATP. By Round 10, just 5% of the pool was eluted by caffeine and 13% was eluted by ADP, while pool elution by ATP was 0.6%. In Round 11, we increased the number of washes for caffeine and ADP to 9 and 12, respectively. We also reduced the concentration of ATP to 25 µm in Round 13 and then to 5 µm by Round 16. From Round 11 until Round 18, pool elution by ADP and caffeine remained steady at ≈12% and ≈4%, respectively. And despite reducing the concentration of ATP, pool elution by ATP remained at ≈1% and consistently above background elution levels. Having observed no further change in the binding profile of the pool, we determined the binding affinity and specificity of the Round 18 pool using the gel‐elution assay. The pool bound ATP with an apparent K D of 3.3 µm and displayed low cross‐reactivity to AMP, hypoxanthine, caffeine, and GTP and moderate cross‐reactivity to adenosine, ADP, and NAD (Figure 2D; Figure S4, Supporting Information). We thus concluded this trial of selection.

HTS Analysis of Trial 2 and Characterization of High‐Specificity ATP Aptamers

We subjected the Round 10, 12, 16, and 18 pools to HTS to identify aptamer sequences from Trial 2. Unlike in Trial 1, the diversity of the pools decreased minimally as the rounds progressed, with 36.4%, 28.4%, 24.6%, and 27.6% unique sequences in Round 10, 12, 16, and 18, respectively (Figure S5, Supporting Information). We attribute this limited decrease in pool diversity to the highly stringent selection conditions we applied via high counter‐target concentrations in conjunction with low target concentrations, which likely prevented any particular sequence from overtaking the pool in terms of abundance. To identify aptamers for further affinity characterization, we plotted the abundance of every sequence in Round 18 against enrichment fold between Round 18 and Round 10 (Figure 2E). We synthesized six sequences that had a population abundance of ≥ 0.1% and an enrichment fold > 50 as well as five sequences with enrichment fold > 50 and abundance of 0.03–0.08% and determined their affinity for ATP using ITC. Eight of these aptamers had a K D of 700–880 nm, with an average value of ≈750 nm; two outlier sequences had a K D of ≈2 µm, and one displayed a K D of 117 µm (Table S4, Supporting Information). All sequences had a binding stoichiometry of ≈1. Next, we determined the specificity of these aptamers using our exonuclease assay, and observed virtually no cross‐reactivity to 250 µm AMP, adenosine, NAD, and caffeine relative to 50 µm ATP, but most aptamers still retained moderate cross‐reactivity to 250 µm ADP (Figure 2F).

To precisely quantify the specificity of these aptamers for ATP versus other analogs, we first performed ITC to determine their K D for ADP, AMP, and adenosine. These aptamers generally had 20–30‐fold lower affinity for ADP relative to ATP, with K Ds of 16–53 µm for ADP (Table S5, Supporting Information). The four aptamers with the greatest discriminatory ability, ATP18‐13, ATP18‐14, ATP18‐15, and ATP‐7 (from Trial 1), respectively had K Ds of 0.72 ± 0.03, 1.76 ± 0.05, 0.70 ± 0.02, and 0.75 ± 0.04 µm for ATP, and K Ds of 24.3 ± 0.7, 53.4 ± 2.5, 22.7 ± 0.7, and 21.6 ± 0.8 µm for ADP (Table 1 ; Figure S6, Supporting Information); this represents a fold‐difference of 34, 30, 32, and 29, respectively. We further examined the specificity of these four aptamers for adenosine, and obtained K D values of 49, 71, 39, and 39 µm (Table 1; Figure S7A–D, Supporting Information), a fold‐difference of 68, 40, 55, and 53 relative to ATP, respectively. Finally, we determined their affinity to AMP and obtained K D values of 126, 260, 105, and 91 µm (Table 1; Figure S7E–H, Supporting Information), representing fold‐differences of 175, 148, 149, and 121 relative to ATP, respectively. Thus, our aptamers display unprecedentedly high target affinity and specificity. For comparison, we independently determined the binding properties of a recently reported adenosine aptamer (Ade1301b)[ 35 ] using ITC, and obtained a K D of 7.1 ± 0.2, 12.8 ± 0.5, 79.0 ± 4.2, and 0.21 ± 0.007 µm for ATP, ADP, AMP and adenosine, respectively (Table 1; Figure S8A–D, Supporting Information). In addition, the original ATP aptamer discovered by the Szostak group[ 23 ] (here used in the form of the 7 base‐pair variant ATP‐33),[ 41 ] which binds two ATP molecules, has a K 1/2 of 6.5 µm (i.e., the geometric mean of the K Ds for the two ligand‐binding sites) (Figure S8E, Supporting Information), whereas our aptamer ATP18‐13 binds one molecule of ATP with a K D of 0.7 µm—a ≈10‐fold improvement in affinity. Regarding specificity, ATP‐33 binds ADP (K 1/2 = 3.2 µm) (Figure S8F, Supporting Information) and adenosine (K 1/2 = 2.6 µm) (Figure S8G, Supporting Information) with even better affinity than ATP, and has similar affinity for AMP (K 1/2 = 9.4 µm) (Figure S8H, Supporting Information), while our aptamer ATP18‐13 has 34‐fold lower affinity for ADP (K D = 24 µm), 68‐fold lower affinity for adenosine (K D = 49 µm), and 175‐fold lower affinity for AMP (K D = 126 µm) relative to ATP. These data indicate that the selection pressure applied in Trial 2 successfully enriched aptamers with superior specificity. The high‐throughput sequencing data also corroborates this fact. Analysis with the motif clustering software RaptGen[ 42 ] demonstrated that the abundance of sequences in the highly specific aptamer family represented by ATP18‐13 and ATP‐7 grew by twofold (from 11.1% to 20.3%) between the final round pools of Trial 1 and 2, while the abundance of members in poorly specific families like those represented by ATP1, ATP2/ATP5, ATP3, ATP4, ATP6, ATP8 significantly decreased (by ≈117‐fold), from 82% in Trial 1 to 0.7% in Trial 2 (Figure S9, Supporting Information). For aptamers related to the Szostak DNA ATP aptamer, their abundance likewise decreased by 70‐fold, from 1.4% in Trial 1 to 0.02% in Trial 2 (Figure S9, Supporting Information).

Exonuclease‐Guided Generation of Structure‐Switching ATP Aptamers

We next modified the aptamer with the best target‐binding affinity and specificity, ATP18‐13, to introduce structure‐switching functionality for use in a sensor. We achieved this using an approach based on exonuclease‐guided truncation,[ 41 ] in which Exo III and Exo I are used to remove non‐essential nucleotides from the 3′ terminus of the target‐bound aptamer; this results in a thermally destabilized truncation product that undergoes a large conformational change upon target binding (Figure 3A). We digested ATP18‐13 with Exo III and Exo I in the absence and presence of 250 µm ATP and analyzed the digestion products via polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). In the absence of ATP, the aptamer was completely digested into short products and mononucleotides within 60 min. With ATP, however, the aptamer was truncated by 4 nucleotides from the 3′ end, resulting in a 42‐nt major product that remained even after 60 min of digestion (Figure S10, Supporting Information). We synthesized the blunt‐ended variant of this product (ATP18‐13‐38), which had a 4‐nt 5′ overhang removed, as well as variants with one more (ATP18‐13‐40) or one less (ATP18‐13‐36) base pair (Figure 3B) and determined their binding affinity to ATP using ITC. Truncated aptamers ATP18‐13‐40, ATP18‐13‐38, and ATP18‐13‐36 had similar ATP‐binding affinities to the parent aptamer ATP18‐13 (K D = 0.7 µm) with K Ds of 1.0 ± 0.1, 1.1 ± 0.1, and 1.5 ± 0.1 µm, respectively (Figure 3C–F). Although the affinities of these aptamers were quite similar, their binding thermodynamics differed considerably. As the parent aptamer was further truncated, the binding enthalpies and entropies became increasingly negative, with ATP18‐13‐36 having by far the most negative binding terms. ΔH bind and ΔS bind for ATP18‐13 were −27.7 kcal mol−1 and −66 cal mol−1·K−1, respectively, whereas those values for ATP18‐13‐36 were −37.7 kcal mol−1 and −100.9 cal mol−1·K−1, respectively (Figure 3C–F). This increased entropic cost of binding implies that the truncated aptamer may undergo a larger conformational change upon binding ATP relative to the parent aptamer. This entropic cost, however, is nearly offset by the more favorable enthalpic binding contribution, which is perhaps greater in the truncated aptamer versus the parent aptamer due to target‐induced stem‐refolding for the truncated aptamer.

To assess which truncated aptamer had the greatest degree of structure‐switching functionality, we performed an assay wherein the aptamers are digested by Exo I without or with target.[ 43 ] Aptamers that are primarily unfolded at equilibrium are digested rapidly into mononucleotides, whereas target‐bound folded aptamers resist digestion in an affinity‐dependent manner. We found that ATP18‐13‐36 seemed to have the greatest degree of target‐induced conformation switching. This is based on the rapid digestion of the aptamer without target (90% digested in 30 min) relative to the other two aptamers (65–75% digested after 90 min) and its high resistance value (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Accordingly, we chose ATP18‐13‐36 for further sensor development.

A Fluorescent Molecular Beacon for ATP Detection in Complex Biological Matrices

We next developed a molecular beacon sensor using ATP18‐13‐36 (termed ATP18‐13‐36 FQ) by modifying its 5′ and 3′ end with an Iowa Black RQ quencher and Cy5 fluorophore, respectively. Without ATP, the beacon remains primarily unfolded, resulting in separation of the fluorophore and quencher and hence high fluorescence. ATP binding induces aptamer stem folding, resulting in fluorescence quenching due to the close distance between the fluorophore and quencher (Figure 4A).

Aptamer‐target binding induces folding of the aptamer, resulting in quenching of fluorescence. B) Design of ATP18‐13‐36‐TT FQ. C) Calibration curve of this beacon for ATP detection. D) Specificity of the sensor against ADP, AMP, adenosine (ADE), and NAD. At a concentration of 1 µm (pink‐shaded region), the response of the beacon to ATP is at least 20‐fold higher compared to ADP, AMP, ADE, and NAD.

To determine the sensitivity of ATP18‐13‐36 FQ, we challenged it with various concentrations of ATP. Unusually, the sensor did not produce the Langmuir‐like response characteristic of biosensors based on a single‐site receptor. Rather, ATP induced 10 ± 5% quenching in fluorescence at concentrations from 250 nm to 100 µm (Figure S12, Supporting Information). We hypothesized that the addition of the fluorophore‐quencher pair to ATP18‐13‐36 may have stabilized its stem such that it was predominantly in a folded state regardless of whether ATP was present or not. To test this, we performed a urea denaturation experiment[ 44 ] wherein ATP18‐13‐36 FQ was mixed with various concentrations of urea in buffer (up to 9 m) in the absence of target. In principle, as the urea concentration increases, the aptamer should become more unfolded, resulting in increasing fluorescence, and this was what we observed experimentally. At urea concentrations of 0–2 m, the aptamer gradually unfolded, and at ≥ 3 m urea, the proportion of unfolded beacon increased exponentially, with a 20‐fold difference in fluorescence at 0 versus 9 m urea (Figure S13, Supporting Information). This data confirms our hypothesis that the aptamer is primarily folded in the target‐free state, resulting in a non‐ideal target‐response.

To improve the sensitivity of our molecular beacon sensor, we further destabilized its stem by replacing the GC base‐pair at the terminus with a TT mismatch (ATP18‐13‐36‐TT). We again modified this construct with an Iowa Black RQ quencher and Cy5 fluorophore at its 5′ and 3′ end, respectively, yielding the ATP18‐13‐36‐TT FQ beacon (Figure 4B). This sensor was much more responsive to ATP and exhibited a typical Langmuir response curve, with a maximum of 60% fluorescence quenching at 64 µm ATP, a linear range of 0–1 µm, and a limit of detection of 125 nm (Figure 4C; Figure S14A, Supporting Information). Based on the calibration curve of the sensor, we determined the K D of this modified aptamer for ATP to be 1.76 µm. We also challenged the beacon with various concentrations of ADP, AMP, adenosine, and NAD (Figure 4D; Figure S14B–E, Supporting Information), and found that the sensor had a 16‐, 55‐, 31‐, and 69‐fold poorer affinity for these analogs, respectively, relative to ATP. In practical terms, the sensor did not yield a meaningful response to ADP, AMP, adenosine, and NAD at concentrations of 1 µm or less, whereas the sensor yielded 38% of its maximal signal with 1 µm ATP (Figure 4D). These data indicate that our molecular beacon is both sensitive and specific.

We next used ATP18‐13‐36‐TT FQ to detect ATP in cell culture medium. For these experiments, we used Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), which is commonly used to culture mammalian cells. The response of the molecular beacon to ATP was less sensitive in this matrix relative to the selection buffer, with a K D of 10 µm and a limit of detection of 2 µm (Figure S15A, Supporting Information). We also assessed the response of the beacon to ADP, AMP, adenosine, and NAD, and obtained K Ds of 25, 220, 48, and 155 µm, respectively (Figure S15B–E, Supporting Information). Interestingly, the apparent affinity of the molecular beacon for ATP was notably worse in DMEM relative to buffer, with a ≈6‐fold decrease in affinity, while the affinity for ADP, AMP, adenosine, and NAD decreased to a lesser degree—specifically, a 1.9‐, 2.5‐, 3.2‐, and 2.0‐fold reduction, respectively. This data implies that ATP in particular may have been sequestered and/or consumed by proteins present in FBS. To better understand this effect, we performed ATP detection in DMEM containing 10%, 5%, 2%, or 0% FBS. As the quantity of serum decreased, the ATP affinity of the beacon increased, yielding a K D of 10, 7.2, 5.4, and 2.7 µm, respectively, while the limit of detection also improved to 2, 1, 1, and 0.25 µm, respectively (Figure S16A–D, Supporting Information). This confirmed that FBS itself was negatively impacting sensor performance. In terms of the influence of DMEM itself, both the K D and limit of detection were ≈2‐fold poorer in DMEM versus selection buffer, indicating that DMEM also had a non‐negligible impact on sensor performance. Nevertheless, our data show that superior sensor performance can be achieved if the amount of serum in the medium is kept ≤5%.

Continuous Analyte Monitoring with the Molecular Beacon

The concentration of ATP in biological systems is dynamic because various biochemical processes continuously utilize it as molecular currency to carry out a wide range of vital processes. We therefore examined whether ATP18‐13‐36‐TT FQ could dynamically monitor changes in ATP concentration. Since our molecular beacon has similar affinity for dATP (K D = 2.6 µm) (Figure S17, Supporting Information) and ATP (K D = 1.7 µm), we used a simple and controllable in vitro system in which a DNA polymerase (Klenow fragment) uses dATP to extend a self‐primed DNA template containing a 10‐bp double‐stranded stem, a 4‐nt loop, and a 5′ 30‐nt poly‐T single‐strand overhang (Figure 5A). We first carried out this experiment in selection buffer and analyzed the results via PAGE. With 40 µm dATP, the Klenow fragment was able to extend 1 µm of the 54‐nt template to an 84‐nt elongated double‐stranded product within 30 min at room temperature (Figure S18, Supporting Information). We next determined whether template elongation could occur with incremental addition of dATP and evaluated the elongated products via PAGE. We first mixed 1 µm of the template with Klenow fragment and then added 10 µm dATP to initiate the elongation reaction. The template was partially elongated after 10 min, but remained the same length over the subsequent 20 min, implying that dATP had been depleted. We then added 10 µm dATP again, and observed that the elongated product was further extended within 10 min, but with no further progress in the following 20 min. We observed the same start‐stop elongation behavior with the third addition of 10 µm dATP; after the fourth addition of dATP, the product was completely extended (Figure S19, Supporting Information). These results therefore demonstrate that the template extension reaction could serve as a viable model system to study dynamic changes in dATP concentration with our molecular beacon sensor.

Real‐time detection of dATP in a dynamic system using an aptamer molecular beacon sensor. A) Working principle of real‐time experiment. The aptamer beacon fluorescently monitors dATP concentration while Klenow fragment consumes dATP to extend a poly T‐containing DNA template. B) Real‐time fluorescence monitoring of template extension reaction with seconds scale time resolution in the presence of limiting dATP and C,D) with excess dATP in cell medium containing DMEM and 10% fetal bovine serum.

We next applied our ATP18‐13‐36‐TT FQ sensor in the same model system to monitor the concentration of dATP in real time. When dATP is abundant, the target binds the aptamer causing it to fold, and the fluorescence of the molecular beacon becomes quenched. As free dATP is consumed by the polymerase, aptamer‐bound dATP dissociates, causing the aptamer to unfold and producing increased fluorescence. We first mixed the template, beacon, and 10 µm dATP in selection buffer. The fluorescence of the beacon was maximally quenched (≈60%) due to the presence of dATP (Figure 5B). We then added Klenow fragment to begin the elongation reaction (Figure 5BI) and noted an immediate increase in fluorescence, indicating that dATP was rapidly being depleted. In the first 2 min, dATP was consumed at an exponential rate, and nearly all dATP was depleted by 4.5 min. When we spiked another 10 µm dATP into the reaction mixture (Figure 5BII), the fluorescence of the solution fell back to baseline levels almost immediately. Soon thereafter, the fluorescence of the solution increased again, indicating that dATP was being consumed, and essentially all dATP was depleted in 3 min. We observed the same pattern after administering three more doses of 10 µm dATP (Figure 5BIII–V), with fluorescence quickly dropping to baseline levels and subsequently rising until all dATP was depleted by ≈3.5 min (Figure 5B). We used a calibration curve (Figure S17, Supporting Information) to convert the fluorescence readout to a dATP concentration, and then used this real‐time concentration data (Figure S20, Supporting Information), to calculate an average velocity of 0.05 µm s−1. As controls, we performed the same experiments but with dATP, template, or Klenow enzyme absent from the reaction mixture (Figure S21, Supporting Information). Without dATP, the fluorescence of the system remained at the maximum value due to the molecular beacon being persistently unfolded. For reactions without template or enzyme, the reaction mixture was quenched throughout the experiment because no dATP was being consumed.

Since biochemical reactions occur in complex chemical environments, we finally assessed whether the molecular beacon could enable real‐time monitoring of dATP consumption during template extension in DMEM containing 10% FBS. Upon initially mixing the template, beacon, and 30 µm dATP in the culture medium, the fluorescence of the solution was quenched. We subsequently added Klenow fragment to initiate the extension reaction, and noted that the fluorescence of the solution recovered much more slowly in DMEM relative to in buffer, implying that dATP consumption and hence enzymatic catalysis was slower. After ≈30 min, the fluorescence of the solution reached its maximum value, indicating that all dATP had been consumed (Figure 5C). Using a calibration curve for dATP in DMEM (Figure S22, Supporting Information), we converted the fluorescence values to dATP concentration (Figure 5D) and used this to extract kinetic parameters for the template extension reaction. The catalytic ability of Klenow was indeed relatively slow in DMEM compared to buffer, with a reaction velocity of 0.02 µm s−1. This reduction in reaction rate can be attributed to the reduced availability of free Mg2+ in the culture medium compared to in buffer, which serves as a vital cofactor for the enzyme.[ 45 ] Although the medium contains 0.8 mm Mg2+, and for this study in particular we spiked an additional 1 mm MgCl2 into the medium (i.e., total Mg2+ ≈1.8 mm), it is unknown what proportion of free Mg2+ was available to the enzyme. These results nevertheless demonstrate that the molecular beacon can be used to monitor ATP or dATP in complex media containing a plethora of different electrolytes and biomolecules.

Conclusion

In this work, we discovered new aptamers that bind to ATP with unprecedently high affinity and specificity. The best of our aptamers (ATP18‐13) has sub‐micromolar affinity for ATP (K D = 720 nm), with excellent specificity against other nucleotide triphosphates and interferents, with 34‐, 175‐, 68‐fold lower affinity for ADP, AMP, and adenosine, respectively. Several aptamers for ATP have previously been reported, and the most well‐known and widely used is the DNA aptamer isolated by the Szostak group, which binds two molecules of ATP with micromolar affinity and cross‐reacts to ADP, AMP, and adenosine with similar affinity. The same ATP aptamer has been discovered multiple times by independent groups, perhaps because the binding motif of such aptamers are simple (13 conserved nucleotides), hence such aptamers are very abundant in libraries, and these selection procedures also employed relatively low stringency. There have been notable efforts to generate ATP aptamers with improved affinity and specificity. The Szostak group, for example, discovered aptamers containing cationic modifications that had one of the highest reported affinities for ATP (K D = 1 µm). However, this aptamer still could not differentiate between ADP, AMP, and adenosine.[ 27 ] In contrast, our natural DNA aptamer has higher affinity for ATP and can also distinguish between these different analogs. These results therefore highlight the incredible importance of tailoring selection conditions to optimize the binding properties of the resultant aptamers.

Using our newly isolated aptamers, we developed fluorescent molecular beacon sensors that can detect ATP with high sensitivity and specificity. The superior binding properties of the new ATP aptamers enable detection of ATP with lower limits of detection and greater selectivity than previously reported aptamer‐based sensors for ATP. For example, our molecular beacon sensor based on ATP18‐13 has a limit of detection of 125 nm, which is 20‐fold better than a fluorescent molecular beacon sensor[ 41 ] and an electrochemical aptamer‐based sensor[ 46 ] previously developed using a truncated variant of the original ATP DNA aptamer. Our sensor could also monitor dynamic changes in analyte concentration with seconds‐scale resolution, even in complex biological matrices such as cell culture media. The performance of our sensor is notably more sensitive compared to other continuous aptamer sensors reported for ATP. For instance, Özalp et al. selected a new ATP aptamer (K D = 700 µm) and used it to develop a fluorescent aptamer sensor based on intramolecular strand displacement that could sense dynamic changes in ATP concentrations in the hundreds of micromolar to millimolar range during the ATP‐mediated phosphorylation of glucose by hexokinase.[ 31 ] As another example, Zheng et al. employed the original ATP aptamer in a nanoscale electrochemical aptamer‐based sensor that could monitor changes in cellular ATP with a limit of detection of 26 µm.[ 47 ] In contrast, our sensor can measure dynamic changes in ATP concentrations from sub‐micromolar to low micromolar concentrations. Given that the concentrations of ATP involved with cell signaling and neurotransmission are typically within this range, we believe our aptamer sensor could prove useful for monitoring such processes in matrices such as cerebrospinal fluid. This is a feasible prospect, given that the Lu group has already used the Szostak aptamer to detect ATP in vivo in the brain of mice.[ 48 ] More complex matrices, such as blood, will necessitate the use of more robust sensing platforms, such as the electrochemical aptamer‐based sensor, which has proven effective in monitoring analytes in the circulatory system of living organisms.[ 22 ]

Our aptamer‐based sensor offers several benefits relative to conventional means of monitoring ATP. First, its sensitivity and specificity is superior to previously described sensors. Second, unlike luciferase‐based assays, our sensor is reagentless. Third, our sensor is more straightforward to use than genetically‐encoded sensors, which require genetic modification of cells. We have demonstrated the detection of ATP in solution in this work, but we believe a similar sensor could also be employed to sense ATP within cells or organelles via simple transfection (e.g., with Lipofectamine) or the use of nanoscale electrochemical sensors.[ 47 ] Since cells contain relatively high concentrations of ATP, however, our aptamer will have to be mutated or truncated to reduce its affinity in order to shift its dynamic range to such concentrations. On the other hand, the detection of even lower ATP concentrations for applications such as the detection of microbial contamination[ 49 ] will require the development of ATP aptamers with greater affinity. We posit that the affinity of our aptamers could potentially be further improved by another order of magnitude using post‐selection engineering such as our recently‐developed Motif‐SELEX strategy.[ 50 ] Nevertheless, the aptamers described here should provide a useful foundation for the development of various analytical tools for biomedical applications, and the workflow presented in this study highlights how SELEX processes can be tuned to generate superior aptamer sensors for other important and challenging analytes.

Experimental Section

Oligonucleotides

All DNA oligonucleotides were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) (see Supporting Information, Table S1, Supporting Information for sequences). DNA purchased for SELEX and the molecular beacon probes were HPLC‐purified, whereas high‐throughput sequencing primers were purchased as PAGE‐purified. Other oligonucleotides were purchased as standard desalt purity. All oligonucleotides were dissolved in molecular biology‐grade water and their concentration was determined using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

SELEX Procedure

The selection buffer employed in this work was 20 mm Tris (pH 7.4), 140 mm NaCl, 4 mm KCl, and 2 mm MgCl2. We utilized the library‐immobilized SELEX method[ 51 ] with a 73‐nt stem‐loop structured randomized DNA library to isolate an aptamer for ATP. The specific selection experimental conditions for Trial 1 and Trial 2 are outlined in Supporting Information, Table S2 and S3 (Supporting Information). To start, the oligonucleotide pool was hybridized with biotinylated complementary (cDNA‐bio) in selection buffer by heating to 95 °C for 10 min and gradually cooled over 20 min in a room temperature water bath. Then, the library‐cDNA complex was loaded into a microgravity column containing 250 µL streptavidin‐agarose resin prewashed with selection buffer. Following library immobilization, the resin was washed multiple times with 250 µL aliquots of selection buffer to remove any sequences that are weakly bound to the cDNA. Following that, counter‐SELEX[ 52 ] was performed as detailed in Tables S2 and S3 (Supporting Information), followed by additional washes with selection buffer. Finally, the library was challenged with three aliquots of ATP, and the eluent was collected and purified with molecular biology‐grade water using a 10 kDa molecular‐weight cutoff filter. The purified sequences were then PCR amplified using GoTaq Hot Start Colorless Master Mix with 1 µm forward primer (FP) and 1 µm biotinylated reverse primer (RP‐bio) utilizing a Bio‐Rad C1000 thermal cycler with the following conditions: 2 min at 95 °C; 11 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; 5 min at 72 °C. The resulting amplicons were converted to single‐stranded DNA using streptavidin‐agarose resin and 0.2 m NaOH as a denaturant. The affinity and specificity of the final‐round SELEX pool for ATP were determined using a gel elution assay[ 53 ] described in the Supporting Information.

ATP Detection with Molecular Beacon Sensor

First, the ATP molecular beacon (either ATP18‐13‐36 FQ or ATP18‐13‐36‐TT FQ, final concentration 75 nm) was prepared at a 2x concentration in selection buffer. The molecular beacon was first dissolved in Tris buffer (final concentration 20 mm, pH 7.4), heated at 90 °C for 10 min, and then immediately cooled to room temperature. Salts and Triton X‐100 were subsequently added to the aptamer solution (final concentration 140 mm NaCl, 4 mm KCl, 2 mm MgCl2, 0.005% v/v). 40 µL of 2x molecular beacon solution was mixed with 40 µL 2x ATP at various concentrations. The solution was incubated at room temperature for 5 min to allow the aptamer to bind the target. Subsequently, 75 µL of samples were loaded into the wells of a black 384‐well flat‐bottom microplate (Corning) and fluorescence was measured using a Tecan Spark plate reader with excitation at 640 nm and emission at 665 nm (bandwidth = 5 nm). Calibration curves were created by plotting ATP concentration against percent fluorescence quenching, which was calculated using the formula (F 0−F)/F 0 × 100% where F 0 and F represent the fluorescence of the solutions without and with ATP, respectively. To assess the specificity of the molecular beacon sensor, the same procedure was performed as above, but instead the 40 µL of 2x molecular beacon solution was mixed with 40 µL of 2x interferent at various concentrations. The fluorescence of the solutions were determined using Tecan plate reader and calibration curves were created by plotting fluorescence quenching a function of interferent concentration. Sensing in cell medium was performed in solutions containing 89% (v/v) Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco), 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) spiked with an additional 1.2 mm MgCl2 (final Mg2+ ≈2.0 mm) and 20 mm Tris (pH 7.4). The aptamer and target or interferent were both prepared at a concentration of 2x as described above in cell medium and then mixed together. After incubating at room temperature for 5 min, 75 µL of the samples were loaded in the wells of a black 384‐well microplate and the fluorescence of the solutions were recorded using a Tecan microplate reader with excitation at 640 nm and emission at 665 nm. Fluorescence quenching was determined as described above.

Real‐Time dATP Monitoring with Molecular Beacon

First, a solution of 2x DNA template (KF‐sub10, final concentration 2 µm) and a solution containing 2x ATP18‐13‐36‐TT FQ molecular beacon (final concentration 50 nm) was prepared by diluting the DNA in Tris buffer (final concentration: 20 mm, pH 7.4), heating the solution at 90 °C for 5 min, and then cooling to room temperature. Then, salts, BSA, Triton X‐100, and dithiothreitol (DTT) were added to the template solution (final concentration: 140 mm NaCl, 4 mm KCl, 2 mm MgCl2, 0.005% Triton X‐100, 1.5 µm BSA, 1 mm DTT). Subsequently, 40 µL of 2x template and 40 µL 2x molecular beacon were mixed together and then 1 µL of 800 µm dATP was added to the solution. After 15 min of incubation, the solution was then loaded in a well of a black 384‐well flat‐bottom microplate and the fluorescence of the solution was recorded every 10 s using a Tecan microplate reader with excitation at 640 nm and emission at 665 nm. After ≈5 min of recording to obtain a baseline fluorescence signal, 2 µL of Klenow fragment DNA polymerase (final concentration: 0.02 U µL−1) dissolved in 1x NEBuffer 2 was added to the sample in the well to initiate template extension. This addition step required 90 s. The fluorescence of the solution was subsequently recorded every 10 s for 300 s. Afterward, 1 µL of 800 µm dATP was added to the sample in the well; this process required 30 s. The fluorescence of the solution was subsequently recorded every 10 s for 300 s as described above. This process of spiking dATP and recording fluorescence was repeated thrice more. The corresponding data in Figure 5 represents the error and standard deviation of data collected from two independent trials. To perform real‐time dATP monitoring in cell medium, a solution of 2x DNA template (KF‐sub10, final concentration 2 µm) and a solution of 2x ATP18‐13‐36‐TT FQ molecular beacon (final concentration 75 nm) were dissolved in cell medium and then 50 µL of both solutions were combined, followed by the addition of 1 µL 3.3 mm dATP (final concentration 33 µm). After 5 min of incubation at room temperature, 80 µL of the sample was transferred to the well of a black 384‐well microplate and the fluorescence of the solution was recorded every 10 s for 5 min using a Tecan microplate reader with excitation at 640 nm and emission at 665 nm. Thereafter, 2 µL of Klenow fragment (final concentration: 0.02 U µL−1) was added to the sample in the well to initiate template extension. The fluorescence of the solution was recorded every 10 s for 40 min as described above. Fluorescence quenching values were converted to dATP concentration using data from calibration curves developed from target detection in buffer or cell medium.
