Machine learning-based approaches to identify key drivers and predict severe food insecurity in Africa
Authors: Mesfin M Ayalew, Zelalem G Dessie, Aweke A Mitku, Temesgen Zewotir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracting knowledge and discovering insights from hidden patterns in food insecurity data through machine learning algorithms remains limited. Therefore, this study employed a variety of machine learning algorithms, including linear regression, ridge regression, LASSO regression, elastic net regression, support vector regression, decision tree regression, random forest regression, gradient boosting regression, extrem gradient bosting (XGBoost) regression, k-nearest neighbors (KNN) regression, and neural networks (NN) to investigate the predictive associations between key drivers and severe food insecurity across African countries from 2015 to 2021. Model performance was improved through hyperparameter tuning via GridSearchCV, with XGBoosting outperforming all other models. Feature importance was assessed using permutation importance techniques, while recursive feature elimination (RFE) with cross-validation identified the most influential national-level predictors. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) further revealed the direction and magnitude of each feature’s effect. Results show that higher national-level of inflation, climate change, unemployment, malaria incidence, GHG emissions, caloric loss, and cereal import dependency are associated with higher predicted rates of severe food insecurity, whereas a higher livestock production, cereal crop production, investment inflows, dietary energy supply, dietary protein supply, electricity access, GDP per capita, and political stability are associated with lower predicted rates. Among these drivers, unemployment, livestock production, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and caloric losses are the most influential drivers in predicting severe food insecurity across Africa, listed here in priority order. In light of these findings, the study recommends that policymakers prioritize interventions targeting these top-ranked factors when formulating and implementing national strategies to effectively mitigate severe food insecurity across African countries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47139-9.Keywords: Feature importance, Feature selection, Severe food insecurity, XGBoost modelSubject terms: Risk factors, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Food insecurity has been a major global challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Based on the 1996 World Food Summit, food security is defined as a condition in which “ all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient safe and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life”1. This conceptual framework encompasses four pillars of food security: availability, access, utilization and stability. A wide range of interrelated socioeconomic, environmental, political and health-related factors jointly contribute to food insecurity, including climate change2–5, inflation6–9, social and political instability and conflicts10–12, unemployment9,13–16, incidence of malaria17–25, greenhouse gas emissions26–31, caloric loss32,33, cereal import dependency34, livestock production or ownership35–40, investment inflow41,42, GDP per capita43,44, crop production, education, sanitation and landholdings40, irrigation45,46, dietary energy supply and protein supply47,48, food production, imports, infrastructure and access to improved water sources49, and electricity access50.

Existing empirical studies have primarily examined these determinants through classical statistical models, spatial models and, most recently, machine learning approaches conducted at the district or household level. For instance, analyses conducted in Ethiopia40, the Democratic Republic of Congo51, South Africa52, and Kenya53 demonstrated pronounced spatial heterogeneity in localized vulnerability to food insecurity. In recent years, data mining and machine learning (ML) techniques have increasingly been used to identify predictors of food insecurity54–58. Their empirical evidence indicates that gradient boosting and random forest54, decision tree algorithms55, random forest56, neural networks57, and multi-layer perceptron deep neural networks58 consistently outperform other candidate models in predicting food insecurity across diverse settings. These studies provide valuable micro-level insights but do not address how broader national structural forces shape food insecurity.

These household- and subnational-level studies are indispensable for identifying localized vulnerabilities, community-specific risk factors and informing locally targeted interventions. However, these localized processes are embedded within broader national and regional contexts that fundamentally shape livelihood opportunities, market functioning, and access to resources. National-level dynamics, including macroeconomic performance, governance quality, climatic variability, trade policies, and infrastructural development, shape the structural conditions within which households operate. In addition, national-level data are systematically collected and made available by international and regional institutions, ensuring standardized measurements, temporal consistency, and cross-country comparability over time. From the policy perspective, most strategic decisions concerning structural determinants of food insecurity are predominantly formulated and implemented at the national level. A national-scale analytical perspective therefore, serves as a complementary lens to subnational research by capturing population-wide exposures and structural vulnerabilities, and by addressing broader policy questions, such as how macroeconomic instability influences national food affordability, how trade dependency alters national food availability, how political instability affects nationwide stability of national food supply and distribution systems, and how climate variability impacts national food availability. Addressing such questions is crucial for informing fiscal policy, agricultural investment strategies, trade negotiations, climate adaptation planning, and regional cooperation frameworks.

The multifactorial and interconnected nature of severe food insecurity further demands unified analytical approaches capable of capturing complex interdependencies. For instance, Inflation influences household purchasing power6–9, political instability disrupts markets, trade, investments and institutional effectiveness10–12, GHG emissions through increased air pollution accelerates climate change, influence agricultural productivity and worsens environmental pressures on the sustainability of food systems26–31 and malaria incidence reduces agricultural labor availability in agrarian economies17–25, thereby aggravating the risk of severe food insecurity. Yet the relationships between certain drivers and food insecurity are highly context-dependent. For example, livestock ownership may enhance food security in many settings but increase vulnerability in arid and semi-arid environments due to climatic variability and resource scarcity59,60. Similarly, agricultural crop production enhances food availability in stable contexts but fails to ensure food security under weak market and governance mechanisms. Intensive crop production can also degrade soil fertility, reduce water availability, and disrupt ecosystem services, thereby undermining long-term food security61. In addition, although foreign direct investment is conventionally expected to reduce severe food insecurity by supporting economic growth, employment creation, and productivity gains, large scale investments in industries, commercial agriculture, or urban real estate can induce land use changes, displacement of smallholder farmers, and reduce availability of land for farming for the local people, thereby exacerbating severe food insecurity in affected areas42. These apparent inconsistencies may be attributable to spatial, institutional, and environmental heterogeneity.

Despite extensive household- and district-level studies on selected micro-level determinants, broader national-level drivers such as climate variability, demographic pressures, agricultural productivity, political instability, inflation, unemployment, trade dependency, macroeconomic performance, etc., remain largely underexplored within an integrated analytical framework using machine learning algorithms, particularly in the African contexts. Most previous studies have examined the impacts of climate change, agricultural productivity, or specific food systems in isolation, without assessing their combined effects on food insecurity. The macro-level drivers are particularly critical because they simultaneously influence entire populations and shape national food systems, rather than affecting only specific communities or households. A national-level approach is therefore essential for understanding systemic risk factors that operate across administrative boundaries and for supporting coordinated regional or continental food security strategies.

These drivers rarely operate in isolation but instead interact in nonlinear and synergistic ways due to spatial heterogeneity and temporal shocks, making classical linear models particularly sensitive to multicollinearity, nonlinearity, and heteroscedasticity. Such limitations often result in unstable parameter estimates and reduced inferential reliability. In contrast, machine learning methods, particularly ensemble tree-based and regularized models, are more robust to multicollinearity and are capable of modeling complex, nonlinear and interdependent relationships by automatically learning hidden patterns without requiring strict parametric assumptions62–65. Through regularization and cross-validation, these methods provide superior predictive performance, enhance generalizability, and limit the risks of overfitting or underfitting66. These characteristics make machine learning well-suited for national-level analyses involving high-dimensional and structurally interdependent predictors.

Although machine learning algorithms, which integrate statistical learning and artificial intelligence, have been increasingly applied to food insecurity research, many existing studies exhibit limited methodological transparency with respect to hyperparameter selection, validation strategies, and model optimization procedures, which may constrain both interpretability and the reliability of predictive performance. To address these gaps, the present study systematically implements a hyperparameter tuning strategy in conjunction with rigorous model validation in order to improve predictive accuracy, stability, and robustness. Accordingly, the study aims to identify and rank the most influential national-level structural determinants in predicting severe food insecurity in Africa using rigorously validated machine learning models. By doing so, it provides evidence-based insights into priority areas for national and regional intervention strategies designed to mitigate severe food insecurity across the continent.

Materials and methods

Study design, settings and populations

A retrospective study design was used to predict and identify risk factors associated with severe food insecurity in Africa. The analysis was conducted at the country level across the African continent, covering the period from 2015 to 2021. It is bordered by the Red Sea (northeast), the Indian Ocean (southeast), the Atlantic Ocean (west), and the Mediterranean Sea (north). The continent is made up of 54 recognized countries, each with its own unique culture, history, and geography.

Target variable and features

The data for this study were taken from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), World Bank (WB), and Malaria Atlas Project (MAP). The data are publicly available online at https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data, https://databank.worldbank.org/source/wdi-database-archives#, and https://data.malariaatlas.org. The target variable is the prevalence of severe food insecurity at the country level across Africa. It refers to the condition in which a person goes without food for a day or longer67. Prevalence was calculated by dividing the number of people who are severely food insecure by the total population of the country.

Covariates used in this study, listed in Table 1, were selected based on their theoretical relevance, empirical support in the previous literature and the availability of reliable cross-country data. To strengthen the theoretical foundation, this study considers an integrated food security framework encompassing the four internationally recognized pillars of food security: availability, access, utilization, and stability. This conceptualizes severe food insecurity as a multifactorial outcome shaped by interconnected socioeconomic, environmental, agricultural, infrastructural, demographic, political, health and related factors, which collectively influence these pillars. For example, crop and livestock production, irrigation, GHG emissions, and climate variability primarily affect food availability. Economic indicators such as GDP per capita, inflation, unemployment, trade dependency, and investment inflow influence access to food. Health and social factors, including malaria incidence, sanitation, education, and access to clean water and electricity affect utilization, while political instability and conflicts affect stability by disrupting markets, livelihoods and food systems over time. Conceptual frameworks.

Models

Food insecurity is a multidimensional phenomenon driven by interrelated economic, agricultural, climatic, demographic, political and health factors that interact dynamically and nonlinearly over time. These characteristics may arise due to spatial heterogeneity, temporal shocks, and cross-country dependencies inherent in the data and make the pattern of food insecurity dynamic and unpredictable. These limit the ability of classical statistical models to adequately represent the underlying complexity in the relationships between these covariates and severe food insecurity.

Modern statistical approaches, including regularized regression models, address multicollinearity and overfitting challenges through coefficient shrinkage, but they remain constrained by linearity and normality assumptions. In contrast, machine learning methods flexibly learn nonlinear relationships and high-order interactions in high-dimensional data without requiring prespecified functional forms or strict distributional assumptions63,65, while the use of regularization and cross-validation supports improved predictive performance. In particular, XGBoost effectively captures nonlinearities and complex interactions while controlling overfitting through ensemble learning and built-in regularization64; support vector machines achieve strong generalization in modeling nonlinear relationships by mapping input features into high-dimensional feature spaces through kernel functions68; and artificial neural network, based on the universal approximation theorem, learns hierarchical feature representation and captures complex nonlinear dependencies and higher-order interactions through multilayer nonlinear activation functions66.

Due to these advantages, this study employed a diverse set of machine learning algorithms including linear models (Linear, Ridge, LASSO, and Elastic Net ), tree-based models (Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Extreme Gradient Boosting), instance-based models (K-Nearest Neighbors and Support Vector ), and Artificial Neural Networks to investigate the relationships between key drivers and severe food insecurity. Each algorithm was trained to learn empirical dependencies from data. To do this, the dataset was split chronologically, with data from 2015 to 2019 (nearly 80%) used for training and data from 2020 to 2021 (nearly 20%) used for testing. This temporal split helps to prevent data leakage and ensures that the model predicts future observations based solely on past information. In the learning process, unknown dependencies were estimated from the training data to build the prediction model, and the learned relationships were subsequently evaluated on the testing data for validation and out-of-sample prediction.

Feature engineering and hyperparameter optimization

The data underwent a preprocessing procedure prior to building the ML models, including data cleaning, handling missing values and feature scaling. Min–Max Normalization was applied to transform all features’ values to a common range, typically [0, 1]. It was computed by subtracting the minimum value of each feature from its current value and dividing it by the range65. This approach preserves the relative distribution of feature values and ensures that no single feature dominates the training process due to scale differences. This normalization improves model performance and convergence, and is particularly suitable for gradient-based methods and distance-sensitive algorithms, which perform better when all features lie in a uniform range77. Previous studies have also shown that Min–Max normalization can improve the performance of linear models, particularly for small datasets78. However, to confirm the superiority of Min–Max normalization over standardization for feature scaling in this study, the study compared the performance of the two methods. The results, presented in the result section, indicate that Min–Max normalization provides the best overall feature-scaling performance.

To build a robust model, the study conducted hyperparameter optimization through systematic fine-tuning. Hyperparameter optimization, or parameter tuning, refers to the process of choosing the best hyperparameter settings before model training begins79. The XGBoosting algorithm uses different hyperparameter settings to control the construction and complexity of the decision trees, as well as the overall behavior of the model and the learning process. The setting includes number of boosting rounds or trees (n_estimators), maximum depth of each tree (max_depth), proportion of training instances used to build each tree (subsample), proportion of features randomly sampled for each tree (colsample_bytree), step size shrinkage used at each iteration to control the contribution of each tree (learning rate or eta), minimum loss reduction required to split a leaf node (gamma), L2 regularization term on weights required to penalize large coefficients (lambda), L1 regularization term on weights to encourage sparsity by driving some coefficients to zero (alpha). Choosing the right set of these hyperparameters improves model performance. The study used a grid search cross-validation method (GridSearchCV) with 5-fold cross-validation (cv = 5) settings, which involves specifying a set of hyperparameters and their possible values, and then exhaustively searching all possible combinations of the values80. The importance is to perform hyperparameter tuning and model selection simultaneously by training a model for every combination of hyperparameters. This makes it an efficient method for optimizing hyperparameters in a model. The best-performing set was selected, and the final model was used to predict new instances.

Feature importance and feature selection

This study used permutation importance techniques to assess feature importance. This method involves randomly shuffling the values of a specific feature while keeping other features constant81. Features that cause a significant decline in performance metrics are considered more important. In the context of linear models, features with larger absolute coefficient values are regarded as more important.

The study considers multiple features that can influence severe food insecurity. The study implemented the selected optimal algorithm to identify the key features contributing to severe food insecurity. Although having more information is beneficial, too much detail or duplication may cause overfitting and result in a poorly performing model82. Machine learning algorithms typically perform better by reducing data dimensionality82. This reduction is achieved by removing irrelevant or redundant features and minimizing noise, allowing the model to focus on key features for optimal performance.

In this study, we applied and compared Forward Feature Selection, Backward Elimination, and Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) techniques to identify and extract the most relevant features from the feature space. Forward feature selection begins with an empty model and progressively adds features that improve the model’s performance based on a chosen evaluation metric. In contrast, backward elimination starts with all features and iteratively removes the least significant ones. RFE recursively removes the least important features and builds the model iteratively until the desired number of features is reached81.

Model validation and performance measures

All models were developed using the training dataset and assessed on previously unseen data. Testing the model on the same data used for training can lead to overfitting, as the model has already learned the existing patterns, which may not apply to new instances. Therefore, evaluating new data offers a more accurate measure of performance and efficiency. We used several performance metrics, including mean squared error (MSE), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and R² for the evaluation. The final model producing the lowest MSE, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE, and the highest R² was selected as the best-fitting model, indicating optimal accuracy and performance.

The data were analyzed using Python 3.10, leveraging its powerful libraries and frameworks for data manipulation, statistical analysis, and visualization. Performance metrics for baseline and optimized algorithms.

After conducting the base model (spot-check algorithms), the study performed hyperparameter tuning using the GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation (cv = 5) setting tailored to each candidate algorithm. During model development, performance metrics were calculated on the training data using cross-validation to guide hyperparameter selection and model tuning. The optimal hyperparameter settings were identified through exhaustive searching of all possible combinations of the hyperparameter values for each candidate model to effectively enhance their performance. In this approach, the training dataset is partitioned into five equally sized folds, with the model trained on four folds and validated on the remaining fold. This process is repeated five times, with each fold serving once as the validation set, and the final performance is averaged across all folds. This technique provides reliable performance estimates, balances the bias–variance trade-off, and reduces overfitting during hyperparameter optimization, ensuring that the selected parameters generalize well to unseen data. The test dataset (unseen data), which was not used during training or hyperparameter tuning, was then used for final evaluation, providing an unbiased estimate of the model’s generalization performance.

For each candidate model, the settings of the searching domain were determined through a combination of theoretical understanding of model behaviour, insights from preliminary experiments and empirical evidence from previous research64,74,83–88. This approach ensured model stability, reliable convergence, and robust predictive performance, while minimizing computational cost and the risks of overfitting or underfitting. For example, the following parameters were tuned for XGBoost in this study: ‘n_estimators’: [50, 100, 150], ‘learning_rate’: [0.01, 0.02, 0.1, 0.2], ‘max_depth’:3,5,7,8, ‘subsample’: [0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0], ‘colsample_bytree’: [0.6, 0.8, 1.0], and ‘gamma’: [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]. Learning rates between 0.01 and 0.2 were selected because smaller values (< 0.01) slowed convergence, while larger values (> 0.2) caused unstable updates and overfitting during preliminary tests. Similarly, a maximum tree depth below 3 leads to high biased predictions that deviate from actual values, whereas depths exceeding 8 caused high variance predictions on unseen data and increased computational cost. Limiting a maximum tree depth between 3 and 8 provided a balance between bias and variance, reducing the risks of both underfitting and overfitting. These empirically guided hyperparameter ranges ensure stable model performance, reduce the risks of overfitting and underfitting, and address convergence issues.

The results of the optimal hyperparameter settings for each candidate model for the testing or unseen datasets provided in Table 3, indicating that performance metrics improved after adjusting the hyperparameters of the baseline models. Similar to the baseline model results, Gradient Boosting, XGBoosting, and K-Nearest Neighbors algorithms provide lower MSE, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE values, along with higher R² values, indicating their superior predictive accuracy and performance compared to other candidate algorithms. Among the tested algorithms, the XGBoost algorithm was identified as the most efficient algorithm, achieving optimal performance with the following hyperparameter settings: ‘n_estimators’: [150], ‘learning_rate’: [0.1], ‘max_depth’:7, ‘subsample’: [0.8], ‘colsample_bytree’: [0.8], and ‘gamma’:1 and it serves as the optimal final model for this study. This finding aligns with the results from previous studies64,65.

The superior performance of XGBoost and Gradient Boosting arises not only from their strong predictive capabilities but also from their ability to reveal structural relationships that simple models cannot capture. First, the drivers of severe food insecurity vary dynamically and interact in nonlinear and context-specific ways due to climatic shocks, economic volatility, political instability, and cross-country dependencies. These complexities cause severe food insecurity patterns to fluctuate both geographically and temporarily, making them difficult to predict and limiting the effectiveness of simpler models such as linear regression, regularized regression, or SVM. Second, the ensemble-based architecture, gradient optimization and regularizations mechanisms of the design of XGBoost and the Gradient Boosting allow them to balance bias and variance more effectively than simpler models, reducing the risk of overfitting or underfitting. Their robustness to heterogeneous data and outliers further minimizes prediction errors and enhances generalizability to unseen data. Moreover, XGBoost leverages the use of second-order gradient information (both gradients and Hessians), shrinkage, and column subsampling, which together improve convergence speed, model stability and predictive performance. Beyond performance gains, XGBoost offers additional insight that simpler approaches cannot. Specifically, XGBoost uncovers nonlinearities, interactions, heterogeneous effects, and risk profiles among the drivers of severe food insecurity. While simpler models provide greater interpretability, they cannot account for these multidimensional and context-dependent relationships. The insights derived from XGBoost are therefore crucial for understanding the complex mechanisms underlying severe food insecurity and for informing evidence-based, context-specific policy interventions.

The models’ performance was further evaluated using three key plots: prediction error plot, learning curve, and validation curve, each offering unique insights into how well the model performs. The prediction error plot in Fig. 3 is a scatter plot that illustrates the closeness of actual values (x-axis) to predicted values (y-axis). Most points are concentrated on the diagonal line. The tight clustering of points around the diagonal line indicates accurate predictions. Points above the line represent overestimation (high variance), while points below the line indicate underestimation (high bias). This deviation from the diagonal line reflects a prediction error. Notably, some points deviate more substantially at higher rates of severe food insecurity, suggesting that extreme or severe cases are more challenging for the model to predict accurately. These higher residuals may be driven by country-specific or time-specific factors, such as sudden climatic shocks, political instability, conflicts, or economic crises, which introduce additional volatility in severe food insecurity outcomes. Consequently, certain countries or years with extreme events may be systematically mis-predicted.

Feature selection

Using the selected XGBoost model, the study applied three feature selection techniques, including forward feature selection (FFS), backward feature elimination (BFE) and recursive feature elimination (RFE) with cross-validation to automatically select the optimal number of features. These methods iteratively or recursively add or remove features based on their contributions to model performance until no further improvements in predictive accuracy are observed65,81. The results were: FFS (MSE = 7.50, R2 = 0.97), BFE (MSE = 7.33, R2 = 0.97), and RFE (MSE = 6.18, R2 = 0.97), with RFE with cross-validation demonstrating the best overall performance. Among the candidate features, RFE with cross-validation selected 15 features (50%) that maximized predictive performance. Figure 7 highlights the selected key features that significantly contribute to severe food insecurity using recursive feature elimination.

Permutation importance does not offer direct insights into the direction of the relationships (positive or negative) between features and the target variable89,90. Instead, it quantifies the effect of all features on the model’s performance. To determine the direction of these relationships, the study employed the SHAP method. A SHAP is a powerful framework used to interpret the contribution of each feature to model predictions.

The SHAP summary plot in Fig. 8 highlights the relative importance of features in predicting severe food insecurity. Positive SHAP values indicate that, conditional on other features in the model, a feature is associated with an increase in the predicted values of severe food insecurity, while negative values indicate an association with a decrease in the predicted levels90–92. The analysis reveals that higher national-level inflation, climate change, unemployment, malaria incidence, GHG emissions, caloric loss, and cereal import dependency are associated with higher predicted rates of severe food insecurity. In contrast, greater livestock production, cereal crop production, investment inflow, dietary energy supply, dietary protein supply, electricity access, GDP per capita, and political stability are associated with reduced predicted rates. Specifically, high unemployment, conditional on other features, is linked to an increased predicted risk of severe food insecurity, while high livestock production, conditional on other features, is linked to a decreased predicted risk.

In addition to the direction of influence, the SHAP results in Fig. 8 highlight the relative weight of each driver. Features at the top of the plot, including unemployment, livestock production, GHG emissions, and caloric losses, are the most important key drivers influencing predictions of severe food insecurity, whereas features at the bottom, including political stability, cereal import dependency, and climate change, have a comparatively less impact on the prediction. These findings provide valuable evidence on the drivers of severe food insecurity, supporting the design of targeted interventions to mitigate its impact across Africa.

Discussion

The study conducted a comprehensive assessment of the key drivers of severe food insecurity in Africa using multiple machine learning regression algorithms, including Linear regression, Ridge, LASSO, Elastic Net, Support Vector, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoosting, KNN, and ANN. The models were evaluated both before and after hyperparameter tuning using the grid search method with customized settings. The results indicated that hyperparameter tuning substantially enhanced model accuracy and predictive capability, consistent with previous studies62–65. Among the algorithms tested, the XGBoost algorithm demonstrated the highest predictive performance for predicting severe food insecurity. This finding is consistent with the previous studies63,64, further confirming the robustness and generalizability of the XGBoost algorithm in predicting food insecurity outcomes.

Using the best-performing XGBoost model, the permutation importance technique was applied to assess the relative importance of each feature to predictive performance. The findings indicate that the drivers had varying degrees of impact on the model’s predictive capabilities. To refine this further, feature selection methods including FFS, BFE, and RFE were applied to identify the most influential features. RFE outperformed the other methods, aligning with evidence reported in a previous study62. Building on these results, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was then used to determine the direction and magnitude of each feature’s effect on severe food insecurity.

The SHAP analysis indicated that increase in inflation, climate change, unemployment, malaria incidence, GHG emissions, caloric loss, and cereal import dependency are associated with higher predicted rates of severe food insecurity, whereas higher livestock production, cereal crop production, investment inflows, dietary energy supply, dietary protein supply, electricity access, GDP per capita, and political stability are associated with reduced predicted rates. These variables collectively encompass economic, environmental, agricultural, health, and political elements. Among them, the study identified unemployment, livestock production, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and caloric losses as the most influential drivers in predicting severe food insecurity, listed here in priority order. These findings provide a foundation for policymakers seeking to prioritize interventions that most effectively mitigate severe food insecurity across African countries.

The analysis revealed that higher national unemployment rates are associated with higher predicted rates of severe food insecurity. This association may reflect broader macroeconomic conditions such as weakened labor markets, reduced aggregate earnings and slowing economic activity that shape unemployment patterns and constrain population-wide access to sufficient and nutritious food. Persistent increase in unemployment at the national scale, therefore, often signals underlying economic stress that can heighten vulnerability to severe food insecurity. Empirical evidence supports this finding9,13–16. For instance9, using ordinary least-squares regression reported that a 1%-point increase in the unemployment rate was associated with a 0.5%-point rise in the prevalence of food insecurity, while16 employing longitudinal analysis reported a similar effect during the 2007–2009 economic recession in the USA. These findings emphasize the critical importance of employment stabilization and income-support policies in mitigating the adverse effects of economic downturns on household vulnerability to food insecurity.

In contrast, an increase in livestock production was associated with lower predicted values of severe food insecurity, suggesting that greater livestock availability contributes to improved nutrition availability, income stability, and overall food system resilience. Empirical studies35–40 have similarly shown that higher livestock production and ownership significantly reduce the risk of household food insecurity. In many African contexts, livestock serves as a source of income, nutrition, and savings. Households can maintain food access during economic or climatic shocks by selling livestock or their products. However, this relationship is context-dependent. In arid and semi-arid regions, higher dependence on livestock can increase food insecurity risks during climate shocks59,60. Recurrent droughts, land degradation, and overgrazing diminish pasture resources, resulting in livestock losses and increased household vulnerability. These dynamics indicate the need for climate-sensitive and context-dependent strategies to strengthen the productivity and sustainability of livestock systems across Africa.

Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions also represent a significant threat to the sustainability of food systems, a finding consistent with previous studies26–31. GHG emissions intensify climate variabilities, including droughts, floods and broader environmental degradation. These, in turn, reduce the nutritional quality and quantity of agricultural products, including crops, livestock, and fisheries. These climate-induced disruptions ultimately aggravate food insecurity among vulnerable populations. National GHG emissions also reflect patterns of economic development, energy use patterns and industrial structure, making a country’s own emissions less likely to generate immediate local climate effects. Instead, they contribute to global climate change, whose impacts are distributed across regions rather than confined within national boundaries. Accordingly, mitigating these emissions and strengthening climate resilience are essential for ensuring long-term food security.

Similarly, caloric loss was found to significantly affect food stability, aligning with previous studies32,33. This may be attributed to inefficiencies in transportation and logistics systems, limited agro-processing capacity, inadequate post-harvest handling and storage infrastructure, and low public awareness regarding food waste. Addressing these systemic inefficiencies could substantially improve food availability and stability across the continent.

The finding further indicates that higher malaria incidence is associated with higher predicted values of severe food insecurity. Empirical studies support this association17–25 and reported that higher malaria incidence exacerbates food insecurity, undernutrition, and poverty, likely through decreased agricultural productivity, decreased household and national incomes and increased healthcare burdens. For instance, household spending on malaria prevention (bed nets, insecticides) and treatment (medications, hospital visits) diverts resources from food consumption. Moreover, malaria interacts bidirectionally with food insecurity and undernutrition, though its effect is stronger on undernutrition. For example21, reported that nutritional status did not significantly influence malaria incidence (Cox regression), whereas malaria infection significantly worsened nutritional status (linear regression), a finding further confirmed by logistic regression analyses22,23. Since undernutrition is a recognized indicator of food insecurity by FAO, these findings suggest that the causes of undernutrition largely mirror the causes of food insecurity. In this study, malaria incidence is treated as a predictive feature of severe food insecurity; therefore, the SHAP-derived associations may partially capture reverse causation or feedback effects inherent in the bidirectional relationship, rather than strictly unidirectional causal effects. These results emphasize the complex interdependence between malaria and food insecurity, highlighting the need for caution in interpreting the observed associations.

Inflation also constitutes a significant threat to food security in African countries. This study found that higher annual inflation is associated with increased predictive values of severe food insecurity, likely by reducing the purchasing power of low-income households and limiting their ability to meet adequate dietary needs. Empirical studies consistently report a positive relationship between inflation and food insecurity6–9. For instance9, employed ordinary least squares regression and found that a 1% increase in annual inflation, measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI-U), was associated with a 0.5%-point increase in the prevalence of food insecurity. Similarly7, used an econometric model and demonstrated that rising inflation reduces consumer purchasing power, elevates food prices, lowers food accessibility and exacerbates the risk of food insecurity. These findings emphasize the importance of effective inflation management and price stabilization policies to protect vulnerable populations from economic shocks, thereby mitigating food insecurity.

Political instability and conflict also pose significant threats to food security. This study found that higher political stability is associated with lower predictive rates of severe food insecurity, suggesting that countries with more stable governance tend to experience lower severe food insecurity rates. This result aligns with the previous studies10–12. Political stability enhances institutional effectiveness, policy continuity, and public trust, which are essential for efficient resource allocation and the successful implementation of food security programs. They also attract both domestic and foreign investment, foster infrastructure development, and reduce conflict-related disruptions in food production and supply chains. In contrast, political instability undermines governance, distort markets, and limit social protection coverages, thereby exacerbating food insecurity.

Furthermore, the study identified cereal crop production, climate change, investment inflow, energy supply and protein supply, access to electricity and GDP per capita as influential predictors in predicting severe food insecurity. This finding is supported by previous studies. For instance, research findings from2–5 indicate that climate change is a critical factor affecting food security. Unpredictable weather patterns, extreme temperatures, and altered rainfall disrupt agricultural productivity, leading to lower crop yields and reduced food supply stability. The findings of this study indicated that a higher value of climate change is associated with increased predicted values of severe food insecurity rates. However, this variable ranks among the least important features in the SHAP analysis, indicating a lower predictive influence than suggested by previous literature. This may be due to the relatively short study period (2015–2021) and the use of temperature change as a proxy for climate impacts, which may not adequately capture climate variability and extreme events. Conversely, higher cereal crop production lowers food insecurity by improving food supply and affordability40,93. In contrast, intensive crop production can degrade soil fertility, reduce water availability, and disrupt ecosystem services, thereby undermining long-term food security61. Higher foreign direct investment (FDI) inflows are associated with reduced predicted values of severe food insecurity rates, potentially reflecting the role of FDI in promoting economic growth and enabling better resource allocation for food systems and infrastructure development94. However, the effects of FDI are context-dependent. While FDI in agriculture, infrastructure, and manufacturing may enhance food security by increasing productivity, generating employment, and facilitating market access, land-intensive or extractive investments may displace smallholders and reduce the availability of food for the local people42. This suggests that the impact of FDI on severe food insecurity depends on investment type, governance quality, and sectoral allocation. Energy supply and protein supply enhance dietary quantity and quality and consequently reduce food insecurity47,48. Access to electricity was associated with lower predictive risk of severe food insecurity, in line with earlier evidence50. This may be due to its contribution to food preservation, processing and irrigation, which are essential for sustaining food production and availability. Furthermore, higher GDP per capita reflects a stronger economy, often associated with employment opportunities and household income, enabling better access to nutritious food43,44.

A key limitation of this study is that it relies on aggregated national-level data, which may obscure subnational heterogeneity and mask local patterns essential for designing context-specific interventions. As a result, the observed predicted associations and relative importance of identified drivers may be over- or underestimated and may not accurately reflect conditions at specific community levels. Consequently, policy recommendations based solely on national-level findings may be less targeted or effective for specific communities. In addition, the study doesn’t assess whether the identified drivers operate uniformly across countries or compare vulnerability across subgroups defined by factors such as the share of agriculture in GDP or geographic characteristics (e.g., landlocked vs. port access). To address these limitations, future research should incorporate district or household level data and apply spatially explicit models to identify spatially varying risk factors and to comprehensively capture local and regional disparities in the dynamics of severe food insecurity across Africa. Comparative analyses across country subgroups could further elucidate how structural, economic, and environmental factors differentially influence severe food insecurity, enabling more context-specific actionable insights for policy interventions.

Another limitation of this study is that it does not explicitly account for the spatio-temporal dependencies inherent in severe food insecurity outcomes. Although temporal dependency was partially addressed using a chronological train-test split, spatial dependence among proximate countries was not incorporated. This is particularly relevant because many of the neighboring African countries share similar climatic conditions, economic characteristics, and food system vulnerabilities. As a result, countries included in the training and testing sets may not be fully independent, potentially affecting the identified predictive associations and the reported performance metrics. Future studies should therefore employ spatio-temporal deep learning models capable of integrating heterogeneous datasets and capturing dynamic spatio-temporal interactions more comprehensively. Such approaches may enhance predictive accuracy, improve detection of emerging severe food insecurity hotspots, and provide more reliable forward-looking insights to inform proactive policymaking and intervention planning.

Conclusions, policy implications and recommendations

The study provides a comprehensive investigation into the key drivers of severe food insecurity in Africa using various machine learning approaches. The comparative evaluation demonstrates that XGBoost emerged as the best-performing model for accurately modeling and predicting severe food insecurity in Africa at the country level. The model result shows that the drivers considered exhibited varying degrees of influence on model performance. Moreover, RFE proved to be the most effective feature selection technique for identifying the key predictors influencing severe food insecurity. The SHAP results further indicate that increase in inflation, climate change, unemployment, malaria incidence, GHG emissions, caloric loss, and cereal import dependency are associated with higher predicted values of severe food insecurity, whereas higher values of livestock production, cereal crop production, investment inflows, dietary energy supply, dietary protein supply, electricity access, GDP per capita, and political stability are associated with lower predicted values. These variables represent a mix of economic, environmental, agricultural, health, and political elements. Among these drivers, unemployment, livestock production, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and caloric losses are identified as the most influential drivers in predicting severe food insecurity across Africa.

Addressing these key drivers has important policy implications for mitigating severe food insecurity in Africa. The findings indicate that a higher national unemployment rate is strongly associated with increased predictive values of severe food insecurity, reflecting the influence of labor-market constraints on household food access. Governments should therefore expand job creation programs through labor-intensive public works projects tailored to local labor market, establishing training programs aligned with labor-market needs (e.g., construction, digital services, agro-processing), promoting youth and women’s entrepreneurship with start-ups, strengthening small and medium enterprises (SMEs) to generate sustainable employment, and expanding social protection and safety net schemes tailored to country-specific vulnerabilities of economic, social, and ecological contexts.

In parallel, the findings further imply that strengthening livestock production can significantly enhance food availability, income stability and dietary diversity in many settings; however, in arid and semi-arid regions, greater dependence on livestock may increase vulnerability during climate shocks. Accordingly, policies should adopt context-dependent livestock interventions, including supporting sustainable livestock productivity in regions where ecological conditions allow, expanding market access, promoting livestock diversification, and improving value chains for dairy and meat products. In pastoral and agro-pastoral regions, policies should emphasize drought-resistant breeds, rangeland restoration, early-warning systems, and livestock insurance schemes. Investment in veterinary services, feed and pasture management, and agricultural extension programs is also essential to boost productivity, household income, resilience and food security.

Similarly, the findings demonstrate that greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions pose significant threats to the sustainability and stability of food systems. Policymakers should promote climate-smart agricultural practices through the adoption of drought-tolerant crop and livestock varieties, support mixed crop-livestock farming system, encourage the use of short-maturity crops, expand renewable energy adoption, and implement sustainable land and water management practices, and strengthen disaster risk reduction measures. These actions can enhance food system resilience, protect vulnerable populations, and ensure long-term food security across Africa. In addition, reducing caloric losses through improved post-harvest handling, storage, and transportation is essential for enhancing food availability. Governments should invest in cold chain infrastructure, agro-processing industries, logistics systems, and implement public awareness campaigns on food loss and waste reduction. These measures can make national food systems more efficient, equitable and sustainable. In general, governments should address macroeconomic instability, climate variability, political unrest, and public health burdens to strengthen the national food systems. In light of these insights, policymakers should prioritize interventions targeting these factors when formulating and implementing national strategies to effectively mitigate severe food insecurity across African countries.
