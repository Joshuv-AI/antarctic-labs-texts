Femtosecond spectroscopy with paired single photons: Emulating a double-slit experiment in the time-frequency domain
Authors: EunHo Hong, EunSeo Jang, JunWoo Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Time-resolved spectroscopy has long served as a cornerstone technique for investigating the photophysics of materials. However, conventional methods based on intense optical pulses and ensemble measurements often fail to resolve quantum dynamics due to spectral overlap, coherent artifacts, and multiphoton effects. To overcome these limitations, single-photon transient stimulated emission (SP-TSE) has been proposed as a femtosecond spectroscopy technique using quantum light and detection principles. Here, we theoretically demonstrate frequency-resolved SP-TSE. Unlike semiclassical techniques, SP-TSE reveals a strong dependence of the time-resolved spectrum on the frequency-resolving method. With a tunable optical filter, the observed spectrum reflects a quantum double-slit interference in the time-frequency domain. In contrast, interferometric detection produces spectra resembling semiclassical results, but the signal emerges specifically from the second harmonic frequency window due to quantum interference. This framework provides insight into ultrafast quantum processes and suggests a foundation for future applications in quantum spectroscopy and technologies.

INTRODUCTION

Femtosecond time-resolved spectroscopy provides an intuitive approach to probing the ultrafast processes that lead to light-induced phenomena in materials. By leveraging ultrashort optical pulses, these techniques achieve femtosecond time resolution, enabling the detailed investigation of dynamic events occurring on ultrafast timescales. In addition, the coherent excitation of multiple eigenstates by femtosecond pulses enables us to detect quantum beatings, which arise from the superposition of excited eigenstates in an ensemble-averaged manner (1, 2). Among these techniques, transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy—also known as pump-probe spectroscopy—has emerged as the most widely used. In TA, an intense optical pulse (pump) photoexcites the material, and the subsequent changes in its spectral response are monitored using a weaker probe pulse. Further extension to two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy allows for the exploration of more complicated dynamics in materials with complex energy structures, such as those found in photosynthetic energy transfer systems (3–6).

Conventional femtosecond time-resolved spectroscopy relies on intense optical pulses and is inherently semiclassical in its theoretical description. In such experiments, the optical fields are treated classically while the material system is described quantum mechanically. Although this approach has been instrumental in advancing our understanding of photoinitiated dynamics (7, 8), it is subject to critical limitations. First, spectral overlap among signals originating from different interaction pathways often results in congested spectra, complicating the disentanglement and analysis of the quantum dynamics of specific eigenstates. Second, coherent artifacts, which are nonlinear signals arising from an unintended order of light-matter interaction, can obscure the desired signal, particularly when optical pulses overlap in time (e.g., at near-zero time delays in TA experiments) (9). Last, the use of intense optical pulses inherently induces higher-order interactions, such as multiphoton absorption or multiparticle excitation (10), which deviate from the operational conditions of natural and artificial photodevices that typically function under weak-light environments. These effects mask the intrinsic dynamics that are of primary interest.

Over the past decade, quantum optical light sources have emerged as powerful tools for advancing femtosecond spectroscopy beyond the limitations of semiclassical methods (11–16). In this context, a quantum-optical femtosecond time-resolved spectroscopy technique, known as single-photon transient stimulated emission (SP-TSE), has recently been introduced to overcome these challenges (17). SP-TSE is conceptually analogous to TA spectroscopy but replaces intense optical pulses with single-photon pairs as the pump and probe. By using coincidence counting methods, SP-TSE selectively detects the nonlinear response arising from the stimulated emission (SE) interaction pathway. This pathway selectivity is a critical advantage, as it removes spectral overlap effects and coherent artifacts present in semiclassical time-resolved spectra. Moreover, coincidence counting effectively reduces dark count noise, a dominant limitation in single-photon experiments.

In this study, we investigate the spectroscopic characteristics of SP-TSE spectra obtained using two distinct detection schemes: a dispersive spectrometer, referred to as a monochromator in this manuscript, and an interferometer. In semiclassical spectroscopy experiments, these instruments yield identical spectral information. However, SP-TSE spectra measured by a monochromator and an interferometer result in distinct time-resolved spectra, reflecting the fundamental quantum mechanical properties of single photons. While the characteristics of the SP-TSE spectrum measured using monochromators are governed by the uncertainty principle, those of SP-TSE interferometry align more closely with semiclassical time-resolved spectroscopy in terms of time and frequency resolution. By elucidating these unique spectral features, SP-TSE not only addresses the inherent limitations of traditional femtosecond spectroscopy but also paves the way for the development of advanced quantum optical methodologies.

RESULTS

Figure 1A illustrates the optical layout of SP-TSE, while Fig. 1B schematically illustrates the interaction pathway in a three-level material system consisting of a ground state, ∣g〉 , and two excited states, ∣e1〉 and ∣e2〉 . Paired single photons are generated via spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) in a nonlinear crystal. One photon (the pump photon) is absorbed by the material, creating a superposition state (left and middle of Fig. 1B). Following a designated time delay, Tw , the second photon (the probe photon) interacts with the superposition state, inducing SE (right of Fig. 1B). The probe photon exiting the sample is directed to a coincidence counter, comprising a beam splitter (BS) and two single-photon detectors (SPDs). Because of the particle nature of photons, simultaneous detection by the two SPDs occurs only when the incident probe photon is in a two-photon state generated via SE. This interaction pathway selectivity is a defining feature of quantum optical spectroscopy, enabling it to overcome the limitations of conventional time-resolved spectroscopy (13, 14, 17).

The final wave function comprises six types of eigenstates, which may be designated as (i) the pump and probe transmitted state, (ii) the pump absorbed state, (iii) the probe absorbed state, (iv) the excited-state absorption (or pump and probe absorbed) state, (v) the pump SE state, and (vi) the probe SE state. The elimination of the other types of eigenstates states the SE pathway selectivity of SP-TSE. This interaction pathway selectivity enables us to exclude the contribution of ground-state bleach and excited-state absorption, as well as coherent artifacts.

Unlike SP-TSEm, SP-TSEi spectroscopy reveals quantum beating even when the two SE bands are clearly distinguishable. SP-TSEm uses an optical frequency-filtering operator for spectral resolution (a Gaussian function in Eq. 5) that modulates the photonic wave function and collapses the superposition state, rendering its properties indiscernible. In contrast, SP-TSEi uses an interferometer for spectral resolution, which preserves the photonic superposition state, thereby enabling the observation of quantum beating. A similar interpretation applies to the observation of coherent wave packets in semiclassical time-resolved spectroscopy (e.g., TA). In TA, coherent wave packets can be observed, even when a monochromator with high spectral resolution is used. This is because TA performs ensemble measurements on multiple particles. Although the spectral response of a particular eigenstate may be selectively measured, the inability to distinguish which specific particle contributed to the response means that the superposition state is not destroyed from the perspective of the entire system, allowing coherent wave packets to be observed.

These findings underscore the fundamental distinction between SP-TSEm and SP-TSEi spectroscopy in their handling of photonic wave functions and spectral resolution. The collapse of superposition states in SP-TSEm due to frequency filtering contrasts sharply with the preservation of these states in SP-TSEi, where interferometric detection retains the superposition state necessary for observing quantum beating. This difference highlights the impact of frequency-resolving techniques in SP-TSE spectroscopy.

DISCUSSION

The quantum mechanical characteristics resulting in the frequency resolution dependence in SP-TSEm spectroscopy (Fig. 2, A and B) resembles Young’s double-slit experiment with a single particle. In the double-slit experiment, the wave function of the particle is diffracted at the first slit, and it meets two slits. This corresponds to the generation of a superposition state consisting of two spatial eigenstates, each associated with one of the two paths through the second slits. The two wave functions interfere when they overlap spatially, so that the interference fringe appears on a detection screen as a periodic probability density distribution (Fig. 3A).

Recently, a phenomenon was reported in which interference fringes appeared in the spectrum of light reflected from a solid material when it was subjected to two intense optical pulses separated by a fixed time interval (23). This was interpreted as the result of placing a double slit in the time domain, leading to diffraction and interference in the frequency domain. In SP-TSE, the interaction between the pump photon and an optical sample creates a superposition state consisting of multiple eigenstates (left in Fig. 3B). This process resembles the creation of the spatial superposition state by the first slit in the double-slit experiment. Subsequently, the superposition state of the sample is observed by using coincidence counting for the probe photon after the probe interacts with the sample. Last, the interference signal originating from the superposition state appears as a quantum beating—an oscillatory signal over Tw (right in Fig. 3B).

It is well known that the interference fringe in a double-slit experiment disappears when the pathway of the particle is distinguishable (24–27). In other words, the spatial superposition state must be preserved until the particle is detected to observe the interference between the two spatial eigenstates. Blocking one of the two second slits in the double-slit experiment removes the wave function generated on the blocked pathway. Because the pathway of the particle is identified, the interference fringe is no longer observable (Fig. 3C).

Using a high resolution monochromator in SP-TSEm spectroscopy can clarify an eigenstate of the sample in which SE has taken place. The identification of the excited state corresponds to revealing the light-matter interaction pathway. Because the monochromator makes the light-matter interaction pathway of the detected photon distinguishable, the interference fringe (quantum beating) attributed to the superposition state cannot be measured (Fig. 3D) like the disappearance of the spatial interference fringe in Fig. 3C. In semiclassical femtosecond spectroscopy, the presence of coherent wave packet oscillations often disturbs the kinetic analysis of a time-resolved spectrum. Such an oscillatory feature can remain observable in SP-TSEm spectroscopy. By adjusting Δ , one can control the spectral resolution, thereby enabling a more accurate measurement of relaxation or energy transfer rates.

In addition, frequency-resolved SP-TSEm enables selective access to quantum beating associated with specific eigenstates, as the spectral resolution allows the isolation of beating signals corresponding only to targeted transition frequencies. In conventional SP-TSE, such selectivity is not available due to the lack of spectral discrimination, making it difficult to identify which eigenstates contribute to the measured dynamics. Unlike semiclassical approaches, which detect all quantum beatings (e.g., coherent vibrational wave packets) associated with a given eigenstate, frequency-resolved SP-TSEm provides greater selectivity. It allows for the extraction of information specific to a particular superposition state, corresponding to a single off-diagonal element (i.e., a coherence term) in the density matrix. This capability makes the method particularly valuable for investigating quantum decoherence processes in a controlled and state-selective manner, with potential applications in quantum devices.

SP-TSEi spectrum exhibits two distinct spectroscopic characteristics compared to SP-TSEm spectrum. One is the fact that both high time resolution and high frequency resolution are achievable. This characteristic, similar to that of semiclassical time-resolved spectroscopy, arises because the interferometer resolves frequencies without disrupting the superposition state of probe photons. The second characteristic of SP-TSEi spectrum is that the spectral response appears at their second-harmonic frequencies ( ωdet=2ω1 and ωdet=2ω2 ). In the classical light interferometry, recording the product of signals from two photodetectors, which correspond to coincidence counting in quantum optics, can yield a second-harmonic component due to the multiplication of two out-of-phase interferograms. However, this merely results from computational postprocessing and is fundamentally distinct from the second-harmonic component observed in SP-TSEi, which arises intrinsically from the quantum mechanical nature of single photons.

To understand the amplitudes of resonant and second-harmonic spectral responses, the relative phase of the photonic wave functions in a Mach-Zehnder interferometer combined with a coincidence counting detector, which is described in Eq. 6, should be explained in detail. The first term ( iTRT ) in Eq. 6 includes the information about two photons transmitted at the first BS, where one of the photons is reflected and the other is transmitted at the second BS (Fig. 4A). Because they do not undergo a phase shift by the delay stage, their photonic wave function can be used as a phase reference ( ϕ=0).

The last term ( −iRRTexp[−i2ωτ] ) shows the relative phase of two photons reflected at the first BS (Fig. 4B). Because the two photons pass through the delay stage together, the relative phase of their wave function would be 2ωτ . As described in Eq. 7, the measured SP-TSEi signal is equivalent to the expectation value of the coincidence counting operator over τ, which includes the modulus square of Eq. 6. A second-harmonic component is formed as the product of the τ-independent term (the first term in Eq. 6) and the term with the phase of 2 ωτ (the last term). Both terms describe two photons that are not separated by the first BS (Fig. 4, A and B), so that it can be said that the interference between the two-photon states propagating over distinct pathways results in the second-harmonic spectral responses from the phenomenological viewpoint.

The second ( iTRTexp[−iωτ] ) and third ( −iRRTexp[−iωτ] ) terms inform the relative phase of two photons separated at the first BS. The second term describes one photon that is transmitted twice, while the other photon is first reflected and then transmitted (Fig. 4C). The third term describes one photon undergoing two reflections, with the other photon first transmitted and then reflected (Fig. 4D). These terms have a relative phase of ωτ because one of the two photons passes through the delay generator in both cases. The ω dependence with respect to the τ scan states that the two terms are the origin of the resonant frequency components. The second and third terms interfere destructively due to a π phase difference (the negative sign of the third term), so that the resonant bands disappear when R=T.

The spectral responses appearing in the resonant frequency window are attributed to the interference between a wave function of two-photon state (the first and last terms in Eq. 6) and a wave function representing the two single photons separated by the first BS (the second and third terms). Accordingly, the amplitude of a resonant band is proportional to RT(T−R)2=R(1−R)(1−2R)2 , while the amplitude of a second-harmonic band is proportional to R2T2=R2(1−R)2 . Ignoring the other parameters but R, the maximum amplitude of the fundamental frequency component is 3/64 at R=1/4 and that of the second-harmonic component is 1/8 at R=1/2 . This implies that setting R=1/2 is advantageous for recording only the second-harmonic component, without measuring the fundamental component, when considering the overall signal magnitude.

From the perspective of data acquisition, removing the resonant component is advantageous. Most materials analyzed by SP-TSE spectroscopy tend to have a long coherence time related to the transitions involved in the experiment. For the interferometry of such systems, undersampling is necessitated for rapid data acquisition. Otherwise, the interferometric time delay scan would require thousands of data points. In undersampled SP-TSE interferometry, aliasing becomes challenging, especially when both the resonant and second-harmonic components coexist ( R≠1/2 ). Therefore, setting R=1/2 to eliminate the resonant component is the optimal condition for acquiring signals rapidly without artifacts.

For studying ultrafast kinetics in single-particle systems, SP-TSE spectroscopy can serve as an effective alternative to fluorescence up-conversion (FLUC). FLUC is a femtosecond time-resolved fluorescence technique that shares the same light-matter interaction pathway selectivity as SP-TSE. However, its time resolution is intrinsically limited by geometrical factors such as group-velocity and phase-front mismatch (28). In contrast, SP-TSE, as a wave mixing method, is free from such constraints. Moreover, FLUC detects a signal formed by the convolution of fluorescence with the gate pulse in both temporal and spectral domains. This spectral convolution effect can suppress quantum beating signals, particularly when a broadband gate pulse is used to improve time resolution. While SP-TSE cannot replace FLUC for ensemble measurements, it provides richer information in single-particle experiments than what time-resolved spectra from FLUC offer.

Since the controllability of superposition state is an essential factor of a quantum device, SP-TSE spectroscopy could pioneer advanced applications in quantum devices such as quantum computing. Quantum devices using nonlinear light-matter interactions have the potential to rapidly evolve due to their structural similarities to devices based on linear quantum optics, which are already widely used in quantum computing (29–31). This convergence of nonlinear and linear quantum technologies could pave the way for a new era of highly efficient and powerful quantum devices, broadening the scope of practical applications in the field.

In addition to its theoretical insights, SP-TSE is highly feasible for experimental implementation. Unlike techniques that rely on relative signal detection, SP-TSE enables absolute signal measurement, similar to transient grating or fluorescence measurements. This characteristic substantially reduces the influence of detector dark counts, thereby improving the signal-to-noise ratio and overall experimental practicality. Recently, quantum-optical picosecond single-photon time-resolved spectroscopy has been successfully demonstrated, and the parameters necessary for implementing SP-TSE are already well documented in that work (32). Given that the theoretical foundation has been established and validated, we believe that the experimental realization of quantum-optical femtosecond time-resolved spectroscopy is within reach.

An important consideration for the experimental implementation of SP-TSE is the impact of multiphoton components originating from the SPDC process as a photon-pair source. SPDC does not generate pure single-photon pairs but rather a two-mode squeezed vacuum containing multiphoton contributions (33, 34). The influence of multiphoton components can be assessed separately for the pump and the probe. For the pump, the probability of multiphoton events such as two-photon absorption or sequential absorption and SE is very low. These processes typically scale with the square of the sample’s absorption probability and can therefore be considered negligible under most experimental conditions. Similarly, the interaction between the multiphotonic probe and the sample is also expected to have minimal impact on the SP-TSE signal.

A more notable concern arises at the detection stage, where the presence of multiphotons in the probe can reduce the purity of the measured signal. Since SP-TSE relies on coincidence counting module using two SPDs, variations in the number of probe photons can lead to changes in the coincidence counting probability. While states with four or more photons cause only minor variations, two- and three-photon states can induce significant changes in the coincidence counting rate, typically in the range of 12.5 to 25%. For example, with a 50:50 beamsplitter and ideal detectors, the coincidence probability is 50% for two photons, 75% for three photons, and 87.5% for four photons. If two-photon states constitute even 10% of the single-photon signal, up to 2.5 to 5% of the total measured signal could be attributed to these undesired contributions, which is clearly not a negligible amount. In an actual experiment, it would be necessary to construct a three-point coincidence counting module composed of two beamsplitters and three SPDs to verify the signal purity and suppress such effects. In this configuration, the SP-TSE signal should ideally yield zero detection probability, and any observed coincidences would indicate contamination from multiphoton components.

By incorporating spectral resolution into the SP-TSE framework, it becomes possible to investigate quantum mechanical features that remain unobservable in frequency-unresolved implementations. When combined with a tunable optical filter, SP-TSE exhibits the disappearance of quantum beating, which can be interpreted as a manifestation of interaction pathway distinguishability and is analogous to the fundamental trade-off between time and frequency resolution. These characteristics enable selective access to quantum decoherence dynamics by excluding quantum beating signals originating from superposition states irrelevant to the target transitions. In contrast, theoretical analysis of interferometric SP-TSE indicates that quantum beating signals persist despite high frequency resolution, due to the indistinguishability of two-photon pathways. Notably, the major spectral response of the system is embedded in the two-photon interference pattern of the probe photons. The theoretical framework presented here may contribute to the foundation for future quantum devices based on nonlinear light-matter interactions involving single photons.

METHODS

To gain deeper insights from TA and other multidimensional spectroscopy experiments, various spectroscopy theories have been developed. Among these, time-dependent perturbation theory in the interaction picture is the most widely used framework for describing nonlinear optical spectroscopy, including femtosecond time-resolved spectroscopy (7, 8). This approach models the interaction between light and matter as perturbations that facilitate transitions between eigenstates, with the time evolution of the material dictated by the time-independent Hamiltonian both before and after the interaction. The light-matter interaction is typically characterized by the product of the transition dipole moment and the electric field. In semiclassical nonlinear spectroscopy, the electric field is treated as a classical entity, which inherently limits the ability to capture the quantum mechanical nature of photons. Consequently, to describe femtosecond spectroscopy using quantum optical light sources, the semiclassical framework must be supplanted by a time-dependent perturbation theory that incorporates the quantum mechanical properties of photons. These quantum features, such as quantum entanglement and the particle-like behavior of photons, grant access to quantum dynamics that remain imperceptible within traditional semiclassical spectroscopy.

Equation 9 does not account for the frequency entanglement commonly observed in photon pairs generated via SPDC. While this simplification facilitates theoretical analysis, it also reflects a necessary condition for the generation of SP-TSE signals. Considering terms up to the second-order interaction is sufficient, as the primary contribution to the SE process arises from precisely two light-matter interactions: one with the pump photon and another with the probe photon. Higher-order terms (third-order or beyond) are therefore deemed negligible in the context of the SP-TSE experiment.

The operator U^0(tf,ti) in Eq. 12 represents the time evolution governed by the time-independent Hamiltonian of the optical sample. The light-matter interaction Hamiltonian, H^I(t;Tw) , appearing in the first- and second-order terms of the time evolution operator, is defined as follows (18)H^I(t;Tw)=eD^⋅E^(t;Tw)(13)where e is the electronic charge, D^ is the dipole operator, and E^(t) represents the electric field operator.

For a more general treatment, the spatial dependence of the electric field, E^(r,t;T) , should be explicitly considered. The electric field propagates with a specific wave vector and may vary spatially across the sample. However, our analysis focuses on the local interaction within the tightly focused region where the two photons overlap on the sample. Consequently, the spatial dependence of the electric field is neglected, allowing us to approximate E^(t;Tw) as acting at the position of the sample. Any angular dependence of the sample response is minimized by ensuring that the beams are tightly focused with a small included angle between the pump and probe, effectively approximating normal incidence conditions.

The transition dipole operator, D^ , can be decomposed into raising ( π† ) and lowering ( π ) operators, which satisfy the relevant selection rules, with respect to the model three-level system. Conceptually, π corresponds to transitions from an excited state to a lower state (e.g., emission), while π† describes the reverse process (e.g., absorption). Only dipole-allowed transitions contribute to the light-matter interaction.
