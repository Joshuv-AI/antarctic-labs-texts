Knowledge, attitudes, practices and ethics related to artificial intelligence among nursing students: a national cross-sectional survey in China
Authors: Hui-Ying Fan, Qing Zhou, Li-Li Deng, Lu-Lu Sun, I-Lam Tam, Zhen Wan, Xiao-Li Zhang, Meng-Qian Wang, Hoi Kei Ho, Chee H Ng, Yu-Tao Xiang, Gang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming healthcare. Current and future healthcare workforce, including nursing students, require sufficient understanding of responsible AI use. However, data about knowledge, attitudes, practices and ethics regarding AI use in this population is scarce. This national study comprehensively assessed AI-related knowledge, attitudes, practices and ethics among nursing students in China, including the socio-demographic and educational factors associated with these professional domains.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted between June and August 2025 across 32 provinces, autonomous regions and municipalities in China. A total of 10,268 nursing undergraduates and vocational college students completed a newly developed 22-item Knowledge, Attitudes, Practices and Ethics questionnaire covering four domains (Cronbach’s α = 0.79–0.96). Univariable analyses and multiple linear regression were used to examine socio-demographic factors of AI-related knowledge, attitudes, practices and ethics.ResultsNursing students reported moderate level of AI knowledge (18.17 ± 4.90, total score 24), moderately positive attitudes (11.96 ± 2.49, total score 20), relatively good ethical awareness (11.60 ± 3.27, total score 16), but only limited engagement in practice (8.29 ± 5.04, total score 28). Of the participants, only 6.8%-9.6% reported “often” and “always” using AI tools for academic and personal tasks. In multivariable models, male gender, urban residence, higher economic status, and intention to pursue nursing as a career were independently associated with higher scores across the knowledge, attitude, and ethics domains (all P < 0.05). Compared with vocational college students, undergraduates had significantly higher scores on attitude, practice and ethics domains (all P < 0.05). Age was positively associated with all domains, although only the 19–20-year group had significantly higher scores in the practice domain (P < 0.001).ConclusionThe findings highlight the need for a supportive educational environment with guidance to enable nursing students to use artificial intelligence appropriately and responsibly when needed, particularly among vocational college students and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. Integrating AI competencies into nursing education and linking them to future career development and clinical practice may help bridge the gap between positive attitudes and limited practical use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04970-9.Keywords: Nursing students, Artificial intelligence, Knowledge, Attitude, Practice, Ethics

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems designed to perform tasks that usually require human-like intelligence such as learning, problem-solving, and decision-making [1]. Over the past decade, AI has rapidly advanced and is widely used in daily life, including in the areas of healthcare, nursing practice and education. In clinical nursing, AI is used increasingly in patient monitoring, decision making, automated documentation, and workflow optimization, offering significant improvements in nursing care quality and efficiency [2–4]. Nursing education is also rapidly evolving, with AI tools, such as virtual simulations and generative AI platforms, enhancing pedagogies and preparing students for a technology-driven practice environment [5]. China is at the forefront of AI developments with innovations such as DeepSeek, which is a generative AI system for knowledge synthesis, and Tsinghua University’s AI Agent Hospital, where trials using AI virtual doctors demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy [6].

Despite the rapid development of AI, its integration into nursing training practice and education is still in its early development, particularly in China. For nursing students, which comprise the future backbone of the healthcare workforce, this situation brings both opportunities and challenges [7]. Students often experience inconsistent curricular exposure to AI, uncertainty regarding its impact on professional roles, and limited opportunities to develop practical competencies [7]. Studies found that the attitudes of practicing nurses, and their use of AI significantly predicted their intent to stay in practice, and barriers to AI were factors that undermined their retention in the workforce [8]. These challenges highlight the urgent need for equipping nursing students with adequate AI knowledge, and developing professional attitudes and practical skills to work effectively in a technology-driven practice environment [9]. Therefore, assessing nursing students across such knowledge, skills and practice domains is essential to identify gaps and inform targeted educational reforms [10].

The knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) model [11] provides a structured framework for assessing of one’s understanding (knowledge), perception (attitudes), and behavior (practices) related to specific issues. In addition, ethical considerations are critical in the context of various risks associated with AI use, such as algorithmic bias, accuracy concerns, dependency, plagiarism and breaches of confidentiality [12]. Future nursing practitioners need to not only acquire proficiency in AI tools but also ensure ethical and responsible use [13]. Therefore, extending the KAP framework to include ethics provides a more comprehensive assessment of AI adoption by nursing students [14].

Recent research on AI-related knowledge, attitudes, practices and ethics (KAPE) has been limited to novel instruments focusing on only one or two areas or those that neglected ethical considerations, rather than all four domains simultaneously [15]. Existing studies have included heterogeneous samples, such as medical students [16], nurses [8], or mixed groups [17, 18], with relatively few studies targeting nursing students specifically. For instance, Mariano et al. [17] examined AI KAP in 349 participants from Saudi Arabia, including nurses, nursing educators and students. To date, the only large-scale survey specifically on nursing students recruited 1,713 participants from 10 Arab countries [19]. However, this study did not assess practice or ethics in relation to AI use. This aspect is important given that a recent qualitative US survey of 99 undergraduate nursing students further revealed frequent use of generative AI tools alongside dominant concerns regarding trustworthiness, dependency, plagiarism, and confidentiality related to AI use [20].

Hence, the generalizability and applicability of current literature are limited due to small sample size, underrepresentation of nursing students as a distinct subgroup, and differences in curricula and resource availability across countries. To fill these gaps, we conducted a large-scale national survey to: (1) comprehensively assess AI-related KAPE among nursing students in China, and (2) identify socio-demographic and educational factors associated with AI-related KAPE. Understanding nursing students’ KAPE on AI is essential to inform curriculum design, professional development, and policy decisions to prepare the next generation of nurses for AI-enhanced care and education.

Methods

Study design and samples

We conducted a nationwide, multicenter, cross-sectional survey across all 32 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities of China from June to August 2025, using a convenience sampling strategy. The recruitment covered both undergraduate and higher vocational programs, ensuring diverse sociodemographic backgrounds (e.g. sex, urban/ rural family residences) in the sample. Eligible participants were: (1) students currently enrolled in nursing programs at either undergraduate or higher vocational colleges, and (2) able to understand the survey purpose and independently complete the questionnaire. Following methods used in previous studies [21, 22], data were collected through the “Wenjuanxing” platform, which was embedded into the widely used WeChat application in China. A Quick Response (QR) code linked to the online invitation and questionnaire was generated and distributed to students by designated coordinators at participating universities, vocational colleges and affiliated teaching hospitals. The survey was shared on the official student communication channels (e.g., class-based WeChat groups and academic administrative platforms), and with different hospitals for nursing interns during clinical placements. Participation was voluntary, and the QR code remained active throughout the survey period, thus allowing convenient access via smartphone.

Measurements

The survey consisted of two parts: socio-demographic information and Knowledge, Attitudes, Practices and Ethics regarding Artificial Intelligence (KAPE) questionnaire. Socio-demographic variables included age, sex, education type (undergraduate vs. higher vocational college), household residence (urban vs. rural), perceived economic status (poor, fair or good), and intention to pursue nursing as a career.

Based on previous studies [23, 24], KAP theory [25] and existing frameworks for AI literacy among college students [26], the self-reported KAPE questionnaire was developed to assess knowledge, attitudes, practices and ethics towards AI among nursing students (see Supplementary Table 1). Prior to the main survey, a pilot study was conducted among 23 nursing students from six universities across five provinces to assess the questionnaire clarity and refine the item wording and response options. The final questionnaire consisted of 22 items across four domains: (1) Knowledge domain (6 items) assessing the students’ factual understanding of AI fundamentals and applications; (2) Attitude domain (5 items) assessing personal beliefs and perspectives on AI in healthcare and nursing education; (3) Practice domain (7 items) assessing the frequency of engaging in AI-related learning or practical activities; (4) Ethics domain (4 items) evaluating perceptions of ethical considerations in AI use. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale (0 = very inconsistent/ strongly disagree/ never to 4 = very consistent/ strongly agree/ always), with the first and second item of ethics domain reverse-coded. Higher scores indicated more favorable knowledge, positive attitudes, higher frequency of use or stronger ethical awareness regarding AI. Domain scores were calculated by summing the relevant items: Knowledge (range 0–24), Attitudes (range 0–20), Practices (range 0–28), and Ethics (range 0–16). The item-level content validity index ranged from 0.88 to 1.00, and the scale-level content validity index was 0.93, indicating good overall content validity. Structural validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin value was 0.93 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (P < 0.001). Four factors with eigenvalues > 1 were extracted, accounting for 72.1% of the total variance. Internal consistency of this questionnaire was satisfactory, with Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.79 to 0.96 across domains and 0.78 for the overall questionnaire [27].

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2) [28]. Normality assumptions were evaluated using Q–Q plots. Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Associations between socio-demographic characteristics and the score of each KAPE domain were explored firstly using univariable analyses. Independent-samples t tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for variables with two and three categories, respectively. Multiple linear regression models were then used to identify independent factors of AI-related knowledge, attitudes, practice, and ethics, separately. The total score of each KAPE domain was treated as a continuous dependent variable. Socio-demographic variables significant in univariable analyses were entered as independent variables into the models. Regression coefficients (β) were used to quantify associations between factors and each KAPE domain. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics and status of KAPE

A total of 10,268 nursing students who were invited to participate in the study met the study entry criteria and completed the assessment. The mean age of the sample was 19.69 years [standard deviation (SD) = 1.37]. As shown in Table 1, most participants were female (85.6%) and from rural households (73.9%). Approximately two-thirds (66.0%) were enrolled in vocational colleges, and 34.0% in universities. In China, nearly all nursing students enter undergraduate or vocational programs at 18 years of age, with program duration of four years for undergraduate and three years for vocational education. Accordingly, age was categorized into ≤ 18, 19–20, and ≥ 21 years, reflecting early, middle, and later stages of nursing education. These groups accounted for 15.4%, 60.9%, 23.8% of the total sample, respectively.

The mean domain scores were 18.17 ± 4.90 for knowledge (75.7% of maximus), 11.96 ± 2.49 for attitudes (59.8%), 8.29 ± 5.04 for practices (29.6%), and 11.60 ± 3.27 for ethics (72.5%). Based on the proportion of the maximum possible scores, knowledge and ethics levels were relatively higher, while attitudes were moderate, and engagement in practice was comparatively low. Item-level distributions are shown in Fig. 1. Over 71% of students reported basic knowledge of AI concepts (items K1-K5). Regarding attitudes, 57.5% agreed that AI should be part of medical training (item A4), whereas about 25% of students were concerned by the Al developments in healthcare (item A2) and perceived AI integration as a potential threat to their future roles (item A1). The engagement in AI use was markedly low, with only 6.8%-9.6% of participants reported “often” and “always” using AI tools for academic and personal tasks (items P1-P7). In the ethics domain, more than 73% recognized AI’s role as a tool to facilitate human tasks (item E4) and expressed confidence in their ability to manage AI-related risks, such as data privacy, accuracy and misinformation (item E1). However, a smaller proportion (approximately 59%) believed AI could possess “free will” (item E2) or meet human emotional needs (item E3), reflecting ambivalence about the autonomy and role of AI.

Univariable analysis

Univariable analyses revealed that AI knowledge, attitude, practice and ethics scores varied significantly across several demographic variables. Students who were older or undergraduates or had reported better economic status consistently showed higher scores across all domains (all P < 0.001). Male students had slightly higher scores than women in the knowledge, attitude, and ethics (P = 0.015, 0.003, 0.025, respectively) domains. Urban residence and having a clear nursing career intention were also associated with significantly higher scores in the knowledge, attitude, and ethics (all P < 0.001) domains.

Factors related to AI knowledge, attitude, practice, and ethics domains

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted separately for each domain (Tables 2 and 3). Assumptions of linearity, normality of residuals and homoscedasticity were examined, and no major violations were detected. All variance inflation factor (VIF) values were < 5, indicating low levels of multicollinearity in the regression models. Age ≥ 19 years (vs. ≤18 years, β = 0.617 for 19–20 years; β = 0.688 for ≥ 21 years; all P < 0.001), male gender (vs. female, β = 0.393, P = 0.004), urban households (vs. rural, β = 0.663, P < 0.001), better economic status (vs. poor economic status, β = 0.511 for fair status; β = 1.731 for good status; all P < 0.001), and pursuing nursing as a future career (vs. other careers, β = 1.260, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with higher AI knowledge score, which accounted for 3.8% of the total variance (Table 2). All these factors, as well as education type, were influencing factors of AI attitudes and ethics, accounting for 3.4% and 3.0% of the variance in AI attitude and ethics scores, respectively (Table 2). However, the practice domain showed a different pattern. Only age (β = 0.557 for 19–20 years vs.≤18 years, P < 0.001) and education type (β = 1.174 for undergraduate vs. vocational college, P < 0.001) were influencing factors of AI practice, accounting for 1.3% of the total variance (Table 3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this was the first multi-center study of nursing students across China that comprehensively examined AI-related knowledge, attitudes, practices and ethics, and their associated factors. We found that students demonstrated moderate levels of AI-related knowledge, ethics awareness, and positive attitudes, but notably low engagement in the practice of AI use. Older age was consistently associated with higher scores across all domains, while male sex, urban residence, higher economic status, undergraduate education, and intention to pursue nursing as a career were associated with higher scores across different KAPE domains. These findings provide important insights for future AI-related curricular design, training and ethical AI use for the nursing workforce.

This study found that nursing students had a moderate level of AI-related knowledge, with approximately 35% and 38% of nursing students fully or partially agreed with having specific AI knowledge items, respectively. This indicates a higher level of understanding reported than a previous multi-professional survey in China [18] that reported only 35.1% of undergraduate nursing students had a complete or near-complete understanding of AI. In contrast, the figures in China are lower than medical students in Lebanon [29], Syria [30] and Australia [31] reported much higher levels of understanding of AI, with figures ranging from 59.7% to 84.8%. The discrepancy might be partly due to the different measurement tools used, for instance, earlier research largely relied on broad self-assessment questions about AI familiarity, whereas our study utilized multiple specific knowledge items addressing fundamental concepts such as AI evolution, interdisciplinary nature, core technologies, and human-computer interaction. Such approach provided a more precise evaluation assessment, highlighting specific knowledge gaps that might be obscured by generic questioning. Such differences could also indicate disparities in AI education integration, AI development in healthcare setting, and accessibility to AI technology across different regions and professional samples.

In terms of attitudes, our findings are broadly consistent with the previous study [18] showing generally positive views toward AI in nursing practice and education. However, approximately 25% of students in our study expressed concerns that AI might replace nurses or having fear of AI development, which is lower than the 53% reported previously [18]. This reduction might reflect growing familiarity with AI technologies and increasing curricular exposure, which could reduce anxiety and reinforce the notion of AI as a supportive tool. Nonetheless, the persistence of negative attitudes underscores the need for educational strategies that clearly frame AI as complementary tool, rather than replacing human nursing roles.

The most dramatic finding was the low practice level of AI use, with fewer than 10% of nursing students reporting frequent use of AI tools in their coursework or personal development. Compared to studies in other parts of the world where nursing and medical students increasingly utilize AI for academic support [16, 20], the use of AI tools among Chinese nursing students remains low. This disparity might be partly attributed to the relatively late accessibility of generative AI platforms in China, such as DeepSeek that only became available in late 2024. Although AI technologies have advanced rapidly, their integration into nursing education remains limited, constrained by slow institutional adoption, insufficient training, and infrastructural constraints [18]. Thus, there is an urgent need for educational reforms to incorporate experiential AI training and supervised use of AI tools to foster competence and confidence among nursing students.

Regarding ethics, most students reported confidence in handling AI-related risks and recognized the facilitatory role of AI rather than a replacement for the role of humans. However, significant concerns remain regarding the risks posed by AI use in healthcare, which aligns with the previous study [18] that found 95.7% of Chinese healthcare professionals, including nursing students, agreed with the need to strengthen AI-related medical ethics, such as clinical responsibility, supervision, and patient privacy. Similar ethical challenges, including algorithmic bias, transparency deficits, and legal liability have been reported globally [2, 12]. These findings highlight the need for nursing education to integrate comprehensive AI ethics training, combining legal, technical, and humanistic perspectives [7]. Coordinated efforts among educators, AI technologists, policymakers, and clinical practitioners are critical to develop clear ethical guidelines and regulatory frameworks that protect patient welfare while supporting the adoption of AI use in nursing practice.

This study found several important socio-demographic associations of AI-related KAPE among nursing students. Age showed consistent positive associations across all domains, indicating that students in higher grades had more comprehensive knowledge and more favorable attitudes, ethical awareness, and engagement related to AI use. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that senior nursing students exhibited better knowledge, literacy and acceptance of AI [18, 30, 32, 33]. However, we found the association with practice was non-linear: students aged 19–20 years reported significantly higher practice scores than those ≤ 18 years, while those ≥ 21 years did not differ significantly from the youngest group. This suggests that AI use might peak during the intermediate undergraduate stage rather than increasing consistently with age or seniority. A possible reason is that mid-program students (i.e., often in their second or third year) can face increasing academic demands and coursework which creates an incentive to adopt AI tools for completion of their assignments and academic tasks. In contrast, final-year students in both Chinese college- and university- nursing programs are primarily engaged in at least 10 months of internship and may rely more on routine clinical activities, resulting in less frequent AI use. This pattern highlights the need to align AI education with the academic training stage, offering practical training and supervision to integrate AI into their learning in a timely manner.

Additionally, sex was a significant associated factor, with male nursing students reporting higher scores in the knowledge, attitude and ethics domains. Similar findings have been found in studies from China and other countries [18, 19, 34], with males often reported greater technological confidence or more positive perception of AI than females. These findings indicate the presence of gender differences in AI-related perceptions and competencies. Household residence and economic status were also strong predictors, with students living in urban areas and from better economic status scoring higher across knowledge, attitude and ethics domains. These findings align with previous research [35, 36], which shows that students from urban and higher socioeconomic level generally have better access to high-quality internet, devices, extracurricular learning resources and institutional supports. Such advantages likely facilitate greater exposure to AI technologies and more informed ethical use of AI. At the population level, differential access to generative AI and other advanced AI tools can amplify pre-existing socioeconomic disparities unless training and infrastructure are equitably accessible [37]. From a practical perspective, our findings highlight the need for targeted investments in digital infrastructure, campus-based AI labs, and subsidized access, especially in rural and economically disadvantaged institutions to promote equity in AI use.

Education type was another significant factor, with undergraduates scoring higher than vocational college students in attitudes, practice, and ethics domains, even after adjusting for covariates. To the best of our knowledge, this was the first study to directly compare the practice of AI use across these two academic pathways. Several factors may explain this disparity. Undergraduate nursing programs generally provide broader curricular exposure (e.g. informatics) [38], more opportunities for research and clinical training, and greater emphasis on critical thinking and ethical reflection of nursing practice [39], all of which may foster more positive attitudes, higher engagement and stronger ethical awareness regarding AI use. In contrast, vocational college students often face more constrained curricula and fewer institutional resources, which may limit their opportunities for meaningful AI use and ethical practice [40]. Future comparative studies should examine how differences in educational structures and learning environments contribute to the development of AI literacy among nursing students.

Career intention toward nursing emerged as the strongest predictor across AI-related knowledge, attitude and ethics domains. This strong association likely reflects the role of professional identity and career motivation, which may lead to greater commitment to acquire relevant competencies, learning opportunities, and ethical practice of emerging technologies [41]. Similarly, a study involving multiple healthcare professions reported that professional identity was moderately associated with AI readiness subscale scores, including AI-related attitude and practice [42]. From an educational perspective, explicitly integrating AI competencies into nursing career development pathways may enhance adoption and support the cultivation of ethically grounded practice.

The strengths of this study included the large and diverse nationwide sample and the integration of an ethics domain within a KAP framework, enabling a multidimensional assessment of AI knowledge and practices. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevented causal inferences and assessment of any temporal relationships [43–45]. Longitudinal studies are hence necessary to explore how changes in the KAPE domains may result from academic reforms and advancements in AI technology [46]. Second, data were self-reported which might introduce social desirability bias or differential interpretation of items. Third, similar to previous studies [19, 47], although the content validity and internal consistency of the KAPE questionnaire were satisfactory, further psychometric validation, including confirmatory factor analysis and test–retest reliability, would enhance the validity of the scale.

In conclusion, this nationwide study provided a comprehensive assessment of AI-related knowledge, attitudes, practices, and ethics among nursing students in China. Students showed moderate levels of knowledge, positive attitudes, and good ethical awareness, but limited use of AI in practice. Socio-demographic factors, including age, sex, residence, economic status, education type and career intention were significantly associated with various KAPE domains. The observed associations could inform ways to create a supportive educational environment to enable nursing students to use AI appropriately and responsibly. Vocational nursing students demonstrated weaker performance in AI attitudes, practices and ethics, indicating a need to strengthen appropriate AI exposure and ethical guidance within vocational curricula. In contrast, students from economically disadvantaged backgrounds showed lower scores primarily in knowledge, attitudes and ethics, indicating a need to increase AI literacy and ethical awareness among such groups. Furthermore, the gaps between positive attitudes and limited practice across groups highlight the importance of integrating AI-related content into clinical training and practice, rather than solely relying on theoretical instructions. Linking AI competencies to nursing career development may also help promote responsible and practical AI use in clinical settings among nursing students.
