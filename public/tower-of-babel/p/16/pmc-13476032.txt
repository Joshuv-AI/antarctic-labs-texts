Rare Right Lung Tumor with Intracardiac Extension: A Case of Successful Multidisciplinary Management
Authors: Lava K. Penumaka, Chitra Rajeswari Thangaswamy, Hemachandren Munuswamy, Sindhu Kashetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe present a challenging case of a high-grade spindle cell sarcoma originating in the right lung with intracardiac extension to the left atrium and left ventricle via the pulmonary veins, complicated by aorto-iliac thromboembolism. An 18-year-old male presented with sudden-onset bilateral lower limb weakness, low-grade fever, cough, and breathlessness. Computed tomography imaging revealed a large mediastinal mass with intracardiac extension and an infrarenal aortic thrombus. A multidisciplinary team performed a successful single-stage procedure involving excision of the intracardiac tumor via median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass, right pneumonectomy under total circulatory arrest, and bilateral transfemoral embolectomy. Histopathology confirmed a high-grade spindle cell sarcoma with negative surgical margins. This case highlights the importance of a collaborative team approach, vigilant intraoperative monitoring, and the strategic use of circulatory arrest in managing complex thoracic tumors with intravascular extension.

INTRODUCTION

Intracardiac extension of complex lung tumors through the pulmonary veins is exceedingly rare and poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. These cases require meticulous preoperative planning with a multidisciplinary team and advanced surgical techniques to achieve complete resection and prevent catastrophic embolization.[1] This case report describes a rare presentation of a high-grade spindle cell sarcoma of the lung with intracardiac and aortic thromboembolic occlusion and its successful single-stage surgical management.

CASE SUMMARY

An 18-year-old male with no significant medical history presented with a three-month history of low-grade fever, productive cough, and breathlessness (NYHA Class II–III). One month before admission, he developed sudden-onset bilateral lower limb weakness. The patient denied any history of smoking or occupational exposure. Physical examination revealed decreased power in bilateral lower limbs (3/5), absent femoral and distal pulses, and diminished breath sounds over the right hemithorax.

On evaluation, his chest X-ray demonstrated a large opacity occupying the right hemithorax [Figure 1]. Computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a 10 × 13 × 11 cm right mediastinal mass extending into the right lung and left atrium [Figure 2]. A large thrombus was identified, causing obstruction from the infrarenal aorta to the iliac bifurcation. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large, mobile, lobulated mass within the left atrium extending into the left ventricle during diastole [Figure 3]. Fine-needle aspiration cytology of the lung mass was initially reported as high-grade carcinoma with mucinous differentiation. His electrocardiogram showed a normal sinus rhythm. The differential diagnosis was primary pulmonary sarcoma, intracardiac myxoma with secondary pulmonary involvement, metastatic tumor, and benign pulmonary lesion with intracardiac extension.

A multidisciplinary team involving cardiac thoracic surgeons, anesthesiologists, and perfusionists was essential. Preoperative considerations included paraparesis due to aortic occlusion and the high embolic risk from the mobile cardiac mass. Emergency surgery was indicated for tumor resection, embolectomy, limb salvage, and preventing further embolic episodes. The initial surgical plan was left atrial tumor thrombectomy with mediastinal mass excision with right upper lobectomy and bilateral transfemoral embolectomy. High risk was informed, and written consent was obtained. General anesthesia was induced, and the patient was intubated with an 8.5 mm endotracheal tube. We had placed a 7F bronchial blocker in the right main bronchus under bronchoscopic guidance prophylactically, in case there is a need if the surgical plan changes intraoperatively. Monitoring included invasive hemodynamic monitoring with right radial arterial and right internal jugular central venous catheters. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was employed throughout for continuous monitoring of cardiac function, tumor manipulation, and assessment of resection completeness. Intraoperative TEE showed a tumor in the left atrium prolapsing into the left ventricle [Figure 4]. Pulse-wave Doppler of the right upper pulmonary vein demonstrated the characteristic S, D, and A waves, but with increased velocities due to the presence of tumor thrombus [Figure 5]. The CT scan revealed a large mediastinal mass extending into the left atrium, while echocardiography confirmed the presence of a mobile mass within the left atrium and ventricle. These findings were critical in planning the surgical approach and ensuring complete resection of the tumor.[2]

Mid-esophageal right upper pulmonary vein pulse-wave Doppler view with S, D, and A waves with increased velocities

Anesthesia was maintained with a balanced technique using intravenous infusions of propofol, fentanyl, and isoflurane. Neuromuscular blockade was maintained with vecuronium to ensure optimal surgical conditions. The patient was positioned supine with a wedge in the interscapular region. The surgeon performed sternotomy and right anterolateral thoracotomy via hemiclamshell incision.

After systemic heparinization, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was initiated. The aorta, superior vena cava, and inferior vena cava were cannulated. The heart was arrested with root cardioplegia. A tumor mass extending from the left atrium into the left ventricle, along with clots in the left ventricle, was excised. There was a mass of size 20 × 15 × 15 cm involving the right upper and middle lobes, completely encasing the right pulmonary artery, right bronchus, and right pulmonary vein. As the surgeon was unable to find a plane and as the hilum was infiltrated with tumor, the surgeon made an intraoperative decision to proceed with right pneumonectomy. During pneumonectomy, the blocker was withdrawn from the right main bronchus. There was bleeding in the field and difficulty in resection as the mass was infiltrating the hilum. So, the surgeon proceeded with right pneumonectomy under total circulatory arrest (TCA). Total CPB time was 3 h 13 min, with aortic occlusion time of 2 h 45 min. TCA was employed for 30 min with core cooling to 18°C. For neuroprotection during TCA, we used surface cooling, thiopentone 10 mg/kg, and methylprednisolone 10 mg/kg. After the procedure, the patient was weaned off CPB uneventfully [Figure 6]. Bilateral mid-inguinal incisions were made. Organized clots were extracted from the infrarenal aorta through bilateral femoral embolectomy.

In summary, the surgical sequence was: (1) institution of CPB after sternotomy, (2) excision of the left atrial and left ventricular intracardiac mass, (3) extension of incision into a hemiclamshell for right thoracic exposure, (4) right pneumonectomy under TCA due to hilar infiltration and bleeding, and (5) bilateral transfemoral embolectomy.

Intraoperative challenges were significant blood loss of approximately 3 L, managed with transfusion of eight units of packed red blood cells (PRBCs), eight units of fresh frozen plasma (FFP), four units of cryoprecipitate, and four units of platelets. For managing arrhythmias, standard precautions were taken, including a defibrillator for providing shock/cardioversion, antiarrhythmic drugs, monitoring, and management of electrolytes in the normal range. Close communication between the surgical and anesthesia teams was vital for managing hemodynamics pre- and post-CPB. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for elective ventilation and was extubated on the first postoperative day. Postoperative analgesia was managed with a multimodal regimen, including intravenous acetaminophen, tramadol, and intercostal nerve blocks performed under direct vision by the surgeon. Anticoagulation was initiated to prevent further thromboembolism. The patient was mobilized with chest physiotherapy. Drains were removed on postoperative day 4 (POD 4), and he was discharged on POD 10 in a stable condition. Histopathological analysis confirmed a high-grade spindle cell sarcoma. Surgical margins were negative. The patient’s recovery was uneventful, and follow-up imaging showed no evidence of tumor recurrence [Figures 7 and 8]. After 6 months of surgery, the patient had brain metastasis and is currently on radiotherapy.

DISCUSSION

Intracardiac extension of mesenchymal tumors through pulmonary veins is exceedingly rare.[3] This case highlights the importance of considering such extensions in patients presenting with cardiopulmonary symptoms. Successful management requires early diagnosis, a multidisciplinary team approach, and meticulous intraoperative planning.[4]

Mesenchymal tumors are often asymptomatic in their early stages, making early diagnosis challenging. In this case, the patient presented with nonspecific symptoms such as fever, cough, and breathlessness, which are common in many pulmonary conditions. However, the sudden onset of bilateral lower limb weakness raised the suspicion of a more complex pathology, leading to further investigations.[5]

Advanced imaging techniques, such as CT scans and echocardiography, played a crucial role in identifying the extent of the tumor and its intracardiac extension. The CT scan revealed a large mediastinal mass extending into the left atrium, while echocardiography confirmed the presence of a mobile mass within the left atrium and ventricle. These findings were critical in planning the surgical approach and ensuring complete resection of the tumor.

The surgical management of such tumors is complex and requires a multidisciplinary team, including cardiothoracic surgeons, anesthesiologists, and perfusionists. In this case, the use of CPB was to safely resect the intracardiac portion of the tumor and prevent embolization. As the tumor was encasing the pulmonary artery and right pulmonary veins, the surgeon performed right pneumonectomy under TCA to operate in a bloodless field, to improve visualization and precision in this complex case. The intraoperative challenges, including significant blood loss and the risk of arrhythmias, were managed effectively through close coordination between the surgical and anesthesia teams.[6] We managed the hemodynamics to prevent tachycardia, hypertension, and maintain sinus rhythm so that the risk of embolization is minimized. However, we could not monitor the near-infrared spectroscopy due to the unavailability of the sensor.

TEE at the end of surgery showed no residual mass in the left atrium [Figure 6]. Postoperative management focused on preventing thromboembolic complications and ensuring adequate pain control. The patient’s recovery was uneventful, and follow-up imaging showed no evidence of tumor recurrence [Figures 7 and 8].[7] After 6 months of surgery, the patient had brain metastasis and is currently on radiotherapy.

This case underscores the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic workup and collaborative surgical management in treating rare pulmonary and intracardiac tumors. Long-term follow-up is essential to monitor for recurrence and ensure optimal outcomes.[8]

CONCLUSION

Meticulous planning and a multidisciplinary approach are essential for complex thoracic sarcomas with intracardiac extension. Success hinges on intraoperative use of TEE to confirm diagnosis, monitor tumour thrombus pre-CPB, confirm complete removal of intracardiac mass, the strategic use of TCA for lung resection, close coordination between the teams, and good postoperative care.
