DUSP6 promotes motility, invasion, and tumorigenicity of thyroid cancer cells via IL8-induced neutrophil extracellular traps
Authors: Wu Xiao, Lianghua Luo, Yong Yang, Wei Chen, Chunping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to investigate the role of DUSP6 in thyroid cancer metastasis and elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms. Immunohistochemistry were performed to explore the expression of DUSP6, IL-8 and PAD4 in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) tissues and adjacent non-cancerous thyroid tissues. Effects of DUSP6 on the proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion of thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and IHH4 were performed through in vitro experiments. A rescue experiment was performed to verified DUSP6 regulated the migration and invasion of thyroid cancer cells TPC-1 and IHH4 through IL-8 and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation. In addition, in vitro cell experiments were conducted to analyze the regulation of NETs formation by DUSP6 through IL-8. Finally, the effect of sh-DUSP6 on the growth of thyroid cancer tumors in mice were conducted through in vivo experiments. DUSP6 expression was significantly upregulated in PTC tissues. Moreover, the expression of DUSP6 in tumor tissues of TPC patients is positively correlated with the expression of IL-8 and PAD4. Overexpression of DUSP6 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of thyroid cancer cells (TPC-1 and IHH4) while inhibiting apoptosis. Conversely, silencing DUSP6 exerts opposing effects. Knockdown of IL-8 reverses the enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities induced by DUSP6 overexpression in these cell lines. NETs treatment enhances the migration and invasion of TPC-1 and IHH4 cells, whereas the application of sh-DUSP6 or sh-IL-8 counteracts this NETs-mediated promotion. Furthermore, DUSP6 overexpression facilitates NETs formation by upregulating IL-8 expression. In vivo experiments demonstrate that sh-DUSP6 suppresses thyroid cancer tumor growth in mouse models. Introduction

Thyroid cancer (TC) is one of the most prevalent endocrine malignancies originating in the head and neck region1–3. In recent decades, the global incidence of thyroid cancer has shown a consistent upward trend4,5; Chen et al., 2023. The current standard therapeutic approaches for TC primarily involve total or partial thyroidectomy, radioactive iodine ablation, and thyrotropin suppression therapy6,7. While the majority of thyroid cancer cases exhibit favorable outcomes with these interventions, aggressive variants of TC often demonstrate limited responsiveness to conventional treatments, resulting in a significantly poorer prognosis. Consequently, investigating the underlying molecular mechanisms driving TC metastasis holds substantial clinical importance for improving patient outcomes8.

Dual specificity phosphatase 6 (DUSP6) is a dual-specificity protein phosphatase predominantly localized in the cytoplasm9–11. Accumulating evidence indicates that DUSP6 is highly expressed in thyroid cancer tissues and contributes to the malignant behavior of thyroid cancer cells. Specifically, Degl’Innocenti et al., demonstrated that DUSP6 was highly expressed in thyroid cancer tissues, and its high expression was conducive to the malignant biological behavior of thyroid cells12. Lee et al., reported that the high expression of DUSP6 was related to the invasiveness of thyroid cancer13. Additionally, Gu et al., revealed that miR-145 exerts antiproliferative effects in thyroid cancer by directly targeting and downregulating DUSP6 expression14. Despite these findings, the molecular mechanisms underlying DUSP6-mediated regulation of thyroid cancer metastasis remain poorly understood. IL-8, as an inflammatory cytokine, plays a significant role in the occurrence and development of thyroid cancer. Previous studies have shown that DUSP6 can regulate the expression of IL-8 in prostate cancer cells15. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are fibrous network structures composed of DNA, embedded histones and various granular proteins, which are released by neutrophils in response to specific stimuli. The formation of NETs is closely related to the progression, metastasis and poor prognosis of various solid tumors16. Interestingly, previous studies on uveitis and atherosclerotic diseases have found that IL-8 can regulate the formation of NETs17,18. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of DUSP6 on the metastasis of TC and investigated its underlying molecular mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Materials

Human thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and IHH4 were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Human peripheral blood neutrophils was Shanghai Yiyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The CCK-8 cell proliferation kit, HE staining kit, Matrigel, Cell Migration And Invasion Stain Kit and Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit were purchased from Solaibao Technology Co., LTD (Beijing, China). The antibodies against β-Actin, DUSP6, IL-8, PAD4, E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Vimentin, MPO and citH3 were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, United Kingdom). Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) was purchased from MedChemExpress LLC (New Jersey, USA). Sh-DUSP6, sh-IL-8, sh-NC, DUSP6 overexpression (OE-DUSP6) and OE-NC vector were obtained from Generay Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Human PTC clinical tissue sample

In total, 20 papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) tissues and adjacent non-cancerous tissues were obtained from PTC patients receiving standard resection without radiation therapy at the Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital from 2023 to 2024. The study, carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Principles of Helsinki, won the approval of the Ethics Committee of the Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital. The license number of the ethical review for the study is KEKUAI2025(62). In addition, before conducting clinical sample testing, the expression differences of DUSP6 in thyroid cancer patients and normal individuals were analyzed using the GEPIA2 website (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index).

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue samples from mouse tumors were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution and processed for paraffin embedding. Paraffin sections of approximately 4 μm thickness were prepared, deparaffinized at 60 °C, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 100 °C for 5 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution at 25 °C for 10 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with diluted primary antibody, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. After PBS washing, sections were developed using DAB substrate, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Finally, the samples were examined under an optical microscope.

Cell culture, treatment and transfection

Human TPC-1 cells, IHH4 cells and peripheral blood neutrophils were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Then, We adopted the methodology described by Wang et al.19, to induce NETs formation by treating neutrophils (1 × 107/well) with 100 nM PMA for 4 h.

Regarding the co - culture protocol of NETs and thyroid cancer cells, the cell processing method reported by Chu et al., was adopted20. Specifically, the DNA concentration of the isolated NETs was quantified using the Quant - iT™ PicoGreen™ dsDNA Assay Kit. The isolated NETs were then diluted in DMEM to a concentration of 500 ng/mL. Subsequently, thyroid cancer cells were co - incubated with either NETs or a solvent control for 24 h prior to subsequent detection experiments.

Regarding cell transfection, cells were prepared once their density reached 70%. Subsequently, different vectors were used to transfect different cells for 48 h with the help of lipofectamine 3000. After transfection, RNA was extracted from different cells in various groups to evaluate the transfection efficiency.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The level of IL-8 in the supernatant of TPC-1 cells was detected by the reference human IL-8 detection kit method.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA samples were collected from TC cells subjected to different treatments. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent, followed by purification via chloroform-isopropanol phase separation. The purified RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a commercial reverse transcription kit. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed under a one-step thermal cycling protocol consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The relative mRNA expression levels in each experimental group were determined using the comparative Ct method. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Different treated TC cell samples were taken and inoculated in 96-well culture plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well. 100 µL of culture medium was added to each well and the plates were placed in a cell culture incubator for 48 h. The 96-well plates were then taken out, and 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. The plates were returned to the cell culture incubator for another 4 h. The culture medium was aspirated, and 150 µL of DMSO was added to each well. The plates were placed in a multi-functional microplate reader and shaken for 10 min to fully dissolve the crystals. The absorbance value was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Cell apoptosis assay

Approximately 1 × 106 TC cells from various treatment groups were collected, and 100 µL of apoptosis binding buffer, 5 µL of Annexin V, and 5 µL of PI were added to each sample. The samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, 200 µL of apoptosis binding buffer was added, and the mixtures were gently resuspended using a pipette. The samples were then transferred to tubes for analysis by flow cytometry.

Wound healing assay

Different treated TC cells were adjusted to a concentration of approximately 5 × 10⁵ cells/mL and seeded into 6-well cell culture plates. Confluent monolayers were scratched using a sterile pipette tip held perpendicularly to the plate surface. Wound healing was monitored under a microscope at designated time points post-scratching, and the wound closure rate was calculated accordingly.

Cell invasion assay. Add the matrix gel to the chamber and incubate at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator to allow gelation. Dilute the cells in serum-free medium to a final concentration of 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL. Seed 200 µL of the cell suspension into the upper chamber, and add DMEM medium supplemented with 20% FBS to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 and 48 h of incubation, carefully aspirate the culture medium, remove non-migrated cells from the upper surface of the membrane using a cotton swab, and wash the membrane three times with PBS. Air-dry the membrane at room temperature, then fix with methanol for 10 min. Stain with 0.1% crystal violet solution at room temperature for 30 min, rinse thoroughly with PBS, and allow to air dry. Capture images under a light microscope for subsequent analysis.

Western blotting

Samples of TC cells subjected to different treatments were collected, and total proteins were extracted. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. A volume of 20 µL of each protein sample was loaded for SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a membrane and blocked to prevent nonspecific binding. The membrane was then incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, unbound primary antibody was removed by washing with TBST. Subsequently, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Unbound secondary antibody was removed by additional TBST washes. Chemiluminescent detection was performed using ECL reagent, and images were captured and developed using a gel imaging system. The relative expression level of the target protein was quantified as the ratio of the grayscale intensity of the target protein band to that of the internal control, β-actin.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Cellular immunofluorescence analysis was carried out in accordance with the method described by Tang et al.21,. DNA was stained using SYTOX Green DNA dye.

Animals

Thirty SPF male Balb/c nude mice aged 5–6 weeks (18–22 g) were purchased from ‌Henan Spex Biotechnology Co., Ltd.‌ (license No. SCXK (Yu) 2020-0005). All animal experiments followed ethical guidelines approved by the Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital (No. NYLLSC20250922).

Establish a PTC mice model

After 7 days of adaptive feeding, all mice were divided into three groups: the Control group, the sh-NC group, and the sh-DUSP6 group, with each group consisting of 5 mice. Initially, different vectors were transfected into TPC-1 cells. Subsequently, different types of cells were resuspended to prepare for subcutaneous inoculation. Specifically, 150 µl of a cell suspension with a TPC-1 cell concentration of 5 × 106 cells/ml was injected into the right axilla of each mouse. The tumor size was monitored and recorded at 3 day intervals throughout the tumor formation period. The experiment should be terminated if mice display any of the following criteria: rapid tumor growth leading to ulceration, infection, or necrosis; a sudden loss of 15–20% or more of baseline body weight; inability to independently consume food or water; signs of depression accompanied by hypothermia in the absence of anesthesia or sedation; or abnormal central nervous system manifestations, including convulsions, tremors, paralysis, or head tilt. Following 24 days of tumor development, mice were euthanized under 2.5% isoflurane inhalation anesthesia, and tumor tissues were subsequently harvested and weighed.

HE staining

Tissue samples from mouse tumors were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, processed for paraffin embedding, and sectioned into slices approximately 4 μm in thickness. HE staining was performed according to the protocol provided in the HE staining kit. The stained sections were subsequently examined and imaged under a light microscope.

Statistical analyses

Graphpad Prism software was utilized for statistical analysis. The experimental data were presented in the form of mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). One-Way ANOVA was employed to compare among different groups, while the t-test was chosen for pairwise comparisons. A P value less than 0.05 was regarded as indicating a statistically significant difference.

Results

DUSP6 is highly expressed in tumor tissues of PTC patients and positively correlated with the expression of IL-8 and PAD4

First, we analyzed the differential expression of DUSP6, IL-8 and PAD4 in thyroid cancer patients compared to normal individuals using the GEPIA2 platform (Fig. 1A, Fig. S5). Compared with the normal group, DUSP6 and IL-8 were significantly highly expressed in patients with thyroid cancer. Then, we used immunohistochemistry to assess the expression levels of DUSP6, IL-8 and PAD4 in thyroid cancer tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1B-D and G-I). Compared with the paracancerous group, the protein expression levels of DUSP6, IL-8 and PAD4 were significantly elevated in the tumor group. Moreover, A correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the expression levels of DUSP6, IL-8, and PAD4 in tumor tissues from each patient with PTC (Fig. 1E-F). The expression of DUSP6 in tumor tissues of PTC patients is positively correlated with the expression levels of IL-8 and PAD4. PAD4 serves as a marker protein for the formation of NETs. These results indicate that the expression level of DUSP6 is positively correlated with NETs formation in PTC.

Effect of DUSP6 on the proliferation and apoptosis of human thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and IHH4

The results demonstrated that transfection of OE-DUSP6 into TPC-1 and IHH4 cells led to significantly increased DUSP6 expression, whereas transfection with sh-DUSP6 resulted in markedly reduced DUSP6 expression in both cell lines. As shown in Fig. 2E-H, the apoptosis of different TPC-1 and IHH4 cells were detected by flow cytometry. Compared with the OE-NC group, the apoptosis of TPC-1 and IHH4 cells were significantly decreased in the OE-DUSP6 group. Compared with the sh-NC group, the apoptosis of TPC-1 and IHH4 cells were significantly increased in the sh-DUSP6 group. Subsequently, the cell viability of TPC-1 and IHH4 cells in different groups were assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 2I). Compared with the OE-NC group, the proliferation of TPC-1 and IHH4 cells were significantly increased in the OE-DUSP6 group. Compared with the sh-NC group, the proliferation of TPC-1 and IHH4 cells were significantly decreased in the sh-DUSP6 group.

DUSP6 promotes the formation of NETs through IL-8

To further explore whether DUSP6 regulates the formation of NETs through IL-8, we transfected OE-DUSP6, sh-DUSP6 and sh-IL-8 into human peripheral blood neutrophils for the following experiments. As shown in Fig. 9A-C, RT-qPCR were employed to assess the transfection efficiency of OE-DUSP6, sh-DUSP6 and sh-IL-8 vectors in neutrophils. The results demonstrated that transfection of OE-DUSP6 into neutrophils led to significantly increased DUSP6 expression, whereas transfection with sh-DUSP6 and sh-IL-8 resulted in markedly reduced DUSP6 and IL-8 expression in both cell lines, respectively. Then, western blotting was used to detected NETs-related marker proteins (MPO and citH3) in different neutrophils (Fig. 9D-I). Compared with the OE-NC group, the protein expression levels of MPO and citH3 were significantly increased in the OE-DUSP6 group. Compared with the sh-NC group, the protein expression levels of MPO and citH3 were significantly decreased in the sh-DUSP6 group. Besides, compared with the Control group, the protein expression levels of MPO and citH3 were significantly decreased in the sh-IL-8 group. Compared with the OE-DUSP6 group, the protein expression levels of MPO and citH3 were significantly decreased in the OE-DUSP6 + sh-IL-8 group. As shown in Fig. S3, we further verified that DUSP6 can regulate the formation of NETs via IL − 8 by detecting DNA and citH3 expression through immunofluorescence staining. The above results indicate that DUSP6 promotes the formation of NETs through IL-8.

Interference with DUSP6 inhibits tumor growth in nude mice with thyroid cancer

As shown in Fig. 10A-C, the effect of DUSP6 on the growth of thyroid cancer was analyzed in vivo. The results demonstrated that suppression of DUSP6 significantly reduced both tumor volume and tumor weight in a mouse model of thyroid cancer. After that, the tumor tissues from each group were harvested for HE staining (Fig. 10D). In both the control group and the sh-NC group, tumor cells were densely packed with minimal intercellular spaces. In contrast, the sh-DUSP6 group exhibited a significantly reduced number of tumor cells with a more dispersed and loosely organized arrangement. In addition, immunohistochemistry was used to assess the expression levels of E-cadherin, N-cadherin and Vimentin in cancer tissues of mice (Fig. 10E-G). Compared with the sh-NC group, the protein expression level of E-cadherin was increased in the sh-DUSP6 group, and the protein expression levels of N-cadherin and Vimentin were decreased in the sh-DUSP6 group. Finally, the WB results of the tumor tissues in nude mice indicated that interfering with the expression of DUSP6 could notably decrease the expression of IL − 8, MPO, and citH3 proteins in the tumor tissues of nude mice (Fig. S4A-D).

Discussion

DUSP6 is a highly conserved gene and a key member of the dual-specificity phosphatase family22. It plays a critical role in the initiation and progression of various malignancies, including thyroid cancer23, gastric cancer24, ovarian cancer25, and breast cancer26. In this study, immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissues from patients with PTC revealed that DUSP6 is significantly overexpressed in PTC tissues. This finding is consistent with previous reports by Degl’Innocenti et al.12,. Furthermore, we demonstrated that overexpression of DUSP6 in the human thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and IHH4 significantly enhances tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas knockdown of DUSP6 markedly suppresses these cellular behaviors. These observations are supported by the study of Gu et al., which also reported that silencing DUSP6 effectively inhibits the proliferation of TPC-1 cells14.

IL-8, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, plays a critical role not only in host responses to infection, allergic reactions, and autoimmune disorders, but also in the initiation and progression of various cancers27–31. Accumulating evidence indicates that IL-8 is expressed in multiple tumor types, including thyroid, pancreatic, and lung cancers, where it contributes to tumorigenesis and cancer development through mechanisms such as induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition and angiogenesis32,33. Fang et al., demonstrated that tumor-associated macrophages enhance the metastatic potential of PTC by promoting IL-8 secretion34. In our study, suppression of IL-8 expression in thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and IHH4 significantly reduced tumor cell migration and invasion compared to control groups. Furthermore, emerging studies suggest a regulatory relationship between DUSP6 and IL-8 expression. Ostadkarampour et al., reported that the inhibitor RG0216 concurrently downregulates both DUSP6 and IL-8 in human gingival keratinocytes35, while Leng et al., observed that the L-homocysteine inhibitor SID suppresses DUSP6 and IL-8 expression in HUVEC cells36. Conversely, Zhai et al., found that overexpression of DUSP6 leads to decreased IL-8 levels, thereby inhibiting prostate cancer cell proliferation and metastasis15. Notably, in our study, interfering with IL-8 in the OE-DUSP6 group further suppressed the migratory and invasive capabilities of TPC-1 and IHH4 cells, indicating a synergistic effect between DUSP6 and IL-8 in modulating thyroid cancer progression.

Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are web-like structures composed of DNA as a structural scaffold, embedded with various granular proteins, proteolytic enzymes, and antimicrobial peptides, which are released by neutrophils in response to diverse stimuli37–39. Accumulating evidence indicates that NETs formation contributes to tumor metastasis in a wide range of cancers. Wang et al., demonstrated that NETs enhance the metastatic potential of non-small cell lung cancer cells19, while Zeng et al., reported that kaempferol inhibits NETs formation via the ROS-PAD4 signaling pathway, thereby suppressing breast cancer lung metastasis40. Clinical findings by Modestino et al., revealed a positive correlation between NETs formation and both clinical malignancy and disease progression in thyroid cancer41. However, the regulatory role of NETs in thyroid cancer metastasis remains uncharacterized. Our experimental results show that, compared with the control group, co-culture with NETs significantly enhances the migratory and invasive capabilities of thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and IHH4. Besides, Compared with the NETs group, the migration and invasion of TPC-1 and IHH4 cells were significantly decreased in the sh-DUSP6 + NETs and the sh-IL-8 + NETs group. Interestingly, our results also showed that transfection of the DUSP6 overexpression vector into neutrophils could significantly promote the formation of NETs (with increased expression of MPO and citH3 proteins). Additionally, in the OE-DUSP6 group, intervention in the expression of IL-8 could significantly inhibit the formation of NETs (with decreased expression of MPO and citH3 proteins).

Based on the results of the present study, it can be concluded that DUSP6 promotes the metastasis of thyroid cancer by facilitating the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps via IL-8. However, the present study exhibits certain limitations. The effect of DUSP6 on NETs formation in the tumor tissues of thyroid cancer mouse model was not further validated through in vivo experiments, and it remains unclear whether a direct targeted interaction exists between DUSP6 and IL-8. Although our current results indicate that inhibiting DUSP6/IL-8 in neutrophils can affect the formation of NETs, we believe that the paracrine effect of tumor cells is indeed the main factor influencing the formation of NETs. Specifically, DUSP6 affects the recruitment of neutrophils and the formation of NETs in thyroid cancer tumors by influencing the IL-8 secreted by thyroid cancer cells. In future studies, we aim to address these limitations through additional experimental investigations.
