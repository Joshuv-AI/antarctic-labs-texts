Fuzzy multi-criteria selection of sensor architectures for coastal pollution monitoring
Authors: Fu Jiaolong, Nor Hasliza Md Saad, Jong Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper evaluates five sensor network architectures for coastal marine pollution monitoring using a fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making framework. Thirty domain experts assessed the alternatives against five criteria (detection performance, spatial coverage, life-cycle cost, energy demand, and robustness) using linguistic ratings, which were converted into triangular fuzzy numbers. A fuzzy decision matrix and the corresponding criterion weights were derived and analysed using Fuzzy TOPSIS. Spatial coverage (w = 0.26) and detection performance (w = 0.24) emerged as the most influential criteria. The glider–satellite architecture achieved the highest closeness coefficient (CC = 0.76), followed by a combined HF radar–innovative mooring system (CC = 0.71); the IoT node grid, fixed buoy network, and vessel-based system obtained coefficients of 0.63, 0.58, and 0.49, respectively. Sensitivity tests across multiple weight scenarios and a robustness check confirm that the top-ranked option is stable under plausible shifts in decision priorities. Methodologically, this study does not propose a new mathematical decision algorithm. Instead, it contributes a rigorously structured decision-support workflow that integrates established techniques, structured expert elicitation with consistency screening, fuzzy aggregation, and multi-scenario weight-sensitivity into a single, replicable pipeline tailored to the selection of coastal pollution monitoring architectures. The methodological contribution, therefore, lies in the systematic integration, operationalisation, and application of these components, rather than in modifying the underlying Fuzzy TOPSIS formulation. For decision-makers, the study clarifies which criteria drive architecture choice, when lower-cost opportunistic or IoT-grid options become competitive, and how to assemble hybrid monitoring designs that combine wide-area coverage with reliable detection.Keywords: Coastal pollution monitoring, Fuzzy TOPSIS, Sensor network architecture, Multi-criteria decision-making, Marine IoTSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing, Ocean sciences

Introduction

Coastal seas are increasingly exposed to multiple pollution pressures, including oil and chemical spills, untreated urban discharges, nutrient enrichment, and plastics1–3. These pressures operate at spatial and temporal scales that are difficult to capture with infrequent ship surveys, so agencies are turning to real-time, in situ sensor networks to monitor water quality and support rapid response4–6. Recent reviews of coastal observing systems emphasise that emerging technologies, including satellite missions, high-frequency (HF) radar, autonomous platforms, and dense in situ sensor arrays, are transforming how coastal ecosystems are monitored7,8. However, these advances also introduce new design, integration, and governance challenges for managers9–11. In parallel, the “digital ocean” agenda highlights the role of Internet of Things (IoT) technologies, low-cost sensors, and AI-enabled analytics in delivering continuous environmental data streams for decision-making12–14.

Within this landscape, a wide variety of sensor network architectures have been proposed for coastal water-quality monitoring15–17. IoT-based buoy and pier systems are now routinely used to measure basic parameters such as temperature, conductivity, pH, and dissolved oxygen in coastal zones, with several prototypes demonstrating multi-parameter nodes and low-cost electronics suitable for extended deployments18,19. Other efforts have developed underwater IoT systems for continuous monitoring at marine outfalls or aquaculture sites, often focusing on a limited set of physicochemical indicators but emphasising high temporal resolution and localised control20. Autonomous underwater gliders, coupled with satellite communication links, have been used to obtain vertically resolved profiles of coastal waters during energetic events, thereby complementing more traditional fixed moorings9. Meanwhile, regional HF radar networks provide near-real-time maps of surface currents over hundreds of kilometres21,22 and are increasingly recognised as core elements of coastal observing systems relevant to pollutant transport prediction23. Consequently, decision-makers now face an expanded set of monitoring options. These include fixed buoy arrays, dense seabed IoT grids, mobile gliders, vessel-based opportunistic systems, and hybrid HF radar–mooring configurations.

Most existing studies on coastal water-quality monitoring focus on the design or demonstration of a single architecture. For example, several recent papers present IoT-based coastal monitoring stations or lake systems, highlight their reliability and cost-effectiveness, and report field validation results, but do not compare these systems quantitatively with alternative architectures18. Reviews of ocean environmental monitoring using wireless sensor networks similarly synthesise available platforms and technologies but stop short of providing a structured method for selecting among competing network designs for a specific coastal application24. In practice, the choice of architecture involves balancing multiple, often conflicting, criteria: detection sensitivity to low pollutant concentrations, spatial coverage, life-cycle cost, energy demand, and robustness to harsh marine conditions. Ad-hoc or single-objective optimisation approaches (e.g., minimising cost at a fixed coverage level) struggle to capture these trade-offs. They also rarely incorporate qualitative expert knowledge about how systems perform under real coastal operating conditions.

Multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) methods provide a natural way to integrate heterogeneous criteria and expert judgement into architecture selection. Among these, the fuzzy extension of the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (Fuzzy TOPSIS) has been widely adopted in environmental and infrastructure planning because it allows both criterion weights and alternative performances to be expressed as fuzzy numbers, thereby capturing the vagueness inherent in linguistic assessments25. Recent applications include the selection of methods for environmental flow assessment in rivers, the siting of transport infrastructure, and the ranking of renewable energy options, all of which demonstrate that Fuzzy TOPSIS remains an active and flexible tool for group decision-making under uncertainty26. Classical Fuzzy TOPSIS has also been extended to more sophisticated frameworks (e.g., interval type-2, complex or Fermatean fuzzy TOPSIS). Still, these variants build on the same core idea of ranking alternatives according to their relative distance from the fuzzy ideal and nadir solutions27.

A closely related precedent for the present work is the study by28, who used Fuzzy TOPSIS to select “smart sensor architectures” for coastal marine monitoring. Their framework evaluates a small number of candidate architectures using fuzzy performance scores and derives closeness coefficients to support architecture choice. That contribution demonstrates the feasibility of applying Fuzzy TOPSIS at the architecture level for coastal monitoring, but it also has limitations that motivate further work. The number of alternatives is limited, the range of platform combinations considered is relatively narrow, and the analysis places less emphasis on explicit sensitivity testing and on linking the numerical ranking to concrete operational scenarios, such as spill response versus long-term trend monitoring. In addition, the treatment of sensor performance does not explicitly foreground sensitivity, the ability to detect low concentrations, as a distinct criterion, despite its central importance for pollution monitoring29.

Fuzzy TOPSIS remains widely used because it provides an interpretable way to aggregate expert uncertainty into trade-offs and rankings; the key contribution in modern studies is typically the workflow design (elicitation, preprocessing, robustness checks) rather than the core distance-based ranking formula25. Rather than being outdated, Fuzzy TOPSIS has become a baseline against which more complex schemes are benchmarked. What has evolved is not the core algorithm, but the way it is embedded in domain-specific workflows, including data preprocessing, expert elicitation protocols, and robustness checks. Accordingly, the present study should be interpreted as a structured, context-specific integration of established decision-analytic components rather than as the development of a new fuzzy multi-criteria method.

Despite rapid growth in coastal observing technologies, the literature still lacks a decision-support framework for comparing multiple competing sensor-network architectures under realistic trade-offs. Most studies either validate a single prototype (often for a limited parameter set) or review technologies, without producing an actionable ranking of architectures for a specific pollution-monitoring mission. Even the closest architecture-level fuzzy TOPSIS precedent24 uses a narrower alternative set and provides limited linkage between rankings and operational decision contexts. As a result, decision-makers lack a transparent workflow that (i) compares a broader architecture set, (ii) foregrounds pollution-monitoring needs (detection/coverage/robustness), and (iii) shows whether recommendations are robust when stakeholder priorities shift. What is new in this paper is not the Fuzzy TOPSIS algorithm itself, but how it is operationalised for decision-making in coastal pollution-monitoring architecture and what decision-relevant outputs it produces. The novelty is threefold: (i) Methodological novelty (workflow-level): we specify a replicable expert-to-ranking pipeline that includes response screening/consistency handling, fuzzy aggregation with transparent mappings, benefit–cost criterion treatment, and multi-scenario weight-sensitivity to test whether a recommended architecture is stable or preference-contingent. (ii) Application-scope novelty (architecture set + criteria): we broaden the architecture space to five realistic options spanning fixed, mobile, opportunistic, and remote-sensing components, and we evaluate them with a compact criterion set tailored to pollution monitoring that explicitly foregrounds detection performance/sensitivity alongside coverage, life-cycle cost, energy demand, and operational robustness. (iii) Novel decision-maker insights (actionable guidance): beyond a single “best” option, we show which criteria dominate the choice, which alternatives are “close seconds” under priority regimes (e.g., cost/energy or robustness emphasis), and what this implies for hybrid system design (backbone platforms vs. complementary components).

To address these gaps, this study develops a structured decision-support framework to compare alternative architectures for coastal pollution monitoring. The framework integrates expert judgement with multi-criteria evaluation to generate architecture rankings, identify dominant decision drivers, and assess the stability of recommendations under alternative decision priorities. The approach is designed to support transparent and practically interpretable infrastructure planning in complex coastal monitoring environments9,18. The evaluation draws on structured expert input to capture domain knowledge that is difficult to represent using purely quantitative data.

In summary, the novelty is explicit in three dimensions demanded by coastal monitoring decisions. Methodologically, we contribute a transparent, end-to-end expert-to-ranking workflow with documented preprocessing/aggregation and multi-scenario sensitivity analysis that reveals whether conclusions are robust or preference-dependent. In the application scope, we extend prior coastal architecture-selection work by comparing five modern architectures (including HF radar, smart mooring, and vessel-based opportunistic sensing) under a pollution-specific criterion set that separates detection capability from other performance attributes. In decision support, we translate closeness coefficients into guidance about which platforms serve as backbone assets, which are best used as targeted supplements, and how rankings change (or remain stable) when agencies prioritise coverage, cost/energy, or robustness. Detailed methodological procedures are presented in the section “Methodology”.

Methodology

The proposed methodology combines expert elicitation with a fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making procedure to compare alternative sensor network architectures for coastal pollution monitoring. First, the decision problem is defined by specifying the competing architectures and a set of evaluation criteria that reflect operational needs in dynamic coastal environments (e.g., detection capability, spatial coverage, cost, energy use, and resilience). A panel of domain experts then rates each architecture against each criterion using qualitative linguistic terms and indicates the relative importance of the criteria. These linguistic assessments are checked for completeness and coherence, converted into triangular fuzzy numbers, and aggregated to obtain a fuzzy decision matrix and a corresponding set of fuzzy criterion weights. Fuzzy TOPSIS is then applied: the fuzzy performance values are normalised, combined with the weights, and compared with the fuzzy positive and negative ideal solutions to compute a closeness coefficient for each architecture. Finally, the architectures are ranked according to these coefficients, and the stability of the ranking is examined through simple weight-perturbation experiments. An overview of these steps is illustrated schematically in Fig. 1. The methodological contribution lies in the structured integration and operational implementation of established components, data-screened expert elicitation, fuzzy aggregation, and multi-scenario weight sensitivity, within a single decision-support workflow.

Evaluation criteria for coastal pollution monitoring

Literature identification and screening for criteria derivation. To derive evaluation criteria that reflect established practice in coastal pollution monitoring and observing-system design, we conducted a structured literature review of peer-reviewed articles and technical/agency reports published between [2018–2025]. Searches were performed in [e.g., Web of Science, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar], using combinations of keywords such as coastal water quality monitoring, marine pollution monitoring, ocean observing system, coastal observatory, IoT water quality sensors, wireless sensor network, HF radar, glider monitoring, deployment cost, energy autonomy, uptime, and system resilience. Records were screened in two stages. First, titles/abstracts were assessed to retain studies relevant to coastal/nearshore monitoring architectures or observing-system performance. Second, full texts were reviewed to extract explicitly reported performance indicators and decision-relevant considerations. Studies focused solely on laboratory sensor calibration, without system/architecture implications, were excluded. From the retained set (approximately 70 sources), recurring indicators were extracted and grouped into conceptually overlapping families (e.g., detection/measurement performance, domain coverage, economic cost, energy/autonomy, and operational resilience). The final criteria set C₁–C₅ was then obtained by consolidating these indicator families and cross-checking their relevance with the expert panel to ensure that the criteria remained practical for real decision-making rather than an excessively long checklist.

The selection of evaluation criteria for this study is grounded in a structured review of prior work on marine and coastal water-quality monitoring, ocean observing systems, and IoT-based environmental sensor networks. In total, approximately 70 peer-reviewed articles and technical reports were examined, with a focus on studies that reported performance indicators for in-situ or networked sensing of coastal pollution. From this pool, we compiled an initial list of recurring indicators (e.g., accuracy, spatial representativeness, cost, and system reliability), then refined and consolidated it through discussion with the expert panel to avoid redundancy and ensure that the final set remained practical for decision-making. Based on this combined literature review and expert consultation, five criteria – were retained as a compact yet comprehensive set for assessing coastal pollution monitoring architectures. (detection performance and sensitivity) quantifies how reliably and precisely an architecture can detect contaminant events at relevant concentrations. (spatial coverage) reflects the horizontal and, where applicable, vertical extent over which the system can provide informative measurements for coastal domains such as estuaries, harbours, and nearshore plumes. (life-cycle operational cost) represents both capital and recurring expenditures needed to deploy and maintain the network over time. (energy demand) describes the power requirements of sensing and communication components, which are critical for long-term autonomous operation at sea. Finally, (operational robustness and resilience) denotes the architecture’s ability to sustain proper data delivery under variable and harsh marine conditions. In the subsequent analysis, C1, C2, and C5 are treated as benefit criteria (larger values are preferable), whereas C3 and C4 are modelled as cost criteria (smaller values are preferable).

Although coastal monitoring studies report many indicators (e.g., sampling frequency, data latency, maintainability, communications reliability, false-alarm rate, detection limit, and platform survivability), many of these are strongly correlated or can be treated as subcomponents of broader decision dimensions. We therefore consolidated the extracted indicators into five criteria to (i) avoid redundancy, (ii) keep the expert elicitation feasible, and (iii) preserve interpretability for coastal managers who must weigh a small number of dominant trade-offs. Specifically, C₁ captures the architecture’s ability to detect pollution signals reliably (including sensitivity), C₂ captures whether the system provides sufficient spatial/vertical representativeness for the coastal domain of interest, C₃ and C₄ capture the life-cycle resource burden (economic and energy/autonomy constraints), and C₅ captures the reliability of sustained operation and data return under harsh coastal conditions. Taken together, these five criteria span the primary decision tensions in pollution surveillance (performance and coverage versus resource constraints and robustness), while remaining compact enough to support transparent sensitivity testing and stakeholder communication.

Expert panel, data collection and preprocessing

The empirical input for the decision model was obtained from a targeted expert survey. In total, 30 domain specialists from Henan University of Science and Technology (China) agreed to participate. The surveys were approved by Henan University of Science and Technology in China, in accordance with applicable guidelines and regulations. They were selected based on their publication record and professional involvement in marine environmental monitoring, sensor and network design, and data-driven decision support for coastal applications. The group was intentionally composed to cover several complementary perspectives (e.g., marine engineering, environmental science, sensing and communication technologies, and data analysis), so that no single discipline would dominate the evaluations. Although a panel of 30 experts is relatively small, it is consistent with many studies in fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making. It provides a workable compromise between the diversity of opinion and the depth of feedback that can be addressed within a single survey. Potential bias arising from the limited panel size is mitigated by the heterogeneity of backgrounds and by using aggregated rather than individual judgements in the analysis.

The questionnaire comprised two main parts. In the first part, each expert indicated the relative importance of the criteria – for coastal pollution monitoring. In the second part, they assessed the performance of each candidate sensor network architecture for each criterion using an ordered linguistic scale (e.g., very low, low, medium, high, very high). Before the responses were translated into fuzzy numbers, the raw data were carefully screened. Questionnaires with large sections left blank or with inconsistent markings were excluded; duplicate markings for individual items were resolved; and all ratings were mapped to a standard linguistic scale to ensure comparability. The distributions of responses were then inspected to spot extreme values that did not align with the rest of the panel; such cases were checked and, when clearly inconsistent, excluded from further processing. Only this cleaned and harmonised set of expert judgements was carried forward to the fuzzification step and used to construct the fuzzy decision matrix.

Fuzzification and aggregation of expert judgements

The cleaned linguistic assessments provided by the experts are converted into fuzzy quantities for processing within the Fuzzy TOPSIS framework. A standard ordered linguistic scale is used for all questions (e.g., very low, low, medium, high, very high). Each term on this scale is associated with a triangular fuzzy number that reflects its lower bound, most plausible value, and upper bound on an underlying numerical axis. Triangular fuzzy numbers (TFNs) are adopted because they provide a transparent and minimally parameterised representation of linguistic assessments in group decision-making. Each TFN expresses a lower bound, a most plausible value, and an upper bound, which naturally maps to how experts typically communicate uncertainty using qualitative terms. Compared with more complex fuzzy forms (e.g., trapezoidal, Gaussian, or type-2 fuzzy sets), TFNs reduce the number of parameters that must be elicited or assumed, thereby improving interpretability and making sensitivity/robustness analysis easier to communicate to practitioners. TFNs are also widely used in fuzzy TOPSIS for group decision-making, enabling comparability with established formulations and distance-based computations within the TOPSIS procedure.

Using this mapping, every evaluation of an architecture with respect to a criterion, as well as every statement of criterion importance, is converted into a triangular fuzzy number. For each pair of architecture and criterion, the individual fuzzy ratings from all experts are aggregated into a single consensus value by averaging the three parameters across respondents, yielding an entry in the fuzzy decision matrix. The same aggregation procedure is applied to the fuzzy importance judgments to obtain a set of fuzzy criterion weights. In this way, the method preserves the intrinsic vagueness of linguistic judgements, incorporates differences of opinion among experts, and produces a structured fuzzy representation of both performance and importance that can be used directly in the subsequent Fuzzy TOPSIS calculations.

Validation and interpretation strategy

The outputs of the Fuzzy TOPSIS analysis are interpreted and validated through expert- and literature-based checks; no empirical or field-based deployment validation is performed in this study. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as a structured synthesis of expert knowledge and published evidence, rather than as a performance benchmark confirmed by observed operational data. Results

This section presents the quantitative evaluation results, including architecture rankings, closeness coefficients, weight-scenario comparisons, sensitivity analysis, and robustness assessment. Interpretation of these findings is provided in the Discussion section.

Performance profiles of the five sensor network architectures

Five candidate sensor network architectures are considered and denoted as –. is a conventional fixed-moored buoy network in which multi-parameter instruments are mounted on anchored platforms at selected points in the coastal zone. combines autonomous underwater gliders with satellite communications, enabling mobile profiling of the water column while exchanging data via spaceborne links. is a dense, IoT-oriented seabed and midwater node grid in which many low-cost sensing units are interconnected via short-range acoustic or optical links. represents an opportunistic “ferry- and vessel-based” monitoring system in which commercial or service ships carry compact sensors along their regular routes. Finally, couples coastal high-frequency radar coverage with fewer “smart” moorings at key locations, providing broad surface current information while still collecting in situ water-quality data at fixed sites. In this study, each performance rating is expressed as a triangular fuzzy number , which can be interpreted operationally as a pessimistic, most likely, and optimistic performance range. The lower bound reflects a conservative scenario (e.g., poorer data return due to harsher sea state, maintenance constraints, or communication losses), represents expected day-to-day performance under typical operating conditions, and the upper bound reflects favourable conditions (e.g., strong telemetry availability, good sensor uptime, or optimal deployment logistics). This representation allows the ranking to incorporate realistic performance variability rather than assuming a single deterministic value.

Closeness coefficients and baseline ranking of architectures

Using the fuzzy decision matrix and criteria weights described in the previous subsections, the Fuzzy TOPSIS procedure is applied as specified in the section “Fuzzy TOPSIS analysis procedure”. Beyond rank order, the magnitude of the closeness coefficients (CC) indicates whether alternatives are decisively different or broadly comparable in practice. In our baseline results (Table 4), the top two alternatives are the glider–satellite system (CC = 0.76) and HF radar plus smart moorings (CC = 0.71), giving a difference of ΔCC = 0.05. At the same time, because both leading architectures achieve CC ≥ 0.70, they can be interpreted as the two strongest “backbone” candidates in this coastal/nearshore setting, with the second-ranked HF radar–mooring option representing a credible close alternative if deployment constraints, governance, or platform availability limit the feasibility of sustained glider operations.

Discussions

This section interprets the architecture ranking results in relation to operational monitoring requirements, existing research on coastal observing systems, and the practical constraints faced by coastal managers. The ranking patterns observed in this study are broadly consistent with previous research emphasising the value of hybrid monitoring systems, but they also highlight important operational trade-offs that have received less attention in earlier conceptual or single-technology evaluations. These results indicate that architectures with mobility and adaptive spatial sampling provide higher monitoring effectiveness in dynamic coastal environments. The fuzzy performance values and the defuzzified criteria weights together provide a consistent numerical basis for the subsequent Fuzzy TOPSIS computation. Architectures differ in their relative scores across benefit and cost criteria, which are incorporated into the ranking procedure through normalisation and weighting.

Comparison with previous studies and conceptual frameworks

The methodological core of this work follows the general structure of fuzzy TOPSIS, as described in surveys and methodological papers, which use triangular fuzzy numbers to represent linguistic expert judgements and derive rankings via distances to the positive and negative ideal solutions30. Our use of a group of 30 experts, aggregation of their fuzzy evaluations, and subsequent defuzzification of the criteria weights is consistent with established approaches to fuzzy TOPSIS for group decision-making, in which uncertainty and heterogeneity in expert opinions are explicitly retained rather than averaged out prematurely39. At the same time, the formulation adopted here remains close to the original TOPSIS logic of Hwang and Yoon, in which the preferred alternative is as close as possible to an ideal solution and as far as possible from a nadir solution.

A first point of comparison concerns applications of fuzzy TOPSIS to wireless sensor networks and related communication problems. Several authors have used fuzzy TOPSIS or closely related fuzzy multi-criteria schemes to guide cluster-head selection, routing, or configuration decisions in wireless sensor networks, often using criteria such as residual energy, node density, and distance to the base station40.

Recent research on underwater Internet of Things (UIoT) and underwater sensor connectivity reinforces the relevance of multi-criteria decision-making in marine sensing. Multi-attribute decision frameworks have been proposed to balance connectivity, latency, reliability, and energy consumption in underwater IoT deployments, sometimes combining feature engineering with optimisation to select appropriate underwater devices and communication modes41. While these contributions focus primarily on connectivity and protocol performance in deep or offshore settings, the present work focuses on nearshore pollution monitoring and explicitly incorporates criteria such as detection performance, spatial coverage, and operational robustness in coastal environments. In this way, our analysis extends the use of fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making from internal network configuration to the choice between fundamentally different platform combinations at the coastal scale.

On the environmental monitoring side, there is now a substantial literature on IoT- and WSN-based water-quality monitoring systems, both inland and coastal. Studies have developed real-time IoT monitoring for treatment plants and distribution systems, as well as intelligent IoT–WSN frameworks that use machine learning or AI-based “virtual sensors” to infer parameters42. These works demonstrate the technical feasibility and advantages of IoT architectures for water quality. Still, they typically present a single architecture or prototype rather than comparing multiple architectures under a unified multi-criteria framework. Our results, which show that an IoT seabed/mid-water grid becomes more attractive when robustness is heavily weighted but loses ground when spatial coverage and cost dominate, provide a structured complement to system-specific IoT case studies by quantifying in which preference regimes such dense networks are most appropriate. This implies that IoT-based architectures are conditionally effective rather than universally optimal.

The analysis and resulting ranking are intended primarily for coastal and nearshore pollution monitoring, where operational constraints (access, maintenance frequency, communication range), spatial heterogeneity, and rapid event dynamics (spill/discharge detection) strongly shape system performance. The relative advantages of architectures may differ in other contexts (e.g., offshore deep-water monitoring, enclosed lagoons, or highly ice-affected regions), where coverage needs, platform survivability, and cost structures change. Accordingly, the presented ranking should be interpreted as context-dependent decision support, and the framework is best used by re-eliciting weights/scores for the local setting.

The ranking obtained here is also consistent with conceptual developments in coastal observing systems that emphasise multi-platform integration. European initiatives such as JERICO have long advocated combining fixed buoys, FerryBox routes, gliders, HF radar, and other platforms to achieve sustained, multi-scale observations of coastal seas43. High-frequency radar networks, which provide near-real-time maps of surface currents over hundreds of kilometres, are now recognised as part of the Global Ocean Observing System and have been widely deployed for applications including oil-spill monitoring and search and rescue33. At the same time, glider-based studies have highlighted the ability of autonomous underwater vehicles to sample the water column and coastal processes during storms and other energetic events, thereby complementing fixed buoys and ship-based surveys44. The fact that our fuzzy TOPSIS model consistently favours a glider–satellite configuration and places an HF radar plus smart mooring architecture in second position aligns well with this broader move towards mobile and remote-sensing platforms as central elements of coastal observing systems, with fixed sensors and IoT nodes playing more targeted roles. Taken together, these comparisons indicate that the present study sits at the intersection of fuzzy TOPSIS methodology, wireless/underwater sensor network design, and coastal observing-system development. It employs a standard yet robust fuzzy TOPSIS formulation. It applies it in a manner that explicitly accounts for the diversity of modern coastal monitoring platforms and the operational trade-offs faced by managers responsible for pollution surveillance.

This study relies on expert judgement to score architectures and assign importance to criteria; consequently, the results are conditional on expert-informed assumptions and may reflect institutional or regional framing. Independent field validation (e.g., deployment records of uptime, detection limits, coverage achieved, and realised OPEX) was not within the scope of the study, and incorporating such data is a priority for future work. In addition, the expert panel was drawn from a single institution; future studies should expand to multi-institution and multi-region panels to reduce potential bias and improve generalisability. Finally, cost and energy demand were treated at a comparative level; integrating more detailed life-cycle costing, logistics constraints, and price/maintenance rate uncertainty would further strengthen the realism of architecture-level recommendations. In practical terms, hybrid architectures distribute monitoring risk across technologies, improve temporal and spatial coverage simultaneously, and allow managers to maintain baseline surveillance while deploying targeted high-resolution sensing when needed. No single architecture satisfies all criteria simultaneously, which supports the need for hybrid system design.

Implications for coastal monitoring decision-makers

For coastal agencies, the analysis provides three directly usable outputs: (1) a baseline architecture ranking with closeness coefficients, (2) a criterion-importance profile showing which objectives dominate the choice, and (3) sensitivity-based scenarios indicating when an alternative becomes preferable under cost/energy or robustness priorities, supporting portfolio-like hybrid design rather than a single fixed recommendation. The ranking results have practical implications for coastal monitoring design. The persistence of A₂ as the top-ranked option across all scenarios indicates robustness of the decision outcome.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, all experts were recruited from a single institution, which may introduce institutional or regional framing effects in the evaluation of monitoring architectures. Although disciplinary diversity was intentionally maintained and an aggregated judgment was used to reduce individual bias, the resulting scores should be interpreted as conditional on this expert context. Second, independent field or deployment data were not available to directly validate performance indicators such as detection limits, spatial sampling density, uptime, or life-cycle cost. Accordingly, the fuzzy evaluations constitute a structured synthesis of expert knowledge and published evidence rather than operationalized performance measures.

The reported rankings should be interpreted as conditional on expert-derived performance assessments rather than empirical deployment measurements. Although structured elicitation and consistency screening were used to enhance reliability, the evaluations reflect informed judgment rather than directly observed operational performance. Consequently, the results should be understood as decision-support guidance under uncertainty rather than definitive performance validation of specific monitoring architectures.

Conclusion

This study provides a structured comparison of coastal monitoring architectures using a fuzzy multi-criteria framework, based on judgments from 30 domain experts. Linguistic evaluations were converted into triangular fuzzy numbers and assembled into a fuzzy decision matrix on a 0–10 scale. The derived criterion weights indicate that spatial coverage and detection performance are the dominant concerns (weights of 0.26 and 0.24), followed by life-cycle cost (0.20), robustness (0.16), and energy demand (0.14). The Fuzzy TOPSIS analysis produced a clear ranking of the five architectures. Sensitivity analysis confirms that the top-ranked architecture remains stable across weight variations, while secondary rankings vary under different priority scenarios. The results support the use of complementary architectures depending on operational priorities.

From a methodological perspective, this study contributes a structured and replicable decision-support workflow that integrates established components, structured expert elicitation, fuzzy performance aggregation, and multi-scenario weight-sensitivity analysis into a single operational evaluation pipeline for coastal monitoring architecture selection. Rather than modifying the underlying Fuzzy TOPSIS algorithm, the contribution lies in the systematic integration, transparent preprocessing of expert judgements, and explicit robustness testing of architecture rankings under alternative decision priorities. From a practical decision-making perspective, the results provide guidance for coastal monitoring agencies in selecting and combining sensor platforms under different operational priorities.

Future research should prioritise integrating empirical deployment data into the decision-support framework. The present study relies on structured expert judgement; however, field observations could be incorporated to calibrate or partially replace expert-derived performance ranges. For example, observed detection limits, spatial sampling density, uptime records, energy consumption, and life-cycle costs could be used to parameterise or validate fuzzy performance representations. A hybrid expert–deployment data approach would enable iterative updating of architecture evaluations and would allow predicted rankings to be compared directly with operational outcomes from pilot or historical monitoring programmes. Further extensions may include incorporating additional decision criteria, more detailed life-cycle costing, and cross-validation using alternative multi-criteria decision-making methods.

Overall, the study demonstrates how structured multi-criteria decision analysis can support transparent, evidence-informed planning of coastal pollution monitoring systems under uncertainty.
