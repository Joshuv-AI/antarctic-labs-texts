A redox flow battery coupling energy storage and chemical manufacturing in a single device
Authors: Yucong Miao, Dawei Gao, Yayue Dai, Fuqiang Shao, Shengnan Li, Chunyu Zhang, Yu Fu, Qinghui Ren, Zhenhua Li, Mingfei Shao, Haohong Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRedox flow batteries provide a competitive solution for renewable energy integration and grid fluctuations stabilization. However, the profit pattern solely relies on the grid price difference between peak and off-peak period. Herein, we report an open-loop flow battery coupling energy storage and chemical manufacturing in a single device. Specifically, we pair VO2+ reduction with organic compounds oxidation to value-added chemicals during discharging, and VO2+ oxidation with hydrogen evolution during charging. The battery demonstrates versatility across 13 organic substrates and compatibility with most typical posolytes. Taking VO2+/VO2+ pairing with ethylene glycol-to-glycolic acid conversion and H2 production as an example, the battery displays >100% voltage and energy efficiencies, and exhibits cycling stability over 200 cycles. By scaling up and integrating with a photovoltaic panel, the device produces 532.2 g of glycolic acid and 272.9 L of H2 over 10 days with daytime charging and nighttime discharging. Techno-economic analysis suggests that the system is profitable with a levelized cost of electricity of –0.26 $/kWh. This work demonstrates the profit pattern diversification potential of redox flow batteries by co-production of value-added chemicals and fuels.Subject terms: Batteries, Electrochemistry, Energy, Batteries

Introduction

The demand for energy decarbonization has driven substantial advancements in renewable energy (such as wind and solar) utilization for electricity generation1. However, their intermittent nature is in conflict with the constant supply of electricity, necessitating an energy storage technique for seamless grid integration2,3. Redox flow batteries (RFBs) have emerged as a promising large-scale energy storage technology due to their high efficiency, safe operation, and long lifetime4,5. The charging−discharging function of a conventional RFB is relying on the coupling of redox pairs in posolyte and negolyte, and the energy density (E = Q * ΔU) and power density (P = I * ΔU) requirements for a battery are satisfied by the potential difference (ΔU) between the redox pairs. Taking vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs) as an example (Fig. 1a), during the charging process, VO2⁺ oxidation to VO2⁺ in the posolyte couples with V3⁺ reduction to V2⁺ in negolyte, while during the subsequent discharging process, reversible redoxes take place (VO2⁺ reduction to VO2⁺ in the posolyte couples with V2⁺ oxidation to V3⁺ in negolyte)6. Therefore, RFBs’ electrolyte is circulated in a closed-loop manner. Despite its technical feasibility, the commercialization of RFBs is constrained by their high cost. Firstly, the profit pattern of RFBs is onefold7, highly relying on fluctuation of electricity price between peak and off-peak periods in power grids ( ~ 0.03–0.18 $/kWh)8. Secondly, the system cost is high. For the most mature RFBs, VRFBs, the system cost is approximately $447/kWh9, in which vanadium electrolyte accounts for ~40%10. Thus, the system capital cost significantly exceeds the cost metrics for the energy storage program ($100/kWh) set by the Advanced Research Projects Agency-Energy (ARPA-E)11. While cutting-edge research has been conducted on the development of novel organic/inorganic redox couples12–16, electrolyte additives17,18, pH-decoupling systems19,20, and high-performance ion-exchange membranes21,22, the high cost of RFBs cannot be fundamentally resolved. It is urgent to develop more cost-efficient RFBs with a diverse profit pattern.

In recent years, some studies have coupled a galvanic battery with chemical production, in which electricity is discharged, and a value-added chemical is produced spontaneously. For example, zinc (Zn) oxidation in Zn battery was reported to couple with reduction reactions, such as hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and NO3⁻/NO2⁻ reductions23–25 (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1), and oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) in fuel cell was described to couple with organic compounds oxidation (e.g., furfural, glycerol, formaldehyde)26–28 (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2). Despite potential profitability during the discharging process by co-producing chemicals, these galvanic batteries are primary batteries and are not rechargeable, hindering their application for the huge demand of electricity storage from fluctuating wind and solar energy. In addition, owing to sluggish negolyte reaction kinetics, these batteries often showed low power density. For instance, the reported power density of Zn battery coupling with chemical production was lower than 80 mW cm−2. In our recent work, a rechargeable battery was developed that reduces furfural to furfuryl alcohol during the charging process and oxidizes furfural to furoic acid during discharging process29. This was achieved by pairing the above reduction/oxidation reactions at the negative electrode with a redox of a cobalt-doped nickel hydroxide at the positive electrode. However, the limited current density (<60 mA cm⁻2) and inherent scalability limitations of this system are significantly inferior to those in RFBs (such as VRFBs, which is typically operated at current density >100 mA cm⁻2 and readily scalable to kW-level and beyond)6, impeding it for large-scale energy storage. In addition, the inherent limitations of the solid material and the limited market demand of furfuryl alcohol and furoic acid also hinder the practical application of this rechargeable battery (Supplementary Note 1). While heterogeneous redox material systems indeed offer higher volumetric and gravimetric energy densities suited for compact applications, such as distributed storage and electric vehicles, the demands of grid-scale, long-duration energy storage require that cost per kilowatt-hour, decoupled power/energy scalability, safety, and operational simplicity take precedence over energy density. Moreover, large-scale demonstration and renewable-energy-driven operation (e.g., solar-powered scenarios) were not explored in prior studies.

To transcend the above limitations, we propose to develop open-loop flow batteries (OLFBs) that pair the redox couples in RFBs with oxidative and reductive half-reactions in one system. By strategically selecting organic compounds as substrates for OLFB half-reactions, this approach enables concurrent energy storage and electrosynthesis of value-added chemicals, potentially yielding high profits. Specifically, during charging, the OLFB couples posolyte oxidation with hydrogenation of organic substrates or analogous reductive reactions in the negolyte (Fig. 1b, right panel) while discharging involves oxidation of organic feedstocks in the negolyte to produce value-added chemicals alongside posolyte reduction (Fig. 1b, left panel). This unidirectional organic reaction mechanism fundamentally distinguishes OLFBs from conventional RFBs, as energy storage and chemical manufacturing are not merely co-located but are electrochemically coupled within a single device. In this design, the chemical reactions are not parasitic but rather integral to the charge and discharge process, thereby establishing a broadly applicable platform for the synergistic coupling of diverse chemical processes with energy storage. Girault et al. previously reported a dual-circuit RFBs (Supplementary Fig. 3a) operates by first charging the electrolytes in a conventional flow cell, then pump charged electrolytes to external reactors30,31. There, the charged electrolytes drive decoupled oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and HER over heterogeneous catalysts (e.g., RuO2 for OER and Mo2C for HER). This setup directly converts the battery’s stored electrochemical energy into chemical energy (H2 and O2) through spontaneous catalytic discharge. Note that Jin and co-colleague recently reported an electrochemical synthesis strategy (Supplementary Fig. 3b) that pairs a heterogeneous redox (for instance, sodium nickel hexacyanoferrate or copper hexacyanoferrate) with oxidative and reductive half-reactions32,33, enabling decoupled and on-demand electrochemical synthesis of valuable chemicals at the cathode (e.g. oxygen reduction reaction to produce H2O2) and anode (e.g., SO42− oxidation to produce S2O82−). However, this system is an electrolytic cell rather a galvanic battery, namely, its necessities constant electricity input and has no energy output capability. In addition, the system was operated at low current density ( < 15 mA cm⁻2) and limited scale. Taken together, a battery (more specifically, a secondary battery) that pairs electricity charging/discharging and chemical production with high-performance metrics is desirable for large-scale energy storage.

In this work, we develop an OLFB capable of simultaneously discharging/charging and producing chemicals and H2 in a single device, specifically targeting cost-reduction requirements for large-scale, long-duration energy storage scenarios. Taking the most mature VRFBs as an example, we pair the oxidation of VO2⁺ to VO2⁺ with HER during charging process, and reduction of VO2⁺ to VO2⁺ with the organic compounds oxidation during discharging process (Fig. 1b, left panel). For a conventional VRFB, the redox potential difference between V3⁺/V2⁺ pair in negolyte (–0.26 V vs. SHE) and VO2⁺/VO2⁺ pair in posolyte (1.00 V vs. SHE) delivers an open circuit voltage (OCV) value of 1.26 V. By selection of organic compounds oxidations with more negative equilibrium potentials than V3⁺/V2⁺ pair, the OLFB demonstrates 13 examples of organic compounds (alcohols and aldehydes) conversion with larger OCV (a maximum OCV of 2.2 V) than those in advanced inorganic and organic RFBs. Taking VO2⁺/VO2⁺ redox pairing with ethylene glycol (EG)-to-glycolic acid conversion (during discharging) and H2 production (during charging) as an example, the OLFBs shows a peak power density of 238.4 mW cm−2 and >96% Faradaic efficiency (FE) for the co-production of glycolic acid and H2 across a wide charging/discharging current density range of 20 to 150 mA cm−2, while achieving voltage and energy efficiencies exceeding 100% at 20 mA cm−2. The OLFBs also showed high stability in 200 cycles, with Coulombic efficiency (CE) and energy efficiency (EE) decay rates below 0.013% per cycle while the discharge voltage was stable. Furthermore, we demonstrate that common polyoxides (such as Fe3+/Fe2+, [Fe(CN)6]3–/[Fe(CN)6]4–, MnO4–/MnO42–, 4-hydroxy-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO-OH)) are applicable to OLFB systems, enabling energy storage coupled with chemical production. To validate the practical application, the OLFB device was scaled up to a total electrode area of 200cm2, and was powered by solar integration with a photovoltaic panel. In a 10-day period test, the OLFB was effectively charged in daytime and discharged in nighttime, during which it delivered 948.6 Wh of electrical energy; meanwhile, 532.2 g of glycolic acid with 97.2% purity and 272.9 L of H2 were produced, demonstrating the feasibility of renewable energy storage/release and chemical/fuel production. Techno-economic analysis (TEA) suggests that renewable electricity-driven OLFB makes a profit during the energy storage process, showing a levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) of –0.26 $/kWh (with an annual revenue of $1.76 million), which is significantly lower than the target (0.05 $/kWh) set by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)34.

Results

Feasibility of coupling organic compounds oxidation with electricity output

The development of the OLFBs systems is initiated by selecting promising organic compounds for oxidation, and comparing corresponding equilibrium potentials with those of typical redox couples in RFBs. As shown in Fig. 2a, commodity alcohols exhibit more negative equilibrium potentials, especially polyols, such as EG, with a reaction potential of only –0.51 V vs. SHE for electrooxidation to glycolic acid, which is much lower than that of V3+/V2+ negative redox couple (–0.26 V vs. SHE) in the VRFBs and of Cr3+/Cr2+ (–0.41 V vs. SHE) in the iron-chromium redox flow batteries. Therefore, when EG-to-glycolic acid conversion is paired with VO2+/VO2+ positive redox couple, a theoretical high open-circuit voltage (OCV) of 1.51 V is anticipated. In addition, aldehydes, such as glucose and furfural, have more negative oxidation potentials (at −0.90 V and −0.83 V vs. SHE, respectively), showing the possibility of constructing OLFBs with high OCV.

We then investigated the possibility of pairing several traditional redox couples in RFBs with a series of organic molecule oxidations with overall 13 examples, including monohydric alcohol (methanol, ethanol, benzyl alcohol), enols (ascorbic acid), polyols (EG, glycerol) and aldehydes (formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, benzaldehyde, furfural, 5-methylfurfural, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural, glucose). By employing linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests in a three-electrode cell, the OCV values and the discharging feasibility of different OLFBs were assessed. As shown in Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 3, the onset potentials of the organic oxidation reactions are distributed from –0.91 to 0.48 V vs. SHE, with monohydric alcohols exhibiting the highest onset potentials, whereas polyols oxidation initiating near –0.3 V vs. SHE due to their more favorable reaction thermodynamics35,36. Most aldehyde oxidation reactions display onset potentials below −0.73 V vs. SHE, representing a ~ 0.5 V negative shift compared to the V3+/V2+ redox couple of VRFBs. Notably, all the typical positive redox couples of RFBs exhibit a large potential difference (>0.5 V) compared with most investigated organic oxidation reactions, theoretically enabling spontaneous discharging when paired. Among the positive redox couples, VO2+/VO2+ redox shows the most positive potential (1.11 V vs. SHE), implying a relatively larger discharge voltage in the OLFBs (Fig. 2c). As a result, the measured OCV values were in the range of 1.1 − 2.2 V when coupling organic oxidation reactions with VO2⁺-to-VO2+ reduction, in line with our expectation of a high output voltage (Supplementary Fig. 4).

To demonstrate the discharging ability of OLFBs when different organic compounds were used as the negolyte, their polarization curves were attained in a 2 × 2 cm flow cell (Supplementary Fig. 5). Among the tested organic compounds, polyol oxidation reactions generally give higher power density, in which EG oxidation shows the highest peak power density of 238.4 mW cm−2 (Fig. 2d). This can be attributed to the rapid oxidation kinetics of polyols containing adjacent hydroxyl groups37. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, enols and aldehydes oxidation also display high power densities, as exemplified by ascorbic acid with a power density of 229.5 mW cm−2 and formaldehyde with a power density of 216.9 mW cm−2. We admit that the power density of OLFBs is still lower than that of the mature flow batteries, such as VRFBs ( > 300 mW cm−2)38, but this gap is expected to be narrowed by enhancing organic oxidation efficiency via advanced electrode designs in future work. Notably, compared to state-of-the-art organic flow batteries and inorganic flow batteries, the OLFBs developed in this work exhibit higher OCV values (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 4). In particular, polyol-based OLFBs display OCV values in the range of 1.6 − 1.9 V, and aldehyde-based ones display OCV higher than 2.1 V because of their low oxidation potentials39.

Performances of open-loop flow battery

Based on the large OCV value and the highest power density when EG was utilized as the negolyte in the OLFBs (Fig. 2e), we focused on the EG oxidation reaction (EGOR) to in-depth investigate the discharge behavior. Moreover, the conventional negative redox couple of RFBs during charging was substituted by hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), thus a reversible OLFB with EGOR/HER | | VO2+/VO2+ configuration was constructed, which is expected to produce glycolic acid and H2 at the negative electrode during discharging and charging process, respectively (Fig. 3a). The cell stack and system of the reversible OLFB is given (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 7). As depicted in Fig. 3c, the OLFB achieves a discharge voltage of 1.15 V at 20 mA cm−2. Even at a higher current density of 150 mA cm−2, a high discharge voltage of 0.75 V was maintained. The observed voltage reduction can be attributed to enhanced polarization during EG oxidation. Benefiting from the reduced polarization during HER, the required charging voltage increases by only 0.07 V (from 1.12 to 1.19 V) as the current density rises from 20 to 150 mA cm−2. Note that the discharging and charging capacities are stable at ~41 Ah L−1, indicating a CE near 100% across the current density range (Fig. 3d). The voltage efficiency (VE) of the OLFB system remains >90% at low polarization current densities (20 and 50 mA cm–2), even exceeding 100% at 20 mA cm–2. Moreover, the energy efficiency (EE) of OLFBs is higher than 100% at 20 mA cm–2, surpassing that of conventional VRFBs within the current density range of 20–50 mA cm–2 (Supplementary Fig. 8). As current density exceeds 100 mA cm−2, the efficiency of OLFBs shows a decreasing trend. This is primarily attributed to the intrinsically slower reaction kinetics of EGOR compared to inorganic ion-based redox couples and is likely further exacerbated by mass-transfer effects under high-current conditions, including rapid local substrate depletion, product accumulation at the electrode surface, and insufficient adsorption sites. Future improvements may focus on designing electrodes with integrated adsorption-catalytic sites and optimizing reactor flow fields to mitigate these limitations.

The products generated during charging−discharging processes of OLFBs were then analyzed. Liquid chromatography (LC) revealing that EG was oxidized to glycolic acid (a key monomer for biodegradable plastics40) during discharging process (Supplementary Fig. 9), and no membrane crossover of organic substrates or products was observed (Supplementary Fig. 10). The glycolic acid production rate increased from 0.19 to 1.47 mmol cm–2 h–1 as the current density rises from 20 to 150 mA cm−2, with FE and selectivity was consistently higher than 96% (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 11). During charging process, H2 was produced with a production rate of 2.79 mmol cm–2 h–1 and FE of approximately 100% at 150 mA cm–2. Monitoring of the discharging process indicates that the state of charge (SOC) of the OLFB decreased to 15% within 2 h at 100 mA cm–2, while the FE remained >98% for glycolic acid production throughout discharging process (Supplementary Fig. 12). Additionally, the OLFB demonstrates satisfactory peak power density at low SOC (180 mW cm–2 at 20% SOC) (Supplementary Fig. 13). The results above indicate that the OLFB can maintain stable electricity output and chemical production throughout the discharge process. Although pairing EGOR with HER could theoretically form a fuel cell to simultaneously generate chemicals and discharge electricity based on their reaction potential difference (Fig. 3a), the performance of such a fuel cell is far inferior to that of the OLFB and can be considered practically meaningless (Supplementary Figs. 14, 15, Supplementary Table 5, and Supplementary Note 2). Furthermore, to accommodate renewable electricity, a secondary battery architecture is essential for energy storage via reversible charge/discharge, whereas fuel cells, as primary batteries, are predominantly employed in scenarios where chemical energy is converted to electricity to supply power. Therefore, integrating organic reactions into the OLFB system remains essential to achieve the dual objectives of energy storage and value-added chemical production. To assess the stability of OLFB, cycling tests were conducted at 100 mA cm–2 over 200 cycles (corresponding to a total of 150 h). As a consequence, the CE and EE decay rate was below 0.013% per cycle, while the capacity decay rate stayed under 0.035% per cycle, with the discharge voltage remaining stable throughout (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 16), and glycolic acid was stably produced at a high production rate ( ~ 0.91 mmol cm–2 h–1) and FE ( ~ 98%) (Supplementary Fig. 17), demonstrating long-term operational durability of OLFBs. Cycling tests reveal that using a cation exchange membrane (CEM) in the OLFB allows crossover of H+ and VO2+ species, which may compromise long-term pH stability and capacity retention under operational conditions. However, these engineering challenges can be mitigated through system design strategies, such as optimization of the membrane and exploring homogeneous pH systems (Supplementary Note 3).

Generality of OLFBs

After demonstrating the high performance of EGOR/HER | | VO2+/VO2+ OLFBs, we then evaluated the generality of OLFBs by replacing EGOR with other organic compounds oxidation in the negolyte during discharging process (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 19). Firstly, aldehydes oxidations was achieved, including formaldehyde-to-formic acid (discharge voltage: 1.68 V at 50 mA cm−2, capacity: 42 Ah L–1, FE: ~100%, production rate: 2.01 mmol cm–2 h–1; Supplementary Fig. 20), furfural-to-furfural acid, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural-to-5-hydroxymethyl-2-furancarboxylic acid, 5-methylfurfural-to-5-methylfuroic acid (discharge voltages: >1.45 V at 50 mA cm−2, capacity: ~39 Ah L–1, FEs: >96%; Supplementary Figs. 21–23), benzaldehyde-to-benzoic acid (discharge voltage: 1.53 V at 50 mA cm−2, capacity: ~39 Ah L–1, FE: ~100%, production rate: 1.88 mmol cm–2 h–1; Supplementary Fig. 24), and glucose-to-gluconic acid (discharge voltage: 1.18 V at 50 mA cm−2, capacity: 41 Ah L–1, FE: ~100%, production rate: 0.95 mmol cm–2 h–1; Supplementary Fig. 25) conversions. Second, alcohol oxidation was accomplished. Besides EG, glycerol represents another polyol, showing efficient glycerol-to-lactic acid conversion (discharge voltage: 0.95 V at 50 mA cm−2, capacity: 39 Ah L–1, FE: 79%, production rate: 0.77 mmol cm–2 h–1; Supplementary Fig. 26). However, monohydric alcohols (benzyl alcohol, ethanol, methanol) showed relatively lower voltages ( < 0.5 V), consistent with their low OCV values. All these three monohydric alcohols were selectively oxidized to benzoic acid, acetic acid, and formic acid with high FEs (76%, 97%, and 88%, respectively; Supplementary Figs. 27–29). It is noted that rational catalyst design is essential for facilitating discharge processes, demonstrating notable performance improvements over pristine graphite felt electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 30 and Supplementary Note 4).

In addition, other typical redox pairs in RFBs excepted VO2+/VO2+ were demonstrated applicable for OLFB (by coupling with EGOR), including Fe3+/Fe2+ pair (discharge voltage: 0.67 V at 50 mA cm−2, capacity: 23 Ah L–1, FE: 94%, production rate: 0.43 mmol cm–2 h–1; Supplementary Fig. 31), [Fe(CN)6]3–/[Fe(CN)6]4– pair (discharge voltage: 0.28 V at 50 mA cm−2, capacity: 10 Ah L–1, FE: 96%, production rate: 0.45 mmol cm–2 h–1; Supplementary Fig. 32), MnO4–/MnO42– pair (discharge voltage: 0.65 V at 50 mA cm−2, capacity: 10 Ah L–1, FE: 95%, production rate: 0.44 mmol cm–2 h–1; Supplementary Fig. 33), and TEMPO-OH pair (discharge voltage: 0.79 V at 50 mA cm−2, capacity: 4 Ah L–1, FE: 91%, production rate: 0.43 mmol cm–2 h–1; Supplementary Fig. 34).

Solar-driven and large-scale OLFB system

To demonstrate its potential for large-scale operation, the OLFB was scaled up in electrode area (from 4 to 100 cm2) and then in number (two cells in series; Supplementary Fig. 35). To explore its potential for storing and utilizing renewable energy, the as-constructed OLFB’s reactor was integrated with a photovoltaic panel (power: 400 W) to allow solar supply. The solar-driven OLFB was charging under natural sunlight during the day, and was discharging at night. We then monitored the solar-to-electricity performance using the photovoltaic panel in a whole day. As shown in Fig. 5a, the output current appeared after sunrise at 6:00, reached ~10 A and remained stable between 11:00 and 13:00, then declined and eventually dropped to 0 A after sunset at 19:00. The intermittency of solar radiation intensity caused fluctuations in the charging current, and it may vary in different regions.

We evaluated the performances of the solar-driven OLFB under natural sun radiation (Fig.5b, top), with charging during the day and discharging at night under a current density of 100 mAcm –2. OLFB with EGOR/HER | | VO2+/VO2+ configuration was selected for its superior performance in the preceding studies. In a single-day test, the solar-driven OLFB was able to be discharged at night to power LED bulbs (Fig. 5b, bottom), preliminarily suggesting the feasibility of storing and utilizing solar energy. Then, a 10-day period test was implemented, corresponding to 10 charging−discharging cycles (Fig. 5c). In all, the solar-driven OLFB was charging for 65.3 h with an average charge voltage of 2.85 V and discharging for 55.1 h with an average discharge voltage of 1.73 V, during which it delivered a total discharge energy of 948.6 Wh. A voltage gap is observed between charge and discharge voltages, arising from electrode scaling that leads to non-uniform flow distribution and intensified electrode polarization at high current densities. Future improvements could focus on optimizing the flow-field architecture and engineering electrode structures with enhanced mass-transport properties to mitigate these challenges. For products, as shown in Fig. 5d, the OLFB shows stable glycolic acid production with >90% FE and 743.1 g accumulation (corresponding to 9.77 mol, with an average production rate of 177 mmol h–1) during the discharging process. It also shows stable H2 production with >90% FE and 272.9 L accumulation (with an average production rate of 4.18 L h–1) during charging process. Note that scaling up the OLFB system—by increasing electrode area and integrating cells in series—delivered a total voltage of 1.73 V and a total power output of 17300 mW, compared to 0.93 V and 372 mW for the initial small-scale prototype, while performance metrics normalized to electrode area (e.g. cell voltage, power density) showed no significant decay and the efficiency of organic chemical production remained uncompromised (Supplementary Table 6). However, a reduction in battery efficiency was observed (Supplementary Fig. 36), which is due to the non-uniform current distribution, enhanced mass transport limitations, and increased ohmic resistance that typically arise during electrochemical scaling. These challenges can be addressed through engineering optimizations, such as refining flow-field designs to ensure uniform electrolyte distribution and mitigating current-distribution inhomogeneity across scaled-up electrodes, as well as catalyst engineering to enhance redox kinetics at the positive electrode.

Moreover, to extend the OLFB’s daily operational duration for amortizing system installation costs, we raised the charging current to 200 mA cm–2 and enlarged the electrolyte volume to enhance the battery’s capacity (Supplementary Fig. 37), achieving 22.5 h of daily operation while maintaining >90% FE for chemicals production, demonstrating the application potential of OLFB system.

To demonstrate the total processing of OLFB, it is important to include product separation and purification into consideration, that is, to collect pure glycolic acid and H2 from OLFB’s electrolyte (with EGOR/HER | | VO2+/VO2+ configuration). For this purpose, we integrated electrodialysis technology with OLFB. As shown in Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 38, the resulting negolyte (containing glycolic acid in KOH) was subject to electrodialysis to recover KOH and a portion of unreacted EG. Then, CaCO3 was introduced to remove by-products, such as oxalic acid by precipitation and filtration. Through rectification, residual EG and other impurities were further eliminated, with the obtaining of glycolic acid solution. After ethanol washing, recrystallization and drying processes, solid glycolic acid was yielded, with mass of 532.2 g and purity of 97.2% as evidenced by LC, X-ray diffraction (XRD), 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Figs. 39, 40). In addition, the purity of H2 was detected to be 99.99% by a hydrogen purity analyzer (Supplementary Fig. 41). Collectively, by implementing a series of operating units in the total processing, we demonstrated the efficacy of OLFB for producing high-purity glycolic acid and H2 during charging−discharging cycles.

Techno-economic analysis

Given that the primary feature of OLFBs lies in the continuous production of value-added chemicals and H2 during charging−discharging cycles, we anticipated that it would show an economic superiority over traditional VRFBs. For economic comparison, a comprehensive techno-economic analysis (TEA) including capital cost, operating cost, and material cost was performed at two scales, 0.33 MW and 1 MW. Based on the foregoing total processing, the process flow diagram of OLFB was illustrated (Fig. 6a). Aspen Plus v12 was employed to simulate the separation process. The levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) was determined by normalizing the TEA calculated installation costs, future costs, and chemical revenue against the annual dischargeable electricity capacity, which serves as a key economic metric for energy storage systems41,42. For practical application, an LCOE value lower than the market electricity price would be attractive and competitive29. The results show that the LCOE of a 0.33 MW-scale VRFB is 0.16 $/kWh, and it further decreases to 0.12 $/kWh when the scale is increased to 1 MW due to the reduction of average operating costs (Fig. 6b). Notably, even considering the additional costs of separation equipment and processes, the extra revenue from the production of glycolic acid and H2 leads to further decrease of LCOE to –0.26 $/kWh for the 1 MW-scale OLFB, which is significantly lower than the target (0.05 $/kWh) set up by DOE34. The sensitivity analysis revealed that the operating current density of OLFB has the greatest impact on LCOE. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 42, at an operating current density of 150 mA cm–2, the LCOE can reach –0.72 $/kWh, which can be attributed to the accelerated chemical production. The increase in glycolic acid selectivity also has the potential to reduce the LCOE to –0.54 $/kWh, while the market price of KOH has the least impact on LCOE. This indicates that the chemical production process is a key factor influencing the profitability of OLFB.

A detailed profitability analysis of the systems was employed. The results suggest that under the current revenue model solely dependent on electricity arbitrage (namely, buying electricity when prices are low, and then selling or using it when prices are higher), a 0.33 MW-scale VRFB is operated at an annual deficit of $0.13 million per year, while this deficit decreases to $0.079 million per year when the scale is increase to 1 MW (Fig. 6c). This trend suggests that further system expansion and business model optimization could unlock promising economic viability for conventional VRFBs, but they necessitate substantial efforts. In comparison, OLFBs gives an annual revenue of $0.13 million for the 0.33 MW scale. After taking the additional electricity selling into account, the annual revenue can increase to $0.31 million. When the scale is expanded to 1 MW, the annual profit can reach a competitive $1.76 million. Note that the increased costs during scaling up primarily originates from material costs (the proportion of total cost increased from 29.2 to 34.8%) and the cost associated with heating steam of the distillation tower (the proportion of total cost increased from 19.7 to 23.4%) in the separation process (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 43). It is anticipated that by optimizing the supply chain and separation process, the raw materials and separation costs can be reduced, respectively, further promoting the profitability of OLFB.

Life-cycle assessment

Furthermore, to evaluate the environmental benefits of OLFB, life-cycle assessment (LCA) was conducted. Electricity generated from hard coal and a photovoltaic system was considered. We evaluate the environmental impacts, including ozone depletion (OD), global warming (GW), air smog (AS), acidification potential (AP), eutrophication potential (EP), human carcinogenic toxicity (HT), human non-carcinogenic toxicity (HNT), respiratory effects (RE), ecotoxicity (ET), and fossil fuel depletion (FFD).

As shown in Fig. 6e, Supplementary Figs. 44–49 and Supplementary Tables 7–8, by adopting photovoltaic-supplied electricity, most environmental impact factors for both OLFB and VRFB were significantly reduced. The deterioration in ecotoxicity is primarily attributed to toxic agents employed during photovoltaic cell manufacturing. The carbon footprint of the OLFB system was 2-fold reduced by changing the electricity supply from hard coal (4.36 kg CO2 eq/kWh) to photovoltaic system (2.29 kg CO2 eq/kWh), highlighting the sustainability of solar-driven OLFB. The associated CO2 emissions predominantly originate from the glycolic acid synthesis process (Supplementary Fig.50), which is expected to be reduced through synthesis optimization in the future.

Discussion

In summary, we reported an OLFB system capable of discharging/charging and chemical manufacturing (chemicals and H2) simultaneously in a single device. We demonstrate that 13 examples of organic compounds are applicable in OLFB, reaching a maximum OCV of 2.2 V and a power density of 238 mW cm–2, which is higher than advanced RFBs. Taking VO2⁺/VO2⁺ redox pairing with EG oxidation to glycolic acid (during discharging) and HER (during charging) as an example, the OLFB achieves >100% VE and EE at 20 mA cm−2, while H2 and glycolic acid were produced with >96% FE across a current density range of 20 to 150 mA cm−2. The OLFB also maintained a high cycling stability that sustained CE and EE (decay rate below 0.013% per cycle) in 200 cycles. We also established a scale-up OLFB system (total electrode area: 200 cm2) and powered it by solar, by which 532.2 g of glycolic acid with 97.2% purity and 272.9 L of H2 were yielded during discharging/charging process over a 10-day period operation. The TEA and LCA reveal that the renewable electricity-driven OLFB system demonstrates an LCOE of –0.26 $/kWh (annual revenue is $1.76 million) along with positive environmental impacts. This work demonstrates a paradigm transformation from conventional flow battery that solely store and release electricity to an economically more competitive flow battery that co-sell chemicals and H2. As energy storage and chemical manufacturing are not merely co-located but are electrochemically coupled within a single device, this work establishes a broadly applicable platform for the construction of cost-effective RFBs, offering significant potential for advancing large-capacity, long-duration energy storage technologies.

To guide the extension and practical implementation of the OLFB systems, the selection of electrochemical reactions should follow several key principles. First, to maximize energy efficiency, organic oxidation reactions with low thermodynamic potentials are preferred for the discharging half-cycle to enlarge the discharge cell voltage, while reductions with high potentials are favored for charging to minimize the charging voltage. Second, to improve operational efficiency and application potential, reactions with fast kinetics—particularly small-molecule substrates that enable efficient mass transport—should be prioritized to improve reaction rates and elevate power density. Third, for practical scalability, bulk commodity chemicals should serve as feedstocks to align with the massive capacity demands of grid-scale energy storage. These principles provide a rational framework for selecting organic reactions in OLFBs to simultaneously optimize electrochemical performance and enable the targeted synthesis of high-value chemicals.

Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%) and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36.5 wt%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Beijing Co., Ltd). Synthesis of the catalysts

For the synthesis of the CuxO catalyst, firstly, the Cu foam was pre-treated by ultrasonic treatment in 2 M HCl, ethanol and deionized water for 10 minutes, respectively. Then, the Cu foam was immersed in a 2.67 M NaOH and 0.13 M (NH4)2S2O8 aqueous solution for 30 min. The obtained catalyst was washed with deionized water and dried at 60 °C, and then calcined in the muffle furnace at 350 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min–1. Afterward, the sample was electrochemically reduced at –3 V in 1 M KOH for 30 min with Ni foam as counter electrode in a two-electrode configuration. SEM characterization reveals the nanowire morphology of the catalyst, while the XRD patterns of the catalyst correspond to the Cu2O and CuO phases (Supplementary Fig. 51).

For the synthesis of the AuPd catalyst, first, the nickel foam (NF) was pre-treated by ultrasonic treatment in 1 M HCl, ethanol and deionized water for 10 min, respectively. Then gold (III) chloride (0.06 g) and Palladium (II) chloride (0.013 g) were dissolved in 25 mL of deionized water, and 0.1 mL of HCl was added to promote the dissolution of the precious metals. The pre-treated NF (2 × 2 cm) was then immersed in the above solution and stirred slowly for 10 min. The obtained catalyst was washed with deionized water and dried in air for 12 h. SEM characterization reveals the nanoparticle morphology of the catalyst, while the XRD patterns of the catalyst correspond to the AuPd alloy phase (Supplementary Fig. 52).

For the synthesis of the Au catalyst, first, 0.085 g of gold (III) chloride was dissolved in 25 mL of deionized water, and 0.1 mL of HCl was added to facilitate the dissolution of the precious metal. Then, the pre-treated NF (2 × 2 cm) was immersed in the above solution and stirred slowly for 10 min. The obtained catalyst was washed with deionized water and dried in air for 12 h. SEM characterization reveals the nanoparticle morphology of the catalyst, while the XRD patterns of the catalyst correspond to the Au phase (Supplementary Fig. 53).

For the synthesis of the Ni(OH)2 catalyst, firstly, 0.727 g of nickel nitrate hexahydrate was dissolved in 25 mL of deionized water. Then, the pre-treated NF (2 × 2 cm) was immersed in the above solution and conducting electrodeposition with 300 s at a constant potential of –1 V vs. Ag/AgCl and employing a Pt foil as the counter electrode. The obtained catalyst was washed with deionized water and dried in air for 12 h43. SEM characterization reveals the nanoparticle morphology of the catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 54).

Before the test, AuPd and Au catalysts undergo activation treatment by cyclic voltammetry (CV) in a three-electrode configuration with a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) used as the reference electrode and the Pt foil used as the counter electrode. The CV process is conducting at a scan rate of 20 mV s–1 from –0.1 V to 0.6 V vs. SCE for 20 cycles in 3 M KOH. For the Ni(OH)2 catalyst, the CV activation is conducted from –1 to 1.6 V vs. SCE.

Structural and morphological characterization

XRD patterns were obtained using a Bruker D8 X-ray diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm), with 2θ ranging from 3° to 80° at a sweep speed of 10° min–1. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi S-4800) was used to characterize the morphologies of the prepared samples. The surface elemental analysis of the materials was performed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer using Al Kα radiation as the X-ray source for excitation. The metal contents were accurately quantified using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) on an Agilent ICPOES730 instrument. The absorption intensity at a wavelength of 360 nm for VO2+/VO2+ was measured on a PERSEE TU-1950 UV-vis spectrophotometer to quantify the state of charge (SOC) of posolytes. All the posolytes before and after reaction were diluted to the appropriate concentration to match the range of calibration curves.

Separation and purification of glycolic acid

Electrodialysis was carried out using a two-compartment bipolar membrane setup, leveraging the weak acidity of glycolic acid (Supplementary Fig. 38). Acidification occurred in the acid compartment, while deionized water in the base compartment enabled KOH recovery. The process was continued until the pH in the acid compartment dropped below 3, yielding a solution containing oxalic acid, formic acid, and glycolic acid. To ensure complete removal of oxalic acid, an equimolar amount of CaCO3 relative to the initial ethylene glycol substrate was added. Rectification was then performed in two stages: formic acid was first removed under mild heating due to its low boiling point, followed by high-temperature distillation to separate glycolic acid from ethylene glycol. Glycolic acid was collected as the distillate at the top of the column.

Life-cycle assessment

A cradle-to-gate LCA was performed for the VRFB and OLFB systems following the ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 standards. The study evaluates environmental impacts across their entire life cycles and identifies critical process hotspots, with a functional unit defined as 1 kWh of discharged electricity. The modeling is based on manufacturer data adapted for the ZHsystem-0.33 MW and ZHsystem−1 MW commercial flow battery systems. The characterized battery systems feature a net power output of 0.33 MW and 1 MW, respectively, with a net capacity of 5.28 and 16 MWh.

The LCA model was constructed using background data from the Ecoinvent 3.9.1 database. Regarding geographic representativeness, the modeling preferentially employed global (GLO) or “Rest of World” (ROW) average datasets; where such data were unavailable, the most representative region-specific data were selected as substitutes. All material flows were estimated in accordance with the law of mass conservation. A detailed compilation of life cycle inventory data sources is provided in Supplementary Table 14. The life cycle inventory for the preparation of the VOSO4 electrolyte was derived from previous studies44. The analysis focuses on the impacts associated with product synthesis and the charge–discharge processes. A complete overview of the inputs and outputs is illustrated in Fig. 6a.

The TRACI 2.1 impact assessment method was employed to quantitatively evaluate resource consumption, energy use, and pollutant emissions across three stages: the preparation of VOSO₄ solution, the charging process of the flow battery coupled with hydrogen production via water electrolysis, and the discharging process coupled with the electrocatalytic oxidation of ethylene glycol to glycolic acid. TRACI 2.1 encompasses three areas—human health, ecosystem quality, and resource scarcity—and assesses ten impact categories. SimaPro software was used to quantify these environmental indicators, including ozone depletion, global warming, air smog, acidification potential, eutrophication potential, human carcinogenic toxicity, human non-carcinogenic toxicity, respiratory effects, ecotoxicity, and fossil fuel depletion.
