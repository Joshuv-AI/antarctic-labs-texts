The translation paradox: how full translation increases cognitive load and impairs medical vocabulary retention
Authors: Yanru Shen, Fang Li, Qiuyan Zhang, Yuan Zhang, Yaoyao Ren, Wei Liu, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReading English biomedical literature is a core competency for medical students, yet many rely heavily on full-text translation tools. Although translation appears to reduce language barriers, its impact on cognitive load and durable learning remains unclear. This study compared three reading modalities to evaluate their effects on cognitive load, immediate comprehension, and delayed vocabulary retention in medical students.MethodsIn this three-arm randomized controlled trial, 160 Chinese medical students were randomized to one of three reading conditions: Original Text Group (OTG), AI-assisted Bilingual Group (ABG), or Full Translation Group (FTG). Immediate post-reading outcomes included intrinsic, extraneous, and germane cognitive load, as well as comprehension performance. One-week delayed productive vocabulary retention was assessed using a prespecified scoring rubric. Group differences were analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted for academic year and CET-6 level.ResultsA total of 148 participants completed the immediate post-reading assessment, and 148 completed the one-week follow-up according to the available dataset. Compared with the OTG and ABG, the FTG showed significantly higher extraneous cognitive load (both p < 0.001). Immediate comprehension scores were high across groups and did not differ significantly (p = 0.157). Delayed productive vocabulary retention differed significantly among groups, with the highest mean score in the OTG (6.38 ± 2.13), followed by the ABG (5.58 ± 1.89) and FTG (4.90 ± 2.00) (overall p < 0.001). Post-hoc analyses showed that the OTG outperformed both the ABG (p = 0.011) and FTG (p < 0.001).ConclusionsAmong Chinese medical students reading an English biomedical article, full translation was associated with higher perceived extraneous cognitive load and weaker one-week retention of methodological English vocabulary than direct reading of the original English text. A bilingual parallel-text format showed intermediate performance. These findings suggest that translation may be more appropriately used as a selective scaffold rather than a default substitute when the educational goal includes durable acquisition of English medical terminology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09472-5.Keywords: Medical education, Cognitive load theory, Vocabulary retention, Translation, English for specific purposes, Randomized controlled trial

Introduction

In contemporary medical education, the ability to access, interpret, and critically appraise English-language biomedical literature has become a core competency for medical students and future physicians. The goals of reading professional medical journals are multi-faceted: clinicians and students must read to extract critical evidence, apply new knowledge to clinical decision-making, and simultaneously socialize into the professional language by mastering specialized English terminology. For non-native English-speaking (NNES) medical students, reading primary research articles often entails a dual challenge: the inherent conceptual complexity of biomedical evidence and the linguistic demands of specialized scientific English. As a result, students may struggle not only to comprehend study findings, but also to convert reading into durable learning, particularly when encountering unfamiliar terminology, methodological detail, and discipline-specific discourse structures [1–3]. Unlike alphabetic first-language (L1) and second language (L2) pairs, the transfer from logographic Chinese to alphabetic English requires a more complex mapping of phonological and orthographic representations, which may increase the baseline cognitive demand for Chinese medical students during literature processing.

Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) provides a useful framework for understanding this challenge. Cognitive load refers to the total mental effort exerted in working memory, comprising three distinct components: (1) Intrinsic load, which is determined by the inherent complexity of the medical content itself; (2) Extraneous load, which is generated by the format of information presentation (e.g., the language barrier for non-native English-speaking students); (3) Germane load, which represents the beneficial mental effort directed toward processing information and constructing durable schemas. According to CLT, instructional effectiveness is maximized when extraneous load is minimized, thereby freeing up limited working-memory resources. This allows students to better manage intrinsic complexity and invest more effort in germane processing, ultimately leading to superior knowledge encoding and retention [4, 5]. In practical terms, excessive extraneous load may occupy working memory without contributing to learning, whereas germane load supports the integration and organization of knowledge. The balance among these components determines whether a given instructional format facilitates or hinders learning. For NNES medical students, language itself may constitute a major source of extraneous load during literature reading [6, 7]. Recent advances in neural machine translation (NMT) have therefore offered an appealing solution by rapidly converting English articles into fluent first-language text [8]. In practice, translation tools are now widely used by Chinese medical students when reading English-language journals, suggesting that AI-assisted translation has become an increasingly common intermediary in medical knowledge acquisition [9]. CLT has been widely applied across domains including L2 learning, multimedia learning, and science education, where instructional format has been shown to influence cognitive efficiency and learning outcomes.

Yet the educational implications of this shift remain uncertain. Although full-text translation may improve immediate accessibility, it may also reduce direct engagement with the source language. This pattern may promote what has been described as linguistic disengagement, which is a learning condition in which students grasp conceptual meaning in their first language without forming robust representations of the original English terminology [10]. This is consistent with the concept of language-dependent learning, which suggests that memory retrieval is most effective when the linguistic context at encoding matches the context at retrieval [11].Such disengagement may be particularly problematic in medicine, where professional competence depends not only on conceptual understanding but also on accurate recognition, retention, and later use of technical vocabulary in English-dominant academic and clinical contexts.

From the perspective of the desirable difficulties (DD) framework, some degree of effortful language processing may in fact be beneficial for long-term learning [12, 13]. While reading in a second language can increase short-term difficulty, that difficulty may deepen encoding of terminology and strengthen retention of discipline-specific language. From a pedagogical perspective, deep learning requires selective attention to key linguistic cues and sustained cognitive engagement with the source text. However, these essential processes may be bypassed when students rely on full-text translations, which offer a path of least resistance to conceptual meaning. The inverse relationship between immediate ease and long-term retention creates a pedagogical tension: reducing linguistic burden through AI translation may facilitate immediate comprehension, but excessive reduction of processing effort may come at the cost of weaker vocabulary acquisition and less durable learning.

A bilingual parallel-text format may represent a more balanced alternative, as it preserves exposure to L2 input while providing L1-based scaffolding for comprehension. Research on glossing has consistently shown that L1-based support can facilitate both reading comprehension and vocabulary learning, with meta-analytic evidence suggesting advantages of L1 glosses over L2-only support in certain contexts [14, 15]. Incidental vocabulary acquisition has been shown to occur as a by-product of meaning-focused reading [16], and the depth of comprehension plays a critical role in determining vocabulary learning outcomes. In parallel, recent studies have demonstrated that AI-powered machine translation can substantially enhance learners’ access to meaning during reading, although it may reduce opportunities for deeper lexical processing [17]. Therefore, providing immediate L1 support without fully replacing L2 input may facilitate both comprehension and incidental vocabulary development.

Despite the rapid adoption of AI-assisted translation in medical education, empirical evidence on its educational impact remains limited. Existing discussions have largely focused on the availability, convenience, or perceived usefulness of translation tools [18, 19]. While students often perceive AI translation as a highly effective scaffold for reducing language barriers and academic anxiety, such reliance often prioritizes immediate task completion over deep cognitive engagement. Empirical studies in second-language reading suggest that although bilingual support can facilitate rapid comprehension, it may simultaneously reduce engagement with target-language forms, which is a phenomenon where learners follow a ‘path of least resistance’ by prioritizing L1input [20, 21].

However, rigorous experimental evidence directly comparing different reading formats (English-only, bilingual, and full translation) in terms of both objective cognitive load and long-term learning outcomes remains scarce, particularly within high-stakes, domain-specific contexts like medical education. Most existing literature focuses on general English as a Second Language (ESL) or Academic Purposes (EAP), leaving a critical gap in our understanding of how these automated tools affect the mastery of specialized clinical terminology. Furthermore, while Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) provides a robust framework for understanding immediate information processing, there is a lack of evidence integrating the DD framework to explain the potential decoupling between immediate reading ease and durable memory retention in a medical context. Specifically, it remains unclear whether full translation, bilingual presentation, and English-only reading differentially affect the immediate comprehension and delayed retention of professional medical vocabulary among NNES medical students. This study addresses this gap by employing a randomized controlled trial to evaluate the cognitive and pedagogical consequences of these increasingly ubiquitous translation modalities.

While reading medical literature is often categorized as a way of incidental vocabulary learning, for medical students, the mastery of professional terms is an intentional terminology acquisition process driven by career requirements. Therefore, drawing on the methodology of incidental learning trials and replicating the rigorous structure of professional reading assessments, this study aims to position itself within this limited body of literature by evaluating the educational “cost” of translation convenience. The selected article, a non-inferiority trial by Li et al. [22], was chosen because it combines clinically familiar content with methodologically demanding elements including non-inferiority margins, intention-to-treat analysis, and evidence interpretation, which are highly relevant to evidence-based medicine training yet often challenging for learners. We compared three reading conditions: English-only, bilingual parallel text, and full Chinese translation. Outcomes included dimensions of cognitive load (intrinsic, extraneous, and germane), immediate reading comprehension, and delayed recall of key professional vocabulary. From the perspective of DD, while full-text translation facilitates immediate comprehension, it may inadvertently lead to linguistic disengagement. In professional medical training, this ease of access might conflict with the student’s goal of terminology mastery, potentially increasing extraneous cognitive load due to a perceived gap in professional skill acquisition. Based on this framework, we anticipated that while Intrinsic Cognitive Load (ICL) would remain consistent across groups due to identical text complexity, the Extraneous Cognitive Load (ECL) would be significantly higher in the FTG. Conversely, the Germane Cognitive Load (GCL) was expected to be highest in the OTG, reflecting more intensive schema construction during active reading.

We formulated the following hypotheses: (1) The full-text translation group (FTG) would exhibit lower or comparable immediate comprehension but higher extraneous cognitive load compared with the Original Text Group (OTG); (2) The OTG would demonstrate superior delayed vocabulary retention compared with both the AI-assisted Bilingual Group (ABG) and FTG; (3) The ABG would show intermediate performance between OTG and FTG across cognitive load and vocabulary outcomes. The study addressed the following research questions: (1) How do different reading modalities affect cognitive load components? (2) Do reading modalities differentially influence immediate comprehension? (3) How do they affect delayed vocabulary retention?

Methods

Study design

This was a three-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio, conducted at Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, an academic medical center in Wuhan, China, in March 2026. The protocol was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07460258) before participant enrollment in March 4, 2026. Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhongnan Hospital, Wuhan University, China (Approval No. 2026013 K). All participants provided written informed consent. The study is reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement. In educational trials, standard reading practice is often treated as the control condition. In this study, the OTG served as the control group (standard practice), while ABG and FTG served as experimental intervention groups.

Participants

Participants were recruited through campus-wide announcements, departmental postings, and communication with medical English curriculum coordinators. All participants had received formal English language instruction as part of the Chinese national curriculum throughout their primary and secondary education (typically commencing at age 10–12), in addition to university-level English requirements. English proficiency was operationalized via standardized test performance rather than self-reported years of study, given the relative homogeneity of English education trajectories in the Chinese academic system. Participants were instructed to read for both comprehension and terminology acquisition as a post-test would follow. No note-taking was permitted, and although the original BMJ article contained multimedia links, participants only accessed the text and static figures on the study platform. All participants completed the task within the 30-minute limit. Eligible participants were required to meet all of the following criteria: (1) full-time enrollment in a clinical medicine or health sciences program (undergraduate years 3–5 or postgraduate level); (2) Mandarin Chinese as the primary language; (3) completion of at least one semester of standardized medical terminology instruction; and (4) a passing score (i.e. 425) on the College English Test Band 4 (CET-4) or a recognized equivalent Exclusion criteria were: (1) cumulative residence in an English-speaking country for more than one month; (2) enrollment in an English-medium degree program; (3) uncorrected visual impairment that could interfere with on-screen reading; and (4) prior formal instruction in non-inferiority trial methodology. Participants with formal prior training in non-inferiority design were excluded; however, variation in informal familiarity may still exist (Fig. 1).

Information on the total duration of English learning was not formally collected, as English education is standardized across participants in the Chinese medical curriculum. However, individual variation cannot be excluded.

To minimize the impact of prior knowledge, we excluded students who had previously participated in research projects involving non-inferiority trials or specialized biostatistics training. While a pre-test was avoided to prevent the testing effect (priming), our focus was specifically on intentional terminology acquisition, which is the ability to map new English labels onto contextually provided concepts, rather than the mastery of the underlying mathematical principles.

Sample size calculation

The target sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9. Based on preliminary pilot data (n = 30) and established cognitive load research in medical education, we estimated a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25) [23] for the primary outcome. A power analysis for an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with one covariate and three experimental groups indicated that a minimum of 128 participants was necessary to achieve 80% power at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Accounting for a projected 20% attrition rate, the final enrollment target was set at 160 participants.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Eligible participants were stratified by baseline English proficiency, defined as a CET-6 score ≥ 520 versus < 520. A cutoff score of 520 was used to distinguish relatively higher versus lower English proficiency, based on commonly used benchmarks in prior educational studies. CET-4 and CET-6 are sequential national English proficiency tests, with CET-6 representing a more advanced level [24]. While passing CET-4 is required for inclusion, not all participants had completed CET-6. Within each stratum, subjects were assigned to one of three reading modalities using a computer-generated block randomization sequence (block size of 6) developed by an independent statistician. Within each proficiency stratum (High vs. Low), subjects were randomized to ensure balance. This ensured that each reading group contained a balanced proportion of students with CET-6 scores ≥ 520 and < 520. Participants without a CET-6 score (n = 9) were categorized into the < 520 stratum. Allocation concealment was ensured through a centralized, password-protected web-based randomization system. After completion of baseline assessments, the study coordinator entered the participant identification number and stratification information into the system, which then automatically revealed the assignment. The randomization sequence was inaccessible to investigators involved in recruitment, enrolment, and outcome assessment.

Blinding

Because of the visible differences between reading formats, blinding of participants and session administrators was not feasible. However, to reduce detection bias, assessors responsible for scoring the comprehension and delayed vocabulary tests were blinded to group allocation throughout scoring and data analysis.

Reading conditions

OTG

Participants read the English-language PDF of the target article on a desktop monitor. Translation browser extensions were disabled on all study computers, and an investigator was present throughout each session to ensure compliance. No dictionaries or reference materials were permitted.

ABG

Participants read the article in a custom bilingual interface in which the original English text appeared in the left column and a Simplified Mandarin Chinese translation appeared in the right column, with synchronized scrolling. The translation was produced using GPT-4 and reviewed independently for medical accuracy by a bilingual medical ophthalmologist. Participants could read either or both columns freely.

FTG

Participants read only the verified Chinese translation, presented in a standard single-column format without access to the English source text. The Simplified Mandarin Chinese translation was generated using GPT-4 and subsequently reviewed and corrected by a bilingual ophthalmologist to ensure accuracy and consistency.

The three-arm design was employed to isolate the effects of different levels of linguistic scaffolding. The OTG served as the active control group representing traditional academic reading, while the FTG provided a baseline for pure conceptual access without linguistic exposure. This structure allowed for a comparative analysis of how varying degrees of translation reliance interfere with the DD required for lexical encoding.

The visual presentation format was kept consistent across groups, and the primary difference between OTG and FTG was the language of the text. All sessions took place in a standardized computer laboratory setting. Participants in OTG and FTG groups were allocated 30 min for the reading task and were instructed to engage with the material as thoroughly as possible within the timeframe. All participants in both groups completed the reading task within the allocated 30-minute period. To monitor protocol adherence, screen recordings were obtained from a random 10% subset of participants in each group; no violations were observed.

Reading material

The stimulus text was a peer-reviewed original research article published in The BMJ in 2024: “Effect of laughter exercise versus 0.1% sodium hyaluronic acid on ocular surface discomfort in dry eye disease: non-inferiority randomized controlled trial” (Li et al., 2024) [22]. This article was selected because it describes a common and clinically recognizable condition (dry eye disease), employs a methodologically sophisticated design (non-inferiority trial with a predefined margin, and both per-protocol and intention-to-treat analyses), contains a high density of standardized research terminology relevant to evidence-based practice. To ensure that prior exposure did not confound the results, we implemented a two-step control: (1) Pre-screening: during recruitment, students were explicitly asked if they had encountered the selected study, and those with prior exposure were excluded; (2) Content Selection: the article was chosen for its specific clinical focus (laughter therapy), which is not covered in the standard medical curriculum or textbooks. Thus, participants across all groups began the task with negligible baseline knowledge of the material.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was cognitive load, assessed immediately following the reading session using a six-item scale adapted from Leppink et al. [25]. This instrument utilized an 11-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 10 (“completely”), to evaluate three distinct dimensions: ICL, which measured the perceived complexity of the study’s non-inferiority design; ECL, which assessed how much the language and format hindered understanding; and GCL, which measured the extent to which the task helped students build new knowledge of research terminology [26]. In our sample, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.79 (ICL), 0.82 (ECL), and 0.76 (GCL). Two independent blinded assessors scored the responses, and the inter-rater reliability for the vocabulary test was high (Cohen’s kappa = 0.89). Each subscale score was calculated as the mean of its two constituent items. Two representative items were selected for each dimension to reduce participant burden while maintaining construct coverage.

Secondary outcomes included an immediate comprehension test and a delayed vocabulary production test. To address the reliability of the comprehension test, we calculated the Kuder-Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20). The KR-20 coefficient was 0.45. We acknowledge that this value is modest, which is primarily attributed to the ceiling effect (high mean scores) observed in the participant population, particularly in the OTG group (e.g., 94–96% correct on factual recall). Despite the restricted variance, the test demonstrated discriminant validity, as significant differences were observed in higher-order reasoning items (e.g., Q17).

The six items were allocated to assess three dimensions including ICL (perceived complexity of content), Extraneous Cognitive Load (ECL) (perceived difficulty of presentation/format), and GCL (perceived effort for schema construction). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) confirmed the three-factor structure of the scale in our dataset. Q1-Q14 included demographic and baseline information and were not part of the comprehension assessment. The comprehension assessment consisted of four items (Q15-Q18) that evaluated both factual recall of the primary outcome and higher-order reasoning regarding non-inferiority margins and intention-to-treat analysis [27]. Each item was scored dichotomously, with a maximum possible score of 4 [28]. Sub-analysis revealed no significant group difference for the factual item (Q15, p = 0.120). For higher-order reasoning items (Q16-Q18), a significant difference was only observed for Q17 (p = 0.031).

Delayed productive vocabulary retention was assessed one week later using an unannounced test. Participants were presented with five methodological terms in Simplified Mandarin Chinese and asked to produce the corresponding English expressions: non-inferiority margin; intention-to-treat analysis; primary outcome; confidence interval; randomized controlled trial. These five terms were selected because they were central, repeated, and methodologically essential in the target article. Responses were scored using a prespecified 3-point rubric: 0 = incorrect or blank; 1 = partially correct response, accepted abbreviation, or predefined acceptable synonym; 2 = fully correct target term with acceptable minor spelling variation. The total score ranged from 0 to 10.

Exploratory outcomes included self-rated comprehension (5-point scale) and reading duration.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 and R version 4.3.1, with a two-sided significance level set at p < 0.05. Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation), and normality was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The primary analysis employed ANCOVA to compare cognitive load subscales among the three groups, with CET-6 score and academic year as covariates. All ANCOVA assumptions, including normality, homogeneity of variance, and homogeneity of regression slopes, were verified and satisfied before result interpretation. Bonferroni correction was used for post-hoc pairwise comparisons, and effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared (ηₚ²). Cohen’s d was calculated with pooled standard deviation for between-group comparisons. Secondary outcomes including comprehension and vocabulary scores were analyzed using the identical ANCOVA model. Two-way ANOVA was performed to examine potential interactions between reading modality and English proficiency. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess linear associations among reading time, self-rated comprehension, and objective test scores. Missing data were minimal (under 10%); therefore, complete-case analysis was performed without multiple imputation. Because 12 randomized participants did not complete the one-week follow-up, outcome analyses were performed on participants with available data (complete-case analysis). Baseline characteristics of completers and non-completers were compared to assess the likelihood of attrition bias. Comprehension was analyzed at the item level because each question targeted distinct methodological concepts (e.g., NI design vs. ITT analysis).

The inclusion of academic year and CET-6 scores as covariates was both theory-driven and empirically justified. Since the academic year showed a significant baseline imbalance (p = 0.005) and English proficiency (CET-6) is an established determinant of cognitive load during L2 reading, controlling for these factors was essential. This approach ensures that the observed differences in extraneous load and vocabulary retention are attributable to the reading modalities themselves, rather than pre-existing differences in the participants’ academic maturity or linguistic baseline.

Ethical considerations

Signed informed consent was obtained from all participants following a detailed verbal and written explanation of study procedures. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed that withdrawal from the study at any time would carry no academic or administrative consequences. All study data were de-identified immediately after collection and stored on password-protected, encrypted institutional servers accessible only to authorized members of the research team. The study complied with applicable institutional requirements for data protection and research ethics.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 160 medical students were randomized for the study. Of these, 148 (92.5%) completed both the immediate reading session and the one-week follow-up assessment, and were included in the primary analysis: OTG (n = 48), ABG (n = 48), and FTG (n = 52). Twelve participants were excluded due to loss to follow-up or technical issues during the survey. Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, frequency of translation tool use, and interest in research methodology, were well-balanced across the three groups (all p > 0.05). While CET-6 proficiency levels were statistically comparable (χ2 = 11.26, p = 0.187), a significant imbalance was noted in academic year distribution (χ2 = 25.36, p = 0.005), with the OTG having a higher proportion of senior graduate students and the FTG having more second-year postgraduate students. To account for this, academic year was included as a covariate in all subsequent ANCOVA models (Table 1).

Primary outcomes: cognitive load

Cognitive load results are shown in Table 2. Contrary to our primary hypothesis, the FTG did not exhibit the lowest extraneous cognitive load; rather, it exhibited the highest. ANCOVA revealed a significant main effect of reading condition on ECL (F (2,143) = 18.7, p < 0.001, =0.21). Post-hoc comparisons confirmed that the FTG (mean 5.23, SD 1.65) had significantly higher ECL than both the OTG (mean 3.92, SD 1.71; p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.79) and the ABG (mean 4.06, SD 1.87; p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.65), while the OTG and ABG were not significantly different from each other (p = 0.711). This pattern held after adjustment for CET-6 level and academic year.

Intrinsic cognitive load was also modestly but significantly higher in the FTG (mean 4.11, SD 1.30) than in the OTG (mean 3.35, SD 1.54; p = 0.010), with the ABG occupying an intermediate position (mean 3.60, SD 1.37; ABG vs. FTG p = 0.064). The overall ANCOVA for ICL was significant (F (2,143) = 4.2, p = 0.027, = 0.06) (Table 2).

Germane cognitive load did not differ significantly across conditions (OTG 5.51 ± 2.15, ABG 5.16 ± 2.16, FTG 4.86 ± 2.12; F (2,143) = 1.1, p = 0.325, = 0.02). All three groups reported broadly comparable levels of active cognitive engagement with the material.

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction revealed specific group differences. For ICL, the FTG scored significantly higher than the OTG (p = 0.010), with a trend toward significance when compared to the ABG (p = 0.064). Regarding ECL, the FTG demonstrated significantly higher load compared to both the OTG (p < 0.001) and the ABG (p = 0.001). In contrast, no significant differences were observed among the three groups for GCL (all p > 0.05).

Secondary outcomes: immediate comprehension

Immediate comprehension scores were high across all groups and showed no significant overall difference (OTG: 3.69 ± 0.74; ABG: 3.56 ± 0.79; FTG: 3.83 ± 0.47 out of 4; p = 0.157). Accuracy on factual recall (Q15) was above 89% in all groups. However, a significant difference was observed for item Q17, which assessed the rationale for the non-inferiority design (p = 0.031); post-hoc analysis indicated that the ABG (81.2% correct) performed significantly worse than both the OTG (95.8%, p = 0.024) and the FTG (96.2%, p = 0.023). Performance on items regarding confidence interval interpretation (Q16) and the concordance of analytical approaches (Q18) remained statistically comparable across the groups (Table 3).

Secondary outcomes: delayed vocabulary production

Results from the unannounced one-week follow-up demonstrated a clear advantage for direct engagement with the original English text. Participants completed an unannounced delayed vocabulary production task one week after the reading session. Each of the five target terms was scored using a prespecified 3-point rubric (0 = incorrect/blank, 1 = partially correct or standard abbreviation, 2 = fully correct term with acceptable minor spelling variation). For example, “RCT” or “randomized control trial” received 1 point. For each item, both the proportion receiving any credit (≥ 1 point) and full credit (2 points) are reported. Total score range: 0–10.

At the 1-week follow-up, delayed productive retention of methodological terminology differed across groups. Based on the prespecified 3-point scoring rubric, the OTG achieved the highest total delayed vocabulary production score (6.38 ± 2.13), followed by the ABG (5.58 ± 1.89) and the FTG (4.90 ± 2.00). After adjustment for academic year and CET-6 level, the overall group difference was significant (ANCOVA, p < 0.001). Post-hoc comparisons showed that the OTG scored significantly higher than both the ABG (p = 0.011) and the FTG (p < 0.001), whereas the difference between the ABG and FTG was not statistically significant (p = 0.118).

This advantage was broadly reflected at the term level. For the complex phrase non-inferiority margin, the OTG achieved 81.3% any-credit and 72.9% full-credit performance, compared with 62.5% / 62.5% in the ABG and 57.7% / 51.9% in the FTG. For intention-to-treat analysis, performance was 77.1% / 68.8% in the OTG, 79.2% / 72.9% in the ABG, and 63.5% / 53.8% in the FTG. For primary outcome (measure), the corresponding values were 81.3% / 70.8%, 62.5% / 58.3%, and 61.5% / 53.8%, respectively. For randomized controlled trial (RCT), retention remained relatively high across groups but was still highest in the OTG (79.2% / 77.1%), compared with the ABG (66.7% / 64.6%) and FTG (67.3% / 63.5%). The term confidence interval was the most difficult item overall, with full-credit rates of only 16.7% in the OTG, 2.1% in the ABG, and 0.0% in the FTG (Table 4).

Exploratory outcomes: reading time and self-rated comprehension

Subjective comprehension ratings (1–5 scale) were: OTG 3.06 (0.80), ABG 3.19 (0.70), and FTG 3.06 (0.69) (p = 0.557). Mean reading times (minutes) were: OTG 25.9 (4.3), ABG 25.4 (4.1), and FTG 25.0 (4.5). One-way ANOVA revealed no significant differences in reading duration among the three conditions (p = 0.524). Pearson correlation showed no significant linear association between reading duration and delayed vocabulary production (r = 0.021, p = 0.792). No linear or non‑linear association was observed between reading time and long‑term terminology retention. Group-stratified correlation analyses also showed no significant association between reading duration and vocabulary retention in the OTG (r = 0.08), ABG (r = 0.03), or FTG (r = -0.05).

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial compared three approaches to engaging with English biomedical literature: original English text (OTG), a bilingual parallel format (ABG), and a full Chinese translation (FTG). Our central finding challenges the intuitive facilitation hypothesis of translation: full translation did not reduce cognitive burden. Paradoxically, the FTG reported the highest extraneous load (format-related difficulty) and intrinsic load (content-related complexity) While immediate comprehension was comparable across groups, a clear performance gradient emerged for long-term vocabulary retention (OTG > ABG > FTG). These results suggest that while translation may immediate access to conceptual meaning with less linguistic effort, it introduces significant cognitive friction, which is the resistance encountered when a human interface (like translation) interferes with the direct mental processing of source information [29], and undermines the durable acquisition of professional language. Our findings are further elucidated by the DD framework. Unlike the FTG, where the ‘ease’ of translation may create an illusion of mastery, the original English text (OTG) introduces a desirable struggle. This increased effort during initial encoding leads to deeper semantic processing and stronger neural traces, which, as Bjork and Bjork (2020) proposed, enhances long-term retention even when immediate performance seems slower [30]. Taken together, these findings do not support a universally optimal reading modality, but rather a goal-dependent framework.

The ECL in the FTG group is perhaps the most counter-intuitive finding of this trial. From the lens of Cognitive Load Theory, this pattern is consistent with, but does not directly establish, a redundancy-like effect within the framework of cognitive load theory [29]. When medical students who already possess some familiarity with English medical terminology, are presented with a full Chinese translation, their working memory is forced to reconcile two competing mental representations. This may have introduced an additional interpretive step not directly aligned with the target learning goal. For instance, understanding a “non-inferiority margin” in its translated form may actually be harder because it lacks the standardized orthographic cues of the original English, thereby increasing the cognitive tax on the learner [10]. This mechanism may not apply to learners with lower English proficiency or minimal prior exposure, for whom full translation may instead reduce cognitive burden. The observed pattern should therefore be interpreted as conditional on learner characteristics.

Furthermore, the higher intrinsic load in the FTG suggests that translation may inadvertently obscure the logical transparency of the research design. In the OTG, the consistency of standardized English terminology provides a stable framework for understanding the study’s non-inferiority logic. In contrast, even high-quality AI-mediated translations may introduce subtle semantic shifts that make the internal complexity of the methodology feel more daunting to the learner.

The observed ceiling effect in immediate comprehension masks a fundamental difference in how information was encoded. While all groups grasped the core findings of the laughter exercise study [22], the mechanisms of understanding differed. The OTG’s superior performance in the one-week delayed vocabulary test (mean 6.38 versus 5.58 in the ABG and 4.90 in the FTG) may be partially consistent with the principle of DD, although a more direct explanation is differential exposure to English lexical forms across groups [12]. The inherent challenge of decoding the original English text, though perceived as difficult in the moment, may trigger deeper lexical encoding and create more robust memory traces. By removing this linguistic struggle, the FTG group may experience a form of “Illusion of Competence,” in which the ease of immediate understanding is not matched by durable retention of the technical terms required for professional communication [13].

The delayed vocabulary findings are particularly instructive because they go beyond a simple “right versus wrong” outcome and reveal a graded pattern of productive recall. Using a prespecified 0-1-2 rubric, the OTG achieved the highest total delayed vocabulary production score, and this advantage remained significant after adjustment for academic year and CET-6 level. Post-hoc comparisons showed that the OTG significantly outperformed both the ABG (p = 0.011) and the FTG (p < 0.001), whereas the difference between the ABG and FTG did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.118). This suggests that exposure to the original English text conferred a measurable advantage in the durable production of methodological terminology, while the bilingual condition offered only partial protection against the erosion of lexical retention seen with full translation. The lack of performance advantage in the ABG group suggests a Linguistic Parasitism effect. When a L1 translation is presented alongside the original text, learners may exhibit a strong tendency to prioritize the L1 version to minimize cognitive exertion, a phenomenon consistent with the Principle of Least Effort. This leads to superficial processing of the English terminology, as the bilingual format inadvertently allows students to bypass the DD necessary for lexical encoding. While the logographic nature of Chinese may impose a unique cognitive demand during English medical reading, the observed ‘translation paradox’ aligns with broader cross-linguistic evidence (e.g., in French-English contexts) suggesting that L1-dependence can facilitate rapid comprehension at the expense of durable L2 acquisition. The superior performance of the OTG in vocabulary recall, despite the lack of formal L1 support, merits particular attention. This result can be explained by the DD framework and the mechanism of active contextual inferencing. In the absence of translation scaffolds, OTG participants were required to engage in deeper semantic processing to derive the meaning of specialized terms from the biomedical context. This effortful mapping between the English term and its inferred meaning strengthens the mental representation and improves later retrieval. Conversely, the immediate access to L1 translations in the ABG and FTG likely induced a sense of ease that led to linguistic disengagement, where students bypassed deep lexical encoding. In this sense, the Chinese prompts in the recall task served as retrieval cues for the semantic concepts that OTG students had independently and rigorously constructed during reading.

We must interpret the vocabulary task results with caution. It is unlikely that a single reading session allows for the full acquisition of complex statistical concepts. However, the significantly higher scores in the OTG indicate that direct engagement with the original text facilitates the first step of professional language socialization: the formation of a stable mental link between a technical term and its clinical application.

At the item level, the pattern was nuanced but broadly consistent with this interpretation. For terms such as non-inferiority margin, primary outcome (measure), and randomized controlled trial, the OTG generally showed the strongest full-credit performance. Notably, confidence interval was the most difficult term across all three groups, with full-credit rates of only 16.7% in the OTG, 2.1% in the ABG, and 0.0% in the FTG. This pattern suggests that some statistical concepts may be less likely to be acquired incidentally through article reading alone and may require more explicit instructional support. Some terms may be sufficiently concrete or familiar to benefit from contextual reinforcement, whereas more abstract statistical concepts may require explicit instructional support. In other words, the advantage of original-text exposure appears real, but it is not universally sufficient for all forms of methodological language learning.

The ABG warrants critical reflection regarding “Selective Attention.” Despite having access to the English text, their retention did not significantly exceed that of the FTG. This suggests a “Minimum Effort Strategy,” where learners instinctively default to the easier linguistic path (the Chinese column), thereby bypassing the semantic inference and lexical processing necessary for intentional terminology acquisition [31]. Without explicit pedagogical guidance, a bilingual scaffold can collapse into a de facto full translation. At the same time, the ABG’s numerically intermediate performance across several items suggests that bilingual presentation may still preserve some exposure benefit, particularly when used intentionally rather than passively. Importantly, participants in the FTG were not exposed to the original English lexical forms, and their lower performance on the vocabulary task may therefore reflect a basic exposure effect rather than a deeper cognitive mechanism alone. Moreover, vocabulary acquisition in the OTG occurred incidentally through reading, and it remains unclear whether this form of exposure represents an efficient strategy compared with explicit vocabulary instruction. Therefore, the observed advantage should be interpreted with caution and not be viewed solely as direct evidence of DD. As AI-driven translation (like GPT-4) becomes ubiquitous, medical educators must resist the urge to prioritize accessible content at the expense of deep learning. Reading duration was comparable across groups and showed no significant association with delayed vocabulary retention. This indicates that longer reading time alone did not enhance lexical retention, and that the observed vocabulary advantage in the original text group likely stemmed from deeper linguistic engagement rather than differences in time spent reading. The lack of a significant correlation between reading duration and vocabulary retention further supports this interpretation. Simply spending more time on the text did not improve long-term lexical memory. Instead, the quality of engagement with the original English terminology, rather than the quantity of reading time, appeared to drive better retention. This reinforces the value of active, language-focused reading over passive or time-extended processing. These exploratory measures serve as internal controls, confirming that the vocabulary advantage in the OTG was not a function of increased reading time or superior subjective confidence.

These findings point toward a practical educational trade-off. If the primary goal is rapid comprehension of a single article, for example, in a time-limited journal club or evidence-based medicine session, translation may appear attractive because it reduces the immediate linguistic burden. However, if the educational objective is long-term academic development, including the ability to recognize, retrieve, and eventually use methodological terminology in English, then exclusive reliance on translation may be counterproductive. In this context, original-text exposure appears to support more durable lexical learning, while bilingual materials may be best positioned as transitional scaffolds rather than endpoints.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, while we compared three reading modalities, the study lacked a no-treatment control group. This precludes an estimation of the absolute learning effect attributable to the reading session alone, as participants’ pre-existing knowledge of the target terms may have influenced their performance. Consequently, our results should be interpreted as reflecting the relative advantages of different reading modalities rather than absolute gains in vocabulary.

Second, the study relied on subjective measures and lacked objective process-tracing data. Without eye-tracking or gaze-transition analysis, we could not verify how participants in the ABG distributed their attention between the L1 and L2 texts. Some students might have prioritized the Chinese translation, effectively reducing their engagement with the English input and causing the ABG to functionally approximate the FTG. Furthermore, while the 30-minute duration was sufficient for completion, the qualitative nature of processing likely varied; the OTG may have engaged in intensive contextual inferencing, while the FTG and ABG potentially prioritized surface-level recognition. Future research should incorporate process-tracing methods to quantify these distinct cognitive pathways and ensure fidelity of engagement in bilingual contexts.

Third, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the scope of the material and assessment. We utilized a single, low-stakes article to ensure accessibility within the 30-minute session. However, for conceptually denser materials, such as advanced pharmacology or complex methodology, full translation might play a more facilitative role. Additionally, our assessment was limited to five methodological terms over a short follow-up interval. Future studies should examine whether these cognitive effects persist across diverse article types, longer retention intervals, and broader measures of academic reading competence.

In addition, while we excluded students with prior formal NI training, we did not conduct a pre-test of specific terminology to avoid priming effects. The vocabulary task thus reflects intentional terminology acquisition during the reading process rather than complete conceptual mastery of the underlying statistical methodologies.

Finally, our findings must be interpreted within a goal-dependent framework. While direct engagement with original English text appears superior for durable vocabulary acquisition and professional identity development, full translation may still serve as a pragmatic strategy when the primary objective is rapid content comprehension. We did not measure individual working memory capacity, which may also moderate how different students benefit from these modalities. Future research should explore these individual differences to better define when translation acts as a selective support versus a detrimental crutch.

Our findings support a more differentiated instructional approach. When immediate comprehension is the primary goal, such as in time-limited teaching sessions, translation may serve as a practical tool. In contrast, when long-term development of English medical terminology is intended, direct engagement with original texts appears more beneficial. A combined strategy, in which translation is used to support comprehension while vocabulary is reinforced through separate, intentional learning activities, may offer an efficient and pedagogically balanced alternative. Rather than endorsing a single modality, our results suggest that reading strategies should be aligned with specific learning objectives and learner characteristics.

In conclusion, our findings do not suggest that translation tools should be categorically discarded, but rather integrated into a goal-dependent framework. If the primary instructional objective is the rapid extraction of clinical information under time pressure, full translation remains a pragmatic utility. However, if the goal is the long-term mastery of professional nomenclature and the development of scholarly literacy, the DD of engaging with original English text is indispensable. Educators should encourage students to view translation as a temporary scaffolding rather than a permanent cognitive substitute.
