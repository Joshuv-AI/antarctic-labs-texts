The bright and dark sides of openness on quality of life: the role of emotions and self-efficacy beliefs within a GAMs, a network approach and mediation
Authors: Sonya Yakimova, Anne Congard, Daniela Jopp, Bruno Dauvier, Cornelia Pocnet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the relationship between personality and quality of life has been extensively studied, openness has received relatively little attention. This is despite its importance in the Big Five model and its ability to change over time. To close this gap, our study explored the link between openness and quality of life. Additionally, we examined whether this relationship is influenced by Dimensions of Openness to Emotion (DOE) and self-efficacy, as research suggests both may play significant roles in the personality–quality of life relationship.MethodA total of 419 participants in a community-based convenience sample of the French-speaking regions of Switzerland and of France aged 20–66 years completed the NEO-FFI-R inventory, the General Self-Efficacy Scale, the Dimensions of Openness to Emotion scale, and the WHOQOL-12 measuring quality of life. GAMs, Network and Mediation analysis were conducted with R software.ResultsOur analysis suggested that the different facets of DOE relate to quality of life in two contrasting ways, with openness influencing it both positively and negatively, depending on the specific facet: the somatic dimensions of DOE had a negative association, while the cognitive dimensions of DOE had a positive one.ConclusionsOpenness contributes to quality of life, especially for individuals who are moderately open to their bodily sensations and who use thoughtful and context-adjusted emotion regulation strategies.Keywords: Openness, Dimensions of openness to emotion (DOE), Self-efficacy beliefs, Quality of life, GAMs, Network analysis

Introduction

Skevington et al. [1] describe quality of life (QoL) as a broad concept influenced by many determinants, including physical and psychological health, degree of autonomy, social relationships, personal beliefs, and their connection to essential aspects of the environment. It corresponds to an individual’s subjective perception of his or her life, in the context of their culture and value system, and in relation to personal objectives, expectations, norms, and concerns.

Given that QoL is influenced by a variety of factors, research has also explored how a person’s traits, such as personality, contribute to these perceptions. Specifically, studies have highlighted the roles of emotional stability, extraversion, and conscientiousness in shaping one’s QoL [2–12]. The effects of openness and agreeableness have received less attention, as these two personality dimensions are only moderately or even poorly related to QoL. However, several studies have pointed out that there could be indirect effects, with certain variables influencing the link between these personality traits and QoL [13]. For example, Pocnet and colleagues [9] showed that emotion regulation and self-efficacy modulate the effects of personality on QoL. According to Eisenberg and Spinrad [14], being able to regulate one’s emotions is important for adapting to different situations, in order to fulfill the demands of cultural and social environments. In turn, feeling capable of addressing successfully a certain task has an impact on the feeling of accomplishment, and can create a virtuous circle. According to Bandura [15], experiences of success enhance self-efficacy which, in turn, increases motivation and performance, widening our interests and realm of activities. In the wake of this research, we sought to investigate the potential nonlinear effect of openness personality trait on QoL. We also wanted to investigate the extent to which Dimensions of Openness to Emotion (DOE) and self-efficacy impact QoL to address specific (somatic, cognitive, and emotional) dimensions of emotion regulation.

Openness and its relationship to QoL

The openness dimension of the Big Five personality model refers to curiosity, interest in novelty, creativity, attraction to new experiences, and openness to esthetics and knowledge. Although the link between openness and well-being is weak or moderate, Fredrickson’s [16, 17] broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions seems to support the role of creative responses to aversive stimuli as a factor in reducing negative emotional experience and promoting positive affect. In line with this, researchers found a positive link between curiosity and satisfaction with life [18–20]. Pereira‑Morales et al. [21] found even in their sample that openness was the best predictor of health‑related QoL along with emotional coping style and depressive and anxious symptoms. Fino and Sun [22] also detected that openness was positively associated with QoL in university students.

Theoretically, there are five pathways by which openness may contribute to QoL. First, individuals with high openness like to engage in reflection and intellectual involvement [23–25] and the pleasure associated with these behaviors could lead to higher QoL. Second, interest in reflection may foster the meta-cognition that make it easier to adapt to aversive situations and implement effective self-regulation processes. This is in line with findings showing that individuals with higher openness benefit more from the positive effects of psychological interventions compared to individuals with low openness scores [26]. For Digman [27] and DeYoung [28, 29], openness also is a factor that captures the plasticity and flexibility that help people to perceive ambiguous situations as opportunities rather than threats [30]. As such, it may contribute to positive emotional experiences. Third, openness allows individuals to enjoy entertainment and leisure activities (e.g., going to museums, movies, concerts) that can provide emotional and stimulating experiences and fuel positive exchanges with others [31]. Fourth, being more open to situations and other people may be associated with lower egocentrism, which may be useful for reducing vicious circles of negative thoughts and rumination [32]. Fifth and last, openness to change impacts the perception of life situations, by facilitating adjustment to transitions and influencing QoL [30].

Thus, although some studies found links between openness and QoL, and that there are various aspects that could be responsible for linking both constructs, the strength of the association between openness and QoL was found to only be weak or moderate in size. This may be related to the fact that openness encompasses very heterogenous components which leads to differential associations and a weaker overall link, as these components will not necessarily act in similar ways, resulting in positive and negative associations. Furthermore, openness may have nonlinear relations with the variables to be predicted. For example, a high level of openness might contribute to positive adaptation in various regards, including adjustment to life transitions and new demands, while a low level of openness might contribute to stability and emotional adaptation in everyday life. Similarly, the setting of new goals and their pursuit have also been found to result in greater life satisfaction [33] By contrary, a high level of daily routine associated with low openness may also contribute to QoL, insofar as it prevents that individuals are exposed to new events, potentially making their everyday lives easier through the avoidance of novelty and challenges. Thus, when routine serves as an environmental control strategy, it probably enhances QoL. In addition, while openness is associated with entertainment and distraction, it is also associated with substance dependence (alcohol, drugs, etc.) which is maladaptive for health [34]. Moreover, openness may be a variable, exerting an indirect influence on QoL [25, 35]. In the present study, we postulated that the relationship between openness and QoL is probably not direct, but modulated by emotion regulation and self-efficacy, as explained below.

However, while openness is a factor contributing to quality of life for various reasons, an excessively high level of openness could lead to a lower appreciation of quality of life. Indeed, a very low openness score may reflect behavioral rigidity and significant discomfort with even minor changes, thereby reducing quality of life [25]. Conversely, highly open individuals may quickly become bored with their activities and feel generally dissatisfied with an ordinary life that appears dull and mundane due to a lack of intellectual stimulation and limited opportunities for change [36].

Furthermore, openness has also been associated with problematic behaviors, such as substance dependence [34]. Excessive cognitive flexibility may complicate decision-making, as highly open individuals tend to consider a wide range of possibilities without setting clear boundaries [37]. These individuals, often imaginative and creative, may sometimes become lost in their ideas or reflections on life in general, running the risk of being carried away by their overflowing imagination [38].

This personality trait may also lead individuals to be critical and disappointed with the functioning of organizations, which can, in turn, affect their quality of life. Additionally, excessive emotional openness can be destabilizing, as analyzing the causes of one’s emotions in great detail may lead to difficulties in dissociating feelings, creating a significant emotional burden [39]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that an excessively high level of openness could be problematic, suggesting the existence of a non-linear relationship between openness and quality of life.

DOE and its links with personality and QoL

Personality traits are not isolated entities but interact with other psychological factors [40, 41]. Individuals with certain personality traits may be more inclined to engage in learning and intellectual exploration, potentially fostering emotional openness and adaptability [42]. DOE (Dimensions of Openness to Emotion) is an interesting construct in the study of the link between personality and QoL, as it plays a central role in the context of emotional well-being. DOE describe if people are interested in and curious about their emotional functioning and we can hypothesize that this kind of interest facilitates emotional processes and therefore improves the QoL. More specifically, DOE plays a key part in curiosity to oneself and emotional perception of the environment [43], allowing a better emotional adjustment [44]. DOE includes different interrelated processes connected to emotional perception that cannot be categorized as either abilities or deficits. Reicherts’ DOE model [43], which allows processes involved in emotional functioning to be represented, identifies five dimensions related to cognitive, social, somatic and interactional aspects.

In the DOE, the cognitive level is represented by the cognitive-conceptual representation of emotions (REPCOG) dimension, which refers to emotional concepts, schemas or scripts [45], including the possibility of verbalizing one’s emotion. This dimension focuses on the ability to detect different affective states, differentiate them from other sensations, be aware of the situations to which emotions are linked (evaluations, reasons, causal attributions), and possibly name them.

The social level is represented by the communication of emotions (COMEMO) dimension, which refers to openness at the interactional level, including the social functions of signaling one’s own emotions and affects to others. While the functions of COMEMO can range from a simple nonverbal contact aimed at establishing an emotional interaction to a real sharing of emotions [46], the main facets considered in the DOE are expression and communication activities, which are used to make known, disclose and share one’s emotional experience with others.

The somatic or bodily level comprises the perception of both internal emotional indicators (PERINT) and external emotional indicators (PEREXT). The PERINT dimension refers mainly to the attention paid by individuals to internal sensations related to their emotional processes that are conveyed by the autonomic nervous system, whereas the PEREXT dimension refers to emotional indicators that can be perceived by other people, such as the motor activities involved in the preparation of an action (e.g., vocal and facial expression, gestures and postures involved in the bodily expression).

The fifth and last dimension in Reicherts’ model [43] is regulation of emotion (REGEMO), which lies at the intersection between the cognitive, bodily and social levels. REGEMO can be defined as a process aimed at initiating, avoiding, inhibiting, maintaining or modulating the onset, form, intensity or duration of either emotional states, physiological or attentional processes related to emotions, and/or behavioral responses to emotions [14]. This dimension regulates all kinds of affective stages.

Thus, DOE is a multidimensional concept that accounts for both the processes, dispositions and skills involved in the handing of emotions. It therefore seems interesting to study what distinguishes it from a less specific concept such as personality, which is also involved in the individual’s emotional experience. Indeed, it is accepted that personality refers to the stable organization of an individual’s mental universe, constant over time and across situations. As such, personality allows us to account for behaviors, attitudes, reactions and emotions and the way in which they are implemented; it therefore constitutes a stable set of characteristics that allows us to describe the individuality of the person. Personality and emotional experience cannot be dissociated. The associations between openness as a dispositional trait and REPCOG, REGEMO, for example, prove that emotional openness maintains coherent links with the personality trait openness, while being distinguished, in particular, from the processual aspect that it represents [47].

A handful of studies have investigated the relationship between DOE and personality. For instance, Genoud et al. [48] found negative correlations between neuroticism and REPCOG, as well as between neuroticism and REGEMO. They also found positive correlations between extraversion and COMEMO, as well as between conscientiousness and REPCOG. However, their research did not seem to identify links to the other personality dimensions, including openness, which is why we focused on the latter in the present study. Nevertheless, other research has indicated that openness was associated with or influenced variables similar to those examined in DOE. Purnamaningsih [49] reported that openness predicted the use of the situation modification emotion regulation strategy. Similarly, Zhu et al. [50] identified a relationship between openness and various emotion regulation strategies: openness had a positive correlation with reappraisal, worry, mindfulness, problem-solving, fantasy distraction, cognitive restructuring, wishful thinking, withdrawal, music modulation and had a negative correlation with denial and suppression.

Concerning the link between DOE and QoL, Reicherts et al. [47] found that patients with psychological disorders scored lower on REPCOG, REGEMO and COMEMO than a nonclinical sample, but higher on PERINT. PERINT and PEREXT can be linked to the observing facet of mindfulness, which was found by Royuela-Colomer and Calvete [51] to increase negative affectivity. We therefore assumed that increasing PERINT or PEREXT and reducing REPCOG, REGEMO or COMEMO is maladaptive for mental health. As such, we predicted that if the link between openness and QoL passes via REPCOG, REGEMO or COMEMO, the intermediated relation will be positive. By contrast, if it passes via PERINT or possibly even PEREXT, it will be negative.

Self-Efficacy beliefs and their links to personality and QoL

Self-efficacy beliefs may be an important factor to consider when seeking to explain how personality is linked to QoL. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s internal appraisal of their ability to execute an action successfully in order to achieve a desired outcome [52]. Contemporary literature has highlighted the importance of self-efficacy for quality of life [53] and shows that self-efficacy is important to manage positive and negative emotions, which contribute strongly to QoL. Extraversion, conscientiousness and emotional stability are the three personality factors most strongly related to self-efficacy beliefs, but there have been only a few empirical studies to investigate how personality traits and self-efficacy beliefs work in concert to promote QoL [54]. Pocnet et al. [9] showed that neuroticism is strongly and negatively related to QoL, but not significantly linked to self-efficacy, whereas extraversion and conscientiousness are positively associated with self-efficacy, which in turn predicted QoL. Similar findings were reported [55], where the effect of extraversion on QoL was mediated by self-efficacy. Personality traits influence not only behavior but also knowledge acquisition, which may shape self-efficacy beliefs and perceptions of competence in daily life [42]. As for openness, no direct link has been found, although some studies have shown that this personality trait was associated to academic success through self-efficacy beliefs [56]. In the present study, we therefore predicted that if a relation of openness with QoL was intermediated by general self-efficacy (GSE), the link between Openness to QoL would be positive. Extending on prior work on DOE and similar variables [57], one could expect that individuals with higher emotional awareness and better emotion regulation skills exhibit greater self-efficacy. Research suggests that emotional intelligence, which overlaps with DOE, is positively linked to self-efficacy and proactive coping [58]. Moreover, individuals with strong emotion regulation abilities tend to perceive greater control over their lives [59], which aligns with self-efficacy theory [15]. This, in turn, may contribute to higher QoL, as self-efficacious individuals are more likely to engage in proactive and problem-oriented behaviors, effectively navigate challenges, and analyze setbacks constructively to develop more competent responses [60].

Personality and QoL within a network framework

Given potentially antagonistic relationships, it may be useful to gain a parsimonious picture of how QoL is related to personality factors by applying a partial correlation method to examine the incremental facet prediction [61]. The partial correlation method is very similar to the network analysis approach (NA) which is becoming increasingly influential in psychological research and social sciences [62–64]. This statistical method has already been applied in the field of personality [65], job satisfaction [66], in QoL of cancer patients [67] and health-related QoL in healthy adults and cancer patients [68]. NA has not been applied, in our knowledge, to QoL in a non-clinical population, nor to examine personality and QoL together. We propose that this method will be particularly valuable for our study, as we lack comprehensive information and a thorough understanding from the literature regarding the direction and strength of the connections or effects between the openness personality trait, DOE, GSE, and the dimensions of QoL. This research represents an initial exploration of the topic. Given that prior studies have generally identified only limited links between openness and QoL, this novel approach holds the potential to yield meaningful insights. However, given that NA is a linear approach and not applicable to non-linear relations which may be at play between openness, DOE, self-efficacy and QoL, it is necessary to verify whether there are any nonlinear relationships between our variable of interest.

Hypotheses

In the present paper, we chose NA as an exploratory statistical method, to gain more insights into the potentially complex underlying relationships linking openness and QoL, which has led to inconsistent findings. Nevertheless, based on prior research, we were able to formulate some hypothesis, to direct our attention. More specifically, we first examined whether there were nonlinear direct relations of openness on the different facets of QoL. We expected that an intermediate level of openness appears to be the most conducive to a higher quality of life, whereas an extremely high or low level in this dimension would undermine various aspects of overall well-being (Hypothesis 1). As the lack of a direct relationship could also be due to the influence of other variables, we made supplementation assumptions. Thus, we expected that the relations of openness with QoL were modulated by DOE and GSE (Hypothesis 2). Our third hypothesis concerned the direction of the intermediated links. We predicted that the different dimensions of DOE would modulate both positive and negative associations on QoL (Hypothesis 3a). More specifically, we expected the link between openness and QoL conveyed by REPCOG, REGEMO or COMEMO to be positive (Hypothesis 3b), and the link conveyed by PERINT or PEREXT to be negative (Hypothesis 3c). Concerning GSE, as mentioned before, we assumed that GSE would intermediate positive links with all four dimensions of QoL (Hypothesis 3d).

Methods

Participants and procedure

We recruited a community-based sample of 419 participants, including 202 women (48.21%) and 217 men (51.79%), aged 20 to 66 years (M = 39.39, SD = 11.35), of whom 260 (62.05%) live in the French-speaking regions of Switzerland, and 159 (37.95%) live in the Aix-en-Provence region of France. Most of them were employed (employee or self-employed), and fewer than 10% were students, homemakers or unemployed. We made our sample size calculation with Gpower software [69] for an effect size of 0.50, which represents a large effect size [70], associated with a statistical power level of 0.80 and probability level p <.050. For our first analysis (i.e., generalized additive models [GAMs], see more details in statistical analysis paragraph), the necessary sample size is 40 individuals. For the second type of analysis, network analysis, there is no clear and definite method [71], however in more restrictive methods such as path analysis with direct and indirect effects, it is recommended to have approximately 400 participants [72]. We retained this sample size to be sure that we have the necessary statistical power for both analyses. We used a convenience sampling method with the following inclusion criteria: be male or female and more than 18 years old, be a native French speaker or fluent in French. Concerning exclusion criteria, retired or hospitalized individuals were not included. As there were no substantial differences between the French and Swiss samples in terms of demographics and key variables, we pooled their data for the purpose of our analysis. The participants were recruited by psychology students as part of a practical assignment. The psychology students had to go in different enterprises, social institutions and universities and try to recruit participants. They had to explain the purpose of the study and precise that the collected data would remain completely anonymous and strictly confidential. Once they found individuals interested in participating in the research, the psychology students proposed them an appointment at Lausanne University or Aix-Marseille University. When the respondents came at the appointment for the participation they read and signed a written informed consent before completing the pencil-and-paper questionnaires on personality, self-efficacy beliefs, DOE, and QoL. The consent form and all our research respected the ethics rules of the Ethics Committee of Nantes University Human and Social Sciences and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards (Ethics committee approval: n°. 19052021).

Measures

Personality

We used the NEO-FFI-3, an abridged French-language version [73] of the Revised NEO Personality Inventory [3], to measure the personality dimensions of the Big Five model: neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness. This 60-item questionnaire (12 items per dimension) has high internal consistency coefficients ranging from 0.70 to 0.82 for the five scales (Mdn = 0.76) [74]. Given the focus of our study, we only analyzed the openness here (example of items: “I have a wide variety of intellectual interests”). The value of Cronbach’s α in our sample was 0.70 (Table 1). Participants responded to the items on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree).

Self-efficacy beliefs

We used the French version [75] of the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) [76] to measure self-efficacy. It has good internal consistency and validity properties [77]. In our sample, Cronbach’s α was 0.84 (Table 1). This 10-item questionnaire (example of items: “I can always manage to solve difficult problems if I try hard enough”) explicitly refers to personal agency (i.e., belief that we are responsible through our actions for successful outcomes) and assesses self-beliefs related to solving a variety of challenging demands in life. The items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all true) to 4 (Completely true).

DOE: interest and curiosity about emotional functioning

We used the DOE-20 [43], a French 20-item questionnaire, which assess five dimensions of emotional openness: REPCOG (e.g., “I know very well in which emotional state I am”), COMEMO (e.g., “For me, it is important to communicate to others how I’m feeling”), PERINT (e.g., “My physical state makes me think about emotions”), PEREXT (e.g., “I know it’s possible to read my internal state on my face”) and REGEMO (e.g., “I am able to mitigate my emotions, even in difficult situations”). The internal consistency coefficients for the five scales range from 0.67 to 0.83 [43]. In our sample, Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.60 to 0.76 (Table 1). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 5 (Extremely).

Quality of life (QoL)

Dupuis et al. [78] recently validated the French version of the 12-item World Health Organization Quality of Life (WHOQOL-12) questionnaire, and we used it to assess QoL in our study. This short questionnaire includes three items for each of the four factors of the WHOQOL-100. The four dimensions are physical health (e.g., “Do you have enough energy for everyday life?”), psychological health (e.g., “How much do you enjoy life?”), social relationships (e.g., “How satisfied are you with your personal relationships?”), and environment (e.g., “How healthy is your physical environment?”). Participants indicated how far they agreed with each statement on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 5 (Extremely). Dupuis et al. [78] emphasized that the WHOQOL-12 has good psychometric properties in terms of internal consistency (in our sample, Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.63 to 0.80, see Table 1.), itemtotal correlations, criterion validity, construct validity, and measurement invariance, verified by means of confirmatory factor analysis.

Statistical analysis

We first analyzed the descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, skewness and kurtosis, alpha coefficients and zero-order correlations between all the variables. We then analyzed the inferential statistics in three stages. First, we calculated generalized additive models (GAMs) [79, 80] to test nonlinear relations of openness with QoL. Second, we conducted a network analysis [63, 81] to investigate the relationships of openness with QoL and the different effects of the DOE dimensions and self-efficacy as intermediates. Third, we did mediation analysis based on the results of the network analysis.

Generalized additive models

As we wanted to examine the nonlinear relations of openness personality with the four dimensions of QoL, we had to find an alternative method to the classic linear regression functions and generalized linear models (GLMs). We chose GAMs, which are regarded as extensions of GLMs and take account of nonlinear relations between explanatory and explained variables [82]. The easiest method of interpreting the results is to look at their graphical representation as a plot. When there is a linear relationship between the variables, the result is a horizontal line. When the relationship is nonlinear, the result is a curved line. However, just a look at the graphical representation is not enough. The effect size’s significance (R²) also needs to be borne in mind for the final interpretation [82]. That is what we chose to do in this paper. We also report the effective degrees of freedom (edf), the value of an F-test and p value. The edf indicate the flexibility level of the smoothed curve for each predictor. In other words, this summary statistics indicates the degree of nonlinearity of a curve [83]. An edf close to 1 suggests an almost linear relationship, an edf between 1 and 2 indicates a weakly nonlinear relationship, while an edf greater than 2 suggests a highly nonlinear relationship [84]. The F-test is often used to check whether the addition of nonlinear terms (e.g., smooth functions) significantly improves the model’s fit compared to a simpler linear model. A high F-value indicates that smoothed terms significantly improve model fit, justifying their use for certain predictors. More precisely, if the p-value is below the chosen significance level (p <.05), this provides sufficient evidence to conclude that the nonlinear model offers a significantly better fit to the data than a simpler linear model. We carried out our GAMs analysis with the mgcv R package [85].

Network analysis

We ran a network analysis to examine the links between openness personality dimension, DOE, GSE, and QoL, in order to better understand the possible links of openness with QoL’s four dimensions. We used a weighted undirected network, based on the partial correlations between the variables. We applied a LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) [86] regularization in our analysis, as it is a more stringent method compared to others [87]. We conducted a network analysis because it allows researchers to identify causal structures without committing to a specific direction of effect between variables [88]. This flexibility is helpful when the relationships between variables are likely to be reciprocal or involve multiple influences that defy simple directional assumptions (like different nonlinear effects). Moreover, they complement GAMs analyses, where only one dependent variable can be studied, by providing a more comprehensive view. Thus, network models could help illuminate previously unconsidered or unexpected connections between variables, providing new insights into the systems being studied [63].

In the graphical representation of a weighted undirected network, the variables were represented by nodes and the links between the variables (partial correlations) by edges. The stronger the link, the broader and darker the edge. The R packages we used were qgraph [89] and bootnet [62]. We used qgraph to provide the graphical representation of the network and bootnet to calculate the partial correlation coefficients as well as to explore the stability of the network. Only partial correlations with values above 0.06 would be visible as edges in our graph (and the other significant partial correlations below are presented in Table 1). We took this decision in order to make the graphical representation clearer, and mainly because of Epskamp et al.’s recommendation [89] that small effects should be interpreted and considered with caution. In line with this logic, we investigated the stability of the network which indicates how accurately the network is estimated [62]. This analysis performs bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals around the edge weights and calculates a correlation-stability (CS) coefficient. CS ranges from 0 to 1, the values above 0.25 are interpreted as a moderate stability and above 0.50 as a strong stability [62]. Often centrality metrics are computed in network analyses but they receive more and more critics when applied in a cross-sectional design [90], like our study, or, more generally, regarding their application in psychological networks [91, 92]. Thus, we did not include centrality metrics in our paper.

As the alpha coefficient have acceptable values, we used composite variables for our different analysis.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis was conducted to verify the results of network analysis. We tested the effects from openness to QoL via GSE and via DOE. Direct, indirect and total effects were taken into account as well as path estimates. R software was used within the package lavaan.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Zero-order correlations between all measures and descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, skewness, kurtosis, and alpha coefficients) are reported above diagonal in Table 1. Age and sex were not correlated with the other variables, with one exception: age was positively associated with psychological health (r =.10, p <.05). Openness was positively correlated with REPCOG (r =.22, p <.001), COMEMO (r =.12, p <.001), PERINT (r =.20, p <.001), PEREXT (r =.16, p <.001), and GSE (r =.11, p <.05). REPCOG had positive correlations with the QoL subscales, namely participants’ evaluation of their physical health (r =.15, p <.01), psychological health (r =.29, p <.001), social relationships (r =.22, p <.001), and environment (r =.18, p <.001). COMEMO was positively correlated with physical health (r =.13, p <.01), social relationships (r =.17, p <.001), and environment (r =.16, p <.001). PERINT was negatively correlated with physical health (r = −.13, p <.01), and PEREXT had a positive correlation with social relationships (r =.13, p <.001). GSE was positively correlated with physical health (r =.26, p <.001), psychological health (r =.43, p <.001), social relationships (r =.18, p <.001), and environment (r =.23, p <.001).

Generalized additive models

We tested the nonlinear relations of openness with QoL by considering the latter’s four dimensions (H1). Results presented in graph (respectively bottom right-hand and top right-hand graph; Fig. 1) showed that the most open and least open participants were the ones who generally rated their psychological health (edf = 1.55, F = 0.48) and their environment (edf = 3.18, F = 2.14) most favorably. Nevertheless, this only represented a nonlinear trend (p =.082), with high variability in QoL assessments for the highest and lowest levels of openness: when we took the effect size and the p-value into account (respectively R² = 0.00, p =.082 for psychological health and R² = 0.02, p =.082 for environment), the results were not significant and the effects were very small. The link between openness and social relations (bottom left-hand graph; Fig. 1) appeared as slightly nonlinear (edf = 1.17, F = 2.03) but it was not significant and the effect was null (R² = 0.00, p =.556). The link between openness and physical health (top left-hand graph; Fig. 1) was linear (edf = 1.00, F = 1.00) and not significant, with a null effect (R² = 0.00, p =.722).

Concerning the positive associations, the indirect links between openness and the QoL dimensions that passed through GSE were significant: the more open participants were, the more convinced they were about their capacities. This led them to be more satisfied with their mental and physical health, which in turn had a beneficial effect on their social relationships and the environment. The indirect links between openness and the QoL dimensions that passed through REPCOG (either directly or modulated by GSE or REGEMO) were also positive: the more open participants were, the better their cognitive representations of their emotional state, which in turn were associated with higher satisfaction with their mental health, their environment and social relationships and, consequently, their physical health. Another positive indirect relation included COMEMO: the more open participants were, the more they communicated their emotions and the higher was their satisfaction with their mental health, their social relationships, their environment and, consequently, their physical health. Openness also had a positive link with QoL when it was intermediated by PERINT or PEREXT and then passed through REPCOG.

In line with our prediction, openness was negatively associated with quality of life, particularly when examining certain facets of emotional processing: more specifically, we found pathways suggesting indirect effects via REGEMO after PERINT, PEREXT or COMEMO. Thus, the more individuals focused on their internal bodily states and the less they regulated their emotions, the less satisfied they were with their mental health and, in turn, their physical health, social relationships, and environment. Similarly, the more they focused on their external bodily features and the less they regulated their emotions, the less satisfied they were with their mental health and, consequently, their physical health, social relationships and environment.

The more open they were, the more they communicated their emotions, and the less they regulated their emotions and the less satisfied they were with their mental health and, in turn, their physical health, social relationships and environment. Similarly, the more open they were, the more they communicated their emotions, the more they focused on their external bodily indicators, the less they regulated their emotions and the less satisfied they were with their mental health and, consequently, their physical health, social relationships and environment.

In summary, when the edges representing the openness links passed through the REPCOG and/or GSE nodes, either directly or via the COMEMO node, there were positive associations with the different dimensions of QoL. There were also positive associations when these edges passed through the PERINT node and then the PEREXT or REPCOG node. However, when the edges passed through the PERINT, PEREXT or COMEMO node, and then through the REGEMO node, the indirect links with the different dimensions of QoL were negative.

Discussion

Complementing work on the important role of three Big Five personality traits extraversion, neuroticism and consciousness in QoL that has been highlighted in several studies, our study investigated the link between openness and QoL, a trait that has been widely neglected so far. The purpose of the present study was to better understand the openness-QoL relationship with a particular focus on the intermediating role of DOE and self-efficacy beliefs.

Theoretical discussion of results

The descriptive statistics showed that openness was not related to age or gender, confirming prior work with life-span samples. Age was positively associated with psychological health. This relation, not in the core of our work, goes in the same direction that previous studies in QoL [93, 94]. Against expectations, our GAMs analysis failed to show a significant nonlinear direct relation of openness with QoL and our first hypothesis was not confirmed. On close inspection of our GAMs findings, we found that QoL was more pronounced among participants with either very low or very high openness, but this was only a descriptive, non-significant trend.

The relationships between openness and QoL, as observed in the literature, are generally of moderate magnitude, which is confirmed by our findings. Fredrickson’s broaden-and-build theory of cognition and emotion [16, 17] highlights the role of creativity in responding to aversive stimuli. This creativity can reduce negative emotional experiences and, more importantly, foster positive affect. However, a more refined assessment of openness, using a tool that accounts for its sub-facets, may provide a more precise estimation of the links between openness and the different dimensions of quality of life [36].

Our analyses of the nonlinear relationships between openness and the four dimensions of quality of life reveal unexpected results that contradict our initial hypotheses. Specifically, participants at both extremes of the openness continuum (either highly open or very low in openness) reported better psychological health and greater satisfaction with their environment. Although these effects remain modest, they suggest that these two distinct openness profiles, despite their differences, may both be conducive to QoL.

Among highly open individuals, our results confirm that the processes associated with this trait—such as intellectual engagement, adaptability, self-regulation, leisure appreciation, and reduced egocentrism—constitute important resources for psychological health [37]. These processes could be leveraged in interventions aimed at enhancing quality of life. In contrast, interpreting the link between low openness and quality of life is more complex and appears to be underexplored in the literature. Individuals with low openness, who tend to avoid change and prefer a stable life that aligns with social norms, seem to benefit from a better quality of life. Their way of functioning may make them less exposed to criticism and more inclined to adhere to health recommendations, thereby reinforcing their well-being [25]. Moreover, their structured lifestyle, characterized by moderate and reasonable habits, could contribute to their overall well-being. Additionally, their preference for established and intellectually comfortable concepts distances them from progressive ideas, which they may perceive as risky and destabilizing [95].

Our findings also indicate that individuals with intermediate levels of openness appear to be more vulnerable in terms of quality of life. One possible explanation is that being “between two opportunities,” without a clear direction, may generate uncertainty and psychological imbalance, which is less conducive to optimal quality of life [96]. However, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution and confirmed through studies with larger samples. In our study, we observed high variability in QoL assessments at the extremes of openness, which could justify differential analyses to better understand these findings. Finally, it should be noted that the relationships between openness and other dimensions of quality of life, such as social relationships and physical health, appear to remain generally linear. That said, trends similar to those observed for psychological health and environmental satisfaction emerge in graphical representations, warranting further exploration.

We confirmed our second hypothesis. Our results indicate that openness has no direct linear relationship with QoL (mediation analysis and NA), which had also been shown in many previous studies [3, 5, 7–9, 11, 12, 97].

Going above and beyond this prior research, our study demonstrated indirect relationships between openness and QoL at the processual level. We showed an indirect link between openness to QoL via DOE and self-efficacy and thus confirmed our hypothesis (H3a).

Considering the positive links first, in the NA, openness had a positive association with all four dimensions of QoL via REPCOG. However, in mediation analysis, only psychological QoL was concerned. More precisely, Openness was positively associated with REPCOG, which in turn predicted higher psychological QoL. This suggests that individuals high in openness may engage more frequently in reflective and exploratory thinking, which supports adaptive emotional processing and meaning-making—a result consistent with prior research on openness and cognitive complexity [23, 98]. Thus, open individuals with a high ability to discriminate between different emotional states and higher awareness of situations to which emotions are linked, seem to feel better. These results are in line with the expectation that individuals with high openness scores are more open to intellectual exchanges and to the development of new behaviors and new processes, including the enhancement of their emotional skills, and can thus better develop their emotional intelligence [99] and feel more positive emotions [13, 20, 35]. And concerning emotions, the links intermediated by REGEMO and COMEMO were more complex than predicted.

In the NA, the somatic dimensions of DOE (PEREXT and PERINT) had a negative link to REGEMO and QoL. By contrast, REPCOG played a relatively positive role in REGEMO: Individuals with high openness were inclined to engage in two different types of behavior: (1) paying close attention to the somatic characteristics of their emotional functioning, which appeared not to be positive for QoL, especially psychological QoL; and (2) trying to understand their emotions and thus develop rich cognitive representations of them, which has a positive influence on QoL. Thus, these opposing underlying effects may be one reason for the lack of relationships between openness and QoL, as they cancel each other out. The other facet of DOE, COMEMO, like REGEMO, was not directly related to QoL, and had both positive and negative links, depending on the other variables to which it was related. The association with QoL was positive when channeled via REPCOG. The association with QoL intermediated by PEREXT could be either positive or negative. It was positive when PEREXT was related to REPCOG and GSE, but negative when PEREXT was related to REGEMO. The mediation analysis did not confirm all these pathways. The results suggested a complex and partially counterintuitive mechanism through which openness contributes to psychological QoL. Specifically, openness was positively associated with emotional expression (COMEMO), indicating that individuals high in openness tend to express their emotions more freely. However, greater emotional expression was linked to reduced emotion regulation (REGEMO), which in turn predicted better psychological QoL. The significant indirect effect from openness to psychological outcomes through COMEMO and REGEMO supported a serial mediation model, but in a negative direction, suggesting that emotional expressiveness may impair reappraisal-based regulation strategies, even as reduced reliance on such regulation appears to be associated with improved psychological outcomes. These findings highlighted the potentially ambivalent role of emotional expressivity in the regulation process [100, 101], particularly among open individuals, and suggested that psychological adjustment may not always rely on increased regulation, but potentially on more fluid or less effortful emotion processing [102]. Thus, the hypothesis that REPCOG, COMEMO and REGEMO would have only positive links with QoL (H3b) was partially confirmed. These results showed that the link between openness and QoL is more complex than it might intuitively appear to be: the different dimensions of DOE can influence each other and thus modulate the link between openness and QoL in contrasting ways.

The two bodily dimensions in Reicherts’s model [43] of DOE (PERINT and PEREXT) channeled the relationship between openness and QoL in the NA. These two dimensions had a negative link with QoL, which therefore went in the opposite direction to that of the cognitive dimension REPCOG. The psychological processes involved in PERINT consist in paying close attention to one’s bodily feelings. Results showed that this process, which open individuals are more likely to engage in than less open individuals, is not positive for QoL. This may explain why we did not find the main link of openness with QoL. It seems that the more open people are, the more they focus on PERINT, which in turn contributes to a feeling of dissatisfaction with their physical health. Thus, this somatic dimension of DOE appears to have a special status in relation to QoL. This result is in line with the findings [51] who showed that the observing facet of mindfulness, which is similar to PERINT and PEREXT, has deleterious effects in terms of negative affectivity. However, these pathways were not confirmed by the mediation analysis where no significant effect was found. Thus, the hypothesis predicting that negative effects were related to PERINT or PEREXT (H3c) was only supported in the NA.

The last hypothesis (H3d) assumed that GSE would intermediate only positive links with QoL. In NA, REPCOG was positively related to QoL via its positive relations with GSE, and REGEMO did the same thing via REPCOG. Meanwhile, PERINT and PEREXT not only had deleterious links on REGEMO, but also had a negative association with QoL via GSE. However, GSE was associated in a serial mediation with REPCOG and did not a simple mediation as the NA results suggested. These findings partially paralleled Hypothesis 3d and the results reported by Strobel et al. [13], who studied the mediating effect of self-efficacy on happiness and concluded that the better their GSE, the happier individuals feel. Nonetheless, it is also important to bear in mind that, according to our findings, GSE could be increased by REPCOG.

Once again, our results showed that the mediators are related to each other in a complex way and can convey both positive and negative connections. They offer a plausible explanation as to why research has found inconclusive association between openness and QoL.

The results also showed significant positive correlations between the DOE and the openness. It suggests that while these constructs are distinct, they share conceptual and empirical overlap. Openness to experience, as defined in the Big Five framework, encompasses intellectual curiosity, aesthetic sensitivity, and emotional depth [25]. Research has shown that individuals high in openness are more likely to engage in introspection and exhibit a greater awareness and differentiation of their emotional experiences [36]. This aligns with findings indicating that openness facilitates cognitive and emotional exploration, which may, in turn, enhance the ability to distinguish and regulate emotions [37]. However, despite this relationship, DOE specifically captures the capacity to identify and differentiate emotions, whereas openness is a broader trait encompassing diverse cognitive and affective dimensions. Thus, DOE and openness remained related but different psychological constructs. Future research could further investigate whether openness moderates the impact of DOE on psychological well-being and adaptive functioning.

Practical implications

Taken together, our results may have practical implications. As already mentioned, interventions have already been designed to enhance individuals’ openness [103]. Our results suggest that clinicians should be cautious when they attempt to promote openness, as this personality trait has both a positive and a negative impact on QoL. Our findings suggest that if these interventions are intended to promote QoL, they should focus more narrowly on the construction of a rich understanding of emotions (i.e., increase REPCOG). Interestingly, some interventions have already been shown to effectively enhance individuals’ understanding of their emotions [104].

There might be also a practical implication in business psychology. Nowadays, it is often asked to improve the imagination and the creativity, which are openness facets, of employees in order to enhance the performance and the progress of the enterprises [105–107]. In other hand, many studies already showed that QoL has an influence on employees’ performance [108–111] or willingness to expatriate [112]. So, it will be important not only to increase the creativity and imagination but also to support this approach through understanding, evaluating and influence the level of DOE in order to ensure a real progress.

Limitations and suggestions for future research

While the present study offers important new insights on the complex structure of direct and indirect relationships between the personality trait openness, openness to emotions, self-efficacy, and QoL, it also has several limitations which may inspire future research. In our study, the participants were adults, most of them being professionally active, and findings tend to look different in such samples compared to studies carried out among students. For instance, Bardi and colleagues [30] found a link between openness and QoL, which may have been because of the participants’ age and life circumstances: Whereas young adults’ QoL is mainly related to academic success and is therefore associated with openness [113], this personality dimension may be less important for adults who are employed or otherwise engaged in daily activities, and therefore may have less of an impact on their perception of QoL. Thus, it would be interesting to investigate how the link between openness and QoL evolves across different state of age.

The mediating role of DOE and GSE within the relationship between openness and QoL is very interesting, but even if we explored it only with adults, it deserves to be analyzed dynamically over time. This may be done by using an ecological momentary assessment (EMA) offereing an interesting longitudinal approach to analyze the dynamics among the concepts [114]. Studying a larger sample would further make it possible to test a more comprehensive (structural) model containing more variables – notably cognitive variables, which were neglected in the present study (e.g., crystallized intelligence). Having a larger sample would also allow the intercultural aspect of openness to be clarified, which may show potential differences between French and Swiss individuals. This represents an important future perspective in this area of research. Furthermore, a larger sample would make it possible to directly test interactions or mediations between personality traits and QoL. For example, Zhang and Tsingan [115] investigated whether extraversion and neuroticism mediate the relationships between openness, conscientiousness or agreeableness and affective well-being. They found that extraversion fully mediated the effect of openness on positive affect, and neuroticism fully mediated the influence of openness or conscientiousness on negative affect. Results also showed that agreeableness had a direct effect on positive and negative affect that was only partially mediated by extraversion or neuroticism [115]. This type of mediation may also occur between the Big Five personality traits and QoL. It would therefore be interesting to investigate different personality profiles, as they may each have a different impact on QoL. Moreover, in line with Meyer et al. [116] reminder that openness in experience is a heterogeneous personality trait, it would also be important to explore its different facets. Thus, a next step to further increase our understanding could be to investigate the relationship and effects between openness and QoL on the facet level. For example, Anglim et al. [2] noted that the prediction of QoL by facets was better than the prediction of higher-order personality factors and that, moreover, the incremental prediction was larger for some well-being dimensions than others.

Finally, the role of other variables (e.g., age, sex, values, income, health status, context) and other mediating processes should also be considered, given that prior work highlighted important differences. For example, in a transitional context, women who are low in openness and endorse traditional gender role beliefs and women who are high in openness and reject traditional gender role beliefs are known to have higher self-efficacy and subjective well-being [117]. Another example is the influence of openness on QoL of older people in function of their education level: Mueller et al. [118] showed that openness is beneficial for less educated, but not for highly educated older persons. Consequently, it would be interesting to explore in more detail the combination of openness with such variables and their influences on QoL.

Conclusions

In sum, the findings of the present study contribute to the understanding of how openness is linked with QoL. We identified DOE and self-efficacy as important underlying mechanisms that channel the associations of openness with QoL. We also showed that openness seems to allow individuals to be more thoughtful about emotional information. Openness may help them to develop rich representations of emotions, which is beneficial for GSE and, as a consequence, for QoL. However, openness also seems tempt individuals to pay close attention to internal and external indicators of their emotions, which may have a negative influence on their emotion regulation and, as a consequence, on their QoL. Openness contributes to quality of life, especially for individuals who are moderately open to their bodily sensations and who use thoughtful and context-adjusted emotion regulation strategies. We have therefore shown both the dark and bright sides of openness in relation to QoL. It is important to investigate the complex relationship openness-QoL relationship, given its practical implications for counseling and business psychology, and future research will yield more information about it in different contexts.
