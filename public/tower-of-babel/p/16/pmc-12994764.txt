Advancing clinical utility of artificial intelligence: lessons from developing a model to predict cochlear implant eligibility
Authors: Valentina Carducci, Horacio Sanchez-Trigo, Yesmeen Elgabori, Matthew Carlson, Nicholas Deep, Santiago Romero-Brufau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo address the gap between artificial intelligence (AI) model development and clinical implementation by developing a machine-learning model to predict cochlear implant (CI) eligibility based on routine hearing tests, while illustrating 3 practical lessons: reformulation for clinical utility, selection of problem-specific metrics, and handling input data variability.Materials and MethodsData were extracted from adult patients who underwent behavioral audiometric and subsequent CI candidacy testing (Arizona Biomedical and Consonant-Nucleus-Consonant tests) at Mayo Clinic between 2011 and 2023. Regression models were initially developed to predict AzBio and CNC scores using features from routine audiograms. Models were then reformulated into binary classification tasks using clinical thresholds (AzBio ≤60%; CNC <50%) and reported sensitivity and positive predictive value (PPV). Input data variability was assessed by analyzing test-retest differences in isophonemes scores.ResultsBinary classification models achieved sensitivity of 90.4% with PPV 80.2% at CNC 50/AzBio 60, outperforming referral heuristics (60/60 and 75/40). Applying the models to 50 700 historical audiograms identified 525 patients at PPV 90% (sensitivity 58%), 396 at PPV 94% (sensitivity 44%), and 1582 at PPV 71% (sensitivity 94%). Variability analyses indicated that attainable precision is bounded by noise in key predictors.ConclusionThis study demonstrates how AI models can be translated into clinically actionable tools by reformulating regression outputs, selecting metrics aligned with clinical priorities, and addressing data variability. These strategies provide a scalable framework for implementing AI in health care and improving decision-making in diverse clinical contexts.

Introduction

The implementation of machine-learning (ML) models to improve clinical decision-making has significantly lagged behind the development of prediction models. At the time of writing, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved only 520 artificial intelligence (AI) or ML-enabled medical devices despite the publication of far more than 10 000 AI/ML models.1 Furthermore, 90%-94% of published AI models remain within the testing and prototyping environment.2 This gap between development and practical application highlights the need for a deeper understanding of the nuances involved in translating AI models into effective clinical tools. This paper aims to describe critical considerations in this translation process, using the development of an ML model in the context of hearing as a real case study, specifically, an automated model for predicting cochlear implant (CI) eligibility based on routine hearing tests. The methodological insights gained from this study are encapsulated in 3 key take-away points that are essential for successfully implementing AI models in clinical settings:

Reformulation of models for clinical utility: AI prototypes often fail in clinical settings due to inadequate accuracy. However, models that may seem insufficiently accurate can sometimes be useful in a given clinical context, if reformulated. We show how a model with a relatively low regression accuracy showed sufficiently high accuracy for clinical utility when transformed into a binary classification. This specific modification significantly improved its relevance and effectiveness in clinical decision-making.

These principles are illustrated through our recent work to develop an automated model for predicting CI eligibility based on routine hearing tests. Disabling hearing loss affects an estimated 430 million people worldwide (5.3% of the world’s population)3 and 30 million people in the United States alone (16.5% of American adults).4 Hearing ability is routinely evaluated with an audiogram, which evaluates the lowest volume of sound that the patient can perceive at different frequencies, as well as a short word recognition test. These routine hearing evaluations take approximately 20 min to complete and more than 10 million are performed in the United States per year.5

Currently, several technological solutions exist to improve hearing in patients with hearing difficulties. For patients with more severe hearing dysfunction, a CI may be beneficial. It is estimated that of the approximately 1.3 million patients that would benefit from a CI, only 170 000 receive one.6 Reasons for this include cost considerations, patient preference, but also under-referral for CI eligibility testing.7 Establishing CI eligibility is required for insurance reimbursement purposes and often involves a lengthy eligibility test that typically lasts 1-2 h. Early approaches have relied on simple heuristic guidelines based on pure-tone average (PTA) and word recognition scores (WRSs), such as the “75/40” rule,8 and the “60/60” rule.9 Briefly, the 60/60 rule recommends referral when the better ear’s unaided PTA at 500, 1000, and 2000 Hz is ≥60 decibels Hearing Level (dB HL) and the unaided monosyllabic WRS is ≤60%. The 75/40 rule recommends referral when the better ear has individual pure-tone thresholds ≥75 dB HL at 250-1000 Hz and/or a WRS ≤40%. These rules are referral heuristics (not definitive candidacy determinations) intended to trigger formal CI evaluation. A routine diagnostic audiogram reports unaided air-conduction thresholds across frequencies and a brief unaided WRS at a comfortable presentation level; it does not assess best-aided, open-set speech understanding or sentence-level comprehension. By contrast, CI candidacy is determined with best-aided open-set speech tests: 2 of the most common eligibility tests are Arizona Biomedical (AzBio) and Consonant-Nucleus-Consonant (CNC),10,11 which test the patient’s ability to recognize spoken sentences and words. The AzBio sentence test reports the percent of everyday sentences correctly understood in quiet (and often also in noise), better approximating functional communication. The CNC word test presents phonetically balanced, open-set monosyllabic words in quiet (eg, “dog,” “chair,” “cup”) and yields percent-correct word recognition. Arizona Biomedical serves as the regulatory/payer anchor for candidacy, while CNC helps clinicians gauge auditory resolution and anticipated benefit. Candidacy typically requires meeting both thresholds because AzBio addresses payer/FDA requirements for sentence understanding, and CNC adds complementary information about phonemic resolution that informs expected clinical benefit.

Our goal was to develop an automated model to predict CI eligibility using information from the routine (shorter) hearing test. The model was designed for 2 applications: identifying potential implant candidates retrospectively and aiding clinicians in making real-time decisions. Recent work has applied ML to predict adult CI candidacy directly from routine audiometric data,12,13 and systematic reviews summarize prediction models for CI outcomes.14 The present study extends this literature by (1) predicting continuous AzBio and CNC scores from routine audiometry and reformulating them into adjustable, clinically aligned candidacy decisions for retrospective and prospective use cases; (2) combining predictions across both tests to mirror programmatic candidacy criteria; and (3) quantifying how test-retest variability in routine predictors constrains achievable accuracy.

By sharing our experiences and insights from this specific application, we aim to contribute to the broader discourse on effectively bridging the gap between AI model development and clinical implementation. The goal of this paper is to provide clear, actionable strategies for overcoming common obstacles in the deployment of AI systems in medical settings.

Methods

Datasets

Following Institutional Review Board approval (#22-000183), we abstracted data from adult patients who received behavioral audiometric testing and subsequent CI candidacy testing across the Mayo Clinic enterprise between January 2011 and March 2023. Data extraction was performed using the Audiology Documentation Program (Mayo Clinic, 2016), a custom-developed audiometric data management system. Our outcomes of interest were the results from the 2 tests used to determine CI candidacy: AzBio and CNC. Predictors were derived from diagnostic audiograms (pure-tone air conduction [ACU] and isophonemes word recognition tests) conducted within 180 days of the AzBio or CNC test. For both outcomes of interest and predictors, we only included unilateral tests done in quiet, in standard condition, before a CI was implanted in that ear. We excluded entries with missing values for one or more predictors. Importantly, our study did not examine whether patients proceeded with cochlear implantation, as there are many other factors that may impact a patient’s decision. Instead, we focused solely on whether the patient met criteria for implantation: primary outcome of preoperative CNC word and AzBio sentence scores. Our approach was motivated by the evolving FDA guidelines and potential variance among providers in offering CI surgery.

Based on our clinicians’ subject-matter expertise as well as data missingness, we determined that the models should incorporate the following predictors: age, legal sex, ear, WRS, isophonemes recognition score, presentation level (for the isophonemes and word recognition test), ACU at 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, 6000, and 8000 Hz, and the average of the patients’ ACU dB HL scores at 500, 1000, 2000 Hz, and 250, 500, 1000 Hz, respectively—which served the purpose of including predictors included in recently published clinical benchmark models.8,9 Categorical variables were one-hot encoded. We created 4 datasets: (1) with the AzBio results and their corresponding predictors (AzBio dataset); (2) with the CNC results and their predictors (CNC dataset); (3) combining entries with both AzBio and CNC scores (combined dataset); and (4) a dataset of entries that included our final set of predictors from patients who had undergone diagnostic audiogram but not a CI evaluation. The AzBio and CNC datasets were split 70/30, with 30% of the data reserved for the test set, which was not utilized for training or model selection. Additionally, during the partitioning process, we ensured that each patient data, identified by their unique Medical Record Number (MRN), was exclusively present in either the training or testing set. The “combined dataset” used for joint evaluation consisted of the subset of ear-level observations with both AzBio and CNC scores available in quiet within 180 days of the paired audiogram. Clinical involvement

Two board-certified otologists (M.C. and N.D.) with more than a decade of experience each in adult otology and cochlear implantation were involved throughout the study. They defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, selected clinically relevant predictors, set candidacy thresholds in accordance with institutional practice and guidelines, reviewed intermediate analyses for clinical plausibility, and co-led the selection of operating points for retrospective and prospective use cases.

Regression model development

We started by constructing 2 regression models using unilateral observations: one to predict the AzBio score (trained on the AzBio dataset) and another to predict the CNC score (trained on the CNC dataset). Both models followed identical preprocessing steps, regression techniques, and evaluation methods. We framed the task as supervised regression, predicting continuous speech-perception scores (AzBio and CNC) from routine audiometric predictors. We compared 3 algorithmic families: (1) linear regression as a parametric baseline to assess linear signal and provide a point of comparison; (2) random forests (RFs), an ensemble of decision trees trained on bootstrap samples and averaged to reduce variance while capturing nonlinearities and interactions15; and (3) gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost), which add trees sequentially to minimize the loss function and model high-order interactions.16 We used 10-fold cross-validation to tune hyperparameters and select the final model family; RFs achieved the best cross-validated performance for both outcomes. For both models, the final selection was a RF model, built using 10-fold cross-validation with 500 decision trees. Each tree was trained on a bootstrap sample of the data with replacement. The maximum number of features considered for each split was set to 4, and the minimum number of samples required to split a node was set to 5. Model performance was evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE).

We intentionally modeled continuous speech-perception outcomes (AzBio, CNC) with regression and applied clinical thresholds post hoc to derive candidacy labels. This choice preserves (1) threshold flexibility as candidacy cutoffs evolve and differ by use case (retrospective vs prospective), (2) combined-test logic (patients must meet both AzBio and CNC cutoffs) without committing to a single, fixed label definition, and (3) decile-level calibration of the probability of qualifying. Training a stand-alone classifier (eg, logistic regression, RF-classifier, XGBoost-classifier) would require fixing a single labeling scheme that is sensitive to label noise near thresholds and to changing guidelines, and would need retraining or recalibration whenever operating points change. Our tree-based regressors capture nonlinearities and interactions in audiometric predictors and produce stable continuous predictions that we can threshold to meet different clinical priorities.

Model refinement and clinical adaptation

To bridge the gap between model performance and clinical applicability, we applied targeted transformations to refine and adapt the regression models. Each transformation, which we frame below as a lesson, addressed specific challenges identified during the initial analysis. By reformulating the regression models and introducing tailored performance metrics, these adjustments aimed to improve both the precision and practical usability of the predictive tools in clinical settings.

Lesson 1: Reformulation of the model for clinical utility

The regression model initially found suboptimal for practical application. However, we reformulated it into a binary classification task to better align with clinical needs and evaluated whether this reformulated model met implementation standards. This transformation focused on answering a key clinical question: whether a patient qualifies for CI candidacy. Recasting the problem as a classification task simplifies clinical decision-making, where binary outcomes are essential for guiding patient care. By focusing on clinically relevant thresholds, this approach enhances the model’s precision, usability, and relevance in real-world clinical environments. Specifically, in the AzBio dataset, a score of 60 or lower was categorized as “Qualifying,” and scores above this threshold were labeled as “Not qualifying.” In the CNC dataset, the qualifying threshold was set at below 50%. This method of classification using established clinical benchmarks allows adapting the initial regression model to a real-world health-care setting.

Lesson 2: Choice of problem-specific metrics

To evaluate the performance of the binary classification models developed through the earlier reformulation, we calculated key metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, and positive predictive value (PPV) at various clinical thresholds. However, instead of relying solely on generic performance metrics such as area under the receiver-operator curve (AUROC), these were adapted to meet the specific requirements of our clinical decision-making process. At our institution, a patient must qualify on both CNC and AzBio tests to be considered a candidate for cochlear implantation. To reflect this, we calculated combined classification metrics for both models (AzBio and CNC), ensuring the models reflect the clinical decision-making process.17

To estimate the combined probability of both models producing correct predictions, we multiplied the probability of each individual model being correct (PPV) for each decile of the predicted score. This approach assumes the independence of predictions from the 2 models—a conservative assumption, given that CNC and AzBio predictions are likely positively correlated. By tailoring these metrics to our specific clinical context, we provide a more nuanced and practical assessment of the model’s utility in guiding real-world decisions.

Additionally, we obtained an estimate of the number of patients who would be predicted to qualify by applying the models to a retrospective dataset of diagnostic audiogram data for patients who had not previously undergone CI evaluation. That is, since ground-truth AzBio/CNC outcomes are unavailable for this cohort, performance metrics cannot be computed on this set. The purpose of this analysis was to estimate the number of patients who would be flagged for referral at different operating points. To ensure consistency, we applied the same filtering criteria used during model training and utilized the models to identify and count patients who met the qualification thresholds.

In summary, the final evaluation of the models included, for the retrospective use case: an estimate of PPV and sensitivity for the combined outcome (both AzBio and CNC) at different thresholds, focusing on thresholds with relatively high PPV, and an estimate of the number of prior patients predicted to qualify. And for the prospective use case: an estimate of PPV and sensitivity for the combined outcome at different thresholds, emphasizing thresholds with relatively high sensitivity. We prespecified a grid of threshold pairs for evaluation (CNC and AzBio ∈ {40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70}). The primary operating point (CNC 50/AzBio 60) was chosen a priori based on institutional candidacy practice and payer expectations. Other operating points (eg, “high-PPV” and “high sensitivity”) are reported descriptively to illustrate tradeoffs. No threshold was tuned by optimizing on test-set labels.

Lesson 3: Addressing input data variability

The accuracy of predictions in ML models is influenced by 3 primary sources of error: model bias, variance, and data noise.18 Bias arises from the limitations in the model’s structure or expressiveness, reflecting the constraints imposed by design choices such as the number of parameters or model architecture. Variance refers to the variability in predictions when the same algorithm is trained on different subsets of data, highlighting sensitivity to the training set. Finally, noise, which is often underappreciated as a source of inaccuracy, stems from the quality and precision of the input data, encompassing measurement errors or inconsistencies in the dataset.

Recognizing that data noise is a critical factor in prediction errors, we sought to evaluate the inherent variability and imprecision in our input data. Our analysis focused on the primary predictor, the isophonemes score, by identifying instances where the same patient had undergone the same test, under the same conditions, within a 6-month period. By comparing the differences between these test results, we estimated the degree of imprecision inherent to the test. These results demonstrate the tradeoffs between PPV and sensitivity when applying different thresholds. A high PPV ensures that most patients identified as eligible for CI are true positives, reducing the risk of false positives. The retrospective application highlights the models’ potential to enhance candidate identification across varying clinical priorities.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study demonstrates how targeted refinements can transform ML models into clinically actionable tools, addressing key challenges in translating AI models from development to real-world applications. By reformulating regression models into binary classification tasks, selecting problem-specific metrics, and addressing input data variability, we successfully developed a model to predict CI candidacy based on routine hearing test data that is ready for implementation into clinical practice.

Our regression-first approach was deliberate: it decouples model training from any single candidacy threshold, enabling tunable operating points (high PPV for retrospective outreach vs high sensitivity for prospective screening) and combined-test decisions without retraining. While dedicated classifiers could be trained against a specific label definition, their performance and calibration would be tied to that definition and to the prevailing thresholds. We therefore prioritized continuous prediction with post hoc thresholding and calibration as a more robust translational strategy.

Reformulating the regression models allowed us to align the outputs with clinically relevant thresholds, enabling binary classification that demonstrated high sensitivity and PPV for CI eligibility. Tailoring metrics to balance sensitivity and PPV ensured the models reflected the nuances of clinical decision-making, addressing the distinct priorities of retrospective and prospective use cases. Additionally, by accounting for test-retest variability inherent to audiometric assessments, we implemented strategies to mitigate its impact, such as considering confidence intervals and repeated testing for borderline cases.

These refinements enabled the development of a streamlined, scalable approach to CI candidacy evaluation. The model has dual applications: retrospectively identifying overlooked candidates and prospectively supporting real-time clinical decision-making. This study not only addresses challenges specific to CI prediction but also offers strategies that can be adapted to other health-care domains.

Translating regression models into clinical tools

Regression models initially showed moderate performance based on metrics such as MAE and RMSE, which were insufficient to capture clinical utility. Reformulating the models into binary classification tasks, using thresholds such as AzBio≤60 and CNC<50, transformed their relevance by directly predicting CI candidacy. This approach enabled alignment with clinicians’ decision-making frameworks, improving sensitivity and PPV.

Starting with regression models provided flexibility to calculate probabilities across score deciles, allowing for nuanced decision-making. For instance, the models identified high-confidence candidates retrospectively while capturing broader populations prospectively. When compared to traditional CI eligibility rules (eg, 60/60 and 75/40), the models demonstrated superior sensitivity and PPV, highlighting the advantages of a data-driven approach tailored to clinical needs.

Relation to prior work and state of the art

Early referral heuristics such as the 60/60 and 75/40 rules remain widely used because they are simple and scalable, but they yield only a binary yes/no output and are not adaptable to evolving candidacy criteria.8,9 In our held-out test set, these benchmarks achieved PPV 65.9%/sensitivity 84.1% (60/60) and PPV 71.4%/sensitivity 94.1% (75/40). By contrast, our combined AzBio+CNC model achieved PPV 80.2% with sensitivity 90.4% at thresholds CNC 50/AzBio 60, and PPV 74.1% with sensitivity 95.7% at CNC 55/AzBio 65—demonstrating superior precision at comparable or higher sensitivity, while allowing users to tune the operating point for retrospective or prospective goals. Unlike the heuristic screens, our approach operates at the ear level, incorporates full-range audiometry (250-8000 Hz) with word and isophoneme scores, and yields probability-calibrated outputs rather than a fixed binary label.

Recent ML studies have begun to classify CI candidacy from routine audiometric inputs and to review predictive models for CI outcomes.13,14 Our work extends this literature in several ways: (1) we predict continuous AzBio and CNC scores and then threshold post hoc to support different clinical operating points without retraining; (2) we implement combined-test logic (patients must meet both AzBio and CNC criteria) and report PR-curve performance across threshold pairs; (3) we provide decile-level calibration of the “probability of qualifying,” enabling nuanced communication of referral likelihood; (4) we quantify test-retest variability in a key predictor (isophonemes), clarifying how input noise bounds attainable accuracy; and (5) we estimate real-world referral volume by applying the models to a large audiogram-only cohort (n = 50 700), which prior reports have not, to our knowledge, operationalized.

Complementary to candidacy screening, a parallel body of work focuses on postimplant outcome prediction (eg, speech performance after CI) using demographic, audiometric, or device-related variables.14 Our contribution targets an earlier decision point, who should be formally evaluated, and is designed for workflow integration (retrospective outreach and prospective decision support). In this way, our model can sit upstream of outcome-prediction tools and downstream of heuristic screens, offering an adaptable, probability-aware layer that aligns with programmatic candidacy criteria.

Importance of problem-specific metrics

Choosing metrics aligned with clinical priorities was essential to ensure the model’s utility. Retrospective use cases prioritized high PPV to identify candidates with the greatest likelihood of meeting CI criteria, while prospective applications emphasized sensitivity to maximize the capture of eligible candidates during routine screening. By combining metrics for AzBio and CNC tests, we ensured alignment with clinical workflows and optimized thresholds to balance competing priorities. Tailoring metrics and thresholds provided flexibility to address diverse clinical goals, enhancing the model’s real-world applicability.

Addressing input data variability

Variability in input data, particularly test-retest variability in audiometric assessments, posed a significant challenge. This variability, stemming from differences in test conditions, patient responses, and clinical practices, was particularly impactful for borderline scores. Mitigating this variability required understanding its effects and incorporating strategies such as CIs and repeated testing. By addressing these limitations, the models remained robust and reliable, serving as supportive tools rather than definitive determinants for CI candidacy.

Challenges and limitations

This study has limitations that must be acknowledged. Test-retest variability in predictors and outcomes could influence model accuracy, especially for mid-range scores. Additionally, the assumption of independence between AzBio and CNC predictions may not fully reflect their correlation, potentially affecting combined probabilities. Dataset biases, such as the inclusion of observations from ears that were not hearing impaired, and the single-institution nature of the study may limit generalizability. External validation in diverse populations and clinical settings is necessary to confirm the models’ utility and robustness.

Conclusion

This study illustrates how methodological refinements can bridge the gap between AI model development and clinical implementation. By reformulating regression models, tailoring metrics, and addressing input variability, we developed a tool for CI eligibility that is both scalable and clinically relevant. These insights extend beyond hearing loss, offering a framework for translating AI models into practice across various health-care domains. By addressing common barriers to AI adoption, this case study underscores the potential of ML models to improve clinical workflows, enhance patient outcomes, and drive innovation in health care.
