Novel readiness index for climate change adaptation in Québec
Authors: Terence Epule Epule, Marco Alberto Nanfouet, Morad Sarkouh, Sambiani D Y Tindjiete, Changhui Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research develops and applies a novel Readiness Index for climate change adaptation in Québec’s agricultural sector. The Readiness Index comprises the adaptive capacity score and the climate score. To derive the adaptive capacity score, literacy and poverty rates for six regions of Québec were obtained from Statistics Québec (ISQ) and Québec’s Ministry of Agriculture (MAPAQ). The climate score data on annual precipitation, annual temperature and freezing degree days from the CMIP6 shared socioeconomic pathways (SSP3-7) were sourced from Ouranos. All data spanned across 1991–2024. The climate data underwent normalisation to render the climate variables comparable and easily integrated into the model. An interpolation technique was employed to estimate the missing adaptive capacity data (literacy and poverty rates). The Readiness Index combines normalised climatic and adaptive capacity data. The results indicate disparities among regions, with southern or central and more urbanised areas demonstrating higher readiness levels due to greater access to economic resources, higher climate literacy, better infrastructure, institutional support and diversified economies, in contrast to the northern and resource-dependent or peripheral regions.Keywords: Readiness index, Climate change adaptation, Precipitation, Temperature, Growing degree days, Adaptive capacity, QuébecSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Climate change presents complex challenges for agriculture, particularly in regions with diverse ecological and socio-economic contexts such as Québec. As weather patterns shift, the sector faces heightened risks from droughts, floods, pest outbreaks and changing growing seasons1.2. These impacts threaten food security, economic stability and rural livelihoods. Adaptive capacity—the ability to adjust practices, processes and structures to reduce potential harm or take advantage of emerging opportunities—is critical for resilience in the face of climate variability and extremes1.

While adaptation strategies are widely discussed in climate policy and research, less attention has been paid to systematically evaluating regional readiness for adaptation3–7. Existing frameworks often lack localised indicators or fail to capture the interplay between economic, institutional and environmental dimensions. There is a pressing need for tools that assess readiness at a finer scale, enabling policymakers to identify vulnerable areas and target resources effectively7.

A region’s readiness for climate change adaptation is influenced by climatic variables as well as its adaptive capacity. While climatic variables can be easily defined, measuring adaptive capacity is certainly difficult to intrinsically measure. This study adopts a proxy-based approach to quantify and measure the adaptive capacity of a region, which depends on a significant set of variables8. In North America, and particularly in most regions of Québec, proxies of adaptive capacity such as literacy and poverty rates provide insights into how adaptive capacity can be better understood. These variables are two of the most important proxies of adaptive capacity across Québec, as they likely impact all other sectors9–12.

To the best of our knowledge, there are currently no studies that have evaluated readiness for climate change adaptation based on climatic and adaptive capacity-related datasets in Québec. The only related studies include6 who developed a readiness index for climate change in Africa; Ford and King13, who developed a framework for assessing climate change adaptation, with adaptation readiness capturing adaptive capacity and demonstrating the strength and existence of governance, democracy and policy structures that influence whether adaptation occurs; Khan and Amelie14, who explored adaptation readiness in Seychelles based on governance frameworks; Tilleard and Ford15, who captured adaptation potential through adaptive capacity and adaptation preparedness or readiness; Lwasa16, who studied the role of institutional readiness and community resilience in climate change adaptation in Kampala, Uganda; and Nhamo4, who explored the concept of readiness regarding adopting a green economy in South Africa, noting that readiness is often interchanged with preparedness. Budreau and McBean17 examined climate change in the context of policy and adaptive capacity in the Canadian North. Readiness is defined as a complex interaction of parameters and conditionalities required to prepare a community against potential climate risks, thereby enhancing adaptations. Key readiness parameters for adopting a green economy include a high level of commitment from stakeholders, enhanced institutional setups, the development of necessary legislation, funding mechanisms and the implementation of on-the-ground programs. These conditions create the right environment to drive preparedness for climate change adaptation6.

This research introduces a Readiness Index to evaluate the preparedness of Québec’s farmers for climate change adaptation. Drawing on interdisciplinary literature and diverse datasets, the index incorporates climatic and socio-economic indicators. It enables a comparative assessment of regional strengths and weaknesses, supporting equitable and effective adaptation planning. The objectives are: (1) to construct a theoretically grounded, empirically robust Readiness Index for Québec’s agricultural sector; and (2) to identify spatial patterns and disparities in readiness across selected central and peripheral regions. In doing so, the study contributes to climate adaptation metrics and offers practical insights for strengthening agricultural resilience in Québec.

Results

Recent climate trends across Québec

This research assesses the readiness of six regions across Québec for climate change adaptation. To evaluate the province’s readiness, this research explores historical time series data for various climate trends. When considering recent trends in annual total liquid and solid precipitation across Québec, it is evident that from 1991 to 2024, total liquid and solid precipitation has increased in most regions of Québec (Fig. 1a). Regarding regional precipitation levels, three regions record the highest levels of annual liquid and solid precipitation in Québec. In order of magnitude, the regions with the highest annual total liquid and solid precipitation are Estrie, Centre du Québec and Saguenay-Lac-Saint-Jean. Interestingly, among the regions with the highest annual total liquid and solid precipitation, the majority are central regions (Estrie and Centre du Québec), while one peripheral region records the third highest annual total liquid and solid precipitation. The regions with the lowest total annual liquid and solid precipitation, in order of magnitude, are Montérégie, Outaouais and Abitibi-Témiscamingue. Among these regions with the lowest levels of annual total solid and liquid precipitation, Montérégie, a central region, leads, while this category is mainly dominated by peripheral regions (Outaouais and Abitibi-Témiscamingue) (Fig. 1a). These findings suggest, the central regions of Québec record higher levels of annual total liquid and solid precipitation compared to peripheral regions.

Recent climate trends for (a) annual total precipitation, (b) annual temperature and (c) freezing degree days.

Considering average annual temperature across Québec, this research observes that the average annual temperature has risen from about − 3 °C in 1991 to approximately − 1.5 °C in 2024; however, temperatures remain generally negative throughout the time series. Regionally, this research has observed that the following central regions record the highest average annual temperatures, in order of magnitude: Montérégie, Centre du Québec and Estrie. Conversely, the regions with the lowest average annual temperatures, in order of magnitude, are peripheral regions, including Saguenay-Lac-Saint-Jean, Abitibi-Témiscamingue and Outaouais. An interesting observation here is that the peripheral regions record the lowest average annual temperatures, while the central regions record the highest temperatures (Fig. 1b).

When considering annual freezing degree days, we observed that freezing degree days have been consistently declining from 1991 to 2024. Regionally, the peripheral regions still record the highest number of freezing degree days, though declining in order of magnitude, as follow (Saguenay-Lac-Saint-Jean, Abitibi-Témiscamingue and Outaouais). Conversely, the central regions record the lowest number of freezing degree days, as detailed below: Centre du Québec, Estrie and Montérégie) (Fig. 1c). This reduction in freezing degree days indicates the reality of climate change across Québec and is consistent with previous observations of consistently increasing temperatures.

Recent trends in poverty and literacy rates across Québec

Regarding annual income poverty rates, it is evident that income poverty rates are generally declining from 1991 to 2024, as depicted by increased gross family incomes across the province (Fig. 2a). Regionally, although income poverty rates are declining, the peripheral regions still record the highest poverty rates, in order of magnitude, as detailed below (Saguenay-Lac-Saint-Jean and Abitibi-Témiscamingue) (Fig. 2a). The regions with the lowest poverty rates are primarily central regions except for Outaouais; they are Centre du Québec, Estrie and Montérégie (Fig. 2a). Regarding literacy rates, the data demonstrate that literacy rates are rising due to increased climate literacy. Again, the central regions record the highest literacy rates, in order of magnitude, except for Outaouais, as detailed below: Centre du Québec and Montérégie. The lowest literacy rates are recorded in peripheral regions, except for Estrie, such as Saguenay-Lac-Saint-Jean and Abitibi-Témiscamingue) (Fig. 2b).

Readiness for climate change adaptation in Québec

The readiness for climate change adaptation across Québec and selected regions was assessed using a newly parameterised measure, the Readiness Index. Findings indicate that the central regions record the highest readiness scores in the province. Centre-du-Québec ranks first with a score of 0.581, followed by Montérégie at 0.578 and Estrie at 0.573. By contrast, peripheral regions record lower scores: Outaouais leads among them with 0.572, followed by Abitibi-Témiscamingue (0.570) and Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean (0.568) (Fig. 3a).

Spatial pattern of (a) poverty rates, (b) literacy rates and (c) readiness indices across Québec.

Furthermore, notably, the central regions with the highest readiness indices also record the lowest poverty rates (Fig. 3b) and the highest literacy rates (Fig. 3c). For example, Centre du Quebec, which registers the highest readiness index of 0.581, also registers the second lowest poverty rate of 15.32 and the highest literacy rate of 53.32 (Fig. 3a, b). Montérégie, which registers the second highest readiness index of 0.578, has the lowest poverty rate of 15.23 and the second highest literacy rate of 52.96 (Fig. 3a, b). Similarly, Estrie which registers the third largest readiness index of 0.573, also registers the highest poverty rate of 15.81 and the lowest literacy rate of 48.26 (Fig. 3a, b). Among the peripheral regions, Outaouais achieves the highest readiness index (0.572), the lowest poverty rate (15.55) and the highest literacy rate (49.32). This is followed by Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean (0.568) and Abitibi-Témiscamingue (0.570), both with relatively lower readiness indices (Fig. 3a, b). Overall, regions with higher readiness scores tend to have higher literacy and lower poverty rates, while those with lower readiness scores show the reverse. In summary, the three central regions—Centre-du-Québec, Montérégie and Estrie—are better prepared for climate change adaptation than the three peripheral regions—Outaouais, Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean and Abitibi-Témiscamingue. Centre-du-Québec emerges as the most prepared region, with the highest readiness index, the highest literacy rate and the second-lowest poverty rate. However, with a readiness score of 0.581 on a scale from − 1 to 1, overall preparedness remains at roughly 50% of the expected capacity. Québec, therefore, can be considered to have an average level of readiness for climate change adaptation.

Scattergrams of the relationship between poverty and literacy rates vs. readiness index

The readiness index for climate change adaptation appears to be primarily driven by the adaptive capacity components of the index. For example, when poverty rates increase, there is a corresponding decrease in the readiness index, indicating an inverse relationship between poverty rates and the readiness index. With an R² of 0.6973 (above average), it can be stated that about 69% of the changes in the readiness index can be explained by its linear relationship with poverty rates (Fig. 4a). Conversely, the literacy rate has a direct relationship with the readiness index, meaning that as the literacy rate increases, so does the readiness index. With an R² of 0.8642 (high), it can be concluded that about 86% of the changes in the readiness index can be explained by its linear relationship with literacy rates (Fig. 4b). Additionally, poverty and literacy rates exhibit an inverse relationship; when the poverty rate increases, the literacy rate decreases, and vice versa (Fig. 4c). With and R2 of 0.6504 between poverty and literacy rates, it can be stated that approximately 65% of the change in literacy rates can be explained by their linear relationship with poverty rates (Fig. 4c).

Scattergrams of (a) poverty rate vs. readiness index, (b) literacy rate vs. readiness index and (c) poverty rate vs. literacy rate.

Scattergrams of the relationship between climate variables vs. readiness index

Analysis of the relationship between climatic variables and the readiness index indicates that climatic factors exert a weaker influence than adaptive capacity components. For example, average annual precipitation shows an inverse relationship with the readiness index—higher precipitation is associated with lower readiness—yet the relationship is extremely weak, with an R² of 0.0039 (Fig. 5a). Similarly, average annual temperature also exhibits an inverse relationship, with a higher mean temperature corresponding to lower readiness and an equally weak R² of 0.0149 (Fig. 5b). In contrast, mean freezing degree days have a positive relationship with the readiness index, whereby an increase in freezing degree days is associated with higher readiness. However, the R² value of 0.0482 still reflects a weak coefficient of determination (Fig. 5c). Overall, these results demonstrate that climatic variables have only a weak association with the readiness index, particularly when compared with the stronger relationships observed between adaptive capacity variables and readiness.

Discussion

Regarding climatic factors, the central regions of Québec record the highest levels of precipitation and temperatures. Conversely, the peripheral regions record the highest number of freezing degree days, as depicted by their relatively lower temperatures and frequent cold days. These results are consistent with previous studies. For example, a comparison of weather observation stations in southern and northern Canada found that the number of summer days when the maximum temperature exceeds 25 °C has increased at most locations south of 65°N, and the number of hot days with daily maximum temperatures above 22 °C have increased at several weather observation stations in southern Canada18. Additionally, regarding precipitation, an increase in the number of days with precipitation and heavy precipitation has been observed at various locations in southern Canada18. The finding that the number of freezing degree days is higher in the peripheral regions resonates with previous findings across Canada, which argue that the number of days with heavy snowfall has increased in northern stations18. This is parallel to the observed decrease in snow depth over much of southern Québec, a phenomenon consistent with decreasing freezing degree days in southern Québec19.

Similarly, a recent study analysed standardised precipitation (SPI) across three peripheral and three central regions of Québec. The results showed that the peripheral regions recorded below-zero SPI results (Abitibi-Témiscamingue − 0.48, Saguenay-Lac-Saint-Jean − 0.14 and Outaouais − 0.10) and more yield gap years, indicating more frequent droughts, while the southern/central regions recorded positive SPI (Estrie: 1.17, Centre du Québec: 0.86 and Montérégie: 0.33) and fewer years with yield gaps, indicating less frequent droughts. This depicts the existence of a south-north gradient in SPI variations and yield gaps. The SPI and yield gaps can be explained by prolonged winters in northern Québec and recent winters without sufficient snowpack. The latter drives and enhances snowmelt and shortens the growing season for crops9. Although the peripheral regions usually have high snowpacks, recent climate stressors have reduced winter snowpack, triggering more frequent droughts in these regions. The aforementioned climate stressors and most especially precipitation create significant uncertainty for agriculture. This situation is daunting, as most agricultural production across Canada and Québec is contingent on the availability of liquid precipitation from direct rainfall and snowmelt20,21. There is evidence that crop yields in Québec are sensitive to climate conditions12,22, and rapid snowmelt due to below-average snowpack and the warmest spring temperatures in over 80 years have been recorded due to recurrent droughts.

Regarding adaptive capacity, these results indicate that the peripheral regions have higher poverty and lower literacy rates. These trends in poverty and literacy rates are consistent with the readiness index results, which show that peripheral regions record lower readiness indices when compared to central regions. These findings align with previous research illustrating that peripheral regions of Québec generally exhibit lower literacy and higher poverty rates than southern or central regions10. Research has shown that the low literacy and high poverty rates recorded in peripheral regions are linked to higher levels of vulnerability in agricultural systems compared to central regions. From these results, it can be concluded that readiness is impacted by all three indicators used (precipitation, temperature and freezing degree days) and adaptive capacity. However, the proxies of adaptive capacity (poverty and literacy rates) exert a more impact in the vulnerability equation, as depicted by higher coefficients of determination between poverty and literacy rates as independent variables and the readiness index as the dependent variable. This contrasts with the relationship (coefficient of determination) between climatic variables (independent variables) and the readiness index (dependent variable). These perspectives underscore the fact that low levels of literacy often correlate with lower access to information related to climate, particularly regarding potential changes in growing season dates, high-yielding and drought-tolerant varieties, or a general decline in access to services or organisations that offer support to enhance resilience and reduce vulnerability. These salient observations highlight that readiness for climate change adaptation is primarily driven by adaptive capacity and its proxies (literacy and poverty rates) rather than magnitude of the climate stressors. Therefore, it is not magnitude of the climate stressors that determines the level of readiness. A relatively low magnitude and impact drought, for example may result in significantly larger ravages if adaptive capacity is low, compared to another location facing a higher magnitude climate stressor that may experience less damage due to enhanced adaptive capacity23,24. The dominance of adaptive capacity as a driver of the readiness index has strong policy and practical implications across Québec. For instance, policies such as the Plan for a Green Economy (PEV2030) and the Québec Plan for Sustainable Development should prompt policymakers to recognise that the first step in encouraging effective adaptation involves assessing the socio-economic and demographic conditions of the farmers concerned. Consequently, when the socio-economic situation of farmers is improved, their capacity to adapt to climate stressors is enhanced.

Across Québec, demographic weight has been proposed and used to explain differences between peripheral and central regions in the context of transportation infrastructure. One study reported higher demographic weights in some central regions, notably Montreal (24.4%) and Montérégie (18.4%), while the lowest demographic weights were recorded in peripheral regions such as Gaspésie (1.1%) and Abitibi-Témiscamingue (1.8%).25 Central regions with the highest demographic weights and the densest transportation networks were also linked to greater accessibility to information that can enhance climate literacy, facilitate access to agricultural revenue security programmes and improve access to extension and information services25. Financial resources also play an important role in shaping readiness for climate change adaptation, influencing awareness of strategies and resources that support adaptive capacity. There is a positive correlation, with a high coefficient of determination, between access to resources and adaptive capacity26. Greater access to financial resources improves the availability of knowledge to address climate stressors such as drought, and further facilitates access to climate-related information—a key driver of adaptive capacity. Knowledge in this context, closely tied to climate literacy, determines awareness of potential resilience-building instruments or services10. In adaptive capacity research, knowledge, climate literacy, or the basic ability to read and understand climate-related information are recognised as essential for strengthening adaptive capacity. Determinants of climate literacy or awareness include formal and informal education, mobility, poverty, gender and location6. Li et al.27 argued that, at the global scale, educational attainment is an important predictor of climate awareness and, consequently, readiness. Across several remote communities in Canada, climate literacy is often low, despite the fact that these communities live daily with climate hazards17,28–30.

Conclusion

The main findings from this research show that the central regions of Québec are better prepared for climate change adaptation due to their higher literacy and lower poverty rates. The adaptive capacity score of the readiness index appears to have a greater influence on overall readiness than the climate score. This is because, regardless of the strength of climate forcing, the pattern of readiness will ultimately be shaped by the socio-demographic conditions of the population7. This study highlights the need to improve literacy by increasing access to climate-related information and training. The two proxies for adaptive capacity used in this research are among many possible measures, but they have broad relevance as they affect other indicators of adaptive capacity. The main challenge was identifying the most important proxies, as adaptive capacity can be represented by a wide range of variables. A literature review was used to select proxies that intersect with multiple others. Literacy and poverty rates are key because they influence factors such as governance and policy frameworks, leadership mechanisms, access to technology, funding and stakeholder engagement. A limitation of this approach is its reliance on three main climatic variables—precipitation, temperature and freezing degree days. Other potentially important climatic factors for agriculture in Québec, such as relative humidity and wind speed, were excluded due to data limitations. Furthermore, the index is context-specific; its application elsewhere would require local benchmarks to ensure the data reflect the realities of the study site. Future research could extend this index to more regions of Québec and other Canadian provinces to compare resilience and readiness for climate change adaptation at a broader scale. At both provincial and national levels, this work underscores the importance of considering socio-economic conditions when developing adaptation plans. For example, under the federal zero-carbon policy, farmers with high poverty rates and low literacy may be unable to access adaptation measures. A better understanding of these socio-economic realities can enable policymakers to tailor climate actions and mitigation strategies accordingly. From a research perspective, climate models should also work to integrate socio-economic conditions to produce more representative and practical outcomes.

Methods

Data collection

To evaluate the readiness of regions across Québec for climate change adaptation, this research first identified six regions—three peripheral and three central—classified as follows: peripheral regions (Estrie, Centre-du-Québec and Montérégie) and central regions (Abitibi-Témiscamingue, Outaouais, and Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean). The regional classification follows the administrative boundaries defined by the Government of Quebec. The distinction between peripheral and central regions is primarily geographical: peripheral regions are located farther from major centres in the south, such as Montreal and Quebec City, while central regions are situated in the south or in proximity to these urban hubs.

To create the climate score, three climate datasets were collected. These include annual total solid and liquid precipitation (mm) for the province of Québec and for each of the six regions identified above. This was followed by average annual temperature (°C) and annual freezing degree days (°C). All data were sourced from Ouranos31: https://portraits.ouranos.ca/en/temporal?a=0&e=CMIP6&i=tg_mean&r=qc000&s=annual&w=0) for the period of 1991–2024. These datasets are based on CMIP6 simulations and the shared socio-economic pathways SSP3-7, which represents an intermediate scenario between the moderate (SSP2-4.5) and high (SSP5-8.5) scenarios (Fig. 6). Data were accessed via the “Platforms and Data” section of the website, under ‘Climate Portraits’, where three visualisation options were available: maps, time series (available as Excel files) and summaries.

Schematic view of the methodology used in fitting the new readiness index.

For the adaptive capacity score, data were collected for two proxies: poverty rates and literacy rates, covering the period 1991–2024. Adaptive capacity refers to the ability to cope with climate stressors such as droughts, floods, heatwaves, or other climate shocks30. Regions with higher adaptive capacities typically demonstrate greater readiness for climate change adaptation. The two proxies selected are critical because they influence all other potential indicators of adaptive capacity. Literacy rates reflect the ability of individuals to read and understand climate information, enabling appropriate responses to climate events. Poverty rates, measured here using agricultural household annual income, indicate the ability to adjust to climate shocks by maintaining alternative livelihoods. Higher poverty rates are associated with lower adaptive capacity. These two variables were selected because they are significant determinants of adaptive capacity, often influencing other indicators such as governance, policy regimes and access to technology30. Data on literacy and poverty rates for 1991–2024 were obtained from the profile sectoriel de l’industrie bioalimentaire au Québec, published by the Institut de la statistique du Québec32 (Fig. 6). Additional data were sourced from the Ministère de l’Agriculture, des Pêcheries et de l’Alimentation33, profile régionale de l’industrie bioalimentaire au Québec (https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/adm/min/agriculture-pecheries-alimentation/agriculture/industrie-agricole/regions/FS_profilregionalbioalimentaire_complet_MAPAQ.pdf).

Readiness index

After processing the literacy and poverty rates data through linear interpolation to determine the missing data, the climate data were subjected to normalisation to facilitate comparison and integration into the same algorithm. Following the above curation procedures, all the data were transformed into the appropriate format for integration into the readiness index equation. This index formally integrates the climate score and the adaptive capacity score into a unique algorithm. The downside of this index is that the adaptive capacity score relies on two proxies: literacy and poverty rates. This is due to the inherent difficulty in measuring adaptive capacity as there exists a ubiquitous number of potential proxies. However, the use of literacy and poverty rates in this research is highly representative, as these proxies of adaptive capacity impact all other proxies and thus provide a comprehensive overview. Existing literature argues that poverty and literacy rates tend to be connected to most socio-demographic variables, thereby providing better insights into adaptive capacity (Epule et al. 2024). Additionally, these are antagonistic variables, meaning that when one increases, the other decreases, and vice versa, making them suitable for computing adaptive capacity. The readiness index is scored on a scale ranging from − 1 to 1; the higher the score, the greater the region’s readiness for climate change adaptation, and vice versa.
