Datura metel and Alcohol Cocktail Potentiates Hepatic Encephalopathy by Exacerbating Impairment via the Hepato-Prefrontal Cortical Axis
Authors: Ephraim Samuel Jen, Adeshina Oloruntoba Adekeye, Mary Oluwatobiloba Adedigba, Edem Ekpeyong Edem, Musa Samaila Chiroma, Martha Orendu Oche Attah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesSubstance abuse among adolescents is a major concern not only in developing countries but also worldwide. Datura metel (D. metel), commonly known as Datura or Devil’s Trumpet, is a plant containing several toxic compounds, including alkaloids (such as scopolamine, hyoscyamine, and atropine). However, consuming D. metel or its derivatives can significantly impact the brain, though its mechanism remains elusive. Therefore, this study evaluated the effects of D. metel and alcohol cocktail on the hepato-prefrontal-cortical axis.MethodsThirty-five male Sprague Dawley rats were divided randomly into five groups (A–E). Group A received distilled water, Group B received 300 mg/kg of D. metel, and Group C received 700 mg/kg of alcohol. Group D received 300 mg/kg of D. metel + 700 mg/kg of alcohol, while Group E received 150 mg/kg of D. metel + 350 mg/kg of alcohol. Tissues from the liver and prefrontal cortex (PFC) were collected for morphological, histological, and immunohistochemical studies.ResultsThere was a significant increase in body weight in control group A compared to other experimental groups. Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed a mild attenuation in the cytoarchitecture of the liver and PFC with increased necrosis, apoptosis, vacuolation, and neuronal death in experimental groups compared to control group A. Increased microglial activity was observed in groups B–E, whereas group A showed normal neuronal arrangement with the NeuN antibody compared to experimental groups. Serum liver function biomarkers (alkaline phosphatase, alanine transaminase, and aspartate aminotransferase) were statistically significantly higher in Groups C and D when compared to Group A.ConclusionCombined D. metel and alcohol negatively impact the liver and PFC of experimental rats.

INTRODUCTION

Substance abuse among adolescents is a significant global health concern [1] and is increasingly becoming a societal problem, putting our youth at increased risk [2, 3]. Psychoactive substances such as Datura metel (D. metel) and alcohol cocktails among rural adolescents have raised global public concern [4]. Substance abuse and addiction affect all socioeconomic, cultural, religious, and ethnic boundaries. Nigeria, the most populous country in Africa, has earned a negative reputation as a hub for drug abuse, particularly among adolescents [5]. Commonly abused substances include alcohol, tobacco, and illicit drugs such as cannabis, benzodiazepines, cocaine, and opioids, especially when compared to other low and middle-income countries [3].

Drug abusers face serious health risks and are more prone to criminal activity and diseases such as HIV/AIDS due to their behaviors [3, 6]. Some adolescents try them, stop using them, or use them occasionally without negative side effects. Others develop drug addictions that drive them to use more harmful substances, putting their loved ones and others at risk [7]. Several studies have shown various consequences of substance misuse, including liver cirrhosis, gastric ulcers, hypertension, neurological disorders, and tuberculosis [8, 9]. Other mental problems include amnesia, dementia, nervous system deficits, delayed motor development, and retardation [10]. Drug addiction and abuse have severe effects, prompting concern from both national and international organizations regarding its rise among youths [11].

D. metel (Linn.), also known as the Jimson weed in English, Zakami in Hausa, and Gegemu in Yoruba, has spreading branches. It is a perennial herbaceous plant in the Solanaceae family, and the fruit is shaped like a capsule and is protected by tiny spines [12]. Despite its medicinal importance, its tendency to create hallucinations has led to widespread usage among Nigerians, particularly among youths [13]. The plant is easily accessible as a growing weed in many parts of tropical regions such as Nigeria. People add the seed decoction to their drinks to feel high. Drug users often substitute it for drugs because it is accessible, available, and not legally restricted [14]. The effects of D. metel on the liver and certain brain regions, including the frontal cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellar cortex, have been widely studied [13-15].

Alcohol abuse is the most common issue across all age groups [16]. In 2015, the National Survey on Drug Use and Health reported 138 million regular drinkers in the U.S., with nearly half exhibiting problem drinking and 15.7 million having an alcohol use disorder (AUD) [16, 17]. In comparison, only 64 million respondents reported using cigarettes in the previous month, 22 million reported using marijuana in the previous month, and fewer than 13 million reported using other illicit drugs. An additional 2.7 million individuals reported substance use disorder and AUD [17]. Alcohol exposure is a common etiological component in various disorders because of the high rates of problem drinking. Recent years have seen increased studies on alcohol's harmful health effects [18]. Data shows that even moderate alcohol consumption leads to over 60 acute and long-term medical issues [19]. Additionally, studies have revealed that alcohol consumption is linked to over 200 diseases, with the pathogenicity and fatality of chronic illnesses depending on the quantity, quality, and patterns of alcohol use [20]. Hence, this study investigated the effects of D. metel and alcohol cocktail on the hepato-prefrontal-cortical axis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Plant collection and identification

The seeds of D. metel were obtained from ATC, Kufai, Jalingo, Taraba State, Nigeria, and were identified and authenticated at the Herbarium of the Plant Science and Biotechnology, Ekiti State University AdoEkiti, with herbarium no. UHAE2022016 given.

2. Experimental animals

Thirty-five Sprague Dawley rats weighing 140-180 grams were purchased from Showgold Laboratory Rats Oye-Ekiti. They were housed in well-ventilated cages at room temperature in hygienic conditions under a natural light (12 hours) and dark (12 hours) schedule with food and water made ad libitum.

3. Ethical considerations

The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the National Institute of Health for the care and maintenance of laboratory animals as suggested by the Declaration of Helsinki, and the approval of protocol (ABUADERC/25/2022) was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Review Board of Afe Babalola University.

4. Treatment protocol

Following a week of acclimatization, the animals were randomly assigned into five groups, namely: Group A (CTRL) received distilled water, Group B (DAT) received 10% (300 mg/kg of D. metel), Group C (ALC) received 10% (700 mg/kg of alcohol), Group D (10% DAT + ALC) received 10% (300 mg/kg of D. metel + 700 mg/kg of alcohol), and Group E (5% DAT + ALC) received 5% (150 mg/kg of D. metel + 350 mg/kg of alcohol). The administration lasted for 14 days. The dosing regimen was determined based on a previous study [14, 15].

5. Sacrifice of animals

The animals were humanely sacrificed after 2 weeks by administering 87 mg/kg of ketamine hydrochloride injection (Ralington Pharma LLP, India) to the right thigh of each rat [21]. A midline incision was made from the thorax to the abdominal region to expose the abdominal organs; the heart was perfused with normal buffered formalin after taking blood samples for biochemical analysis from the heart. Liver was harvested, trimmed, and analyzed histologically for microscopic images. The heads of the rats were removed and the skulls were opened to harvest the brain tissue which was also prepared for routine histological and immunohistochemical observation.

6. Immunohistochemical analysis

Sections of 5 µm thickness obtained from routine paraffin were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval by heating in a citrate-based antigen unmasking solution, pH 6.0, for 30 mins in a steamer and allowed to cool on the bench at room temperature for 30 mins. Endogenous peroxidase blocking was performed in 0.3% hydrogen peroxide in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) for 10 mins. Sections were blocked in animal-free serum and blocker for 20 mins. Sections were then incubated at room temperature for 2 hours in primary rabbit antibodies: ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule (IBA1) at 1:2,500 and neuronal nuclear antibody (NeuN) at 1:1,500. Sections were washed in PBS and incubated in ImmPRESSTM Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP) Anti-Rabbit IgG (Peroxidase) Polymer Reagent, made in horse (Vector Labs, USA). Color was developed with the Diaminobenzidine HRP Substrate Kit, and sections were counter-stained in hematoxylin.

7. Macrometric studies

Animal organs were weighed in grams with a weighing scale (EK5350) prior to the experiment and before sacrifice. Initial and final body weights were compared to assess weight gain [22].

8. Photomicrography of processed tissues

An Olympus binocular microscope (Evident, Hamburg, Germany) and a 5.1-megapixel MV550 research camera (Shenzhen Hifly Technology Company Limited, China) were used. These were linked to a personal computer with image-capturing and analyzing software. Quantitative immunohistochemical analysis was performed using the ImageJ app (version 1.8.0_345) to analyze and quantify the number of neurons and glial cells present in each group after the experiment; furthermore, the values obtained were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0).

9. Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Significant statistical difference was considered at p < 0.05. GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0) was used for graphical representations, and values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

RESULTS

1. Combined effect of D. metel and alcohol cocktail on body weight of exposed rats

There was an increase in body weight observed in Groups A and B, though significant weight gain was recorded in Group A, and significant weight loss was recorded in Groups C (**p < 0.01) and D (**p < 0.01) compared to Group A (Fig. 1).

2. Behavioral studies

1) Y-Maze test

Group A showed a high level of right arm entry (alternation), while Groups B, C, D, and E demonstrated a significant reduction in the levels of right arm entry, which was lowest in Group D. Furthermore, a comparison between groups using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test showed a decrease in % alternation in Groups B (*p < 0.05), C (*p < 0.05), D (****p < 0.0001), and E (***p < 0.001) compared to Group A (Fig. 2A).

2) Novel object recognition (NOR) test

Animals in Group A showed a high memory index compared to animals in Groups B, C, D, and E. Additionally, the lowest memory index was recorded in Group D. A comparison between groups using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test showed a decrease in % alternation in Groups B (*p < 0.05), C (*p < 0.05), D (***p < 0.001), and E (**p < 0.01) compared to Group A (Fig. 2B).

3. Combined effect of D. metel and alcohol cocktail on activity of serum liver function: alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine transaminase (ALT)

1) ALP

Group A showed normal levels of ALP, while Groups B, C, D, and E showed a significant increase in ALP levels, which was the highest in Group D. Additionally, a statistically significant difference was observed in Group A compared to Groups B (**p < 0.01), C (**p < 0.01), D (****p < 0.0001), and E (**p < 0.01).

2) AST

A significant difference was observed in AST levels across Groups B, C, D, and E compared to Group A. However, the increase was higher in Group D. Additionally, a statistically significant difference was observed in Group A compared to Groups B (*p < 0.05), C (**p < 0.01), D (****p < 0.0001), and E (**p < 0.01).

3) ALT

A significant difference was observed in ALT levels in Groups B, C, D, and E compared to Group A. Additionally, the increase was higher in Group D. A statistically significant difference was observed in Group A compared to Groups B (**p < 0.01), C (***p < 0.001), D (****p < 0.0001), and E (***p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

4. Histological analyses

1) Histological observation of liver sections through hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Liver sections from Group A displayed normal hepatic structure with healthy hepatocytes, clear sinusoids, and central veins, though mild vacuolation was noted. Liver sections from Group B showed areas of necrosis and intense vacuolation, as well as hepatocytes with pyknotic nuclei. Liver sections from Group C showed areas of necrosis and intense vacuolation, alongside hepatocytes with pyknotic nuclei and areas of vacuolation (suggestive of fat accumulation). Liver sections from Group D showed histology comparable to Groups B and C, though there was more prominent vacuolation and necrosis. Group E showed liver sections with histology comparable to that of Groups B and C (Fig. 4).

2) Histological observation of liver sections with Masson’s trichrome staining

Liver sections from Group A showed normal hepatic structure of hepatocytes having normal collagen fibers. Liver sections from Group B showed areas of more collagen fibers around central veins and in between hepatocytes. Furthermore, liver sections from Groups C, D, and E showed collagen fiber dispersions comparable to Group B (Fig. 5).

3) Histological observation of prefrontal cortex (PFC) using H&E staining

PFC sections from Group A showed normal distribution of pyramidal cells and their structure. PFC sections from Group B showed a reduced number of pyramidal cells, increased vacuolation, and necrosis. PFC sections from Groups C, D, and E showed similar observations comparable to Group B, though a reduced number of pyramidal cells, increased vacuolation, and necrosis were more prominent in Group D (Fig. 6).

4) Immunohistochemical observation of PFC using NeuN antibody

PFC sections from Group A showed normal distribution or viable neurons. PFC sections from Group B showed a reduced number of neurons. PFC sections from Groups C, D, and E showed similar observations to Group B, though a reduced number of neurons was more prominent in Group D (Fig. 7).

5) Immunohistochemical observation of PFC using IBA1 antibody

PFC sections from Group A showed microglia cells at the resting phase or inactivated microglia. PFC sections from Group B showed activated microglia with various shapes and structures. PFC sections of Groups C, D, and E showed similar observations to Group B, though the number of activated microglia was more prominent in Group D (Fig. 8).

DISCUSSION

Substance abuse in adolescents is a significant issue globally, affecting both developing and developed countries. D. metel, commonly known as Devil’s trumpet or thornapple, is an annual medicinal herb of the temperate zone distributed globally [23]. However, this plant contains several toxic compounds, such as tropane alkaloids, which are ignorantly consumed copiously in the local environment without the comprehensive knowledge of their effects. Consumption of D. metel or its derivatives may have profound toxic effects on the central nervous system. First-pass metabolism and its connection to the brain is crucial for diagnosing specific diseases, particularly regarding drug abuse in the population [24]. This study investigated the effects of combined D. metel seed extract and alcohol cocktail on the hepato-prefrontal-cortical axis to assess potential implications of their use.

Food intake was significantly reduced following administration of aqueous seed extract of D. metel and alcohol at the higher concentration, and since food consumption was reduced in this group, there was a statistically significant reduction in body weight observed when comparing the groups treated with ALC only and 5% DAT + ALC. Rats in the control group consistently gained weight, indicating that D. metel and alcohol may negatively impact body weight, as supported by the findings of Orisakwe et al. [25] that stated that an increase or decrease in either absolute or relative weight of an organ after administering a chemical or drug indicates its toxic effect. Additionally, histochemical analysis revealed no gross alterations in the cytoarchitecture and morphology of the liver and PFC of animals; they all appeared morphologically normal after animal sacrifice. Behavioral studies revealed that D. metel and alcohol intake can cause brain damage leading to executive, cognitive, and memory impairment in experimental animals, which aligns with the findings of Abrahao et al. [26]. In this research, spatial memory was assessed using both the Y-maze and NOR tests for both short- and long-term memory indexes. Alternations were recorded as the animals explored the different arms of the Y-maze. Statistical data showed a significant decrease in the groups with D. metel, alongside Groups B, C, D, and E when compared to Group A.

NOR tests measured the animals’ memory index by recording their total exploration time on both the old and novel objects. In this study, the memory index significantly decreased in the treatment groups when compared to groups A, B, C, D, and E. Our findings on behavioral analysis aligned with Ishola et al. [14], confirming that D. metel consumption is toxic and may cause memory deficits.

Serum liver function markers (AST, ALT, and ALP), commonly known as liver damage indicators according to Das et al. [27], significantly increased in the treatment group animals, indicating that liver damage caused the leakage of these enzymes located within hepatocytes into the bloodstream. Our results revealed that serum activities of these enzymes increased in all treatment groups when compared to the control group. Liver enzyme activities increased in all treatment groups and were highest in group D, thereby indicating that combined intake of D. metel and alcohol increased liver function biomarkers even more. This result matched those of Allen et al. [28]. The study showed distortion in the architecture of PFC of the cerebral cortex, aligning with findings from Tijani et al. [13] and Ishola and Adeniyi [15]. Furthermore, histoarchitectural changes included perineuronal vacuolations of pyramidal cells, indicating imminent cell death. These features reported in this study reported those observed by Maharazu et al. [29] and Muhammad et al. [30]. Cell death that happens pathologically or accidentally is termed necrotic, resulting from external factors or cell disturbances, including toxic or traumatic effects [31]. Many slowly progressive diseases destroy hepatocytes and distort the cytoarchitecture of the liver [32-35]. The majority of inflammatory and metabolic diseases of the liver happen due to prolonged toxic damage [36]. This may distort hepatic architecture and cause hepatocyte nodule regeneration, potentially increasing portal vein pressure and leading to portal hypertension. Liver disease can affect its functions when a significant number of hepatocytes are damaged, leading to impaired liver function [37, 38]. Cellular response to neurotoxins is a major cause of cell death, potentially leading to apoptotic death in various tissues throughout the body [39, 40]. The histological observations of the liver recorded in the present study suggest that the pattern of cell death within the liver of rats in the treatment groups (B, C, D and E) could be as a result of deleterious effect(s) of combined intake of D. metel and alcohol. The effects included vacuolation, pyknotic nuclei, distortion in sinusoidal radiation, and enlarged central vein. There was also minimal collagen between hepatocytes in the control group compared to other groups, where an increased amount of collagen fibers was deposited in the sinusoidal spaces between hepatocytes and also located around the central vein as highlighted with the use of Masson’s trichrome staining. Similar results were reported in studies carried out by [29], which demonstrated that alcoholic hepatitis is characterized by ballooning degeneration of hepatocytes, neutrophilic infiltration, and development of aggregates of insoluble proteins within hepatocytes, and central to hepatitis development is the activation of Kupffer cells and collagen deposition in the sinusoidal spaces, leading to pericellular fibrosis and, in turn, cirrhosis. The mechanism involves the instigation of the inflammatory response by resident liver macrophages (i.e., Kupffer cells). Endotoxin, or lipopolysaccharide (LPS), is a cell-wall component of Gram-negative bacteria that migrates from the gut lumen through the portal circulation to reach the liver. Increased circulating levels of endotoxin in alcoholic hepatitis are caused by alcohol-induced qualitative and quantitative changes in gut microbiota and increased gut leakiness. In the liver, LPS activates Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells by interacting with toll-like receptor 4, and these cells produce reactive oxygen species and proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines that interact with alcohol and contribute to hepatocyte damage [33, 39]. Additional factors contributing to hepatocyte damage include alcohol-induced activation of various immune cells (neutrophils, T cells, and other leukocytes), along with alcohol’s impact on adipose tissue, leading to the production of damage-associated molecular patterns [39].

Histological findings from this study showed distortion in the architecture of PFC, which matches the observations of Tijani et al. [13] and Ishola et al. [14]. The histoarchitectural changes included perineuronal vacuolations of pyramidal cells, indicating imminent cell death. These features reported herein are similar to the findings of histoarchitectural changes observed by Muhammad et al. [30]. Cell death, whether pathological or accidental, is termed necrotic and may arise from external factors or cellular disturbances, including toxic or traumatic effects.

This study’s histological observations of PFC may reflect liver cell responses to neurotoxin-induced damage. Hepatocyte damage is a major cause of apoptotic death [40].

Herein, the NeuN marker was used to assess the number of viable and nonviable neurons present in each group to compare different groups of rats. Furthermore, NeuN and IBA1 marker were used. NeuN is a neuronal nuclear antigen commonly used as a biomarker for neurons in immunochemical assays. The IBA1 marker was used to assess for microglial activation in the nervous tissue. Microglia are immune cells resident in the brain and when activated, in response to injury, toxins or inflammation, tend to become bigger and show a higher expression of IBA1. Hence, activation of microglia has been linked to neurodegeneration due to the production of neurotoxic factors such as proinflammatory cytokines and free radicals. Neurons were visible in the control group while compared to the treatment groups (B-E).

These findings show that treatment groups with 10% D. metel and alcohol activated microglia, resulting in increased size, irregular shape, increased IBA1 expression, and greater immunoreactivity, more pronounced than in the control group or groups treated with only alcohol or D. metel. The combination of D. metel and alcohol may cause inflammation or toxicity in the brain, leading to neuroinflammation or neurotoxicity and potential neuronal damage.

Herein, seven rats were used in each group. Future studies should consider a larger sample size, using power analysis to determine the minimum sample size required to detect a statistically significant difference with the desired confidence level. Further studies should explore samples that include female rats or mixed groups of male and female rats to determine if effects differ by gender. The study duration can be extended to explore the long-term effects of D. metel administration on the liver, brain, and other organs. The study is also limited by the lack of characterization of the phytochemical constituents of D. metel. Future studies are necessary to determine the active phytochemicals in the plant.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that combined intake of D. metel and alcohol cocktail has a deleterious effect on the liver–brain axis and calls for urgent health concern.
