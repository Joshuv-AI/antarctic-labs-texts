Comparative effectiveness and safety of enzalutamide versus abiraterone in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer: a nationwide registry-based cohort study from Taiwan
Authors: Wen-Kuan Huang, Po-Jung Su, Chun-Chi Chen, Ching-Fu Chang, Shao-Ming Yu, Ming-Jer Hsieh, Ming-Lung Tsai, Tien-Hsing Chen, Pao-Hsien Chu, I-Chang Hsieh, See-Tong Pang, Dong-Yi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe real-world effectiveness and safety of abiraterone or enzalutamide in the Asian population with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) remains unclear.MethodsUsing the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database, we identified a cohort of 3056 patients diagnosed with mCRPC receiving abiraterone or enzalutamide from January 1, 2017 through April 30, 2020, followed until December 31, 2021. We applied inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to balance the treatment groups and compare outcomes between the two treatment groups. Effectiveness outcomes included all-cause death, prostate cancer-related death, and treatment failure. Safety outcomes included major adverse CV events (MACE), fractures, and venous thromboembolism (VTE).ResultsEnzalutamide use was associated with superior overall survival (hazard ratio 0.88, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.82–0.96) compared with abiraterone use, which was majorly contributed from pre-docetaxel use (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.81–0.98) but not post-docetaxel use (HR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.80–1.07). The median overall survival of pre-docetaxel abiraterone and enzalutamide was 36.1 and 39.9 months, respectively. In contrast, the median overall survival of post-docetaxel abiraterone and enzalutamide was 22.5 and 24.2 months, respectively. In terms of safety outcomes, enzalutamide use was significantly associated with lower VTE events compared with abiraterone use, but there was no difference in MACE or fracture risk between enzalutamide and abiraterone.ConclusionsWe demonstrated the survival of enzalutamide and abiraterone use in Taiwanese patients with mCRPC is consistent with pivotal trials. The higher VTE risk with abiraterone use should be monitored.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-025-06335-2.

Introduction

Prostate cancer remains a significant global health concern, with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) presenting substantial challenges in terms of mortality and quality of life (Cai et al. 2023; Ronningas et al. 2024). The therapeutic landscape for mCRPC has evolved over the past decade, with androgen receptor inhibitors such as abiraterone and enzalutamide demonstrating impressive efficacy in clinical trials. These agents have shown the ability to reduce the risk of death by 17–19% in pre-docetaxel settings and 26–37% in post-docetaxel settings, as shown by landmark studies including COU-AA-302, PREVAIL, COU-AA-301, and AFFIRM (Beer et al. 2014; de Bono et al. 2011; Ryan et al. 2013; Scher et al. ).

The therapeutic landscape for advanced prostate cancer has evolved over the past decade. Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) continues to be the foundational approach, aiming to suppress testosterone levels and impede cancer growth. However, the introduc2012tion of novel hormanal agents, particularly abiraterone acetate and enzalutamide, has markedly expanded treatment options. Abiraterone, a CYP17 inhibitor (Yin and Hu 2014), and enzalutamide, an androgen receptor antagonist, have demonstrated impressive efficacy in prolonging survival across various disease states, from hormone-sensitive to castration-resistant prostate cancer (Davis et al. 2019; Fizazi et al. 2017).

Despite their clinical benefits, these newer agents are not without potential adverse effects. Of particular concern are their cardiovascular (CV) implications, especially given that many prostate cancer patients are older and may have pre-existing CV risk factors (Moningi and Nguyen 2023). Abiraterone use involves the endocrine system, potentially leading to mineralocorticoid excess and associated CV sequelae (Attard et al. 2012). Enzalutamide, while working through a different pathway, has also been associated with hypertension in some studies (Lee et al. 2023; Zhen et al. 2024). These CV considerations are particularly crucial as they may impact treatment decisions and long-term patient outcomes.

While randomized clinical trials have provided valuable data on the efficacy and safety profiles of abiraterone and enzalutamide, there is growing recognition of the potential discrepancies between trial populations and real-world patient cohorts. Clinical trials often apply strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, potentially excluding patients with significant comorbidities who might receive these treatments in everyday clinical practice (Westgeest et al. 2018). Furthermore, the intensity of monitoring in trial settings may differ from real-world follow-up, potentially affecting the detection and management of adverse events. Consequently, there is a unmet need for robust real-world evidence to complement trial data and provide a more comprehensive understanding of these agents’ efficacies and CV safety profiles in diverse patient populations.

Our study aims to evaluate both the effectiveness and CV risks associated with abiraterone and enzalutamide in a real-world cohort of mCRPC. We provided a comprehensive assessment of these agents in both pre- and post-chemotherapy settings, complementing the findings of pivotal clinical trials such as COU-AA-302 and PREVAIL (pre-chemotherapy), and COU-AA-301 and AFFIRM (post-chemotherapy). Understanding the risk-benefit profile of these agents in real-world settings is crucial for informed decision-making and optimal patient care.

Methods and materials

Data source

Data of the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) of Taiwan were utilized for this retrospective and comparative cohort study. This database includes claims data from the single-payer National Health Insurance Program, which covers approximately 99.8% of Taiwan’s population. Taiwan’s NHI program is mandatory and affordable, which minimizes socioeconomic bias in studies on medication use (Hsieh et al. 2019; Lin et al. 2018). The database provides longitudinal information including demographics, diagnoses, treatment procedures, and prescription records. The NHIRD is currently managed by the Health and Welfare Data Science Center (HWDC), with database coverage extending from 2009 to 2022 at the time of our application. The Taiwan Cancer Registry and Taiwan Death Registry databases can be linked to the NHIRD within HWDC. Patient data are anonymized to protect privacy, with unique identification numbers assigned to each individual.

Study population

Patients diagnosed with prostate cancer (PCa) were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM: 185) codes (prior to December 31, 2015) and Tenth Revision (ICD-10-CM: C61) codes (after January 1, 2016) between 2009 and 2020. Abiraterone was reimbursed solely for mCRPC from December 1, 2014, to April 30, 2020, and enzalutamide was reimbursed exclusively for mCRPC from January 1, 2017, to February 28, 2022. Therefore, this comparative study was conducted by including PCa patients who were firstly prescribed with abiraterone or enzalutamide between January 1, 2017 and April 30, 2020. This comparative study included PCa patients who were initially prescribed abiraterone or enzalutamide between January 1, 2017, and April 30, 2020. The index date was defined as the first prescription date of either abiraterone or enzalutamide. Exclusion criteria included missing demographic data (i.e., zip code of the residence and age), follow-up time less than 3 months (e.g., early death), and history of any other cancers. Patients were categorized into pre- and post-docetaxel groups based on whether they had received docetaxel prior to abiraterone or enzalutamide initiation. Docetaxel use prior to the study period can be traced back as far as 2009.

Covariates measurement

Baseline characteristics included demographics (i.e., age, urbanization level of residence and region, type of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), anti-androgen medications, comorbidities, history of events, and concurrent medications. The ADT was mutually exclusive classified into gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRH), GnRH antagonist (degarelix) and bilateral orchiectomy. Comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, and eight other conditions, were defined by the presence of at least two outpatient diagnoses or any inpatient diagnosis in the prior year. Historical events included myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, heart failure, and any strokes requiring hospitalization, traceable back to 2009. We also extracted medication usage, defined as medications taken within 90 days before the index date, primarily covering antithrombotic, antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, and lipid-lowering agents. Anti-androgen medications included flutamide, bicalutamide and cyproterone. In the analysis of the entire cohort, pre- and post-docetaxel status was also included as a covariate. ICD disease codes were provided in the supplements (Supplemental Table 1).

Outcome definitions

The study evaluated two categories of primary outcomes. For effectiveness assessment, we examined all-cause mortality, prostate cancer-specific mortality, and treatment failure. Safety outcomes were also tracked, focusing on major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), occurrence of fractures, and new-onset venous thromboembolic. Information about date and causes of death were available from the Taiwan Death Registry database. MACE consisted of myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke and CV death. Events of myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke required hospitalization. New-onset venous thromboembolism required at least two outpatient diagnoses or anyone inpatient diagnosis during the follow-up. Fracture required anyone record of emergency department or hospitalization. Treatment failure was defined by discontinuation of the study drug, switching to alternative therapy (abiraterone to enzalutamide or vice versa), or death from any cause. Patients were followed from the index date until the first occurrence of an outcome, death, or the end of database (December 31, 2021), whichever came first.

Statistical analysis

To reduce the possible confounding between the study groups (enzalutamide vs abiraterone), we created an inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) adjusted cohort based on propensity score to balance baseline characteristics between groups. The propensity score was derived from a multivariable logistic regression model that included all baseline covariates, with follow-up duration replaced by the index date. Standardized differences (STD) were calculated to assess covariate balance between groups, with an STD < 0.1 indicating a negligible difference. The IPTW was conducted separately on the whole cohort, pre-docetaxel group and post-docetaxel group. The risk of fatal outcomes (i.e., mortality outcomes, treatment failure and MACE) between groups was compared using Cox proportional hazards model. The incidence of fracture and venous thromboembolism between groups was compared using Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model which consider death a competing risk. Additionally, the median overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier estimator. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Study population

A total of 3056 patients with prostate cancer who received abiraterone or enzalutamide between January 1, 2017 and April 30, 2020 were eligible for analysis after applying exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Of these, 2170 (71%) patients received treatment pre-docetaxel and 886 (29%) received treatment post-docetaxel. In the pre-docetaxel group, 1112 patients received abiraterone and 1058 received enzalutamide. In the post-docetaxel group, 509 patients received abiraterone and 377 received enzalutamide.

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of patients before and after IPTW adjustment. Before weighting, there were some imbalances between the abiraterone and enzalutamide groups, with standardized differences > 0.1 for region, history of atrial fibrillation, use of oral hypoglycemic agents, and post-docetaxel inhibition status. After IPTW, all baseline characteristics were well-balanced between groups, with all STD values < 0.1. The median age of patients was 75.6 years. Most patients resided in areas of moderate to high urbanization (84%) and in the northern or southern regions of the country (71.2%). The majority of patients (93.7%) were on GnRH agonist at the index date. Common comorbidities included hypertension (53.2%), diabetes mellitus (27.6%), and hyperlipidemia (27.3%). The median time from prostate cancer diagnosis to index date was 37.2 months. Effectiveness outcomes

Figure 2A presents the effectiveness outcomes. In the overall cohort, enzalutamide was associated with a significantly lower risk of all-cause death compared to abiraterone (hazard ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.82–0.96). This benefit was primarily driven by the pre-docetaxel group (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.81–0.98), while no significant difference was observed in the post-docetaxel group (Supplemental Fig. 1A–1 C). Similar results were observed for prostate cancer-related death. Enzalutamide was also associated with a lower risk of treatment failure in the overall cohort (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85–0.96) and pre-docetaxel group (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.93). Figure 3 illustrates the median overall survival for each group. In the overall cohort, median OS was 36.0 months for enzalutamide vs 32.3 months for abiraterone (P = 0.003). This difference was more pronounced in the pre-docetaxel group (39.9 vs 36.1 months, P = 0.018) and not significant in the post-docetaxel group (24.2 vs 22.5 months, P = 0.290).

Safety outcomes

Figure 2B presents the safety outcomes. There was no significant difference between enzalutamide and abiraterone in the risk of MACE or fractures in any cohorts. However, enzalutamide was associated with a significantly lower risk of venous thromboembolism compared to abiraterone in the overall cohort (subdistribution hazard ratio [SHR] 0.46, 95% CI 0.32–0.68), pre-docetaxel group (SHR 0.48, 95% CI 0.31–0.75), and post-docetaxel group (SHR 0.37, 95% CI 0.17–0.80). The detailed results of the effectiveness and safety outcomes were provided in the supplements (Supplemental Table 4).

Discussion

This large-scale, real-world study demonstrates that enzalutamide is associated with improved overall survival and a lower risk of treatment failure compared to abiraterone in patients with advanced prostate cancer, particularly in the pre-docetaxel setting. Additionally, enzalutamide showed a significantly lower risk of venous thromboembolism across all cohorts, while no significant differences were observed in the risk of major adverse CV events or fractures.

Our real-world study demonstrates the effectiveness of novel oral targeted therapies in mCRPC patients, both before and after docetaxel chemotherapy. In the pre-docetaxel setting, we observed a median overall survival (OS) of 39.9 months for enzalutamide and 36.1 months for abiraterone, which compares favorably to the PREVAIL (36.0 vs 31.0 months) and COU-AA-302 (34.7 vs 30.3 months) trials, respectively (Beer et al. 2014; Ryan et al. 2013). In the post-docetaxel setting, our study showed median OS of 24.2 months for enzalutamide and 22.5 months for abiraterone, surpassing the outcomes reported in AFFIRM (18.4 vs 13.6 months) and COU-AA-301 (15.8 vs 11.2 months) trials (de Bono et al. 2011; Scher et al. 2012). Of note, our real-world data consistently demonstrated longer survival times compared to the clinical trials, particularly in the post-docetaxel setting. This suggests that the benefits of novel hormone agents may be more pronounced in routine clinical practice, possibly due to improved management of adverse events or evolving treatment landscapes. The relative efficacy of enzalutamide and abiraterone appeared consistent across pre- and post-docetaxel settings in both our study and the clinical trials.

The PREVAIL and COU-AA-302 trials demonstrated survival benefits for both drugs in the pre-chemotherapy setting, but direct comparisons were limited (Beer et al. 2014; Ryan et al. 2013). Our real-world data suggest a potential superiority of enzalutamide in this context, with a 3.8-month improvement in median overall survival compared to abiraterone in the pre-docetaxel group. While this observed difference in effectiveness may be linked to the distinct mechanisms of action, the precise reasons remain unclear and warrant further investigation. Enzalutamide directly inhibits the androgen receptor, while abiraterone inhibits androgen biosynthesis by targeting CYP17A1 (Cheong et al. 2020). The more comprehensive androgen signaling blockade achieved by enzalutamide might explain its superior efficacy, particularly in the earlier stages such as the pre-chemotherapy settings.

In addition to randomized trials, multiple real-world comparative studies across health-care systems have reported highly consistent findings when contrasting abiraterone and enzalutamide in chemotherapy-naïve mCRPC. In the French SNDS SPEAR cohort, enzalutamide was associated with longer overall survival versus abiraterone (IPW-adjusted HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.85–0.96 ; median 34.2 vs 31.7 months) (Scailteux et al. 2021). Complementary French safety analyses showed higher hospitalization risks under abiraterone, including acute kidney injury (IRR 1.42), liver test abnormalities (IRR 3.06), and atrial fibrillation (IRR 1.12) (Scailteux et al. 2022). U.S. Veterans Affairs studies likewise observed longer survival with enzalutamide overall and among patients with cardiometabolic comorbidities (propensity-matched HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.84–0.96) (Schoen et al. 2023) and substantially lower increases in hospitalization rates after treatment initiation (3% with enzalutamide vs 22% with abiraterone) (Riekhof et al. 2024). These concordant real-world data signals align with our Taiwan findings and strengthen the external validity and generalizability of our results.

A novel finding in our study was the higher risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) associated with abiraterone use compared to enzalutamide with hazard ratio of 2.17 (95% CI 1.47–3.13). This increased VTE risk with abiraterone may be attributed to the mandatory long-term use of prednisone in combination with abiraterone therapy (Johannesdottir et al. 2013; Orsi et al. 2021). Glucocorticoids, such as prednisone, are known to induce a hypercoagulable state through various mechanisms, including increased levels of clotting factors, decreased fibrinolysis, (Stuijver et al. 2013) and enhanced platelet activity (Fardet and Feve 2014). Additionally, steroid use can lead to endothelial dysfunction and increased blood viscosity, (Gokalp et al. 2017) further contributing to VTE risk. This finding is particularly important as it represents a previously underreported adverse event in the context of abiraterone therapy for mCRPC. These real-world data provide additional insights that complement long-term safety results of abiraterone use.

The CV toxicity associated with the addition of abiraterone or enzalutamide to GnRH agonist therapy is an important consideration in the management of advanced prostate cancer. Several recent studies have explored this issue, with somewhat mixed results. While some investigations, such as those by Lai et al. and Kulkarni et al., have reported an increased CV risk with abiraterone compared to enzalutamide, (Cone et al. 2021; Kulkarni et al. 2021; Lai et al. 2022) our study found no significant difference in CV toxicity between the two agents. This finding is partially supported by Conover et al., who reported no significant difference in acute myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke risk between abiraterone and enzalutamide, although they did observe an increased risk of heart failure hospitalization with abiraterone (Conover et al. 2023). George et al. (2021) found modestly increased risks for both drugs compared to GnRH agonists alone, with abiraterone showing a slightly higher risk. These varying results underscore the complexity of this issue and could be attributed to various factors, including differences in study populations, follow-up durations, definitions of CV events, and methodological approaches. For instance, the heterogeneity in baseline CV risk factors among study cohorts, variations in concomitant medication use, or differences in healthcare systems and monitoring practices could influence the observed outcomes. Additionally, the relatively short follow-up periods in some studies may not capture long-term CV effects. The conflicting results highlight the need for further prospective evaluation. Future research should aim to standardize outcome measures, account for potential confounders, and include longer follow-up periods to better elucidate the comparative CV safety profiles of abiraterone and enzalutamide in real-world practice.

Our study’s strengths include its large sample size, real-world setting, and the use of inverse probability of treatment weighting to balance baseline characteristics. However, limitations include the potential for residual confounding and the lack of data on certain clinical variables such as PSA levels and imaging results. All patients included in our study were patients with mCRPC. Given that abiraterone and enzalutamide now have new indications for metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer(mHSPC), our results may not be generalizable to this population. Future research should focus on prospective, head-to-head comparisons of enzalutamide and abiraterone in patients with mHSPC, with particular attention to their CV toxicities including on thromboembolic risk.

In conclusion, this real-world study suggests that enzalutamide may offer superior effectiveness and a more favorable thromboembolic risk profile compared to abiraterone in patients with advanced prostate cancer, particularly in the pre-docetaxel setting. These findings have important implications for clinical decision-making and underscore the need for individualized treatment approaches in the management of advanced prostate cancer.
