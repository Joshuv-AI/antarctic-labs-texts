Ni-Doped MoS2/N-Doped Carbon Hollow Nanostructures for Sodium-Ion Half and Full Batteries
Authors: Songya Cui, Jiashuo Wang, Chengxiang Tian, Xiaoyu Xu, Dongxue Han, Guang Chen, Yufeng Yu, Liang Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sodium-ion batteries (SIBs) are restricted in their energy storage capacity primarily because the considerable size of Na+ and inherently slow redox processes limit efficient charge transfer. Transition metal chalcogenides with layered structures have considerable promise as advanced materials of SIBs. Herein, the Ni-doped hollow MoS2/NC spheres with nitrogen-doped carbon are fabricated by a simple water/ethanol system combined with thermal annealing. Nickel doping effectively modifies the electronic properties of MoS2, leading to a substantial improvement in electrical conductivity and an increase in the density of electrochemically active sites. The addition of nitrogen-doped carbon within the composite helps to improve redox kinetics, ensuring improved structural integrity. Consequently, the Ni-MoS2/NC anode demonstrates excellent cycling stability, delivering a capacity of 176 mAh g–1 at 5.0 A g–1 for 10,000 cycles. Moreover, a full battery paired with a Na3V2(PO4)3/C cathode exhibits impressive rate performance, when the current is restored to 0.1 A g–1, and it showed a high specific capacity of 326 mAh g–1, which indicates that the Ni-MoS2/NC||Na3V2(PO4)3/C full battery exhibits robust performance across varying current densities, maintaining high electrochemical reversibility. This effective approach ensures improved electrochemical performance, prolonged cycling life, and higher efficiency, making hollow nanostructures a promising design for next-generation energy storage systems.

Introduction

Sodium-ion batteries (SIBs) have emerged as a promising candidate for energy storage applications due to the abundance of sodium resources and their cost-effectiveness. However, the larger ionic radius and higher reduction potential of sodium ions compared to lithium ions introduce significant challenges, such as ion diffusion barriers, sluggish redox kinetics, and pronounced volume expansion during cycles. To address these issues, researchers have explored various strategies such as integrating transition metal compounds with carbon-based materials, tailoring material morphologies, designing nanostructured porous architectures, and so on. Among these, transition metal sulfides have garnered considerable interest as anode materials for SIBs, owing to their high theoretical capacities and superior electrochemical activity, such as MoS2, NiS, WS2, and ZnS.

Among different anode materials, MoS2 has emerged as a highly promising candidate due to relatively large interlayer spacing of 0.62 nm and high theoretical capacity of 670 mAh g–1. , Although MoS2 primarily behaves as a conversion-type anode material, its layered structure with expanded interlayer spacing critically governs the Na+ diffusion kinetics and electrochemical performance. The wider interlayer spacing facilitates easier Na+ diffusion, while its tunable electronic structure enhances conductivity, making MoS2 suitable for efficient sodium storage. The two-dimensional (2D) structure makes the ion diffusion channel short and the interface effect significant, which is conducive to the stability of the voltage platform. For example, Chen et al. fabricated MoS2@MXene that shows the specific capacities of 354.8 mAh g–1 after 1000 cycles at 1 A g–1 and 261.2 mAh g–1 at 5 A g–1. Nevertheless, MoS2 also face challenges such as significant volume expansion, rapid capacity fading, limited intrinsic conductivity, and suboptimal rate performance, which hinder their broader application as anode materials. To tackle these challenges, MoS2 is often integrated with carbon-based materials like graphene and carbon nanotubes to enhance their electrochemical properties. Notably, nitrogen-doped carbon layers, featuring abundant defects, can significantly boost electronic conductivity while facilitating Na+ diffusion during charge and discharge cycles. For instance, Zhou et al. have reported that MoS2@NC presents the capacity of 428 mAh g–1 after 10,000 cycles without obvious capacity loss. This approach leverages the high capacity and superior electronic conductivity of carbon materials to achieve improved performance.

Moreover, introducing structural defects, such as vacancies, edges, gaps, or chemical doping, can effectively tune the electronic structure and physical properties of MoS2 to address the aforementioned challenges. These defects can enhance the electronic conductivity, reduce ion diffusion barriers, and increase electrochemical active sites. Among these strategies, metal doping (Sn, Co, Ni) is particularly advantageous as it redistributes electron density, promoting the formation of active sites and accelerating electrochemical reaction kinetics. Compared with pure MoS2, MoS2 doped with transition metals showed superior hydrogen evolution reaction performance due to the increase of significant active sites. Notably, the atomic radius of nickel is close to that of molybdenum, and the d-orbital electronic structure of nickel can effectively regulate the band structure of MoS2 and enhance the adsorption and deintercalation kinetics of sodium ions. Ni doping regulates the interlayer spacing and electronic structure, optimizes the van der Waals gap, and achieves a balance between a high rate and low self-discharge. Therefore, metal doping strengthens lattice bonding, mitigating structural degradation during charge–discharge cycles, thereby improving cycling stability and prolonging battery lifespan.

In addition, morphology regulation of MoS2 nanostructures plays a crucial role in enhancing their electrochemical performance, particularly in mitigating the volumetric expansion during charge–discharge processes. Commonly adopted morphological designs include nanosheets, flower-like structures, core–shell architectures, and porous structures. Among these, flower-like morphologies, consisting of numerous interwoven nanosheets, stand out due to their unique advantages. For example, the Ni-doped MoS2/reduced graphene oxide (rGO) cathode demonstrates exceptional capacity retention and superior long-term cycling performance for lithium–sulfur batteries. Three-dimensional (3D) printing of porous hollow nanostructures MoS2@NiS/rGO shows 289.5 mAh g–1 at 0.1 A g–1 and 66.8 mAh g–1 at 5 A g–1. The interconnected layered nanostructures of flower-like MoS2 provide a substantial specific surface area and abundant active sites, which facilitate enhanced Na+ storage. Furthermore, the multidimensional configuration significantly shortens the Na+ diffusion pathways, improving the ion transport kinetics. During cycling, the structural flexibility of the flower-like design effectively alleviates stress accumulation caused by volume changes, thereby enhancing cycling stability.

Herein, a simple one-step solution-phase method combined with high-temperature annealing was used to prepare hollow nanostructured flower-like sphere nickel-doped MoS2 with nitrogen-doped carbon (Ni-MoS2/NC). Nickel doping introduces notable changes to the electronic structure of MoS2, significantly enhancing its electrical conductivity and increasing the number of electrochemically active sites. The integration of carbon materials into the composite prevents particle agglomeration during the charge–discharge cycles, further boosting the conductivity and reinforcing structural stability. Meanwhile, the design of a hollow nanostructured flower-like morphology offers a highly effective strategy to address the volume expansion issue of MoS2 during cycling. Consequently, the Ni-MoS2/NC anode achieves excellent cycling stability over 10,000 cycles with a capacity of 176 mAh g–1 at 5.0 A g–1.

Experimental Section

Materials and Synthesis

Ammonium molybdate tetrahydrate ((NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O, AR), dopamine hydrochloride (98%), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O, AR), ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3, AR), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3, AR), and ammonium phosphate monobasic (NH4H2PO4, AR) were purchased from Aladdin Chemistry Co., Ltd. Ammonium hydroxide (GR) and citric acid (AR) were obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. All chemicals were utilized as received.

Synthesis of Ni-MoS2/NC

First, 500 mg of (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O was dissolved into 70 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 600 mg of dopamine hydrochloride was added into the solution. After stirring for 30 min, absolute ethanol (150 mL) was added into the solution. Following an additional 20 min of stirring, 0.6 mL of ammonium hydroxide solution was added drop by drop using an injection syringe. Then, 410 mg of Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O was added, and the mixture was stirred continuously for 2 h. Finally, the Ni/Mo–polydopamine precursors were collected by centrifugation after being washed several times with deionized water and absolute ethanol.

In a typical synthesis of Ni-MoS2/NC, thiourea and Mo/Ni–polydopamine precursors were loaded into two crucibles situated at the upstream and downstream sections of a tube furnace, respectively, followed by annealing at 600 °C for 2 h under an argon atmosphere. For the preparation of MoS2/NC (Ni/NC), the process is similar, except that Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O ((NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O) is omitted.

Preparation of the Na3V2(PO4)3/C

The mesoporous carbon network with Na3V2(PO4)3 (NVP/C) was synthesized via a facile process. First, 2.34 g of NH4VO3 was dissolved in 40 mL of deionized water at 80 °C, and 4.49 g of citric acid was added to the solution. Citric acid serves as the carbon source, reducing agent and chelating agent. The orange solution turned dark blue, indicating the reduction of vanadium. Subsequently, 1.59 g of Na2CO3, 3.52 g of NH4H2PO4, and 1.0 g of urea were added to the solution to facilitate the formation of Na3V2(PO4)3/C, and the mixture was stirred to obtain a homogeneous solution. The mixture was then dried with stirring at around 60 °C. The precursor was calcined in an Ar/H2 atmosphere at 750 °C for 10 h.

Characterization

The structure and morphology were characterized by using field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, INSPECT F50) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, ZISSLibra 200). X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to analyze the phase composition of the samples. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) tests were conducted on a Kratos AXIS Ultra DLD instrument. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm tests were completed on the JW-BK132F analyzer at 77 K. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Instrument Q600 analyzer) was used to evaluate the content of MoS2.

Electrochemical Measurements

The anode electrode material, Ni-MoS2/NC (MoS2/NC, Ni/NC), acetylene black, and poly­(vinylidene fluoride) in a weight ratio of 8:1:1 were thoroughly mixed with N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone as the solvent. The resulting slurry was coated onto copper foil, dried at 60 °C under vacuum for 12 h, and punched into round slices (10 mm) with a mass loading of approximately 0.8–1.1 mg cm–2. The obtained slices were used as the anode electrode in SIBs, assembled with a counter electrode (metallic sodium) and a separator in an argon-filled glovebox. The electrolyte consisted of 1 M sodium perchlorate (NaClO4) in a mixed solvent of ethylene carbonate/dimethyl carbonate (1:1 by volume) with 5 wt % fluoroethylene carbonates as an additive. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves were measured using a Biologic-SP50 device within a voltage range of 0.01–3.0 V. Galvanostatic discharge/charge tests were performed on a LAND battery-test instrument (CT2001A) within the same potential window. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted by using a Biologic-SP50 device in the frequency range of 0.01–100 kHz.

For the cathode material in full batteries, the NVP/C (80 wt %), the acetylene black (10 wt %), and polyvinylidene fluoride (10 wt %) were dispersed in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone solution to form a slurry, which was then coated onto aluminum foil. After drying 12 h at 60 °C under vacuum, the aluminum foil was punched into a round slice with a diameter of 12 mm. As the cathode materials, the cycling performance of NVP was examined under the potential range of 2.5–3.8 V.

The full batteries were assembled using NVP/C as the cathode and Ni-MoS2/NC as the anode, with the proportion of capacity being 1.2:1. The same electrolyte as in the half-cell continues to be used. Before assembling the full batteries, the half-cell assembled with Ni-MoS2/NC anode and sodium counter electrode was cycled with a small current 3 times in order to have relatively stable full battery performance. Galvanostatic measurements were conducted on LAND CT2001A within a voltage range of 1.0–3.8 V. The mass of the anode materials served as the basis for calculating the current densities of the full batteries.

Calculation Method

The theoretical calculations to determine the physical properties of MoS2 and Ni-MoS2 were performed using density functional theory (DFT) calculations, as implemented in the plane-wave pseudopotential code CASTEP package. The exchange-correlation functional was calculated using the generalized gradient approximation with Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof parametrization and OTFG-type ultrasoft pseudopotential. An energy cutoff of 422 eV for the plane-wave basis set was used to describe the electron–ion interaction. The K-point was obtained from the Monkhorst–Pack scheme with a (3 × 3 × 1) mesh for geometry optimization of MoS2 and Ni-MoS2. The convergence criteria for SCF iteration, maximum force, maximum stress, and maximum displacement were set to 1.0 × 10–6 eV atom–1, 0.03 eV Å–1, 0.05 GPa, and 0.001 Å, respectively. The vacuum between slabs is 15 Å to avoid unnecessary interaction between neighboring slabs.

Results and Discussion

A schematic diagram of the samples is presented in Scheme . Initially, an aqueous solution containing dopamine hydrochloride and ammonium molybdate was prepared. Upon chelation between molybdate anions and dopamine, the originally colorless solution quickly turned into a clear orange-red solution. Upon the addition of ethanol, the chelate exhibited low solubility in ethanol, which induced dopamine to self-polymerize at the water/alcohol interface, forming an orange suspension instead of the previously clear solution. Next, ammonia and nickel nitrate were sequentially added to the above solution. Ultimately, Ni-doped hollow nanostructured MoS2/NC (Ni-MoS2/NC) spheres with nitrogen-doped carbon were obtained by annealing at 600 °C under an Ar2 atmosphere. Compared with Ni-MoS2/NC, the only difference between MoS2/NC and Ni/NC is the absence of nickel nitrate and ammonium molybdate.

As depicted in Figure a, the XRD patterns show the hexagonal MoS2 (JCPDS No. 37-1492), which conforms to the standard JCPDS card. The XRD pattern of Ni-MoS2/NC is similar to that of MoS2/NC, which indicates that nickel doped by this method does not alter the structure of MoS2. Additionally, the XRD diffraction patterns of Ni-MoS2/NC and MoS2/NC do not display the (002) diffraction peak, suggesting that the growth of the (002) crystal plane is suppressed. Additionally, the surface chemical states and compositions were further analyzed by XPS. The survey spectra revealed the presence of Mo, S, Ni, C, N, and O (Figure S3). In the Mo 3d spectra of MoS2/NC, peaks at 228.7 and 231.9 eV correspond to Mo4+ 3d5/2 and 3d3/2, respectively (Figure c). Compared to MoS2/NC, the Mo 3d peak of the Ni-MoS2/NC exhibits a significant shift to lower binding energies, further confirming the successful incorporation of nickel. Additionally, due to potential surface oxidation upon exposure to air, Mo6+ species with higher binding energies can also be observed in the Mo 3d spectra. The electronic structure regulated by Ni doping can effectively inhibit interlayer peeling during charge and discharge and improve the rate performance and cycle stability of the electrode.

The observed decrease in the Mo 3p ratio is attributed to the partial substitution of Mo by Ni in the lattice.

The deconvoluted spectrum reveals that the peak pair centered at 853.3 eV for Ni 2p3/2 and 871.0 eV for Ni 2p1/2 could be assigned to the Ni2+. Additionally, the peak pair located at 855.4 eV for Ni 2p3/2 and 873.6 eV for Ni 2p1/2 corresponds to the Ni3+ state.

The intensity ratio of the D peak to the G peak (I D/I G) in the Raman spectrum serves as a critical indicator of defect density in carbon materials. The peak intensity ratios of MoS2/NC and Ni-MoS2/NC are 1.07 and 1.12, respectively, which is of positive significance for introducing active sites and improving ion diffusion, as shown in Figure S5. This structural modification enhances the introduction of active sites and facilitates ion diffusion kinetics. Furthermore, controlled defect engineering in carbon matrices can prevent structural collapse while generating additional edge and defect sites, which are advantageous for sodium-ion intercalation and overall energy storage optimization.

Moreover, as shown in Figures e and S6a,b, Ni-MoS2/NC and MoS2/NC both exhibit a high specific surface area (84.1 and 80.7 m2 g–1) and pore volume (0.29 and 0.27 cm3 g–1), respectively. The larger surface area and increased pore volume can enhance the availability of active sites and facilitate the interaction between electrolytes and sodium ions, thereby improving the Na+ diffusion and storage capabilities. In order to analyze the mass ratio of carbon material to MoS2, thermogravimetric (TG) analysis was conducted (Figure f). The TG curve revealed a weight loss of about 5% below 100 °C, attributed to the evaporation of H2O molecules. Based on the weight ratio from the ICP results, the carbon contents of the Ni-MoS2/NC and MoS2/NC can be calculated to be 52.1 and 59.5 wt %, respectively. It should be noted that electrodes include carbon materials when calculating specific capacity.

The sodium storage performance is assessed in half-cell, utilizing sodium metal as the counter electrode, and cycling tests were conducted under full state-of-charge and full depth-of-discharge conditions within a voltage range of 0.01–3.0 V. First, the specific capacity and cycling performance of the Ni-MoS2/NC, MoS2/NC, and Ni/NC electrodes were analyzed at 1.0 A g–1 (Figure S8). Due to the low specific capacity and poor cycling performance of the Ni/NC electrode, further experiments will not be conducted on this material, which is primarily attributed to its pronounced block-like structure, resulting from the lack of a polymerization reaction between molybdate and dopamine.

The cycling performances of the Ni-MoS2/NC and MoS2/NC were evaluated at 0.1 A g–1 (Figure b). The Ni-MoS2/NC delivered a reversible capacity of 501 mAh g–1 after 100 cycles, whereas the MoS2/NC exhibited a lower capacity, retaining only 325 mAh g–1. The initial capacity loss is primarily ascribed to the formation of SEI, aligning with findings reported in the literature. The Ni-MoS2/NC delivers an initial charge and discharge Coulombic efficiency of 61.2%, which is obviously higher than the MoS2/NC (54.3%). As depicted in the illustration of Figure b, the light-emitting diode powered by two batteries incorporating Ni-MoS2/NC electrodes remains illuminated for over 10 min without any reduction in brightness, showcasing its effective energy storage capability. The voltage platform in the galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) curve is consistent with characteristic peaks presented by the CV curves shown in Figure c, showing good consistency.

For slope-type materials without a flat voltage platform, traditional voltage-based state-of-charge (SOC) lookup tables become less accurate. Battery management systems typically rely on high-precision Coulomb counting combined with periodic full-charge/full-discharge recalibration to correct cumulative errors. In high-reliability applications, online electrochemical impedance spectroscopy or impedance-based state-estimation can provide additional diagnostics of internal resistance changes, aiding SOC tracking for slope-type electrodes. Although the Ni-MoS2/NC composite exhibits a sloping voltage profile without a plateau, the above BMS strategies are well-established for pseudocapacitive and slope-type materials and can effectively mitigate the challenges in SOC estimation and ensure safe and precise charge/discharge control.

As depicted in Figure S9, EIS measurements were conducted on the Ni-MoS2/NC and MoS2/NC electrodes to evaluate electrode dynamics. The charge transfer resistance (R ct) is represented by the semicircle in the high-frequency region at the electrode–electrolyte interface, while the Warburg resistance, associated with ion diffusion, appears in the low-frequency region. EIS demonstrates that nickel doping significantly reduces the charge transfer resistance from 578 to 238 Ω. Therefore, Ni doping significantly enhances electron transfer, and the distinctive structure further facilitates efficient ion diffusion.

The rate performances of Ni-MoS2/NC and MoS2/NC from 0.1 to 5.0 A g–1 are shown in Figure d. Obviously, the Ni-MoS2/NC possesses the highest capacity of 228 mAh g–1 at 5 A g–1. The capacity can return to 525 mAh g–1 after the current density returns to 0.1 A g–1, demonstrating its superfast reaction kinetics. This is clearly higher than that of MoS2/NC (163 mAh g–1 at 5 A g–1). The GCD curves of Ni-MoS2/NC and MoS2/NC at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 5.0 A g–1 exhibit a clear voltage plateau and small polarization, as shown in Figure S10a,b. This also indicates that interlayer spacing engineering concurrently optimizes intercalation/conversion kinetics, ultimately improving rate capability and cycling durability.

The remarkable cycling stability of Ni-MoS2/NC is primarily due to the recombination of nitrogen-doped carbon and the outstanding sodium storage properties of Ni-doped MoS2. More importantly, after 10,000 cycles at 5 A g–1, Ni-MoS2/NC still has a capacity of 176 mAh g–1, as shown in Figure e. The C-rate is defined as the charge/discharge current divided by the material’s theoretical or practical capacity. Therefore, a current density of 5 A g–1 corresponds to approximately 10 C (based on the theoretical capacity). The capacity of the MoS2/NC composite exhibits rapid decay after ∼4500 cycles. This degradation primarily stems from structural instability during prolonged cycling: repeated sodium-ion insertion and extraction induce interlayer fracture and partial structural collapse in Ni-doped MoS2, which progressively reduces the electrochemically active surface area. Notably, while nickel doping significantly enhances the kinetics of Na+ intercalation (demonstrating design superiority in ionic mobility), it concurrently exacerbates mechanical stress accumulation, highlighting a critical trade-off between fast charge transfer and long-term structural integrity. Compared with Ni-MoS2-related studies in recent literature, the capacity and rate performance show distinct competitiveness (Table S2). This further shows that Ni-MoS2 can effectively improve its electrochemical performance.

The primary ion storage mechanism is pseudocapacitive, attributed to the flower-like spherical structure, which facilitates the swift insertion and extraction of Na+ ions. Sodium ions undergo an intercalation process prior to conversion reactions, where the interlayer spacing directly determines ionic diffusion rates. Even during conversion reactions, the reversible structural reorganization of layered frameworks and interfacial active site distribution remain intrinsically linked to the initial interlayer distances, thereby collectively influencing reaction kinetics and electrode stability. Unlike metal deposition, it is an electrochemical energy storage mechanism that quickly stores and releases charge through surface reactions, thereby improving the rate performance and cycle stability of the battery. The higher pseudocapacitance indicates that Na+ can quickly enter the electrode and fully contact the active site, which helps to maintain the stability of the structure, resulting in a longer cycle life and good rate performance, which is consistent with the reports in the literature. ,

Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was employed to analyze the reaction kinetics based on Fick’s second law: DGITT=4πτ(mAVmMAS)2(ΔEsΔEτ)2 Here, τ represents the constant current titration time; V m is the molar volume; m A denotes the mass; M A is the molecular weight; S refers to electrode surface area; ΔE s represents the steady-state voltage change after a current disturbance; and ΔE τ represents the voltage change in the course of the constant current titration. Based on this, the diffusion coefficient of Na+ can be determined according to this law at distinct charge/discharge stages. Compared with the MoS2/NC electrode (3.1 × 10–9–3.2 × 10–8), the Ni-MoS2/NC electrode manifests an obviously higher Na+ diffusion coefficient (3.5 × 10–9–7.1 × 10–8 cm2 s–1) during the complete charging and discharging procedure (Figures e and S12). The higher diffusion coefficient of sodium ions indicates that the designed 3D flower hollow MoS2/NC sphere structure facilitates the transport of sodium ions. The GITT curve charge/discharge plateau and the steady-state open-circuit voltage recovery curve indicate the stability of the voltage, avoiding the supercapacitor-like rapid linear decay. Moreover, this indicates that the nickel-doped MoS2 is crucial for boosting the reaction kinetics for the Na+ transfer, leading to improved sodium storage capability.

Conclusions

This study aims to propose a novel nickel-doped MoS2/nitrogen-doped carbon (Ni-MoS2/NC) composite for sodium-ion batteries. Nickel enables localized regulation of the electronic structure within the MoS2 nanosheets, moderately reducing the band gap and enhancing the intrinsic electronic conductivity. Furthermore, N-doped carbon layers with defect-induced ion pathways facilitate efficient Na+ transport, promoting rapid ion kinetics. The hollow nanostructures, composed of interconnected nanosheets, provide a high specific surface area and plentiful active sites while simultaneously allowing for stress accommodation and improving ion diffusion. This multidimensional architecture mitigates the mechanical strain associated with volume changes, thereby enhancing the material’s cycling stability. As a result, the 3D hollow nanostructured MoS2/NC spheres electrode demonstrates remarkable cycling stability at 5.0 A g–1, retaining 176 mAh g–1 after 10,000 cycles, and delivers an outstanding rate capacity (228 mAh g–1 at 5.0 A g–1). These findings highlight the potential of Ni-MoS2/NC as an advanced electrode material for stable and efficient SIBs.
