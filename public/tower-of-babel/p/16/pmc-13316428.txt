The role of ATF4 in neurons under mitochondrial stress
Authors: Maria Bilen, Yubing Liu, Imane Chakroun, Mohamed Ariff Iqbal, Bensun C Fong, Jingwei Chen, Smitha Paul, Alexandre Blais, Ruth S Slack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial dysfunction and fragmentation are observed in various circumstances, such as neurodegeneration and aging. Studies have shown that altered mitochondrial function activates the integrated stress response (ISR), with ATF4 serving as a major mediator of adaptation to stress. Presently, little is known about the role of ATF4 in neurons under mitochondrial stress. Using primary cortical neurons, we demonstrate that inhibiting ATF4 under OPA1-mediated mitochondrial stress accelerates the impairment of neuronal differentiation, as evidenced by smaller dendrites and lower dendritic spine density. To better understand the role of ATF4 in this context, we investigated the global binding sites of ATF4 using chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) and examined the chromatin accessibility changes that occur following the loss of ATF4 in neurons under conditions of mitochondrial stress. We found that ATF4 binds to a wide range of targets and alters the chromatin accessibility of genes involved in metabolism, neuronal fate, and neuron maturation. The downstream targets of ATF4 identified in this study can reveal novel and direct targets of ATF4 in neuronal survival and maturation. These adaptations are the hallmarks of stress response in mitochondrial dysfunction-mediated neurodegeneration.

Introduction

Mitochondria are essential for meeting the energetic needs and maintaining metabolite homeostasis of highly energetic organs, such as the brain. The function of mitochondria extends beyond energy production; they are also involved in maintaining essential cellular functions, such as differentiation, growth, and cell death [1–3]. Dysfunctional mitochondria are observed during aging and are the root cause of several neurodegenerative disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [4, 5]. Mutations in mitochondrial dynamic proteins such as Mfn1/2 and OPA1 have been linked to cognitive impairment, motor dysfunction, and neurodegeneration [6, 7]. To maintain cellular homeostasis, mitochondria communicate with the nucleus through various cellular signaling pathways. One such pathway is the integrated stress response (ISR) signaling with ATF4 as a mediator of mito-nuclear communication under stress. The ISR is a crucial pathway in the context of aging and neurodegeneration, responsible for communication between organelles, including the mitochondria and nucleus, among others. Multiomics approaches have demonstrated that various mitochondrial stressors activate the ISR, with ATF4 serving as a central transcription factor (TF) [8]. This TF is responsible for mediating survival or cell death depending on the type and duration of the stress [8–10].

In contrast to healthy neurons, where ATF4 facilitates long-term potentiation and synaptic plasticity, neurodegeneration is characterized by the activation of ATF4 and the ISR, as well as the upregulation of pro-apoptotic factors, leading to cell death. Recent studies suggest a dual role of ATF4 in disease progression, consisting of a switch from pro-survival to pro-death over time [11]. The role of ATF4 is context-dependent and activates different gene expression signatures depending on the type and duration of stress, which could explain its dual role in disease models [1]. Due to the implication of this interplay between mitochondria and ATF4 in neurodegenerative disorders, we sought to identify the novel role of ATF4 in inherent neuronal differentiation under OPA1 loss, as a model of mitochondrial dysfunction typically seen in aging or neurodegeneration. We show that upregulation of ATF4 occurs under OPA1-mediated mitochondrial stress in neurons. Loss of ATF4 in neurons leads to an exacerbation of the impaired differentiation phenotype under mitochondrial stress, highlighting the neuroprotective role of ATF4. Given that ATF4-regulated genes are context dependent [1, 12, 13], we sought to investigate known and novel ATF4 binding targets and chromatin accessibility under mitochondrial stress, allowing for such adaptation. We found that manipulation of ATF4 levels in the context of mitochondrial stress alters chromatin accessibility. Differentially accessible gene loci responding to the loss of ATF4 were associated with neuron differentiation, cell proliferation, and stem cell maintenance genes. These findings suggest a role of ATF4 in modulating neuronal differentiation, potentially through chromatin structure remodelling.

Materials and methods

Primary embryonic neuronal cultures

Primary cortical neurons were prepared from embryonic cortices at embryonic day E14.5–15 of CD1 mice (Charles River Laboratories; Montreal, QC) and cultured for 11 days in vitro (DIV) or DIV 20 as described previously [14]. All experiments were approved by the University of Ottawa, according to the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Briefly, cortices were dissected out and dissociated chemically with trypsin (cat #T4549) and seeded at a density of 1 million cells/ml. Neurons were cultured in neurobasal media containing 1× B27 (cat #7 504 044); 1× N2 (cat #17 502 048); 100 units/ml penicillin/streptomycin (cat #15 070 063), and 0.6 mM L-glutamine (cat #25 030 081). Cells were treated with the mitotic inhibitor cytosine arabinoside (Ara-C, C1768-1G) at a concentration of 2 μM. A 50% media change was performed every 3–4 days with a complete media change 3 days following Ara-C treatment. Lentiviral transduction was performed at DIV 6, and cells were harvested for downstream experiments on DIV 11 or DIV 20.

Viruses and transfection

Lentiviruses were generated in HEK 293T cells by co-transfecting the packaging plasmids and the gene of interest as described previously [15]. Lentivirus carrying pLKO-shOPA1 plasmid was used to knockdown OPA1 in vitro, with the scrambled equivalent pLKO-shCtrl as the control [3, 16]. Lentivirus carrying pLKO-shATF4 plasmid was used to knockdown ATF4 in vitro, with scrambled mCherry as a control (a gift from Dr. Sean Cregan’s Laboratory). Neurons were transduced with a multiplicity of infection of 6 on day 6 for OPA1 and day 0 for ATF4.

Immunohistochemistry

Neurons were grown on glass-bottom dishes (Mattek dishes, cat #P35GC-0-14-C). After the endpoint, cells were washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 20 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed three times for 10 min with PBS. Fixed cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4⁰C. The primary antibodies used were: Synapsin I (Synaptic Systems Mouse, cat #106 011) and MAP2 (Abcam chicken, cat #ab5392). After washing 3 × 10 min with PBS, secondary fluorophore antibodies with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) were incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Images were taken using a Zeiss Observer D1 fluorescent microscope at 40×. Measurements were performed with Fiji ImageJ. Images were imported into Fiji, and the pixel size was adjusted to obtain measures in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}m. Primary dendritic length was measured from the cell body to the tip of the dendrite, and puncta of synaptic density were counted and normalized to the number of nuclei per field.

RNA extraction and Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

RNA was extracted using Arcturus PicoPure Kit (Invitrogen, cat #12 183 025) following the manufacturer’s instructions [17]. RNA from three biological replicates was submitted to the National Research Council (NRC) Canada facility for library preparation and high-throughput sequencing. A complementary DNA (cDNA) library was prepared using TruSeq Stranded messenger RNA (mRNA) kit (cat #20 020 594) and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 550 sequencing system. Sequencing results are available on NCBI GEO under GSE242441.

For target validation, RNA was converted to cDNA using the Super Script IV VILO Master Mix (Invitrogen; cat #11 756 050) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Converted cDNA was diluted to 1 ng/μl in 10 mM Tris–HCl. Gene expression was quantified using the HostStarTaq DNA polymerase (Qiagen; cat #203 205) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative gene expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method. The geometric mean of Gapdh, actin-β, Rps30, and Appl2 was used as the normalizer. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Bioinformatics analysis for RNA sequencing

Differential gene expression analysis was established following the protocol described in Fong et al. (2022) [17]. In short, to generate BAM files from FastQ files, sequences were aligned to GRCmm10 index using HiSat2 [18]. Differential gene expression was determined using DESeq2 [19] and Independent Hypothesis Weighting (IHW) was performed with the IHW package [20]. Genes were considered differentially expressed in shOPA1 versus shControl if they met the following criteria: (i) adjusted P-value <.05, and (ii) log2 fold change (log2FC) > 0.5 or < −0.5. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed using DAVID Bioinformatics [21]. Z-scores were assigned to each differentially expressed gene based on read counts, and heat maps were generated using Java TreeView and ClusterW or Prism 6 [22]. iRegulon was used to identify regulatory elements and enriched TF binding motifs as described previously [17].

For Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), our RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data was ranked by log FC after Opa1 knockdown (shOpa1 minus shCtrl) in differentiated cortical neurons, and a chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) atlas were curated from publicly available database [23] that meet all these criteria using a strategy previously described [24]: (i) belongs to mouse TFs or other chromatin binding proteins; (ii) TF or chromatin binding protein ChIP-seq datasets with at least eight different experiments, in any cell or tissue type; (iii) ChIP-seq peaks with q-value < 10E-20; (iv) Binding sites found in at least 20% of experiments of a specific protein; (v) Binding sites <10 kb from a gene transcription start site (TSS); (vi) Record all genes within this distance limit and make a gene set. A total of 595 different proteins match all these criteria. GSEA was used to determine the normalized enrichment score (NES) of a given TF or a chromatin binding protein on differentially expressed genes from RNA-seq, from most upregulated (red) to most downregulated (blue). In addition to the ChIP-seq Atlas data, gene sets from GO biological processes and Reactome were also included in the GSEA search, as long as the gene sets contained no more than 5000 genes. GSEA was performed using the “fgsea” algorithm [25] through the clusterProfiler package [26] in R/Bioconductor.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and ChIP-seq

A chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay followed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed as described previously [27, 28]. Briefly, primary cortical neurons infected with shOPA1 or shControl lentivirus on DIV 6 were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, scraped, collected, and lysed on DIV 11. For ATF4 ChIP, chromatin was cross-linked for 30 min and sonicated in lysis buffer 3 using a Covaris S220 Ultra-Sonicator, with a duty factor of 5 and incident power of 175 W for 15 min (ChIP-seq) and 10 min (ChIP). ATF4 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, CST, cat #11815S) was used for ChIP with normal IgG as the control (Sigma, I8140). DNA libraries were prepared using the NEB ChIP-seq Library Preparation Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform using an SP flow cell with single-end reads. qPCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1. ChIP-seq data are deposited on NCBI GEO under GSE242440.

Bioinformatics analysis for ChIP-sequencing

Fastq files were aligned to GRCm38/mm10 using Bowtie2. Duplicates were removed using Java MarkDuplicates. Files were then indexed using Samtools. Quality control of ChIP-seq was performed using the R/Bioconductor ChIPQC package (v.40.0) [29] and SSP (v1.3.2) [30]. For visualization, read coverage from BAM files was calculated using deepTools (v. 3.5.6) [31] - bamCoverage function and saved as bigWig files, extending reads by the fragment length estimated by SSP and normalizing each sample using the counts per million total aligned reads in sample. ATF4 ChIP-seq peaks were differentially called using macs14 with mapq20 over input DNA. ChIP-seq read density heat maps were generated using deepTools—bamCoverage, computeMatrix, and plotHeatmap functions, using peaks called in shCtrl and shOPA1 samples. De novo identification of DNA sequence motifs was performed using MEME (v5.5.7) with the meme-chip function [32] on 500 base pairs centered on the peak summits from the shOPA1 sample and analyzing central distribution with the Centrimo function [33]. Sequences from the two sets of peaks were scanned for enrichment in known TF motifs from the JASPAR 2024 database [34] in R/Bioconductor using the universalmotif package [35], employing a hit identification threshold of 1E-4, as previously done [36]. Control sequences were a shuffled versions of the input sequences using shuffle_sequences function with the “euler” method and k-let = 2. Motif enrichment was defined as the ratio of number of target sequences with at least one motif hit over total number of target sequences, divided by the same ratio for the control shuffled sequences. To identify putative target genes of these ATF4 binding sites, peaks were assigned to the nearest TSS of genes based on the UCSC mm10 gene annotation, provided that the distance was <50 kb. GO analysis was performed on gProfiler or David Bioinformatics [21, 37]. The distribution of distances between peaks and genes was performed using ChIPSeeker (v1.46.1), specifying promoters as the 2 kb region centered on the TSS. As an additional quality control step, the peak calling results were compared with those of all mouse ATF4 ChIP-seq datasets reported in the ChIP-Atlas [38] (queried in April 2026).

Western blot

Protein extracts were run on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel (20 µg). A wet transfer was performed, and membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk in tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C. The antibodies used were: OPA1 (Abcam cat #ab42364), ATF4 (CST cat #11815S), and Actin-horseradish peroxidase (Santa Cruz cat #sc-47778). Membranes were washed 3× in 1× TBST before incubation with the appropriate secondary antibody. Blots were incubated in Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad; cat #170-5061) for 5 min and imaged.

Assay of transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing

Assay of transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-sequencing or ‘ATAC-seq’) was performed based on the OMNI-ATAC protocol [39, 40] on differentiated DIV11primary cortical neurons under the following conditions: (i) shControl;shmCherry, (ii) shControl;shATF4, (iii) shOPA1;shmCherry, (iv) shOPA1;shATF4. The Tn5 transposase was prepared in-house following an established protocol [41]. Neurons (5 × 104) were processed for ATAC-seq in three to five biological replicates, and transposed DNA was purified on Zymo DNA Clean & Concentrator-5 columns (cat #D4013). Transposed DNA libraries were prepared according to [36], using NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix (cat #M0491L) and Illumina-compatible unique 5′-i5 and 3′-i7 barcode sequences. Barcoded libraries were double-sided purified with MagBio HighPrep polymerase chain reaction (PCR) clean-up beads (MagBio Genomics cat #AC-60005) to remove fragments below 150 bp and above 1 kb. Real-time PCR was performed on known accessible and inaccessible regions for quality control purposes (Supplementary Table S1). Pooled libraries were sequenced in paired-end mode on an S1 flowcell on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at the TCAG facilities for DNA Sequencing in Toronto. Sequencing results are available on NCBI GEO under GSE242581.

Bioinformatics analysis for ATAC-seq data processing

Quality control analysis of the ATAC-seq data was performed using ataqv (v1.2.1) [41]. All samples had comparable alignment rates, percentages of mitochondrial reads, percentage of reads in peaks and percentage of reads near gene TSS (Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Fig. S2). All samples had a discernable mono-nucleosomal bump, in addition to the sub-nucleosomal size fragments (Supplementary Fig. S2). Low-quality sections of reads and sequencing adapter contamination were removed using fastp v0.20.1. Fastq files were aligned using STAR v2.7.5a on the mouse mm10 genome, using an index built without a GTF file (gene structure information) and specifying parameters that would prevent gapped alignment to introns: –alignIntronMax 1, –alignEndsType Extend5pOfReads12, –alignMatesGapMax 2000, –outFilterMatchNminOverLread 0.50, –outFilterScoreMinOverLread 0.50, –outSAMmapqUnique 40, –outFilterMultimapNmax 1. Files were then indexed using samtools. Duplicates were removed using Java MarkDuplicates. For visualization, bam files were converted to bigwig files using deepTools bamCoverage v3.5.0. Read density distribution graphs were created using deepTools or Prism-GraphPad. Bam files were converted to bed files using bedtools v2.29.2. Peaks of ATAC-seq signal were called using macs2 v2.2.7.1 using the ends of sequenced fragments, representing Tn5 transposase insertion sites, provided as BED files of individual reads, using the options -f BED –nomodel –shift -37 –extsize 73 –keep-dup all. Peak calls from each of the replicates were analyzed using rmspc v1.2.0 to generate a set of consensus peaks, requiring a peak to be found in at least six samples to be retained. Differential accessibility testing was performed using edgeR v3.38.4, comparing shATF4 to shmCherry in control (shCtrl) or mitochondrial stress (shOpa1) conditions, after filtering out peaks to retain only those with at least 25 Tnp insertions in at least two samples. Analysis of DNA motif enrichment was performed using MEME AME on differentially accessible DNA loci [42]. GO analysis was performed on gProfiler or David Bioinformatics.

Results

OPA1 loss-mediated mitochondrial stress activates ATF4

To identify gene regulatory events downstream of mitochondrial stress in differentiating neurons, and to assess whether ATF4 plays a critical role in the process, we examined gene expression in primary cortical neurons using RNA-seq following OPA1 knockdown using lentivirus (Fig. 1A and B). The rationale underlying the choice of OPA1 is that its loss is associated with neurodegenerative diseases and is also known to cause neurogenesis and learning and memory deficits [16, 43, 44]. After performing differential gene expression analysis, we retained only genes that met the following criteria: the adjusted P-value < 0.05 and absolute log2FC > 0.5. We identified genes for which the expression level was significantly upregulated (1723) or downregulated (2446) upon OPA1 knockdown. To explore the biological function of these genes, we performed a GO term enrichment analysis (Fig. 1C and D). We observed that downregulated genes are enriched in neuron differentiation, synaptic transmission, axon guidance, and neurotransmitter secretion (e.g. Snap47, Syt9, Snap25) (Fig. 1E, and Supplementary Tables S2 and S4). Upregulated genes were enriched for those involved in the stress response signal, DNA damage stimulus, DNA repair and regulation of apoptotic signaling (Fig. 1F, and Supplementary Tables S2 and S4). To identify putative common transcriptional regulators to the deregulated genes, an unbiased motif search was performed using the upregulated gene list obtained from RNA-seq data. A putative regulatory region spanning 20 kb centered around the TSS was analyzed, using a motif rankings database from 10 species, and significant genes (P <.05) were used as input. Among enriched motifs, ATF4 was the most enriched sequence-specific TF in this list with a positive NES and a top P-value (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Table S3). Furthermore, to achieve unbiased analysis, we curated a ChIP-seq atlas from publicly available database and included 595 proteins in this analysis. Interestingly, ATF4 binding was found to have the most positive NES, indicating that ATF4 tends to bind genes that are upregulated after Opa1 knockdown (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Table S3). Combined with the observation that the ATF4 protein is itself upregulated (Fig. 1B), our results suggest that OPA1 deletion triggers a stress response of ATF4-dependent gene circuit, suggesting that ATF4-mediated stress response is activated in neurons under mitochondrial dysfunction.

Neuron differentiation is affected by ATF4 loss under mitochondrial stress

To further characterize the role of ATF4 in neuron differentiation, we knocked down ATF4 in control cells or cells with loss of Opa1-mediated mitochondrial stress (Fig. 2A). Knock down of shOpa1 and shAtf4 is validated by Western blot and RT-qPCR, with good transfection efficiency (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S1). The following markers were examined on neurons through immunohistochemistry: MAP2 for the size of dendrites and Synapsin I for the synaptic density (Fig. 2C and D). ATF4 knockdown alone leads to shorter dendrite length when compared to control neurons. More profoundly, we find that OPA1 knockdown leads to the formation of shorter dendrites as represented by a smaller proportion of dendrites between 120 and 180 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}m. Moreover, loss of ATF4 under mitochondrial stress results in even smaller dendrites (Fig. 2D). Similarly, ATF4 knockdown also aggravates deficits in synaptic density (Fig. 2E). This suggests that ATF4 has a role in neuron differentiation and maturation under mitochondrial stress.

To globally delineate ATF4 targets and identify the mechanism regulating neuron differentiation under mitochondrial stress, we performed ChIP-seq of ATF4 on primary cortical neurons with or without OPA1-mediated mitochondrial stress at DIV 11. Quality control assessment shows comparable values for each ChIP-seq sample (Supplementary Figs S3 and S6). Overall, the signal-to-noise ratio was relatively low, with a small proportion of reads falling into peaks and low normalized strand coefficients (NSC). The NSC was marginally higher in the shOPA1 sample, and this coincides with a larger number of peaks in this sample (Supplementary Fig. S3). To examine if the ATF4 ChIP-seq had performed in a sub-optimal manner, we compared our results with those reported in the ChIP-Atlas [38] (Supplementary Fig. S6). We found that our results are similar to other anti-ATF4 ChIP-seq datasets previously published, both in terms of total number of peaks identified and peak score value distribution. We were able to identify 93 and 952 ATF4 binding peaks in shCtrl and shOPA1 samples, respectively (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S6). The read density at the two sets of peaks, in the two ChIP samples (along with the control IgG and the input samples) was examined (Fig. 3B and C). The mean ChIP-seq signal was higher in the shOPA1 sample. Interestingly, the mean signal at shCtrl peaks was higher than in the shOPA1 peaks, possibly indicating that the few condition-independent ATF4 peaks have “stronger” binding by this TF (e.g. bound in a larger fraction of cells). De novo motif search on the ATF4-bound regions in shOPA1 cells revealed a strong enrichment and marked central localization for a motif highly similar to the ATF4 motif (Fig. 3D, [45]). Known motif enrichment analysis using all motifs from the JASPAR database confirmed ATF4 motif enrichment among the two sets of peaks (Fig. 3E).

In order to connect ATF4 binding sites to ATF4-regulated genes, we cross-referenced the ChIP-seq and RNA-seq dataset under these conditions: (i) Opa1-induced mitochondrial stress; (ii) shRNA-mediated ATF4 knockdown [46]; and (iii) ATF4 over-expression [47] (cortical neuron differentiation from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)), to identify potential targets that are directly regulated by ATF4. A few genes in the MAPK pathway are downregulated upon Opa1 knockdown in the mouse primary cortical neurons, which are also downregulated when ATF4 is over-expressed in the human iPSC-differentiated neurons, supporting the relevance and involvement of ATF4 in neuron function (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Among ATF4-binding targets, we identified 295 genes whose mRNA expression were altered under OPA1 knockdown and 265 genes under ATF4 over-expression (Fig. 4D). We verified that loss of ATF4 decreases the expression level of genes involved in mediating the antioxidant response (Chac1 and Slc7a11), amino-acid metabolism (Psph), and one carbon folate pathway (Mthfd2) under Opa1-mediated mitochondrial stress (Fig. 4, and Supplementary Figs S4 and S5). In support of the findings in Fig. 2, we show that manipulating ATF4 alters gene expression of a group of genes that regulate neuron differentiation such as Bdnf and Nrg3 (Fig. 4G and Supplementary Fig. S5). Taken together, the data from ChIP-seq, RNA-seq, and immunostaining highlight a possible role of ATF4 in regulating metabolic rewiring, neuroprotection, and differentiation under mitochondrial stress.

Chromatin accessibility changes in response to ATF4 loss under mitochondrial stress conditions

Our ChIP-seq data indicate that chromatin remodeling genes are enriched near the binding sites of ATF4, suggesting that ATF4 may indirectly regulate DNA accessibility in neurons. We observed an increased occupancy of ATF4 in the vicinity of genes coding for chromatin-modifying factors (Fig. 4C) and we verified significantly enhanced binding of ATF4 on the regulatory regions of Jdp2, Smyd3, and Foxj3 by ChIP-qPCR (Fig. 4B). Among the 360 TFs screened in the ChIP-seq atlas, the ATF4 DNA-binding motif exhibits the strongest association with gene upregulation in RNA-seq in response to Opa1 knockdown (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Table S3).

In order to determine whether ATF4 could have an impact on chromatin accessibility, we performed ATAC-seq on primary differentiated neurons with and without ATF4 knockdown under mitochondrial stress with shOPA1 (Fig. 5A). Quality control assessment shows comparable values for each ATAC-seq sample (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S5). Differentially accessible regions (DAR) between shCtrl and shATF4 samples were presented in a Volcano plot (Fig. 5B), highlighting the genome-wide effect of ATF4 on chromatin accessibility. Under OPA1 knockdown, the loss of ATF4 results in increased accessibility of 1717 loci and decreased accessibility of 409 loci. DARs were assigned to the nearest gene and divided into groups with increased or decreased accessibility. By comparing ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq, we identified 79 out of 897 ATF4 binding targets with DARs in the gene proximity upon ATF4 knockdown in conditions of Opa1 loss-of-function (Fig. 5C). Of the 79 genes, 46 showed a decrease and 33 an increase in chromatin accessibility (Fig. 5C). GO term enrichment analysis showed that genes that are both bound by ATF4 as shown in ChIP-seq and have an altered chromatin structure upon ATF4 knockdown are enriched for amino acid metabolism and transport such as Chac1, Mthfr, and Got1 (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Table S8). Genes involved in antioxidant response (Slc7a11), amino-acid metabolism (Psph), folic acid metabolism (Mthfd2), central cellular energy sensor (Prkaa2), and transcription repressors (Trim66, Hes7) were selected to show ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq signals, with chromatin accessibility changes in response to ATF4 knockdown (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S4). Taken together, these findings indicate that ATF4 has the potential to alter gene expression and chromatin accessibility of some of its targets under mitochondrial stress.

Characterizing chromatin changes associated with ATF4 knockdown under mitochondrial stress

To identify biological pathways associated with ATF4-mediated changes in chromatin accessibility, we analyzed the gene lists for potential enrichment of genes belonging to KEGG pathways or GO categories using DAVID bioinformatics on all DAR in the ATAC-seq. The results showed increased accessibility in pathways associated with cancer, MAPK signaling, autophagy, stress, and proliferation, and decreased accessibility in axon guidance and AD-related genes (Fig. 6A and B). Mertens et al. published interesting comparisons in induced neurons from AD patients and population controls and found neuronal pathways that indicate immature neurons, such as cell cycle re-entry, apoptosis, and cancer-related genes. In normal neuronal differentiation, these immature neuronal pathways were silenced. Interestingly, a list of genes in these pathways showed increased accessibility following ATF4 knockdown in our dataset [48]. While immature neuronal pathways, cell cycle re-entry, apoptosis, and cancer-related genes were silenced during neuronal differentiation, several genes showed an increased accessibility following ATF4 knockdown in our dataset. Interestingly, pathways involved in proliferation, survival, and inflammation, such as the NFκB pathway, showed increased accessibility at some of the downstream effectors such as Map3k14, Nfκbia, and Tlr4 (Fig. 6C, D, and F). Proliferation and invasion mediators such as Ccl11, Cxcl12, Cd28 are more open following ATF4 knockdown (Fig. 6C). Regulators of the NOTCH pathway, HIF1α, and the Yap1/Taz pathway which play a role in stem cell maintenance have also greater accessible chromatin (Fig. 6D). Developmental and cancer-related TFs such as Foxo1, Runx1, and Klf4 were consistently presenting enhanced accessibility (Fig. 6E). To identify potential TFs that are involved in increased chromatin accessibility, we performed DNA-binding motif search on the DNA sequences showing altered accessibility following the knockdown of ATF4. The analysis revealed several sequence-binding motifs that were significantly enriched among the genomic regions where DNA becomes more accessible after ATF4 knockdown, for example a few induced by cellular stress (Stat2 and Egr1), a few involved in cell proliferation (Klf9 and Foxm1), and AP1 (e.g. Atf3, Bach1, Jdp2) both in cellular stress and cell proliferation (Fig. 6G). Taken together, our findings suggest a role for ATF4 in preserving cellular identity through modulation of chromatin accessibility.

Discussion

The results of this study support a number of conclusions. First, we show that OPA1 knockdown in neurons activates the ISR, and ATF4 is implicated in orchestrating this response. Second, ATF4 activation is essential for adaptation to stress, and knocking down ATF4 aggravates the differentiation defect observed under mitochondrial stress. Third, ATF4 regulates the expression of genes involved in metabolic rewiring, neuronal fate, and differentiation genes under mitochondrial stress. Fourth, ATF4 appears to exert its function, at least in part, by causing alterations in DNA accessibility at target genes. These observations suggest that ATF4 is required to maintain proper neuronal differentiation and sustain the metabolic needs and function of neurons. Our data also suggests that ATF4 can regulate its target genes by altering chromatin accessibility.

Our lab has demonstrated that mitochondrial dysfunction alters the fate and differentiation of neural stem cells (NSCs) and subsequently impacts learning and memory [3, 16]. Andraini et al. also reported that OPA1 haploinsufficiency leads to learning and memory defects [43]. Here, we demonstrate impaired cortical neuron differentiation under OPA1 knockdown, as indicated by smaller dendrites and a decreased synaptic density. While several mitochondrial stressors activate the ISR along with the ATF4 TF [9], little is known about the role of ATF4 in neurons under OPA1-dependent mitochondrial dysfunction. Our data suggest that ATF4 plays a protective and adaptive role in response to OPA1 knockdown in cortical neurons, which is consistent with our previous report on NSCs [16]. In fact, inhibiting ATF4 under OPA1-induced mitochondrial stress aggravates the differentiation defects (Fig. 2). The protective role of ATF4 under mitochondrial stress is further supported by both our ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq data, which show that ATF4 has the potential to bind and regulate the expression of neuronal differentiation genes, partially by altering chromatin accessibility. Interestingly, ATF4 is essential for learning and memory by hippocampal-specific knockdown of ATF4 [49]. Inhibiting ATF4 also leads to a decrease in synaptic plasticity and impairment in glutamatergic neuronal function [49, 50], suggesting that a delicate balance of ATF4 expression may be beneficial, especially in neurodegenerative diseases.

While the role of ATF4 in neurodegeneration is the subject of intense investigation, conflicting findings have been reported, and the role of ATF4 in neurons remains unclear. The ambiguity could arise from the context-dependent role of ATF4 in neurons under different stages of maturation [51, 52] or the use of drugs and reagents that target the ISR in a non-specific manner [51, 53]. A recent study by Demmings et al. (2021) demonstrated the detrimental role of ATF4 in an acute model of PD [54], whereas Sun et al. (2013) reported a neuroprotective role of ATF4 through the Parkin gene [52]. These findings indicate that the outcome of stress depends greatly on the timing, level, and context of ATF4 upregulation. Indeed, we showed in this paper that under mitochondrial stress, ATF4 upregulation is neuroprotective.

ATF4 has a dual role under stress, being either protective or maladaptive, depending on the cell type and the duration of the insult. The results of our study suggest that ATF4 would target some genes in a tissue-specific manner, while ATF4 also regulates other genes in a broader spectrum irrespective of the cell types. Among the shared targets, Prkaa2 encodes the α2 catalytic subunit of AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) and AMPK is activated in response to mitochondrial dysfunction. AMPK is a central cellular energy sensor in neurons, especially under metabolic stress with dual roles, neuroprotective or maladaptive, depending on the duration and intensity of mitochondrial stress in neurodegeneration [55]. Methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 2 (Mthfd2) is a mitochondrial enzyme and contributes to mitochondrial NAD(P)H production, which is critical in neuronal stress resilience and epigenetic regulation [56]. In addition, by regulating methionine levels and production of the cofactor s-adenosylmethionine in the cell, the one-carbon pathway factor Mthfd2 could contribute to alter the global methylation levels and the chromatin accessibility [57, 58]. Slc7a11 was another ATF4-dependent target that we observed under OPA1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S4). A recent study has shown that glutathione is essential for stem cell fate decisions under OPA1 loss [2]. Our lab reported this mechanism in the neural stem cells model, where ATF4-mediated glutathione production plays a critical role in protecting NSCs function under mitochondrial stress [16]. As a direct ATF4 target, Slc7a11 is required to maintain intracellular glutathione homeostasis under Opa1-induced oxidative stress in NSCs [16] and in neurons [59]. Psph (Phosphoserine Phosphatase), catalyzes the final step of de novo L-serine biosynthesis and contributes to neuronal metabolism and synaptic function [60]. Reduced ATP and altered redox state under mitochondrial stress can limit serine biosynthesis flux, which in turn could impair synaptic maintenance and excitatory signaling fidelity, making neurons more vulnerable to excitotoxic or metabolic stress.

Hes7 (Hairy and Enhancer of Split 7) is a transcriptional repressor and a downstream effector of Notch signaling [61]. HES7 represses transcription of differentiation-related genes and can shape cell state transitions. Trim66 (Tripartite Motif Containing 66) is a member of the TRIM family of E3 ubiquitin ligases and also functions as an epigenetic repressor in olfactory sensory neurons [62]. ATF4 regulation on both repressors could suggest a potential role of ATF4 in maintaining cell identity under mitochondrial stress. We also identify tissue-specific targets of ATF4 under stress, such as differentiation, proliferation, stemness, and apoptotic genes. Of the direct ATF4 target genes, we identified chromatin remodelers, chromatin assembly, and modifying factors that could mediate the phenotype observed (Supplementary Table S7). An example is Jun dimerization protein 2 (Jdp2), a TF that was previously shown to be targeted by ATF4 in the HeLa cell line [63]. Jdp2 possesses both a DNA and a histone binding domain, which allows it to bind to promoters and regulate the recruitment of histone deacetylases [64]. Motif search shows an enrichment in AP1 motifs, also recognized by Jdp2, on genes with increased chromatin accessibility following ATF4 knockdown. Jdp2 is believed to be involved in the process of cell proliferation and senescence in mouse embryonic fibroblasts [65, 66]. An interplay exists between Jdp2 and Slc7a11 to regulate ROS (reactive oxygen species) levels and apoptosis [67]. Most importantly, Jdp2, along with six other factors, is used in reprogramming fibroblasts into pluripotent stem cells and alters the chromatin accessibility around cell fate and differentiation genes [68]. TFs Foxo1 and Runx1 are implicated in oxidative stress response, which were also enriched among the regions that change in DNA accessibility following ATF4 knockdown. Enrichment of stem cell genes, proliferation, and oncogenic-related genes suggests an immature phenotype similar to the one observed in AD patient-derived induced neurons, where a change in neuron epigenetic identity with aging and AD is reminiscent of immature neurons and malignant transformed cells, suggesting a similar response to different cellular stressors [48]. These findings are supported by shorter dendrites and reduced synapse density under ATF4 knockdown and smaller dendritic length.

While ATF4 has multiple targets, it remains to be determined which one(s) mediates the survival phenotype under mitochondrial stress. It could be attributed to the changes in metabolites under stress or chromatin modifiers activated by ATF4 that are responsible for the changes in chromatin structure. While ATF4 expression is increased in PD patients [52], it remains debatable whether inhibiting it would be beneficial or detrimental. Our findings suggest that ATF4 may have a beneficial role in the early stages of neurodegeneration.
