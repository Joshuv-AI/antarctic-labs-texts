Molecular basis of target RNA cleavage by Cas13
Authors: Joe K. C. Lam, Summerloretta S. K. Leung, Jason Ying Ki Li, Ezra C. K. Cheng, S. Chul Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA-targeting CRISPR-Cas13 enzymes are robust RNA knockdown tools with both on-target and collateral cleavage activities. However, to date, the in vivo RNA cleavage mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we combine in vitro and in vivo methods to elucidate the exact cleavage sites of Cas13. We reveal that some subtypes of Cas13, including Cas13b and Cas13bt, cleave the target RNA at predominant positions, and rational engineering of Cas13 further improves precision. Building on these findings, we develop RNA segment editing (RSE), a targeted RNA cleavage and repair method, to restore dysfunctional RNA in cells. We anticipate that RSE will enable precision RNA engineering for therapeutics and basic research.

Introduction

RNA-targeting CRISPR-Cas systems are divided into Class I with multi-subunit effectors and Class II with single-protein effectors1. The Csm complex belongs to Class I and consists of usually 8 or 10 protein subunits from 5 Csm proteins (Csm1–5)2. The major RNA cleavage subunit, Csm3, is found 3 or 4 copies in one effector complex and simultaneously cleaves 3 or 4 sites with 6-nt intervals on an RNA. In contrast, Cas13 belongs to Class II and is classified into several subtypes, including Cas13a, Cas13b, Cas13bt (recently classified as Cas13b3), and Cas13d3. Each Cas13 protein contains two HEPN (higher eukaryotes and prokaryotes nucleotide-binding) ribonuclease domains, which are brought together to form a catalytic center4–7.

Because of its small size and ease of programmability, Cas13 has been used for various applications, including rapid in vitro detection of viral RNAs and effective RNA knockdown in human cells. Especially, the RNA-knockdown ability of Cas13 has gained attention due to its higher on-target knockdown specificity compared to short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) in certain contexts8. In addition, it was shown that Cas13 can knock down up to 10 different genes simultaneously, enabling combinatorial perturbation screens9. Furthermore, Cas13 was shown to be useful in some organisms where RNA interference is inefficient, such as zebrafish10.

Although we use Cas13 extensively in our experiments and are exploring its therapeutic potential, the precise cleavage sites on the target RNA and the mechanism of Cas13-mediated cleavage remain poorly understood. In the case of AGO2, another programmable ribonuclease, structural studies clearly showed the relative positions of the catalytic residues in the PIWI domain and the scissile phosphate in the target RNA11. Transcriptome-wide sequencing has revealed the precise cleavage sites of AGO2 in XRN1 knockdown samples, where the 5´−3´ exonuclease-mediated degradation is inhibited12.

Uncovering in vivo Cas13 cleavage sites and elucidating the mechanism of Cas13-mediated target RNA cleavage have been challenging for several reasons. One significant factor is the location and enzymatic properties of the HEPN domain. The catalytic center of the HEPN domain lies on the opposite side of the protein from the target RNA-binding region in Cas134–6,13,14. This means that the target RNA must fold back onto the catalytic center to be cleaved. Also, the interaction between the RNA and the HEPN domain appears unstable, as suggested by the surface residues near the catalytic center. Additionally, because the HEPN domain functions as a metal-independent ribonuclease, it may be difficult to determine the structure that captures the target RNA at the catalytic center.

Another complication arises from the fact that the cleavage motifs of many Cas13 proteins are largely unknown. The gold-standard approach for determining these motifs is the use of quenched fluorescent RNA reporters composed of simple mono- or dinucleotide sequences15. However, this strategy is impractical without prior knowledge of cleavage preferences, and if the recognition motif extends beyond a dinucleotide, the number of required reporters grows combinatorially (e.g., 64 for a 3-nt motif). Moreover, fluorescent reporter assays do not reveal the precise cleavage position on the RNA substrate.

In this study, we use a high-throughput in vitro cleavage assay to identify the cleavage motifs of Cas13 proteins in an unbiased manner. We then map in vivo Cas13 cleavage sites using the in vivo adaptor ligation method, which precisely captures the RNA termini before exonuclease degradation. Together, these efforts provide a robust model for Cas13-mediated target RNA cleavage and introduce an RNA segment editing tool capable of removing or replacing large RNA regions in vivo.

Results

Target RNA must fold back onto Cas13 to be cleaved

A distinct evolutionary characteristic of Cas13 proteins is that the region responsible for binding target RNA is separated from the HEPN domain, which cleaves the target RNA. This makes it challenging to understand how Cas13 proteins cleave their on-target RNAs. To enhance the on-target RNA cleavage activity of Cas13 while minimizing collateral cleavage activity, we conducted a “bind-and-wash” experiment (Fig. 1a). In this procedure, Flag-tagged recombinant Cas13 proteins were immobilized on anti-Flag beads (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). We then sequentially added crRNAs and target RNAs, either 5´ end-labeled or unlabeled, allowing them to bind to the beads. Between each step, we extensively washed the beads to remove unbound materials. We began by examining the cleavage characteristics of CasRx (also known as RfxCas13d) and Cas13bt3 (also known as Cas13X.1)8,16,17. The target RNAs have 13 copies of the UUCU or ACAC repeats (52 nt) on both the 5´ and 3´ sides of a crRNA-binding site (23 nt) (Fig. 1b). Previous studies have shown that CasRx preferentially cleaves UU sequences, while Cas13bt3 cleaves UC sequences14,18. Therefore, both CasRx and Cas13bt3 can cleave the UUCU sequences. In contrast, the ACAC repeat sequences served as non-cleaving controls.

Bind-and-wash experiments revealed multiple cleavage products from 5´ end-labeled target RNAs, particularly where the UUCU “cleavable” sequences were positioned at the 5´ end of the crRNA-binding site (Fig. 1c, d; TG11 and TG13 target RNAs). CasRx produced doublets from UUCU repeats, while Cas13bt3 generated singlet bands, confirming previously reported cleavage preferences (Fig. 1c, d; lanes 5 and 7 in c, lanes 4 and 6 in d). The lane profiles showed that many 5´ fragments were shorter than 30 nucleotides. Since the crRNA-binding site is located at positions 55–77 nt (spanning 23 nt), this indicates that both CasRx and Cas13bt3 preferentially cleave sequences at least ~25 nt away from the 5´ end of the crRNA-binding site.

We observed one or a few predominant cleavage products from the 5´ end-labeled TG12 target RNA, which contains the ACAC “non-cleavable” sequences at the 5´ region and the UUCU sequences at the 3´ region of the crRNA-binding site. This pattern suggests that Cas13 proteins grasped the 5´ end-labeled target RNA during the multiple cleavage reactions on the 3´ side, leading to an enrichment of the closest 3´ cleavage sites. Interestingly, the nearest 3´ cleavage site for CasRx is farther than that for Cas13bt3 (Fig. 1c, d; lane 6 in c and lane 5 in d). In the structural models, the geodesic distances between a protein residue near the 3´ end of the target RNA and a HEPN catalytic residue are ~91 Å (~15 nt) for CasRx and ~46 Å (~8 nt) for Cas13bt3 (Fig. 1e, f). Although the RNA-binding track on the surface of the protein may not be the shortest route, the structural models help explain why CasRx has a longer minimum 3´ cleavage distance compared to Cas13bt3.

To further delineate the cleavage feature, we conducted experiments using 3´ extended crRNAs for CasRx cleavage (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In this experiment, the crRNAs were labeled at the 5´ end, while the target RNAs were unlabeled. Similar to the results observed with target RNA cleavage, the UUCU repeats located in the 3´ extended region of the crRNA were cleaved by CasRx (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Several dominant cleavage products were enriched due to the continuous binding between CasRx and the crRNA (Supplementary Fig. 2b; lane 5). These cleavage products, which are ~70 nt in length, indicate that there is ~15 nt distance between the 3´ end of the crRNA and the HEPN catalytic center. In a structural model of RNA-bound CasRx, the geodesic distance between a protein residue near the 3´ end of the crRNA and a HEPN catalytic residue is ~73 Å (~12 nt), supporting the biochemical data (Supplementary Fig. 2c). In summary, the bind-and-wash in vitro cleavage assays demonstrate that the target RNA must fold back onto the protein to be cleaved, and there are minimum cleavage distances of ~15–25 nt in the flanking regions of the crRNA-binding site.

High-throughput in vitro cleavage assay reveals heterogeneous cleavage motifs of PspCas13b

While the cleavage motifs of CasRx and Cas13bt3 are already understood, many other Cas13 proteins have unknown cleavage preferences. To explore the cleavage preferences of various Cas13 proteins in an unbiased way, we developed a high-throughput RNA cleavage assay, inspired by primary microRNA processing experiments19–21. We first prepared three RNA components: a target RNA, a crRNA, and a bystander RNA containing 35 random nucleotides. This configuration allows a maximum of 435 distinct sequences as substrates, and we adjusted the size of diversity using bottleneck PCR (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Figs. 3–5; see Methods). Activated Cas13 proteins exhibit trans-cleavage activity, enabling them to cleave bystander RNAs. To prevent the cleavage of the primer-binding site necessary for sequencing library preparation, we incorporated this site into the hairpin structure of the bystander RNA, since Cas13 proteins do not effectively cleave double-stranded RNAs. By mapping the cleaved sequences onto the input sequences, which served as a dictionary, we inferred the exact cleavage site and determined the cleavage efficiency. Consistent with previous studies, both LbuCas13a and LwaCas13a demonstrated clear mono-U sequence motifs, where the cleavage site occurs before U (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Data 2)15,22,23. Interestingly, LwaCas13a prefers the motifs UU, AU, and GU, but not CU (Fig. 2c), suggesting that our random substrate cleavage assay can effectively identify precise cleavage motifs. Cas13bt3 and CasRx exhibited distinct UC and UU sequence motifs, and furthermore, we could map the cleavage position in the middle of the dinucleotide motif. It is important to note that the precise mapping of the cleavage position could not be achieved using fluorescence-based approaches.

Our most striking finding came from PspCas13b. First identified in 2017, PspCas13b has been widely used in human cell applications due to its robust activity24–28. However, its exact cleavage preference has remained unknown, likely because of a complex cleavage motif that could not be addressed with simple polymer reporters. Our assay revealed that PspCas13b exhibits heterogeneous cleavage preferences (Fig. 2d). Interestingly, unlike the other Cas13 proteins we tested, the –2 position is also crucial for PspCas13b cleavage. The top seven cleavage motifs identified were UAA, UAU, UAG, UAC, UCA, GCA, and CAA (Fig. 2e). Further classification according to the relative cleavage efficiency suggests that the primary cleavage motif of PspCas13b is UA | N (where ‘|’ denotes the cleavage position) and the less effective secondary cleavage motif is NC | A (Fig. 2f, g, Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Notably, beyond these top seven motifs, other sequences also showed detectable cleavage efficiency, indicating that PspCas13b can accommodate certain sequence variations. This flexibility may have been advantageous for maintaining activity against rapidly evolving pathogenic RNAs.

Cas13 cleavage sites cannot be precisely identified in XRN1 knockout cells

Next, we investigated the in vivo RNA cleavage sites of Cas13 proteins in human cells. The HEPN domain generates 2´,3´-cyclic phosphate (2´,3´-cP) and 5´-hydroxyl (5´-OH) ends after cleavage29,30. We hypothesized that the 3´ fragments are degraded by XRN1 and that XRN1 knockout (KO) cells would enable precise mapping of in vivo cleavage sites12. Because XRN1 is predominantly cytosolic, we sought to promote Cas13 activity in the cytosol by combining NES-tagged PspCas13b with a U1 promoter-driven crRNA. We previously developed this U1-crRNA system and demonstrated its superior cytosolic localization, which is not achievable with a conventional U6 promoter-driven crRNA26.

We first assessed the levels of 3´ cleavage fragments of endogenous VDAC2 and EZH2 mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 7). In this experiment, we used SthCsm (Streptococcus thermophilus Csm), a type III RNA-targeting CRISPR-Cas system with defined cleavage sites, which also generates 2´,3´-cyclic phosphate (2´,3´-cP) and 5´-hydroxyl (5´-OH) ends after cleavage31. In wild-type (WT) cells, VDAC2 and EZH2 mRNA levels were lower than in controls after SthCsm treatment, indicating that SthCsm had cleaved the RNA and that the 3´ cleavage products were degraded. However, in XRN1 KO cells, the levels of the 3´ cleavage products were comparable to controls, suggesting that these 3´ RNA fragments were not degraded, and their 5´ end sequences could be identified.

To map the 5´ end of the Cas13 3´ cleavage products, we conducted 5´ RACE (rapid amplification of cDNA ends) using a template-switching oligo as we identified the 5´ end of crRNA in our previous study (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Data 3)26. We knocked down EGFP mRNA using PspCas13b and observed a strong amplicon band of the 3´ fragment of EGFP mRNA on an agarose gel in XRN1 KO cells, but not in WT cells (Fig. 3d; compare lanes 2 and 4). The size of this band corresponded to the position of the crRNA-binding site on the mRNA, indicating that 3´ cleavage occurred near this binding site. We initially expected to see heterogeneous 3´ cleavage products, as RNA foldback can occur at any site along the RNA. It is important to note that this method visualizes the 3´ RNA fragments that have been cleaved only once, as the 3´ fragments are released from the Cas13 protein after cleavage. Subsequent Illumina sequencing revealed four major peaks within ~200 nt downstream of the crRNA-binding site (Fig. 3e). Notably, motif analysis showed that the 5´ end of the 3´ cleavage products contains an N | GG motif, where ‘|’ indicates the 5´ end of the RNA. Since the in vivo PspCas13b cleavage motif (N | GG) differed from the in vitro motif (U | AN and NC | A), we hypothesized that an unknown exonuclease might degrade the 5´ end of the RNA in the absence of XRN1 in the cytosol, stopping at the 5´ GG sequences. Therefore, we sought to develop an alternative method to preserve cleavage products from exonuclease trimming.

In vivo adaptor ligation allows precise mapping of Cas13 cleavage sites

The HEPN domain is found in several human ribonucleases, including IRE1 and RNase L29,30. IRE1 is well known for cleaving XBP1 mRNA during the unfolded protein response, and the cleaved XBP1 exons are subsequently ligated by RTCB, an RNA 2´,3´-cyclic phosphate/5´-OH ligase in the tRNA ligase complex32,33. RTCB also ligates cleaved pre-tRNAs, which carry the same 2´,3´-cP and 5´-OH ends as the cleavage products by the HEPN domain34,35.

We hypothesized that if we overexpress an RNA adaptor with a 2´,3´-cP, this RNA could be ligated to the Cas13 3´ cleavage products in vivo by RTCB. This idea was inspired by the recently developed StitchR method, which enables the production of long mRNA species via in vivo ligation36. In that method, the authors expressed two RNA fragments containing ribozymes at their 5´ and 3´ ends, allowing the ribozyme-induced 2´,3´-cP and 5´-OH ends to be ligated by endogenous RTCB36. We fused a twister ribozyme to the 3´ end of a short RNA and expressed this RNA construct using a U1 promoter cassette, resulting in a cytosolic donor RNA that contained a 5´ cap and a 2´,3´-cP (Fig. 3b, c)26. We confirmed that the original StitchR method worked effectively with the U1 promoter-driven short donor RNA on the EGFP fragment (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b).

As a proof-of-concept, we applied this in vivo adaptor ligation method to the cleavage products of SthCsm (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b, Supplementary Data 3). SthCsm is known to cleave at 3 or 4 sites with 6-nt intervals within the crRNA-binding site31. The ribonuclease subunit, Csm3, generates 2´,3´-cP and 5´-OH RNA fragments, similar to Cas13. Notably, gel electrophoresis and amplicon sequencing showed enriched cleavage products at the expected sites, indicating that the Csm cleavage products were ligated to the donor RNA by RTCB in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c).

We then applied this method to PspCas13b, using the same crRNA from the 5´ RACE experiment. Gel electrophoresis revealed strong cleavage products even though we used WT cells, not XRN1 KO cells (Fig. 3d; lane 6). Motif analysis demonstrated a similar cleavage preference (UA | N) to that observed in the in vitro cleavage assay (Fig. 3e), suggesting that in vivo ligation occurred before exonuclease-mediated degradation. Furthermore, the cleavage sites identified by the in vivo adaptor ligation method in WT cells showed a slightly shifted pattern towards the 5´ end compared to those from the 5´ RACE in XRN1 KO cells (Fig. 3e). This supports our hypothesis that an additional exonuclease-mediated 5´ end trimming occurs in the absence of XRN1. Overall, we established a method for identifying in vivo Cas13 cleavage sites with single-nucleotide resolution.

Next, to comprehensively examine the in vivo cleavage sites of different Cas13 proteins, we tested PspCas13b, Cas13bt3, and CasRx on exogenous EGFP and endogenous VDAC2 mRNA using crRNAs targeting the same positions (Fig. 3f, g, Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Notably, Cas13bt3 showed strong predominant bands near the binding site, similar to what we observed with PspCas13b (Fig. 3f, g). Cleavage distance analysis showed that PspCas13b and Cas13bt3 cleave predominantly within 100 nucleotides of the crRNA binding sites (Fig. 3h). In contrast, CasRx exhibited less distinct cleavage patterns, indicating that CasRx does not have a strong preference for sites proximal to its binding location (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Since PspCas13b and Cas13bt3 are evolutionarily closely related, while CasRx belongs to the Cas13d subtype, this suggests that different Cas13 families exhibit varying preferences for cleavage positions in relation to their binding sites. In summary, our findings indicate that certain Cas13 systems, specifically PspCas13b and Cas13bt3, preferentially cleave target RNA near the crRNA-binding site.

Engineering HEPN domains to modulate the cleavage specificity

The HEPN domain contains the consensus Rx4H motif (where x4 denotes any 4 residues) on an alpha helix, and two HEPN domains form a catalytic center using two Rx4H motifs29,30. The first Arg and the last His are critical for the ribonuclease activity. Due to the lack of a structure that captures the RNA cleavage state, the exact cleavage mechanism of the paired Rx4H motifs remains unknown. Based on the analogy with RNase A, it is suggested that one His residue acts as a general base, deprotonating the 2´-OH group of RNA, and another His residue acts as a general acid, protonating the leaving group37. Arg residues are suggested to stabilize the transition state37. To investigate how PspCas13b recognizes the specific sequence, we generated 19 PspCas13b variants by mutating several amino acids located near the paired Rx4H motifs, as the position of the scissile phosphate is predictable (Fig. 4a, b). We confirmed that most variants retained knockdown activity comparable to that of the WT protein (Fig. 4b). Notably, the N1054D and N1054H mutations significantly decreased cleavage activity, indicating that N1054, located at the second position in the Rx4H motif of the HEPN2 domain, plays a critical role in this process.

Next, we mapped the in vivo cleavage sites of the PspCas13b variants that maintained knockdown activity. While some mutants exhibited cleavage patterns similar to WT, others showed distinct patterns (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 10a, Supplementary Data 4). For example, the D1057A mutant showed a promiscuous cleavage pattern with a single nucleotide preference for adenine (NA | N). In contrast, the T136A mutant exhibited a more homogeneous cleavage pattern than WT and preferred the UC | A motif, suggesting that the specificity at the –1 position is related to the T136 residue. Similarly, we found that D129H and N1060E corresponded to CA | A and UA | U motifs, respectively, implying that the –2 position nucleotide is near D129, while the +1 position nucleotide is close to N1060 (Supplementary Fig. 10b). This led us to infer the direction of the target RNA within the HEPN catalytic center (Fig. 4d). Also, this highlights the potential to rationally engineer the HEPN domain to confer more homogeneous or promiscuous cleavage preferences, depending on the application.

We hypothesized that the T136A mutant might enable more precise cleavage of the target RNA. To test this, we compared the cleavage patterns of WT PspCas13b, the T136A mutant, and Csm, which served as a positive control because it cleaves the target RNA at predetermined sites (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b, Supplementary Data 5). WT and the T136A mutant displayed similar cleavage sites on the endogenous CKB mRNA; however, the peaks were more singular for the T136A mutant (Supplementary Fig. 11b). These findings demonstrate that Cas13 proteins can be engineered to be more precise RNA endonucleases.

RNA segment editing with Cas13

As a proof-of-concept, we applied PspCas13b to the mCherry-linker-EGFP construct. Originally designed as a Cas9 activity reporter, this construct contains an in-frame mCherry coding sequence (CDS) and an out-of-frame EGFP CDS separated by a short linker (Fig. 5b)38. Interestingly, when PspCas13b was coupled with the 5´ donor RNA, we observed a strong induction of EGFP expression alongside a decrease in mCherry levels (Fig. 5c, d). In contrast, PspCas13b alone, without the 5´ donor RNA, could not achieve strong EGFP signals, highlighting the importance of the 5´ cap structure in the donor RNA, which was expressed from a U1 promoter26.

To investigate the distribution of the cleavage sites of Cas13 on the mCherry-linker-EGFP construct, we first employed an EGFP-specific reverse PCR primer located near the 3´ end of EGFP. Gel electrophoresis revealed that PspCas13b predominantly cleaved near the crRNA-binding site (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Data 5). Additionally, the Agilent fragment analyzer system provided quantitative confirmation of the distribution of the cleavage sites (Supplementary Fig. 12a). To visualize cleavage and ligation sites at single-nucleotide resolution, we also prepared sequencing libraries. Amplicon sequencing revealed that PspCas13b cleaved at a UAA site precisely within the linker region, suggesting that PspCas13b can target a specific position depending on the sequence composition near the binding site (Fig. 5f).

In the same experimental context, we also tested Cas13bt1, which has been tested in an AAV delivery system16. While Cas13bt1 effectively restored the EGFP signal similarly to PspCas13b, it showed a more heterogeneous cleavage pattern (Fig. 5c–f, Supplementary Data 5). This suggests that PspCas13b may be more suitable for RNA segment editing applications.

One of the primary goals of RNA segment editing is to remove toxic RNA repeats and restore functional RNAs. To this end, we utilized another reporter construct containing the 5´ fragment of HTT exons (i.e., full-length exon 1 with 5´ UTR and partial exon 2) and a downstream EGFP (Fig. 5g, h). In the reporter, the HTT exon 1 includes 68 copies of CAG repeats, mimicking an allele associated with Huntington’s disease39. We employed two crRNAs targeting the 3´ end of the CAG repeats and identified a predominant cleavage and ligation site through gel electrophoresis and the fragment analyzer (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Fig. 12b). Further amplicon sequencing showed that PspCas13b-WT generated several peaks clustered together within the HTT exon 2 region, indicating that it is possible to predict the major repaired products (Fig. 5g, h, Supplementary Data 5).

To improve the specificity of RNA segment editing, we also tested PspCas13b-T136A. As anticipated, PspCas13b-T136A produced more homogeneous cleavage and repair products compared to WT, suggesting that it is indeed possible to engineer Cas13 for specific RNA segment editing (Fig. 5g, h, Supplementary Data 5). In summary, we present the concept of RNA segment editing, which can engineer a large segment of RNA sequences in vivo.

Discussion

In this study, we report how Cas13 finds and cleaves specific positions on target RNA (Fig. 6). We successfully visualized the RNA foldback cleavage mechanism using the bind-and-wash cleavage assay. Additionally, we developed two methods capable of identifying in vitro cleavage motifs and in vivo cleavage sites for any Cas13 protein, without prior knowledge. By integrating data from both in vitro and in vivo experiments, we established a reliable model for Cas13 cleavage. Interestingly, we found that the in vivo cleavage sites of PspCas13b and Cas13bt3 are clustered very close to the binding site. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the cleavage sequence motif can be altered by engineering specific residues in the HEPN catalytic center. Lastly, we provided a proof-of-concept for Cas13-mediated RNA segment editing (RSE). The endogenous RTCB ligase joins the 5´ donor RNA and the 3´ Cas13 cleavage fragments of target RNA, consequently creating a functional chimeric RNA. Recently, several research groups have developed methods for the in vivo manipulation of endogenous RNAs. Wiedenheft and colleagues utilized the multiple-cleavage property of the Csm complex to excise a short sequence patch from endogenous RNAs40. Specifically, the 6-nucleotide interval cleavage products produced by a single Csm complex were seamlessly ligated by the RTCB ligase. Following this, Zhang and colleagues engineered the Csm guide RNA to enable the excision of sequences beyond 6-nucleotide intervals, allowing correction of pathogenic frameshift mutations41. While targeting precision is ensured, the size of the Csm complex (6.4 kb) poses challenges for in vivo delivery via adeno-associated virus (AAV), which has a smaller packaging limit.

In a different approach, Asokan and colleagues developed a trans-splicing strategy using nuclease-dead PspCas13b, which has strong RNA-binding properties42. This strategy can replace mutation-containing exons of an endogenous RNA with a normal counterpart from an exogenous source. Given that splicing sites are common in most human mRNAs including highly abundant species, this method requires improvements to minimize off-target trans-splicing events. In contrast, our method ensures superior specificity because it relies on the cleavage activity of PspCas13b, which is completely inhibited when more than 4 consecutive or 6 non-consecutive mismatches occur at any position43,44.

Our study presents the first version of RNA segment editing using Cas13. Similar to the ongoing advancements in base editing and prime editing techniques, we expect that improvements in specificity and efficiency will come from protein and RNA engineering. For example, we demonstrated that specific point mutations near the catalytic center can alter the target recognition specificity, either making it more promiscuous or more selective. Moreover, ribozymes from different families need to be tested to develop a more efficient donor RNA. Additionally, Cas13 effector proteins should be screened for their effectiveness in RNA segment editing. Finally, further investigations into the tRNA ligase RTCB complex are necessary to improve in vivo ligation efficiency.

In our experiments, we found that PspCas13b and Cas13bt3 predominantly cleave near their binding sites, whereas CasRx exhibits less distinct cleavage patterns on target RNA. We speculate that these differences reflect the dwell time of the RNA within the HEPN active site. Enzymes that require longer dwell times are more likely to cleave proximal sequences. In contrast, those capable of cleaving transiently bound RNAs may act at more distal sites. The difference in dwell time within the HEPN active site may also underlie the distinct collateral cleavage activities observed among Cas13 effectors. For example, PspCas13b has been reported to exhibit lower collateral activity than CasRx45. Therefore, it would be interesting to expand the Cas13 mutagenesis effort to modulate dwell time and enhance or reduce collateral cleavage activity, depending on the application.

Recently, several groups attempted to probe the collateral cleavage sites of Cas13a in bacteria and in human cells. Semenova and colleagues showed that LshCas13a, upon activation, cleaves uridine-rich tRNA anticodon loops in bacteria46. Subsequently, Geijsen and colleagues harnessed nanopore sequencing and demonstrated that active LbuCas13a ribonucleoprotein complex cleaves uridine residues, often within stem-loop structures, in human cells47. It would be interesting to adapt the in vivo adaptor ligation method to map transcriptome-wide collateral cleavage sites of Cas13 and, potentially, to reveal RNA–RNA interactions using Cas13 variants with strong collateral cleavage activity.

Methods

Protein purification

The recombinant Cas13 proteins were expressed in the E. coli Rosetta2 (DE3) strain. 2 mL of bacterial culture was inoculated in 750 mL of Terrific Broth (BD Difco) in a 2-L baffled flask. IPTG was added with the final concentration of 0.1 mM when the OD600 reached 1. The cultures were then incubated and shaken at 18 °C for 24 h.

The bacterial cultures were then pelleted by centrifugation at 7000 g at 4 °C for 30 minutes. The pellet was then resuspended in 30 mL of lysis buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 10% Glycerol, 1 μg/mL RNase A, 3 μg/mL SaNuclease-MBP, 5 mM CaCl2, 0.1 mM of phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 2 mM βME). A sonicator (Emerson SFX 550, Emerson) was then used to shatter the cells with the following program: 40% power and 60 rounds of [2 seconds of on-time and 8 seconds of off-time].

For His-tag pulldown, a 5-mL HisTrap FF Crude histidine-tagged protein purification column (Cytiva) was washed with Milli-Q water and equilibrated (equilibrium buffer: 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, and 2 mM βME) before protein loading. To wash the sample, 50 mL of wash buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM βME, and 20 mM imidazole) was used. For sample elution, an elution buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM βME, and 200 mM imidazole) was used.

For buffer exchange, Slide-A-Lyzer Dialysis Cassettes, 10 K MWCO (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to perform dialysis. The protein solution was firstly added into the cassette followed by removing additional air from the cassette. The cassette with the sample was then incubated at 4 °C overnight on a magnetic stirrer. During dialysis, TEV protease was added to separate the His-tag from the protein (molar ratio of 100:1 of protein and TEV protease).

To remove the cleaved tags, cation exchange columns were used. HiTrap SP HP cation exchange chromatography column 5 mL (Cytiva) was first equilibrated with the equilibrium buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT) using the ӒKTA go protein purification system (Cytiva). The protein was then eluted by slowly passing the elution buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 1 mM DTT). Each fraction (3.5 mL, 30 fractions in total) was collected using the fraction collector.

To reduce the volume of the protein solution, the Pierce Protein Concentrators PES, 50 K MWCO (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. The sample was added to the concentrator column and centrifuged at 4 °C at 15,000 g for 1 minute. After removing the supernatant, the sample was added again, and the process was repeated until the volume of the solution was less than 500 μL.

As a final purification step, size exclusion chromatography, the Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL (Cytiva) was used. After loading the sample, the equilibrium buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 600 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT) was passed through the column, and the protein fractions were collected using the fraction collector. To store the protein for long-term storage, the aliquoted proteins were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C freezer.

In vitro RNA preparation

To prepare in vitro RNA substrates, T7 in vitro transcription was performed (HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit, NEB). For the DNA template, plasmid or PCR products were used. The in vitro transcription reaction was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To label RNA substrates with radio-isotopes, ATP [γ-32P] was used to label the 5´ end of the RNA substrate. The RNA was treated with Antarctic phosphatase (NEB) to remove 5´ triphosphate, followed by phosphorylation with T4PNK (TaKaRa) and ATP [γ-32P].

In vitro cleavage assay (Bind-and-wash)

First, anti-FLAG M2 agarose gel beads were washed with the protein binding buffer three times (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM EDTA) by centrifugation. Recombinant Cas13 proteins were then incubated with the beads at 4 °C on a rotator. Second, protein binding buffer and pre-incubation buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM EDTA) were used to wash the beads sequentially to remove unbound proteins. Third, crRNA was added and incubated at 37 °C for 10 minutes. The mixture was then washed with the pre-incubation buffer 3 times. Fourth, target RNA was added and incubated at 37 °C for 10 minutes. The mixture was then washed with the pre-incubation buffer 3 times. As a final step of the cleavage reaction, MgCl2 (final 6 mM) was added to facilitate the reaction7,8.

To purify the in vitro cleavage products, acid-phenol:chloroform (Invitrogen) was used. The cleavage products were first mixed with RNA elution buffer (2% SDS, 0.3 M NaOAc) and subsequently acid-phenol:chloroform and vortexed. After centrifugation, the upper phase was then collected and mixed with absolute ethanol. RNA was then pelleted after incubation at –20 °C or –80 °C.

To analyze the cleavage products labeled with radio-isotope, the RNA was loaded onto a Urea-PAGE gel. Then, the gel was incubated with a phosphor screen at –20 °C overnight. To visualise the cleavage products, the phosphor screen was then imaged by the Amersham Typhoon imager (Cytiva).

High-throughput in vitro cleavage assay: sample preparation

The in vitro transcription template for the random RNA substrate was prepared by PCR. To reduce the complexity of random substrates, several rounds of bottleneck PCR were performed. T7 in vitro transcription was done as aforementioned. The Cas13 protein was mixed with crRNA, short target RNA, and random substrate (molar ratio of 10:10:10:1) in the reaction buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 U/µL SUPERaseIn (Thermo Fisher); final volume: 20 µL). All components except short target RNA and random substrate were incubated at 25 °C for 10 mins. The hairpin-containing random substrate and short target RNA were then added and incubated at 37 ˚C for 1 h. RNA was then purified and extracted with acid-phenol:chloroform and ethanol precipitation.

For 3´ adaptor ligation, the RNA was first treated with T4PNK without ATP to remove the 3´ phosphoryl groups. A 3´ adaptor was ligated with T4 RNA ligase 2, truncated KQ (NEB) according to manufacturers’ instructions. To degrade the unligated adaptors, lambda exonuclease (NEB) and 5´ deadenylase (NEB) were added and incubated at 30˚C for 30 mins followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 mins. Reverse transcription was then performed using Maxima H minus Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo) according to manufacturers’ instructions followed by PCR to amplify the cDNA.

For the cleaved samples, to remove uncleaved templates, NdeI restriction digestion was done as the 3´ hairpin sequence contains an NdeI site and this would remove the reverse primer binding site for the uncleaved templates. A second round of PCR was performed to enrich the cDNA of cleaved RNAs. After AMPure purification, a third round of PCR was performed to introduce the index sequences for NGS. The sequences of the adaptors and primers are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

High-throughput in vitro cleavage assay: analysis

Adaptor sequences were trimmed using cutadapt48. The insert sequences were then collapsed and exported with fastx_collapser and seqkit49. The uncleaved input sample was used as a dictionary for mapping the cleaved sequences. Sequences with more than 100 (Replicate 1) and 50 reads (Replicate 2) were kept from the dictionary library, resulting in 50,787 unique dictionary sequences from Replicate 1 and 25,972 sequences from Replicate 2 (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). The total read numbers for dictionaries are 14,831,460 and 2,223,872 for Replicates 1 and 2, respectively.

From cleavage libraries, sequences with more than 20 reads (both Replicates 1 and 2) and 15 to 24-nucleotide-long sequences were kept, resulting in a minimum of 9031 to a maximum of 48,853 unique cleaved sequences (Replicate 1) and a minimum of 15,580 to a maximum of 32,434 unique cleaved sequences (Replicate 2) (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). The total read numbers for cleaved sequences are a minimum of 874,608 (Replicate 1) and 1,937,389 (Replicate 2).

Next, cleaved sequences were used to identify the original uncleaved sequences in the dictionary. The cleavage site and the 3´ cleavage product sequences were inferred from the dictionary sequences. Relative cleavage efficiencies were calculated by dividing the number of cleaved reads by the number of reads found in the dictionary.

The 8-nt cleavage motifs were then determined by comparing the top 4000 sequences of the cleaved samples to the top 4000 sequences of the corresponding no-protein control using pLogo with the option ‘remove duplicate sequences.’ We chose the top 4000 sequences because they showed high cleavage efficiency across all samples, regardless of the different Cas13 proteins (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b).

For the scatterplots to study the cleavage efficiencies of 2-nt (–1, +1) or 3-nt (–2, –1, +1) motifs, the average count of [cleaved / dictionary] of each motif was tabulated for each biological replicate group. Next, the average count of [cleaved / dictionary] of each motif between Replicates 1 and 2 was calculated and compared, and the outliers represent the most efficiently cleaved motifs.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293E cells (human embryonic kidney 293 EBNA1; authenticated by ATCC STR profiling) and XRN1 knockout HEK293 cells50 (kindly provided by Alfredo Castello) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Thermo) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Gibco). All cultures were incubated at 37 ˚C in 5% CO2 supplemented with humidified air. Cells were seeded at 50–60% confluence the day prior to transfection using GeneJuice Transfection Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) or TransIT-X2 Transfection Reagent (Mirus Bio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed for assays 48 h after transfection.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using the RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA concentrations were measured with NanoDrop 2000c (Thermo). To minimize plasmid DNA contamination, total RNA was subjected to DNase I (TaKaRa) treatment according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For qRT-PCR, 500 ng total RNA was subjected to reverse transcription using RevertAid RT Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNAs were diluted 5-fold and subjected to qPCR with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNase H Plus) (TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s instructions on CFX Opus 384 Real-Time PCR System (BIO-RAD). qPCR primers are listed in Supplementary Data 1. Relative expression differences were calculated with the 2-∆∆Ct method.

5´ RACE

The purified RNAs were subjected to reverse transcription with oligo-dT as the RT primer and a template-switching oligo (TSO; Supplementary Data 1) using Maxima H minus Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was then amplified with TSO-specific forward primer and gene-specific reverse primer.

In vivo cleavage site mapping: sample preparation

In addition to the plasmids encoding Cas13 protein and crRNA, a plasmid encoding a short donor RNA was also transfected. RNA extraction and reverse transcription were performed as aforementioned, excluding a TSO. The cDNA was then amplified using a donor RNA-specific forward primer and a gene-specific reverse primer.

In vivo cleavage site mapping: analysis

Adaptor sequences and the donor RNA sequences were trimmed using cutadapt. The insert sequences were mapped onto target gene sequences using bowtie2 (--score-min C,0,-1 --norc -k 1)51. The insert sequences were then collapsed with fastx_collapser. Next, read counts and start positions were obtained using awk, sort, and uniq. Cleavage site distributions were visualized with Pandas and Matplotlib. Cleavage preference was determined and visualised with pLogo as aforementioned. The target gene sequences were tiled to generate 8-mer background sequences for pLogo52.

Cloning

For PspCas13b, Cas13bt1 and CasRx crRNA, phU1-PspCas13b-non-targeting, phU1-Cas13bt1-non-targeting and phU1-CasRx-non-targeting (Addgene #229053, #229055 and #229050) were used. For Cas13bt3 crRNA, phU1-Cas13bt3-non-targeting was used. To insert the spacer sequence, the empty vectors were digested using BbsI. A pair of complementary oligonucleotides was annealed to make the insert. crRNA plasmid was then constructed with T4 DNA ligation. To make the HTT-EGFP reporter, polyQ74-GFP plasmid (Addgene #231893) was used as a template. 5´ UTR of HTT and a nuclear localization signal (NLS) were added with overlapping PCR and Gibson assembly.

Flow cytometry

After detaching and homogenization with trypsin and full culture media, cells were pelleted and resuspended with cold PBS. The Accuri C6 Plus Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences) was used to detect enhanced green fluorescent protein (GFP) and mCherry proteins (RFP) with 488 nm laser and 561 nm laser, respectively.

Fragment analyzer

To assess the homogeneity of the amplicon, the Agilent Fragment Analyzer system was used. Purified PCR products were subjected to analysis to detect the size distribution of PCR products.

Estimation of geodesic distances

The CasRx structural model was generated using I-TASSER53. Protein surface objects were exported from PyMOL. A protein residue near the 3´ end of RNA (crRNA or target RNA) and another protein residue at the HEPN catalytic center were selected using ParaView. The geodesic distance between two residues was estimated using PyVista54.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. All experiments were performed with at least two biological replicates (Supplementary Figs. 13–16).
