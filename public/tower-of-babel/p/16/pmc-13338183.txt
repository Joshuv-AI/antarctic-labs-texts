Effects of acoustic enrichment on settlement of a critically endangered broadcast spawning coral
Authors: Nadège Aoki, Sierra Jarriel, Youenn Jézéquel, Benjamin S Weiss, Alexandra Gutting, Jessica Ward, Weifeng Gordon Zhang, T Aran Mooney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoral reefs are in global crisis, with larval recruitment occurring at historically low rates. Soundscapes provide important recruitment cues for reef larvae, but the effect of sound on settlement is understudied, particularly in broadcast spawning corals. We investigated effects of sound playback on settlement of the endangered reef-building coral, Diploria labyrinthiformis. In a 2023 field study, we placed D. labyrinthiformis larvae at six sites spread across two reefs on St. Croix, U.S. Virgin Islands. We exposed larvae in-situ to 72-h playbacks of recordings from acoustically active reefs. When larvae were held in static cups of filtered seawater, settlement rates were 1.79x higher at acoustically enriched sites. In comparison, larvae enclosed in flow-through mesh tents showed minimal responses to sound playback but exhibited a tenfold difference in mean settlement between the two reefs. This is the first study to our knowledge to demonstrate settlement responses to replayed sound in broadcast spawning corals. Our results suggest that archival recordings of healthy reefs can be used cross-regionally to support coral breeding efforts, while emphasizing that corals are sensitive to multiple cue types in addition to sound. Introduction

Coral reefs globally sustain thousands of marine species and support the livelihoods of millions of people1,2. Unfortunately, over the last century these critical ecosystems have been significantly degraded by overfishing, dynamite blasts, pollution, disease, and recurrent heat waves3–6. These stressors have negatively impacted coral fecundity7, lowered coral broodstock densities (leading to decreased fertilization rates)8,9, reduced availability of hospitable habitats for settling larvae10,11, and further increased already high levels of post-settlement juvenile mortality12,13. Collectively, these changes have impaired the reproductive capacity of wild coral populations, putting the long-term survival of corals and the ecosystems they support at significant risk14,15.

Considerable effort is now being expended to restore at-risk coral populations through active sexual propagation, rearing, and seeding of threatened corals onto restoration sites16,17. Coral reproduction is a complex multi-stage process, with each life stage representing unique challenges and potential bottlenecks for breeding success18. One example of a challenge faced in coral breeding is optimization of the coral settlement process, during which swimming larvae attach to reef substrates and metamorphosize into sessile polyps. Coral settlement is driven by a host of environmental factors including light availability, hydrostatic pressure, substrate color and topography, and different chemical cues, particularly the presence of microbial biofilms and metabolites associated with various crustose coralline algae (CCA) species19–22. Larval responses to these sensory cues are highly variable and species-specific, often showing divergence among species, for example between brooders and broadcast spawners23,24. Current methods to optimize the coral settlement environment typically require preconditioning specially designed substrates in seawater for weeks or months before use, allowing them to take on attractive chemical “smells”16. Although this approach is widely used, it is challenging to scale, given the cost, time, and effort involved in preparing conditioned settlement surfaces. Results can also vary depending on the coral species and other settlement-inductive or inhibitory features of the restoration site. Large knowledge gaps remain in our fundamental understanding of how different sensory factors combine to impact settlement in natural environments; these gaps make it challenging to standardize techniques to maximally facilitate settlement across coral species under variable in situ and ex situ rearing conditions.

The relatively recent discovery that coral larvae are sensitive to sound25 has added a further dimension of complexity to our understanding of coral larval sensory behaviors. Healthy coral reefs constitute complex acoustic environments, rich with biological sounds of fishes and invertebrates26,27. The ecological relevance of these sounds to processes of settlement, metamorphosis, and growth has been investigated in fishes28–30 and is increasingly being explored in marine invertebrates including crustaceans31,32, bivalves33,34, and corals. Coral larvae of multiple species have been experimentally observed swimming towards underwater speakers playing sound25,35 and settling preferentially at sites with higher mean sound pressure levels36,37. As awareness of the importance of acoustic cues to corals and other reef species grows, concerns are also being raised regarding how changes to natural soundscapes on degraded reefs may negatively impact key ecological processes including settlement38–40.

The addition of broadcasted sound, also known as acoustic enrichment, has been proposed as a method to restore positive soundscape cues to areas where they are minimal or absent (e.g. degraded or artificial reef sites, ex situ coral nurseries). Acoustic enrichment could subsequently promote attraction to and settlement of marine larvae in these areas41. Recording sounds from healthy, biologically active sites and replaying them at restoration sites has promise as a non-invasive, easily removable tool, that harnesses naturally occurring processes to promote settlement and reef rebuilding at targeted locations. Addressing how sound functions within the sensory hierarchy of corals could lead to more effective methods for settling corals en masse, particularly if the addition of sound cues to the settlement environment is able to provide a compensating effect for lack of extensive chemical preconditioning of substrates.

Initial studies have successfully used acoustic enrichment to attract wild fish communities and oyster larvae to settle on artificial reef structures42,43, but studies of corals remain limited. Two recent publications using Porites astreoides and Favia fragum corals found significant increases in settlement in both aquaria and field settings when larvae were exposed overnight to replayed Caribbean reef sounds44,45. While promising, these results are difficult to generalize as both studies used brooding corals, a reproductive mode exhibited by a small minority of coral species46. Furthermore, these previous experiments exposed larvae to sound solely under strictly controlled filtered seawater conditions, a method which isolated the effect of the sound cue from other confounding environmental factors such as water quality. In the wild and in situ nurseries, larvae are subject not only to variable soundscapes but also to local hydrodynamics and site-specific water and environmental conditions, factors which can greatly affect settlement outcomes47–50. To determine whether acoustic enrichment can be effective for large-scale coral restoration, there is a clear need for research which tests broadcast spawning species in addition to brooders, and studies that examine sound-mediated settlement responses in both highly controlled settings and across conditions with wider environmental variability.

In this study we sought to address these knowledge gaps by examining how a broadcast spawning coral responded to acoustic enrichment under both static and flow-through water conditions in two distinct Caribbean reef environments. We collected gametes from wild colonies of the hermaphroditic grooved brain coral, Diploria labyrinthiformis, fertilized them, and allowed the embryos to develop in aquaria at The Nature Conservancy’s (TNC) Coral Innovation Hub on St. Croix, U.S. Virgin Islands. We then deployed larvae at six sites spread across two reef areas (three experimental sites per reef) to the north of St. Croix: Buck Island South Forereef (BISF) and Llew’s Reef (LR). These two reefs were selected for their relative accessibility from the mainland as well as similarities in their baseline ambient soundscapes. Two sites per reef (four sites total) received an acoustic enrichment treatment of healthy reef sounds delivered by Reef Acoustic Playback Systems (RAPS)51 while one site per reef (two sites total) served as a control with no sound playback.

We used two containment methods to seed D. labyrinthiformis larvae onto settlement substrates at the sites, with trials of both methods conducted concurrently from May 19–22, 2023, using larvae from the same fertilization batch. In the first method, larvae were added to small acoustically transparent cups along with “clean” filtered seawater (FSW) and a standardized preconditioned settlement substrate37,44,45. All cups were filled using the same batch of FSW obtained from TNC’s seawater intake system. In the second method, larvae were held in small 100 μm mesh tents that allowed flow-through of site-specific ambient seawater from the surrounding reef throughout the experiment. Subsets of these tents contained either preconditioned or unconditioned settlement substrates. Comparison of these two methods allowed us to examine the effect of acoustic enrichment when including or excluding certain waterborne microbial and chemical signals. The goals of this study were to (a) determine if larvae of a broadcast spawning coral species would settle in response to acoustic cues, (b) examine if coral larvae would respond to playback of sounds recorded from reefs distinct and distant from their parent-colony reef, and (c) compare settlement rates for larvae receiving sound cues in isolation versus those receiving acoustic enrichment along with local waterborne cues. Our aim was to provide a broader perspective on how acoustic cues may play an ecological role in natural settlement and be practically used for reef restoration purposes.

Results

Pre-experimental site surveys

Pre-experimental scoping surveys of BISF and LR revealed notable commonalities and some differences between these two reef environments. Benthic surveys showed BISF had three times as much living hard coral cover and significantly less macroalgal coverage than Llew’s Reef, potentially indicating overall better conditions for coral habitability at BISF (Fig. 1A and 15% vs. 5% hard coral, 50% vs. 62% algae). While their benthic communities differed, the natural acoustic environments of both reefs were similar. Pre-experimental recordings of the ambient soundscapes of BISF and LR showed similar daily patterns (Fig. 1B). Both sites showed evidence of broadband peaks at dawn and dusk, likely corresponding to crepuscular fish and/or snapping shrimp activity. Slight elevations in frequencies below 1 kHz at both sites during daytime hours were likely caused by transiting motorboats.

Settlement: specimen cups

We observed D. labyrinthiformis settlement in all cups at all sites and distances, but rates differed significantly based on treatment. The four acoustically enriched sites (B1, B2, L1, and L2) showed significantly higher settlement compared to the two control sites (B3 and L3) (Fig. 2A-B). Mean settlement at the sound-enriched sites was 32 ± 4 (64% ± 8%) larvae compared to 19 ± 2 (38% ± 4%) larvae at the control sites, representing a significant 1.79 times increase in average settlement at the acoustically enriched sites (Fig. 2B; β = −0.583 ± 0.105 SE for control relative to sound, z = −5.56, p < 0.001).

Settlement was consistently higher in cups at the sound-enriched sites at all distances compared to the control sites (Fig. 2C). Yet settlement also varied somewhat with respect to distance from the speaker. This difference between sound and control was most pronounced at 1 m from the speaker, where the RAPS impact on received sound levels was most apparent (Fig. 2D - Sound: 35 ± 2 (70% ± 4%) larvae, Control: 16 ± 0.2 (32% ± 0.4%) larvae). Mean settlement at the sound sites dropped slightly at 5 m compared to 1 m but remained nearly 2x higher than controls (Fig. 2D - Sound: 29 ± 4 (58% ± 8%), Control: 16 ± 9 (32% ± 18%)). Settlement rates at both the sound and control sites increased slightly at 20 m distance (Fig. 2D - Sound: 32 ± 3 (64% ± 6%), Control: 25 ± 4 (50% ± 7%)) but sound-enrichment cups remained elevated compared to the controls. Despite these trends, the statistical model indicated that distance from the speaker did not significantly affect settlement within sound-treated sites (β = −0.020 ± 0.056 SE, p = 0.726).

Settlement: mesh tent method

Coral settlement within mesh tents exposed to flow-through local water showed a considerably different trend from the cup data. Zero settlement was observed in tents with unconditioned settlement surfaces (gears – Fig. 7C) in either the sound or control treatments. In tents with preconditioned gears, settlement was observed in all tents and across all sites, yet all BISF gears showed significantly higher settlement than gears placed in tents at the LR sites (Fig. 3A). At the offshore BISF sites, the average total number of settlers found per tent summed across all settlement gears was 91 ± 9 larvae, corresponding to a rate of approximately 22% overall settlement (this may be a slight underestimation since the PVC tent structures were not inspected for stray settlers). The LR tents by contrast showed an average of 10 ± 2 total settlers per tent, corresponding to an overall settlement rate of less than 1%. Within a given tent, mean settlement per gear ranged from 9 ± 2 to 15 ± 3 larvae at BISF sites and from 0.6 ± 0.4 to 2 ± 1 at LR sites. There were no statistically significant differences between gear settlement rates at the sound and control sites (Fig. 3B; β = 0.244 ± 0.174 SE, p = 0.161)). However, there was a significant reef effect as reflected by the higher settlement at the BISF sites, and this likely drove most of the observed variation in the results (β = −2.26 ± 0.194 SE, p < 0.001).

Acoustics

Acoustic analyses showed that RAPS playback had a transformative effect on the soundscape at our sites (Fig. 4). Hydrophone monitoring confirmed that the RAPS at the four acoustic enrichment sites successfully operated autonomously to deliver three 18-hour bouts of continuous reef sound playback, with a few caveats on the third day of playback. At the L1 site, there was a temporary decrease in the output amplitude from the speaker between 20:00 on May 21 and 10:00 on May 22, but playback never ceased, and output levels returned to normal following the system’s scheduled rest period. At the L2 and B2 sound sites, an equipment malfunction caused playback to halt prematurely during the night of May 22, meaning larvae at these sites ultimately received between 8 and 18 fewer hours of total sound exposure.

Examination of the median power spectral densities at the sound and control sites showed that the playback treatment significantly elevated power levels across the total recording bandwidth particularly at frequencies between 500 Hz and 18 kHz (Fig. 5). This power elevation showed some inter-site variation and was not fully flat, with increases at specific frequencies ranging from ~ 5–35 dB re 1 µPa2 Hz− 1.

Received levels in the 100–1000 Hz frequency band were highest at 1 m from the speaker and rapidly dropped off with increasing distance. Median SPLrms values at the B1 site during one 18-hour playback period were 117, 107, and 105 dB re 1 µPa at 1 m, 5 m, and 20 m, from the speaker, respectively, and median SPLrms at the L1 site were 117, 105, and 101 dB re 1 µPa at the same three distances (Fig. 6A). Median PALrms levels were 83 dB re 1 μm s− 2 at 1 m at the B1 and L1 sites and decreased to 68 and 69 dB at 5 m, showing a similar drop-off to sound pressure levels (Fig. 6B). In comparison, control soundscape recordings from B3 and L3 during an 18-hour cycle when no speaker was playing showed median SPLrms values of 92 and 93 dB re 1 µPa, and median PALrms values of 54 and 60 dB re 1 μm s− 2, respectively. Acoustic levels at distances beyond 1 m were not measured at B2 and L2 due to limitations in field days and available recorders (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this study, Diploria labyrinthiformis larvae exhibited significantly increased (average of 1.79 times higher) settlement rates when exposed to an acoustic environment enriched via playback of recorded Caribbean reef sounds. The positive effects of acoustic enrichment on settlement were most pronounced when larvae were placed in cups of filtered seawater 1 m from an underwater speaker, but settlement increases over controls occurred in cups placed as far as our maximum measured distance of 20 m. These results are noteworthy as it is the first time, to our knowledge, that a broadcast spawning coral has been experimentally shown to settle in response to replayed sound cues. They reinforce findings from two recent studies that used a comparable experimental design and found similar effects of ~ 2 times higher settlement under acoustic enrichment conditions in the brooding corals Porites astreoides and Favia fragum44,45. Our demonstration of acoustically mediated settlement responses in D. labyrinthiformis represents an important expansion in understanding of shared responses to sound among scleractinians and has implications for the broader applicability of acoustic enrichment as a method to restore and rebuild key populations on coral reefs.

We notably found that D. labyrinthiformis larvae settled in response to playback of healthy reef sounds recorded nearly a decade ago at Tektite Reef (St. John), a location approximately 60 km north of our St. Croix experimental sites. This is an encouraging finding for practical reasons, as it suggests reef sound cues recorded in one location can be transplanted across substantial distances and continue to induce positive settlement responses. It also underscores the value of long-term reef soundscape projects, demonstrating how archival recordings from historically healthy reefs can be leveraged for present-day restoration efforts52. Yet the result raises questions regarding which specific elements of the replayed Tektite soundscape (e.g. elevated sound levels, altered frequency spectrum, presence/absence of specific local call types) contributed to D. labyrinthiformis settlement. Current understanding of the mechanism behind coral sound sensitivity is quite limited. It remains unknown whether coral larvae use fine-scale acoustic features to discriminate between habitats or if they are simply sensitive to acoustic energy above a certain threshold53. Previous work by Lillis et al. theorized that low-frequency (< 1000 Hz) sound content, particularly fish calls, are a likely driver of coral settlement responses36,37, but dedicated studies are needed to investigate this topic further. One straightforward yet revealing next step would be to do a more extreme acoustic-transplantation test than we undertook here – e.g. compare settlement rates using the same level of Pacific and Caribbean sounds on corals from either region - to evaluate how far the potential transferability of reef sound cues extends.

In the present study, we did not test the relative effectiveness of sound treatments with different frequency content, but we did assess settlement rates at increasing distances (and therefore decreasing received levels) from our sound source. We found that cup settlement rates decreased somewhat with distance from the speaker but did not fully plateau or match control level values even at the furthest measured distance of 20 m. Because SPLrms and PALrms values at our sound sites remained elevated above control levels at this distance, we suspect that the 20 m distance was not far enough to fully assess the range of the acoustic enrichment treatment. Indeed, a recent propagation study has suggested the maximum effective radius of a RAPS can be many tens to 100’s of m from the speaker (dependent on playback content and propagation conditions)54. We were unfortunately limited in the number of distances from the speaker we were able to test in this study, largely due to constraints in our larval supply. We recommend that future work include propagation measurements and larval exposures at distances up to and including where acoustic enrichment effects are no longer detectable above ambient sound levels, as such data would allow a more exact determination of acoustic thresholds for larval behavior. Since sound propagation varies greatly based on individual site conditions, it is critically important for studies to parameterize their experimental acoustic fields, in both particle motion and pressure whenever possible.

This study also found limitations to the efficacy of sound as a settlement cue for D. labyrinthiformis. Notably, sound-mediated effects on settlement rates were only observed when larvae were held in cups of filtered seawater. When larvae were seeded in mesh tents instead of closed cups, sound playback ceased to influence settlement. Instead, we noted a significant difference in settlement rates between the BISF and LR sites, with approximately 10x more settlement occurring at the offshore, federally protected, BISF. In fact, although all tents were seeded with the same number of larvae, and settlement surfaces for both the cups and tents were preconditioned together for the same duration and under the same conditions, little to no settlement occurred on gears in any of the LR tents.

Our most likely explanation for the contrasting results between the cups and tents is that the mesh tents exposed larvae to a wider suite of waterborne microbial and chemical cues than were present in the cups, and these external environmental factors drove reef-specific settlement responses, masking any signal of an acoustic enrichment effect. While the cups were not necessarily completely free of biochemical cues, cells over 0.2 μm (including bacteria, protozoa, microalgae) were removed via filtration and sterilization steps, resulting in a static environment with a low microbial density55 that was highly controlled and standardized across the cups compared to the tents. It seems that while D. labyrinthiformis larvae were responsive to sound in this controlled scenario, ambient water conditions played a dominating role for settlement decisions in the tents, even when substrates were appropriately conditioned.

As is true of many shallow reef locations56, we lack comprehensive hydrodynamic and water quality monitoring data for BISF and LR. This knowledge gap constrains our ability to hypothesize specific environmental factors which may have caused the difference in tent settlement rates between the two reefs. However, our benthic surveys indicated that BISF had much higher hard coral cover than LR and significantly less macroalgal coverage, suggesting a difference in overall habitability for hard corals and potentially higher nutrient loading leading to macroalgal growth at LR. It is well established that local chemical cues strongly influence fine-scale coral settlement. Metabolic and microbial signatures of crustose coralline algae species have been shown to induce settlement, substrate attachment, and metamorphosis57,58 while chemical exudates from certain macroalgae are hypothesized to inhibit settlement59,60. The more dominant macroalgal presence at LR and/or the limited density of neighboring adult corals, may have contributed to the available metabolite signals in the water column that led to a negative settlement response from corals in the seeding tents. Other local physical and hydrodynamic factors including waves, flow rates, turbidity, sedimentation, and temperature may have also contributed to differential settlement choices between the two reefs22,47,61. It is also possible that larvae settled at similar rates between the two sites, but polyps on the LR substrates experienced rapid post-settlement mortality over the course of the experiment. One recent study62 found that coral juveniles seeded at sites separated by < 100 m showed threefold differences in post-settlement survival, suggesting that fine-scale variation of biotic and abiotic conditions can be highly impactful for coral survival and in turn lead to a high degree of site selectivity in coral settlers. This comparison between settlement rates using ‘clean’ water in cups vs. offshore (BISF) and inshore (LR) reef water highlights a challenge of settlement-based restoration for degraded reefs. Larvae are sensitive to multiple sensory cues; therefore, an area may still be perceived as “unfavorable” despite the addition of suitable settlement structures and enhancement of certain cues (like sound). Put another way, we must address these multiple environmental factors to restore degraded areas and in turn increase settlement rates and survival for juveniles.

Deployments of the RAPS tool were largely successful, as the speaker systems and moorings proved robust to ocean conditions. However, there were minor inconsistencies in playback delivery across sites which resulted in some hours of missed playback at the B2 and L2 sites on the third night of the experiment. These challenges in consistently delivering an ambient settlement cue were unfortunate but are to be expected when trialing developing ocean technology. Despite minor playback issues, all sound treatment sites demonstrated elevated settlement rates in cups compared to no-sound control sites across both tested reefs, supporting our decision to combine treatment sites in our analysis. Notably, the cups at B1 and L1 (which received the full planned sound treatment) had higher mean settlements than B2 and L2, possibly related to the prolonged duration of exposure to the sound cue at the former sites. Previous studies have noted that coral settlement responses to sound began to manifest within the first 24 h of exposure to the cue, but treatment duration can have variable influence based on species characteristics such as typical larval duration44,45. Depending on their individual provisioning, larvae within a cohort may be ready to settle at slightly different timepoints. Ensuring larvae are provided with prolonged opportunities to sense positive cues during their critical competency periods is an important consideration for settlement studies and must be considered on a species-specific basis.

An overarching implication of this study is that site selection for in situ coral settlement, seeding, and outplanting efforts is critically important. Water quality, temperature, light, turbidity, hydrodynamics and sound can all highly influence settlement rates and juvenile coral success and the relative importance of each of these factors has yet to be determined. Local hydrodynamics also play a critically important role in driving larval transport at fine scales but are often deemphasized in the selection and design of restoration sites due to a lack of high-resolution local data63,64. Increasing evidence now shows that sound also influences coral larval behavior and that responses to sound may be widespread among corals. Acoustic characteristics should therefore be taken into consideration when selecting sites for restoration, along with other structural, environmental, and climate considerations56.

There are limitations in this study design and appropriate caution should be taken regarding the generalizability of our results. Field efforts such as these inherently cope with multiple variables (e.g., sound treatment, water quality, reef site, distance from source) and we sought to sample across this variation. With only four sound and two control sites, our replication is relatively small. This is not uncommon for field playback studies, where each replicate requires deployment of an independent speaker system at an acoustically isolated, yet ecologically comparable, site65. Still, in the future, one might place a greater emphasis on equally balancing replicates between treatments and controls to increase statistical rigor. Additionally, logistical challenges associated with rearing large quantities of fragile coral larvae for study constrained our sample sizes. Despite these constraints, we maximized the informative value of our study by incorporating distance-based sampling approaches into our experimental design66, examining how settlement rates changed with both the addition of sound and increasing distance from the RAPS speakers. Indeed, we saw trends of decreasing settlement rates as sound level decreased, out to at least a 20 m radius, underscoring the positive potential for acoustic enrichment.

Our results strongly suggest no single sensory cue alone can be considered a panacea to increase settlement or ensure coral survival. The results of our tent experiments demonstrate that the addition of sound alone is not sufficient to compensate for a lack of biofilm growth and preconditioning of settlement surfaces or for other potentially inhibitory site-specific environmental conditions. Further testing is necessary to determine if and how acoustic enrichment can be used to increase in situ settlement rates when these other factors are less controlled. Natural experiments testing the use of acoustic enrichment for fish recruitment have shown similar variability. While preliminary trials addressing reefs rich with fish and limited dispersal capacity have shown early success42,67, further studies on fringing reefs with longitudinal currents have demonstrated acoustic enrichment has variable effects depending on local currents and specific oceanographic conditions30. Further, those influences are species specific with some fish larvae even showing deterrence to reef sounds rather than attraction (ibid.).

Sound has now repeatedly been shown to increase coral settlement in controlled settings across multiple species of corals, fishes, and invertebrates. There is a need to further develop the tools for acoustic enrichment - e.g. robust speaker systems and recording libraries - and test applications at broader scales. This is in addition to continuing to address the fundamental sensory mechanisms involved in sound reception and diversity of coral species which likely respond, and perhaps respond differently, to sound. Future studies should continue to incorporate coral species representative of different regions, taxonomic lineages, reproductive modes, and ecological niches, to broaden baseline knowledge about coral sound-sensitivity. Given the efficiency with which many animals produce and detect sound cues across considerable ranges, that sound can travel essentially irrespective of hydrodynamic and light conditions, and the increasing diversity of noted responses among invertebrate taxa, acoustics remains an intriguing modality for settlement and restoration efforts. Acoustic enrichment is a valuable tool for practitioners to consider when looking for methods to expand their restoration toolboxes and may already be viable for use in ex situ settings with controlled conditions. We must continue to address how this method can best be leveraged to support in situ settlement, perhaps in tandem with other settlement cues, to maintain natural recruitment of critically important species.

Methods

Gamete collection and larval rearing

D. labyrinthiformis (Fig. 7A) gametes were collected and fertilized in mid-May 2023 according to TNC best practices for coral spawning at that time. From May 14–16 (9–11 days after the May 5 full moon), beginning approximately one hour before sunset68, selected colonies in Cane Bay, St. Croix (17.773804°N, 64.811762°W) were enclosed with mesh catchment nets and monitored by divers for gamete release. Positively buoyant egg and sperm bundles were collected in 50 mL conical tubes and immediately transported in coolers back to the field station where they were mixed in large fertilization batches for 105 min until cell divisions were observed. Embryos were rinsed and transferred to flow-through raceways in the TNC aquaculture system maintained at approximately 28 °C and left to develop undisturbed for 3 days. All larvae used in experiments were fertilized on the evening of May 15 and pooled and portioned into experimental settlement chambers on the morning of May 19.

Settlement chambers

We used two containment methods to expose larvae to acoustically enriched and control environments in situ. In the first method, 50 larvae each were pipetted into polypropylene specimen cups along with 140 mL of FSW and a tri-pronged ceramic stilt (Fig. 7B). FSW was obtained from the TNC seawater intake system, which draws water from a reef nearby the facility. Incoming seawater was passed through TNC’s standardized filtration, UV sterilization, and chilling protocol before finally being forced through 0.22 μm Sterivex pressure filters using a peristaltic pump. Ceramic stilts were preconditioned in local seawater for 2 months prior to deployment. Filled cups of larvae were then attached with zip ties to rebar stakes installed at set distances (1, 5, and 20 m) from an underwater speaker. This method provided a static chamber with a standardized settlement surface area where larval behaviors under sound exposure could be quantified without the influence of confounding waterborne chemical and microbial cues. Similar methods have been used in previous studies to quantify coral and oyster larval settlement responses to replayed sound in small closed containers33,37,44,45,69.

The second method of containing larvae attempted to more closely replicate natural settlement conditions by seeding larvae within mesh-lined structures that allowed for constant ambient water flow-through rather than the closed systems of the cups. We constructed 18 small larval tents, each consisting of a cubical (0.4 × 0.4 × 0.4 m) PVC frame enclosed by 100 μm mesh on all sides. Within each tent we suspended eight ceramic settlement gears (Fig. 7C) developed by SECORE International, Inc70. Three tents were placed 1 m from speakers at each of the six experimental sites, and each tent was seeded with approximately 2,000 D. labyrinthiformis larvae (Fig. 7D). Mesh enclosures are regularly used at larger scales by TNC and other restoration practitioners to seed large quantities of larvae onto structures at in situ settlement plots or mesocosms is. This method has been shown to significantly increase coral settlement and recruitment compared to natural rates of settlement in control plots71,72. Here, our small replicate tents allowed us to test for the influence of sound cues on larval settlement across different ambient reef conditions. Larvae were kept in place by the mesh but still exposed to local seawater flow, along with any site-specific waterborne cues.

Experimental design

The experimental sites were spread across two reefs off the northeast shore of St. Croix: Buck Island South Forereef (BISF), located within the Buck Island Reef National Monument, and Llew’s Reef (LR) (Fig. 8A and B), a nearshore fringing reef within the East End Marine Park. Prior to the experiment, both reefs were surveyed for their benthic composition. SCUBA divers surveyed six 100-point 10 m transects to estimate the relative coverage of hard coral, soft coral, algae, sand, and other substrates.

Three sites on each reef separated by 100 m are labeled (B1, B2, B3 and L1, L2, L3). Filled circles indicate sound sites and empty circles indicate control sites. (C) Top-down schematic showing experimental setup at each site including placement and number of mesh tents (1 m) and settlement cups (1 m, 5 m, 20 m) relative to the speaker.

Following surveys, we marked three experimental sites per reef (Fig. 8B) and set up identical larval cup and tent arrays at each site. Two of the three sites per reef (BISF sites B1 and B2, LR sites L1 and L2) were sound treatment sites, and one was a control (sites B3 and L3). Sites on the same reef were separated by 100 m to ensure sounds broadcasted from one did not influence the soundscape of another. BISF sites ranged in depth from 12.2 to 13.7 m and the LR sites ranged from 9.1 to 10.7 m. At the control sites, a RAPS was deployed for the duration of the experiment but not powered on. The RAPS is a solar-powered, programable tool developed primarily for field-based sound playback on shallow coral reefs51. At the sound sites, the RAPS produced continuous playback of recorded reef sounds for 18 h stretches between 18:00–10:00 local time.

Reef playback treatments were constructed from four 18-h soundscape recordings made in August 2013 at Tektite Reef in St. John, USVI. Files were synched to play at the same local time as when they were originally recorded. To avoid pseudoreplication of treatments, at the start of each hour of the playback window the RAPS randomly selected one of the four soundscape recordings and played an hour-long clip of that recording corresponding to the local time of day. This randomization repeated each hour from 18:00–10:00 over three days. Further details of the soundscape recordings can be found in Kaplan et al. (2015). Tektite is located approximately 60 km from our St. Croix sites and is a highly bioactive reef which has retained > 15% coral cover over the last decade despite significant degradation of reefs in the region73.

We deployed a RAPS in the sand off the reef at each site and installed larval cups and tents at set distances from the RAPS speaker. We placed three larval cups at 1 m, two larval cups at 5 m, and two larval cups at 20 m from the speaker along a transect oriented perpendicular from the reef into the sand (Fig. 8C). We allocated a larger portion of available larvae towards replication at the 1 m distance since we expected larval responses to be greatest at this proximity. We also placed larval cups at 5 m and 20 m to establish a potential range and dose-based effect of the sound stimulus. These expectations were based on results from a previous study which showed that acoustically mediated settlement effects persisted up to 30 m from a speaker44.We also placed three tents, each containing eight settlement gears, 1 m from the speaker at each site. Two out of three of the mesh tents contained gears that had been preconditioned in the same condition as the ceramic stilts in local seawater for two months, while one tent per site contained unconditioned gears. The purpose of using unconditioned gears was to evaluate whether sound enrichment could provide a compensating effect for a lack of preconditioning settlement surfaces.

Larvae were deployed into cups and tents at all sites on the afternoon of May 19 and retrieved on the morning of May 22. Larvae therefore received a total of 66 h of exposure to the 18 h on/6 h off playback cycle prior to retrieval, with the speakers at the playback sites broadcasting reef sounds for 54 of those 66 h or 82% of the total experimental duration.

At the end of the experiments, settlement surfaces from the cups and tents (stilts and gears respectively) were examined under stereomicroscopes for settled D. labyrinthiformis larvae using handheld blue dive lights (Sola NightSea) and yellow filter glasses to observe green autofluorescence of the corals. For each cup, a total number of settlers was determined from the sum of settlers found on the lid, cup, and ceramic stilt. For the tents, the total number of settlers on each gear was recorded. All cups and stilts were scored for settlement on May 22 (the same day they were retrieved) and all gears were scored on May 23, with cups and gears held in a filtered flow-through seawater tank to maintain the larvae until all scoring could be completed.

Acoustic monitoring

Five days prior to the first RAPS deployments, single-channel recorders (SoundTrap ST-600) were placed at the B2 and L2 sites to capture representative recordings of the ambient soundscapes at BISF and LR. These recorders remained in place for the duration of the experiment; when RAPS speakers were subsequently deployed at B2 and L2, the recorders monitored the received sound field at 1 m from the speakers.

At the four other study sites (B1, B3, L1, and L3), an orthogonal array of four hydrophones (HTI-96-MIN) attached to a four-channel acoustic recorder (SoundTrap ST-4300) was deployed concurrently with the RAPS and monitored the acoustic field at 1 m from the speaker for the duration of the acoustic enrichment experiment. All single and four-channel acoustic recorders in this study were set to record on a 10% duty-cycle, capturing 1 min of audio every 10 min at 48 kHz.

In addition to the recordings prior to and during the experiment, the sound field produced from the RAPS speakers at the B1 and L1 sites was recorded at distances of 1, 5, and 20 m during an extra 18-hour playback period on May 22–23 (outside of the experiment period) to characterize acoustic propagation from the speaker in terms of sound pressure and particle acceleration levels. Four-channel arrays were placed at 1 m and 5 m, and a single-channel recorder was placed at 20 m. Distance propagation data using this setup was only captured at the B1 and L1 sites to due to our limited number of recorders and boat days.

Data analysis

Settlement

All settlement analyses and visualizations were conducted using R Statistical Software v. 4.2.2 (R Core Team). Settlement results from the cup and tent datasets were analyzed separately. Mean settlement rates per cup were calculated for each of the six sites as well as for each distance from the speaker and are reported as means ± standard error. One cup of larvae was excluded from analysis after a small crab was found to have been inadvertently added to the assay, likely having a confounding impact on larval mortality. Mean settlement rates for the conditioned and unconditioned gears at each tent and site were similarly calculated and are reported as means ± standard error.

Because sound treatments were applied at the site level, each site ultimately functioned as a statistical replicate for the sound (N = 4) and control (N = 2) treatments. Differences between the mean larval settlement rates at the sound sites compared to the control sites for both cups and tents were tested using generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMM) with a Poisson error distribution and log link function. In the cup model, fixed effects included treatment (sound vs. control), reef, and distance from the speaker (standardized to mean 0 and standard deviation 1). Site was included as a random effect, and an observation-level random effect was added to account for overdispersion in the count data. In the tent model, fixed effects were treatment and reef. Tent identity was included as a random effect, and an observation-level random effect was added to account for overdispersion in the count data. Site was initially included as a random effect for the tents but was removed after it explained negligible variation and resulted in a singular fit. Models were fitted using the lme4 package in R, and statistical significance of fixed effects was assessed using Wald z-tests.

Acoustics

Acoustic recordings were analyzed in MATLAB R2020b (The Mathworks, Inc., 2020) and visualized using MATLAB and R. For each 1-minute recording taken every 10 min of the 66-hour experimental period, we generated and inspected spectrograms (Hann window of 12000 samples, 50% overlap, 1-s windows, FFT length = 16384) to validate that the RAPS were functioning throughout the three playback days and that noise from the sound sites was not present in recordings from the control sites 100 m away. We computed long-term spectral averages from these power spectrograms for easy visualization of the entire playback period in a single image. We applied the same methods to pre-experimental recordings at B2 and L2 to visualize the ambient soundscapes of the reefs. Power spectral densities (PSD) were estimated (Welch’s method, Hann window, 50% overlap, 1-s windows, NFFT = 48,000) for each clip using data from a single hydrophone placed 1 m from the speaker at each site. When a 4-channel recording array was present, PSDs were calculated using data from the hydrophone at the center of the array (Fig. 7D)

Sound pressure and particle acceleration data were also calculated at 1, 5, and 20 m from the RAPS speakers at the B1 and L1 sites for one 18 h playback period. Particle acceleration along three axes was calculated based on the difference in sound pressure between each pair of hydrophones in the four-channel arrays74. Data were passed through a zero-phase digital filter with an 8th order Butterworth filter and subsequently, root-mean square (rms) levels were calculated for both pressure (SPLrms) and the three-dimensional vector norm of particle acceleration (PALrms) within a low frequency band of 100–1000 Hz. These playback levels were compared in the pressure and particle motion domains with an 18-hour recording of control sound levels at the B3 and L3 sites to characterize features of the acoustically enriched and unenriched soundscapes.
