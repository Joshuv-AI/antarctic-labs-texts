The role of traditional ecological knowledge and ecosystem quality in managing ecosystem services
Authors: Azam Khosravi Mashizi, Francisco Escobedo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEcosystem services management is not merely a scientific or economic approach, but rather a participatory and indigenous-based approach that, by integrating ecological assessments and indigenous knowledge, contributes to the sustainable and effective utilization of ecosystem services and reduces the gap between theory and practice.this study aimed to spatially link ecosystem services, traditional ecological knowledge, and ecosystem quality for optimal ecosystem services management, based on indigenous communities’ preferences and ecological capacities. Eleven ecosystem services (aesthetics, nursing function, beekeeping, education, soil stability, gas control, soil stability, soil retention, medicinal plants, recreation, and water yield) were modeled in an Iranian semiarid socio-ecosystem. Field data collection, the InVEST model, and GIS technique were used to sample, map and integrate traditional ecological information and habitat quality. Additionally, a suite of direct and indirect relationships between social-ecological variables and ecosystem services were assessed using Structural Equation Modeling. The findings showed that land covers varied in their capacity to significantly deliver social-ecological quality and ecosystem services (p < 0.05). Also, cultural, provisioning, regulatory, and supporting services showed a high synergy with social-ecological quality, suggesting that social-ecological quality can be an effective proxy for ecosystem services, particularly cultural services. Results show that the most significant component influencing cultural and provisioning services was traditional ecological knowledge, while the most significant factor influencing supporting and regulating services was habitat quality. This study presented a comprehensive model for ecosystem services management which is integrated with traditional ecological knowledge of local communities to provide realistic and feasible solutions for sustainable exploitation of natural resources.Keywords: Habitat quality, Ecological knowledge, Socio-ecosystems, Ecosystem services managementSubject terms: Ecology, Evolution, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Ecosystem services are the benefits that ecosystems offer humans and are crucial to their sustainability1. In the discourse surrounding intergenerational sustainability and the potential for raising knowledge in ecosystems, the idea of ecosystem services has steadily gained traction2. Well-managed ecosystems function properly and have enough capacity to offer a suite of services3. Studies of ecosystem services concentrate on ecosystem structure and function, but also ecosystem health and quality4. Effective management of natural ecosystems thus depends on knowing how ecosystem services are distributed spatially and figuring out what factors influence ecosystem services5. Ecosystem security and ecosystem services are diminished when an ecosystem is threatened4. For sustainable ecosystem management, numerous studies have created several evaluation methods to assess ecosystem services and ecosystem quality6–8. Ecosystem services are a crucial part of sustainable development and were initially significant in ecological studies but are now progressively being used in the social sciences and becoming an emerging topic in macroecological4,9 as well as social-ecological systems studies10. When the ecosystems are healthy, ecosystem services can improve human well-being11. Natural capital, or the planet’s ecosystem stocks and natural resources, are essential to human well-being since it provides ecosystem services12. By acknowledging important human actors, their means of subsistence, and their dependence on ecosystems, as well as the potential for incorporating them into roles related to conservation and ecosystem management, is vital and highlights the interdependence between ecological and social domains in order to maintain ecosystems13.

The foundation of ecosystem services is the idea that biodiversity and nature are essential to human well-being14,15. As such ecosystem services are impacted by both natural and human variables in addition to particular landscape patterns16,17 (Linking social well-being and ecosystem services increases people’s motivation to protect ecosystems and the services they value18,14. Ecosystem services serve as the “bridge” connecting ecosystems, which provided services, and social systems, which demanded services from social19. All relations that connect ecosystems and social system should be preserved to sustain well-being. Economic valuation, or the quantitative importance of ecosystems, can raise decision-makers’ knowledge of the significance of ecosystem quality and promote sustainable management in the context of ecosystem services20. The argument that the idea of ecosystem services primarily defines values in terms of the market and ignores social factors, social norms, and those who gain and occasionally lose2,21,22. Although in recent years many efforts have been made to attract the attention of policymakers and communities to ecosystem services through economic valuation23,24the social and cultural values of ecosystem services have still received less attention. Emphasis solely on economic criteria, such as financial profit or productivity, although it can be a useful tool for decision-making, cannot alone guarantee the sustainable management of ecosystem services; because these services include not only material aspects, but also social, cultural, spiritual and identity dimensions25,26. In many societies, especially in rural and indigenous areas, people’s connection with nature is rooted in traditions, beliefs and ways of life. Through continuous interaction with the natural environment, people in these areas have acquired valuable indigenous knowledge in the areas of resource conservation, recognition of ecological functions, and sustainable use of nature’s services27–30. Therefore, effective participation of people in management processes can only be achieved when their value systems, perspectives and traditional knowledge are recognized and taken into account in decision-making. In other words, to achieve sustainable management of ecosystem services, it is necessary to include social and cultural values, along with economic values, in assessments and policy-making. This not only strengthens the sense of belonging and responsibility of local communities, but also increases the effectiveness of conservation measures. As a result, integrating social values with scientific and technical approaches to natural resource management provides a more comprehensive and sustainable approach that covers both ecological and human needs31–33,10.

Political ecology asserts that social and environmental situations are intimately related, and that biodiversity conservation and natural resource management are fundamentally political34. According to several studies, the understanding of nature is also social and political35,36. Traditional ecological knowledge is the body of knowledge, values, practices, and behaviors that local, indigenous, and peasant people have developed and maintained via their interactions with their biophysical environment37. Previous studies have demonstrated that traditional ecological knowledge helps to improve livelihoods38,39 and conserve biodiversity40. A greater understanding of the interdependence of human and environmental relationships may be advanced by traditional ecological knowledge12,39. The role of traditional ecological knowledge can boost resilience in social-ecological systems and has gained increased attention in light of the overall decline in ecosystem services and the rate of global change. Traditional ecological knowledge can enhance human societies’ capacity to adapt to disturbances and maintain environmental services in situations of uncertainty and change39. Studies have shown that people’s emotional attachment to a landscape increases with the amount of time or frequency of visits41. As a result, researchers frequently involve indigenous people in order to collect traditional ecological knowledge -related data, which is then incorporated into management structures42,40.

Although the ecosystem services framework has been widely emphasized in environmental policy and studies, the practical application of this concept in real-world social systems still faces challenges. There is little empirical evidence that local communities and stakeholders are effectively involved in the processes of identifying, assessing, and managing ecosystem services43. In many areas, especially in arid and semi-arid ecosystems, there is still limited understanding of the concept of ecosystem services among local communities, and traditional ecological knowledge is often ignored in these assessments. From this perspective, the restoration of ecosystem services in arid regions is not possible without the participation of local communities and without utilizing their lived knowledge. This participation can be achieved through documentation of indigenous knowledge, two-way training, and its integration into models for assessing and managing ecosystem services. Such an approach can simultaneously promote social and ecological resilience and provide a path to sustainable development in these regions.

Athough much research has been done to quantitatively assess ecosystem services and assess habitat quality, few studies have examined the spatial relationship between ecosystem services, habitat quality, and indigenous knowledge; Especially in vulnerable socio-ecological systems such as the arid and semi-arid regions of Iran, which are facing increasing pressures such as land use change, overexploitation, and climate change on the one hand, and on the other, they are home to communities with rich indigenous knowledge capital. In most existing assessments, the spatial and cultural dimensions of indigenous knowledge have been ignored, which has led to a decrease in the effectiveness of sustainable natural resource management plans. Also, the simultaneous analysis of spatial synergies and trade-offs between different ecosystem services and socio-ecological quality in these areas has received less attention. Accordingly, the present study seeks to fill this conceptual and practical gap and provide an integrated framework for integrating indigenous knowledge, habitat quality, and ecosystem services in semi-arid regions, thereby paving the way for participatory, conservation, and resilient ecosystem management… Hence, the study’s goals are to: 1) quantify eleven ecosystem services (beekeeping, education, gas regulation, soil stability, soil retention, healthcare plants, recreation, water yield, and aesthetics, 2) map and integrate traditional ecological knowledge and habitat quality into a framework, 3) declare the spatial trade-offs and synergy relationships between ecosystem services and social-ecological quality, and finally 4) highlight the significance of social and ecological factors in provisioning, regulating, supporting, and cultural services.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Bardsir County in southeastern Iran (at 56° 4ˊ 12″ to 57° 0ˊ7″ east longitude and 29° 23ˊ 26″ to 30° 9′ 11″ north latitude) and has an area of 3941 km2. The average annual rainfall is 210 mm, which occurs in winter. It is located in the northern part of the plain with a minimum elevation of 1862 m and in the southern part of the highlands with a maximum elevation of 4264 m (Fig. 1). Bardsir has a significant cultural history and this region is one of the first places where human social systems first developed 6000 years ago44. The best evidence for this claim is the presence of many historical monuments and monuments such as Tell Iblis; indicating that settlements occupied the study area in the 5th, 4th and 3rd millennia and where domesticated goats were first bred and perhaps also sheep and cattle by humans45. This long history of civilization has led to the formation of a dynamic and efficient indigenous knowledge related to the exploitation of natural resources, adaptation to drought, soil conservation, traditional management of water resources such as Qanats, and the use of native plant and animal species. Due to its location in an arid climate and facing severe environmental challenges such as water scarcity, desertification, and soil erosion, Bardsir is a suitable example to examine how indigenous knowledge can increase socio-ecological resilience in such fragile areas. Local people in this region, drawing on intergenerational experiences and cultural traditions, have developed specific indigenous approaches to natural resource management that can advance our understanding of the sustainable management of ecosystem services in harsh climates. Therefore, the selection of Bardsir as a case study is not only based on its historical and cultural significance, but also because of its high potential to demonstrate the linkages between indigenous knowledge, ecosystem services, and socio-environmental sustainability in drylands.

Data collection

This study considers three aspects of ecosystem services, ecosystem health and local knowledge. Ecosystem services are divided into four classes of provisioning, regulating, supporting and cultural services46. For each class, ecosystem services were selected based on drylands and local uses. The livestock rates, water yield, medical plants and beekeeping were considered as provisioning services47–49. Gas regulation and soil retention for regulating services50soil stability, and nursing function for support services were considered51. Provisioning, regulating and supporting services were quantified according to field data and the InVEST model. Cultural services was measured based on aesthetic value, education services and recreation using field and questionnaires data52. InVEST habitat quality was used to assess ecosystem health53. Traditional ecological knowledge was measured using socio-ecological quality indices54.

Nursing function

By establishing microclimates, shrubs in arid and semi-arid areas provide shelter for the growth of other understory plants. Accordingly, to document species types and numbers, a 50 × 50 cm plot was established beneath perennial shrub species at each land cover. Species diversity was evaluated using the Simpson index46.

Stocking rate

The metabolisable energy in plant fodder and the metabolic energy needed by livestock were used to calculate the stocking rate57,58. Forage quantity and quality were used to assess the amount of metabolized energy in the feed. Quadrats using the clip-and-weigh method were used to measure the amount of forage produced at each site. Forage’s metabolizable energy was also calculated using the acid detergent fiber (ADF) and nitrogen content (N)29. The Kjeldahl method was used to determine the number of samples59. Additionally, the MAFF approach60 was used to determine the daily energy requirements of animals.

Soil stability

Qualitative methods of landscape function analysis were used to evaluate soil stability. Using a landscape function analysis approach, soil surface indicators are employed to evaluate soil stability. For every perennial species (patches) in the land covers, soil surface indicators were graded. Simply adding up the values of the associated indicators allowed for the calculation of soil stability.

Beekeeping

The establishment of beehives depends on the quantity of pollen and nectar plant species61. The plant composition and nectar and pollen plant flowering periods were then used to assess whether vegetation types were suitable for beekeeping. According to Faqih et al. (2004), the time between the earliest and last blooming dates is known as the flowering period. Based on Asadi et al. (2017) and Brenton (2015), the flowering period for nectar and pollen plant species was established.

Education services

Based on earlier researches66–69 biodiversity (species diversity, and diversity of flowering plants), management (ecosystem condition), and structural indicators (road access, and water bodies) were chosen to evaluate educational services. Using a linear regression approach based on the Khosravi Mashizi and Sharafatmandrad (2024) method, the potential of education services was mapped according to the contribution of drivers. Using Google Earth and a 2008 land use map of the catchment from the Iranian Forests Range and Watershed Management Organization, roads, rivers, and springs were spatially mapped. The study basin’s roads, rivers, and springs were coded as either 0 (absence) or 1 (presence). In each land cover 50 plots of 400 m2 were randomly placed and recorded the number of plant species of trees and shrubs, Semi shrubs in 2 m2 and herbs were measured in 1m2 plots. Simpson’s index was used to measure species diversity and diversity of flowering species. Ecosystem condition was determined for each land cover based on FAO and UNEP model55,70.

Medicinal plants

Medicinal plants were identified by interviewing locals. In each land cover, medical plants was measured using the clip-and-weigh method across 50 plots, each 2 m² in size.

Gas regulation

Using the approach of Guo et al. (2001) and Li et al. (2006), the gas regulation function was estimated based on the amount of oxygen emitted during photosynthesis. For the rangeland ecosystem to produce 162 g of dry matter and 193 g of plant oxygen, roughly 108 g of water and 264 g of carbon dioxide are required. About 191 g of oxygen are emitted under Iranian conditions. Consequently, fifty 2 m2 plots for each land cover were used to assess the annual biomass increase of plant species, which was then utilized to determine ecosystem efficiency in oxygen production.

Recreation

Using a linear regression, the contribution of landscape features to the visiting rate was used to map the potential for recreation. Eighty tourists were chosen at random, and they were asked to rate the following characteristics of the destination (0–10) according on how much they could contribute to their recreational potential: distance from the city, distance from the river, distance from the road, canopy distance, and dominance species.

Traditional ecological knowledge

Community engagement activities were conducted through in-depth interviews with pastoralists and farmers, as well as field visits to key sites such as sacred forests, shrines, rangelands, and farms; places that were culturally and ecologically rich and where traditional ecological knowledge was likely to be practiced or observed. Indicators of traditional ecological knowledge were then extracted and developed through analysis of qualitative data from these observations and interviews. These indicators were selected based on the repeatability of participants’ statements, their functional role in human-environment interaction, and their prominence in daily and ritual activities. Also, seasonal activities such as collecting edible plants, holding traditional festivals, and livestock grazing management patterns in different seasons, along with social rituals and ceremonies such as marriage and funerals, were used as practical contexts reflecting traditional ecological knowledge in the process of identifying and validating these indicators (Table 1).

Indicators of traditional ecological knowledge.

Non-proportional quota sampling was used to gather data from 315 participants using in-person interviews and a standard questionnaire. The purpose of this survey was to find out participants preferences and values about traditional ecological knowledge in general. Respondents who were older (over 60) and adults (30–60) with a high level of interaction with natural ecosystems were chosen. Individuals were asked to score their perceptions of traditional ecological knowledge indicators (Table 1) based on a scale of 1 (not at all), low (2), moderate (3), high (4), and very high (5). A higher score in the evaluation of traditional ecological knowledge indicators indicates the level of awareness, practical experience, and cultural appreciation of individuals towards those indicators, which can reflect the active role of this knowledge in daily life and its continuity in collective memory. Using GS+ (Gamma design software Version 5.1.1, 2001) and Arc GIS, the spatial distribution of traditional ecological knowledge in the watershed was mapped using ordinary kriging interpolation. A socio-ecological quality index was estimated by combining habitat quality and traditional ecological knowledge.

Measuring synergy and trade-offs relations

The Jenks-natural-breaks method was used to classify social-ecological quality and ecosystem services in three levels, high, moderate and low, with codes 3, 2 and 1, respectively. Then the interaction codes (synergy or trade-offs) were determined by combining the different levels of the two groups social-ecological quality and ecosystem services using the following equation:

IC1 = SEQ×10 + ES

Where IC1 is a two-digit code that specifies the type of interaction between social-ecological quality and ecosystem services, SEQ is the level of social-ecological quality class and ES displays the ecosystem services class level. Output codes of IC1 changed from 11 to 33. Codes with similar numbers indicated synergy (11, 22, and33), and codes with different numbers indicate trade-offs between habitat quality and people health (12, 21, 31, 32, 13, 23).

Data analysis

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test were employed to compare land covers in terms of ecosystem services, habitat quality and traditional ecological knowledge. A structural equation model (SEM) was used to assess the important social-ecological drivers of ecosystem services directly and indirectly. A SEM is a statistical technique combining regression and confirmatory factor analysis that has recently been used to explore the complex direct and indirect relationships of ecosystem components. Composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) were respectively used for the reliability and validity of SEM using smartPLS v2. The AVE measures the level of variance captured by a construct versus the level due to measurement error; the level of 0.5 is acceptable. CR is a less biased estimate of reliability than Achenbach’s Alpha the acceptable value of CR is 0.7 and above.

Discussion

Spatial distribution of ecosystem services

The distribution of ecosystem services in the study area was heterogeneous. The lowest level of ecosystem services was frequently found in the plains, where anthropogenic activity intensity was higher. Highland included the most promising for beekeeping. As ecosystems degraded plains, there are fewer flowering plants and fewer honey bee food sources. According to Malkamäki et al. (2016), rangeland degradation also reduced the potential for beekeeping because it reduced the amount of flowering plants and the sources of protein and vitamins that bees need. Although this effect was not large, some agricultural crops can partially offset the loss of floral plants. There are also more flowering plants in highland, where the humidity is higher. Previous studies have demonstrated that higher humidity increases the quantity of bees that return to the hive, bringing pollen and nectar from flowers, and increased nectar production by blooming plants74. Promoting beekeeping and preserving wild bees depend on ecosystem management’s ability to protect flowering plants75,76.

Highlands also had the highest rates of medical plants and livestock rate in our study area. In this region, rangelands and forests are the primary habitats for palatable plants, and strong grazing pressure has caused the extinction of palatable species in downstream regions with more villages and easier access. According to Rokaya et al. (2016), flora is more severely damaged by livestock grazing at lower elevations than highlands. In rangeland ecosystems, the most common human activity and predominant land use is livestock use77. Forests, however, had the highest concentrations of cattle grazing. According to Willemen (2012) findings, palatable species can find suitable cover in the forest canopy. The potential for medicinal plants is greater in the highland with abundant vegetation cover. Additionally, prior research has demonstrated that medicinal plants are positively correlated (to a dense vegetative cover78. One of the biggest risks to the growth of medicinal plants was the destruction of rangeland ecosystems as a result of exploitation and conversion to agricultural land. In order to control production, guarantee quality, and enable standardization of herbal medicines—all of which can be utilized to advance medicinal plants later on—appropriate agricultural methods and sustainable exploitation strategies have been developed79. The highland had the largest water output. Previous research showed that rainfall had a significant impact on water yield49. Shrub plants can effectively contribute to water supply by increasing root depth80even though vegetation cover reduces water yield by increasing leaf area and transpiration49.

Plant growth affects climate regulation services; higher levels are seen in forests and xerophyitic shrubs with more vegetation cover. Vegetation cover is one of the most significant variables influencing climate regulation, despite the complex links between weather conditions and carbon stored in natural ecosystems81. The potential for climate regulation services is lowest in plain, where plant cover has been drastically diminished. The effects of climate change may worsen if carbon sequestration capability is reduced, underscoring the pressing need for sustainable land management techniques82,83. In addition to vegetation function, land cover significantly affected soil-related services (i.e., soil fertility and retention). The greatest potential for soil fertility and conservation services was found in forests. Additionally, prior researches have demonstrated how vegetation cover affects soil performance84,85. The stability and size distribution of soil aggregates are greatly impacted by land management, which in turn impacts soil carbon and structural stability86–88. Therefore, downstream, where land use is degrading ecosystems, the lowest level of soil services is observed.

Social-ecological quality and the ecosystem services

Our research showed a robust and favorable correlation between ecosystem services and social-ecological quality in the semi-arid area. According to the previous studies, the process of producing services involves important ecological, social, human, cultural, and financial elements89. Thus, the creation of ecosystem services can be viewed as a co-production of various forms of social, human, and financial capital as well as natural capital90,91. According to previous studies, habitat quality is essential for a number of reasons that benefit human societies, such as improved ecosystem services, food diversity, biotic and abiotic resilience to stress, and biological productivity and sustainability92 as well as human adaptation and agency related to these changes .Therefore, to achieve sustainable management in semi-arid ecosystems, it is suggested that policymakers develop a comprehensive and sustainable framework for the protection and utilization of ecosystem services by establishing participatory mechanisms, investing in habitat quality improvement, and supporting indigenous knowledge.

Because social-ecological quality and ecosystem services have a high degree of synergy, social-ecological quality using mixed methods can be offered as an alternative to biophysically based, quantitative ecosystem service assessments. Proper landscape connectivity helps sustain habitats for several species in landscapes that offer ecosystem services to humans as well as habitat quality93. To improve ecosystem services, people’s cultural practices and environmental consciousness are also crucial94. However, plains, where natural habitats have been fragmented by historical agricultural development, social-ecological quality and ecosystem services have decreased. A previous study examined the detrimental effects of landscape fragmentation on ecosystem services and habitat quality53,5. Ecosystem services and habitat quality depend on the coexistence of human and non-human species32. When aiming for a wide range of ecosystem services, it is expected that combining the conservation and management of ecosystem services with habitat quality will result in a net improvement in ecosystem conservation10,1.

Cultural services had the strongest synergistic relationship with social-ecological quality. Prior research has shown that cultural services and social-ecological quality are strongly related95–97. Because habitat quality enhances human experiences and relationships with nature, it is crucial for the delivery of cultural ecosystem services. By addressing aesthetic, recreational, spiritual, and educational demands, habitat quality enhances people’s cultural identities98,99. To satisfy the varied demands of persons with varying levels of physical fitness and awareness of various facets of nature, services like recreation must be offered100. Identifying and enhancing cultural services is an important tool for effective ecosystem conservation. The use of these services can help promote social participation, increase environmental awareness, and strengthen the cultural identity of local communities. Land-use planning, sustainable tourism development, and the integration of cultural values into environmental policies are among the most important management strategies in this area. In addition, cultural services can be used as a driver for maintaining habitat quality and promoting socio-ecological sustainability in decision-making processes101.

The role of habitat quality and traditional ecological knowledge in the managing of ecosystem services

The study’s findings demonstrated that traditional ecological knowledge (i.e., ecosystem service demand) can play a bigger impact in providing cultural services than habitat quality (i.e., ecosystem service supply). According to earlier research, changes in social traits—like following cultural norms—rather than ecological traits like patterns of habitat quality—are primarily linked to geographical patterns of cultural services102. From a management perspective, these findings emphasize the importance of directly addressing people and their preferences in environmental policymaking. Cultural services, which reflect people’s cultural values, identities, and well-being, are effective tools for engaging community participation in environmental protection103,97. In this regard, greater attention to these services in ecosystem assessments can make them contextual, culturally sensitive, and acceptable to local communities104.Cultural services, such as recreation and nature experiences, also play a significant role in human well-being105. From a sustainable development perspective, these services not only increase the quality of life, but also have a high potential for socio-ecological policymaking by creating job opportunities, reducing poverty, and improving income distribution in rural areas34,25,22. As a result, it is recommended to include these services in management and development plans as one of the main pillars of natural resource management. Habitat quality and traditional ecological knowledge both had an impact on overall service provision. According to Das et al. (2021), provisioning services are more significant to people and have a higher correlation with indigenous knowledge than other services. According to Boafo et al., traditional ecological knowledge is mostly associated with cultural and provisioning functions. Provisioning services are crucial to people’s well-being since they are the advantages that humans directly obtain from the ecosystem. Indigenous knowledge is crucial for managing ecosystem services, according to earlier research106,107,39.

Because of their long-standing engagement with ecosystems, local people have a deep attachment to, extensive knowledge of, and adaptive integration with their local natural settings38,39. To sustainably maintain ecosystem services and advance people’s well-being, it can be crucial to integrate indigenous knowledge and local culture with ecosystem services into decision-making frameworks107. In addition to being ecosystem service consumers, humans also contribute to the creation and upkeep of ecosystem services. Ecosystems are frequently actively altered by human cultures to improve, alter, or inadvertently or purposely diminish their functions108,109. The significance of environmental protection can be demonstrated by provisioning and cultural services96. These services are most strongly associated with traditional ecological knowledge because they are directly experienced and understood by people and are most directly tied to human society and well-being69. In general, it is clear that both habitat quality and traditional ecological knowledge complement each other in the sustainability and continuity of ecosystem services. However, the type of service and the socio-ecological context determine which factor plays a more prominent role. This result also highlights the importance of adaptive and context-specific policymaking in provisioning services management. Habitat quality was more important in providing regulation and supporting functions that indirectly benefit human societies than cultural variety. The significance of habitat quality in regulating and sustaining services has also been highlighted by earlier studies91. Through carbon sequestration and storage in plant biomass, litter, and soil organic matter, habitat quality plays a significant role in the carbon cycle. As a result, plant diversity can improve ecosystems’ ability to provide air purification services83. Restoring degraded native vegetation may present a chance to both improve habitat quality and produce carbon, according to Bond et al.‘s (2024) findings, which also demonstrated a favorable correlation between habitat quality and plant carbon storage. Accordingly, restoring degraded native vegetation can not only improve habitat quality but also help increase the ecosystem’s capacity to produce and store carbon. This is of great importance from the perspective of ecosystem management, especially in the face of climate change.

Findings also show that soil performance is significantly influenced by habitat quality in addition to plant performance. Soils retain carbon for decades, whereas plants absorb it from the atmosphere83. Thus, the establishment and restoration of soil fertility may be influenced by habitat quality. Because increased primary production and soil carbon storage are linked to better plant habitat quality110. If the decomposition of leaf litter returns the increased nutrients in plant biomass to the soil, higher plant habitat quality may boost soil fertility111,112. Prior research on soil conservation has demonstrated that plants have varying impacts on soil conservation. Minimizing soil erosion requires the preservation and regeneration of different plant communities113,114. The findings of this study emphasize the need to simultaneously understand and manage the relationship between natural ecosystems and human communities. These results call on policymakers to design and implement conservation programs and ecosystem management laws that take into account the role of local communities and their cultural and biological interactions with nature. Only in this way can the long-term conservation of soil and land resources, as well as the sustainability of ecosystem services, be achieved at local and regional levels.

Limitations of the study

Although this study, by utilizing an innovative and integrative approach, has provided valuable insights into the role of traditional ecological knowledge in ecosystem service management, it also has several key limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, this study was conducted in a specific region with semi-arid climatic characteristics and a specific socio-cultural context. Such characteristics may make the research results not generalizable to other regions with different ecological, cultural, or economic contexts. Therefore, comparative studies are necessary to assess the applicability of the findings on larger scales or in other regions. Second, the reliance on field interviews and indigenous knowledge, while a strength in understanding human-environment relationships, is inherently fraught with challenges such as subjectivity, respondent bias, and differences in access to or transmission of knowledge across generations. This can affect the accuracy of quantitative analyses and the quality of final interpretations. Third, in this study, it was not always easy to determine the relative contribution of habitat quality versus traditional ecological knowledge in the formation of ecosystem services, especially for services that are multi-source or overlapping (such as regulating and supporting services). The interactions between these two components are sometimes complexly intertwined, and their quantitative disentanglement requires more advanced modeling methods and more detailed data. Finally, the institutional and policy dimension is not directly included in the analysis, while local governance frameworks, conservation policies, and the degree of community participation in decision-making can play a decisive role in maintaining or weakening ecological knowledge and the continuity of ecosystem services. Considering these limitations, the results of this study can be used as a basis for future research in broader geographical and institutional contexts.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel, integrated, mixed methods approach using, plant and soil field data, interviews and geospatial methods and model to assess the role of traditional ecological knowledge and management of ecosystem services in a historically used semi-arid ecosystem. Ecosystem services were integrated according to ecological quality-ecological knowledge hotspots, where ecosystems can support ecosystem services, and where societies have desire for their conservation. Therefore when taking into account the variety of rangeland ecosystem services, the relationship between social-ecological quality and ecosystem services becomes increasingly important but complicated in terms of the management. In particular, we found a type of social-ecological supply and demand equilibrium where habitat quality and traditional ecological knowledge had an equal role in supplying provisioning services, whereas traditional ecological knowledge was more important for cultural functions. Regulation and supporting services that indirectly benefit people were more heavily influenced by habitat quality compared to ecological knowledge. Thus the relationship between traditional ecological knowledge and ecosystem services becomes more useful in management by enhancing the role of humans and communities in sustaining rangeland ecosystem services, both as drivers and beneficiaries. This is important since local knowledge has a greater impact on certain services than is typically perceived. Understanding the relationship between habitat quality and traditional ecological knowledge remains inherently complex. Although this study attempts to view the two components as complementary, the precise boundary between their impacts on each category of ecosystem services is not clearly delineated, which is a significant source of uncertainty. Environmental, climatic, and social changes that may occur in the short or medium term could affect the sustainability of the relationships examined in this study. As this study is based on cross-sectional data, the sustainability of these relationships in the long term is uncertain and requires prospective monitoring. In studies of ecosystem services and social-ecological, traditional ecological knowledge can be seen as an additional – and key - component that strengthens the connection between environmental services and human populations. These metrics can be sued as proxies for the human-nature relationships and to clarify a number of possible connections between habitat quality and traditional ecological knowledge. This is particularly relevant in situations where ecosystem-service related policies and science are difficult to communicate incorporated in the long-term conservation of socio-ecosystem and their services.
