Prevalence and psychosocial and occupational predictors of suicidal behaviors among medical residents in Iran
Authors: Ali Mivehchi, Mehdi Khezeli, Nader Rajabi-Gilan, Habibeh Matin, Hamid Jafaralilou, Maryam Mazaheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical residency is a demanding period often associated with elevated psychological distress and increased risk of suicidal behaviors (SB). This study aimed to assess the prevalence and identify key predictors of suicidal behaviors among medical residents.MethodsThis online survey was conducted in 2025 with 270 medical residents from Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, Iran. The samples were selected through census, where the questionnaire link was sent to the samples via the most widely used social media in Iran (WhatsApp and Telegram). Data were collected using validated questionnaires including Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R), General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-28), Suicide Resilience Inventory-25 (SRI-25), and a resercher made questionnaire on occupational-academic problems. Logistic regression was used to identify predictors of suicidal behaviors.ResultsNearly one-third of residents reported suicidal behaviors. Suicidal behavior was significantly higher among those had substance use (OR = 3.89, 95% CI: 1.32–7.49, P < 0.01), chronic disorders (OR = 2.78, 95% CI: 1.02–7.57, P < 0.05), and female gender (OR = 3.24, 95% CI: 1.19–8.63, P < 0.05). Higher occupational-academic problems (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.06–1.44, P < 0.01), lower resilience (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–0.99, P < 0.05), and higher problems in mental health (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01–1.10, P < 0.05), were associated with a higher likelihood of suicidal behaviors. The model explained up to 56% of the variance in suicidal behaviors.ConclusionsThe findings emphasize the urgent need for comprehensive mental health strategies in residency programs, combining individual resilience training with systemic reforms addressing workload, gender-specific challenges, and substance use. Institutional policies must focus on reducing stigma and improving access to mental health resources to safeguard resident well-being.Keywords: Drug abuse, Medical resident, Mental health, Resilience, Suicidal behaviors

Introduction

Suicide is a critical public health issue worldwide, with medical professionals, particularly medical residents, demonstrating disproportionately high rates of suicidal ideation (SI) and behavior compared to the general population [1, 2]. The demanding nature of residency training, characterized by long working hours, sleep deprivation, high responsibility, and emotional strain contributes significantly to psychological distress among residents [3, 4]. Few studies have reported alarming rates of suicidal behaviors among medical students and residents globally [4, 5]. However, there is a lack of studies on suicidal behaviors in residents compared to other medical students [6]. The critical need for understanding the multifaceted factors influencing suicidal behaviors in medical residents is evident to inform effective prevention and intervention strategies in this vulnerable population.

Globally, the mental health of medical professionals, especially those in training, has been a subject of increasing concern. Medical residency is a pivotal stage in a physician’s career, characterized by the transition from medical school to professional practice. The psychological strain faced by residents during this period has been shown to contribute significantly to depressive symptoms and SI [7, 8]. The phenomenon of physician suicide is not new, but it is only in recent years that more attention has been paid to understanding the factors that make medical residents susceptible to suicidal thoughts and behaviors [9]. This issue is particularly relevant in countries like Iran, where cultural, social, and economic factors intertwine with the high-pressure nature of medical training to exacerbate mental health concerns [10].

A range of factors contributes to SI and behaviors among medical residents. These include work-related stress, excessive working hours, sleep deprivation, emotional exhaustion, and a lack of adequate support systems [9]. West et al. [3], highlighted the importance of addressing mental health issues among medical professionals, noting that early intervention could significantly reduce the incidence of suicide attempts and improve overall well-being. Substance use is another critical factor associated with suicidal behaviors. Medical residents, given their high-stress environment, are often at risk for turning to substances as a form of coping, which may further exacerbate their mental health problems [11]. Specialties such as general surgery, psychiatry, and anesthesia are at higher risk of suicidal behavior due to access to anesthetic drugs such as ketamine and benzodiazepines [12]. As a result, the use of potent and readily available drugs in suicide attempts by healthcare professionals usually has a fatal outcome [13].

Despite these significant risks, there are protective factors that can reduce the incidence of suicidal behavior among medical residents. In addition to stress and work-related factors, individual psychological traits, such as resilience, also play a crucial role in shaping the risk of suicidal behaviors [14–16]. Studies have shown that medical students and residents with higher levels of resilience are less likely to experience severe mental health challenges, including suicidal thoughts [17]. Studies have also shown that organizational support, either from peers, mentors, or authorities, plays a key role in reducing stress and mitigating suicidal thoughts [18]. Medical residents with strong support systems are better able to cope with the emotional and psychological challenges of their training. Moreover, institutional efforts to reduce the stigma surrounding mental health, improve work-life balance, and offer psychological support have been shown to reduce suicide rates among medical professionals [19].

In Iran, similar trends are evident, with studies indicating that medical residents face heightened levels of stress, anxiety, and depression, which increase their vulnerability to SI and attempts [10]. Residents in hospitals suffer from problems such as aggression and violence from patients [20]. Other studies indicate that Iranian medical residents may be particularly vulnerable to the pressures of their profession, given the additional cultural and societal challenges they face, such as the stigma surrounding mental health issues and the lack of widespread mental health support within the healthcare system [21].

Studies on suicidal behaviors and related factors in medical residents in Iran are more limited than in other groups. Therefore, the present study aims to explore the factors contributing to suicidal behavior in medical residents at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences in Iran. Using a combination of demographic, psychological, and behavioral measures, this study examines variables such as gender, substance abuse, mental health status, resilience, and work-related stress in predicting suicidal ideation and behaviors. By identifying key risk factors, the study aims to inform future intervention strategies that can help reduce the incidence of suicide attempts among medical residents and promote a healthier, more supportive work environment for future generations of healthcare professionals.

Methods

Design and participants

This online survey was conducted among medical residents at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, western Iran, from April 2025 to June 2025. First, the questionnaire was designed online and the questionnaire link was sent to the students via social media (WhatsApp and Telegram). The sample selection was by enumeration because the number of residents studying at the university was 350. During the 3 months that the questionnaire page was available online to the respondents, 270 completed questionnaires were received (response rate 73.1%). The inclusion criteria for the study were consent to participate in the study, and studying at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences. If the questionnaire was incomplete, the subjects were excluded from the study. In order to comply with ethical principles, an informed consent form was provided on the first page of the online questionnaire, in which the participants were assured that all their information would be confidential and anonymous, and the participants were directed to the questions page after confirming the consent form.

To assess potential non-response bias, aggregated administrative data on field of study and year of residency for all medical residents at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (N = 350) were obtained. The distribution of these variables was compared between respondents (n = 270) and non-respondents (n = 80) using the chi-square test.

Measures

In this study, data were collected using the demographic information form, Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R), General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-28), Suicide Resilience Inventory-25 (SRI-25), and checklist of occupational-academic problems.

Demographic information form included demographic questions (age, expert, gender, tobacco use, the amount of social media use, drug abuse, and chronic diseases, field and year of study, and number of shifts per month). Chronic disorders were assessed using a self-reported multiple-choice question asking participants to indicate whether they currently had any of the following physician-diagnosed conditions: chronic cardiovascular disease, endocrine disorders, chronic pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, chronic gastrointestinal disorders, musculoskeletal disorders, immune or allergic disorders, or psychiatric disorders. Participants could select more than one option or indicate “none.” Drug abuse was assessed using a self-reported question asking participants whether they had a history of substance abuse or dependence. Participants who answered affirmatively were further asked to indicate the type of substances used, including alcohol, opioids (opium and its derivatives), tramadol, hallucinogens (e.g., LSD), cannabinoids (e.g., marijuana), sedative-hypnotics (e.g., benzodiazepines), amphetamines or amphetamine-type stimulants, and inhalational or intravenous anesthetic agents. and three questions related to the history of suicide attempts in the friends, suicide attempts in the family members, and self-harm. Suicidal behaviors was assessed using Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R), developed by Osman et al. [22]. This scale consists of four questions on lifetime suicide ideation and/or suicide attempt, the frequency of SI over the past twelve months, the threat of suicide attempt, and self-reported likelihood of suicidal behavior in the future. The total score ranges from 3 to 18, with a higher score indicating greater severity of suicidal behaviors. The validity and reliability of the original version were confirmed by the developers. The cutoff scores on the SBQ-R to determine individuals with suicidal behavior are set at 7 for non-suicidal samples and 8 for clinical samples [22]. Amini-Tehrani et al. [23], evaluated the validity and reliability of the Persian version of the SBQ-R. Confirmatory factor analysis confirmed the model with sound goodness-of-fit, with factor loadings of 0.70 to 0.83. Regression analysis confirmed the ability of both cut-off points to discriminate participants with high SI. Composite reliability was also reported to be 0.87. The internal consistency of the SBQ-R questions in the present study was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86.

General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-28) designed by Goldberg [24] is one of the most well-known screening tools for mental disorders, available in 12, 28, 30, and 60-item forms. In this study, the 28-item form was used, which includes four subscales: somatic symptoms, anxiety and insomnia, social dysfunction, and depression. In this study, the Likert method was used for scoring, with the options being scored as (0-1-2-3). The maximum score of the subject with this method in the aforementioned questionnaire is 84, and a lower score indicates better general health [25]. Nazifi et al. [26], confirmed the validity of the Persian version of GHQ-28 and confirmed its reliability with Cronbach’s alpha more than 0.70 for its four subscales. The internal consistency of the questions of GHQ-28 in the present study was confirmed through Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95.

Suicide Resilience Inventory-25 (SRI-25) developed by Osman et al. [27], is a 25-item questionnaire to measure individuals’ resilience to suicide. This questionnaire has three dimensions: internal protector, external protector, and emotional stability. The questions are scored on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater levels of resilience. Osman et al. [27] reported a well-established factor structure for scale. The estimated alpha for the entire questionnaire and the scales was high (0.90–0.95). Mehdiyar and Nejati [28] translated this questionnaire into Persian and confirmed its validity using factor analysis. The reliability of the questionnaire was confirmed through test-retest and Cronbach’s alpha with coefficients of 0.93 and 0.83, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha of the SRI-25 questions in the present study was 0.91.

Occupational-educational problems were assessed using a researcher-made questionnaire with 10 questions. The questions specifically addressed workload, salary, resident status, career prospects, immigration, career confusion, educational trends, intra-group problems, security, and abuse. The questions were scored on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating more occupational-academic problems. The validity of this questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of experts consisting of 5 experts in the fields of health services management, health policy, health promotion, statistics, and psychology, and 5 medical residents. The experts confirmed the necessity of the questions with a content validity ratio (CVR) of 0.92. In addition the content validity index (CVI) of 0.86 confirmed the relevance, simplicity, and clarity of the questions. Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85 indicated that the acceptable internal consistency of the questions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS-18 and STATA-8 statistical software. Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate demographic data and suicidal behaviors as mean (standard deviation) or frequency (percentage). Given the relatively small number of participants reporting chronic disorders and substance abuse, detailed subgroup analyses were not feasible; therefore, these variables were treated as dichotomous (presence vs. absence) in the analyses. Logistic regression test was performed to determine predictors of suicidal behavior. We checked and adhered to the regression assumptions as much as possible. According to the SBQ-R guideline, we classified scores less than 7 as the group without suicidal behavior and scores 7 and above as the group with suicidal behavior. Correlation test confirmed the absence of collinearity between numerical variables. In terms of independence of observations, the data lacked repeated measures, clustered and within-group data in favor of demographic variables. Outliers were checked and managed. According to the rule of thumb proposed by Tabachnik and Fidell [29], (N ≥ 50 + 8k). sample size was confirmed to be sufficient for regression. Predictor variables were first examined through univariate analysis, and significant variables were entered into the model using the forward stepwise method.

Ethical aspects of the study

The methods and procedures of this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (IR.KUMS.MED.REC.1402.180). An informed consent form was embedded on the first page of the online questionnaire, and participants were redirected to the questions page by confirming it. On this page, the study objectives were presented and, given the sensitivity of the subject, individuals were assured of confidentiality.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 270 medical residents participated in the study, of whom 57.4% were female. The mean age of participants was 35.44 ± 9.66 years. The mean number of monthly on-call shifts was 11.15 ± 1.45. Residents from a wide range of medical specialties participated, with internal medicine (11.5%), otorhinolaryngology (9.3%), cardiology (8.5%), neurology (7.8%), and surgery (7.4%) being the most common fields. Participants were almost evenly distributed across the four years of residency training.

More than one-fifth of residents (20.7%) reported having at least one chronic disease, and 10.0% reported a history of substance abuse. Regarding tobacco use, 20.4% were cigarette smokers and 7.8% reported waterpipe use. Nearly half of the participants were single (49.6%), and the majority (64.4%) reported using social media for more than two hours per day. According to the results, age, marital status, and history of SA in family members and friends, history of self-harm, social media use, tobacco use were not significant predictors of suicidal behaviors. In contrast, female gender was a significant predictor of SB (Odds Ratio [OR] = 3.24, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 1.19–8.63, P < 0.05). In fact, women were approximately 3 times more likely to have suicidal behaviors than men. Individuals who had drug abuse had a higher likelihood of reporting SB (OR = 3.89, 95% CI: 1.32–7.49, P < 0.01). Those reporting a chronic disorders were more than twice as likely to report SB (OR = 2.78, 95% CI: 1.02–7.57, P < 0.05). Higher occupational-academic problems (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.06–1.44, P < 0.01), lower resilience (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–0.99, P < 0.05), and higher problems in mental health (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01–1.10, P < 0.05), were associated with a higher likelihood of suicidal behaviors. Specifically, for each unit increase in occupational-academic problems, the likelihood of reporting SB increased by approximately 24%, for each unit increase in resilience score, the likelihood decreased by approximately 4%, and for each unit increase in problems in general health, the likelihood of reporting SB increased by approximately 6%. These findings highlight the protective role of resilience and the exacerbating role of academic-occupational problems and general health problems in relation to suicidal behaviors.

The Cox & Snell R2 and Nagelkerke R2 ​​for the model were 0.40 and 0.560, respectively. These values indicate that approximately 40% and 56% of the variance in SB can be explained by the independent variables: gender, drug abuse, chronic disease, occupational-academic problems, general health, and resilience. This suggests that 44% to 60% of the likelihood of SB is attributable to other unassessed variables.

Discussion

The present study highlights a critical and concerning prevalence of suicidal behaviors (SB) among medical residents at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, reinforcing the global recognition of mental health crises within this vulnerable group [2, 4]. In the present study, 31.5% of medical residents reported suicidal behaviors. Suicidal behaviors, including suicidal ideation and risk of suicide, are recognized challenges among medical trainees globally, reflecting the intense psychological pressures inherent in clinical training [30]. Previous studies have reported varying prevalence rates of suicidal ideation among medical residents and students, highlighting substantial regional and methodological differences. For example, in a multicenter study from Iran, approximately one-half of medical students and residents reported suicidal thoughts, with 12% indicating readiness to attempt suicide, underscoring the high psychological burden in this population [31]. Another investigation among medical residents in Tehran found that about 34.3% of participants experienced suicidal ideation, with 10.2% at high suicide risk [2]. International evidence also supports elevated rates of suicidal ideation among medical trainees: several global studies indicate that prevalence can range widely, with some systematic reviews reporting rates of suicidal ideation among medical trainees from approximately 4% to over 30% in various countries [5, 30, 32]. These figures suggest that the 31.5% prevalence observed in our study is broadly consistent with existing international and domestic research, and further highlights the urgent need for targeted mental health support within medical training environments.

The significantly higher odds of suicidal behaviors among female residents mirror global trends [33], yet this finding demands a nuanced interpretation in the Iranian cultural context. Women in medicine often face compounded pressures, including gender discrimination, societal expectations, and limited social support, which may exacerbate vulnerability to psychological distress [34, 35]. These findings underscore the urgent need for gender-sensitive approaches in mental health support within residency programs. Without addressing systemic gender biases and providing tailored resources, interventions risk failing a substantial proportion of trainees who are disproportionately affected.

Our data also reveal substance abuse as a potent predictor of SB, highlighting a well-documented but persistently challenging issue in medical training environments worldwide [36]. The paradoxical role of medical knowledge and access to medications, intended to save lives but sometimes facilitating fatal suicide attempts, poses a unique dilemma [37]. Residency programs must therefore adopt proactive substance use screening and confidential support systems, recognizing that stigmatization often prevents residents from seeking help, thus perpetuating cycles of distress and risk [2].

Chronic physical health conditions were associated with increased suicidal behaviors, a relationship reflecting complex biopsychosocial interactions [38]. The dual burden of managing illness alongside grueling training schedules may contribute to both physical and psychological exhaustion, diminishing coping capacities [39]. This finding calls for integrative health services within residency programs, where physical health management is coordinated with mental health support to address residents’ holistic well-being [2].

The significance of occupational-academic problems in predicting suicidal behaviors underscores the profound systemic challenges faced by medical residents. Excessive workloads, unclear career pathways, and perceived lack of institutional support not only foster burnout but also elevate suicide risk [39, 40]. This persistent problem challenges the medical education system to rethink training paradigms. It is no longer sufficient to view suicide risk as an individual pathology; rather, institutional reforms targeting workload regulation, mentorship quality, and workplace culture are imperative. Failure to do so risks perpetuating a toxic environment detrimental to residents’ mental health and patient care quality [41].

Our finding linking general health issues with increased suicidal behavior emphasizes the inseparable nature of physical and mental health. Symptoms such as sleep disturbance, fatigue, and somatic complaints can both signal and exacerbate mental health decline [42, 43]. Previous studies have emphasized the association between mental health problems assessed with the GHQ-28 and suicidal behaviors in medical students, but no study in this area was found in medical residents [44, 45]. Residency programs should prioritize comprehensive wellness initiatives that encompass physical health monitoring alongside psychological support to detect early warning signs and intervene effectively.

While resilience emerged as a protective factor, its relative modest effect size indicates that enhancing resilience alone is insufficient to counterbalance pervasive systemic stressors. Recent studies suggest that structured resilience training, combining mindfulness, cognitive behavioral approaches, and peer support can mitigate psychological distress [46–48], but these interventions must be embedded within broader organizational strategies [49]. Building resilience in isolation risks placing undue responsibility on medical residents to ‘cope better’ rather than addressing modifiable external risk factors [50].

The findings of this study can be interpreted within a multi-level prevention framework, emphasizing the need for both individual-level and systemic-level interventions. At the individual level, enhancing psychological resilience, improving access to mental health services, and promoting coping skills may reduce vulnerability to suicidal behavior. However, individual-focused interventions alone are insufficient. Although resilience emerged as a protective factor in this study, it should not be interpreted as a substitute for systemic reform. Overemphasizing individual resilience risks shifting responsibility from institutions to trainees, reinforcing a culture of individual bulletproofing [51]. Sustainable improvements in trainee well-being require addressing structural determinants of distress, thereby reducing the need for residents to adapt to inherently harmful training environments [40].

At the systemic level, our results highlight the critical role of occupational-academic stressors, including excessive workload, lack of professional dignity, job insecurity, and educational dissatisfaction. These findings align with international reviews emphasizing the importance of organizational culture change within medical training programs. Structural reforms such as workload regulation, fair compensation, supportive supervision, secure reporting systems, and implementation of prevention strategies after adverse events occur are essential components of effective suicide prevention among medical residents.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the relatively small sample size (270 participants out of 350 residents) reflects the inherent size of the residency programs at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences. Although the limited sample size may restrict statistical power, the high response rate enhances the representativeness of the findings within this population. Given the relative similarity of residency training conditions across Iran, cautious generalization to other Iranian medical residency settings may be reasonable; however, extrapolation to other countries or healthcare systems with different cultural, social, or institutional contexts should be made with caution.

Second, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes any inference of causal relationships. The observed associations indicate correlation rather than causation, and the directionality of these relationships cannot be determined. For example, it remains unclear whether occupational-academic problems contributed to suicidal behaviors or whether the presence of suicidal behaviors influenced residents’ perceptions of their work and educational environment. Reverse causality therefore cannot be ruled out.

Third, data were collected through an online, self-reported survey addressing sensitive topics such as suicidal behaviors and substance use. Despite assurances of anonymity, stigma surrounding mental health and substance use in Iran may have led to underreporting of these behaviors. Conversely, residents who were experiencing greater psychological distress or who had been more directly affected by suicide-related events may have been more inclined to participate, potentially introducing selection bias. Additionally, the timing of the study coincided with a recent increase in suicide attempts among residents at this university, as reflected by the high proportion of participants reporting a friend’s suicide attempt, which may have influenced awareness and reporting.

Fourth, occupational-academic problems were assessed using a researcher-made questionnaire developed to capture context-specific stressors within the local training environment. While this instrument demonstrated acceptable content validity and internal consistency, it does not encompass broader, well-validated constructs such as burnout, which has been consistently identified as a major risk factor for suicidality among physicians and medical trainees. Future studies would benefit from incorporating standardized measures of burnout alongside locally relevant tools to provide a more comprehensive assessment of occupational stress.

Finally, several potentially relevant factors, including social support, personality traits, and financial stress, were not assessed in the present study. Longitudinal and multicenter studies are recommended to better clarify causal pathways and to deepen understanding of suicidal behaviors among medical residents over time.

Conclusion

This study highlights several critical factors associated with suicidal behaviors among medical residents at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences. Female gender, drug abuse, presence of chronic disease, occupational-academic problems, poor general health, and lower resilience emerged as significant predictors of suicidal behavior. These findings emphasize the complex interplay between personal, psychological, and occupational factors in shaping mental health outcomes in this vulnerable population.

From a practical standpoint, the high prevalence of suicidal ideation and behaviors among residents demands urgent and targeted interventions within medical training environments. Institutions should prioritize the development and implementation of comprehensive mental health programs that include regular screening for SI, substance abuse, and psychological distress. Enhancing resilience through resilience-building workshops, stress management training, and peer support groups can serve as effective protective strategies.

Furthermore, addressing systemic issues related to workload, educational pressures, and workplace environment is essential. Residency programs must strive to improve work-life balance by optimizing duty hours and providing accessible mental health resources. Institutional policies should also focus on reducing stigma surrounding mental health care seeking, especially for women and those with chronic health conditions who appear to be at higher risk. Given the strong association between occupational stressors and suicidal behaviors, collaboration between academic institutions, healthcare administrators, and mental health professionals is critical to design evidence-based interventions tailored to the specific needs of residents. Regular mental health education, confidential counseling services, and mentorship programs can help create a supportive culture that fosters well-being and reduces suicide risk.

In summary, the findings from this study contribute valuable insights for healthcare policymakers and medical educators aiming to safeguard the mental health of medical residents. Proactive and sustained efforts to identify at-risk individuals and address modifiable risk factors can ultimately improve resident well-being, reduce suicide rates, and promote a healthier, more productive healthcare workforce.

For future research, longitudinal studies should be designed in several Iranian centers to establish causality and follow up on the results. It is suggested that future research should include standard measures of burnout (e.g., Maslach Burnout Inventory) and job strain. Qualitative studies can also provide deeper insight into the complexities of suicidal behaviors in residents and their specific relationship with occupational-academic problems.
