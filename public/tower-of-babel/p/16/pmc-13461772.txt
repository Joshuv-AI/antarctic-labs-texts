Biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease to differentiate normal, SCD, and MCI subjects and their correlation with cognitive function
Authors: Allal Boutajangout, Ricardo S. Osorio, Arjun V. Masurkar, Ludovic Debure, Mobeena Ghuman, Wajiha Ahmed, Elizabeth Pirraglia, Alok Vedvyas, Jon Links, Brianna Vega, Karyn Marsh, Joshua Chodosh, Yongzhao Shao, Thomas Wisniewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe assessed plasma biomarkers for the diagnosis of early Alzheimer's disease (AD).METHODS:Subjects were divided into three groups: cognitively unimpaired (CU) (without subjective cognitive decline [SCD]) (n = 113), CU with SCD (n = 152), and mild cognitive impairment (MCI, n = 45). Plasma assays for amyloid beta (Aβ) 40, Aβ42, neurofilament light chain protein, glial fibrillary acidic protein, and phosphorylated tau181 levels were measured using single molecule array (Simoa) technology. Neuroinflammation and blood‐brain barrier (BBB) biomarkers were measured using the Corplex cytokine 10‐Plex kit and the angiogenesis 6‐Plex kit, respectively.RESULTSBiomarker levels were regressed by cognitive group, age, sex, race, and apolipoprotein E apoE ε4 status, yielded significant positive associations between age and numerous AD, neuroinflammation, cytokine, and BBB plasma markers.DISCUSSIONLinear regression analysis, adjusted for age, sex, race, and ApoE status, revealed significant differences between cognitive groups in levels of several plasma biomarkers and associations with age and sex. Neuroinflammation and BBB dysfunction showed significant positive associations with age across different stages of AD.

BACKGROUND

The early diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease (AD)‐related pathology enables patients to receive treatments to delay the onset of AD symptoms and slow progression from cognitively unimpaired (CU) to subjective cognitive decline (SCD) and progression to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 The various stages of pre‐dementia leading up to AD can last for many years. It was reported that the risk of development of MCI and dementia is increased in individuals with SCD, and most SCD patients undergo progressive cognitive decline. 5 , 6 , 7 Ideal AD biofluid biomarkers would not only reliably discriminate between patient groups in the pre‐dementia stages but also predict transitions from SCD to MCI and MCI to AD. The most established CSF and blood‐based biomarkers are Aβ42, phosphorylated tau (p‐tau), and total tau, which are used to identify AD pathology in individuals with MCI. 8 , 9 , 10 CSF is an excellent source of biofluid AD biomarkers. However, this poses challenges as lumbar punctures are invasive and patients can be reluctant to consent to the procedure. 11 , 12 With the recent approval of the Lumipulse G1200 technology for measuring the p‐tau217/Aβ1‐42 ratio in plasma, measuring biofluid biomarkers is becoming more common for the facilitation of the diagnosis of AD and AD and related dementias (ADRD). 13 , 14 , 15 However, given the rapidly aging world population, it is imperative to study strengthen the robustness of available biomarkers for assessing and monitoring disease development and progression. Blood biomarkers are suited for studying longitudinal molecular changes and predicting clinical outcomes, including SCD. 16 , 17 , 18 Published studies on plasma samples show different levels of biomarker expression in AD and in CU individuals. However, the accuracy of plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and neurofilament light change (NfL) in predicting AD pathology in SCD and MCI patients has thus far been largely unexplored. 19 , 20 Further investigations are needed to identify specific biomarkers for each stage of pre‐dementia AD and other neurodegenerative diseases (ND) and to improve the diagnostic and prognostic capabilities of blood tests for the AD/ADRD diagnosis. 16 , 21 , 22 In this manuscript, we discuss various plasma markers of AD, neuroinflammation, and blood‐brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction among subjects with varying cognitive status. We compare all biomarker levels among subjects categorized as CU, CU with SCD, or MCI and simultaneously examine differences in these markers relative to scores on the Mini‐Mental Status Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).

METHODS

Participants

Three hundred ten participants >60 years of age were recruited from the New York University (NYU) Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) using consecutive recruitment. Inclusion criteria included fluency in English or Spanish, a study partner fluent in English or Spanish, and a willingness to undergo neuroimaging and phlebotomy. Exclusion criteria included significant non‐neurodegenerative neurologic disease (e.g., multiple sclerosis), organ failure, organ transplant, HIV/AIDS, ongoing alcohol or drug abuse, and a history of severe psychiatric illness. This cohort includes CU without SCD (CU) (n = 113), CU with SCD (n = 152), and MCI (n = 45). Participants were evaluated through a review of medical history and interviews with patients and their designated study partners. Physicians conducted neurological and neuropsychological assessments. Participants underwent the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set version 3.0 (UDSv3) clinical evaluation and psychometric testing, with additional NYU‐specific clinical measures. These data informed a diagnosis of CU, SCD, or MCI determined by a clinical consensus conference.

Plasma biomarkers

Plasma samples were collected from participants at the NYU ADRC using our standard protocol, and plasma biomarkers were evaluated at the NYU ADRC Biomarker Core (BMC). Plasma levels of Aβ40, Aβ42, NfL, and GFAP were measured using the Neurology 4‐Plex E Advantage Plus Kit. P‐tau181 levels were measured using the p‐tau V2 kit. Neuroinflammatory markers (i.e., interleukin [IL] 12p70 [IL‐12p70], IL‐1b, IL‐4, IL‐5, interferon gamma (IFNℽ), IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐22, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α), and IL‐10) and markers of blood‐brain‐barrier dysfunction (i.e., human angiopoietin‐2 [hang2], heparin‐binding epidermal growth factor (EGF)‐like growth factor [HB‐EGF], placental growth factor [PlGF], fibroblast growth factor [FGFb], vascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF], hepatocyte growth factor [HGF]) were measured using the Corplex Cytokine Panel 1 10‐Plex kit and angiogenesis 6‐Plex kit, respectively, via the Simoa SP‐X. The Aβ42/Aβ40 and p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratios were calculated.

Cognitive tests and staging

Comprehensive neuropsychological tests were conducted for all patients. Cognition was assessed using the NACC UDSv3 psychometric battery, which included the MoCA, and other tests, including the MMSE. Cognitive impairment on psychometric tests was defined by a Z‐score < −1.5, corrected for age, sex, and education. SCD was evaluated using the Global Deterioration Scale (GDS) staging system. 23 Function was determined using the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR). Participants were considered CU if their CDR was 0 and GDS stage was 1. Participants were considered to have SCD if they had a CDR of 0 and a GDS stage of 2. A diagnosis of MCI was made if participants were cognitively impaired and had a CDR of 0.5.

Statistical analysis

Positively skewed plasma biomarkers were log‐transformed to approximate normality to conduct robust linear regression analyses for various plasma markers as outcome variables. All continuous variables were then standardized to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 using the formula (X − mean(X))/SD(X), where X denotes the variable of interest. In the linear regression models, biomarker levels were regressed on cognitive group, age, sex, race, and ApoE4 (non‐carriers as the reference group compared to ε4 heterozygotes and ε4 homozygotes). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate‐adjusted q values additionally reported for the primary biomarker association tests using the function p.adjust() in R. 24 Multiple‐comparisons adjustment was applied within a priori defined biomarker families (AD biomarkers, cytokines, and BBB‐related biomarkers) and analyzed separately because they represent biologically distinct hypothesis sets. Covariates included for confounder adjustment (i.e., race and ApoE ε4) were not treated as primary inferential tests and were therefore not included in the multiple‐comparisons correction. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1), and models were evaluated for violations of standard regression assumptions.

Correlation between plasma biomarkers and cognitive groups

In linear regression models, biomarkers were regressed on cognitive group, age, sex, and race. We found significant positive associations with age for Aβ40, GFAP, NfL, and p‐tau181 (all at p < 0.001), Aβ42 (p = 0.008), and for the p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio (p = 0.004) (Table 2). Among cognitive groups, MCI had lower Aβ42 levels than CU participants (p = 0.042) (Table 2), and the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio was significantly higher in CU participants than in MCI participants (p = 0.007) (Figure 1A; Table 2) and in SCD (p = 0.008, table not shown). The p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio was significantly higher in MCI than in CU (p = 0.019) (Figure 1B). GFAP levels in plasma differed significantly between the MCI and CU groups (p = 0.012), and similar effects were observed when comparing MCI with SCD (p = 0.027) (Figure 1C). Correlation between plasma markers and MMSE, age, sex, and race

Cognitive tests showed higher MMSE scores associated with lower GFAP (p < 0.001), lower NfL (p = 0.02), lower Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio (p = 0.024), and lower PlGF levels (p = 0.008) (Table 3).

Examination of all plasma biomarker assays using multiple comparison corrections

Multiple‐comparisons adjustment was applied within AD biomarkers, cytokines, and BBB‐related biomarkers, excluding race and ApoE ε4. Multiple comparisons show a significant positive association with age for Aβ40, GFAP, NfL, and p‐tau181 (all at p < 0.004), Aβ42 (p = 0.028), and p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio (p = 0.014) (Table S1). Significant results were also obtained with sex, with women exhibiting higher GFAP (p = 0.004) and p‐tau181 (p = 0.005). The p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio was significantly lower in women (p = 0.025). Among cognitive groups, MCI exhibited trends of lower Aβ42 levels, as opposed to CU participants (p = 0.098) (Table S1). The Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio was significantly higher in CU participants than in MCI participants (p = 0.033) (Table S1). The p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio shows a higher trend in MCI versus CU (p = 0.053). Plasma GFAP levels were significantly higher in the MCI versus CU groups (p = 0.045), and a trend was observed between MCI and SCD, with MCI patients having higher amounts of GFAP plasma (p = 0.056) (Table S1).

Neuroinflammatory biomarkers associated with age

Neuroinflammatory markers had statistically significant positive associations with age: IL‐22 (p = 0.020) and TNF‐α (p = 0.020) (Table S1).

BBB dysfunction associated with age

Markers of BBB dysfunction exhibited statistically significant associations with age: Hang2 (p = 0.012) and HGF (p = 0.012) (Table S1).

Correlation between plasma biomarkers and MoCA

Cognitive tests showed that a higher MoCA score was associated with lower GFAP (p = 0.046) and p‐tau181 levels (p = 0.014) and a lower p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio (p = 0.028) (Table S2).

Correlation between plasma biomarkers and MMSE

Cognitive tests showed that higher MMSE scores were associated with lower GFAP levels (p = 0.0060), and a trend was observed with lower NfL levels and higher Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

SCD represents a late preclinical stage of AD in which individuals report SCD but perform within normal limits on objective testing. 18 , 25 As pathology accumulates, cognitive performance eventually crosses below age‐, sex‐, and education‐adjusted normative thresholds, marking the transition to MCI (i.e., the prodromal phase of AD) and subsequently to dementia. Characterizing plasma biomarker profiles across these pre‐dementia stages is therefore critical for identifying individuals at greatest risk of progression and informing the timing of therapeutic interventions. 10 Aβ42, Aβ40, and p‐tau (p‐tau181, p‐tau231, and p‐tau217) levels in neurodegenerative diseases play a crucial role in diagnosing AD and ADRDs. These p‐tau protein levels increase in association with the degree of Aβ and tau pathology 26 , 27 , 28 and also predict cognitive impairment in the future. 15 , 29 , 30 Previously published data have demonstrated that CSF biomarker analysis is crucial for detecting early signs of AD and for tracking disease progression at various stages. 31 However, in recent years, there has been a collective shift toward investigating plasma and/or serum for this purpose. Blood tests are easily accessible, well suited for repeat and follow‐up visits in large, longitudinal cohorts, non‐invasive and inexpensive, and a highly accurate way to measure biomarkers diagnostic of AD/ADRD.

It has been reported that the Aβ42/Aβ40 and p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratios in CSF are strong predictors of PET amyloid positivity, improving diagnostic accuracy compared with Aβ42 alone. Plasma biomarkers represent an alternative for monitoring AD progression that could overcome some limitations associated with CSF. 32 , 33 , 34

The age‐related increases observed across Aβ40, Aβ42, GFAP, NfL, and p‐tau181 are consistent with the established accumulation of AD pathology and neurodegeneration across the lifespan and confirm that these biomarkers behave as expected in an aging community‐based cohort. The significantly higher GFAP levels in women, independent of cognitive group, are consistent with emerging evidence of sex‐specific differences in astrocyte reactivity and may partly explain the well‐documented higher AD risk in women, though longitudinal data will be needed to determine whether this reflects differential vulnerability or disease trajectory. Among cognitive groups, the significantly higher p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio and lower Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio in MCI compared to CU participants suggest greater amyloid burden and tau co‐pathology at the MCI stage, consistent with the ATN framework and supporting the biological validity of the clinical classification used here.

The lower Aβ42 levels and Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio observed in MCI relative to CU, combined with the higher p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio, together indicate a pattern of amyloid accumulation and tau co‐pathology that is consistent with established AD biomarker trajectories at the MCI stage. Notably, Aβ42 did not differ significantly between MCI and SCD, which may reflect early amyloid dysregulation already present at the SCD stage, though this interpretation requires confirmation with amyloid PET. The age‐related increases in pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐5, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, alongside anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 and IL‐22, are broadly consistent with the phenomenon of inflammaging and do not in themselves distinguish AD‐related neuroinflammation from general immune senescence. The absence of significant cytokine differences across cognitive groups, therefore, limits conclusions about neuroinflammation as a disease‐stage‐specific mechanism in this cohort. Among BBB markers, the significantly lower HB‐EGF levels in MCI participants compared with CU participants are noteworthy, given HB‐EGF's role in supporting vascular integrity and cellular repair. 35 , 36 A reduction in circulating HB‐EGF at the MCI stage may reflect impaired neurovascular maintenance, though the directionality and mechanistic significance of this association require longitudinal and experimental follow‐up. PlGF emerged as the most consistently associated BBB marker across cognitive groups, with higher levels in MCI compared to SCD, and significant inverse associations with both MoCA and MMSE scores. PlGF is a proangiogenic factor implicated in pathological neovascularization and vascular inflammation, 37 and its elevation at the MCI stage is consistent with compensatory but dysfunctional vascular remodeling in the context of advancing AD pathology. The age‐related increases in Ang2 and HGF reflect known vascular changes with aging and are not specific to AD stage in this cohort. FGFb results should be interpreted cautiously, given the substantially reduced sample size available for that assay (n = 40). Collectively, these findings support the involvement of BBB dysfunction in the transition from normal cognition to MCI, with PlGF representing a candidate marker warranting further investigation in longitudinal cohorts.

Cognitive testing showed that higher MoCA scores were associated with lower GFAP, p‐tau181, and p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio. Patients with MCI (i.e., lower MoCA scores) were found to have significantly higher levels of PlGF. MMSE test results showed a significant effect with GFAP, NfL, and the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio (i.e., the lower the MMSE score, the higher the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, GFAP, and NfL, indicating an increased risk of amyloid plaque deposition, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration).

For multiple‐comparisons adjustment within AD biomarkers, cytokines, and BBB‐related biomarkers, positive associations were observed with age for Aβ40, GFAP, NfL, p‐tau181, Aβ42, and the p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio. A significant effect distinguishing MCI from CU is seen with GFAP levels and the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio. Trends were observed with the p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio and Aβ42 levels. Biological sex difference was observed, with women exhibiting higher GFAP, p‐tau181, and p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio. Elevated biomarkers indicate strong neuroinflammation and steeper cognitive decline in women, especially during the MCI stage. Significant effects were observed for TNF‐α and IL‐22 when their associations with age were studied. In normally aging individuals, TNF‐α levels rise, and can be used to determine significance levels for AD risk and progression. IL‐22 infiltrates the brain via the BBB and contributes to chronic neuroinflammation. A trend was also observed between MCI and SCD for PlGF levels, indicating the activation of the angiogenesis cascade, which may not bring about an ideal consequence for brain tissue. Higher MoCA scores were associated with lower GFAP, p‐tau181, and the p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio. MMSE scores are highly associated with GFAP and show trends with NfL levels and the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio. Hence, these multiple comparisons lowered the false‐positive rates of biomarker panels. In this study, we analyzed p‐tau181, but both p‐tau181 and p‐tau217 are highly specific for AD. In terms of diagnostics, p‐tau217 in plasma has a higher area and larger fold changes than p‐tau181. P‐tau217 manifests earlier in the Alzheimer's continuum and correlates with higher amyloid plaques and tau PET. P‐tau181 was the first validated blood‐based tau biomarker using Quanterix's ultrasensitive Simoa technology and has since proven to be a superior marker with higher throughput precision and low variability. Future studies will aim to combine both markers into a multimodal diagnostic model. Furthermore, the small MCI sample size was a core limitation. This limitation may affect the power analysis performed in this group. Our study aligns with the current needs of an adversely effected population and will serve as an important pilot investigation. As a future direction, we will expand the sample size to allow for a more robust statistical analysis to confirm our findings.

These results stipulate the need to clearly identify pre‐dementia stages of AD, to aid in the provision of a targeted course of treatment and management for patients, regardless of the state of disease progression. Accurately distinguishing pre‐dementia stages has the potential to improve disease outcomes and also to identify novel therapeutic targets. Developing an improved panel of clinical and blood‐based biomarkers is critical to improving the accuracy of pre‐dementia and AD/ADRD diagnoses.
