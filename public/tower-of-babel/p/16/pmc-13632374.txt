Organizational determinants of professional ethics among healthcare workers: implications for health system performance
Authors: Khalil Maleki Chollou, Towhid Babazadeh, Amir Hossien Rostami, Saba Arabnezhad, Fatemeh Mousavinezhad, Erfan Saeedi Tazekand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProfessional ethics is a critical component of healthcare workforce performance and plays an essential role in patient safety, service quality, and organizational accountability. However, professional ethics may be influenced by workplace conditions and individual competencies. This study aimed to evaluate the predictive role of occupational stress and information literacy on professional ethics among healthcare employees while accounting for the potential confounding effects of sociodemographic characteristics.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among 336 staff members of Sarab University of Medical Sciences selected through multi-stage cluster random sampling (April–August 2024). Data were collected using standardized instruments: the Cadozier Professional Ethics Questionnaire, the Information Literacy Scale, and the HSE Job Stress Questionnaire. After applying rank-based inverse normal transformation to non-normal variables, analyses included descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlations, and hierarchical multiple regression to assess predictive factors.ResultsProfessional ethics was strongly correlated with information literacy (r = 0.702, p < 0.001) and with higher HSE job stress scores, indicating lower levels of occupational stress (r = 0.543, p < 0.001), whereas demographic characteristics showed no significant associations except income (p < 0.001). In the final regression model, information literacy was the strongest predictor of professional ethics (β = 0.561, p < 0.001), followed by lower occupational stress (β = 0.213, p < 0.001). Demographic factors accounted for only a small portion of the variance (R² = 0.135), while the full model explained 55.6% of the variance in professional ethics scores.ConclusionsProfessional ethics among healthcare employees is shaped not only by individual values but also by workplace stressors and cognitive competencies. Interventions aimed at reducing occupational stress and strengthening information literacy may enhance professional ethics, improve workforce performance, and contribute to health system quality improvement. Future research should explore causal mechanisms and evaluate targeted organizational interventions.Keywords: Professional ethics, Information literacy, Occupational stress, Healthcare workers, Ethical decision-making

Introduction

The efficiency and caliber of healthcare systems are intrinsically linked to the competence, psychological well-being, and professional integrity of the medical workforce [1]. Beyond clinical proficiency, the moral conduct of employees is a cornerstone for ensuring patient safety, fostering public trust, and enhancing organizational accountability [2]. In today’s increasingly sophisticated clinical environments—marked by rapid technological shifts and mounting service demands—identifying the determinants of principled behavior has become a priority in health services research [3].

Historically, adherence to social and moral norms has been a fundamental pillar of human civilization. As societal structures have evolved, these considerations have become more multifaceted [4], leading the modern world toward a paradigm centered on rationality and value-based frameworks [5]. Within the humanities, the study of such values examines the essence of human behavior [6], rooted in an inherent inclination toward virtue that governs all interpersonal relationships [7].

In the organizational context, realizing these ideals requires a clear distinction between general and vocational standards [8]. Professional ethics, a specialized branch of this field, provides a framework for addressing dilemmas specific to various occupations [9]. It encompasses a set of internalized rules that practitioners follow voluntarily, driven by intrinsic motivation rather than external compulsion [10]. Specifically, within the framework of organizational psychology, this construct is often operationalized as a set of ethical attitudes and perceptions that guide individual decision-making [11]. Rather than measuring direct behavioral outcomes or the broad organizational climate, this approach focuses on the practitioners’ internal commitment to core values—such as accountability, honesty, and loyalty—which collectively constitute their professional identity.For healthcare providers—including clinicians, nurses, and pharmacists—this responsibility is particularly grave, as their actions directly impact societal health outcomes [12, 13]. Consequently, many nations have implemented rigorous regulations to institutionalize these standards [14].

Concurrently, occupational strain has emerged as a pervasive challenge, significantly undermining individual performance [15]. Often characterized by unstable work conditions, anxiety, and exhaustion, job-related stress poses a threat to both physical and mental health [16, 17]. When these workplace pressures remain unmanaged, they can lead to debilitating psychological conditions [18]. While a core tenet of professional conduct is the optimal execution of duties through personal responsibility [19], empirical evidence suggests that high levels of distress can impair this capacity. Under significant pressure, individuals may struggle to maintain high standards, potentially leading to a compromise in professional integrity [20].

Furthermore, the rapid advancement of technology has made information literacy a critical determinant of professional success [21]. The modern information explosion necessitates sophisticated strategies for navigating, evaluating, and synthesizing data [22]. In nursing, for instance, a high level of information competency enables practitioners to remain updated on evidence-based protocols and clinical innovations [23]. From an ethical standpoint, such proficiency ensures that care is not only respectful but also grounded in the most current and effective practices [24].

Despite growing recognition of these factors, the interplay between job stress, information literacy, and professional ethics in healthcare settings remains insufficiently explored. Several studies have examined job stress and ethical behavior in isolation [25, 26], and others have investigated information literacy as a standalone competency [27, 28]; however, few have simultaneously assessed the combined predictive contribution of both variables on professional ethics while controlling for sociodemographic confounders. This gap is particularly notable in low- and middle-income country contexts, where healthcare workers face compounded occupational pressures and uneven access to information resources [29]. Addressing this gap is not merely an academic exercise—understanding how these factors jointly shape professional ethics can directly inform workforce development policies and quality improvement frameworks at the organizational level.

Given that job stress is a significant factor influencing workforce performance and information literacy represents a key competency for evidence-informed practice, understanding their collective impact on professional ethics is essential for healthcare quality. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the predictive role of job stress and information literacy on the professional ethics of healthcare employees, while accounting for the potential confounding effects of sociodemographic characteristics (such as age, sex, income, and department). Specifically, this study aimed to determine: the extent to which information literacy and job stress correlate with professional ethics, and how much of the variance in professional ethics can be uniquely explained by these cognitive and occupational factors after controlling for demographic variables. By clarifying these interactions, the findings may inform targeted organizational strategies to enhance ethical standards within healthcare systems.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted on employees working at the Sarab University of Medical Sciences and its affiliated centers (including Imam Khomeini Hospital of Sarab and the county health centers) from April to August 2024.

Participants selection

To determine the minimum sample size for this study, Morgan’s table was employed [30]. Considering a population size of 1036 individuals, Morgan’s table estimated an initial sample size of approximately 280. Given factors such as potential non-response, sampling error, and the need to ensure adequate representation of the population, a 20% attrition rate was anticipated. Consequently, the final sample size was adjusted to 336 to account for this potential loss.

A multi-stage cluster random sampling technique was adopted for sample selection. This method was chosen due to the hierarchical structure of the study population. In the first stage, the departments affiliated with the Sabar Medical University (such as the departments of treatment, education, health, and development) were designated as primary clusters. These departments were considered homogeneous units due to differences in their functions and employee characteristics. The sample size for each cluster was determined proportionally to the number of employees within that cluster. In other words, larger departments contributed a larger proportion to the sample. The following formula was used to calculate the sample size for each cluster i:

Sample size of cluster i = (Number of employees in cluster i / Total number of employees) * Total sample size.

After determining the sample size for each cluster, a random number table was used to randomly select employees from each department. A complete list of employees in each department was compiled and numbered sequentially. Subsequently, using the random number table, the required number of employees were selected from each department.

Data gathering process

Following ethical approval, participants were recruited from the four primary departments of Sarab University of Medical Sciences: Treatment, Health, Education, and Development. The primary researcher visited these departments and their affiliated centers (including Imam Khomeini Hospital and county health centers) to recruit participants through a face-to-face approach.

To ensure comprehensive coverage across all sectors, recruitment was conducted during various work shifts. In the Treatment and Health departments, where staff are involved in direct patient care or community services, data collection was scheduled during shift handovers or break periods to avoid any disruption to clinical services. In the Education and Development departments, recruitment took place during regular office hours.

After a brief explanation of the study’s goals and ensuring data confidentiality, paper-based questionnaire packets were distributed to eligible employees. To encourage honest responses and maintain anonymity, participants were provided with envelopes to seal their completed questionnaires before returning them to a designated collection point in each department. This process continued until the calculated sample size for each cluster was achieved.

Data collection instruments

Demographic questionnaire

This researcher-made form included variables such as age, sex, marital status, education level, occupation, number of children, and years of service. Family income was assessed using a subjective self-report measure where participants categorized their financial status into three levels relative to their monthly expenses: “income is less than expenses” (low), “income is equal to expenses” (moderate), and “income exceeds expenses” (high).

Professional ethics questionnaire

To measure participants’ level of professional ethics, the Persian version of the standardized Cadozier Professional Ethics Questionnaire was employed [31]. This questionnaire consists of 16 items using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) to assess various dimensions of professional ethics, including responsibility, honesty, justice, loyalty, competitiveness, respect for others, empathy, and adherence to social values and norms. This instrument is specifically designed to capture the attitudinal dimension of professional ethics, reflecting the internal disposition and moral perceptions of employees regarding their professional duties, rather than evaluating direct behavioral performance or the overall organizational ethical climate. The total score ranges from 16 to 80, with higher scores indicating higher levels of professional ethics. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire have been confirmed by Razavi et al. with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.83, demonstrating high internal consistency [32]. In the present study, the questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.898. While the Cadozier questionnaire covers multiple dimensions of professional ethics, the present study focused on the total professional ethics score as the primary outcome variable. This approach was adopted to provide a holistic assessment of professional ethics in relation to job stress and information literacy, and to ensure sufficient statistical power for the hierarchical regression models. Consequently, sub-dimensional analyses were not performed separately.

Information literacy questionnaire

The information literacy level of participants was measured using the employee information literacy questionnaire developed by Siamak and Davarpanah [33]. This instrument was originally developed in Persian and is specifically designed to assess information literacy within the Iranian organizational environment This questionnaire comprises 15 items on a 5-point Likert scale, assessing components such as the ability to determine the scope and nature of information, the ability to effectively access information, the ability to critically evaluate information, the ability to apply information purposefully, and the understanding of legal and economic implications of information use. The total score ranges from 15 to 75. The reliability of this instrument, as calculated by Siamak and Davarpanah using Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.85, indicating high reliability [33]. In the present study, the Information Literacy Questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.877.

The HSE (Health and Safety Executive) job stress questionnaire

The HSE questionnaire was used to measure job stress [34]. For the purpose of this study, we utilized the Persian adaptation of the HSE questionnaire, which was translated and culturally validated for the Iranian population by Azad-marzabadi and Gholami. Job stress encompasses dimensions such as demands, control, managerial support, peer support, relationships, role, and change, which serve as indicators of job stress and are measured through the questionnaire. This questionnaire consists of 35 items on a 5-point Likert scale, with a minimum score of 35 and a maximum of 175. Crucially, the scoring of this instrument is inverse; higher total scores indicate lower levels of occupational stress (reflecting better psychosocial working conditions), while lower scores represent higher levels of stress. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire have been confirmed by Azad-marzabadi and Gholami, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.78, demonstrating high internal consistency [35]. In the present study, the HSE Job Stress Questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.890. For ease of interpretation, all reported associations involving the HSE questionnaire are described in terms of occupational stress levels (i.e., higher HSE scores indicate lower occupational stress).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27. Prior to performing inferential tests, data screening procedures including assessment of normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity were performed. While the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests indicated deviations from normality for some variables, the large sample size justifies the use of parametric tests due to the Central Limit Theorem. To ensure maximum robustness, we initially compared results using both raw and rank-based inverse normal transformed (INT) data; as both yielded identical patterns of significance, the final models were performed using the original scores to facilitate the direct interpretation of coefficients. Following descriptive statistics, group differences were examined using independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA. Pearson correlation coefficients and hierarchical multiple regression analysis were employed to evaluate the predictive role of information literacy and job stress. To ensure the validity of the regression models, several diagnostics were performed. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance values; all VIF values ranged from 1.028 to 3.112, and tolerance values ranged from 0.321 to 0.973, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Furthermore, residual analysis was conducted by examining P-P plots and scatterplots of standardized residuals, confirming that the assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity, and independence of errors were met. For each model, R², ΔR², F-change, standardized coefficients (β), and significance values were reported.

Hierarchical multiple regression was performed to assess the contribution of information literacy and occupational stress in predicting professional ethics after controlling for demographic characteristics. In the first step, demographic variables explained a modest proportion of the variance in professional ethics (R2 = 0.135, p < 0.001). Income and sex were significant positive predictors, whereas departmental affiliation had a negative effect. Adding information literacy in the second step significantly improved the model (R2 = 0.527, ΔR2 = 0.392, p < 0.001). Information literacy was the strongest predictor of professional ethics. In the third step, occupational stress was included, leading to a further improvement (R2 = 0.556, ΔR2 = 0.029, p < 0.001). Since the HSE scale is scored such that higher values represent lower stress, the positive coefficient (β) observed in this step indicates that lower levels of occupational stress are significantly associated with higher professional ethics scores. Information literacy continued to remain the most powerful predictor. Overall, these findings emphasize that professional ethics among medical university staff is shaped primarily by information literacy and, to a lesser extent, by reduced occupational stress, whereas demographic variables play only a minor role. Discussion

The present study examined professional ethics as a workforce-related outcome influenced by cognitive and psychosocial determinants within a healthcare organization. The findings indicate that information literacy and occupational stress, rather than most demographic characteristics, were significant predictors of professional ethics among employees. This pattern is consistent with contemporary perspectives in organizational and health services research, which increasingly conceptualize professional ethics not merely as an individual moral trait but as being associated with workforce capacity and organizational conditions.

The strong association between information literacy and professional ethics represents one of the central findings of this study. Although empirical research directly linking information literacy to professional ethics remains limited, theoretical and indirect evidence supports this relationship. Ethical decision-making models emphasize the role of cognitive processing, critical evaluation, and access to reliable information in shaping moral judgments [36, 37]. In healthcare settings where decisions are often complex, evidence-based, and time-sensitive the ability to identify credible sources, interpret guidelines, and apply updated knowledge may reduce reliance on heuristics and minimize ethically problematic actions. Furthermore, research on health information technology has demonstrated that improved information handling and system literacy contribute to patient safety and error reduction [38]. While these studies do not measure professional ethics directly, they suggest that cognitive competencies are structurally linked to responsible professional conduct. Our findings provide empirical support for these conceptual assumptions by demonstrating that information literacy independently is associated with ethical attitude scores even after controlling for demographic variables and occupational stress.

However, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously. The information literacy instrument assessed self-perceived competencies rather than objective performance. Prior research has shown that perceived competence does not always correspond to actual skill level [39]. Therefore, the observed association may partly reflect self-efficacy or confidence rather than purely technical information-processing ability. Moreover, because both information literacy and professional ethics were assessed using self-report questionnaires, common method variance and some overlap in participants’ self-perceptions may have modestly strengthened the observed association, although the two instruments assess conceptually distinct constructs. Future research employing performance-based assessments of information literacy could clarify whether the predictive effect remains robust when objective measures are used.

Occupational stress also showed a significant, though smaller, contribution to professional ethics. This finding aligns with experimental and observational studies indicating that stress impairs moral reasoning and increases cognitive-effort avoidance [40, 41]. Chronic stress has been associated with reduced moral sensitivity and diminished ethical courage in healthcare trainees [42]. In organizational contexts, stress has been identified as a barrier to ethical decision-making and adherence to professional standards, particularly in high-demand environments such as infectious disease control settings [26]. From a health services perspective, occupational stress may be reflective of systemic factors, including workload pressure and role ambiguity. The concurrence observed in this study aligns with workforce-centered quality models that highlight the interplay between employee well-being and ethical service delivery. However, the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes establishing a causal pathway, leaving it unclear whether stress coexists with or is exacerbated by ethical dilemmas. Future longitudinal research would be beneficial to better understand the temporal relationship between these variables.

The non-significant effects of most demographic variables are also consistent with previous literature suggesting that structural and psychosocial factors explain more variance in professional ethics than static characteristics such as age or sex [43, 44]. The exception in this study was income level, which showed a significant association with professional ethics. This finding may indicate that economic stability reduces financial strain and psychological distress, thereby indirectly supporting ethical adherence. However, alternative explanations are plausible. Employees in higher income categories may occupy positions with greater autonomy, stronger professional identity, or more favorable working conditions factors not directly measured in this study. Consequently, income should not be interpreted as a purely economic determinant but rather as a potential proxy for broader organizational advantages.

An important theoretical implication emerging from these findings is the possibility of a dual-path mechanism in which information literacy and occupational stress operate through complementary processes. Previous studies have shown that greater access to information and higher decision-making self-efficacy are associated with reduced anxiety and improved decision quality in healthcare environments [45–47]. It is plausible that information literacy is linked to enhanced cognitive control and reduced uncertainty, thereby potentially buffering stress and facilitating ethical reasoning.

Ultimately, the findings of this study contribute to health services research by repositioning professional ethics as a measurable workforce outcome influenced by cognitive and psychosocial determinants, rather than a mere individual moral attribute. The observed link between information literacy and professional ethics points to a synergy where the ability to critically evaluate and apply information is closely aligned with safe decision-making and responsible conduct in complex healthcare environments. Similarly, the observed association with lower occupational stress—reflective of systemic pressures like workload intensity and role ambiguity—indicates a coexistence between compromised psychological well-being and the availability of cognitive resources for ethical reasoning. Taken together, these results suggest the potential value of system-level strategies, such as positioning information literacy training as a governance tool and exploring stress-reduction initiatives through workload optimization. For policymakers, this highlights that ethical standards often operate in tandem with broader organizational structures, indicating that health system reforms may benefit from integrating cognitive skill development with psychosocial risk management to support overall quality, safety, and accountability.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to draw causal conclusions. Because all variables were measured at a single point in time, the observed associations between information literacy, occupational stress, and professional ethics should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal; it remains unclear, for instance, whether higher information literacy is associated with higher professional ethics scores or whether individuals with stronger ethical tendencies are more inclined to develop literacy skills. Furthermore, a significant limitation regarding the external validity of this study is that it was conducted within a single medical university and its affiliated healthcare facilities in Iran. Since organizational culture, administrative structures, and ethical frameworks vary considerably across different health systems and geographic regions, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other healthcare environments, different professional groups, or diverse cultural contexts. A significant methodological concern is the exclusive reliance on self-report questionnaires, which introduces the risk of Common Method Variance (CMV). As noted in methodological literature [48], collecting data for both independent and dependent variables from the same source at the same time point can lead to inflated correlations, as the variance may be partially attributed to the measurement method rather than the constructs themselves. Furthermore, the sensitive nature of “professional ethics” makes the study susceptible to social desirability bias, where participants may overreport “ideal” behaviors to align with professional norms, potentially masking actual workplace conduct [49]. Certain measurement-related constraints must also be acknowledged. The information literacy instrument assessed self-perceived rather than objective competencies, which may not perfectly reflect true performance. Similarly, while the HSE job stress scale is validated, its sensitivity to the specific socio-cultural stressors within Iranian medical universities may be limited. While statistical rigor was maintained through the assessment of normality and regression diagnostics, the potential influence of non-normality in certain variables remains a consideration. Additionally, several potentially confounding variables—such as organizational ethical climate, leadership style, burnout, and access to information technologies—were not measured. The absence of qualitative data also limits the depth of understanding regarding the nuanced ethical challenges faced by staff. Finally, data collection occurred during a specific period (April to August 2024), which may not account for seasonal variations in workload; and despite an acceptable response rate, non-response bias remains a possibility if those experiencing extreme stress or ethical dilemmas chose not to participate.

Conclusion

This study highlights a significant interplay between professional ethics, occupational stress, and information literacy among healthcare employees. The findings point to a dynamic where professional ethics among healthcare employees is closely associated with both workplace pressures and cognitive competencies, rather than individual values alone. Specifically, lower levels of occupational stress (reflected by higher HSE scores) were associated with higher professional ethics scores, whereas stronger information literacy was positively associated with professional ethics. From a health services perspective, these results underscore the relevance of workforce-focused strategies in supporting professional ethics within healthcare organizations. Prioritizing stress reduction and fostering information literacy may be linked to enhancements in employee performance, service quality, and patient safety. Integrating stress management initiatives with training in information literacy could potentially align with broader goals of organizational effectiveness and health system quality improvement. Further research, particularly longitudinal designs, is needed to establish definitive causal pathways and evaluate targeted interventions in diverse healthcare contexts.
