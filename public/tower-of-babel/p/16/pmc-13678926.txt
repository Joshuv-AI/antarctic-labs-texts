Prevalence and associated factors of depression, anxiety, and stress among undergraduate health professions students in Borama, Somaliland: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Kahsay Hailu Negash, Abdilahi Abdkarim Osman, Ahmed Moumin Abdilahi, Farhan Muse Omar, Osman Said Guelleh, Mohamed Abdi Farah, Mekonin Meskelu Shegere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental disorders are characterized by significant impairments in an individual’s thoughts, emotional control, and patterns of behavior. University students frequently suffer from mental disorders like depression, anxiety, and stress.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of depression, anxiety, and stress among undergraduate health professions students in Borama, Somaliland.MethodsAn institution-based quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted among 315 undergraduate health professions students from October to December 2025. A simple random sampling technique was employed to select the participants. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire and analyzed using SPSS version 22.0. Depression, anxiety, and stress were assessed via the DASS-21, with subscale scores multiplied by two and classified by severity. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.ResultsThe prevalences of depression, anxiety, and stress among undergraduate health professions students in Borama, Somaliland, were 47.6% (95% CI: 42.2–53.2), 49.5% (95% CI: 43.9–55.0), and 55.6% (95% CI: 50.0–61.0), respectively. Depression was associated with academic failure [AOR = 3.73, 95% CI (1.98; 7.01)], poor time management [AOR = 4.46, 95% CI (1.41; 14.17)], lack of supportive friends/family [AOR = 3.43, 95% CI (1.70; 6.92)], drug use [AOR = 3.69, 95% CI (1.14; 11.95)], and diagnosed health conditions [AOR = 2.45, 95% CI (1.20; 4.97)]. Anxiety was associated with academic failure/probation [AOR = 3.91, 95% CI (2.13; 7.18)], lack of supportive friends/family [AOR = 3.78, 95% CI (1.91; 7.48)], and diagnosed health conditions [AOR = 2.82, 95% CI (1.44; 5.55)], whereas being single was protective [AOR=0.34, 95% CI (0.14; 0.82)]. Stress was associated with feeling academically overwhelmed sometimes [AOR = 2.05, 95% CI (1.12; 3.70)], academic failure/probation [AOR = 3.88, 95% CI (1.99; 7.56)], poor time management [AOR = 4.49, 95% CI (1.27; 15.85)], lack of supportive friends/family [AOR = 2.27, 95% CI (1.12; 4.61)], and diagnosed health conditions [AOR = 3.09, 95% CI (1.46; 6.54)], whereas being single was protective [AOR=0.30, 95% CI (0.11; 0.79)].ConclusionsThis study revealed a high prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress among undergraduate health professions students. Academic failure, poor time management, lack of social support, marital status, health conditions, drug use, and academic challenge stressors were identified as potential correlates in exploratory analyses. Screening students using DASS-21 every semester and establishing peer support programs would reduce mental health challenges and enhance academic performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08438-7.Keywords: Mental disorders, Medical students, Depression, Anxiety, Stress, DASS-21

Introduction

Mental disorders, also known as mental health conditions, are characterized by significant impairments in an individual’s thoughts, emotional control, and patterns of behavior [1]. Globally, mental health illness is one of the most common lethal factors of early death. It affects people of all ages, and it affects all communities worldwide [2]. Mental disorders include anxiety disorders, depressive disorders, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorders, developmental disorders, bipolar disorders, conduct disorders, autism spectrum disorders, schizophrenia, and eating disorders. According to the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, one in seven individuals, accounting for 1.1 billion people, had a mental disorder in 2021. Among these people, 359 million were living with anxiety disorders, and 280 million were living with depression [3]. Countries are working to alleviate mental health problems. However, the prevalence of mental disorders has increased, especially since the outbreak of COVID-19 [4, 5].

The prevalence of mental disorders varies across continents and countries. For example, one in five people in Europe experiences mental disorders [4]. One in seven children has mental health problems in Sub-Saharan Africa [5]. Moreover, the reported prevalence of mental disorders across WHO regions in 2019 was 10.9% in Africa, 14.7% in the Eastern Mediterranean, 14.2% in Europe, 15.6% in the Americas, 13.2% in Southeast Asia, and 11.7% in the Western Pacific [6]. In Somaliland, one person in every two households is projected to experience psychiatric disorders [7]. This figure is extremely high compared with the previously mentioned mental disorder prevalence figures.

Mental disorders among university students are prevalent in many countries. The prevalence of depression among first-year university students in Ghana was 49% [8]. A similar study conducted in Trinidad and Tobago reported 63% anxiety, 51% depression, and 48% stress prevalence among medical students [9]. Other studies conducted in a neighboring country, Ethiopia, revealed a high prevalence of mental disorders among university students. For example, a study conducted at Debre Berhan University reported a prevalence of 63.1% [10], whereas the reported prevalence of mental disorders among medical school students at Jimma University was 35.2% [11]. Studies conducted in Addis Ababa revealed that the prevalence rates of depression and anxiety among undergraduate medical school students were 51% and 30.1%, respectively [12]. A similar study conducted at the University of Gondar reported 45.5% mental distress [13]. The prevalence of one of the types of mental disorders, i.e., depression, in the Somali region of Ethiopia was 47.1% [14]. The prevalence rates of stress and anxiety among undergraduate medical students at Haramaya University were 44% and 48.9%, respectively [15]. The overall prevalence of common mental disorders across three selected communities in Somalia was reported to be 78.1% [16]. The prevalence of depression among medical students in Mogadishu, Somalia, was found to be 58.6%. Nursing students had the highest rate of depression (68.6%), and medical students with depression accounted for 52% of the sample [17].

The research results show that university students are at high risk of mental disorders compared with other societies [13]. Attending a university itself poses a risk for the development of mental disorders [18]. Undergraduate university students, including medical students, are vulnerable to stress as a result of academic pressure, empowerment, and life span transitions [19]. Medical students encounter mental disorders stemming from a lack of adjustment to the new educational environment and traumatic patient experiences [11]. In addition, students face challenges such as new university life and social, emotional, financial, and family problems [20]. Identifying these challenges helps design mental health and well-being coping strategies for university students [21].

However, more than 95% of the people who experience mental illness in Somalia (Somaliland included), including students, do not receive proper medical care [22]. Understanding this mental health problem among communities, including university students, and its effect on well-being [23]; and its impact on students’ academic performance [24] can help universities and stakeholders mitigate this problem by designing coping mechanisms and mental health treatments. However, very few mental disorder treatments are provided for university students [25].

Although mental disorders are prevalent among university students worldwide and have been studied in numerous countries, they are less likely to be recognized, and attention is not given in Somaliland. This context is particularly relevant given Somaliland’s post-conflict recovery phase, limited mental health services, and the additional pressures faced by students in resource-constrained educational settings. Therefore, this study assesses the prevalence and associated factors of depression, anxiety, and stress among undergraduate health professions students in Somaliland.

Methods

Study design, setting, and period

The study employed an institution-based cross-sectional study design. It was conducted in Borama town, Somaliland. Borama is the capital of the Awdal region. The town hosts several institutions, including higher education institutions and other higher education facilities. Borama has eight university campuses, which are recognized by Somaliland’s Ministry of Education and Science and adhere to Somaliland’s education policies. For this study, all batches (from the first year through the fifth year) of undergraduate health professions students from five universities were selected. The study was conducted between October and December 2025.

Source and study population

The source population of this study consisted of undergraduate university students in Borama town. The study population consisted of undergraduate health professions students from five university campuses found in Borama town.

Eligibility criteria

Regular undergraduate health professions students who were studying at the selected universities during the study period, enrolled in the selected universities for at least one academic semester, and were available during the data collection period were eligible for inclusion. However, students who did not study for one academic semester, those on academic leave, those who were critically ill or unable to complete the questionnaire, and those with communication or cognitive impairments that prevented reliable self-reporting were excluded.

Sample size

A single population proportion formula (n = z2*p*(1-p)/d2, where n is the sample size, z is the standard value at a desired confidence level, p is the estimated prevalence of the condition, 1-p is the proportion of the population without the condition, and d is the margin of error) was used to calculate the sample size. The sample size was determined at a 95% confidence level, with a 5% margin of error and a 59.1% recent anxiety prevalence among medical students from the neighboring country of Ethiopia [26]. Consequently, the total sample size was calculated to be 315 by adding a 10% nonresponse rate.

Sampling techniques

All five university campuses that had undergraduate health professions students were included in the study. The sample size was then distributed proportionally to the five universities. The total number of eligible undergraduate health professions students across the five universities was 1,248, from which the sample of 315 was proportionally allocated. After obtaining the lists of students from each university campus, a simple random sampling technique was employed to select the participants. Respondent recruitment was conducted between 10/10/2025 and 15/12/2025.

Data collection techniques and tools

Data were collected through a Google online form using a structured questionnaire, which was developed after reviewing relevant literature (Appendix A) [9, 10, 27]. The questionnaire contains four sections. The first section contains the sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents (age, gender, year of study, living arrangement, and marital status). The second and third sections contain questions regarding academic, psychosocial, lifestyle, and behavioral factors, respectively. The fourth section is a validated and widely used self-reported mental health screening tool, commonly known as the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21); see Appendix B.

Tools for measuring depression, anxiety, and stress

The presence of depression, anxiety, and stress was measured using DASS-21, which is validated in several African countries [8, 15]. The subscales exhibit a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 for depression, 0.87 for anxiety, and 0.87 for stress. All three subscales of the DASS-21 demonstrated significant reliability. Scores were derived via the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress scale-21 (DASS-21), a simplified version of the DASS-42. The rating scale contains 7 items rated from 0 to 3 (did not apply to me at all, applied to me to some degree or some of the time, applied to me to a considerable degree or a good part of the time, or applied to me very much or most of the time). Subscale scores on the DASS-21 were multiplied by two and classified according to the Lovibond and Lovibond [28, 29] severity cutoff points. The depression scores are interpreted as normal (0–9), mild (10–13), moderate (14–20), severe (21–27), or extremely severe (≥ 28). Anxiety scores are interpreted as normal (0–7), mild (8–9), moderate (10–14), severe (15–19), or extremely severe (≥ 20). Stress rating scores are interpreted as normal (0–14), mild (15–18), moderate (19–25), severe (26–33), or extremely severe (≥ 34). Scores of mild and above for each of the mental disorders are considered in computing prevalence [28, 29].

Data processing and analysis

Data quality was assessed through a critical review of completeness, editing, coding, and data entry. The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) V.22 software to calculate frequencies and percentages, and to perform bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. Frequencies and percentages were used to illustrate the study respondents in relation to the study variables. Bivariate logistic regression was used to assess the association between the independent and dependent variables. Variables with p values less than 0.25 were included in the multivariate logistic regression. The multivariate logistic regression analysis with the backward elimination method was employed to identify the independent associations and statistical significance declared at p < 0.05. The Hosmer and Lemeshow test showed a p value of > 0.05 and a small χ² value, indicating the model fitted the data.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents

This study included a total of 315 undergraduate health professions students with a 100% response rate. In cases where selected participants couldn’t be reached or opted not to respond, substitute participants were randomly selected from the remaining eligible students following the original sampling procedure. The majority of the respondents, 214 (67.9%), were in the 21–25 years age range, followed by 79 (25.1%) in the 18–20 years age group. However, none of the respondents was above 30. The gender composition of the respondents was 196 (62.2%) females and 119 (37.8%) males. A significant number of the respondents, 118 (37.5%), were in their third year of study. Most respondents were living with their families, with 254 (80.6%) reporting this status. Notably, 285 (90.5%) of the respondents were single (Table 1).

Academic, psychosocial, lifestyle, and behavioral characteristics of the respondents

The data show that the majority of the respondents, 169 (53.7%), felt overwhelmed in terms of academic workload, and most of the respondents, 229 (72.7%), experienced academic failure or probation in the past year. The majority of the respondents, 181 (57.5%), rated their overall time management skills as good. Most of the respondents, 255 (81%), reported that they had supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed. The number of sleep hours per school night for the majority of the respondents falls between 4 and 6 h and between 6 and 8 h, with 126 (40%) and 136 (43.2%) reporting these hours, respectively. Fewer than half of the respondents, 129 (41%), exercised 1 to 2 times a week, and 74 (23.5%) exercised daily, with 64 (20.3%) who never exercised, followed by 48 (15.2%) who exercised 3 to 4 times a week. Most of the respondents, 285 (90.5%), did not use drugs such as khat, cigarettes, or other stimulants. The majority of the respondents, 162 (51.4%), reported having a good overall diet, and most of the respondents, 250 (79.4%), had not been diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions (Table 2).

Prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress

Among the 315 respondents, stress emerged as the most prevalent mental disorder, accounting for 55.6% (95% CI: 50.0–61.0) of the sample, affecting 175 individuals. The second most prevalent mental disorder was anxiety, with a 49.5% (95% CI: 43.9; 55.0) prevalence reported by 156 respondents. Anxiety was followed by depression, with 47.6% (95% CI: 42.2; 53.2) affecting 150 individuals. Out of the 315 assessed participants, 198 (62.9%) were normal, 37 (11.7%) had mild stress, 45 (14.3%) had moderate stress, 25 (7.9%) had severe stress, and 10 (3.2%) had extremely severe stress. Among the participants assessed for anxiety, 141 (44.8%) were normal, 18 (5.7%) were mild, 50 (15.9%) were severe, and 73 (23.2%) were extremely severe. Out of the participants assessed for depression, 165 (52.4%) were normal, 44 (14%) were mild, 43 (13.7%) were moderate, 32 (10.2%) were severe, and 31 (9.8%) were extremely severe (Table 3).

A sensitivity analysis was performed using a more stringent cutoff for extremely severe anxiety (DASS-21 anxiety score ≥ 24 instead of ≥ 20). This sensitivity analysis was undertaken to test the robustness of the extremely severe anxiety prevalence estimate, as somatic symptoms in anxiety may inflate scores in this population. The prevalence decreased from 73 (23.2%) to 45 (14.3%) participants, resulting in the reclassification of 18 participants from the extremely severe to the severe category. This indicates that a subset of cases was clustered near the original threshold and suggests that the initial estimate was not driven exclusively by scores well above the standard cutoff. The mean anxiety score was 11.04 ± 10.40, and the median anxiety score was 8 (IQR: 0–18). The mean (11.04) is greater than the median (8). The 25th percentile (Q1) is 0, meaning at least 25% of participants had an anxiety score of 0. Therefore, the distribution is right-skewed.

Factors associated with depression

In the bivariate analysis at p < 0.25, several factors, such as academic failure or probation in the past year, time management skills, having supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed, drug use, and being diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions, were associated with depression. Moreover, factors such as year of study, marital status, and feeling overwhelmed by one’s academic workload emerged as potential correlates with depression in bivariate analysis (p value < 0.25). However, no associations were found for age, sex, department, living arrangement, number of hours of sleep on a school night, physical exercise, or overall diet.

In multivariate logistic regression analysis at a p value < 0.05 with a backward stepwise selection method, academic failure or probation in the past year, time management skills, supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed, drug use, and being diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions co-occurred with depression. Some variables, such as year of study, marital status, and feeling overwhelmed by one’s academic workload, that initially showed a significant association with depression did not maintain a significant association in the final model.

The results show that students who experienced academic failure or probation in the past year were 3.73 times more likely to have depression than those who did not experience academic failure or probation in the past year [AOR = 3.73, 95% CI (1.98; 7.01)]. Compared with students who had excellent time management skills, those who had poor time management skills were 4.46 times more likely to have depression [AOR = 4.46, 95% CI (1.41; 14.17)]. Students who had no supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed were 3.43 times more likely to have depression than those who had supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed [AOR = 3.43, 95% CI (1.70; 6.92)]. Compared with students who did not use drugs, those who used drugs were 3.69 times more likely to have depression [AOR = 3.69, 95% CI (1.14; 11.95)]. In addition, students who were diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions were 2.45 times more likely to have depression than those who were not diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions [AOR = 2.45, 95% CI (1.20; 4.97)] (Table 4).

Factors associated with anxiety

In the bivariate logistic regression analysis at p value < 0.25, a significant association was found between living arrangements, feeling overwhelmed by one’s academic workload, time management skills, and drug use with anxiety. Furthermore, marital status, academic failure or probation in the past year, supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed, and being diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions were associated with anxiety. However, factors such as age, sex, year of study, department, number of sleep hours on a school night, physical exercise, and overall diet did not show an association with anxiety.

The multivariate logistic regression analysis at a p value < 0.05 using a backward stepwise selection method revealed that marital status, academic failure or probation in the past year, supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed, and being diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions showed exploratory association with anxiety. Some variables, such as living arrangements, feeling overwhelmed by one’s academic workload, time management skills, and drug use, that initially showed a significant association with anxiety did not maintain a significant association in the final model.

Compared with married students, single students were 66% less likely to experience anxiety [AOR=0.34, 95% CI (0.14; 0.82)]. Students who experienced academic failure or probation in the past year were 3.91 times more likely to have anxiety than those who did not experience academic failure or probation in the past year [AOR = 3.91, 95% CI (2.13; 7.18)]. Students who had no supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed were 3.78 times more likely to have anxiety than those who had supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed [AOR = 3.78, 95% CI (1.91; 7.48)]. Moreover, students who were diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions were 2.82 times more likely to have anxiety than those who were free of these conditions [AOR = 2.82, 95% CI (1.44; 5.55)] (Table 5).

Factors associated with stress

In the bivariate logistic regression analysis at p value < 0.25, marital status, feeling overwhelmed by academic workload, time management skills, having supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed, and being diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions emerged as potential correlates with stress. Additionally, drug use and overall diet showed an exploratory association with stress. However, there was no association between age, sex, year of study, department, living arrangement, number of study hours on a school night, and physical exercise with stress.

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis at p value < 0.05 with a backward stepwise selection method, marital status, feeling overwhelmed by academic workload, time management skills, having supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed, and being diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions were associated with stress. Some factors, such as drug use and overall diet, that initially showed a significant association with stress did not maintain a significant association in the final model.

Compared with married students, single students were 70% less likely to experience stress [AOR=0.30, 95% CI (0.11; 0.79)]. Students who sometimes felt overwhelmed by academic workload were 2.05 times more likely to have stress than those who never felt overwhelmed by academic workload [AOR = 2.05, 95% CI (1.12; 3.76)]. Students who had poor time management skills were 4.49 times more likely to have stress than those who had excellent time management skills [AOR = 4.49, 95% CI (1.27; 15.85)]. Students who did not have supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed were 2.27 times more likely to have stress than those who had supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed [AOR = 2.27, 95% CI (1.12; 4.61)]. Students diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions were 3.09 times more likely to have stress than those who were not diagnosed with these conditions [AOR = 3.09, 95% CI (1.46; 6.54)] (Table 6).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of depression, anxiety, and stress among undergraduate health professions students in Borama, Somaliland. The results of the study revealed that a significant proportion of the students experienced depression [47.6% (95% CI: 42.1–53.2)], anxiety [49.5% (95% CI: 43.9–55.0)], and stress [55.6% (95% CI: 50.0–61.0)]. Almost every other student had depression (0.91:1), anxiety (0.98:1), and stress (1.25:1).

In this study, multiple statistical tests were performed, which may increase the risk of Type I error. Formal corrections for multiple comparisons were not applied as the analyses were primarily exploratory and aimed at identifying potential associations for further investigation. However, findings with statistical significance (0.01 < p < 0.05) were interpreted with caution.

The study revealed that the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress was 47.6% (95% CI: 42.1–53.2), 49.5% (95% CI: 43.9–55.0), and 55.6% (95% CI: 50.0–61.0), respectively. These rates are broadly consistent with findings from comparable settings; for instance, depression prevalence aligns with studies from Saudi Arabia, 43.4% [27]; Ghana, 49% [8]; and Ethiopia, 51.30% [12], while anxiety prevalence mirrors findings from Eastern Ethiopia, 48.9% [15], and stress prevalence is comparable to 58.3% findings reported in Saudi Arabia [30]. However, all three exceed global benchmarks, such as the prevalence of depression among students in Chinese universities, which accounts for 9.8% [31]; 33.8% reported global anxiety prevalence among medical students [32]; 48% in Trinidad and Tobago [9]; 44% in Ethiopia [15]; and 32.2% in Bangladesh [33] anxiety prevalence among undergraduate health professions students. Conversely, all three remain below rates reported in high-burden contexts such as the Gaza Strip [34], where depression, anxiety, and stress reached 69%, 77.3%, and 65.2%, respectively. Similarly, depression rates are lower compared to studies from Somalia (58.6%) [17] and Kenya (57.7%) [35]; anxiety rates are lower compared to studies showing 63% in Trinidad and Tobago [9] and stress studies reporting 70% in Ghana [36], 63.7% in Ethiopia [37], 48% in Trinidad and Tobago [9], 44% in Ethiopia [15], and 32.2% in Bangladesh [33]. The variations may stem from the impact of political instability, differences in sample size, and variations in data collection tools.

The multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that students who experienced academic failure or probation in the past year were 3.73 times more likely to have depression and 3.91 times more likely to have anxiety than those who did not experience academic failure or probation in the past year. The depression result resonates with studies conducted in Ghana [8] and Ethiopia [26], which indicates that academic problems such as poor performance or failure in courses are correlated with depression, while the anxiety result aligns with multiple studies from Ethiopia [26], Saudi Arabia [38], Brazil [39], and Nigeria [40]. However, among undergraduate South African university students, there has been no significant association between academic stress and depression [41]. Similarly, a study conducted in Malaysia [42] revealed no significant correlation between anxiety and academic performance. Therefore, universities should intervene by arranging additional theoretical and health facility-based practical classes for students. This would help the students understand their lessons and tasks, which, in turn, can raise the possibility of passing their courses. In addition, universities should monitor academic systems and strengthen academic and mental health support services for students to reduce anxiety.

In the present study, students with poor time management skills were 4.46 times more likely to have depression and 4.49 times more likely to have stress than students who had excellent time management skills. The results correspond with studies from different universities across countries. For example, studies conducted in Iran [43] and Germany [44] indicate that procrastination co-occurred with higher depression among undergraduate university students, and similar associations between poor time management and stress were reported by studies from Malaysia [45] and Iraq [46]. The results underscore the significant effect of time management skills on depression and stress. To understand this significant factor, universities should provide effective time management and study skills training programs to students to improve their planning, prioritization, and task organization skills. Improving these skills may reduce depressive symptoms. Students should also prepare study programs with clear schedules.

Students who did not have supportive friends or family to talk to when stressed were 3.43 times more likely to have depression, 3.78 times more likely to have anxiety, and 2.27 times more likely to experience stress than those who had supportive friends and family. The depression result aligns with those of multiple studies conducted in the USA [47] and China [48], which demonstrated higher odds of depression due to lower social support, such as from friends and family members. The anxiety finding is consistent with findings across students from different countries, such as Ecuador [49], Somalia [50], China [51], and Canada [52]. The stress finding also demonstrates convergence with research results from Iraq [46] and Malaysia [53]. They demonstrated that social support reduces stress levels among students. However, a study conducted in China [54] reported a negative correlation between social support and stress. The results highlight the critical role of social support in reducing depressive symptoms, anxiety, and stress. Universities should consider involving peer and family support as part of their mental health intervention strategies. Increased and strengthened social support at the individual, family, institutional, and policy levels is needed to achieve the desired objectives. Universities should implement counseling services for students to provide emotional and social assistance. It is, therefore, recommended that universities expand social support by promoting family engagement, peer networks, and accessible counseling services to reduce depression, anxiety, and stress and improve the mental well-being of students.

Students who used drugs were 3.69 times more likely to have depression than those who did not use drugs. These results are consistent with the findings of other studies conducted in this area. A study conducted in Nigeria on medical students indicated that substance users had greater odds of depression [55]. A similar study from India [56] and Ethiopia [57] also revealed a significant association between drug use and depression. This means that depression is much more common among drug-using students regardless of their geographical location. The relationship between drug use and depression could also be bidirectional. As reported in the research results, drug use is associated with depression, and students with depressive symptoms may use drugs as coping strategies. Given the strong association between drug use and depression, universities must establish mental health centers and implement drug/substance use awareness and prevention programs. Universities can also screen students for depression. Further longitudinal research could aid in understanding the causal relationship between drug use and depression.

Students who were diagnosed with medical or psychiatric conditions were 2.45 times more likely to have depression, 2.82 times more likely to have anxiety, and 3.09 times more likely to have stress than those who were free from the health condition. While the depression result aligns with those studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries [58], a meta-analysis conducted in developing countries [59], and a study conducted in Kuwait [60], the anxiety result corresponds with studies from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Pakistan, and Iran [61]; Ecuador [62]; the USA [63]; and Sudan [52]. The stress finding is also congruent with study results from the USA [64] and Saudi Arabia [65]. The results suggest that having a medical or psychiatric condition is a major factor in the onset of depression, vulnerability to anxiety, and high levels of stress among the students. This necessitates prevention, early screening, and support for students. It also requires the integration of mental health care with medical care. Regular counseling can improve students’ mental well-being by mitigating depression, anxiety, and stress. Universities and health care institutions must develop counseling and support programs for affected students and prioritize stress management interventions for students diagnosed with health conditions to reduce stress levels.

Single students were 66% less likely to have anxiety and 70% less likely to experience stress than their married counterparts. However, studies from around the world, such as those from Malaysia [66] and the USA [67], have shown no significant association between marital status and anxiety. The stress result aligns with similar findings from Ethiopia [10], whereas marital or relationship status shows no statistical significance in studies from Saudi Arabia [68] and Malaysia [69]. The anxiety and stress findings in the context of Somaliland could be new. Students who marry early may differ systematically from those who remain single on unmeasured variables such as religion, family expectation, or socioeconomic status. This reflects a discrepancy in sociocultural and contextual differences. Assumptions may include a high value given toward marriage and family responsibility. Married people may feel pressured by these expectations. Future studies in Somaliland should explore why married students experience higher anxiety and stress levels than single students do and investigate the determining factors. On the other hand, in some African and South Asian countries, marriage among students is associated with other responsibilities that may contribute to a higher level of stress. However, marriages in other settings, such as Malaysia and Saudi Arabia, do not necessarily predict stress. As a result, universities should provide stress management and mental health support services for married students.

Finally, the study analysis revealed that students who sometimes felt overwhelmed by their academic workload were 2.05 times more likely to experience stress than those who never felt overwhelmed by their academic workload. Studies from across the globe also show similar results. For example, results from China and South Korea [45] report that academic workload is strongly associated with stress among students. Therefore, feeling overwhelmed by one’s academic workload is strongly correlated with stress across various countries and education systems. As a result, universities must adjust the academic workload of students, implement time and stress management programs, enhance flexible learning policies, and elevate support levels.

Contrary to expectations, age, gender, number of sleep hours on a school night, frequency of physical exercise, and living arrangement were not associated with depression, anxiety, and stress in this study. Similarly, a study conducted in Canada reported that gender and physical activity are not associated with any of the three mental health conditions [70, 71]. However, previous studies conducted in countries such as the UK and China report that age, gender, and physical exercise are associated with mental health [72, 73]. Another study also shows that sleep duration is associated with mental health [74]. Associations between sleep and physical activity with mental health were reported in many studies among youth [75]. Gender and physical exercise among the elderly were associated with anxiety and depression in China [72]. Age among Canadian university students was associated with anxiety [76]. Age and gender were found to be factors for mental health among adults in Sweden [77]. In addition, studies indicate that living arrangements are associated with mental health. People who live alone have a high risk of depression [78]. Therefore, the absence of such associations in the present study may reflect religious, cultural, and living environments. The absence of associations for variables such as sleep and exercise may also reflect limited statistical power in certain subgroups (such as married students, n = 30), and these findings should be interpreted with caution. The result may emanate from genuine cultural or contextual differences. It may also reflect variations in sample size, data collection tools used (measurement), and research setting. Future research in Somaliland should examine variables with larger and more diverse groups to determine if the contextual phenomena are authentic or a result of statistical power limitations.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several strengths and limitations. One of the strengths of this study is that it has a multiple institution-based cross-sectional design, which allows the recruitment of relatively large and representative populations. This approach augments the external validity of the study compared with that of a single institution-based study. The study used a standardized and validated data collection instrument. Using the Lovibond and Lovibond [28], severity cutoffs also ensure standardized classifications. However, the study has some limitations. First, the study focused on undergraduate health professions students. The results from this particular group of students may not be generalizable to other groups of students enrolled in nonmedical disciplines. Second, it remains limited due to social desirability bias, recall bias, and reliance on self-reported screening, which does not provide a clinical diagnosis. Third, the small number of married students (n = 30) limits the precision of estimates in this subgroup. Findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and require replication in larger samples. Fourth, the cross-sectional nature of the research does not establish causality between the independent and dependent variables. Finally, pooling students from different health disciplines may have introduced heterogeneity due to potential differences in academic experiences, training environments, and mental health profiles. This disciplinary heterogeneity may constrain comparisons across the fields of study and should be considered when concluding the results.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study provides empirical evidence regarding the prevalence and associated factors of depression, anxiety, and stress among undergraduate health professions students in Somaliland. The results of this study show that depression, anxiety, and stress are prevalent among undergraduate health professions students and affect their daily lives, including their education. Academic failure or probation, poor time management skills, lack of supportive friends or family, drug use, and being diagnosed with health conditions were identified as associated factors of depression. Similarly, marital status, academic failure or probation, lack of supportive friends or family, and being diagnosed with health conditions were determined to be associated factors of anxiety. Marital status, academic workload, poor time management skills, lack of supportive friends or family, and being diagnosed with health conditions showed an exploratory association with stress. The results of this study could help professionals and experts, such as university lecturers, curriculum developers, counselors, and higher education institution administrators, consider the mental health conditions of students. Awareness creation and intervention by different stakeholders could help improve the mental well-being of students and enhance their academic performance. Education and health policymakers in Somaliland should prioritize the integration of mental health screening and support services into the national higher education policy framework. The Ministry of Education, the Ministry of Health, and universities in Somaliland should acknowledge this reality and take action to assist students, primarily through awareness creation and the establishment of mental health service providers. Universities should screen students using the DASS-21 every semester and establish peer support programs.
