The effect of peer-assisted episiotomy repair simulation on anxiety and self-efficacy levels in midwifery students
Authors: Ebru Inan Kirmizigul, Gurbet Yetiskin Erogul, Sumeyra Damsarsan, Didem Simsek Kucukkelepce, Zehra Golbasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackroundPerineal injury repair skills should be taught to students in midwifery education. One of these methods is peer-supported simulation training in simulation environments. This study aimed to determine the effect of peer-assisted episiotomy and perineal injury repair simulation on midwifery students’ anxiety and self-efficacy.MethodsA pretest-posttest, one-group quasi-experimental design. The study was carried out with 39 third-year midwifery students studying at a foundation university. Episiotomy repair simulation was carried out peer-assisted using sponge and chicken breast. The “Introductory Information Form,” the “State-Trait Anxiety Inventory,” and the “General Self-Efficacy Scale” were used as data collection tools. In the analysis of the data, descriptive statistics and dependent group t-tests were employed to evaluate the difference between the pre-test and post-test.ResultsThe mean age of the students was 21.44 ± 1.68 years (Min = 20; Max = 30). According to the results, there was a significant decrease in the mean scores of the State and Trait Anxiety scales in the pre-and post-simulation periods (Pre: State Anxiety mean 34.41 ± 9.58, Trait Anxiety 44.79 ± 9.57; Post: State Anxiety mean 30.48 ± 9.31, Trait Anxiety 41.33 ± 10.82; p < 0.05). While no difference was observed between the pre-test and post-test in the total score of general self-efficacy (Pre: mean 62.48 ± 9.74; Post: mean 62.59 ± 11.92; p > 0.05) of the students, there was a significant difference in the sub-dimension of Sustaining Effort & Persistence (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe findings of this study demonstrated that peer-assisted episiotomy repair simulation training effectively reduced students’ anxiety. It is recommended that peer support be included in pre-clinical episiotomy and perineal injury repair simulation practice.Keywords: Episiotomy, Midwifery, Simulation, Peer, Perineal injury

Background

Episiotomy and perineal lacerations are common traumas that disrupt the integrity of the perineal tissue during labor. Repair of these traumas is critical for the physical and psychological health of the mother. From a psychological perspective, women who undergo episiotomy experience feelings of shock, anger, and frustration when they are not adequately informed prior to the intervention [1]. In the postpartum period, the risk of depression has been reported to be higher, particularly among young and primiparous women [2]. In a community-based study involving 3,307 women, those who experienced perineal trauma were found to have a higher likelihood of reporting symptoms of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress three months after birth [3]. If perineal damage is not repaired appropriately, serious complications such as infection, hematoma, perineal pain, dyspareunia, incontinence, aesthetic concerns and decreased quality of life may occur. In addition, inadequate repair may negatively affect neonatal care and breastfeeding processes [4]. Therefore, perineal injury repair skills should be taught to students in midwifery education.

Gaining episiotomy and perineal repair skills is of vital importance for midwifery students, both to protect maternal health and to prevent complications. Although the World Health Organization does not recommend routine episiotomy, it supports its implementation and repair with appropriate techniques when necessary [5, 6]. This requires students to develop the skills to correctly evaluate episiotomy indications and to make appropriate interventions. However, anxiety and stress may develop due to the feeling that the student is being monitored and controlled by the instructor during skill training. Stress and anxiety experienced by midwifery students during clinical practice may negatively affect their learning processes and reduce both their self-efficacy and success [7, 8]. High anxiety levels may also negatively affect the student’s ability to make rapid and effective decisions during birth. Therefore, it is important to design learning environments in a way that reduces students’ anxiety [9]. One of these methods is peer-supported simulation training in simulation environments.

Peer-supported skills training can reduce anxiety by encouraging supportive relationships among students. Peer support facilitates the learning process by providing students with guidance, sharing information, and emotional support [10, 11]. Simulation methods provide a safe and effective environment for the transformation of theoretical knowledge into practical skills. Simulations performed with materials resembling human tissue in perineal injury repair skills training increase students’ technical skills and self-confidence [12, 13]. Although there is no study in the literature on episiotomy and perineal injury repair simulation training integrated with peer support, it is known that this method has positive effects on anxiety and self-efficacy levels in other simulation trainings [8, 14]. The existing literature demonstrates the effectiveness of various simulation techniques in episiotomy and perineal repair training. For example, a study conducted with medical students showed that a porcine perineum–based learning model increased students’ confidence in intrapartum practice and enhanced their surgical skills [15]. Similarly, findings from a multicenter study revealed that episiotomy simulation training led to significant improvements in the practical skills of midwives and obstetricians [16]. Another study reported that an interactive online episiotomy simulation improved midwifery students’ manual skills and confidence in clinical practice [17]. Comparable studies have also shown that simulation-based training positively affects students’ anxiety and self-efficacy levels before actual clinical practice [8, 14].

The contribution of simulation practices conducted in different environments to face-to-face training remains a subject of interest. In a study by Brereton et al., a blended learning model was reported to play a supportive role in improving perineal suturing skills. This model consists of initiating practice at home, reinforcing it through online videos, and finalizing it with face-to-face laboratory sessions. The findings indicated that students felt more confident in performing the skill in a face-to-face environment after having initiated practice at home [18].

In our study, there are two main reasons for adopting a peer-supported simulation training model. The first is the expectation that simulation-based skills training enhances students’ self-efficacy. The second is that peer-assisted learning provides students with a less stressful and more supportive environment in which they can practice and thereby consolidate their skills. Accordingly, the preliminary findings of our study suggest that this approach may be integrated as a new learning model that contributes both theoretically and practically to simulation-based education with proven effectiveness.

Study objective

The aim of this study is to evaluate the effect of peer-supported perineal repair training on midwifery students’ anxiety and self-efficacy levels.

Materials and methods

Study design

This quasi-experimental study employed a one-group pretest-posttest design and was conducted in the Midwifery Department of a foundation university in Turkey between December 2022 and January 2023.

Participants and eligibility criteria

The study included 40 third-year midwifery students who had enrolled in the birth course for the first time. Eligibility criteria required voluntary participation as well as access to the standard equipment (suture set) and materials (sponge and chicken breast) necessary for skill practice. Furthermore, enrollment in this course required students to have successfully completed previous practical courses. No sampling procedure was applied; instead, all eligible and willing students from the target population were included. All participants had access to the required equipment and materials, and none had prior hands-on experience with episiotomy or perineal repair.

The birth course in the third-year fall semester consists of 4 h of theoretical and 8 h of practical training (laboratory + hospital) per week. Attendance of at least 80% is required for the practical component; students exceeding this limit are considered unsuccessful. One student was excluded from the study due to absenteeism, resulting in a final study group of 39 students.

In Turkey, midwifery education is a four-year undergraduate program that includes a module on “vaginal birth and postnatal management” [19, 20]. As part of the graduation criteria, students are required to independently manage 40 vaginal births [21]. The peer group consisted of fourth-year students who had completed the obstetrics course, including both theoretical and practical training, in the previous year. During their summer hospital internship in the third year, they performed 40 vaginal births under their own responsibility and gained experience in perineal repair, thereby achieving clinical competence. At the end of the internship, the hospital’s education unit provided sealed and officially signed documents certifying each student’s competency. All students evaluated as ‘competent’ by the hospital were considered eligible to serve as peer instructors in our study. The peer group consisted of 37 4th year students.

Two 4th-year students who failed the obstetrics course were excluded from the peer support group. The simulation group had only one exclusion criterion: students who did not attend the skill training session on the day of the simulation were to be excluded. However, since no student was absent on that day, no exclusions were applied. Therefore, the peer group consisted of 35 students and the simulation group consisted of 39 students. Students in the simulation and peer groups were seated voluntarily at the tables in the order of their arrival, without applying any specific allocation criteria. The observing researchers subsequently verified the one-to-one pairing and verbally confirmed student satisfaction before proceeding to the next stages of the training. The 4 students who were not matched were reassigned to 4 peers and 4 peers mentored two students instead of one. To ensure that all students received equal levels of peer support, all peer mentors underwent the same standardized training prior to the intervention, and every student was provided with equal time, identical materials, and the same practice opportunities. In addition, the observers continuously monitored each student’s training process to guarantee comparable conditions in terms of both duration and quality for all participants. All participants in the study provided their consent before starting.

Intervention process and data collection

Intervention materials

Sponge and chicken breast were used as intervention material. The sponge material is a commonly used, inexpensive, practical, and readily available simulation model for perineal repair training. It provides students with a convenient environment to practice suturing techniques prior to live tissue application [8, 14, 22]. However, its limitation lies in the difficulty of visualizing anatomical structures [23]. For episiotomy repair training, chicken breast has been used as a live tissue model because of its similarity to human tissue in terms of elasticity and muscle structure. The literature highlights its low cost and ease of use for incision and repair practice (8,17,22]. In addition, its capacity to allow incisions of different lengths and depths, as well as providing an experience comparable to human tissue during suturing, makes it a suitable training material for students.

Other materials were gloves, suture threads (Vicryl 0*0, Vicryl 2*0) and suture set. Two weeks before coming to the simulation environment; the subject titled perineal injury repair was theoretically explained to the 3rd grade students during the lecture hour. The theoretical session lasted 4 h, with each class consisting of 50 min of lecture and a 10-minute break (total of 240 min). The content included perineal anatomy, indications for episiotomy, diagnosis and classification of perineal lacerations, repair methods for first- and second-degree lacerations, and suturing techniques. Theoretical knowledge was delivered through slide presentations, and two videos demonstrating suturing techniques were shown as part of the lecture. All 39 students attended the face-to-face lesson, which included the use of visual materials. The peer group of fourth-year midwifery students received a 20-minute training session on the importance of peer support and its implementation. The peer-support training session covered the following topics: the importance of peer support and its role in learning, communication strategies, and feedback methods. The researchers shared the objectives and aims of the skills training with the peer group and reviewed their existing knowledge of suturing techniques. The session was delivered using face-to-face lectures, a question–answer format, and two videos serving as reminders of suturing techniques. Subsequently, the procedure of the simulation-based skills training was explained (Table 1). At each stage of the simulation, peers were asked to conduct observational assessments of expected student performance indicators (e.g., correct handling of surgical instruments, application of suturing techniques) and to provide feedback accordingly. It was also emphasized that the researchers would provide continuous observation and evaluation throughout the process. Following the collection of the peer group’s expectations, the training session was concluded. In the simulation environment, sponge, chicken, suture threads, and surgical instruments were placed on six separate rectangular tables with plastic bags.

Data collection

Four researchers, experienced midwives who had performed normal vaginal deliveries and episiotomy and perineal injury repairs, observed each group. The primary role of the researchers was to observe (supervise) and evaluate the extent to which the objectives of the skills training were achieved in the simulation environment. While three members of the research team were aware of the study objectives, one researcher was kept blinded to ensure impartiality. The researchers created a 10*3 cm longitudinal incision line on the muscular side of the chicken breast and prepared it for repair. The tissue in the incision area mimicked in the chicken breast, which was the intervention material, was clean. The incision was made on muscle tissue to simulate the anatomical site where episiotomy or perineal trauma typically occurs. To facilitate uninterrupted practice of suturing techniques by the students and to preserve the integrity of the ‘clean tissue,’ the incision length was created longer than that of a typical episiotomy (4–5 cm). The term ‘clean tissue’ refers to an intact muscle area without any prior incisions, where a standardized incision line was created for each student. This approach eliminated variables such as irregular wound edges or pre-existing tears, thereby ensuring standardization across all practice sessions. Pre-tests (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory and General Self-Efficacy Scale) were collected in the training environment before starting perineal injury repair. Afterwards, each peer mentored student practiced the suturing techniques first on the sponge and then on the chicken breast. For the suturing techniques, continuous (continue) suturing in muscular areas and individual (flat) suturing on the skin surface were practiced. At all stages, the peer first demonstrated and then asked the student to practice. Adherence to the procedure was monitored in accordance with the flowchart outlined in Table 1. The peers were familiar with the procedure and provided mentorship to each student to ensure that the process progressed as planned. In addition, the researchers conducted continuous observations at each stage to evaluate compliance with the procedure. The completion time of the entire application took a total of 180 min. This period referred exclusively to the activities conducted in the simulation setting, while theoretical lectures were not included. After the students in the simulation group completed the perineal injury repair, post-tests (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory and General Self-Efficacy Scale) were administered in the same environment. The post-tests were administered 20 min after the completion of the simulation training. The flow chart of the research is presented in Fig. 1.

Outcome measures

Data collection was conducted using three instruments: the “Introductory Information Form,” the “State-Trait Anxiety Inventory,” and the “General Self-Efficacy Scale.”

The Introductory Information Form consisted of six questions designed to gather information about the students’ ages, educational backgrounds, and previous experiences with episiotomies.

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, developed by Spielberger et al. in 1970, was utilized in this study to assess anxiety levels [24]. Its Turkish validity and reliability were established by Öner in 1994. The scale comprises two parts: the first twenty items evaluate the level of state anxiety, and each statement is rated on a scale of “Not at all” (1), “A little” (2), “A lot” (3), and “Completely” (4). However, items 1, 2, 5, 8, 10, 11, 15, 16, 19, and 20, which measure state anxiety, are reverse-coded. To compute the state anxiety scores, a constant value of 50 is added to the total score of directly coded items after subtracting the total score of reversely coded items. Conversely, items 21 to 40 of the scale measure the level of trait anxiety, and individuals rate each statement on the same 4-point scale. Among these items, 21, 26, 27, 33, 36, and 39 are reverse-coded. To calculate the trait anxiety level of the individual, the total score of reverse-coded items is subtracted from the total score of directly coded items, and 35 (the constant value of the trait anxiety scale) is added. The interpretation of scores on the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory is as follows: 0–19: no anxiety; 20–39: mild anxiety; 40–59: moderate anxiety; and 60–79: severe anxiety. Those with a score of 60 and above are considered to need professional help [25]. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.90 for the State Anxiety subscale and 0.88 for the Trait Anxiety subscale in this study.

The General Self-Efficacy Scale used in this study was originally developed by Sherer et al. and consists of 23 items [26, 27]. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the scale were established by Yıldırım and İlhan in 2010. The items formulated as “How well does this describe you?” are answered as “Not at all = 1” and “Very well = 5” and aim to measure self-efficacy. Items 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 12, 14, and 16 are reverse-coded. The total score on the scale ranges from 17 to 85, with scores closer to 85 indicating higher self-efficacy levels. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Turkish version of this scale was found to be 0.80 [27, 28]. In this study, the Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.84.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 statistical software. Frequency and percentage analyses were used for descriptive characteristics, and mean and standard deviation statistics were used for scale examination. Kurtosis and Skewness values were assessed to determine a normal distribution. Since the variables showed a normal distribution, parametric methods were used for data analysis. A dependent group t-test was used to analyze the change between the pre- and post-simulation measurements. A statistical significance level of p < 0.05 was accepted.

Ethics approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Lokman Hekim University Scientific Research Board (dated: 21.12.2022 decision number:2022/195), and informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study commenced.

Discussion

Simulation-based learning provides a controlled environment for students to safely develop clinical skills and confidence, consistent with World Health Organization recommendations for patient safety [29]. In the WHO framework Strengthening Quality Midwifery Education for Universal Health Coverage 2030, the following recommendations are outlined: (1) educators should continue not only with theoretical instruction but also with clinical teaching and practice; (2) theory, simulation, and clinical practice should be integrated within the learning process; and (3) mentorship should be incorporated into both pre-service and in-service education to provide midwives with training that fosters confidence in midwifery care [30]. This framework specifically emphasizes the integration of theory, simulation, and clinical practice. In line with this, our study is considered significant as it incorporates peer mentorship and the use of simulation methods within pre-clinical training.

In this study, the significant decrease in students’ State Anxiety (pre-training x̄=34,41; post-training x̄=30,48; p < 0.05) and Trait Anxiety scores (pre-training x̄=44,795; post-training x̄=41,33; p < 0.05) after the peer-assisted episiotomy repair simulation is noteworthy. The clinical success of midwifery students is influenced by factors such as anxiety, stress, perceived control, motivation, and self-efficacy. Even when theoretical knowledge is sufficient, anxiety during the process of learning and performing new skills negatively affects both skill acquisition and clinical performance [8, 17, 31]. High levels of anxiety weaken self-regulated learning and reduce self-efficacy, thereby decreasing engagement and motivation. As a result, the effectiveness of education diminishes and academic achievement declines [7, 17, 31, 32]. Thus, reducing anxiety is educationally critical for strengthening self-efficacy and ensuring the retention of skills. Clinically, lower anxiety is associated with improved decision-making under pressure, a reduced likelihood of errors, and enhanced patient safety [8, 16, 33, 34]. Our findings indicate that simulation-based and peer-assisted training has the potential to reduce anxiety and thereby improve learning outcomes and clinical preparedness. The observed reduction may be attributed to the psychologically safer, less stressful learning environment provided by peer support, as well as structured feedback and repeated practice opportunities.

Anxiety, can have a detrimental impact on the effectiveness of education and students’ self-efficacy [35]. The literature reports that mild to moderate levels of anxiety support the learning process, whereas high levels of anxiety hinder learning [31]. In our study, students experienced mild levels of state anxiety (pre-test x̄=34.41) and moderate levels of trait anxiety (pre-test x̄=44.795) before the simulation. This suggests that the students may have entered the learning environment in a more prepared and motivated manner. Considering the negative relationship between anxiety and self-efficacy, this finding is also consistent with evidence reported in the literatüre [36–38]. One study by Demirel et al. [8] explored the effect of episiotomy repair simulation on anxiety and self-efficacy levels in midwifery students, indicating a significant reduction in state-anxiety scores after simulation [8]. Similarly, in another study by Erkek et al. [1 [4], midwifery students who underwent episiotomy repair skill evaluation through simulation achieved significantly higher scores compared to the control group, although no significant difference in anxiety levels was observed [14]. Simulation in education affects the level of proficiency in clinical skills.

Simulation-based education provides students with the opportunity to learn and practice skills in a safe environment without the fear of making mistakes, thereby reducing anxiety and stress [39]. In a study by Evci Kiraz et al., training with a high-fidelity simulator was shown to decrease learning anxiety and enhance the acquisition of psychomotor skills [33]. Similar findings have been reported across various simulation settings. For example, in Thailand, a porcine perineum–based episiotomy and perineal repair training significantly improved medical students’ self-efficacy and satisfaction levels [15]. Likewise, midwifery students who received birth skills simulation training performed these skills at higher levels in clinical practice compared to those in the control group [40]. Studies utilizing bovine tongue simulators and other models have yielded consistent results [16, 22, 41]. Moreover, online simulation training in episiotomy or perineal repair has also been reported to yield positive outcomes in terms of knowledge, skills, and self-efficacy [17, 18]. Collectively, these findings suggest that simulation-based training reduces anxiety and stress while enhancing motivation, thereby contributing positively to learning outcomes and clinical performance. Contrary to expectations, this study found no significant change in the total Self-Efficacy scores (pre-training x̄=62,48; post-training x̄=62,59; p > 0.05) or the “Initiation” sub-dimension. This may be explained by the fact that students entered the study with above-average levels of self-efficacy prior to the simulation. In addition, the limited exposure time and the lack of practice in a real clinical environment may have influenced students’ perceptions of their ability to successfully perform episiotomy or perineal repair, which could account for the absence of a significant change in self-efficacy scores.

In the context of Social Cognitive Theory, Bandura defines self-efficacy as an individual’s belief in initiating an effort that can be effective in their environment and sustaining it until a result is achieved [42]. An important component of self-efficacy development is vicarious experience; observing the successes or failures of similar others shapes an individual’s judgment about whether they can accomplish the same tasks themselves [7, 42]. In the context of the present study, the authors found a significant increase in the sub-dimensions of Sustaining Effort & Persistence following simulation training. This finding may be attributed to the fact that peer-assisted simulation training strengthens students’ beliefs and efforts toward achieving the intended learning objectives. The prior successful experiences of peer mentors may have enhanced communication and knowledge-sharing within the simulation environment, thereby facilitating goal attainment. In the literature, it has been reported that instructor-related factors are a major source of stress in midwifery students’ clinical learning [34]. Peer support, on the other hand, allows students to ask questions without hesitation, fosters a stress-free learning environment, and promotes mutual assistance, which in turn enhances academic achievement and satisfaction [43–46]. Similarly, Orsega et al. demonstrated that a coaching-based simulation delivered by postgraduate students to undergraduate peers improved students’ self-confidence [39]. Our results regarding the ‘perseverance–persistence’ sub-dimension are consistent with these findings, supporting the integration of peer-assisted education into midwifery curricula as a strategy to enhance students’ motivation, resilience, and learning outcomes.

In the literature, experimental studies conducted with a pre-test–post-test design have only limitedly addressed the relationship between students’ individual characteristics and their anxiety and self-efficacy scores. In the study by Aker et al., it was found that variables such as midwifery students’ income level, type of high school graduated from, experience with episiotomy suturing, and the material used for practice did not affect their episiotomy self-efficacy scores [47]. In contrast, the study by Türkmen and Karaca Sivrikaya reported that graduating from a health-related high school created a significant difference in students’ episiotomy knowledge and skill performance [41]. In our study, students from families with healthcare professionals also demonstrated higher baseline self-efficacy levels in the Initiation sub-dimension, suggesting a potential familial influence on confidence in clinical tasks. Future studies could further explore how simulation training may address these discrepancies to ensure equitable skill development across diverse student backgrounds.

This study supports the broader adoption of peer-supported simulation in midwifery programs to cultivate competent and confident practitioners. The findings of this study should be interpreted with caution for several reasons. First, the small sample size and the absence of a control group may have limited the statistical power to fully assess the effectiveness of the peer-assisted training model. In addition, the allocation of participants was based on voluntary seating according to arrival order rather than randomization, which may have introduced selection bias. Furthermore, in some cases, peer mentors supported two students instead of one, which could have influenced learning motivation. While these factors may partially limit the representativeness and generalizability of the results, the findings nonetheless highlight the potential benefits of integrating peer-assisted simulation training into midwifery education.

Conclusion

In conclusion, as technology advances, the utilization of simulation in midwifery education has been shown to enhance students’ knowledge, skills, self-confidence, and satisfaction levels. In this study conducted with midwifery students studying at a foundation university, episiotomy repair simulation was simulated with peer support using chicken breast. The decrease in state and trait anxiety scores before and after the simulation was found to be significant. In addition, in terms of general self-efficacy scores, a significant difference was observed before and after the simulation in the sub-dimension of Sustaining Effort & Persistence. In light of these results, it is recommended that peer support be integrated into the pre-clinical episiotomy repair simulation application to improve students’ skills and reduce their anxiety, enabling them to adapt to clinical practice faster. At the same time, studies can be planned to re-evaluate learning in hospital practices.

Limitations of research

The most important limitation of this study is that the post-tests were evaluated 20 min after the simulation session and there was no control group. Thus, the study data can only be generalized to the students included in the study. In addition, the relatively short learning period due to the evaluation of learning only in the educational environment and the inability to assign one-to-one peers to a few students are among the limitations of the study. Moreover, further studies are required to conduct more randomized controlled trials using different simulation materials, such as silicone perineum models.
