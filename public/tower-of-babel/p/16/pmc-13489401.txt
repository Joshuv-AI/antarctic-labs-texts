Unraveling cell–cell communication through spatial transcriptomics: a review of computational methods
Authors: Jing Yang, Yu Shyr, Qi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpatial transcriptomics (ST) has enabled direct interrogation of cell–cell communication (CCC) within intact tissues, providing critical spatial context that is lost in single-cell RNA-sequencing-based inference and allowing more accurate identification of physically plausible and spatially organized interactions. A rapidly expanding community of computational tools has emerged to decode CCC from ST data. Here, we provide a comprehensive review of the conceptual evolution and methodological landscape of spatial CCC inference, classifying existing approaches into two major trajectories. One trajectory, spatial pattern-based methods, assumes CCC events manifest as identifiable spatial patterns, such as colocalization, coordinated spatial signals, or higher-order spatial organization captured by deep learning models. The other trajectory, expression modulation-based approaches, assumes that CCC events influence the transcriptomic state of receiver cells. We systematically dissect their biological assumptions, statistical and deep learning frameworks, strengths, and limitations, and highlight emerging challenges in validation, benchmarking, multimodal integration, and tissue-specific modeling. Finally, we outline future directions toward achieving dynamic, multilayered reconstruction of inter- and intracellular communication, de novo signaling, and integrative multi-omics modeling.

Introduction

Cell–cell communication (CCC) is fundamental to the organization, coordination, and homeostatic maintenance of multicellular systems. It enables cells to orchestrate developmental programs, maintain tissue homeostasis, and respond to physiological cues, as well as drive pathological processes such as cancer progression and immune dysfunction [1–5]. CCC refers to the process by which a sender cell transmits signals to a receiver cell through molecular interactions, most commonly mediated by ligands (signaling molecules) and receptors (proteins that detect and respond to signals), thereby coordinating gene expression, cellular behavior, and function within tissues. A ligand–receptor (LR) interaction occurs when a ligand produced by one cell binds to its cognate receptor on another cell, triggering downstream signaling cascades and transcriptional responses. For example, CCC can reveal how tumor cells evade immune surveillance through inhibitory signaling pathways such as Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD-1)/Programmed Cell Death Ligand 1 (PD-L1) interactions [6], or how stromal cells promote tumor progression through growth factor signaling [7]. In developmental systems, CCC analyses uncover how spatially organized signaling gradients guide cell fate decisions [8]. In inflammatory and immune responses, it can identify coordinated signaling between immune cell subsets that regulate activation, recruitment, and resolution processes [9, 10]. Therefore, computational inference of CCC is essential for understanding tissue organization, disease etiology, and therapeutic response [4, 11].

Earlier analysis of CCC relied on bulk transcriptomic or proteomic assays, limiting resolution to tissue-level averages [12, 13]. The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has enabled quantification of ligand and receptor expression at single-cell resolution, giving rise to the first generation of computational CCC methods that infer interactions based on LR co-expression [12, 14–17]. While these tools provide valuable insights into cellular signaling networks, a critical limitation remains because scRNA-seq lacks spatial context. Consequently, inferred communication merely reflects potential signaling events but does not capture microenvironmental specificity and may not even be spatially plausible [3, 12, 18, 19].

The emergence of spatial transcriptomics (ST) has therefore provided a transformative framework to directly profile gene expression within intact tissue architecture, enabling systematic inference of CCC in its native spatial context [20–22]. Through platforms such as 10× Visium, 10× Visium HD, Slide-seq, Multiplexed Error-Robust Fluorescence in situ Hybridization (MERFISH), CosMx SMI, and Xenium, ST enables targeted or transcriptome-wide profiling of cells in situ at varying spatial resolutions [23]. The rapid expansion of ST data has catalyzed a new era of computational methods that incorporate physical proximity, tissue topology, and microenvironmental organization into CCC inference [24, 25]. A growing number of computational methods have been developed to infer CCC from ST data [4, 5]. These tools vary widely in their biological assumptions, modeling strategies, and input requirements, creating both opportunities and challenges for users seeking appropriate resources.

In this review, we classify spatial CCC methods into two primary conceptual trajectories (Table 1). One trajectory, spatial pattern-based methods [26–43], extends scRNA-seq CCC principles into spatial contexts by detecting co-localization and/or co-enrichment patterns of two entities, such as known LR pairs. These tools assume that CCC events manifest as identifiable spatial patterns between cell types, genes, or cells. Early approaches employ permutation-based tests to assess spatial co-localization against spatial randomness [28–30], while later models extend to coordinated spatial signals, such as spatial co-enirchment and cross-correlation, or even higher-order spatial organization captured by deep learning models [34–36, 39–43]. These methods are popular due to their conceptual simplicity, interpretability, and compatibility with existing LR databases. In parallel, the other trajectory, expression modulation-based methods have emerged [44–51]. This trajectory assumes that CCC events influence the transcriptomic state of receiver cells, which moves beyond spatial pattern to explicitly model how the local cellular environment influences gene expression. Early variance-based models decomposed gene expression into intrinsic and spatial components [44, 45], whereas more recent methods employ regression, probabilistic graphical modeling, or graph neural networks (GNNs) to infer nonlinear effects of neighboring cells on gene expression in receiver cells [47, 49, 50]. Here, we synthesize the current methodological landscape of spatial CCC infererence, offer a unified conceptual framework for classifying approaches, and discuss current challenges and future opportunities for achieving mechanistic, dynamic, and multi-omic modeling of CCC.

Two trajectories of spatial cell–cell communication inference

Broadly, existing CCC methods can be classified into two major trajectories based on their underlying biological assumptions and mathematical objectives (Table 1). One trajectory, spatial pattern-based methods, assumes that CCC events follow specific spatial patterns. These methods infer CCC by testing whether non-random spatial patterns exist between two entities, including LR pairs, cell-type pairs, or even cell pairs. Although they range from simple statistical approaches to advanced deep learning models, they share a common objective, i.e. to identify the existence and structure of communication links. This category encompasses a broad spectrum of mathematical strategies designed to detect diverse spatial patterns of communication (Table 2). These approaches range from spatial co-localization or co-enrichment analyses to frameworks that capture higher-order organization. At the simplest level, co-localization or co-enrichment methods identify LR or cell-type pairs that are proximal more frequently than expected by chance (Fig. 1a), or detect local regions with enriched co-expression of LR pairs. Beyond proximity or co-enrichment, spatial cross-correlation methods (e.g. bivariate Moran’s I) quantify coordinated variation in expression across tissue coordinates, capturing spatial structure. Moving further, flow-based approaches, such as optimal transport, model directed communication across spatial gradients as a distributional problem rather than a static relationship. Finally, representation learning methods leverage deep learning to uncover non-linear and latent spatial patterns, enabling the characterization of complex tissue organization.

In contrast, the second trajectory, expression modulation-based methods, assumes that CCC events alter the transcriptomic state of receiver cells. These approaches model how the composition and activity of neighboring cells drive transcriptomic changes within the target cell (Fig. 1b). Specifically, they treat the local spatial context as an independent variable to explain or predict gene expression in the target cell (Table 3). This is often achieved through predictive modeling frameworks, such as regression models, GNNs, or Random Forests, which are trained to reconstruct a cell’s expression profile based on its local niche. Alternatively, some approaches use variance partitioning to decompose gene expression into intrinsic components and extrinsic, spatially driven components. By quantifying these transcriptomic responses, such methods not only infer CCC but also identify “response genes” that are significantly modulated by neighboring contexts, thereby providing a direct measure of the functional consequences of CCC.

Spatial neighborhood construction

While the two trajectories differ in their biological assumptions and modeling targets, all methods share a foundational step: constructing spatial neighborhoods. This process transforms raw spatial coordinates into an adjacency or spatial weights matrix that dictates which cells are allowed to “talk.” These neighborhood definitions generally fall into three strategies based on how they model the “strength” of neighbor influence: discrete binary cutoffs, continuous decay functions, and global graph representations.

Discrete binary cutoff methods represent the simplest form of neighborhood definition, in which a neighbor is either included or excluded. For example, k-nearest neighbors assigns a fixed number k of neighbors to each cell regardless of physical distance, making the approach robust to variations in cell density but potentially including distant cells in sparse regions. Delaunay triangulation links the two cells if they share an edge. Alternatively, radius-based methods include all cells within a predefined physical distance, capturing constraints of local signaling (e.g. paracrine diffusion) but potentially leaving cells isolated in low-density regions.

In contrast, continuous decay functions model neighbor influence as a function of distance, assuming that interactions weaken with increasing separation. These approaches typically use Gaussian or exponential kernels, assigning higher weights to nearby cells while progressively down-weighting more distant ones. Compared to binary cutoffs, this formulation better reflects biological processes such as morphogen gradients and molecular diffusion.

Finally, global graph representations move beyond local, independent neighborhood definitions by constructing a unified graph over all cells. These methods learn or optimize global structures, such as optimal transport couplings or graph diffusion operators, to encode spatial relationships. In this formulation, the signaling environment is treated as a collective system in which the position or influence of a single cell can affect the inferred relevance of others across the tissue. This enables the capture of higher-order spatial organization and long-range dependencies that cannot be recovered from pairwise distance calculations alone. For example, cytokines such as Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha (TNFa) or Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) diffuse across compartments, creating tissue-scale gradients that depend on the positions of all cytokine-producing cells. The downstream activation of fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells is determined by this emergent spatial field rather than pairwise distances alone [52, 53].

Spatial pattern-based methods

Spatial pattern-based methods assume that CCC events manifest as identifiable spatial architectures (Table 1). Consequently, these methods model the structural relationships or co-occurrence between two entities, denoted as (I, J) ~ f(expression, spatial), where I and J may represent two cell types, two individual cells, two specific genes, primarily focusing on LR pairs, as well as a ligand in one cell type and a receptor in another. By evaluating the joint spatial distribution of these entities for non-random association, these methods identify where signaling is potentially occurring without predicting the downstream transcriptional response. Depending on the underlying metric, they can be further classified as spatial co-localization or co-enrichment methods that detect significant physical proximity or “hotspots,” spatial cross-correlation methods that quantify coordinated distributions, optimal transport-based methods that model the “optimal flow” of multiple signals, or deep learning-based methods that capture higher-order patterns through representation learning.

Spatial co-localization/co-enrichment methods

Spatial co-localization/co-enrichment methods quantify cell-type or LR/target pairs colocalization or co-enrichment within spatial neighborhoods, typically using permutation-based tests or co-occurrence statistics.

Both CellPhoneDBv5 [26] and SpatialCellChat [27] represent an evolution in intercellular signaling analysis, adapting frameworks originally optimized for scRNA-seq to spatially-informed CCC inference. Both tools rely on the mean expression of LR pairs between two cell types (A and B), denoted as S(LA,RB), against those in random cells to estimate their communication probability p(LA,RB). To extend to spatial analysis, CellPhoneDBv5 introduces a “gating” strategy that restricts analysis to LR pairs between cell types known to co-exist in user-defined “microenvironments” (A,B∈ M ) without changing the underlying cell-label shuffling statistical foundation. In contrast, SpatialCellChat incorporates a discrete binary cutoff or continuous decay kernel to weight the communication probability, ensuring that signaling intensity is a spatially-weighted function of the interaction score (S(LA,RB)*WAB). Consequently, SpatialCellChat tests the observed interaction score against a spatially-weighted null distribution generated through label-shuffling.

Giotto [28] and Squidy [29] are both comprehensive frameworks for spatial omics analysis. They identify two distinct levels of CCC, probability of cell-type co-localization p(A,B) and probability of LR co-enrichment in two cell types p(LA, RB). To identify cell-type co-localization, both tools build binary neighbor graphs and utilize cell-label shuffling to determine if two cell types A and B are significantly more proximal than random, denoted as p(A,B) = p(N(A,B)obs > N(A,B)perm). However, they diverge significantly at estimating p(LA, RB). Giotto calculates a communication score based on the expression of L and R specifically at the interface where cell types A and B are in physical contact. Instead of label-shuffling, Giotto evaluates communication significance through intra-cell-type coordinate shuffling, which maintains cell labels while permuting spatial locations. This approach specifically seeks the spatial co-enrichment of the signal at the boundary, testing whether the LR signal between A and B where they touch is significantly higher than the signal between A and B anywhere else. In contrast, Squidy implements the original CellPhoneDB designed for scRNA-seq to estimate p(LA, RB), which is essentially a spatially-unaware LR interaction model that does not incorporate physical distance in the permutation.

SpaGene [30] utilizes a topology-centric framework to identify LR co-localization and infer communication potential. The method first extracts a subnetwork with only cells or spots with high L and R expression from the binary neighbor graph and then calculates the earth mover’s distance (EMD) score based on the observed degree distribution. This observed EMD score is compared against a random topology null model generated by sampling an equivalent number of cells or spots. A key advantage of SpaGene is the use of a weighted spatial degree, which allows more weight to regions of dense, multi-neighbor contact between the high L and R domains. By prioritizing cells with dense inter-domain connectivity, SpaGene effectively highlights robust signaling “hotspots” and ensures that high-density topological patterns are statistically distinguishable from sparse, coincidental adjacencies.

stLearn [31] implements a “Spatially-Constrained Two-Level Permutation” framework to identify spatial co-enrichment of ligand and receptor expression. stLearn first constructs a binary neighborhood graph through a fixed radius, k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN), or Delaunay triangulation. Within this spatial framework, stLearn first performs a spot-level significance test by shuffling “decoy” genes, which are selected for their similar expression quantiles to the ligand and receptor, to ensure that identified hotspots are biologically robust rather than artifacts of high gene abundance. Following this functional validation, stLearn can optionally attribute these signals to specific cell-type pairs (A and B) by evaluating their spatial overlap within significant spot-neighbor adjacencies against a background generated by cell-label shuffling.

Renoir [32] moves beyond traditional LR analysis by focusing on Ligand–Target (LT) pairs, which are derived from NicheNet [17]. It estimated the spatial co-enrichement of LT pairs by calculating their neighborhood activity score, which is the product of expressin correspondence score (ECS) and inherent similairy measurement (ISM) of ligands and targets. In this way, Renoir filters out high baseline expression that isn’t actually part of a coordinated signaling event and ensures that a high score reflects both co-enrichment of LT pairs and a spatially synchronized signaling relationship between the sender and receiver. Finally, these spot-level activity scores are aggregated to identify spatial niches and are summarized at the cell-type level, allowing researchers to identify the specific “sender” and “receiver” cell types driving influential communication pathways across the tissue microenvironment.

Instead of generating a null distribution, BAyesian Tweedie modeling of COMmunications (BATCOM) [33] employs a generalized linear model to deconvolve spot-level LR co-enrichment into cell-type-specific interactions. By regressing spot-to-spot LR co-enrichment against the proportional of cell types (MA and MB) and continuous exponential distance decay, BATCOM statistically evaluates whether an LR interaction is significantly elevated between specific cell-type pairs (A and B) relative to other cell-type pairs.

Spatial cross-correlation methods

While spatial co-enrichment identifies the local co-expression of LR pairs, spatial cross-correlation detects spatially coordinated signals, wherein the expression of a ligand at one location is statistically correlated with the expression of its receptor in neighboring locations. Both MERINGUE [34] and SpatialDM [35] utilize a Bivariate Moran’s I statistic to quantify the degree of spatial cross-correlation between a LR pair. MERINGUE implements a binary spatial neighbor graph, typically derived from kNN or Voronoi tessellation, while SpatialDM uses the continuous distance decay strategy to model the natural decay of signaling strength over space. To ensure robustness, SpatialDM incorporates a multilayer permutation test that constructs a rigorous null distribution for each LR pair. SpatialDM is extremely effective at filtering out false-positive LR pairs but the extensive permutation testing makes it significantly more computationally expensive and slower to run on high-resolution or large-scale datasets compared to MERINGUE.

Unlike MERINGUE and SpatialDM, Intercellular Gene Association Network (IGAN) [36] identifies significantly correlated gene pairs beyond standard LR pairs using a non-parametric statistical model. Furthermore, IGAN does not analyze spatially coupled patterns across all cells or spots globally; instead, it focuses specifically on cell-type pairs. By pairing every cell with its adjacent neighbors and grouping these pairs by cell-type combinations, IGAN establishes a robust statistical basis for inference. This identification of spatially coupled gene pairs enables the model to not only infer LR interactions but also explore the upstream and downstream pathways of ligands and receptors from a network perspective, providing a novel panoramic cell-interaction-pathway graph.

Optimal transportation-based methods

While spatial co-enrichment and cross-correlation methods primarily evaluate the spatial patterns of individual LR pairs separately, biological signaling is often characterized by multi-molecular competition, where a single ligand may interact with multiple receptors, and vice versa. To resolve these complex, many-to-many dependencies, optimal transport-based methods (e.g. COMMunication analysis by Optimal Transport (COMMOT) [37], Spatially Optimal Transporting the single cells (SpaOTsc) [38]) model intercellular communication as a resource allocation problem, identifying the most probable signaling “flow” by globally optimizing the transport of ligands to receptors across the tissue.

Instead of building local binary or continuous neighbor graphs, COMMOT [37] and SpaOTsc [38] utilize a global cost manifold (a global graph representation) where the “cost” of communication is defined by the physical distance between any two spots in the tissue. COMMOT solves this as a collective optimal transport problem to balance the “mass” of multiple competing ligands and receptors, while spaOTsc often employs unbalanced optimal transport to integrate multi-omic data and infer the effective spatial range of signaling. While this global approach inherently captures directed signaling and handles molecular competition, it is significantly more computationally intensive than spatial co-enrichment or cross-correlation methods and requires careful tuning of the entropy regularization parameter to prevent mathematically “blurred” or biologically unrealistic long-range interactions.

Deep learning-based methods

Recent advances in deep learning have transformed spatial CCC analysis by enabling models to capture complex and high-order spatial patterns. These methods model spatial relationships between gene pairs (gene-centric) or cell pairs (cell-centric).

Graph Convolutional Neural networks for Genes (GCNG) [39] and spaCI [40] are gene-centric, as they focus on modeling gene–gene interactions within a spatial context. GCNG utilizes a curated list of interacting LR pairs to train a Graph Convolutional Network. By taking the expression of ligand and receptor and a binary spatial neighborhood graph as input, GCNG is trained via supervised learning to capture spatially coordinated patterns of interacting LR pairs. Once trained, the model can predict the interaction potential of any arbitrary gene pair. In contrast, spaCI uses a gene-based linear encoder as well as a cell-based attentive graph encoder to project genes g into a latent space Zg. The model is optimized using triplet loss, which ensures that interacting LR pairs are mapped to proximal locations in the latent space while non-interactive pairs are pushed apart. This triplet-based approach simplifies the optimization landscape and ensures a clear separation between true biological interactions and spatial noise. While spaCI runs at the single-cell level, it produces attention weights for every cell–cell pair. Because a single-cell pair is very noisy, spaCI aggregates these weights by cell type to show that gene–gene interaction or LR interaction at cell-type level.

Conversely, Deep earning framework for Landscapes of Interacting Cells (DeepLinc) [41], DeepTalk [42], and Cell Neural Networks on Spatial Transcriptomics (CellNEST) [43] adopt a cell-centric perspective, modeling CCC under the assumption that interacting cells are governed by spatially dependent gene programs. All three methods embed cells C into a latent space Zc that captures cell–cell interactions and global spatial organization. DeepLinc employs a graph variational autoencoder (GVAE) to project cells into a latent representation optimized to explain observed interactions. It is trained in a self-supervised manner by minimizing reconstruction loss of the spatial adjacency matrix A, thereby identifying gene programs that best recapitulate the neighborhood graph. Similarly, DeepTalk learns cell embeddings Zc through a self-supervised framework that maximizes the likelihood of “positive” (neighboring) cell pairs while minimizing that of “negative” (non-neighboring) pairs defined by binary cross entropy. After reconstructing the interaction network, it overlays LR pairs onto the inferred links and evaluates significance via cell-label shuffling. Finally, CellNEST integrates a graph attention network (GAT) encoder with Deep Graph Infomax (DGI) for unsupervised contrastive learning. By maximizing the discriminator score between local cell embeddings (Zc) and a global tissue-level summary (s) (denoted as D(Zc,s)), while minimizing the score for corrupted embedding (Zc’) (denoted as D(Zc’,s)), CellNEST enforces consistency with broader spatial architecture, enabling the identification of complex CCC niches.

Overall, deep learning-based methods are uniquely capable of capturing complex spatial patterns beyond simple co-localization, co-enrichment, or cross-correlation. However, they are typically computationally expensive and require large, high-quality datasets for effective training. Furthermore, they tend to be less transparent than traditional statistical models, as gene and cell embeddings and their encoded patterns are difficult to interpret, and their performance can be highly sensitive to hyperparameter tuning.

Expression modulation-based methods

While spatial pattern-based methods provide a high-resolution map of potential interactions, they conceptualize CCC as identifiable spatial patterns between two genes, cells, or cell types. As a result, they ignore the actual functional consequence of the CCC event. In other words, they indicate where the “conversation” is likely happening without confirming if the “message” was received and acted upon. In contrast, expression modulation-based methods shift the focus from spatial pattern to the functional response, assuming that a CCC event should trigger a measurable change in the receiver cell’s transcriptome. This framework can be denoted as Y ~ f(expression, spatial), where Y represents the expression of a target gene (or a full expression vector) in a receiver cell, modeled as a function of its inherent state and its neighboring spatial niche. This approach is inherently predictive and directional, aiming to quantify how the external molecular environment drives internal transcriptional shifts and thus provide a more mechanistic understanding of how the tissue microenvironment shapes cellular identity and function.

Existing approaches include conventional statistical models that estimate how much of a cell’s gene expression is affected by the composition and state of its spatial niches. Others use deep learning to capture non-linear modulation of gene expression by neighborhood context.

Statistical modeling methods

Spatial Variance Component Analysis (SVCA) [44], Multiview Intercellular SpaTial modeling (MISTy) [45], Cell–Cell communications analysis by Partial Least Square regression modeling (CCPLS) [46], Spacia [47], SpaCeNet [48], and stMLnet [49] all utilize predictive modeling frameworks that treat neighboring features as independent variables to model target gene expression as the dependent variable, yet they differ significantly in their modeling strategies and output granularity.

SVCA [44] decomposes the expression variation of individual genes into additive components: intrinsic cell state effects, environmental factors, and cell–cell interactions. While it quantifies the relative contribution of cell–cell interactions to the total variance of the gene, it does not pinpoint specific interacting cells or gene pairs.

MISTy [45] implements a flexible framework in which multiple “views” capture different sources of variation, including intrinsic (intraview) and spatially derived (juxtaview and paraview) components. The intraview represents within-cell molecular information, whereas juxtaview and paraview encode local and broader spatial context, respectively. Each view can incorporate arbitrary molecular features or pathway activities, enabling flexible modeling of factors influencing gene expression. While MISTy quantifies the relative contribution of each view to a target, thereby estimating the influence of intrinsic and spatial factors, it does not inherently infer specific gene–gene interactions. However, such interactions can be estimated by encoding them as dedicated views. For example, modeling LR scores as a view allows MISTy to quantify the importance of specific LR interactions in explaining variation in a target gene’s expression.

CCPLS [46] utilizes partial least squares regression to model gene expression in a receiver cell type as a function of the neighboring cell-type composition. Consequently, it identifies which sender cell-types modulate specific genes in the receiver cell type. Finally, Spacia [47] employs a multi-instance learning (MIL) framework integrated with Bayesian Hierarchical Probit Regression. By treating the neighborhood of a receiver cell as a “bag” of potential senders, it detects which genes in the sender cell type drive the target gene expression in the receiver cell type.

Unlike standard regression or Random Forest approaches, SpaCeNet [48] is built on Probabilistic Graphical Models (PGM). It seeks to identify conditional relationships where target gene expression is modeled as a function of the expression levels of all neighboring genes. This distinguishes it from methods like CCPLS, which uses cell-type composition, or MISTy, which uses aggregated spatial views. The PGM structure of SpaCeNet is designed to provide statistical proof that a neighbor’s gene expression is necessary to explain the target gene’s state, even after accounting for the target cell’s own internal regulatory network and other surrounding molecular signals. As a result, it extracts a global gene–gene interaction network that defines the tissue’s expression architecture.

stMLnet [49] is a knowledge-driven framework designed to infer multilayer signaling networks, specifically the Ligand–Receptor–Transcription Factor (TF)–Target Gene axis, by integrating ST with biological priors. Unlike methods such as SVCA, MISTy, SpaCeNet, or CCPLS, which often model the spatial variance of user-defined genes without pre-selecting for specific biological functions, stMLnet specifically filters for target genes known to be downstream of signaling cascades derived from multiple data sources. By modeling the interaction between a ligand in a sender cell type and its corresponding receptor in a receiver cell type, the framework employs Random Forest regression to quantify which specific LR pairs are most responsible for driving the expression of known downstream target genes within the receiver cell type.

Overall, statistical modeling methods provide a mechanistic and interpretable understanding of CCC that highlight which neighbor types or spatial relationships have measurable transcriptional impact on the receiver cell. However, nonlinear, higher-order, or relay interactions might not be detected because these methods assume extrinsic effects are additive or linear decomposability.

Deep learning-based methods

To capture the non-linear and high-order effects of spatial context on target gene expression, two deep learning-based methods have been developed. Although they often utilize similar convolutional neural network structures as those in the spatial pattern-based category, these tools are fundamentally bifurcated by their primary objectives. Deep learning methods in the spatial pattern category, such as spaCI [40] and DeepLinc [41], focus on reconstructing latent gene–gene or cell–cell interaction topologies through relational gene or cell embeddings. In contrast, those in the expression modulation category, such as Node-centric Expression Models (NCEM) [50] and HoloNet [51], aim to reconstruct the target gene expression or the transcriptional profile of a cell.

NCEM [50] offers a versatile, graph-based framework designed to reconstruct a cell’s entire transcriptional profile by disentangling its intrinsic identity from extrinsic spatial modulation. NCEM scales across multiple levels of complexity. Its linear mode utilizes regression for interpretable “fold-change” estimates, e.g. how the presence of a neighboring cell type shifts the expression of genes in the receiver cell. In contrast, its non-linear modes employ GNNs, MLPs, or Conditional Variational Autoencoders (CVAE) to capture non-linear and high-order effects that the combined influence of multiple different neighbors may exert on the target cell’s profile. Furthermore, an LR mode incorporates biological priors to quantify functional impact of the ligand “dosage” in the neighborhood on the receiver cell’s transcriptome. Collectively, these allow NCEM to identify both significant cell-type interactions and the underlying molecular signals driving functional heterogeneity.

HoloNet [51] extends the expression modulation framework by utilizing a multi-view GNN to reconstruct target gene expression through the lens of LR signaling dynamics. The method first constructs LR activity maps by modeling ligand diffusion and receptor binding across multiple spatial scales, ranging from direct contact to long-range paracrine signaling. These potential maps function as the foundational edges of a GNN, which is trained to predict a receiver cell’s transcriptional response based on its cumulative signaling input. By optimizing the model to minimize the variance between predicted and observed expression, HoloNet identifies the specific LR axes responsible for expression modulation. Consequently, it can determine that cell type A influences expression of gene X in cell type B through a specific LR axis.

In summary, expression modulation-based methods model target gene expression as a direct function of the spatial niche, providing a framework that is inherently predictive. They move beyond descriptive spatial patterns to provide a mechanistic understanding of how specific cell-type interactions and LR axes drive functional heterogeneity across a tissue. Within this category, statistical modeling methods prioritize interpretability, utilizing frameworks like linear regression to quantify specific contributions. For example, determining that neighboring T-cells explain 15% of the variance in a stress-response gene. In contrast, deep learning methods are optimized for complexity. By employing different neural network architectures, deep learning frameworks capture how the entire neighborhood geometry and multi-scale signaling axes influence expression in non-linear, non-additive ways that simple regression cannot detect. However, these deep learning approaches often operate as “black boxes,” where the high-capacity learning that enables them to model complex biological systems also makes the specific decision-making process less transparent compared to traditional statistical models.

Challenges and opportunities

Despite rapidly evolving methodologies, computational inference of CCC from ST data still faces substantial conceptual, computational, and validation challenges. Unlike conventional classification tasks, CCC inference lacks a gold standard for true LR interactions between specific cell types. As a result, most validation efforts rely on simulation, or indirect and partial evidence from the literature, which are biased toward well-studied pathways and poorly capture novel or context-specific signaling. Experimental validation remains the most reliable approach but is extremely limited in scale, typically confirming only a few interactions through immunohistochemistry, in situ hybridization, or perturbation (e.g. knockout or Perturb-seq) assays. Indirect validation, such as proteomics or additional spatial modalities, can provide supporting evidence but remain largely correlational rather than causal. Synthetic or simulated data validation, where ground-truth interactions are artificially defined, offers controlled benchmarking but fails to capture the biological variability and noise inherent to real data.

Another related challenge is the absence of comprehensive, large-scale benchmarking studies for spatial CCC tools. Although some scRNA-seq-based CCC benchmarking studies include early spatial CCC tools or adapted versions of scRNA-seq CCC methods [3, 54], no systematic evaluation has been conducted specifically for spatial CCC tools. Numerous spatial CCC algorithms have been developed, each differing in modeling assumptions, required inputs, neighborhood graph construction, null models, and output granularity. However, there is no standardized framework to evaluate their accuracy, robustness, or biological interpretability across diverse datasets or biological contexts, making it difficult for users to choose the most appropriate method for their data. Although several tools such as SpaTalk [55], spaCI [40], DeepTalk [42], and HoloNet [51] reported limited comparisons against selected methods, these evaluations are typically restricted to a small number of datasets or specific performance metrics, providing limited guidance on method selection.

A further challenge is that the accuracy of many spatial CCC methods depends heavily on the quality and completeness of LR databases, especially for approaches based on spatial co-localization, co-enrichment, or cross-correlation [56, 57]. Existing LR databases vary substantially in interaction coverage, species and tissue specificity, and their representation of simple pairs versus multi-subunit complexes. For example, connectomeDB2025 [56] emphasizes curated multi-subunit complexes, CellChat [27] incorporates signaling pathway annotations, and NicheNet [17] extends LR interactions to downstream target genes. As a result, the choice of database can markedly influence CCC results. Highly curated resources improve specificity but may miss context-specific interactions, whereas broader databases increase coverage at the cost of potential false positives. In addition, current LR catalogs remain incomplete and biased, and largely lack context-dependent modeling. Most resources are also static and manually curated, typically omitting essential biological features such as ligand isoforms, binding affinities, or competitive inhibitors [57, 58].

Furthermore, the applicability of certain CCC methods is constrained by the spatial omics technology, particularly its gene coverage. Whole-transcriptome platforms, such as 10× Visium, provide broad gene coverage and are therefore appropriate for methods that directly rely on observed LR expression. These include spatial pattern-based approaches such as Giotto [28], Squidpy [29], CellPhoneDB v5 [26], SpatialCellChat [27], and stLearn [31], which require sufficient coverage of both ligands and receptors to reliably detect co-enrichment signals. In contrast, targeted gene-panel platforms, such as 10× Xenium, measure a limited set of genes and may not capture many LR pairs. Missing ligand or receptor genes can substantially reduce sensitivity and lead to false negatives in LR-dependent methods, particularly when key signaling molecules are absent from the panel. In such settings, methods that infer communication without directly relying on LR pairs are more appropriate, including approaches such as DeepLinc [41], MISTy [45], Spacia [47], and NCEM [50]. Overall, LR-dependent methods are most appropriate for whole-transcriptome data, whereas LR-independent approaches are better suited for panel-limited platforms. In practice, integrating external data sources (e.g. matched scRNA-seq or prior signaling networks) can further improve CCC inference when gene coverage is incomplete.

Since inferenced CCC may overlook context-specific signaling [17], integrating multiple predictive tools holds promise to improve accuracy. Structural prediction methods such as AlphaFold [53, 59] can evaluate the physical plausibility of LR interactions by assessing binding interfaces and structural compatibility, thereby refining existing LR databases. In parallel, large-scale protein–protein interaction networks (such as OmniPath [60], HINT [61], and IntAct [62]) offer complementary information that can expand candidate interactions and assign confidence weights for inferred interactions. These structural and network-derived features can be incorporated into CCC models as priors, edge weights, or additional features in statistical or deep learning frameworks, enhancing both accuracy and biological interpretability. In addition to global and cross-tissue resources, there is growing recognition that tissue-specific LR databases are essential for improving inference precision [25, 63]. Different tissues exhibit unique repertoires of signaling molecules and receptor isoforms, as well as distinct microenvironmental contexts that shape ligand availability and receptor sensitivity [64, 65]. Accordingly, databases tailored to specific biological systems, such as NeuroChat [66], which compiles neural-specific signaling interactions, might improve biological reliability of spatial CCC predictions and thus reduce false positives.

Conclusion and future directions

CCC is fundamental to multicellular organization, enabling coordinated cellular behavior and maintenance of tissue homeostasis. ST has transformed CCC analysis by preserving tissue architecture while capturing high-dimensional gene expression. Over the past several years, numerous computational methods have been developed to infer CCC from spatial data, revealing complex communication patterns across diverse biological contexts, including development, immune responses, and tumor–stroma crosstalk, as well as tissue homeostasis, inflammation, regeneration, and neural systems [14, 19]. Despite this remarkable progress, the field remains in a formative stage. Current methods differ in their assumptions, input requirements, and interpretability, leaving several key challenges unresolved while highlighting opportunities for future development.

Most spatial CCC tools are designed for static, single-condition data. However, intercellular signaling is dynamic, continuously responding to development, therapeutic interventions, or environment [67–69]. This highlights the need for the development of spatial CCC methods that can identify differentially regulated interactions across conditions. Building on concepts introduced by scLR [70], spatial CCC methods could be extended to model how communication networks are rewired between conditions. Such differential spatial CCC methods would be particularly valuable for studying developmental trajectories and treatment response, where CCC rewiring both drives and marks biological state transitions.

Spatial pattern-based and expression modulation-based approaches offer complementary strengths while harboring distinct limitations. Spatial pattern-based methods are highly sensitive to “communication hotspots,” but they predominantly rely on predefined LR databases and cannot guarantee that those hotspots translate into a functional biological response. In contrast, expression modulation-based methods provide deeper functional insights. While many are LR-independent, they prioritize the receiver cell’s transcriptomic response over the sender’s identity, often making it difficult to pinpoint the specific molecular drivers triggering those changes. A hybrid approach may bridge these gaps by utilizing pattern-based analysis to define the “search space” of potential LR interactions, then employing expression modulation as a rigorous functional filter. For instance, while spatial co-enrichment identifies the physical boundaries of a communication hotspot, neighborhood expression-modulation quantifies how that environment reshapes the transcriptome of focal receiver cells. Interactions supported by both spatial co-localization and significant downstream expression shifts are more likely to represent true, functional communication events. This hybrid strategy not only enhances the specificity of CCC inference but also facilitates the discovery of context-specific or novel interactions that might be overlooked by static, predefined databases alone.

Current CCC methods primarily work on a single spatial modality, most commonly transcriptomics. However, intercellular communication is inherently multimodal, involving proteins, peptides, lipids, nucleic acids, and metabolites whose activities are regulated by complex posttranscriptional and posttranslational mechanisms [4, 25, 71, 72]. As a result, transcript-based CCC inference often fails to fully capture signaling activity, particularly when ligand availability, receptor activation, or downstream signaling depends on protein modification states or metabolic contexts. For example, glycosylation and phosphorylation influence LR binding and signaling outcomes [73], while chromatin accessibility controls ligand or receptor transcription [74]. Recent advances in spatial multi-omics technologies provide new opportunities to overcome these limitations by enabling the measurement of multiple molecular layers. Emerging platforms now support joint profiling of transcriptomes alongside proteins (e.g. spatial-CITE-seq, Xenium protein panels), metabolites, or epigenomic features, allowing more signaling molecules in spatial context. In particular, protein-level measurements provide more proximal data of LR interactions, while spatial metabolomics can reveal signaling gradients that are not captured by transcriptomics alone. Despite these technological advances, most existing CCC methods remain primarily designed for single-modality data. While some expression modulation-based methods (e.g. MISTy [45] and NCEM [50]) can, in principle, incorporate measurements from other modalities as additional features or constraints, true multi-omics integration remains rare. Such integration can occur both within spatial assays—where jointly measured RNA and protein data resolve transcription–protein discordance—and across disparate datasets, linking ST with scRNA-seq, multiplex imaging, or established signaling networks. Leveraging these complementary sources can refine cell-type annotations, expand gene coverage, and strengthen the inference of downstream signaling. Therefore, there is an increasing need for CCC inference methods that natively integrate multi-omics data to achieve reliable and comprehensive CCC maps.

Overall, developing methods that capture CCC rewiring between conditions, combine the two methodological trajectories, incorporate multilayer constraints, and integrate multi-omics data are expected to significantly improve the accuracy and reliability of CCC inference, achieving a truly mechanistic and holistic understanding of the multicellular ecosystem.
