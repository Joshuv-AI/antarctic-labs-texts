SsCyp86 modulates sporisorium scitamineum mating/filamentation and pathogenicity through regulating fatty acid metabolism
Authors: Enping Cai, Jiaru Deng, Ruqing Feng, Wenqiang Zheng, Yifang Wang, Meixin Yan, Changqing Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFatty acid metabolism constitutes a significant and intricate biochemical process within microorganisms. Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes are found in most organisms and occupy a pivotal position in the metabolism of fatty acids. However, the role of CYP450 enzyme mediated fatty acid metabolism in the pathogenicity of pathogenic fungi remains unclear.MethodsIn this study, a CYP450 enzyme-encoding gene, SsCYP86, was identified in the sugarcane smut fungus Sporisorium scitamineum and its functions were characterized using a target gene homologous recombination strategy and metabonomics.ResultsWe found that the expression of SsCYP86 was induced by or sugarcane wax or under the condition of mating/filamentation. Sexual reproduction assay demonstrated that the SsCYP86 deletion mutant was defective in mating/filamentation and significantly reduced its pathogenicity. Further fatty acid metabolomic analysis unravelled the levels of fatty acid metabolites were reduced in the SsCYP86 deletion mutant. Exogenous addition of fatty acid metabolites cis-11-eicosenoic acid (C20:1N9), pentadecanoic acid (C15:0), and linolenic acid (C18:3N3) partially restored the mating/filamentation ability of the SsCYP86 deletion mutant and restored the transcriptional level of the SsPRF1, a pheromone response transcription factor that is typically down-regulated in the absence of SsCYP86. Moreover, the constitutive expression of SsPRF1 in the SsCYP86 deletion mutant restored its mating/filamentation.ConclusionOur results indicated that SsCyp86 modulates the SsPRF1 transcription by fatty acid metabolism, and thereby regulate the sexual reproduction of S. scitamineum. These findings provide insights into how CYPs regulate sexual reproduction in S. scitamineum.KEYWORDS: Sporisorium scitamineum, fatty acid metabolism, cytochrome P450 enzyme SsCyp86, mating/filamentation, pheromone response transcription factor SsPrf1

Introduction

Sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum) is a crucial global source of sugar and a sustainable bioenergy source, contributing for about 85% of the world’s sugar supply [1]. However, the sustainability of sugarcane production and the broader sugar industry is significantly threatened by S. scitamineum, a pathogenic fungus responsible for sugarcane smut [2–4]. It has been well established that the formation of dikaryotic mycelium, an important stage during the sexual reproduction between haploid cells of different genetic mating types leads to play a pivotal role in the fungal infection and subsequent pathogenesis [5,6]. Deletion of the pheromone response transcription factor 1 encoding gene (SsPRF1) led to the loss of mating/filamentation ability and pathogenicity of S. scitamineum [7], thus indicating its pivotal regulatory function in sexual reproduction and virulence. Our previous research found that the transcription of SsPRF1 is subject to modulation by both intracellular ROS levels and the phosphate status of SsHog1 [8,9], implying that SsPRF1 can be transcriptionally activated by certain endogenous factors.

In the process of interaction between pathogens and plants, the plant wax as a natural physical barrier protect the plant from pathogen invasion and modulate the activity and growth of these pathogens [10,11]. On the other hand, pathogens could also utilize plant-derived substances to promote the growth and infection. Recent research found that the waxy components on the surface of sugarcane seedlings, specifically 26-carbon alcohol and 28-carbon alcohol, were utilized by S. scitamineum to promote the germination of teliospores [12]. However, the mechanism that S. scitamineum utilizes these wax components for teliospore germination is unclear.

Fatty acid metabolism plays an important role in maintaining the normal physiological function of microorganisms. For instance, the transcriptional factor FdmR of Mycobacterium marinum serves as a crucial regulator of fatty acid catabolism, regulating the expression of multiple genes involved in fatty acid modification and degradation, ultimately enhancing antibiotic tolerance [13]. Furthermore, fatty acid metabolites can serve as important secondary messengers in terms of signaling pathways and lipid homeostasis [14,15]. Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes are a class of monooxygenases that typically employ the heme porphyrin iron as a cofactor to catalyze the oxidative metabolism of fatty acids [16–18]. CYP450 enzymes are widely distributed in living organisms, of which CYP86 family members are P450-dependent fatty acid-ω-hydroxylases that play crucial roles in catalyzing the hydroxylation of various fatty acids [19,20]. It has been reported that CYP86 plays an important role in sexual reproduction and immune system activation in plants [21,22]. Nevertheless, the mechanism of actions of CYP86 enzymes in plant pathogenic fungi remains elusive.

In this study, we identified a CYP450 enzyme-encoding gene, SsCYP86, which was induced by sugarcane wax in S. scitamineum, indicating that SsCyp86 responds to sugarcane wax signal. Furthermore, transcriptional induction of SsCYP86 was observed during mating/filamentation, and the SsCYP86 deletion mutant displayed significantly reduced SsPRF1 transcription, mating/filamentation, pathogenicity, and H2O2 tolerance. By conducting comparative analysis of fatty acid metabolism, we observed a significant reduction in the intracellular content of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids in the SsCYP86 deletion mutant. Further results indicated that the exogenous addition of fatty acid metabolites (C20:1N9, C15:0, and C18:3N3) partially restored the mating/filamentation of the SsCYP86 deletion mutant and also restored the transcriptional level of the SsPRF1. In short, our results indicated that SsCyp86 mediated the transcriptional of SsPRF1 by regulating fatty acid metabolism, thereby influencing the mating/filamentation of S. scitamineum, providing a better understanding of CYP450 enzymes in plant pathogen infection and pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Domain prediction and phylogenetic analysis

The amino acid sequence of the SsCyp86 protein was obtained from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/; Sequence ID: CDS82158.1), and the SsCyp86 protein domains were predicted using the SmartBLAST tool (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/smartblast/). The distribution map of the SsCyp86 protein domains were drawn using IBS 1.0.1 software. Phylogenetic analysis of the Cyp86 protein from various basidiomycetous and ascomycetous species was performed using Maximum likelihood in MEGA 7. Bootstrap percentages out of 1,000 replicates are shown on the branches.

Growth conditions and construction of strains

The wild-type S. scitamineum (MAT-1 and MAT-2) was isolated and identified by Yan [23] and maintained in our lab. Haploid sporidia were grown in YePS medium, as described previously [24]. A small amount of S. scitamineum haploid sporidia was removed from the plate and transferred to 5 mL of sterile YePS (Yeast extraction-Peptone-Sucrose; pH 6.5; containing 100 μg/mL of Ampicillin), and then incubated at 200 rpm and 28°C overnight to obtain the seed solution.

To knockout the SsCYP86, we used PEG-mediated protoplast homologous recombination technology. Protoplasmic cells were obtained using 10 mg/mL of Lysing enzymes (Sigma, Louis, MO, USA), as described previously [25]. Initially, we used the SsCYP86-LB-F/SsCYP86-LB-R primer to amplify the DNA sequence located 1.5 kb upstream of the SsCYP86 and designated it as the left homologous arm (LB). Subsequently, we fused the left homologous arm with the truncated HYGR-up sequence of the Hygromycin resistance (HYGR) gene. Similarly, we used the SsCYP86-RB-F/SsCYP86-RB-R primer to amplify the DNA sequence from the 1.5 kb downstream region of the SsCYP86 and named it the right homologous arm (RB). This arm was then fused with another truncated HYGR-down sequence of the HYGR gene. After completing these steps, we introduced the two truncated fusion DNA fragments of the HYGR gene into the protoplast cells of the wild-type MAT-1 and MAT-2 of S. scitamineum. The transformants were selected by media amended with 200 μg/mL of Hygromycin B (Merck, Saint Louis, MO, USA). We used SsCYP86-in-F/SsCYP86-in-R and SsCYP86-out-F/SsCYP86-out-R primers for PCR amplification and qRT-SsCYP86-F/qRT-SsCYP86-R primer for qRT-PCR assays to verify correct integration. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

For reintegration of the SsCYP86, we used the SsCYP86-com-F/SsCYP86-LB-R primer to amplify the DNA sequence (including 1.3 kb of the SsCYP86 open reading frame and the complete SsCYP86), using genomic DNA as a template. The resulting PCR product was seamlessly integrated into the pEASY-COM vector using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), as described previously [9], ultimately yielding the plasmid pEASY-COM-SsCYP86. Subsequently, we amplified two DNA fragments, labeled as COM-SsCYP86-LB and COM-SsCYP86-RB, both containing truncated Zeocin resistance gene (ZEOR), using the primers COM-LB-F/COM-LB-R and COM-RB-F/COM-RB-R with the correct pEASY-COM-SsCYP86 plasmid as a template. Finally, we introduced these two DNA fragments into the sscyp86Δ-1 and sscyp86Δ-2 mutant cells using the PEG-mediated protoplast transformation method, successfully generating the reintegration strains sscyp86Δ/CYP86-1 and sscyp86Δ/CYP86-2. The transformants were selected by media amended with 100 μg/mL of Zeocin (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). We used the SsCYP86-in-F/SsCYP86-in-R primers for PCR amplification and the qRT-PCR assays with the qRT-SsCYP86-F/qRT-SsCYP86-R primers to identify the correct transformants. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

For the enhanced constitutive expression of SsPRF1, we used the SsPRF1-con-F/ SsPRF1-con-R primer to amplify the DNA sequence (encoded sequence of SsPRF1) by PCR from cDNA. The resulting PCR product was seamlessly integrated into the pEASY-OE vector using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), ultimately yielding the plasmid pEASY-OE-SsPRF1, as described previously [8]. Subsequently, we amplified two DNA fragments, labeled as con-SsPRF1-LB and con-SsPRF1-RB, both containing truncated Zeocin resistance gene (ZEOR), using the primers con-LB-F/con-LB-R and con-RB-F/con-RB-R with the correct pEASY-COM- SsPRF1 plasmid as a template. Finally, we introduced these two DNA fragments into the sscyp86Δ-1 and sscyp86Δ-2 mutant cells using the PEG-mediated protoplast transformation method, successfully generating the enhanced constitutive expression strains sscyp86Δ/con-PRF1-1 and sscyp86Δ/con-PRF1-2. The transformants were selected by media amended with 100 μg/mL of Zeocin (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). We used the qRT-PCR assays with the qRT-SsPRF1-F/qRT-SsPRF1-R primers to identify the correct transformants. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Induction analysis of sugarcane wax and fatty acid metabolites to S. scitamineum

The sugarcane wax was extracted from the ROC22 cultivar, as previously described [12]. The wild-type MAT-1 × MAT-2 strain was cultivated on YePS agar, with or without the addition 1.0 μg/mL sugarcane wax, at 28°C for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were gathered for total RNA extraction. For fatty acid metabolites treatments, the fresh haploid sporidia of MAT-1 were grown on YePS agar, with or without the inclusion of 1.0 mM C20:1N9, C15:0, C18:3N3, or a combination of C20:1N9-C15:0-C18:3N3. These plates were incubated at 28°C for 42 h, followed by the extraction of total RNA. Additionally, the wild-type (MAT-1 × MAT-2) and the SsCYP86 deletion mutants (sscyp86Δ-1 × sscyp86Δ-2) was grown on MM agar containing 1.0 mM C20:1N9-C15:0-C18:3N3 at 28°C for 42 h. Following this incubation, the cells were collected for total RNA extraction.

Extraction of total DNA and RNA

The genomic DNA of S. scitamineum was extracted using a SDS-based DNA extraction method, previously described [26,27]. The fresh haploid sporidia of S. scitamineum were allowed to grow in YePS agar at 28°C for 24 h, and then the cells were collected for total DNA extraction. For total RNA extraction, the fresh haploid sporidia of S. scitamineum were grown on YePS agar at 28°C for 24–60 h and the cells were collected by scraped off or centrifugation. Subsequently, total RNA was extracted with the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA), following manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative real time-pcr (qRT-pcr) assay

The qRT-PCR primers were design using the PrimerQuest™ Tool (https://sg.idtdna.com/pages/tools/primerquest?returnurl=%2Fprimerquest%2FHome%2FIndex). The GC content of the primers is between 45% and 55%, and the Tm value is about 55–65°C. The amplification efficiency was calculated by standard curve method. When the amplification efficiency of primers falls within the range of 90% to 110%, it is deemed satisfactory. Total RNA was utilized for cDNA synthesis using the HiScript® II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). Subsequently, qRT-PCR analysis was conducted on the QuantStudio 6 Flex (ThermoFisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) using specific primer pairs for each gene. The Melt Curve Plot is unimodal and usually Tm > 80°C, the amplification specificity is considered to be good. The relative expression levels of the genes were determined using the -ΔΔCt method, as previously described [28], using the cytoskeleton-encoding gene ACTIN as internal reference [29]. The experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates and two technical replicates each.

Analysis of H2O2 tolerance

The haploid sporidia seed solution, which had been prepared previously, was cultured in YePS solution at 200 rpm and a temperature of 28°C overnight, until an OD600 of 1.0 was reached. Subsequently, the sporidia underwent three consecutive rounds of 10-fold dilution. Then, 1.5 μL of the resulting cells suspension was spotted onto YePS agar with or without 1.8 mM H2O2. Growth was evaluated and recorded photographically after 3 to 4 days. The experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates and two technical replicates each.

Mating/Filamentous growth assay

The mating/filamentation procedure for S. scitamineum was executed as outlined in a previous study [29]. The haploid sporidia seed solution was cultured in YePS solution at 200 rpm and 28°C overnight and then adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0 with sterilized ddH2O. Subsequently, haploid strains of opposite mating types were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and spotted onto YePS agar or minimal medium (MM) supplemented with fatty acid metabolites. Finally, the resulting plates were then incubated at 28°C for 24–48 h. This experiment was replicated three times independently, with two replicates for each repeat.

Fatty acid metabolome analysis

To conduct a targeted metabolomic analysis of medium- and long-chain fatty acids utilizing GC-MS, we first cultured the MAT-1 and sscyp86Δ-1 strains overnight in YePS solution at 200 rpm and 28°C. We then centrifuged the cultures, washed the collected cells once with sterilized ddH2O, resuspended the cells, and diluted the suspension to achieve an OD600 of 1.0. Equal volumes of the resulting cell suspension were then evenly distributed onto YePS agar. The plates were incubated at 28°C for 42 h, and then the yeast colonies of S. scitamineum were scraped off and sent to Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. for comprehensive metabolomic analysis.

To prepare the sample for analysis, 60 mg of the thawed sample was transferred into a 2 mL glass centrifuge tube. To this, 1 mL of chloroform-methanol solution was added, and the mixture was ultrasonicated for 30 min. The supernatant was collected, and 2 mL of 1% sulfuric acid-methanol solution was added. The mixture was then incubated in an 80°C water bath for 30 min to facilitate methylation. Following this, 1 mL of n-hexane was used for extraction, and the mixture was washed with 5 mL of pure water. From the resulting supernatant, 500 μL was taken and spiked with 25 μL of methyl nonadecanoate as an internal standard. The sample was then thoroughly mixed.

Gas chromatography analysis was performed using an Agilent DB-WAX capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.25 μm). The temperature program was initiated at 50°C, maintained for 3 min, and then raised to 220°C at a rate of 10°C/min, holding at this temperature for an additional 5 min. Helium was used as the carrier gas, with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. To ensure system stability and reproducibility, QC samples were periodically introduced into the sample queue. For mass spectrometry analysis, an Agilent 7890/5975C GC-MS instrument was utilized. The inlet temperature was set at 280°C, the ion source temperature at 230°C, and the transfer line temperature at 250°C. Electron impact ionization (EI) was used with SIM scanning mode and an electron energy of 70 eV.

Chromatographic peak areas and retention times were extracted using MSD ChemStation software. Standard calibration curves were constructed, and the contents of medium- and long-chain fatty acids in the samples were accurately determined.

Pathogenicity analysis of S. scitamineum

The haploid sporidia seed solution was cultured in YePS solution at 200 rpm and 28°C overnight, and then adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0 using sterilized ddH2O. Subsequently, haploid strains of opposite mating types were mixed in a 1:1 ratio, and 0.2 mL of the resulting cell suspension was used to inoculate the susceptible sugarcane cultivar ROC22 (at the 5/6-leaf stage) via injection. To ensure reproducibility, three biological replicates were done per experiment with each replicate involving the inoculation of at least 10 plants. The inoculated plants were grown in a greenhouse with a natural summer day-night cycle for 180 days and ambient temperatures. The plants were examined and watered weekly, and when symptoms appeared, they were photographed and disease was assessed by looking at the proportion of “black whip” plants out of total seedlings.

Statistical analysis

To assess statistical significance, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Bonferroni’s multiple-comparison test to separate means. Results were considered significant if the p value was < 0.05.

Results

A sugarcane wax-induced expression gene SsCYP86 is identified in S. scitamineum

In Arabidopsis thaliana, the CYP450 enzyme is involved in the synthesis of cuticular wax [30,31]. Therefore, we tested sugarcane wax on some CYP450 enzyme-coding genes (CDS82158.1, CDR88137.1, and CDR98946.1) expression by qRT-PCR. The results showed that sugarcane wax did not affect the transcription of CDR88137.1 and CDR98946.1, but significantly induced the transcription of CDS82158.1 (Figure 1(a)). Then, we compared the protein sequence encoded by CDS82158.1 through NCBI BLASTp and found that it belonged to cytochrome P450 family 86, and CDS82158.1 was named SsCYP86. Domain analysis suggested the SsCyp86 protein was composed of two low complexity regions (LCRs) and a conserved p450 domain (Figure 1(b)), which may catalyze the oxidation of organic species by molecular oxygen. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted for the SsCyp86 protein and its orthologs from various basidiomycetous and ascomycetous species. The results suggested that SsCyp86 was highly conserved among its orthologs in the Ustilaginomycete (smut fungi) clade, while it was distantly related to ascomycetous species (Figure 1(c)). These results indicate that SsCyp86 was a conserved CYP450 enzyme.

SsCyp86 positively modulates S. scitamineum mating/filamentation

Mating/filamentation as part of sexual reproduction is critical to the pathogenicity of S. scitamineum since infection only occurs with hyphae formed by the dikaryon [29,32]. We hence measured the transcriptional profile of SsCYP86 under mating/filamentation (dikaryon formation) in S. scitamineum, and the results showed that SsCYP86 was induced during mating/filamentation, with peak expression at 36 h (Figure 2(a)). We generated SsCYP86 deletion mutants (sscyp86Δ-1 and sscyp86Δ-2) and genetic reintegration strains (sscyp86Δ/CYP86-1 and sscyp86Δ/CYP86-2) using a homologous recombination method, which were validated by PCR amplification and qRT-PCR analysis (Figure S1). Detailed information of the wild-type, the SsCYP86 deletion mutant, and the SsCYP86 reintegration strain used in this study is provided in Table S1.

The plates were incubated at 28°C for 60 h and photographed.

Subsequently, we analyzed the mating/filamentation of the SsCYP86 deletion mutant on YePS agar. As expected, the mixture of sporidia of sscyp86Δ-1 × sscyp86Δ-2 showed significant defects in the dikaryotic hyphae and failed to form white colonies after 48 h of incubation, compared to the mixture of sporidia of MAT-1 × MAT-2 or sscyp86Δ/CYP86-1 × sscyp86Δ/CYP86-2 (Figure 2(b)). To further explore how SsCyp86 regulates the mating/filamentation of S. scitamineum, we assessed the transcriptional levels of genes involved in the regulation of mating/filamentation. While the results showed that the transcriptional levels of the core component genes of the cAMP/PKA pathway (SsGPA3, SsUAC1, and SsADR1) were comparable between the sscyp86Δ-1 strain and the wild-type MAT-1 (Figure S2(a)). However, the transcriptional levels of the pheromone response factor SsPRF1 and its downstream mating type genes (SsMFA1, SsPRA1, SsbE, and SsbW) were significantly decreased in sscyp86Δ-1 mutants (Figure 2(c)), indicating that SsCyp86 mediated the transcriptional of SsPRF1 to regulate S. scitamineum mating/filamentation. To validate the hypothesis that the defective mating/filamentation observed in the SsCYP86 deletion mutant was attributable to the inadequate expression of SsPRF1, we generated strains constitutively expressing SsPRF1 in both the sscyp86Δ-1 and sscyp86Δ-2 strains, utilizing the GPA promoter. The constitutive expression strains (sscyp86Δ/con-PRF1-1 and sscyp86Δ/con-PRF1-2) were confirmed and characterized by qRT-PCR analysis (Figure S2(b)), and their mating/filamentation was observed on YePS agar. The results showed that the mating/filamentation of sscyp86Δ/con-PRF1-1 × sscyp86Δ/con-PRF1-2 was significantly restored on YePS agar (Figure 2(d)). Taken together, these results indicated that SsCyp86 positively regulated mating/filamentation of S. scitamineum by mediating SsPRF1 transcription.

SsCyp86 is indispensable for fatty acid metabolism and H2O2 tolerance of S. scitamineum

To evaluate the roles of the SsCyp86 protein in fatty acid metabolism, we examined the lipid metabolism in MAT-1 and sscyp86Δ-1 strains. The results showed that the levels of total saturated, total monounsaturated, and total polyunsaturated fatty acids in the sscyp86Δ-1 mutant were significantly lower than that of the wild-type MAT-1 (Figure 3(a)). Further functional enrichment of differential fatty acid metabolites showed that these were primarily associated with fatty acid synthesis and mid-long chain fatty acid β-oxidation pathways (Figure 3(b)). Furthermore, the heatmap depicting the levels of fatty acid metabolites revealed a substantial reduction of these in the SsCYP86 deletion mutant, primarily within the C14-C25 range (Figure 3(c)). It was also observed that an increase in fatty acid metabolites that was mainly concentrated in the C4-C14 range (Figure 3(c)). A comprehensive analysis of these differential fatty acid metabolites highlighted a significant decrease in 20 metabolites in the sscyp86Δ-1 mutant, with only 6 metabolites exhibiting an increase (Figure S3). In short, our data demonstrated that SsCyp86 was crucial for fatty acid metabolism in S. scitamineum.

Fatty acid metabolism is essential for cell membrane homeostasis and plays an important role in protecting cells from oxidative stress [33–35]. Given that the loss of SsCyp86 disrupted the metabolism of fatty acids, we tested the tolerance of MAT-1, sscyp86Δ-1, and sscyp86Δ/CYP86-1 strains to H2O2. The results showed that there were no differences in the growth of MAT-1, sscyp86Δ-1, and sscyp86Δ/CYP86-1 strains on YePS agar. However, compared with wild-type MAT-1, the growth of sscyp86Δ-1 mutant on YePS agar containing 1.8 mM H2O2 was severely inhibited (Figure S4). Notably, the growth pattern of the scyp86Δ/CYP86-1 strain under oxidative conditions closely resembled that of the wild-type MAT-1 strain (Figure S4), which indicated that SsCyp86 was an essential antioxidant factor for S. scitamineum to participate in antioxidant stress.

SsCyp86 mediates fatty acid metabolism to regulate mating/filamentation of S. scitamineum

To further investigate the impact of fatty acid metabolism on mating/filamentation of S. scitamineum, we conducted an assessment of the mating/filamentation capabilities of SsCYP86 deletion mutants in the presence of exogenous cis-11-eicosenoic acid (C20:1N9), pentadecanoic acid (C15:0), linolenic acid (C18:3N3), stearic acid (C18:0), tetracosanoic acid (C24:0), tricosanoic acid (C23:0), behenic acid (C22:0), arachidic acid (C20:0), and heptadecanoic acid (C17:0). The results revealed that cis-11-eicosenoic acid (C20:1N9), pentadecanoic acid (C15:0), and linolenic acid (C18:3N3) significantly restored the mating/filamentation in SsCYP86 deletion mutants (Figure 4(a) and S5). Notably, the combined effect of these three metabolites was even more pronounced (Figure 4(a)), indicating a synergistic interaction between them. Additionally, we found that the transcriptional level of SsCYP86 could be induced by cis-11-eicosenoic acid (C20:1N9), pentadecanoic acid (C15:0) and linolenic acid (C18:3N3) to different degrees (Figure 4(b)). Furthermore, these three metabolites also restored the transcriptional level of SsPRF1 in SsCYP86 deletion mutants (sscyp86Δ-1 × sscyp86Δ-2) (Figure 4(c)). Collectively, these data suggested that SsCyp86 mediated fatty acid metabolism to regulate the mating/filamentation in S. scitamineum.

SsCyp86 is essential for pathogenicity of S. scitamineum

Given that SsCyp86 was important for fatty acid metabolism and mating/filamentation of S. scitamineum, we examined if it might also be involved in pathogenicity. The pathogenicity assay was conducted with the wild-type (MAT-1 × MAT-2), SsCYP86 deletion mutants (sscyp86Δ-1 × sscyp86Δ-2), and SsCYP86 reintegration strains (sscyp86Δ/CYP86-1 × sscyp86Δ/CYP86-2). Inoculation results showed that both the wild-type (MAT-1 × MAT-2) and SsCYP86 reintegration strains (sscyp86Δ/CYP86-1 × sscyp86Δ/CYP86-2) were capable of inducing sugarcane seedlings to exhibit the typical “black whip” symptoms (Figure 5(a)), with no detectable differences in disease incidence between the two at around 60–75% (Figure 5(b)). However, compared to the wild-type (MAT-1 × MAT-2), the SsCYP86 deletion mutant (sscyp86Δ-1 × sscyp86Δ-2) showed a significant reduction in the typical whip symptoms in sugarcane seedlings, with only 7.6% disease incidence. Discussion

CYP450 enzymes are widely distributed in plants, bacteria, fungi, and animals [36], playing a crucial role in the oxidative stress response of organisms [37,38]. Our previous research indicated that the MAP kinase Hog1 of S. scitamineum mediates oxidative stress responses through the CYP450 pathway [39]. In this study, we reported that the deletion of CYP450 enzyme SsCyp86 resulted in decreased tolerance to H2O2, suggesting that SsCyp86 is also involved in the oxidative stress response of S. scitamineum. Furthermore, comparative analysis revealed that the differences in fatty acid metabolites between the SsCyp86 deletion mutant and the wild-type MAT-1 were primarily enriched in the β-oxidation pathway of mid- and long-chain fatty acids, indicating that SsCyp86 plays an important role in intracellular oxidative reactions. In Panax ginseng, the CYP86 family gene PgCYP704B1 plays an important role in male reproductive processes by regulating lipid biosynthesis, and its overexpression results in increased cutin monomers [21]. Our finding that the SsCyp86 deletion mutant exhibits significantly reduced mating/filamentation and transcriptional levels of SsPRF1 and mating type genes, indicated that SsCyp86 also participates in the regulation of sexual reproduction in S. scitamineum. Additionally, our findings suggest that SsCyp86 mediates the transcriptional of SsPRF1 by regulating fatty acid metabolism, thus regulating the sexual reproduction of S. scitamineum. However, the precise mechanism by which fatty acid metabolites affect SsPRF1 transcription requires further investigation.

Fatty acids serve as a crucial carbon source obtained by pathogenic fungi from the host [40,41]. Simultaneously, fatty acid metabolites can be used as fuel sources for energy production to maintain cell survival, and serve as important secondary messengers to regulate cell physiological activities [14,15]. However, fatty acid metabolism, which involves complex biochemical enzymatic reactions, is essential for living organisms. The CYP450 enzyme superfamily plays a pivotal role in this process [16,19]. The CYP86 subfamily, among the numerous CYP450 families, primarily catalyzes the hydroxylation of acyl-CoAs at the ω-site, generating ω-hydroxyacids [42]. In A. thaliana, AtCYP86A2, AtCYP86A4, and AtCYP86A8 are three highly homologous genes that contribute significantly to the hydroxylation of long-chain fatty acids [22]. In addition, the deletion of the AtCYP86A1 in A. thaliana has been shown to significantly reduce the content of C16 and C18 monolignols, leading to a 60% decrease in the total aliphatic suberin content [43]. Silencing of StCYP86A1 in potato resulted in a 70–90% reduction in ω-hydroxyacids, α- and ω-dicarboxylic acids of C16-C18 fatty acids [44]. Similarly, we demonstrated that deletion of the SsCYP86 leads to a significant reduction in the intracellular content of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids, indicating that SsCyp86 is essential for fatty acid metabolism in S. scitamineum. Notably, the majority of these decreased fatty acid metabolites were C12-C18 saturated and unsaturated fatty acids (such as pentadecanoic acid (C15:0), linolenic acid (C18:3N3), stearic acid (C18:0), heptadecanoic acid (C17:0), and tetradecanoic acid (C14:0), consistent with previous findings that CYP86 preferentially acts on saturated and unsaturated fatty acids with chain lengths of C12-C18 [20].

The natural barrier posed by plant waxes can hinder the invasion of pathogens or impact their activity and growth [10,11]. During the interaction between the pathogen and the host plant, the pathogen may evolve a mechanism to utilize wax components to facilitate invasion. For instance, S. scitamineum utilizes plant wax-derived alkanol to induce the germination of teliospores [12]. In this study, we found that sugarcane wax can induce the expression of SsCYP86. This result furhter supports previous results that CYP450 enzyme-coding genes can be transcriptionally activated by a range of endogenous and exogenous substrates via receptor-dependent mechanisms, thereby playing a crucial role in cell metabolism [36,45]. However, further investigation is needed to elucidate which specific components of sugarcane wax trigger the expression of SsCYP86. Furthermore, our findings indicated that the SsCYP86 maintains a high transcriptional level during the formation of infectious dikaryotic hyphae, and the virulence of the SsCYP86 deletion mutant were significantly reduced, confirming that SsCyp86 regulates the pathogenicity of S. scitamineum. Conversely, CYP86 plays an important role in plant defense against pathogen infection. For example, suppressing the expression of GbCYP86A1-1 in Gossypium barbadense leads to decreased resistance against Verticillium dahliae invasion or overexpression of GbCYP86A1-1 activates defense immune pathways [22].

Conclusion

In summary, this study uncovered a crucial role of SsCyp86 in regulating fatty acid metabolism, oxidative stress, sexual reproduction, and pathogenicity in S. scitamineum. Furthermore, SsCyp86 mediates the transcription of SsPRF1 by regulating fatty acid metabolism, thereby affecting sexual reproduction in S. scitamineum. These findings provide another layer of insight into the regulation of sexual reproduction in plant pathogenic fungi by CYP450 enzymes and lay a theoretical foundation for the development of innovative disease control strategies.
