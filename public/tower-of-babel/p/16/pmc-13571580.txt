Sex Disparities in In-Hospital Outcomes After Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Mitral Valve Repair: A National Inpatient Sample Analysis, 2016–2022

Transcatheter edge-to-edge mitral valve repair (TEER) has expanded rapidly in US inpatient practice since the Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip Percutaneous Therapy for Heart Failure Patients With Functional Mitral Regurgitation (COAPT) trial.1 Trial substudies2 and earlier registry analyses3 have suggested differential outcomes for women, although contemporary national-scale evidence on sex-specific outcomes through 2022 remains limited. Using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) 2016–2022, we characterized contemporary sex disparities in in-hospital TEER outcomes with explicit attention to the post-2012 NIS data structure and the often-overlooked distinction between in-hospital pacemaker placement and in-hospital implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) or cardiac resynchronization-defibrillator (CRT-D) placement.

A Broader, Older US TEER Population, 2016–2022

We identified TEER hospitalizations using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision Procedure Coding System codes 02UG3JZ and 02UG3JE under Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project survey design (DISCWT probability weight, NIS_STRATUM, HOSP_NIS PSU). Comorbidities were derived from all 40 diagnosis slots, and diagnosis-coded outcomes were restricted to secondary slots (DX2–40) to enrich for in-hospital events, as NIS lacks a present-on-admission flag.4 Procedure-coded outcomes (pericardiocentesis, transfusion, pacemaker, ICD/CRT-D, surgical conversion, and mechanical ventilation) occurred during the index admission. Survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age, race/ethnicity, payer, zip-income quartile, hospital region/size/teaching status, year, elective status, sampled hospital annual TEER-volume tertile, and 18 comorbidities. All covariates were specified a priori; collinearity was minimal (mean variance inflation factor 1.74), and complete code lists, the major-bleeding composite definition, events-per-variable for each outcome, and robustness analyses for low-frequency outcomes are provided in the Supplementary Material. An etiology-proxy sensitivity (heart failure or cardiomyopathy co-occurrence as a marker of secondary mitral regurgitation), complete-case race, and elective-only strata were prespecified.

Among 64,905 weighted TEER hospitalizations (12,981 unweighted), women comprised 45.8% overall, and the weighted volume tripled from approximately 4365 in 2016 to approximately 13,405 in 2022. Women were older than men (77.3 vs. 76.3 years) and had lower ischemic disease burden (coronary artery disease 52.4% vs. 70.5%, prior coronary artery bypass grafting 9.8% vs. 24.6%) and lower prevalence of the functional mitral regurgitation proxy (43.7% vs. 53.1%). Women’s share of TEER recipients declined from 50.2% (2016) to 44.8% (2022) (per-year odds ratio 0.975, p = 0.015) even as overall volume tripled, a pattern that may reflect indication expansion into functional mitral regurgitation, differential referral, or both.

Bleeding, Disposition, and Device-Placement Disparities Despite Matched In-Hospital Mortality

In-hospital mortality was 1.86% overall (women 1.98%, men 1.75%), with no sex difference after adjustment (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.96; 95% CI 0.71–1.30; E-value 1.25). Women, however, experienced significantly higher rates of cardiac tamponade (aOR 2.05, 1.08–3.87), major bleeding (1.23, 1.05–1.46), red blood cell transfusion (1.31, 1.06–1.63), and nonhome discharge (1.40, 1.26–1.55, p < 0.001). Major bleeding was a prespecified secondary-diagnosis composite of intracranial, gastrointestinal, intrathoracic, intrapericardial, and retroperitoneal hemorrhage and acute blood-loss anemia (Supplementary Material), distinct from the separately coded vascular-access complications. The bleeding/tamponade cluster aligns with sex-disaggregated structural heart literature documenting higher access-related complication rates in women.5 Smaller vessel caliber, greater anticoagulation exposure relative to body surface area, and transseptal access geometry are plausible contributors, but NIS contains none of these variables, so they remain hypotheses rather than tested mediators. Adjusted length of stay did not differ (incidence rate ratio 1.04, 0.98–1.10), and estimated cost was modestly lower in women (adjusted cost ratio 0.96, 0.94–0.99). The 40% relative excess in nonhome discharge persisted after volume-tertile adjustment and may reflect older age at procedure, differences in social support, and downstream care-planning gaps, warranting attention despite matched in-hospital mortality.

We separated pacemaker placement (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision Procedure Coding System 0JH device codes 4–7) from ICD/CRT-D placement (device codes 8–9) and validated each procedure code in our cohort. Women had a significantly lower rate of in-hospital ICD or CRT-D placement (aOR 0.47, 0.25–0.89) but no significant difference in in-hospital pacemaker placement (aOR 1.36, 0.69–2.71). These procedure codes identify in-hospital generator implantation but cannot distinguish de novo from replacement implants (planned from complication-driven procedures) or capture ejection fraction, QRS duration, or device eligibility; lower rate in women is therefore hypothesis generating. For the lowest-frequency outcomes (tamponade, surgical conversion, and ICD/CRT-D placement), effect estimates were materially unchanged under parsimonious and penalized-likelihood (Firth) models (Supplementary Material), indicating that the associations are not artifacts of sparse-data instability. In-hospital stroke, acute myocardial infarction, vascular complication, and surgical conversion (DX2–40) did not differ by sex (Figure 1). The year-by-sex interaction in the adjusted mortality model was nonsignificant (p = 0.46), and prespecified sensitivity analyses (etiology-proxy strata, complete-case race, elective-only admissions, and any-slot vs. secondary-slot diagnostic definitions) yielded findings consistent with the primary null mortality conclusion.

Methodological Considerations and Unresolved Questions

Our findings extend the prior 2012–2016 NIS sex-disparities analysis3 through 2022 and incorporate a temporality-aware outcome-coding framework that addresses the post-2012 absence of present-on-admission flags. NIS administrative coding lacks left ventricular ejection fraction, mitral regurgitation mechanism and severity, pulmonary and right ventricular hemodynamics, New York Heart Association functional class, frailty indices, and procedural detail (device generation, clip number, residual regurgitation, transmitral gradient, and transseptal and access site characteristics). Mitral regurgitation etiology was inferred by proxy. Although imprecise, the bleeding, tamponade, and nonhome-discharge disparities were directionally consistent across functional- and degenerative-proxy strata (Supplementary Material). Sex was a binary administrative variable rather than self-reported gender identity, with potential misclassification of transgender or intersex individuals. Race/ethnicity was hospital assigned and treated as a social construct rather than a biologic variable. Secondary-slot definitions captured >0.98 of all-slot events for most diagnoses, with acute myocardial infarction as the notable exception, suggesting that some patients admitted primarily for acute coronary syndromes received TEER during the same hospitalization. Without a present-on-admission flag, on-admission events may be misclassified as in-hospital complications, particularly for stroke, acute myocardial infarction, and vascular complication. Operator-level volume could not be modeled, and post-2012 NIS hospital-volume tertiles reflect sampled rather than true national counts4; both contribute residual confounding. Mortality was ascertained only during the index hospitalization. Sex differences that emerge across 30-day, 1-year, or longer follow-up—when readmission, heart failure events, and device-related complications accrue—cannot be excluded and may carry greater prognostic weight than the matched in-hospital mortality observed here. Despite these limitations, the bleeding/tamponade cluster, nonhome-discharge gap, and differential in-hospital ICD/CRT-D placement are coherent signals that warrant prospective characterization with self-reported demographics, etiology adjudication, and operator-level granularity.

Ethics Statement

This study used the publicly available, de-identified Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project National Inpatient Sample and was therefore exempt from institutional review board review; informed consent was not required because the dataset contains no identifiable private information.
