Global burden of gout attributable to high BMI (1990–2021) and causal link with obesity: a GBD and Mendelian randomization study

Published: 2026

Background

Over the past few decades, overweight and obesity have emerged as a global epidemic, posing a major challenge to public health systems around the world. 1 Driven by changes in nutritional transitions and lifestyle shifts, the prevalence of high body mass index (BMI) has continued to rise worldwide. 2 Notably, this health crisis is no longer restricted to high-income countries; the burden of high BMI-related diseases is escalating rapidly in many low- and middle-income regions undergoing economic development and urbanization. 3 High BMI directly or indirectly contributes to the development of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and various malignant tumors by inducing chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and endocrine dysregulation.4,5

Gout is a metabolic rheumatic disease characterized by the deposition of monosodium urate crystals in the joints and periarticular tissues, representing the most common form of inflammatory arthritis worldwide. 6 The latest data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study revealed that the disease burden of gout is escalating at an unprecedented rate. Over the last three decades, both the global prevalence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of gout have exhibited a continuous upward trend with no obvious signs of decline.7,8 High BMI (BMI ⩾25 kg/m2) has been recognized as a pivotal modifiable risk factor driving this epidemic. Obesity not only directly induces hyperuricemia by stimulating endogenous uric acid production and impairing renal excretion but also establishes a vicious cycle with gout through the exacerbation of insulin resistance and the modulation of inflammatory pathways.9,10

The GBD database provides a comprehensive, standardized assessment of disease burden across 204 countries and territories. It offers standardized estimates for over 300 diseases and injuries and more than 80 risk factors, encompassing metrics such as incidence, prevalence, mortality, years lived with disability, years of life lost, and DALYs. 11 These standardized data effectively eliminate methodological heterogeneity across national statistics, making it possible to rigorously analyze the temporal and spatial trends of disease burden caused by high BMI. Consequently, this study uses GBD 2021 data to evaluate the global burden of high BMI-related gout and to project future disease trajectories, aiming to provide a scientific basis for obesity prevention, healthcare resource allocation, and the formulation of public health policies. Furthermore, we conducted Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to investigate the causal relationship between obesity grades and gout, specifically elucidating the magnitude of this association across different obesity levels.

Methods

Data source

The data used in this study were sourced from the GBD 2021 database (https://gbd2021.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). This study extracted disease burden data for high BMI-related gout from 1990 to 2021 at the global level, for 204 countries and territories, 21 regions, and 5 socio-demographic index (SDI) regions, among the population aged 20–95+ years, with projections extending to 2040. To address potential underdiagnosis and handle sparse data in low-SDI regions, the GBD estimation framework utilizes DisMod-MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta-regression tool, and spatio-temporal Gaussian process regression to reliably impute and smooth estimates by borrowing strength from spatial and predictive covariates. 11 The extracted metrics included DALYs and age-standardized DALY rates for gout attributable to high BMI. The research process strictly adhered to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) statement. 12 Compliance with the TITAN 2025 guidelines was ensured, with a completed checklist verifying that the use of artificial intelligence in this manuscript adheres to established reporting standards. 13

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Given the macro-epidemiological and genetic nature of this study, subjects were not directly recruited; instead, rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to the utilized secondary datasets. For the GBD 2021 analysis, the inclusion criteria were: (1) data spanning from 1990 to 2021; (2) populations encompassing 204 countries and territories; and (3) adults aged 20–95+ years. Populations under 20 years of age were excluded due to the extremely low incidence of high BMI-related gout in this demographic and the lack of robust epidemiological representation. For the MR analysis, inclusion criteria for the genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets required participants to be exclusively of European ancestry to minimize population stratification bias. Genetic instruments were included if they reached genome-wide significance (F < 5×10−8). Exclusion criteria for genetic variants involved the removal of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in linkage disequilibrium (r2 > 0.001; clumping window: 10,000 kb) and the exclusion of palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies to prevent strand mismatches.

Socio-demographic index

The SDI is a key composite indicator for measuring the developmental level of a country or region, providing a comprehensive assessment of socioeconomic status by integrating three core dimensions: total fertility rate, educational attainment, and per capita income. 14 The SDI value ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher level of socioeconomic development in the region. In the GBD 2021 analysis, 204 countries were categorized into 5 distinct developmental levels: high SDI, high-middle SDI, middle SDI, low-middle SDI, and low SDI. 15 We systematically analyzed the temporal trends of gout DALYs attributable to high BMI across 21 regions from 1990 to 2021, focusing on their association with levels of economic development.

Joinpoint regression analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis was employed to evaluate temporal differences in age-standardized DALY rates at the global level and across SDI regions. This method accurately identifies temporal inflection points by partitioning long-term trends into several linear segments via fitting and optimization.16,17 Model selection and optimal joinpoints were determined using Monte Carlo permutation tests. To ensure terminological precision, the Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC) was utilized to describe the overall long-term trend derived from log-linear regression. At the same time, the Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) and Annual Percent Change were strictly used as summary measures for the segmented Joinpoint regression trends. An AAPC > 0 indicates an upward trend in age-standardized metrics, while an AAPC < 0 indicates a downward trend, and an AAPC = 0 suggests a stable trend.

Decomposition analysis

Decomposition analysis was employed to quantify the impacts of population aging, epidemiological changes (including shifts in dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, and improvements in healthcare levels, etc.), and population growth on gout DALYs attributable to high BMI at the global level, across 21 GBD regions, and within 5 SDI regions. 18 Subsequently, analyses were performed separately for male and female populations to explore sex-specific differences in the impacts of the aforementioned factors.

Frontier analysis

Frontier analysis is a critical tool in GBD database analysis, typically using SDI as the independent variable and observed metrics as the dependent variables. By constructing a frontier of the lowest disease burden across different SDI levels, it quantifies the “effective difference” between the actual observed values and the theoretical optimal values. 19 This method aims to determine which countries successfully controlled the epidemic through risk prevention and policy interventions under comparable resource conditions.

Cross-country inequality analysis

Cross-country inequality analysis uses statistical tools such as the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and Concentration Index (CI) to accurately quantify the distributional disparities in disease burden across countries with varying socioeconomic statuses. The CI value ranges from −1 to 1; a persistently negative value indicates that the health loss is disproportionately concentrated among populations with lower socioeconomic status relative to their population size. It not only focuses on the absolute gaps in burden but also examines whether the disease burden shifts across nations in conjunction with global development.

Bayesian age-period-cohort analysis

The Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model is an advanced statistical method used for analyzing and projecting trends in disease incidence or mortality. By integrating information across the three dimensions of age, period, and cohort, and utilizing Bayesian inference to account for parameter uncertainty, it performs exceptionally well in long-term forecasting. 20 The model utilizes Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations (INLA) with Bayesian random walk priors across the age, period, and cohort dimensions. This approach automatically smooths random fluctuations in historical data and prevents biologically implausible long-term variations. Due to its capacity to automatically smooth random fluctuations in historical data while maintaining biological plausibility in long-term projections, the model has become a standard approach for trend forecasting within GBD studies. 21

MR analysis

This study employed a bidirectional two-sample MR approach to explore the causal relationship between obesity grades and gout. Instrumental variables (IVs) for obesity and gout were obtained from the IEU Open GWAS database, including the GWAS datasets ieu-a-90 (Obesity Class 1, BMI: 30–34.9 kg/m2), ieu-a-91 (Obesity Class 2, BMI: 35–39.9 kg/m2), and ieu-a-92 (Obesity Class 3, BMI ⩾40 kg/m2). 22 The GWAS dataset utilized for gout was ieu-a-1054. To ensure the validity of causal inference, three core assumptions were rigorously evaluated: (1) Relevance assumption: genetic instruments must exhibit significant associations with exposure factors; (2) Independence assumption: genetic variants must remain independent of confounders affecting both exposures and outcomes; (3) Exclusion restriction assumption: genetic instruments influence outcomes exclusively through the specified exposure, without alternative causal pathways (i.e., absence of horizontal pleiotropy). 23 Furthermore, essential data harmonization procedures were conducted: effect and non-effect alleles were aligned, palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were excluded to prevent strand mismatches, and proxy SNPs (r2 > 0.8) were utilized when primary SNPs were unavailable. 24 Causal associations between BMI and gout were primarily assessed using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method. To ensure the robustness of the findings, complementary methods were also applied, including MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode. 25 Genetic variants with a p-value <5 × 10−8 were selected as IVs. In the reverse analysis, the significance threshold was relaxed to a p-value <5 × 10−6 to account for potential insufficient statistical power. 26 This study was reported in adherence to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) statement.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study and visualizations for the GBD 2021 data were conducted using R Studio (version 4.4.2, https://www.r-project.org/). Joinpoint regression analysis was performed using Joinpoint software (version 5.4.0; National Cancer Institute, Rockville, MD, USA). BAPC analysis was carried out using the R packages BAPC (version 0.0.36; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and INLA (version 24.06.27; Norwegian Computing Center, Oslo, Norway).MR analysis was primarily executed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.8; MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit, University of Bristol, Bristol, UK). The IVW method was used as the primary reference for the MR analysis results. False alarm rates were assessed using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. A suggestive association between obesity and gout was considered to exist when p < 0.05 and q < 0.1. An odds ratio (OR) > 1 signified a positive association. To ensure the robustness and reliability of the findings, heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests were also conducted.

Results

Analysis of the global burden of gout attributable to high BMI from 1990 to 2021

From 1990 to 2021, the disease burden of gout attributable to high BMI increased across all GBD regions, though the magnitude of this increase varied geographically. The global age-standardized DALY rates attributable to high BMI increased steadily between 1990 and 2021, with an EAPC of 1.723% (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.648–1.798), indicating a sustained upward trend in the global gout burden attributable to high BMI. At the global level (Table 1), the age-standardized DALY rates rose from 4.391 per 100,000 population (95% uncertainty interval (UI): 2.889–6.188) in 1990 to 6.888 per 100,000 (95% UI: 4.571–9.628) in 2021, representing an absolute increase of 56.9%. Meanwhile, the total number of DALYs increased from approximately 1.842 million to 6.000 million, corresponding to a 2.26-fold increase.

At the regional level, disparities were substantial. The three regions with the highest burden (ranked by 2021 age-standardized DALY rates in descending order) were High-income North America (23.966 per 100,000; 95% UI: 15.33–34.876), Australasia (20.684 per 100,000; 95% UI: 12.535–30.145), and Southern Latin America (14.038 per 100,000; 95% UI: 8.575–20.482). Although the High SDI region possessed the highest absolute age-standardized DALY rates, its EAPC (1.818%) was lower than that of other SDI regions (Table S1). Age and sex disparities in the disease burden of gout attributable to high BMI

Between 1990 and 2021, the global disease burden of gout attributable to high BMI grew rapidly, with the percentage increase in males (229%) slightly higher than that in females (217%). Furthermore, the burden in males was significantly higher than in females across all age groups, a trend consistently observed across different SDI regions and GBD regions (Figure 2(a), Figure S1).

The number of DALYs initially increased and then decreased with advancing age. The peak age for males shifted earlier from 60–64 years in 1990 to 55–59 years in 2021, suggesting that the impact of obesity on young and middle-aged males is intensifying. In contrast, the peak age for females remained stable at 65–69 years (Figure 2(b)). The age-standardized DALY rates followed a different pattern, continuing to rise with age. The peak age-standardized DALY rates for males shifted earlier from the 80–84 age group (30.7 per 100,000) in 1990 to the 75–79 age group (46.02 per 100,000) in 2021. Similarly, the peak age-standardized DALY rates for females shifted from 85–89 years (14.8 per 100,000) in 1990 to 80–84 years (20.7 per 100,000) in 2021 (Figure 2(c)).

Joinpoint regression analysis

We employed Joinpoint regression analysis to examine the temporal trends in the age-standardized DALY rates of gout attributable to high BMI. As illustrated in Figure 3, the global age-standardized DALY rates for gout attributable to high BMI exhibited an overall upward trajectory over the past three decades, with an AAPC of 1.474 (95% CI: 1.425–1.524). The growth rate peaked between 2006 and 2009 (APC = 2.857; 95% CI: 2.481–3.234). Analysis across different SDI regions revealed an inverse relationship between SDI level and age-standardized DALY rates growth rate: regions with lower SDI levels experienced more rapid increases. For instance, the low-middle SDI region recorded an AAPC of 2.633 (95% CI: 2.623–2.643), substantially exceeding the global average. Globally and across various SDI regions, the growth rate of age-standardized DALY rates was generally higher in males than in females (Table S2).

Correlation between SDI and the burden of gout attributable to high BMI across 204 countries and territories in 2021

In 2021, a positive correlation was observed between national SDI and the age-standardized DALY rates of gout attributable to high BMI (R = 0.600, p < 0.001; Figure 4(a), Table S3). Countries such as the United States of America, Canada, New Zealand, and Australia were positioned significantly above the fitted line, suggesting that their disease burden is more severe than expected given their SDI levels. In contrast, high-SDI nations like Japan and Norway, despite their economic advancement, exhibited relatively lower age-standardized DALY rates. At the GBD regional level, a similar correlation trend was observed between SDI and disease burden (R = 0.672, p < 0.001; Figure 4(b)). Regions with higher SDI were generally associated with a heavier disease burden. For instance, age-standardized DALY rates in high-SDI regions such as High-income North America (SDI: 0.86; age-standardized DALY rates: 23.97 per 100,000) and Australasia (SDI: 0.85; age-standardized DALY rates: 20.68 per 100,000) were significantly higher than those in low-SDI regions like South Asia (SDI: 0.56; age-standardized DALY rates: 2.69 per 100,000) and Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa (SDI: 0.40; age-standardized DALY rates: 2.52 per 100,000).

Frontier analysis based on the age-standardized DALY rates

To assess the potential for health improvement relative to national development levels, we conducted a frontier analysis using SDI and age-standardized DALY rates data from 1990 to 2021 (Figure 5(a)). The results reveal a significant non-linear relationship between SDI and the gout burden caused by high BMI, suggesting that even at high SDI levels, it is theoretically possible to maintain a relatively low disease burden.

The distance between a country’s actual observed age-standardized DALY rates and its corresponding frontier value is termed the “effective difference.” This metric quantifies the potential reduction in disease burden achievable at the current development stage through optimized health policies and lifestyle interventions. In 2021, the 11 countries with the largest deviations from the frontier are labeled in black font (Figure 5(b)), with effective differences ranging from 12.25 to 23.59 (Table S4). Additionally, the five countries with lower SDI values (SDI < 0.5) and the smallest deviations (Burundi, Somalia, Ethiopia, Haiti, Niger) are labeled in blue font; these countries closely track the frontier curve, exhibiting minimal effective differences. Although these are predominantly low-SDI nations, their data establish an achievable low-burden benchmark for countries at similar levels of development. Conversely, the five countries with higher SDI values (SDI > 0.8) and the largest deviations (United States of America, New Zealand, Canada, Greenland, Australia) are labeled in red font. The actual burdens in these nations far exceed the theoretical low values expected for their SDI levels, suggesting substantial room for improvement in high BMI prevention and gout management.

Analysis of health inequalities in gout attributable to high BMI

The SII was 6.17 per 100,000 population (95% CI: 5.16–7.17) in 1990, rising to 11.91 per 100,000 (95% CI: 10.57–13.25) by 2021. The elevation in SII indicates a positive association between the age-standardized DALY rates of gout attributable to high BMI and SDI (Figure 6(a)), suggesting that countries with higher SDI bear a heavier disease burden. Furthermore, the CI increased from −0.38 (95% CI: −0.6096 to −0.1005) in 1990 to −0.32 (95% CI: −0.53 to −0.11) in 2021 (Figure 6(b)). The persistently negative CI values indicate that, relative to population size, the health loss was disproportionately concentrated among populations with lower or intermediate socioeconomic statuses.

Future projections of the gout burden attributable to high BMI

Utilizing the BAPC model and GBD data from 1990 to 2021, we projected the disease burden of gout attributable to high BMI for the ensuing 19 years (up to 2040) at the global level and across various SDI regions (Figure 8). The global age-standardized DALY rate is expected to climb to approximately 7.1 per 100,000 (95% CI: 6.5–7.8) by 2040. Among the different SDI regions, the high SDI region exhibited the highest baseline burden (age-standardized DALY rates >10 per 100,000); however, this trend is expected to plateau, with a projected age-standardized DALY rate of approximately 11.7 per 100,000 (95% CI: 10.4–12.9) by 2040. Conversely, the middle SDI region is experiencing rapid growth, with the age-standardized DALY rates projected to reach 6.4 per 100,000 (95% CI: 5.9–6.8) by 2040, rapidly approaching the global average. The high-middle and low-middle SDI regions also exhibit upward trends in the age-standardized DALY rates. In the absence of effective interventions, these regions are poised to become the primary drivers of the global growth in gout burden. Analysis of sex disparities reveals that the projected age-standardized DALY rates for males remain consistently higher than those for females throughout the period from 2021 to 2040 (Figure S2).

Causal relationship between high BMI and gout

This study investigated the potential causal association between obesity severity and gout. Detailed characteristics of the SNPs utilized as IVs are provided in Table S6. After applying the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction, the IVW analysis provided evidence supporting a potential causal relationship between all classes of obesity and gout: Obesity Class 1 (OR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.06–1.43; p = 0.008), Obesity Class 2 (OR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.04–1.43; p = 0.015), and Obesity Class 3 (OR = 1.15; 95% CI: 1.05–1.27; p = 0.004; Table 2). Notably, while all evaluated degrees of obesity independently increased the risk of gout, the effect size did not strictly escalate in a linear dose-response gradient with increasing severity. Figure 9(a), (c), and (e) presents scatter plots of the distribution of individual SNP effects across different obesity grades. Leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that the influence of individual SNPs on the overall estimate was balanced, effectively ruling out the potential bias of outlier SNPs on the reliability of the results (Figure 9(b), (d), and (f)). Furthermore, the Cochran’s Q test (Table S7) and MR-Egger intercept analysis (Figure S3) effectively excluded potential biases arising from heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy. The results of the reverse MR analysis revealed no significant causal effect of gout on obesity (Table S8, Figure S4). Collectively, these findings provide evidence supporting a potential unidirectional causal relationship: varying degrees of obesity increase the risk of gout, whereas gout does not appear to lead to obesity. Discussion

Based on GBD 2021 data, this study systematically analyzed the evolving disease burden of gout attributable to high BMI at the global, regional, and national levels from 1990 to 2021. Furthermore, using MR approaches, we investigated potential causal associations between obesity grades and gout, thereby providing novel scientific evidence for the prevention and control of gout. This study builds upon and significantly expands prior research in this field. While Jin et al. 27 previously analyzed the GBD burden of gout attributable to high BMI up to 2019, our research utilizes the updated GBD 2021 framework and moves beyond descriptive trends by introducing macro-level health inequality tracking (Frontier Analysis, SII, and CI) alongside BAPC modeling for long-term projections. Furthermore, although a recent GBD 2021 analysis by Chen et al. 28 examined this association, their focus was exclusively restricted to early-onset gout in adolescents and young adults (aged 15–39). By mapping the burden across the entire lifespan, our study uniquely enables the explicit characterization of the population-wide “age-shift” phenomenon. Finally, unlike foundational MR studies that treated adiposity as a continuous trait,29,30 our MR analysis uniquely employs GWAS datasets stratified by clinical obesity severity (Classes 1–3), facilitating the investigation of causal effect magnitudes across distinct clinical thresholds.

Our analysis highlights a substantial increase in the global burden of high BMI-related gout over the past three decades. While high SDI regions currently bear the highest absolute burden, the rapid rise in EAPC within low-middle and low SDI regions suggests that the geographic center of the disease is shifting. In these developing regions, rapid urbanization has facilitated a nutritional transition toward Westernized diets rich in calories and fructose, leading to a surge in obesity rates. 2 Additionally, occupational shifts and prevalent sedentary behaviors have significantly reduced physical activity levels. 31 However, heterogeneous trends among High-SDI nations provide critical insights. For instance, the United States and Canada exhibit burdens far exceeding expectations, whereas Japan and Nordic countries maintain relatively low obesity rates through healthy dietary patterns.32,33 The substantial “effective differences” in countries like the United States highlight policy gaps in preventing obesity-related metabolic diseases. 34 Frontier analysis confirms that a severe gout epidemic is not an inevitable consequence of economic progress.

Consistent with existing literature, our findings reveal a significant sex disparity and an “age-shifting” phenomenon, with males bearing a heavier burden and experiencing peak incidence at progressively younger ages. This shift underscores the urgent need for early monitoring of metabolic health in young and middle-aged demographics.35 –37 Concurrently, the persistently negative CI underscores that, relative to population size, health loss remains disproportionately concentrated among nations with intermediate development levels, necessitating proactive healthcare resource allocation as these populations grow and age. 38

Projections from the BAPC model indicate that the global age-standardized DALY rates will climb to approximately 7.1 per 100,000 by 2040. To align strictly with our empirical findings, future prevention strategies should focus heavily on clinical weight management. Integrating comprehensive body weight assessment and routine serum uric acid monitoring into primary care is essential for early diagnosis. 29 In clinical practice, prioritizing scientific weight management alongside standard pharmacotherapy is crucial to help mitigate the recurrence of gout and the risk of associated metabolic complications.

To avoid ecological fallacy, it is critical to emphasize that our MR analysis provides complementary, individual-level genetic supporting the biological plausibility of the obesity-gout relationship. This genetic evidence complements, rather than mechanistically explains, population-level epidemiological trends, which are primarily driven by environmental factors, demographics, healthcare access, and evolving diagnostic practices. Through forward MR analysis, we observed evidence suggesting a potential positive association between obesity classes 1–3 and gout incidence. Interestingly, the MR estimates did not demonstrate an escalating dose-response gradient (ORs: Class 1 = 1.23, Class 2 = 1.22, Class 3 = 1.15). This counterintuitive trend may be attributed to methodological factors, such as reduced statistical power and weaker IVs due to smaller GWAS sample sizes for extreme obesity phenotypes (Class 3). Alternatively, biological explanations may involve a physiological saturation threshold, where the hyperuricemic effect of adiposity reaches its maximum capacity. 39 Furthermore, this trend could reflect survival bias, wherein individuals who endure extreme obesity inherently harbor unmeasured, metabolically protective phenotypes. 40 Reverse MR analyses yielded no significant effect of gout on obesity, suggesting that elevated BMI precedes gout pathogenesis rather than the reverse.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations warrant consideration. First, GBD estimates in low-SDI regions may suffer from underdiagnosis due to limited medical resources. Second, our MR analysis relies exclusively on European GWAS data. Due to established trans-ethnic heterogeneity in urate transporter genes (e.g., SLC2A9 and ABCG2), 41 the causal magnitude of the BMI-gout association may vary across ancestries, limiting the global generalizability of our findings. Future multi-ancestry MR studies are required. Third, BMI serves as an imperfect surrogate marker for adiposity. It cannot differentiate lean muscle mass from fat mass, nor evaluate fat distribution. Visceral adipose tissue exhibits higher metabolic activity and stronger links to insulin resistance and hyperuricemia; thus, relying solely on BMI may partially obscure true metabolic drivers. 42 Fourth, while the MR analyses underwent strict Benjamini–Hochberg FDR corrections, the descriptive GBD epidemiological trend analyses did not incorporate formal adjustments for multiple comparisons. Fifth, because this study relied on secondary analyses of pre-existing global databases and aggregated GWAS summary statistics, a formal a priori statistical power analysis for sample size estimation was not prospectively conducted. Finally, historical trend-based BAPC models cannot anticipate future disruptions caused by novel weight-loss pharmacotherapies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, over the past three decades, driven by epidemiological changes and a demographic shift toward younger male populations, the global burden of gout attributable to high BMI has surged precipitously. While high-income countries currently bear the heaviest burden, the most rapid growth is observed in developing nations. The widening inequality gap and the suboptimal performance of numerous high-SDI countries underscore an urgent need for targeted health policies. The genetic evidence supporting a potential causal relationship between graded obesity and gout further reinforces the urgency of weight management in clinical practice. Future strategies must prioritize obesity prevention, promote healthy dietary patterns, and address early-onset metabolic risks in males to collectively combat this anticipated epidemiological trend.
