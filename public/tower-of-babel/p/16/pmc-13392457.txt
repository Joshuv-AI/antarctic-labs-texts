5-Formylcytosine functions as a chemical regulator of nucleosome positioning
Authors: Takafumi Furuhata, Yijing Lin, Genki Hino, Akimitsu Okamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract5-Formylcytosine (5fC) has attracted increasing attention because of discoveries of its unique epigenetic functions as a gene activation marker beyond its role as an intermediate in the demethylation of 5-methylcytosine (5mC). However, there are scant clues to understanding the mechanisms by which 5fC affects nucleosome structures for regulating gene expression, except that 5fC promotes nucleosome formation and cross-linking with histone units via a Schiff base. The present study demonstrates that 5fC acts as a chemical cue that modulates the nucleosome positions preferred by a given DNA sequence. A series of nucleosomes designed with linker regions and 5fC at defined sites allowed systematic evaluation of the effect of formylation on the nucleosome positions, revealing site-dependent impacts of 5fC distinct from those of 5mC. The 5fC-mediated changes of relative preference for the possible nucleosome positions were not only associated with the cross-linking with histone units, but also energetic tolerance that was imparted by 5fC in terms of the base-stacking interaction against the DNA configuration characteristic at specific sites of nucleosomal DNA. These findings provide chemical insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying 5fC-mediated nucleosome repositioning and gene expression in important biological events, such as cellular reprogramming.Subject terms: DNA, Nucleic acids

Introduction

DNA modification is an important chemical marker that controls gene expression1–4. In addition to 5-methylcytosine (5mC)4, three oxidized derivatives of 5mC, 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC)5,6, 5-formylcytosine (5fC)7–9, and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC)8,10, have been found to play distinct epigenetic roles in different tissues2,3,11. Although the epigenetic functions of 5mC and 5hmC have been studied extensively4,12–17, 5fC and 5caC have attracted less attention because they are less abundant in tissues and have been regarded as mere intermediates during the demethylation of 5mC2,18–22. Indeed, the abundance of 5fC is at the level of 1–5 per 107 cytosines (C) in genomic DNA in various tissues, approximately 4–5 orders of magnitude lower than 5mC2,23. However, 5fC is enriched in embryonic stem cells by 10–100-fold relative to non-stem cells, and persists stably for long periods at specific genomic regions2,24,25. Significant accumulation of 5fC is found at active and poised promoters26 and enhancers20,27,28 in embryonic cells, implying unique epigenetic roles in regulating gene expression27,29–31. As a representative observation, 5fC was recently reported to be enriched at chromocenters and required for recruiting RNA polymerase III (Pol III) at Pol III target genes, particularly oocyte-type tandem-arrayed tRNA genes, to promote their transcription in Xenopus embryos31. These findings suggest that 5fC plays essential roles in cellular reprogramming, particularly as an activation marker of gene expression2.

A characteristic feature of 5fC is its ability to promote nucleosome formation. Raiber et al.27 demonstrated that the presence of 5fC significantly increases nucleosome occupancy in nearby genomic regions. They observed distinct 5fC distribution patterns in the brain and heart of mouse early embryos, which indicates that the 5fC patterns are associated with tissue-specific gene expression. The nucleosome formation facilitated by 5fC was reproduced in vitro; on average, only one unit of 5fC per nucleosome was sufficient to increase the nucleosome occupancy dramatically. The consistency between the observations in vivo and in vitro suggests that 5fC intrinsically alters the chemical properties of DNA to promote nucleosome formation.

It has been reported that 5fC changes two chemical properties of DNA related to nucleosome formation. The first is the imposition of flexibility. Ngo et al.32 found that 5fC enhances DNA flexibility of the nucleosome-forming Widom 601 DNA sequence most dramatically among 5mC and its three oxidized derivatives. Their study also showed that the mechanical strength of nucleosomes is reinforced by 5fC modification, suggesting that the increase in DNA flexibility by 5fC is advantageous to DNA bending upon nucleosome formation. The flexibility is imparted by 5fC partially because of the electron-withdrawing nature of its formyl group, which reduces the proton-accepting capacity of the 3rd nitrogen of cytosine, thereby weakening Watson–Crick base-pairing33,34. The second property is the cross-linking ability of 5fC with histone proteins via Schiff base formation with the side and main chains of the histone units35,36. Histone–DNA cross-linking mediated by Schiff base formation has been demonstrated in vitro35,36 and in cells27, indicating its role in regulating nucleosome organization and gene expression.

Although the ability of 5fC to promote nucleosome formation has been demonstrated, the ways in which it controls the structural properties of nucleosomes, which are strongly associated with access to DNA and gene expression, remain unclear. Chromatin accessibility is not simply determined by nucleosome occupancy37. Indeed, some genomic regions retain high chromatin accessibility despite high nucleosome occupancy38,39, as observed at tissue-specific enhancers, including formylated sites27. To gain a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying the high chromatin accessibility characteristic of those regions, it is important to capture the structural features of nucleosomes modified with epigenetic markers. Two important properties of the nucleosomes that are closely related to chromatin accessibility are (1) nucleosome positioning on DNA40–42, and (2) DNA unwrapping (breathing)43–46, which is the repetition of partial dissociation and rebinding of DNA near the entry and exit sites of the nucleosome. An investigation of the effects of 5fC on the positioning and unwrapping of the nucleosome may provide insights into the chemical mechanisms by which 5fC epigenetically regulates gene expression. It is also unknown how essential the cross-linking between DNA and histones mediated by 5fC is to the 5fC-mediated regulation of the nucleosome properties.

Here, we demonstrate that 5fC serves as a chemical modulator of nucleosome positions preferred by a DNA sequence. We found that 5fC at specific CpG sites on DNA alters the relative abundance of nucleosomes at each position allowed on a given sequence. The 5fC-mediated modulation of nucleosome positional preference was associated not only with cross-linking with histone proteins as proposed previously by Raiber et al.27, but also with energetic tolerance imparted by 5fC to the DNA configurational changes following the nucleosome positional changes, in terms of nucleobase stacking interaction. Importantly, the effect of 5fC on the nucleosome positioning independent of the DNA–histone cross-linking represents an overlooked property of 5fC in terms of the regulation of nucleosome positions. The control of the nucleosome positioning through the 5fC-mediated regulation of histone–DNA cross-linking and nucleobase stacking represents a potential mechanism underlying gene activation essential for embryonic development and cellular reprogramming.

Results

Difference in the effects of 5mC and 5fC on nucleosome positioning

The effects of 5fC on nucleosome formation have been evaluated using nucleosomes reconstituted with DNA that is 145–147 base pairs (bp) long, the minimal length for nucleosome formation27,35. However, chromatin constituted with arrays of nucleosomes has linker regions between nucleosomes that are free of histone proteins and accessible to DNA-binding proteins. It would therefore be essential to evaluate the properties of a nucleosome with a histone-free linker region to deepen our understanding of how the nucleosome structure changes on DNA formylation. To this aim, we prepared a 192-bp-long DNA sequence comprising Widom 601 DNA with a slight mutation in the sequence and a 45-bp-long additional sequence (AS1) (601-AS1) (Fig. 1a). The additional sequence fused to the 601 sequence contained a recognition sequence for the restriction enzyme BstXI, which allowed the evaluation of the enzyme’s accessibility to the AS1 region. 5fC was designed to be introduced on 601 DNA around 20 bp upstream from the boundary between the 601 DNA and the AS1 region for testing the effects of 5fC near the nucleosome entry site on nucleosome positioning (Fig. 1a, b). We divided 601-AS1 into 10 fragments (top strand: four fragments [TOPi-1–4]; bottom strand: six fragments [BOTi-1–6]). Using the fragment BOTi-3 with a phosphate group at the 5′ end and 5fC in the middle of the sequence (Supplementary Fig. 1), the 601-AS1 modified with 5fC at a desired site was reconstructed by ligating the fragments enzymatically with T4 DNA ligase (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1). BOTi-3 harboring 5fC was synthesized by solid phase DNA synthesis using 5fC phosphoramidite, whose aldehyde was protected by a six-membered acetal ring (Supplementary Fig. 1)47. The 5′-end of TOPi-1 was modified with fluorescein (FAM) for stain-free detection. In addition to the 5fC-modified DNA (601-AS1-5fC), unmodified 601-AS1 (601-AS1-UnM) and 601-AS1 with 5mC at the same site (601-AS1-5mC) were also constructed for comparison (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Fig. 2). The nucleosomes of 601-AS1-UnM, 5mC, and 5fC were reconstituted using a salt dialysis method. For this purpose, a histone octamer used for the nucleosome reconstitution was refolded using human histone units expressed in Escherichia coli (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 2). When we analyzed the nucleosomes reconstituted at different histone-to-DNA molar ratios (HO/DNA = 1.0, 1.2 and 1.4), nucleosomes with lower mobility emerged in the characterization based on 6% native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) in addition to a major band of well-positioned nucleosomes under the conditions of HO/DNA = 1.2 and 1.4 (Supplementary Fig. 4). The emergence of the nucleosome with lower mobility suggested that the excessive amount of histone octamer itself can perturb the proper formation of the well-positioned nucleosomes, possibly due to the nonspecific interaction between the nucleosome and excessive histone octamers during the reconstitution. We thereby adopted HO/DNA = 1.0 to reconstitute the nucleosomes as test samples (Fig. 1c).

We performed exonuclease III (Exo III) position mapping of the reconstituted nucleosomes to evaluate their positions. DNA wrapping around the histone is protected from Exo III digestion, which enables assignment of nucleosome positions by analyzing the length of the FAM-labeled TOP strand remaining after Exo III digestion. A 10-min treatment of the nucleosomes with Exo III yielded clear bands that are around 150 nucleotides (nt) and 135 nt in length (lower bands), in addition to a 192 nt long band (upper band) corresponding to full-length intact DNA for all 601-AS1-UnM, 5mC and 5fC nucleosomes (Fig. 1d). Given that the length of DNA wrapping around a single nucleosome is 145–147 nt, the 150 nt long band indicates a nucleosome positioned at the edge of the 601 region. In addition, we observed a gradual increase in 135-nt bands accompanied by a gradual decrease in 150-nt bands during 10 min to 20 min after the reaction started. This gradual increase in the 135-nt band was kinetically different from the rapid emergence of the 150-nt band, which was probably produced by the digestion of the free linker DNA that is not wrapping around the core histone. The 135-nt band was thereby attributed to products generated by excessive digestion of DNA at the entry site of the nucleosomes that stochastically peeled off and became accessible to Exo III (nucleosomal DNA unwrapping)48. Indeed, the total intensity of the 150- and 135-nt bands relative to that of the 192-nt band was almost consistent through 10–20 min of the Exo III digestion (Supplementary Fig. 5), which supported the notion that both the 150- and 135-nt bands (lower bands) are derived from the nucleosome at the same position, more specifically at the edge of the 601 DNA side. By contrast, the bands of 192-nt or similar (upper bands) are attributed to the nucleosome at the edge of the AS1 side.

We then examined the intensity of the upper bands relative to that of the lower bands at 15 min to compare the proportion of the nucleosome positioned at the AS1 side between the 601-AS1-UnM, 5mC and 5fC nucleosomes (Fig. 1e). We observed strong enhancement of the proportion of the upper bands (%Upper/total) for the 601-AS1-5fC nucleosome (47%) relative to that for the 601-AS1-UnM and 5mC nucleosomes (37% and 33%, respectively) (Fig. 1e, left). By contrast, the difference in %Upper/total of Cy5-labeled 601-AS1-UnM added as an internal control between the three groups was not significant, indicating that the activity of Exo III in each test sample was controlled to a similar level throughout all test samples (Fig. 1e, right). The clear enhancement of %Upper/total of 601-AS1-5fC over that of the others shows that 5fC changes the relative preference of the 601-AS1 for the two possible nucleosome positions. We also performed the position mapping of the nucleosomes heated at 60 °C for 30 min to examine whether the proportion of the nucleosomes at the respective positions changed when the nucleosome is allowed to move by heat exposure. Heating at 60 °C for 30 min was reported to induce the sliding of the nucleosome reconstituted with 601 DNA to reach its steady state at 60 °C49. Even after the nucleosome was heated at 60 °C for 30 min, %Upper/total of the 601-AS1-5fC (51%) was higher than that of the 601-AS1-5mC (41%) (Supplementary Fig. 6). The preference of 601-AS1-5fC for the position at the AS1 side over 601-AS1-5mC was thereby consistent regardless of heating of the nucleosomes. Related to the effect of the epigenetic marks on nucleosome properties, 5mC has been studied extensively. 5mC is generally considered to increase the affinity of DNA for the histone octamer through cell-based, in vitro, and theoretical studies, partially because the bulky methyl group protruding into the major groove of DNA enhances the local electrostatic potential of the minor groove to strengthen its interaction with the histone core50,51. In addition, a theoretical study proposed that 5mC at the entry site of the nucleosome facilitates contact of DNA with arginine residues of histone H2A and H3 tails52. By contrast, 5mC was also reported to destabilize the nucleosome in different cases, at least partially, by stiffening the DNA32, particularly when 5mC is located at major groove sites where the minor groove of nucleosomal DNA faces away from the histone core53. These controversial findings suggest that the effects of 5mC on the nucleosome are sensitive to its location on the nucleosome and the DNA sequence around 5mC. Because no obvious change was observed in the proportion of the nucleosome positions for 601-AS1-5mC compared with 601-AS1-UnM, it seems that 5mC exhibited the same degree of stabilizing and destabilizing effects on the nucleosomes positioned at the AS1 and 601 sides. Conversely, 5fC of 601-AS1-5fC increased the proportion of the nucleosome position at the AS1 side, indicating that 5fC exerts a pronounced stabilizing effect on the nucleosome when it is located at the internal site closer to the nucleosome dyad. The 5fC-mediated enhancement of the proportion of the nucleosome positioned at the AS1 side was further supported by the decrease of susceptibility to BstXI-mediated cleavage of AS1 for the 601-AS1-5fC in the presence of 5 mM magnesium chloride (MgCl2) the same condition used in the Exo III mapping assay (Supplementary Fig. 7). Because a high concentration of MgCl2, typically over 10 mM, is known to induce nucleosome aggregation54, 5 mM MgCl2 might also affect enzyme accessibility. However, little change in BstXI-mediated cleavage efficiency was observed even when the MgCl2 concentration was reduced to 1 mM (Supplementary Fig. 7), which barely induces the aggregation of mononucleosomes54. The consistency of the BstXI-mediated cleavage efficiency between the two conditions in terms of MgCl2 concentration supports that the enzyme accessibility to the AS1 linker regions tested under 5 mM is governed by the positions of the nucleosomes, but not by aggregation states. Consistent with this observation, Poirier et al. observed that packing of 17-mer nucleosome arrays in the presence of 10 mM MgCl2 had only a modest impact on the enzyme accessibility to nucleosomes, suggesting that the nucleosome aggregates formed by approximately 10 mM MgCl2 are still dynamic and accessible55. Taken together, these results demonstrated that 5fC has an impact on the positions of the nucleosome and the concomitant protein accessibility in a manner distinct from 5mC.

Site-dependent effects of CpG formylation on nucleosome positioning

We constructed nucleosomes with formylated CpG at different locations in DNA to evaluate the site-dependent effects of 5fC on the positional preference of the nucleosome (Fig. 2a, b). We designed AS2-601, in which an extra sequence (AS2) containing a BstXI recognition site was introduced upstream of the 601 sequence. The 601 sequence was partially mutated to 5′-CGCG-3′ as the sites for CpG formylation (Fig. 2a). Specifically, we introduced CGCG sequences at five different locations: upstream (site I) and downstream (site II) of the BstXI recognition site of AS2, the entry site (site III), the midpoint between entry and center (site IV) and the center (site V) of the 601 sequence (Fig. 2a, b). Five types of AS2-601 harboring 5fC at sites I–V (unmodified: AS2-601-UnM, formylated at sites I–V: AS2-601-I–V-5fC) were constructed by ligating 11 fragments of DNA using T4 DNA ligase (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9), as for 601-AS1. The 5′ end of the bottom strand of each sequence was modified with FAM for stain-free detection and quantification in biochemical assays, including Exo III position mapping. The unmodified and formylated AS2-601 sequences were subjected to salt dialysis to reconstitute nucleosomes. The nucleosomes detected as major bands in 6% native polyacrylamide electrophoresis (PAGE) exhibited almost identical mobility. The same mobility of the major bands between the nucleosomes suggests that all nucleosomes had a similar topology (Fig. 2c). Although lower mobility bands (nucleosomes with minor configurations) were also detected as minor bands (<10%), the largest variation in the proportion was 6.0% between AS2-601-UnM and AS2-601-V-5fC (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, the variation between the other five nucleosomes was within 2.2%, indicating that the nucleosomes are assembled in similar quality (Fig. 2c). In addition to the 5fC-modified nucleosomes, three constructs (i.e. AS2-601-II-5mC, AS2-601-III-5mC and AS2-601-IV-5mC) in which 5′-CGCG-3′ at sites II, III and IV were methylated, were also synthesized and used to reconstitute nucleosomes to compare the effects of formylation and methylation on nucleosome positioning (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Exo III position mapping was performed to investigate positions of the nucleosomes with different types of modification at different sites (Fig. 3a). Regardless of the modification type, two bands were observed at around 192 bp (upper) and 145 bp (lower) for all AS2-601s after 15 min of Exo III treatment (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 11). In addition, shorter fragments emerged just below the two bands when the reaction time was extended up to 60 min (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 11). These shorter fragments were attributed to products generated by excessive digestion of DNA at the entry site as observed for AS1-601 (Fig. 1d)48. Indeed, the ratio of band intensities combined for the upper and lower regions was almost unchanged between the 15- and 60-min treatments (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 11). Thus, there are two possible nucleosome positions allowed on the AS2-601 sequence: one at the edge of the 601 side (Pos-1), and the other at the end of the AS2 side (Pos-2) (Fig. 3a). This interpretation is also consistent with the result of native PAGE analysis of the reconstituted AS2-601 nucleosomes in which major products were detected as a single dominant band (Fig. 2c), because two types of the nucleosomes positioned at the edges of the AS2-601 sequence should be structurally symmetrical to have similar mobility in native PAGE. We note that, in the PAGE analysis of the nucleosomes (Fig. 2c), faint bands whose mobility was lower than that of the major band were also observed. Nucleosomes with low mobility generally correspond to nucleosomes positioned at the central part of the DNA. However, bands indicative of nucleosome positions other than Pos-1 and Pos-2 were not obvious in Exo III position mapping (Fig. 3b), indicating that the existence of centrally positioned nucleosomes was negligible in Exo III position mapping due to their small amount. Thus, the primary positions allowed on the 601-AS2 nucleosomes are Pos-1 and Pos-2.

We compared the ratio of Pos-1 to Pos-2 between nucleosomes to investigate the site-dependent effect of 5fC on nucleosome positions. Notably, the ratio of Pos-1 to Pos-2 between nucleosomes obviously differs depending on the types and locations of the modification (Fig. 3c). AS2-601-UnM preferred Pos-1 over Pos-2 (Pos-1: 60%, Pos-2: 40%). Similar proportions of the Pos-1 and Pos-2 nucleosomes were observed between AS2-601-UnM and V-5fC, indicating that 5fC at site V did not have a significant impact on the relative preference of the nucleosome for Pos-1 and Pos-2. By contrast, there was a significant increase in the percentages of Pos-2 for AS2-601-II-5fC and AS2-601-III-5fC. This increase was particularly pronounced in AS2-601-5fC-III (Pos-2: 50% for AS2-601-II-5fC and 55% for III-5fC) (Fig. 3c). Conversely, we observed the opposite change in the nucleosome positions for AS2-601-IV-5fC, where Pos-1 preferred by AS2-601-UnM was further stabilized (Pos-1: 68%) (Fig. 3c). The changes in the nucleosome positions induced by 5mC were clearly different from those caused by 5fC. Although the 5mC at site IV (AS2-601-IV-5mC) stabilized Pos-1 (Pos-1: 71%) as observed for AS2-601-IV-5fC (Pos-1: 68%) (Fig. 3b, c), 5mC at site II also strongly suppressed the formation of the nucleosome positioned at Pos-2. This positional profile of AS2-601-II-5mC is in stark contrast to that of AS2-601-II-5fC (Pos-1: 80% for AS2-601-II-5mC, 50% for AS2-601-II-5fC) (Fig. 3c). In addition, we observed no increase in Pos-2 occupancy for AS2-601-III-5mC (Pos-2: 36%), which was seen for AS2-601-III-5fC (Pos-2: 55%) (Fig. 3c). These characteristic preferences for Pos-1 and Pos-2 that are different between the AS2-601-II, III and IV nucleosomes harboring 5mC and 5fC were also recaptured using sets of nucleosomes that were prepared independently of those evaluated in Fig. 3 (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Thus, 5fC had a significant impact on the positional preference of the nucleosomes in a manner distinct from 5mC. The preference of the AS2-601-II-5fC and AS2-601-III-5fC nucleosomes for Pos-2 over Pos-1 (Fig. 3a, c) is consistent with the observation that 5fC was preferably located at the internal site of the 601-AS1 nucleosome (Fig. 1d, e). By contrast, Pos-1 was strongly preferred by the AS2-601-IV-5fC nucleosome, whose 5fC is located on the nucleosomal DNA and away from the entry site at both Pos-1 and Pos-2 (Fig. 3a, c). These observations collectively indicate not only that 5fC stabilizes nucleosomes to increase occupancy, as pointed out in previous studies27, but also that the degree of stabilization provided by 5fC significantly varies depending on its location on the nucleosome to enable precise modulation of the positional preference of the nucleosomes.

To verify the difference in the nucleosome positions, we performed a DNA cleavage assay on AS2-601-III-5fC, AS2-601-IV-5fC, and AS2-601-V-5fC, which exhibited different ratios of the nucleosome positions (Fig. 3c). We used BstXI for cleavage (Fig. 3d). If the nucleosome covered AS2, AS2 would be protected from BstXI-mediated digestion (Fig. 3d). The digestion of the AS2-601-UnM nucleosome after 90 min of treatment with BstXI was suppressed by 11% relative to that of the naked AS2-601-UnM DNA (Fig. 3d–f). The AS2-601-IV-5fC nucleosome, which was dominantly positioned at Pos-1, was found to be more susceptible to BstXI-mediated digestion such that the inhibition rate of the AS2 cleavage was only 4% (Fig. 3e, f). Conversely, the digestion of AS2-601-III-5fC in the nucleosomes was suppressed by 25%, which was consistent with the higher proportion of the nucleosome positioned at Pos-2, the AS2 side (Fig. 3e, f). We observed no significant suppression or enhancement of the digestion of the AS2-601-V-5fC nucleosome (Fig. 3e, f), which showed little difference in the proportion of the nucleosome positions from AS2-601-UnM (Fig. 3b, c). Because the difference in the percentage of the nucleosomal DNA protected from BstXI (7% between AS2-601-UnM and IV-5fC, 14% between AS2-601-UnM and III-5fC) was much larger than the difference in the contents of free DNA included in the nucleosome samples (0.5% between AS2-601-UnM and IV-5fC, 2.5% between AS2-601-UnM and III-5fC), the results clearly reflect the difference in the protein accessibility of the AS2 region of the nucleosomes. Thus, the enzyme accessibility to AS2 was associated with the ratio of Pos-1 and Pos-2 determined by Exo III, which supports the notion that 5fC can regulate nucleosome positioning to control the enzyme accessibility to DNA wrapped around the nucleosome.

Suppression of nucleosomal DNA unwrapping by formylated CpG

Nucleosomal DNA unwrapping, another important property of nucleosomes, regulates enzymatic accessibility and organization of the nucleosome43,44,46, in addition to nucleosome positioning. We evaluated the effect of 5fC on nucleosomal DNA unwrapping by further analyzing the results of Exo III mapping. As described in the previous section (Fig. 3b), we observed the clear emergence of shorter DNA fragments after the nucleosomes were treated with Exo III for 60 min, which were presumably produced by the over-digestion of unwrapped DNA at the entry site (Fig. 4a, b)48. Next, we calculated the percentages of the bottom band intensity (Truncated 601) of the total intensity of the top (Full 601) and bottom bands around the 140 bp region (%Truncated 601) as a measure of the ease of unwrapping at the entry site of the Pos-1 nucleosome. The truncation percentage of AS2-601-III-5fC (40%) was significantly lower than that of AS2-601-UnM (50%) by 10 percentage points, indicating that 5fC at site III suppressed unwrapping (Fig. 4c, left). The suppression of unwrapping by 5fC near the entry site of the nucleosome is consistent with a previous study reporting that 5fC at the entry site imparts resistance to the nucleosome against mechanical unwrapping32. By contrast, the %Truncated 601 of AS2-601-IV-5fC and AS2-601-V-5fC were not significantly different from that of AS2-601-UnM, demonstrating that 5fC remote from the entry site had little effect on unwrapping (Fig. 4c, left). For comparison, we also performed a similar evaluation with methylated AS2-601 (AS2-601-II-5mC, AS2-601-III-5mC, and AS2-601-IV-5mC). The reduction of %Truncated 601 was the greatest in AS2-601-IV-5mC (Fig. 4c, right). The decreased susceptibility of AS2-601-IV-5mC to over-digestion suggests that 5mC has a stronger long-range influence on DNA unwrapping at the nucleosome entry site. By contrast, no significant difference in truncation was observed between AS2-601-III-5mC and AS2-601-UnM (Fig. 4c, right), suggesting that 5mC at the entry site does not necessarily suppress the unwrapping of DNA. Previous studies have reported that 5mC narrows the minor groove of DNA by bulky methyl groups protruding into the major groove, thereby enhancing the local electrostatic potential of the minor groove to strengthen its interaction with the histone core50,51. However, 5mC at site III of Pos-1 is located at the major groove block where the major groove contacts the histone, which prevents 5mC at site III from suppressing unwrapping by reinforcing the DNA–histone interaction. Conversely, 5fC at site III reduced nucleosomal DNA unwrapping (Fig. 4c, left). Thus, 5fC at the nucleosome entry site suppresses nucleosomal DNA unwrapping even when it is located at the major groove site, which demonstrates its different effect on nucleosome unwrapping from that of 5mC.

Nucleosome positions stabilized by cross-linking between 5fC and histone near superhelical location (SHL) ±3.5

We explored the mechanism by which 5fC affects the nucleosome positions differently depending on its location. In previous studies, cross-linking of 5fC and histone proteins via Schiff base formation has been proposed to play an important role in organizing nucleosome structure27. We therefore evaluated the cross-linking efficiency of nucleosomes harboring 5fC to examine its relationship with the positional preference of the nucleosome. Exo III position mapping showed that the proportion of Pos-2, the nucleosome position in which a histone core is positioned at the edge of the AS2 side of AS2-601 (Figs. 3a and 5a), increased for the AS2-601-II-5fC and III-5fC nucleosomes compared with the case of the AS2-601-UnM nucleosome (Fig. 3c). By contrast, 5fC introduced at site IV stabilized Pos-1 (Fig. 3c), in which the histone core was completely positioned on the 601 region (Figs. 3a and 5a). When the locations of 5fC at Pos-1 and Pos-2 were virtually mapped on the nucleosome, 5fC, which stabilizes the corresponding nucleosome positions, was found to be located around the SHL ± 1.5–2 (site III of Pos-2) and ±3.5–4 (site II of Pos-2 and site IV of Pos-1) (Fig. 5b). If SHL ± 1.5–2 or ±3.5–4 is the hotspot of the cross-linking to stabilize the corresponding nucleosome positions, efficient cross-linking should be observed for AS2-601-III-5fC or AS2-601-II-5fC and AS2-601-IV-5fC, respectively. To test this hypothesis, nonfluorescent nucleosomes (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15) were subjected to reductive amination using NaBH4 to convert the reversible imine into an irreversible amine. The cross-linked products were then examined with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-PAGE followed by SYBR Gold staining, which yielded strong bands above the free DNA for AS2-601-II-5fC and AS2-601-IV-5fC (Fig. 5c). These bands disappeared upon proteinase K treatment, proving that they are DNA–histone conjugates (Supplementary Fig. 16). The efficiencies of cross-linking of AS2-601-II-5fC and AS2-601-IV-5fC were almost the same and were the highest among the six nucleosomes (Fig. 5c). This observation indicates that SHL ± 3.5–4 is a hotspot for cross-linking between 5fC and histones. The major groove around SHL ± 3.5 is open to the N- and C-terminal tails of H2A and H2B (Fig. 5d), which is likely to make 5fC more accessible to these histone units. The 5fC-histone cross-link near SHL ± 3.5, accordingly, should be an important factor in changing the relative stability of nucleosome positions upon formylation.

When it comes to AS2-601-III-5fC, 5fC at the –16 position included in site III of the Pos-2 nucleosome (Fig. 5b) was previously reported to cross-link with the N-terminal tail of histone H4 at 20% efficiency by a similar evaluation in vitro35. However, the cross-linking efficiency of AS2-601-III-5fC was unexpectedly less than that of AS2-601-II-5fC and AS2-601-IV-5fC (Fig. 5c), despite the previous report above and the large change in the ratio between Pos-1 and Pos-2 from that of AS2-601-UnM (Fig. 3c). The possible factor that might cause this apparent low cross-linking efficiency of AS2-601-III-5fC is the difference in the local DNA sequence around 5fC: 5fC at the –16 position of 601 DNA was flanked by T and A in the previous study35, whereas C and G are neighboring to that of AS2-601-III-5fC. Given that the C-G base pair is more stable than the A-T base pair and the base-stacking at CpG and GpC steps is reported to be stronger than that at TpC and CpA steps56, it is plausible that the local dynamics around 5fC at the –16 position is different between the two 601 DNA sequences to affect their accessibility and the concomitant reactivity to histone H4. The discrepancy between the degree of the position change and the cross-linking efficiency observed for AS2-601-III-5fC strongly suggests the existence of an important determinant other than cross-linking behind the 5fC-mediated alteration of the nucleosome positions.

Nucleobase stacking interactions favored or tolerated by CpG formylation at a specific location on the nucleosome

We examined the effect of cytosine formylation on the interactions between nucleobases to gain insight into the mechanism by which formylation of 5′-CGCG-3′ at site III induces the change in nucleosome positions (Fig. 6). Previous studies have shown that the DNA sequence is one of the most important determinants of the nucleosome position57–59. As a typical observation, periodic enrichment of specific bp steps is observed in nucleosomal DNA sequences at approximately 10 bp intervals58–61. For example, AT-rich sequences pay a smaller energetic penalty than GC-rich sequences for bending DNA into a minor groove and are thereby favored in the minor groove blocks, in which the minor groove of DNA contacts the histone core, appearing with 10 bp periodicity58. These observations suggest that nucleosomes are positioned on the sequence whose base step pattern is the most energetically compatible with the structural pattern of DNA on the nucleosome. Given that nucleosome positioning is determined by the correspondence between the structural pattern of the nucleosomal DNA and that tolerated by the DNA sequence, it is plausible that cytosine formylation at site III drove the position change of the AS2-601-III-5fC nucleosome by rendering the structural properties of 5′-CGCG-3′ more compatible with the configuration at site III of Pos-2 over that of Pos-1. Two factors dominantly determine the compatibility of DNA with given conformations: (1) the interaction energy between two nucleobases forming a Watson–Crick bp (Einter,BP), and (2) the stacking energy between a pair of two adjacent bp (Estack)62. To investigate the interaction energy associated with the characteristic positional preference of the AS2-601-III-5fC nucleosome, we calculated the interaction energies (1) and (2) of possible sets of nucleobases in unmodified and formylated 5′-CGCG-3′ using the double-stranded 5′-CGCG-3′ modeled with the conformations at site III of Pos-1 (Pos-1-III) and Pos-2 (Pos-2-III). The energy calculation was performed by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. We used B97M-V as a density functional, which was reported to exhibit superior performance for inter-base interactions56, and the balanced polarized triple-zeta basis set (def2-TZVD). In all calculations, counterpoise correction was applied to remove basis set superposition error.

First, we evaluated Einter,BP for unmodified and formylated 5′-CGCG-3′, and the degree of stabilization brought by formylation (ΔEinter,BP = Einter,BP [formylated] – Einter,BP [unmodified]) for Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III. The structures of 5′-CGCG-3′ were modeled for Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III using Web 3DNA2.0 server63 by applying the intra-base pair (Intra-BP) and inter-base pair (Inter-BP) parameters that were extracted using CURVES+ software64 and averaged over three reported structures of the 601 nucleosome (Protein Data Bank [PDB]: 7VZ4, 8JBX65, and 8YBJ66) (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). The calculated ΔEinter,BP of each bp in 5′-CGCG-3′ was positive for both Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III, indicating that the C-G bp is destabilized by cytosine formylation (Supplementary Fig. 19). The calculation result was consistent with previous studies reporting that the electron-withdrawing formyl group weakens base-pairing33,34. However, the difference in ΔEinter,BP between Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III was only 0.06 kJ/mol in total for the four bp of 5′-CGCG-3′ (Supplementary Fig. 19). The small difference in ΔEinter,BP indicates that the formylation of 5′-CGCG-3′ destabilizes base-pairing at Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III to the same degree, suggesting that the formylation is neither favorable nor unfavorable for Pos-2-III over Pos-1-III with respect to base-pairing. The minor impact of cytosine formylation on the structure dependency of base-pairing stability is also supported by a calculation showing small differences in ΔEinter,BP, 1 kJ/mol at most, within the ranges of the Intra-BP parameters observed for Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III (Supplementary Fig. 20).

We calculated the stacking energy between adjacent pairs of bp (Estack,4bases) as another important factor that affects DNA stability (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. 21). In contrast to the case of base-pairing interaction (Supplementary Fig. 19), there was an obvious difference in the degree of stabilization of base-stacking upon formylation (ΔEstack,4bases = Estack,4bases(formylated) – Estack,4bases(Unmodified)) between Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III. Specifically, the total difference in the ΔEstack,4bases values between Pos-2-III and Pos-1-III (ΔΔEstack,4bases = ΔEstack,4bases(Pos-2-III) – ΔEstack,4bases(Pos-1-III)) was –6.53 kJ/mol (Fig. 6a, b). In more detail, ΔΔEstack,4bases values for the first CpG step (CpG-1: –2.05 kJ/mol) and the second GpC step (GpC-2: –4.88 kJ/mol) accounted for most of the total value, indicating that DNA structures characteristic of CpG-1 and GpC-2 at Pos-2-III are favored when formylated (Fig. 6a, b).

We explored the structural features of Pos-2-III that were different from those of Pos-1-III and favored by cytosine formylation. To specify the substructure that accounts for the negative ΔΔEstack,4bases, we calculated the stacking energies between the pairs of nucleobases involved in the CpG-1 and GpC-2 steps (ΔEstack,1–2, ΔEstack,3–4 and ΔEstack,GG) of Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III (Fig. 6b). In the CpG-1 step, ΔΔEstack calculated for C3 and G4 (ΔΔEstack,3–4 = –0.93 kJ/mol) accounted for the largest part of ΔΔEstack,4bases (Fig. 6b), indicating that the change in relative configuration between C3 and G4 accompanied by the shift from Pos-1-III to Pos-2-III was particularly favored by formylation. The notable structural difference in the CpG-1 step between Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III is the degree of tilt of G4 toward G2 that narrows their gap (Fig. 6c, top). The distance between the nitrogen atom of the 2-amino group of G4 and carbon 2 of G2 is 2.9 Å in the CpG-1 step of Pos-1-III, which is narrower than the typical distance between the stacked bases (3.4 Å) of B-form DNA (Fig. 6d, top). Conversely, the aromatic plane of G4 at the opposite side of paired C1 is tilted away from C3 (Fig. 6 d, top). This tilt of G4 is relieved in the conformation of Pos-2-III (Fig. 6 d, bottom), so G4 and C3 get spatially closer. This parallel alignment of G4 and C3 with the appropriate distance is likely to be strongly advantageous to a formylated C3 (Fig. 6b). The stabilization of the stacking interaction enhanced by cytosine formylation is consistent with previous studies reporting that impairment of π-electron delocalization increases the energy gain of the π–π stacking associated with the change to an energetically favorable configuration67,68. This stabilization arises because the π electrons are depleted on 5fC by the electron-withdrawing nature of the 5-formyl group and its intramolecular hydrogen bonding with the amino proton at the carbon 4 position34. In addition to the C3-G4 stacking case, ΔΔEstack calculated for C1-G2 stacking (ΔΔEstack,1-2 = –0.47 kJ/mol) (Fig. 6b) was also negative. The structural difference between the C1-G2 pair of Pos-2-III and that of Pos-1-III was the lateral drift of C1 relative to G2 toward the center of the bp (Fig. 6e). The energy gain in the stacking interaction associated with the C1 drift also seems advantageous to 5fC.

In the case of GpC-2, there was little difference in the relative alignment of G3 and C4 between Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III (Fig. 6c, bottom). Conversely, that of G1 and C2 was different between the two configurations, which are represented by the large drift of G1 relative to C2 at Pos-2-III, typically associated with the positive Inter-BP slide value (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Fig. 18). This change in the molecular configuration assumably weakens the interaction between G1 and unmodified C2 because of the loss of overlap between the hydrogen at carbon 5 of C2 and the π orbital of G1, and their associated van der Waals interaction (Fig. 6f, top). By contrast, the same configuration change does not reduce the overall interaction energy between G1 and formylated C2 as much as the case of G1 and unmodified C2, probably because the loss of their interaction is compensated by the displaced alignment67 between the five-membered ring of the G1 purine and the exocyclic π orbitals of C2 involving its 5-formyl group (Fig. 6f, bottom). Indeed, ΔΔEstack,1-2 between G1 and C2 was calculated to be –4.23 kJ/mol, accounting for most of ΔΔEstack,4bases (–4.88 kJ/mol) of the GpC-2 step (Fig. 6b). Thus, the formyl group mitigates the destabilization of the base-stacking interaction associated with the drift of nucleobases at the GpC step. Taken together, the structure of SHL ± 1.5, which is characterized by the planar and positively sliding alignment at the CpG and GpC steps relative to those at SHL ± 6–6.5, is energetically favored by the formylated 5′-CGCG-3′ over the unmodified one. Thus, the stability of base-stacking between C and G is likely to be one of the key factors in the control of nucleosome positioning mediated by 5fC.

Discussion

Three chemical properties of 5fC that are important to nucleosome formation and organization have been proposed previously: it imparts flexibility to DNA32, it mediates cross-linking with histones via Schiff base formation27, and it recruits specific protein binders, including chromatin remodelers. These effects of cytosine formylation have been thought to promote nucleosome formation and to increase nucleosome occupancy in cells. However, the mechanism by which chromatin remains highly accessible despite high nucleosome occupancy at 5fC-enriched sites remains elusive. Nucleosome positioning is a key factor to investigate because it is associated with nucleosome phasing, the structural compactness of chromatin, and the openness of genomic elements40–42. Related to this issue, previous studies reported that 5fC can recruit a specific chromatin remodeler that may support the alteration of nucleosome positions triggered by 5fC. By contrast, from the chemical point of view, we found that 5fC alters nucleosome positions by favoring specific locations on nucleosomes, presumably depending on cross-linking with histone (Fig. 5) and the characteristics of DNA structures at the locations (Fig. 6), whereas 5mC was likely to be located at the entry site or outside the nucleosome (Fig. 3b, c). It is thereby clear that 5fC and 5mC have distinctive impacts on the positional preference of nucleosomes. From a physiological viewpoint, 5mC and 5fC also play opposing roles: 5mC is generally repressive for gene expression, whereas 5fC is often associated with gene activation2,11,22. The characteristic effects of 5fC on nucleosome positions might provide insights into the mechanisms by which 5fC accumulated at specific enhancers and promoters activates genes during cellular reprogramming and embryonic development. Related to this point, nucleosomes phased at regular intervals have been found densely retained at tissue-specific enhancers38,69. The phased nucleosome array at tissue-specific enhancers may serve as a scaffold for “pioneer transcription factors (TFs)” represented by FoxA, which can recognize their target sequences on nucleosomal DNA for gene activation38. Given that 5fC is enriched at tissue-specific enhancer sites in embryonic cells20,27,28, 5fC may assist in the positioning and phasing of nucleosomes at enhancer sites to make them more accessible to pioneer TFs during embryonic development. Further, it was recently revealed that 5fC in gene bodies and promoters of Pol III target genes can promote the accumulation of proteins required for transcription during zygotic gene activation31. Given that the positions of nucleosomes are precisely controlled to regulate protein accessibility around the transcription start site42, 5fC might also be a potential regulator of nucleosome positioning around CpG-containing promoters for tissue-specific gene activation.

5hmC represents another pivotal epigenetic modification, serving as a key intermediate in the oxidative pathway from 5mC to 5fC.2,16 Its abundance in tissues is approximately 100–1000 times greater than that of 5fC.2 Similar to 5fC, 5hmC is thought to be involved in the reactivation of gene expression previously silenced by 5mC. Mechanistically, it has been reported that 5hmC enhances DNA flexibility and increases its affinity for nucleosomes.32 Consequently, 5hmC and 5fC are considered to share significant overlap in their biological and chemical functions. However, distinct differences exist between their functional roles. From a biochemical perspective, evidence suggests that 5hmC possesses fewer specific binder proteins compared with 5fC.29 Furthermore, 5hmC tends to inhibit the binding of 5mC-recognizing proteins, functioning prominently as an anti-binding modification that excludes these proteins.70 By contrast, numerous specific binders have been identified for 5fC, including various chromatin remodelers and TFs belonging to the FOX family.29 Thus, although 5hmC acts primarily as a marker to disrupt repressive environments by excluding 5mC-recognition proteins, 5fC is inferred to be a more proactive factor that accelerates gene readout by recruiting factors essential for gene expression. Moreover, clear differences emerge from a chemical standpoint regarding the regulation of nucleosome formation and positioning. First, 5hmC lacks the ability, seen in 5fC, to form covalent cross-links with histones. Second, although the formyl group of 5fC is electron-withdrawing, the hydroxymethyl group of 5hmC is electron-donating. Therefore, these modifications are expected to exert entirely different effects on base-stacking, which is closely linked to the electron density of nucleobases. Given these considerations, the regulation of nucleosome positioning mediated by 5fC, through histone cross-linking and the modulation of base-stacking, should be fundamentally distinct from that of 5hmC. In comparison with 5hmC, the study findings highlight the unique functional role of 5fC in controlling nucleosome positioning and gene expression.

The effect of 5fC on the positional preference of the nucleosome was site-dependent (Fig. 3b, c). The 5fC-mediated alteration of nucleosome positions seemed to be driven at least by two distinct mechanisms: cross-link-mediated organization and the modulation of local nucleobase stacking. Independent of cross-linking, the latter mechanism is not a minor effect but is effective enough to induce the change in the nucleosome positions, as shown for the AS2-601-III-5fC nucleosome (Fig. 3b, c). The difference in the DNA structures at SHL ± 6–6.5 and ±1.5–2, on which the formylated cytosines of AS2-601-III-5fC of Pos-1 and Pos-2 are respectively located, provides an important clue to understanding the mechanism. One notable structural feature around SHL ± 1.5 of the nucleosome is the large bend in DNA57,71,72. DNA around SHL ± 1.5 is markedly deformed, probably because of the attractive force from the histone core, from the salt bridges with Arg70 on H3 (Fig. 6g), and the interaction with the H4 tail45. This characteristic structure around the SHL ± 1.5 region, which was typically captured by a positive slide and large curvature at the location (Supplementary Fig. 18), is likely to induce the lateral drift of the alignment of stacked nucleobases (Fig. 6e, f). The DFT calculation of stacking energies between G and C suggested that their configuration at SHL ± 1.5 was favored over that at SHL ± 6.5 when CpG was formylated (Fig. 6a, b). To support this idea, the energy calculation shows that formylation of cytosine imparts tolerance to the lateral drift of C against G with positive slides at both the CpG and GpC steps with respect to the stacking interaction (Supplementary Fig. 22). Conversely, the slide is negative to zero at SHL ± 6–6.5 (Supplementary Fig. 18), where the stabilization of base-stacking brought by formylation is expected to be small. In addition, the large difference in the buckle angle between adjacent nucleobases characteristic of SHL ± 6–6.5 (Supplementary Fig. 17) might perturb the planar alignment of adjacent C and G residues stabilized more strongly by formylation (Fig. 6b, d). Thus, the 5fC-mediated stabilization of base-stacking with planar alignment and a positively sliding configuration of stacked C and G may account for one important chemical mechanism underlying the change in the positional preference of nucleosomes mediated by 5fC.

By contrast, the changes in the nucleosome positions were strongly associated with cross-linking with histone for AS2-601-II-5fC and AS2-601-IV-5fC (Fig. 5c). From the perspective of DNA structure as discussed for AS2-601-III-5fC, the slide value is positive at site IV of Pos-2 (Supplementary Fig. 18). The total ΔΔEstack,4bases of 5′-CGCG-3′ calculated for site IV was –4.8 kJ/mol (Supplementary Fig. 23), implying that cytosine formylation at site IV is advantageous to Pos-2 from the perspective of base-stacking. However, Pos-1 was found to be the dominant position of the AS2-601-IV-5fC nucleosome by Exo III position mapping (Fig. 3c). Given the high cross-linking efficiency of the AS2-601-IV-5fC nucleosome over that of the AS2-601-III-5fC nucleosome, the stabilization of Pos-1, through covalent but reversible cross-linking with histones, transcended the effect of 5fC on base-stacking interaction. The association between cross-linking and preferred positions was also observed for AS2-601-II-5fC, in which 5fC was located outside the 601 region. As confirmed by the Exo III position mapping of the AS2-601-I-5fC nucleosome, 5fC on AS2 (or off 601) did not necessarily increase the preference for Pos-2 over Pos-1, but decreased the proportion of the nucleosome positioned at Pos-2 (the AS2 side) (Supplementary Fig. 24). Though the base-stacking of the formylated 5′-CGCG-3′ at site II of Pos-2 is favorable over that at site I of Pos-2 by –1.22 kJ/mol (Supplementary Fig. 25), this energy difference does not seem sufficient to account for the large difference in the percentage of Pos-2 between the AS2-601-I-5fC and AS2-601-II-5fC nucleosomes (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 24). Thus, the ease of 5fC-histone cross-linking near SHL ± 3.5 might be a key to driver of the position change of the AS2-601-II-5fC nucleosome (Fig. 5c).

Collectively, it is plausible that 5fC regulates nucleosome positions precisely by cooperative and/or competitive balances between histone–DNA cross-linking and the modulation of nucleobase stacking. However, whether 5fC generally prefers to be located at SHL ± 1.5 and ±3.5, even in sequences other than AS2-601, as observed in this study, remains to be investigated. The DNA–histone cross-linking and the modulation of base-stacking proposed to underlie 5fC-mediated alteration of nucleosome positions are inherently dependent on the local DNA sequence and its structural characteristics. It is also known that the local DNA structure at the same location on a nucleosome can vary depending on the sequence of nucleosome-forming DNA. Consequently, the efficiency of DNA–histone cross-linking and the modulation of base-stacking mediated by 5fC may differ from one sequence to another, which would lead to sequence-dependent variation in the location of nucleosomal DNA preferred by 5fC. Investigating how the effects of 5fC on nucleosome positions differ across various sequences would further deepen understanding of the general principles of 5fC-mediated regulation of nucleosome positions.

We observed little emergence of new positions upon formylation that were not detectable on the unmodified DNA, regardless of the 5fC location. The formylation of four copies of cytosine in a single CGCG sequence seems unable to stabilize new positions over the positions originally programmed on the unmodified DNA sequence. Although there is still a possibility that massive formylation at multiple sites on the nucleosomal DNA can stabilize new positions strongly, the essence of 5fC functions may be the regulation of the relative stability of the positions programmed on given DNA sequences. Related to this point, the precise repositioning of nucleosomes between OFF and ON states in promoter and enhancer regions is vital to the appropriate switching of gene expression in cells73–75. The 5fC-mediated control of nucleosome positioning may be energetically well balanced to enable precise control of OFF and ON positioning without allowing abnormal mispositioning that would perturb the proper regulation of transcription.

In summary, this study demonstrates that DNA formylation is a chemical cue that modulates nucleosome positional preference on a given DNA sequence. The positional changes induced by 5fC are associated not only with site-selective cross-linking to histones via Schiff base formation as described previously27,35, but also with relative enhancement of local base-stacking at specific locations on nucleosomal DNA. The alteration of the positional preference of the nucleosome that depends on histone–DNA cross-linking and/or nucleobase stacking modulated by 5fC provides a plausible mechanism for 5fC-mediated repositioning. Notably, 5fC stabilizes nucleosome positions in a site-dependent manner, particularly when they are located around SHL ± 1.5–2 and 3.5–4 in the case of 601-AS2 examined here. These positions are clearly different from those stabilized by 5mC, which is more likely to localize at the entry site or outside the nucleosome. The site-dependent abilities of 5fC to stabilize the nucleosome identified here provide insight into how 5fC regulates nucleosome positioning at promoters and tissue-specific enhancers, processes that are essential for gene activation during cellular reprogramming and embryonic development.

Methods

General information

Reagents and solvents used for organic synthesis were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Tokyo Chemical Industry, Kanto Chemical, and Sigma-Aldrich, and used without further purification. Phosphoramidite compounds used for DNA synthesis were purchased from Glen Research. Unmodified and methylated DNA fragments used to synthesize 601-AS1 and AS2-601 were purchased from FASMAC. Immobilized metal affinity chromatography for the purification of expressed histone units was performed using an ÄKTA purifier 10 (Cytiva). The final purification of the histone units by reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) was also performed on an HPLC system comprising JASCO modules (photo diode array detector: MD-4015, RHPLC pump: PU-4180, Software: ChromNAV, version 2) using a Cosmosil Protein R packed column (Nacalai Tesque, 20 mm × 150 mm). The analysis and purification of oligonucleotides by RP-HPLC was also performed on the HPLC system using Cosmosil 5C18-MS-II packed columns (Nacalai Tesque, 3.0 mm, 4.6 mm, and 10 mm × 150 mm). Concentrations of proteins and those of oligonucleotides, DNA, and nucleosomes were determined by the absorbance at 280 and 260 nm, respectively, as measured on a NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific) instrument. Mass spectrometry was performed using a microflex-NAC (Bruker) for matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), and a microTOF II-NAC (Bruker) for electrospray ionization (ESI)-MS. Nucleosome concentrations were expressed as DNA concentrations that were determined by absorbance at 260 nm, measured by the NanoDrop One.

Ligation of DNA fragments to synthesize 601-AS1

Three groups of DNA fragments (Group 1: 5′-FAM TOPi-1, TOPi-2, and BOTi-1; Group 2: TOPi-3, BOTi-2, and BOTi-3; Group 3: TOPi-4, BOTi-4, BOTi-5, and BOTi-6) (Supplementary Fig. 2) were mixed separately in 150 μL of 1× ligation buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.9, 10 mM MgCl2, 20 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 1 mM ATP) (Takara). Tubes containing the DNA fragments of Groups 1–3 were heated at 80 °C for 20 min and cooled gradually to room temperature for 2 h. Then, 0.75 μL of T4 DNA ligase (500 U/μL, Takara) and 1.5 μL of 100 mM ATP were added to the mixture, which was held to react at 16 °C for 3 h. Next, the mixtures containing the three different groups of DNA fragments were combined into a 1.5 mL tube. The resulting solution was heated at 90 °C for 20 min and then gradually cooled to room temperature over 2 h. Next, 4.5 μL of T4 DNA ligase (500 U/μL, Takara) and 4.5 μL of 100 mM ATP were added, and the mixture was maintained at 16 °C for 16 h. To this was added 50 μL of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) and 1 mL ethanol (EtOH), followed by cooling at –20 °C for 1 h. The tubes were centrifuged (12,000 × g) at 4 °C for 30 min to precipitate DNA, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was then dissolved in 50 μL of Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0 and 0.1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA]) and mixed with 60 μL of 2× urea loading buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA and 8 M urea). After the solution was heated at 70 °C for 10 min, DNA fragments were resolved by 8% urea-denaturing PAGE. The desired 192 bp DNA in the gel was detected by UV shadowing, and the gel fragment was dissected into a 1.5 mL tube. DNA was then extracted by incubating in 600 μL of TE buffer at 37 °C for 6–14 h. The extraction process was repeated three times, and the supernatants containing extracted DNA were combined in a 15 mL plastic tube. After freeze-drying the DNA solution to remove water, the remnant was dissolved in 450 μL of TE buffer and transferred to a 1.5 mL tube. After 50 μL of 3 M AcONa and 1 mL EtOH were added, the mixture was cooled at –20 °C for 1 h. The DNA was precipitated by centrifugation (12,000 × g) for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was rinsed twice with ice-cold 70% ethanol. The precipitate was then dried in a centrifugal evaporator (CVE-2200; Eyela) for 10 min and dissolved in 20 μL of TE buffer. The final DNA product was annealed by heating at 90 °C for 20 min and gradually cooled to room temperature over 2 h and characterized by 8% denaturing and native PAGE (Supplementary Fig. 2). The sequences and modification sites of the DNA fragments used to synthesize unmodified, formylated, and methylated 601-AS1 are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Ligation of DNA fragments to synthesize AS2-601

Three groups of DNA fragments (Group 1: TOPii-0, BOTii-0, and BOTii-1; Group 2: TOPii-1, TOPii-2, and BOTii-2; Group 3: TOPii-3, TOPii-4, BOTii-3, BOTii-4, and 5′-FAM BOTii-5) (Supplementary Fig. 9) were mixed separately in 300 μL of 1× ligation buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.9, 10 mM MgCl2, 20 mM DTT, 1 mM ATP). Tubes containing the mixture of DNA fragments were heated at 80 °C for 20 min and cooled to room temperature over 2 h. Then, 1.5 μL of T4 DNA ligase (500 U/μL, Takara) and 3.0 μL of 100 mM ATP were added into the mixture, which was followed by the reaction at 16 °C for 3 h. After the reaction mixtures with the three different groups of DNA fragments were combined, the solution was divided into two 1.5 mL tubes (450 μL each). These solutions were heated at 90 °C for 20 min and gradually cooled to room temperature. T4 DNA ligase (2.3 μL, 500 U/μL) and 4.5 μL of 100 mM ATP were added, and the solution was maintained at 16 °C for 16 h. The resulting AS2-601 was purified as described for 601-AS1. The purified DNA was then dissolved in 40 μL of TE buffer and annealed by heating at 90 °C for 20 min and cooling to room temperature. The characterization of the product was performed by 8% denaturing and native PAGE (Supplementary Fig. 9 for FAM-labeled AS2-601 containing 5fC; Supplementary Fig. 10 for FAM-labeled AS2-601 containing 5mC; Supplementary Fig. 14 for AS2-601 containing 5fC with no fluorescent label). The sequences of the DNA fragments used to synthesize AS2-601 are provided in Supplementary Figs. 8, 9 and 14.

Nucleosome reconstitution

601-AS1 and AS2-601 between 1 and 3 μM were individually mixed with 1.0 (601-AS1) and 1.2 (601-AS2) molar equivalents of refolded histone octamer, otherwise indicated, in 40 μL of a Tris buffer mixture comprising 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer pH 7.5, 1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME) and 2 M NaCl, and then transferred into a dialysis cup with a 7 kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane (Thermo Fisher). The first round of dialysis was performed at 4 °C, starting with over 500 mL of a Tris buffer containing 2 M NaCl (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 2 M NaCl, and 1 mM 2-ME), which was gradually diluted by adding a Tris buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 and 1 mM 2-ME) at 0.5 mL/min up to 1 L using a peristaltic pump. The NaCl concentration was reduced to 1 M by this process. After the Tris dilution buffer was consumed, the volume of the exterior buffer was reduced to 250 mL. The concentration of NaCl was further lowered by continuous dilution of the exterior buffer with 750 mL of the Tris dilution buffer again at the flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The volume was next reduced to 200 mL, and dialysis was continued with the Tris dilution buffer up to 1 L. Finally, the dialysis cup holding the mixture of DNA and histone octamer was transferred into 500 mL of the Tris dilution buffer and dialyzed twice at 4 °C over 2 h. The solution containing reconstituted nucleosomes was collected into a 1.5 mL plastic tube and analyzed by 6% native PAGE (29:1) using 0.5× Tris-borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer. After PAGE, the gel was imaged by detecting fluorescence from FAM on the DNA without staining and/or from GelRed after 30 min staining in 1× TBE buffer containing GelRed at a 1:20,000 dilution rate.

Nucleosome position mapping of 601-AS1 nucleosomes

A solution (8 μL) containing a FAM-labeled test nucleosome (601-AS1-UnM, 5mC or 5fC) (40 ng/μL) and a Cy5-labeled control nucleosome (Cy5-601-AS1-UnM) (10 ng/μL) was mixed with 2 μL of 5× Exo III solution on ice. This 5 × Exo III solution comprised 250 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 25 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT, and 2 U/μL Exo III (Takara). After mixing by repeated pipetting, the solution was held at 20 °C for 0–20 min. At the chosen times (10, 15, and 20 min), 2.3 μL aliquots of the reaction mixture were mixed with 1 μL of 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8.0) to stop the reaction. Next, 0.5 μL of proteinase K solution (1 mg/mL proteinase K [Kanto Chemical], 0.1% SDS) was added to each aliquot and warmed to 37 °C for 1 h to deproteinate the nucleosomes. The resulting solution was mixed with 4 μL of 2 × urea loading buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, and 8 M urea), and the DNA fragments were resolved by 8% urea-denaturing PAGE using a warmed PAGE gel containing 8 M urea. The resolved DNA fragments derived from the FAM-labeled test nucleosome and the Cy5-labeled control nucleosome were detected by Typhoon FLA 9500 (Cytiva) using a blue laser diode (LD) excitation laser (473 nm) and long-pass blue filter for the FAM-labeled fragments and a red LD excitation laser (635 nm) and long-pass red filter for the Cy5-labeled fragments. The position mapping shown in Fig. 1 was replicated by three independent experiments using a single nucleosome stock solution for each nucleosome group. The band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Nucleosome position mapping of AS2-601 nucleosomes

A nucleosome solution (16 μL at 50 ng/μL) was mixed with 4 μL of 5 × Exo III solution on ice. This 5 × Exo III solution comprised 250 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 25 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT, and 2 U/μL Exo III (Takara). After mixing by repeated pipetting, the solution was held at 20 °C for 0–60 min. At the chosen times, 4.5 μL aliquots of the reaction mixture were mixed with 1 μL of 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8.0) to stop the reaction. Next, 0.5 μL of proteinase K solution (1 mg/mL proteinase K [Kanto Chemical], 0.1% SDS) was added to each aliquot and warmed to 37 °C for 1 h to deproteinate the nucleosomes. The resulting solution was mixed with 6 μL of 2× urea loading buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA and 8 M urea), and the DNA fragments were resolved by 8% urea-denaturing PAGE using a warmed PAGE gel containing 8 M urea. The resolved DNA fragments were visualized by detecting fluorescence from FAM modified at 5′-end of bottom strand without a staining process using a GelDoc Go Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The position mapping shown in Fig. 3 was replicated by four independent experiments using a single nucleosome stock solution for each nucleosome group. The band intensity was quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Evaluation of enzyme accessibility to AS2-601 DNA and nucleosomes

A nucleosome or DNA solution (12 μL at 63 ng/μL) was mixed with 3 μL of 5 × NEB buffer 3.1 (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.9, 50 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 50 μg/mL bovine serum albumin) (New England Biolabs, NEB) containing 2 U/μL BstXI (NEB) on ice. After mixing by gentle pipetting on ice, the solution was held at 20 °C for 0–90 min. At the chosen times, 3.5 μL aliquots of the reaction mixture were sampled and mixed with 1 μL of 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8.0) to stop the reaction. Then, 0.5 μL of proteinase K solution was added to the mixture to deproteinate the nucleosome at 37 °C for 1 h. The resulting solution was mixed with 1 μL of 6 × loading buffer (for 601-AS1), or 5 μL of 2× urea loading buffer (for AS2-601), and DNA fragments were resolved by 8% native (601-AS1) or urea-denaturing PAGE (AS2-601). The resolved DNA fragments were visualized by detecting fluorescence from FAM modified at 5′-end of bottom strand without a staining process using a GelDoc Go Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The BstXI accessibility assay shown in Fig. 3 was replicated by three independent experiments using a single nucleosome stock solution for each nucleosome group. The band intensity was quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Evaluation of cross-linking between DNA and histone

A nucleosome solution (10 μL at 20 ng/μL) was transferred into a dialysis cup and dialyzed over 500 mL of 10 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.6) at 4 °C for 2 h. The nucleosome solution was then collected into a 0.2 mL plastic tube and held at 37 °C for 13 h. After NaBH4 was added to the solution to a final concentration of 100 mM, the reduction reaction was conducted at 37 °C for 3 h. This was then mixed with 4 × SDS loading buffer (240 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 8% [w/v] SDS, 40% [v/v] glycerol, 5% [v/v] 2-mercaptoethanol). The DNA and DNA cross-linked with histone were resolved by 12% SDS-PAGE (29:1). The gel was stained with SYBR Gold at a 1:20,000 dilution in 1 × TBE, and the bands were visualized by a GelDoc Go Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The assay of cross-linking efficiency was replicated by three independent experiments for AS2-601-I-5fC and four independent experiments for the others using a single nucleosome stock solution for each nucleosome group. The band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Modeling of DNA structures

In this study, Intra-BP (shear, stagger, stretch, propeller-twist, buckle, and opening) and Inter-BP (roll, slide, shift, helical-twist, tilt, and rise) parameters were calculated by CURVE+ web server64 from three reported structures of nucleosomes reconstituted using human histone units and Widom 601 DNA. These were PDB: 8JBX65, 8YBJ66, and 7VZ4. For each nucleosome, the parameters were calculated from two opposite directions and averaged. The parameters averaged for each bp and bp step were further averaged over the three distinctive structures to yield sixfold mean values. Then, the averaged Intra-BP and Inter-BP parameters at sites I–IV of Pos-1 and Pos-2 were applied to rebuild the DNA structures at each location using the Web 3DNA2.0 server.63 The actual sequences at sites I–IV of Pos-1 and Pos-2 are provided in Supplementary Figs 17 and 18 and Supplementary Table 1. The coordinates of the atoms in the modeled DNA structures were downloaded in PDB file format and visualized using UCSF Chimera software.

DFT calculation of interaction energies between nucleobases

The models of DNA bp for the calculation were constructed by removing the sugar and phosphate backbone atoms from the full DNA structures modeled in the “Modeling of DNA structures” section and adding hydrogen atoms at appropriate positions on the nucleobases. Addition and deletion of atoms on the modeled DNA and subsequent DFT calculations were conducted using the Spartan 18 package (Wavefunction). For each bp, the geometry of exocyclic atoms was optimized using the B97M-V functional and the def2-SVP basis set. During the optimization, the endocyclic atoms within the purine and pyrimidine rings of each nucleobase were constrained to their initial positions, whereas the positions of the exocyclic atoms and the added hydrogen atoms were allowed to relax. Single-point energy calculations were subsequently performed on the optimized geometries of the respective bp using the B97M-V functional and the def2-TZVP basis set to evaluate the base-pairing interaction energies. The base-pairing energy between “X” and “Y” (Einter,BP(X:Y)) was calculated by subtracting the energies of individual bases (E(X) and E(Y)) from the total energy calculated for the bp (E(X:Y)) according to Eq. (1).

To calculate the interaction energies between stacked nucleobases, the individual bp whose geometry of the exocyclic atoms was optimized above were rearranged into the template DNA structure, which was modeled in the “Modeling of DNA structures” section, by superimposing their purine and pyrimidine rings using PyMOL software. Single-point energy calculations were then conducted on the respective pairs of the adjacent nucleobases of interest using the B97M-V functional and the def2-TZVP basis set. The stacking energies between a pair of bp “X1–Y1” and “X2–Y2” (Estack,4bases) were calculated by subtracting the energies of the individual bp (E(X1-Y1) and E(X2–Y2)) from the total energy calculated for the pair of bp involving both “X1–Y1” and “X2–Y2” (E4bases), as described in Eq. (2).

The stacking energies between two adjacent nucleobases X1 and X2 (Estack(X1:X2)) were calculated similarly from Eq. (3).

In all calculations, the basis set superposition error was corrected using the counterpoise method.

Structural comparison of base pairs at CpG and GpC steps

Superposition of nucleobases (Fig. 6c, e, f) was performed using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System 3.0.0 (Schrödinger). First, the model structures of the CpG-1 or GpC-2 steps in Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III were displayed using the software. Next, for one of the four bases within each base step, C3 for the upper panel of Fig. 6c, G4 for the lower panel of Fig. 6c, G2 for Fig. 6e, and C2 for Fig. 6f; three endocyclic atoms were selected, and the corresponding atoms in the Pos-1-III and Pos-2-III structural models were fitted to occupy the same coordinates using the Pair Fitting function in PyMOL. Through this procedure, partially superposed base structures were obtained (as shown in Fig. 6c, e, f). The coordinate information for the resulting structures was then exported in a PDB format. Interatomic distances in the model structures (Fig. 6d) were measured using UCSF Chimera 1.18. Specifically, distances between the atom pairs shown in Fig. 6d were calculated using the Tools/Structure Analysis/Distances function. The structures obtained by nucleobase superposition and the model structures used for interatomic distance measurements were visualized using UCSF Chimera 1.18.
