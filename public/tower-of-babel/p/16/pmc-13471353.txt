Analysis of the effect of variable frequency fan on tobacco metabolites during curing via untargeted metabolomics
Authors: Di Shi, Xiaoqiang Zhang, Guanhui Li, Hongfeng Liu, Zhengfa Shi, Jiawei Luo, Xin Jin, Shengjiang Wu, Ning Huang, ZeYu Zhao, Lingli Xie, Kesu Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Variable-frequency (VF) fan regulation is a promising technique for flue-curing of tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.), and exploring its mechanism is of great significance for improving the curing quality. The middle leaves of “Yunyan 87” were used as experimental materials for exploring the changes of metabolic components by untargeted metabolomics and monitoring dynamic changes of key physiological indicators during flue-curing, VF curing (dynamic frequency: 35–40–45–40 Hz) and constant-frequency curing (50 Hz, CK). VF treatment resulted in a more uniform yellowing process, reduced leaf browning, and improved color retention compared with the control. Leaves under VF treatment showed enhanced superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activities, accompanied by lower malondialdehyde (MDA) accumulation. At 48 h, Pathway enrichment analysis revealed the glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism pathway was most affected, with significantly enriched metabolites including isocitrate, citric acid, and glyoxylate. At 120 and 168 h, the most significant metabolic pathways affected by variable frequency fan were flavonoid and flavanol biosynthesis, accompanied significantly enrichment of kaempferol, quercetin, and quercetin. Notably, total aroma substances were reduced under VF (Neophytadiene − 42%, benzaldehyde − 28%). This study provided a metabolomics-based framework for improving intensive curing processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09101-9.

Introduction

Intensive curing barns provide high efficiency and capacity [1, 2], with the circulating fan being central to regulating the internal environment critical for tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) quality [3–5]. However, the resulting high inter-leaf wind speed can cause uneven curing and compromise leaf quality by shortening the yellowing stage [6, 7]. While controlled dehydration stress can benefit this stage [8], VF treatment offers a precise solution. It optimizes fan speed in real-time, providing energy-efficient airflow control to achieve uniform temperature and humidity, which enhances overall tobacco quality [9, 10].

After harvest, tobacco leaves enter a phase of starvation metabolism, where sustained metabolic activity and the degradation of internal reserves become the sole source of energy for physiological processes, as external nutrient supply ceases [11]. Following entry into the curing chamber, tobacco leaves primarily rely on glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle for energy metabolism [12]. From the initial to mid-curing stages, the respiratory metabolism is reprogrammed, characterized by a constrained TCA cycle and a relative enhancement of glycolysis [11, 13]. This metabolic shift accelerates carbon metabolism, ensuring rapid ATP production, supplying critical metabolic intermediates, and generating precursors for stress-related compounds like proline [14]. The intensified metabolic flux may also promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, thereby linking carbohydrate metabolism with oxidative stress during curing [15].

Carbohydrate-related indicators-including starch content, starch-degrading enzyme activity, total sugars, and reducing sugars—exhibit high sensitivity to temperature and moisture conditions during the yellowing stage [1]. Rapid heating or excessive airflow can accelerate starch breakdown and promote sugar loss, thereby altering the carbon substrate supply necessary for pigment degradation and subsequent aroma precursor formation [14, 16, 17]. The regulation of temperature and humidity inevitably affects leaf moisture status, carbon metabolism, and the balance between ROS production and scavenging in tobacco leaves. Therefore, monitoring key physiological indicators such as reducing sugars, MDA, SOD, POD, and PPO can provide crucial insights into carbohydrate dynamics, lipid peroxidation, and oxidative stress during the curing process [15, 18, 19].

Untargeted metabolomics technology can compensate for the limitations of traditional research methods by analyzing all metabolites present in tobacco and characterizing tobacco metabolomics from a global and overview perspective, which has the advantage of breadth, comprehensiveness, efficiency and convenience [10, 20].

However, how VF treatment affects the metabolic network of tobacco leaves remains unclear, and no study has yet integrated untargeted metabolomics, physiological indicators, and aroma compounds to assess the metabolic consequences of VF treatment. “Yunyan 87”, a widely planted variety in Southwest China, is sensitive to curing temperature and humidity, making it an ideal model for investigating metabolic responses to dynamic microenvironment regulation. With “Yunyan 87” as the experimental material, non-targeted metabolomics was used to profile metabolite changes and to assess how shifts in metabolite abundance correlate with key physiological indicators across critical curing stages. We hypothesized that VF treatment during curing would suppress sugar degradation and mitigate oxidative stress, and that these physiological changes would be reflected in distinct metabolomic shifts, particularly in pathways related to aroma volatiles.

Materials and methods

Material source and experimental design

The experiment was conducted from February 2024 to February 2025. The flue-cured tobacco (N. tabacum) cultivar “Yunyan 87” served as the test material, and seeds were provided by the Guizhou Academy of Tobacco Science. Field trials were carried out at the Xiuwen County Tobacco Planting Base, Guiyang, Guizhou Province, China (06°42′E, 26°51′N, altitude ~ 1240 m). Subsequent laboratory analyses were performed at the College of Agriculture, Yangtze University.

The total experimental field area was 34,000 m2, which was divided into three replicate blocks (field replicates) arranged in a randomized complete block design. Row spacing was 110 cm, plant spacing was 55 cm, ridge height ranged from 25 to 30 cm, and ridge width ranged from 80 to 90 cm. Standardized cultivation and management practices based on high-quality flue-cured tobacco production theory were followed. During the 2024 growing season (April to August), the average temperature was 23.5℃, total precipitation was 685 mm, and sunshine duration was 812 h. The soil was classified as yellow loam with the following basic fertility parameters: organic matter 18.6 g/kg, available nitrogen 102 mg/kg, available phosphorus 28.5 mg/kg, and available potassium 156 mg/kg. Nitrogen fertilizer was applied at 90 kg/hm2 (N: P₂O₅: K₂O = 1: 1: 2), with phosphorus and potassium applied as base fertilizer and nitrogen applied as a combination of base and topdressing.

Tobacco leaves were harvested at maturity from the middle position (the 9th to 10th leaf from the top). Immediately after harvest, visibly damaged leaves, leaves showing signs of nutrient deficiency, and leaves exhibiting disease symptoms were discarded. The remaining healthy leaves were hung on rods for sample collection. Fresh leaf samples (Fresh) were collected prior to curing as the baseline control. Curing was performed in dense curing barns equipped with electric heat pumps (dimensions: 8.0 m × 2.7 m × 3.5 m).

The experiment consisted of two treatments: a CK and a VF. For CK, the fan operated at a constant frequency of 50 Hz throughout the three-stage curing process (yellowing, color-fixing, and stem-drying). For VF, the fan frequency was dynamically adjusted according to the dry-bulb temperature. The curing process under VF was divided into four stages: ignition to 38 °C (35 Hz), 40 °C (40 Hz), 42 °C to 48 °C (45 Hz), and 54 °C to 68 °C (40 Hz) (Fig. 1B). Thus, the frequency regime was 35 Hz → 40 Hz → 45 Hz → 40 Hz, corresponding to the key temperature thresholds.

Samples were collected at three critical time points during the curing process: after 48 h (yellowing), after 120 h (color-fixing), and after 168 h (steam-drying). At each sampling time point, six biological replicates (n = 6) were collected per treatment, with two leaves taken from each of the three field replicate blocks. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80℃ until further analysis.

Measurement of physiological indices

Reducing sugar content

Reducing sugar content was determined using the anthrone–sulfuric acid method with glucose as a standard. Tobacco leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 10 mL of distilled water and extracted in a water bath at 95 °C for 30 min. After cooling and centrifugation (10,000 × g, 10 min), 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 5 mL of anthrone–sulfuric acid reagent (0.2% anthrone in concentrated H₂SO₄). The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min, rapidly cooled, and the absorbance was recorded at 630 nm using a spectrophotometer (Synergy H4 Hybrid, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). A standard curve was constructed using known glucose concentrations, and the reducing sugar content was expressed as mg g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW) [21, 22].

Total sugar content

Total sugar content was determined using the anthrone-sulfuric acid method with glucose as a standard. Tobacco leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 10 mL of 80% ethanol and incubated at 80℃ for 30 min. After centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected. The extraction was repeated twice, and the supernatants were combined and diluted to 50 mL with distilled water. An aliquot (1 mL) of the diluted extract was mixed with 5 mL of anthrone-sulfuric acid reagent (0.2% anthrone in concentrated H2SO4), heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min, rapidly cooled, and the absorbance was measured at 630 nm using a spectrophotometer (Synergy H4 Hybrid, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). A standard curve was constructed using glucose as a standard. Total sugar content was expressed as mg/g FW [21].

Starch content

Starch content was determined using the anthrone-sulfuric acid method after removal of soluble sugars. Tobacco leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 10 mL 80% ethanol and incubated at 80℃ for 30 min. After centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded. The extraction was repeated twice to remove soluble sugars. The pellet was then extracted with 5 mL of distilled water and 5 mL of 9.2 mol/L perchloric acid on ice for 30 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 10 min and the supernatant was collected. The extraction was repeated once, and the supernatants were combined and diluted to 50 mL with distilled water. 1 mL of the diluted extract was mixed with 5 mL of anthrone-sulfuric acid reagent (0.2% anthrone in concentrated H2SO4), heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min, rapidly cooled, and the absorbance was measured at 630 nm using a spectrophotometer (Synergy H4 Hybrid, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). A standard curve was constructed using glucose as a standard. Starch content was calculated as glucose equivalents multiplied by 0.90 and expressed as mg/g FW [23].

Amylase activity

Amylase activity was assayed following the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) colorimetric method [24], with extraction and reaction conditions adapted from previous studies on tobacco leaves [25]. Fresh tobacco leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 5 mL of ice-cold phosphate buffer (0.1 mol/L, pH 6.0) and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract. The assay mixture contained 0.5 mL of crude enzyme extract, 0.5 mL of 1% (w/v) soluble starch solution, and 1.0 mL of phosphate buffer (0.1 mol/L, pH 6.0). After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, the reaction was stopped by adding 1.5 mL of DNS reagent [1], followed by heating in a boiling water bath for 5 min. The mixture was cooled to room temperature, diluted with 10 mL of distilled water, and its absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a spectrophotometer (Synergy H4 Hybrid, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). A standard curve was prepared using glucose as a standard. One unit (U) of amylase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that produces 1 µmol of reducing sugar per minute per gram of fresh weight under the assay conditions. Amylase activity was expressed as U/g FW.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content

The MDA concentration, as an indicator of lipid peroxidation, was determined using a modified thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay according to the basic procedure of Heath and Packer [26] with optimizations from Hodges et al. [27]. to correct for interference from sugars and other compounds in tobacco tissue. Briefly, fresh leaf samples (0.5 g) were homogenized in 5 mL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) containing 0.01% butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min. A 2 mL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 2 mL of 0.6% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid (TBA) in 10% TCA (test) or with 2 mL of 10% TCA alone (control). After heating at 100 °C for 30 min and cooling, the absorbance was measured at 440, 532, and 600 nm using a spectrophotometer (Synergy H4 Hybrid, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). The MDA concentration was calculated from the corrected absorbances at 532 and 440 nm using the interference‑correction formula described by Hodges et al. [27]. MDA content was expressed as nmol/g fresh weight.

Antioxidant enzyme activities

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) activity

SOD activity was determined by the inhibition of the photochemical reduction of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT). Leaf tissues (0.5 g) were homogenized in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) containing 1 mM EDTA, 1% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and 0.1 mM Na₂CO₃. The extract was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for enzyme assays. The 3 mL reaction mixture contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 130 mM methionine, 0.75 mM NBT, 0.02 mM riboflavin, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 0.1 mL enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by illumination (4000 lx) for 15 min and terminated by dark incubation. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm, and one unit (U) of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to cause 50% inhibition of NBT reduction [28].

Peroxidase (POD) activity

POD activity was determined following the guaiacol oxidation method. Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was extracted with 5 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 1% PVP and centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C.

The reaction mixture contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 20 mM guaiacol, 40 mM H₂O₂, and 0.1 mL enzyme extract in a total volume of 3 mL. The increase in absorbance at 470 nm due to guaiacol oxidation was recorded for 3 min. One unit of POD activity was defined as a 0.01 increase in absorbance per minute per mg protein [29].

Polyphenol Oxidase (PPO) activity

PPO activity was assayed by monitoring the oxidation of catechol at 420 nm. Leaf samples (0.5 g) were extracted in 5 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) containing 1% PVP, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The 3 mL reaction mixture consisted of 2.8 mL of 0.05 M catechol and 0.2 mL of enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by substrate addition and the increase in absorbance at 420 nm was recorded for 3 min. One unit of PPO activity was defined as a 0.001 ΔA₄₂₀ min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ protein [30].

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Freeze-dried tobacco leaf samples were pulverized using a JXFSTPRP-24 homogenizer (Shanghai Jingxin, China). Approximately 25 mg (± 1 mg) of the resulting powder was accurately weighed into a 2.0 mL microcentrifuge tube containing homogenization beads. Metabolites were extracted with 1000 µL of a pre-cooled methanol: acetonitrile: water (2:2:1, v/v/v) solution containing internal standard L-2-chlorophenylalanine (0.5 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, homogenized at 35 Hz for 4 min, and then sonicated in an ice-water bath for 5 min. This homogenization-sonication cycle was repeated three times. The extracts were incubated at -40 °C for 1 h and then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C (Heraeus Fresco17, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A 400 µL aliquot of the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter plate under 6 psi positive pressure for 120 s. The filtrate was collected for UHPLC-MS/MS analysis. A quality control (QC) sample was prepared by combining equal volumes of filtrate from each sample.

The chromatographic separation was conducted on a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 2.6 μm). The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 2 µL. The mobile phases employed in this study comprised 0.01% acetic acid in water (A) and 2-propanol: acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) (B). The gradient elution program that was utilized is outlined below: The initial phase (0–1 min) was set at 5% B. This was followed by the main phase (1–9 min), during which the percentage of B ranged from 5% to 95%. The third phase (9–10 min) was characterized by a rise in the percentage of B to 95%. The final phase (10–10.1 min) saw a decrease to 5%, followed by a re-equilibration phase (10.1–12 min) at 5% B. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. The mass spectrometric analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with an electrospray ionisation (ESI) source operating in both positive and negative ionisation modes. The data were acquired in a data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. Full MS scans were acquired from m/z 100 to 1500 with a resolution of 60,000. The top three most intense precursor ions were selected for fragmentation with stepped normalized collision energies (NCE) of 20, 40, and 60 eV. MS/MS spectra were collected at a resolution of 15,000. The spray voltage was set to 3.80 kV for positive mode and 3.20 kV for negative mode. The ion transfer tube was set to a temperature of 325 °C, while the vaporizer was set to 350 °C. The flows of sheath, auxiliary, and sweep gases were set to 50, 10, and 1 arbitrary units, respectively.

Raw data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for peak picking, alignment, and integration. Metabolite identification was performed by matching the acquired MS/MS spectra against the BiotreeDB (V3.0) standard library and the BT-Plant (V1.1) plant-specific database. To ensure data quality, a pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing equal aliquots of all samples and injected at regular intervals throughout the analytical run. Features with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% in QC samples were excluded from further analysis. Metabolite identification confidence levels were assigned according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines, including Level 1 (confirmed by authentic standards), Level 2 (putative structure based on MS/MS matching), and Level 3 (putative molecular formula).

Multivariate statistical analysis was performed to visualize metabolic differences between CK and VF groups at each curing stage. Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted using SIMCA 14.1 (Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). Model overfitting was evaluated by permutation tests with 200 random permutations; models with Q² intercept values below zero were considered valid. Differential metabolites were identified based on a combination of variable importance in projection (VIP) scores ≥ 1.0 from OPLS-DA, fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.667, and p < 0.05. To assess the false discovery rate, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied, and the resulting q-values are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S3. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) and heatmap visualization were performed using the heatmap package in R (version 4.5.1). Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Pathways with a hypergeometric test p < 0.05 after FDR correction were considered significantly enriched.

Fragrance component detection

Following the method of Zhang et al. [31], aromatic compounds were extracted using distillation, and their content was determined by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS; Agilent 7890B GC/5977B MSD, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) .

Data processing and analysis

Primary data organization and preliminary calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel 2021. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). For physiological and biochemical data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used to compare means across treatments and curing stages at p < 0.05, with normality and homogeneity of variances assessed by Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Mantel analysis with permutation-based testing was performed using an online platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) to assess associations between metabolite profiles and physicochemical indices, with correlation heatmaps generated accordingly. Data visualizations were created using the ggplot2, pheatmap, and ggpubr packages in R, and figures were assembled using Adobe Illustrator 2024 (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). Unless otherwise specified, data are presented as mean ± SE of biological replicates.

At 48 h, VF treatment significantly (p < 0.01) decreased starch (-17.1%), total sugar (-18.5%), reducing sugar (-25.2%) and MDA (-38.8%) content, and significantly reduced (p < 0.05) the activities of SOD (-11.6%) and PPO (-24.3%), while having no significant effect on amylase and POD activity (p > 0.05). Screening of metabolite differences in tobacco leaves under different curing methods

OPLS-DA was performed to discriminate between CK and VF groups at each time point, with permutation tests (n = 200) to validate the models (Fig. 4A1−3). The OPLS-DA models showed R²X values of 0.354, 0.344, and 0.325, R²Y values of 0.988, 0.991, and 0.988, and Q² values of 0.765, 0.707, and 0.727 at 48 h, 120 h, and 168 h, respectively. The Q² values were all significant (p < 0.05), indicating that the models were not overfitted. The relatively low R²X values suggest that a substantial portion of metabolic variation was not captured, which may reflect biological variability or technical noise.

There were significant differences in the metabolite composition of tobacco leaves between the two curing treatments at 48 h, 120 h, and 168 h. At the three specified time points, the principal components (PC1 and PC2) contributed 21.5% and 13.9% (48 h), 19.0% and 15.4% (120 h), and 19.2% and 13.3% (168 h) of the total variance, respectively. The VF and CK samples showed a trend of separation in the PCA score plots at each time point, suggesting treatment-dependent metabolic shifts, although the low total explained variance (~ 35%) indicates considerable influence from other sources of variation.

Correlation between flavonoid metabolites, tobacco physiological indicators, and aroma compounds

During the yellowing stage (Fig. 5A), flavonoid metabolites exhibited correlation strength-dependent associations with physiological indicators. Flavonoids showing moderate to strong overall correlations (Mantel’s r ≥ 0.2) with the physiological dataset were generally positively correlated with antioxidant enzyme activities and negatively correlated with oxidative damage markers, a pattern consistent with a role in alleviating curing stress and promoting uniform yellowing. In the color-fixing stage (Fig. 5B), flavonoids with moderate to strong correlations (Mantel’s r = 0.2–0.4 and r ≥ 0.4) displayed predominantly positive correlations with hydrolase and oxidase activities, supporting color stabilization and the related metabolic processes during moisture loss, whereas flavonoids with weaker overall associations (Mantel’s r < 0.2) showed no consistent relationship with these enzyme groups.

Metabolic pathway analysis of differential metabolites

To clarify the material changes caused by VF group, the key pathways with the highest metabolic differences were identified by enrichment analysis and topological analysis (Fig. 6). At 48 h of curing, the metabolic pathway with the greatest influence and most significant enrichment was glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism. At 120 h and 168 h, the most influential pathway was the metabolic pathway, with the most significant enrichment being flavone and flavonol biosynthesis. Thus, in the later stages of curing, fan frequency modulation primarily affected the metabolism of flavonoid compounds.

KEGG enrichment analysis of metabolite differences in tobacco leaves under different curing methods

The metabolic network schematic (Fig. 7) summarizes the observed changes in metabolite abundance and pathway enrichment across the flue-curing period. Metabolites serving as substrates for the Maillard reaction, including certain reducing sugars and amino acids, showed altered abundance during the drying stage, suggesting their potential involvement in shaping the final chemical profile of cured leaves. Carbohydrate metabolism appeared to be the primary source of carbon source skeletons throughout the process: maltose abundance decreased continuously, accompanied by increased levels of glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), sucrose. These metabolites were relatively stable during the early and color-fixing stages, while accumulating significantly in the late stage. Proline and Phe-Pro, which accumulated during the early stress response and mid-term color-fixing stage, are known nitrogen-containing substrates for the Maillard reaction and may have contributed to the formation of heterocyclic aroma compounds in the late stage. The observed changes in sucrose and proline levels were consistent with their known roles as precursors for pyrroles, pyrazines, and other heterocyclic compounds that contribute to cured tobacco aroma. The carbon allocation patterns observed during curing shifted over time: during the early stage, carbohydrate-derived carbon was accompanied by enhanced proline synthesis, lipid metabolism, and the accumulation of aroma precursors such as phenylalanine. In the later stage, metabolites linked to the Maillard reaction showed enrichment, while flavonoids and terpenoids exhibited abundance changes consistent with their conversion to aroma-related compounds. This sequential pattern, with Maillard-associated metabolites enriched in the late stage, suggests a temporal shift in metabolic priorities across the flue-curing period.

Discussion

Environmental conditions during the growing season influence the baseline carbohydrate reserves of tobacco leaves, with temperature, rainfall, and sunshine hours significantly affecting total sugar, reducing sugar, and starch contents prior to curing [32]. The relatively uniform cultivation practices in this experiment minimized pre-existing variation in leaf material, making it unlikely that baseline differences confounded the metabolic comparisons between treatments. Consequently, the metabolic and physiological differences observed between CK and VF groups during curing are unlikely to be confounded by baseline variation in growing conditions. After harvest, tobacco leaves enter a starvation-driven physiological state, in which metabolic activity is sustained exclusively by internal reserves. During flue-curing, increasing temperature and progressive dehydration impose strong thermal stress, while photosynthesis has already ceased. Under these conditions, respiratory metabolism becomes the sole source of ATP required for cellular maintenance, pigment degradation, and stress responses [33–35]. Previous studies have shown that the yellowing stage is characterized by pronounced reprogramming of carbon metabolism, typically involving accelerated carbohydrate mobilization and altered respiratory flux [4, 14, 36, 37]. In the present study, VF treatment regulation was associated with significant changes in carbohydrate metabolism during the yellowing stage. Compared to CK, VF treatment significantly (p < 0.01) reduced starch (17.1%), reducing sugar (25.2%), and total sugar content (18.5%) at 48 h (Fig. 2E, F and H). These data suggest a potential impact on carbon allocation during the yellowing stage. These data suggest a potential impact on carbon allocation during the yellowing stage. The divergence in metabolic profiles between VF and CK groups was already evident at 48 h, as shown in the PCA score plot (Fig. 4B1). Compared with the constant-frequency control, leaves under VF treatment showed a greater decline in total sugar and reducing sugar levels at 48 h, accompanied by sustained starch degradation and relatively elevated amylase activity, suggesting that VF airflow promoted the mobilization of carbohydrate reserves during the yellowing stage. Rather than suppressing carbon mobilization, VF treatment appeared to facilitate the utilization of carbohydrate reserves, thereby sustaining respiratory metabolism during the yellowing stage [35, 38].

As curing progresses and temperatures rise, the demand for respiratory ATP increases substantially [3]. To meet this demand, carbon metabolism often shifts toward glycolysis, which provides rapid ATP generation and key metabolic intermediates [12, 39]. At the same time, the TCA cycle may become partially constrained under thermal or dehydration stress, leading to a relative enhancement of glycolytic flux [34, 40]. This metabolic adjustment has been widely reported in plant tissues subjected to abiotic stress [19, 41]. Under the elevated temperatures during curing, sustained respiratory metabolism inevitably increases the activity of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, which can elevate the risk of ROS production [27, 39]. Excessive ROS accumulation can damage cellular membranes through lipid peroxidation and accelerate cellular senescence [26]. Therefore, maintaining redox balance is essential for preserving leaf integrity during curing [3]. In the present study, leaves under VF treatment consistently exhibited lower MDA levels (e.g., by 38.8% at 48 h and 15.4% at 120 h; Fig. 2B), suggesting reduced oxidative damage. The concurrent decrease in SOD activity at 48 h (− 11.6%; Fig. 2G) and MDA levels suggests that VF treatment alleviated oxidative stress during the yellowing stage. The observed decline in SOD activity is consistent with a decreased demand for superoxide dismutation under reduced oxidative stress. In contrast, POD activity increased at 120 h (+ 29.6%; Fig. 2C), indicating that VF treatment enhanced peroxidase-mediated defense rather than impairing enzymatic antioxidant capacity. Our metabolomic data further revealed a trend toward proline accumulation in leaves under VF treatment toward the end of the yellowing stage (VIP = 1.56, p = 0.0367, FC = 2.22; Supplementary Table S2). It should be noted that the FDR-adjusted p-value (0.232) did not reach the conventional significance threshold of 0.05; this finding is therefore regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and further targeted validation is required to confirm its biological relevance. The elevated POD activity, together with the trend toward proline accumulation, points to a coordinated enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant response that contributes to the protection of cellular membranes during curing [19, 42].

While previous studies have reported that VF treatment is associated with increased accumulation of aroma-related compounds [4], our integrated metabolomic and physiological analysis revealed a more complex metabolic pattern. VF treatment was associated with indicators of reduced oxidative stress, as reflected by lower MDA levels and elevated POD activity; however, certain aroma precursors and flavonoids showed decreased rather than increased accumulation. This pattern is consistent with a carbon allocation trade-off in which mobilized carbon appears to be preferentially directed toward stress-related pathways rather than secondary metabolism. Consistent with this interpretation, carbon derived from sugar degradation can be diverted into amino acid synthesis, contributing to the observed trend toward proline accumulation [19, 43, 44]. This carbon reallocation is reminiscent of the growth-defense trade-off widely documented in plant biology [45, 46], but to our knowledge, it has not been explicitly linked to VF treatment in postharvest tobacco leaves.

Flavonoids represent an important class of secondary metabolites in tobacco, serving both protective functions and acting as aroma precursors [16, 47, 48]. Their biosynthesis depends heavily on carbohydrate availability and the allocation of carbon toward the phenylpropanoid pathway [49, 50]. Pathway enrichment analysis in this study revealed that flavonoid and flavanol biosynthesis pathways were significantly affected during the color-fixing stage. Despite this enrichment, key flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol showed reduced accumulation in leaves under VF treatment. This apparent contradiction may reflect carbon allocation trade-offs documented in previous studies [35, 37]. During color fixing, no significant difference in total sugar levels was observed between the two groups (Fig. 2H), while starch content continued to decrease (by 29.6%, p < 0.001) and reducing sugar levels remained significantly lower (1.53%, p < 0.01) in leaves under VF treatment (Fig. 2E and F). The sustained decline in starch and reducing sugars, without a corresponding accumulation of total sugars, suggests that the mobilized carbon was largely consumed by respiratory metabolism, leaving limited substrate for flavonoid biosynthesis. These findings are consistent with a scenario in which the preferential channeling of carbon toward respiratory metabolism limits the substrate available for flavonoid biosynthesis, thereby restricting flavonoid accumulation to basal levels [18]. Flavonoids such as quercetin are recognized as important aroma precursors in tobacco, as they can degrade into phenolic compounds, aldehydes, and ketones during curing, aging, and combustion [17]. In the present study, reduced flavonoid accumulation in leaves under VF treatment was accompanied by significantly lower levels of several aroma-related compounds, including benzaldehyde, phenylethyl alcohol, 2,6-nonadienal, neophytadiene, and 2-Giant soybean trione (Table 3). Correlation and Mantel test analyses further indicated a significant negative association between flavonoid metabolites and aroma compounds during the later curing stages [50], suggesting that diminished flavonoid pools may limit the availability of downstream aroma precursors [13]. Mantel test analysis further confirmed a significant overall association between flavonoid metabolite profiles and the five aroma compounds (Fig. 8A). Beyond flavonoid-derived volatiles, terpenoid degradation also contributes to the aroma profile of cured tobacco leaves. Neophytadiene, a major diterpenoid derived from chlorophyll degradation, imparts a fresh hay-like odor and was significantly lower in leaves under VF treatment (Table 3). This reduction may therefore directly affect the terpenoid-derived volatile pool. Mantel test analysis further confirmed that overall terpenoid metabolite profiles were significantly correlated with the five aroma compounds (Fig. 8B), with all 20 terpenoid metabolites screened being down-regulated, consistent with the general decline in volatile aroma compounds. Additionally, Maillard reactions between reducing sugars and amino acids during curing generate nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds, including pyrazines and furans, which are key contributors to tobacco aroma [51]. The sustained decrease in reducing sugars and the alteration of amino acid pools under VF treatment may therefore influence both the extent and the direction of these reactions. Mantel test analysis indicated that certain reducing sugars and amino acids serving as Maillard reaction substrates showed significant correlations with neophytadiene, 2-Giant soybean trione, and phenylethyl alcohol (Fig. 8C), suggesting that substrate availability may be a factor influencing Maillard-driven aroma formation. Among the differentially accumulated volatiles, benzaldehyde and phenylethyl alcohol are linked to phenylpropanoid metabolism, whereas 2,6-nonadienal is associated with the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids. This structural diversity among the affected compounds suggests that VF treatment exerts a broad influence on multiple aroma-forming pathways. Such outcomes highlight an intrinsic trade-off in curing process: carbon preferentially directed toward respiratory energy production and stress defense during early stages may constrain secondary metabolite accumulation and aroma formation during later stages [1, 11]. Taken together, these findings indicated that VF treatment was associated with improved visual appearance and enhanced antioxidant capacity, but this was accompanied by a reduction in total aroma compounds (Table 3).

Integrating physiological and metabolomic data, we propose that the starch–amylase–total sugar axis functions as a central carbon hub during flue-curing. Variable-frequency airflow modulates this axis by regulating the timing and rate of carbon release from starch reserves [52, 53]. During the yellowing stage, moderated carbon mobilization was associated with sustained respiration and antioxidant defense, which contributed to alleviating thermal and oxidative stress (Fig. 2E, F and H). In contrast, during the color-fixing and drying stages, restrained carbon flow correlated with limited flavonoid biosynthesis and subsequent aroma precursor formation (Table S3). This stage-dependent regulation suggests that airflow control not only influenced the rate of metabolism, but also altered carbon allocation priorities across the curing process. The OPLS-DA models showed significant Q² values (p < 0.05 for all three time points) based on permutation tests (n = 200), confirming reliable metabolic discrimination between VF and CK groups. However, the modest R²X values (0.34–0.46) indicate that additional biological or technical variables contribute to the metabolic variation, and the pathway enrichment analysis relied on putative metabolite annotation and statistical correlation. The integrated metabolic model should therefore be regarded as a working hypothesis. Several additional limitations should be acknowledged. This study was conducted over a single growing season with a single cultivar (“Yunyan 87”), which limits the generalizability of the findings. The VF frequency was tested as a comparative treatment rather than being systematically optimized. Metabolite identifications were based on spectral library matching at various MSI levels (≥ 3.2), and confirmation of key metabolites using reference standards with targeted LC-MS/MS would increase confidence in their assignments. Further validation through multi-omics approaches, multi-year trials, and multi-cultivar experiments is warranted to substantiate the proposed model.

Conclusions

VF treatment was associated with glyoxylate and dicarboxylate pathways during the yellowing stage, which corresponded with improved visual appearance but was accompanied by a reduction in total aroma compounds. During the early curing stage, enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant responses contributed to reduced oxidative damage. In the later stage, Maillard reactions, together with the degradation of flavonoids and terpenoids, may have contributed to the integrated aroma profile. Taken together, these findings suggest that VF treatment influences a carbon allocation trade-off between stress defense and secondary metabolism during curing, and that tailored VF frequency regimes may help balance oxidative protection and aroma formation.
