Canadian female perspectives on training in the boys club: advanced therapeutic endoscopy
Authors: Avni Jain, Madeleine Barker, Jennifer Telford
Published: 2024

Introduction

Diversity and equity in healthcare are paramount to provide opportunities to those who are underrepresented in medicine, to improve patient outcomes and to enhance medical education.1 Female representation in gastroenterology has been an ongoing challenge. In Canada in 2022, 50% of those matched to an internal medicine training program were female, however just over a third of matched gastroenterology trainees were female.2 In leadership roles, the national and provincial organizations including the Canadian Association of Gastroenterology, Ontario Association of Gastroenterology and Alberta Society of Gastroenterology have seen females in presidential roles a mere 0 %-20% of the time. Furthermore, only 0 %-13% of University Divisions have been headed by female gastroenterologists in the recent years.

This disparity is further depicted in the evolving field of advanced therapeutic endoscopy (ATE). From 2018 to 2019, women accounted for just 12% of the ATE fellows that had matched through the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE).3 A survey of program directors at the time yielded some potential explanations including inflexible schedules, a shortage of women endoscopists at national conferences, exposure to fluoroscopy and a lack of female mentorship. The fellows were also surveyed to determine the degree of interest in a career in the field.4 Upon entry into a gastroenterology fellowship, the interest in ATE was greater amongst men (78%) than women (59%). However, by the end of their gastroenterology training, this gap had widened with only 22% of women maintaining an interest in ATE, while the interest amongst men remained unchanged. The potential reasons for decreased interest include radiation exposure, work-life balance, lack of mentorship, high-risk procedures, and less flexible schedules.

Unfortunately, similar data for Canada is lacking largely due to the lack of a national matching system for ATE in Canada. The exposure to ATE for trainees in Canada and whether there are differences in male versus female experiences is not well understood. Investigating the male–female disparities in gastroenterology and ATE in Canada can guide appropriate actions to eliminate them.

Methods

Objectives

We sought to describe differences in perspectives on ATE training between male and female gastroenterology trainees in Canada. We also aimed to determine the differences in career goals within gastroenterology between males and females, and reasons for the same. Finally, we sought to determine the barriers to pursuing a career in ATE and determine any differences between male and female gastroenterology trainees.

Study population

General gastroenterology residents enrolled in training during the 2021-2022 academic year from Royal College accredited training programs were invited to participate in the survey. The programs were identified through the Canadian Residency Match System (CaRMS) and the trainees were identified through the program administrators.

Survey design

A survey instrument was developed based on the objectives of the study. The survey comprised of 18 multiple-choice questions with an option for entering a free text answer. It was not mandatory to answer all questions to complete and submit the survey. Prior to administration, the survey was reviewed by 2 peers in medical training as well as by an advanced endoscopist for review conciseness. The survey did not collect personal identifiers and was anonymized. The survey was administered via an online survey platform (SurveyMonkey). Gastroenterology residents in all training programs were contacted directly by electronic mail (email). The email contained survey information, consent, as well as an electronic link to access the survey. Anonymity was emphasized. A second reminder email was sent after 2 weeks. The responses were collected via the online platform 1 week following the reminder email. The responses were stored anonymously, and the individual results of the survey were kept confidential.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata (Version 17.0 BE, College Station). Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages and were compared using a 2-sample test of proportions. A P-value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Responses were received from gastroenterology fellows at 12 of the 14 Canadian universities with Royal College-accredited programs. The response rate was 46% (n = 42, 16 identified as female and 26 identified as male).

Career goals

An equal proportion of male (42%, n = 11) and female (38%, n = 6) trainees indicated interest in a career in ATE. A greater proportion of female trainees had indicated nutrition as a career goal versus male trainees (25% vs. 3.8%, P = .03). Inflammatory bowel disease was the most popular career goal amongst gastroenterology trainees (45%, n = 19), with no statistically significant difference between males and females(P = .26).

Exposure to ATE

A minority of respondents thought that they would ‘probably’ or ‘definitely’ have adequate exposure to ATE during their core gastroenterology training (38%). Twenty-eight per cent of respondents felt that they ‘may or may not’, and 26% felt that they ‘probably’ or ‘definitely’ would not have adequate exposure. There was no relationship between exposure to ATE and training programs or between male and female trainees.

Self-perceived knowledge

Eighty-one per cent (n = 34) of gastroenterology trainees felt that they would be extremely comfortable or somewhat comfortable with the indications, contraindications, risks, and benefits of performing advanced endoscopic procedures by the end of their training. There was no significant difference between male and female trainee responses. When asked about diagnostic cholangiograms, the majority of trainees (64%) indicated that they would be somewhat uncomfortable or extremely uncomfortable with independent interpretation at the end of their training. There was no difference in this self-perceived competency between male and female trainees.

Mentorship and barriers

Thirty-eight per cent (n = 6) of female trainees felt that they had inadequate mentorship opportunities/role models within ATE, in comparison to 4% (n = 1) of males (P = .004) (Figure 1). Furthermore, 19% (n = 3) of females felt that this lack of mentorship/role models was a primary deterrent from pursuing ATE as a career, in comparison to 0% of males (P = .02). There was no significant difference in responses between males and females for other deterrents to pursuing further training in ATE, including lifestyle considerations, family considerations, inadequate ATE exposure during core GI training, lack of interest, radiation exposure, work environment/sexism, and concerns regarding employment prospects.

Discussion

This nationwide survey study demonstrates that female gastroenterology trainees in Canada lack mentorship and role models in ATE, which is a primary deterrent from pursuing it as a career when compared to their male counterparts. It is crucial to identify these barriers to training in rapidly advancing fields, such as ATE, in order to correct the disparities earlier in the career pathway. To our knowledge, this is the first Canadian nationwide survey to examine the perspectives of female gastroenterology trainees on barriers to pursuing a career in ATE.

Interestingly, there was no difference in the proportion of males and females that were interested in the field of ATE; however, prior publications have clearly demonstrated that this does not translate to women pursuing a career in ATE.3,4 This highlights the importance of mitigating the career barriers that perhaps only female gastroenterologists and trainees face. Interestingly, nutrition tended to be a more popular choice amongst female trainees. This may reflect a perception of nutrition allowing for a better work-life balance than ATE, which the female trainee may be more inclined towards.5

Our findings are supported by a previously published survey study in the United States, in which Yu et al. found a very strong association between the percentage of female ATE trainees and the number of females ATE faculty in the program.3 Their study emphasized the importance of having female representation in faculty and leaders in endoscopy.

Currently, there are 38 practising female advanced therapeutic endoscopists across Canada. Across the 7 ATE fellowship programs, there are typically 8-14 total ATE fellows accepted across the nation per year. However, since the inception of these programs, there have only been a total of 5 female trainees to graduate from these programs Canada-wide. Although not all the 38 females therapeutic endoscopists are faculty at centres where there are training programs, there is clearly an abundance of opportunity for mentorship and networking that is going amiss.

In the United States, the development of the Women in Endoscopy (WIE) association is attempting to mitigate the concerns that we have identified through this study. WIE serves to ‘champion the advancement of women through education, professional growth and leadership development’ while increasing their visibility in the world of endoscopy.6 The association recognizes this as a global issue and has made headway in promoting events and discussions to improve parity and foster careers for women in ATE.7 In Canada, although a special interest group for Diversity and Equity exists within the Canadian Association of Gastroenterology, further efforts are required in order to engage trainees and staff in meaningful interventions. Examples of interventions include the development of an active national mentorship program, events to promote and support women in leadership roles and ultimately create a work environment that is not catered to and is comfortable for only men. Recently, EndoEmpower Women (EEW), an educational live podcast series, has been launched with the aim of connecting women in ATE across Canada. The attendees are both faculty and trainees, with guest speakers being women leaders in their respective interests within ATE. Topics include medical and technical advances within ATE, as well as topics that are specific to women in the field, for example, women in leadership and radiation and pregnancy. The association aims to forge lasting mentorships as well as allow for invaluable collaboration and networking opportunities.

Our study did not find any difference in comfort levels in the field of ATE between male and female trainees. The self-perceived competency and knowledge were comparable, as was how they felt about the adequacy of exposure to ATE during their training. It was imperative to determine that this was not contributing to the deterrence from pursuing this field. The overall majority feeling of adequacy of exposure to ATE during core training however was poor. With Canadian training programs being only 2 years, it is a relatively short period of time to have dedicated ATE rotations when compared to programs in the United States. A way to overcome this may be to incorporate more didactic teaching into the curriculum in Canadian programs.

It is important for trainees to recognize that perceived barriers to their pursuing ATE have been navigated by successful women before them.8 Formal mentorships will provide this guidance and possibly eliminate pre-conceptualized barriers. It is well known that mentors in medical education and training play a significant role in determining how medical trainees mature professionally.9 A previous study had suggested that women may place more importance on a relationship with a role model than men10 and have a stronger response to mentors who display the integration of professional and personal responsibilities more readily. Furthermore, role model influence is dependent on not only technical observation but on the degree of similarity perceived by the trainee.11 The influence, therefore, of a female advanced endoscopist on a female trainee could be far greater than that of a male mentor.

Limitations

This was a web-based survey with a 46% response rate. A meta-analysis on survey modes published in 2020 revealed that web-based surveys had a 12% lower response rate compared with other modalities.12 Although the response rate of our survey was strong (46%), an alternate modality may have yielded in a more comprehensive representation across the nation. Men and women were proportionally represented amongst the respondents (39% female respondents representing 33% female gastroenterology fellows in Canada) thereby reducing response bias. A subgroup analysis of PGY-4 and PGY-5 trainee responses may also have further highlighted our findings of reduced female interest in ATE, as trainees solidify their subspecialty of choice later on in their training. However, fellows with a specific interest in the topic may have been more likely to respond to the survey, leading to selection bias.

As demonstrated by our nationwide survey study, ongoing efforts to recognize and address the lack of female leadership, mentorship and visibility are necessary to improve parity and encourage women to train in the male-dominated field of ATE.
