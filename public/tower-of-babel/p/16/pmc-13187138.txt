Design of a user-centered intelligent shoe cabinet for smart home hygiene using Kano–AHP–TRIZ
Authors: Rui Xu, Linyao Ma, Jiale Liu, Maowei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Internet of Things (IoT)–enabled smart home environments, managing entryway hygiene is becoming increasingly important for residential health and indoor environmental quality. Intelligent shoe cabinets are expected to integrate functions such as sterilization, drying, energy efficiency, noise reduction, and space optimization. However, most existing designs address these functions independently and lack a systematic, user-centered design framework. This study proposes an IoT-enabled intelligent shoe cabinet design based on an integrated Kano–Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)–Theory of Inventive Problem Solving (TRIZ) methodology. User requirements were first classified using the Kano model and then quantified through AHP and fuzzy comprehensive evaluation (FCE), allowing for the prioritization of 16 functional and experiential attributes. The results indicate that must-be attributes have the greatest influence on overall user satisfaction and system performance. To translate these prioritized user needs into technical solutions, TRIZ was applied to resolve engineering contradictions and guide innovative system design. The proposed intelligent system incorporates a hybrid heat-pump low-temperature drying mechanism, a modular UV–ozone disinfection unit, and a localized dynamic retrieval structure. The design received 80.814 points, achieving the “Welcome” level, and the feedback on functional interaction was positive. By integrating user perception modeling with IoT-oriented intelligent system design, this study presents a replicable framework for smart home hygiene products and suggests potential applications in healthcare monitoring and hygiene-sensitive environments within smart cities.Keywords: Intelligent Systems, Smart Homes, Kano Model, Analytic Hierarchy Process, Theory of Inventive Problem Solving, Shoe Cabinet DesignSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The rapid expansion of intelligent terminals and the increasing integration of smart products have accelerated the development of fully interconnected living environments. Emerging technologies—such as autonomous systems, AI-enhanced mobile devices, service robots, smart home platforms, and wearable electronics—are reshaping everyday interactions and fostering the creation of an integrated, multi-scenario intelligent ecosystem1. Within this broader trend, the smart home industry has experienced significant growth driven by the convergence of the Internet of Things (IoT) and artificial intelligence (AI). Global household penetration of smart devices is projected to increase from 77.6% in 2025 to 92.5% in 2029, reflecting strong consumer demand for more convenient, efficient, and health-oriented home environments2.

Smart shoe cabinets have emerged as a new category within the smart home sector, gaining attention due to changing living patterns and an increased focus on home hygiene. As urbanization intensifies and living spaces shrink, households prioritize effective space utilization and cleanliness. The entryway acts as a transitional zone between public outdoor areas and the private home interior, influencing both first impressions and overall hygiene. Traditional shoe cabinets—originally designed solely for storage—have evolved to incorporate functions related to health protection and intelligent control. However, many conventional products still suffer from poor ventilation, inadequate deodorization and antifungal performance, and limited spatial efficiency, rendering them insufficient for contemporary needs.

This issue is particularly relevant in China and other East Asian regions, where the custom of removing shoes before entering the home is deeply rooted in cultural practices that emphasize cleanliness and respect for the household. This practice not only helps maintain the hygienic condition of indoor flooring but also mitigates the introduction of external contaminants into living spaces3. The soles of shoes are known to carry various bacteria, such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli4, posing a potential risk when brought indoors. Following the COVID-19 pandemic, awareness of household hygiene increased significantly, highlighting the sanitation challenges associated with entryway management. As a result, smart shoe cabinets equipped with disinfection, deodorization, antifungal treatment, and air purification functions have gained popularity as essential products for maintaining healthy living environments.

Shifts in demographic structure are also shaping the evolving expectations for smart shoe storage. Younger consumers tend to value aesthetic design and intelligent features, while older adults prioritize safety and ease of use, such as motion-sensing lighting and slip-resistant surfaces. Families with children often seek enhanced antibacterial and mold-resistant functions. These diverse needs are driving the transition of shoe cabinets from basic furniture to more personalized, health-supportive, and intelligent household products5.

Market projections highlight strong commercial potential for this category. The 2025 Smart Multi-functional Bacterial-Kill and Odor-Removing Shoe Cabinet Market Research Report estimates that the Chinese market for such products will reach 75–150 billion RMB by 2025 and exceed 200 billion RMB by 2030, representing a compound annual growth rate of 12%–15%6. Government initiatives further support this growth trajectory. The national Implementation Plan for the Digital Transformation of the Light Industry promotes the adoption of IoT technologies and intelligent control in household furniture, while encouraging cross-brand product interoperability, thereby establishing a solid policy foundation for the advancement of smart shoe cabinets7. Current smart shoe cabinets on the market primarily utilize PTC heating for their drying function, typically achieving an energy efficiency ratio (COP) of less than 3.0, while often exceeding a noise level of 45 dB(A)8. Health-oriented smart home innovations have also been extensively explored in academic research. Recent studies include the development of eye-tracking systems for smart home control9, IoT-based food management platforms10, user experience optimization of smart appliances for older adults through QFD11, and user-centered design strategies for dementia care within smart home environments12. Additional research has focused on enhancing interface visibility and usability for elderly users through iterative interaction design13. Collectively, this body of work underscores a growing emphasis on improving usability, safety, and well-being in intelligent living spaces.

Despite these advancements, the development of smart shoe cabinets remains limited due to insufficient attention to user experience and behavioral needs. Existing designs often fail to align with real-world usage patterns and do not provide systematic methods for reconciling conflicting functional requirements. To address these gaps, the present study applies the KANO model, AHP, and TRIZ inventive problem-solving theory to examine user needs related to shoe storage and entryway behavior. The KANO model categorizes requirements based on user perceptions, AHP quantifies the relative importance of these requirements, and TRIZ identifies and resolves key contradictions in functional and structural design. Through this integrated approach, the study enhances the functional configuration, performance optimization, and innovative problem resolution of smart shoe cabinets, ultimately improving convenience and safety while contributing new theoretical and methodological insights to smart home product design.

Theoretical background exploration and literature review

Analysis of the Kano-AHP-TRIZ research model

Kano model

The Kano model is a method used to analyze how different product features influence customer satisfaction. Originally introduced by Japanese researcher Noriaki Kano in the 1980s, the model helps identify and prioritize the features that matter most to users14. It categorizes customer requirements into five types, each reflecting a different impact on satisfaction. Must-be attributes (M) represent basic functions that users consider essential. One-dimensional attributes (O) have a proportional relationship with satisfaction, meaning better performance directly leads to higher satisfaction. Attractive attributes (A) exceed user expectations and significantly boost satisfaction, although their absence does not cause dissatisfaction. Indifferent attributes (I) have little effect on satisfaction, while reverse attributes (R) trigger dissatisfaction when present and improve satisfaction when removed15. To identify user needs, the Kano model is typically combined with interviews and market research. For each potential requirement, questionnaires are designed with paired functional and dysfunctional questions. After collecting responses, the data are analyzed to determine Kano categories, calculate the Better–Worse (BW) coefficient, and generate a BW quadrant plot. These steps clarify the relative importance of different user needs.

In recent years, the model has been widely applied in smart home research, particularly for classifying features such as disinfection, deodorization, space use, and automatic identification. This classification provides a structured foundation for determining which functions should be prioritized in later design and engineering stages. This analytical foundation also sets the stage for applying the AHP, which further quantifies the relative importance of user requirements and supports more precise design decision-making.

AHP analytic hierarchy process

The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), developed by Thomas L. Saaty in the 1970s, is a structured method for breaking down complex decisions into hierarchical levels, typically including an overall goal, evaluation criteria, and alternative options 16. Through pairwise comparisons, experts assess the relative importance of each element on a 1–9 scale. These comparisons are then converted into numerical weights, allowing subjective judgments to be expressed as quantifiable data and enhancing the rigor of decision-making17.

In product design, AHP is frequently used to determine which factors should be prioritized during development. These factors often influence performance, cost, usability, and overall product competitiveness. For instance, Tao Wang et al.(2025)18 used user journey mapping to identify user needs and applied the Kano model to categorize them. AHP was then employed to assign weights to these needs, helping to identify the most critical requirements for guiding design decisions. Similarly, Liu Z. et al. (2024)19 developed an evaluation system for products serving maternal and infant users by integrating Kano, AHP, and Quality Function Deployment (QFD), resulting in a structured model for assessing user experience. Hui Liu et al.(2024)20 created a combined KANO–AHP–Axiomatic Design (AD) approach that translates the subjective needs of special education students into measurable design parameters, thereby improving the practicality of interactive space design. Dionicio Neira-Rodado et al. (2020)21 further expanded this methodological integration by combining fuzzy Kano, AHP, DEMATEL, and QFD to link medical device requirements with design characteristics, ultimately enhancing decision-making and user satisfaction in hip replacement assistive devices.

By synthesizing qualitative insights with quantitative evaluation, AHP provides a bridge between user research and design decisions, laying a foundation for addressing deeper engineering contradictions, which are further explored through TRIZ in the next stage of the methodology.

Theory of inventive problem solving

The Kano model provides a multidimensional framework for evaluating product attributes, addressing a key limitation of the AHP, which may yield an incomplete assessment when factors are considered from a single dimension. When applied to user needs, AHP enhances the design process by quantifying the relative importance of each requirement, leading to a more structured and evidence-based approach. This combination improves the efficiency of design decisions and helps establish a clearer hierarchy of functions. However, the integration of Kano and AHP primarily focuses on identifying user requirements and assigning their weights, without fully addressing how these analytical results can be translated into specific, innovative design solutions. This gap highlights the need to incorporate TRIZ into the design methodology.

TRIZ provides a systematic approach for transforming analytical insights into practical innovation strategies. It includes tools such as contradiction analysis, inventive principles, and laws of technical evolution, all of which support designers in resolving conflicts that emerge during product development. Originally formulated by Genrich Altshuller and his colleagues from the 1940s to the 1980s, TRIZ was derived from an extensive analysis of patents that revealed recurring patterns of effective innovation22. Its structured tools–most notably the contradiction matrix and 40 inventive principles–offer guidance for identifying and resolving design challenges23. When applied to intelligent furniture or storage systems, TRIZ helps create design pathways that align user needs with technological feasibility.

Recent studies have explored the integration of Kano, AHP (or QFD), and TRIZ to enhance end-to-end product innovation. For example, Jinyang Xu et al. (2024)24 examined the preferences of Chinese camper users and developed an optimization framework that links functional requirements with aesthetic considerations using the KANO–AHP–TRIZ approach. Kenan Melemez et al. (2013)25 combined QFD, TRIZ, and AHP to design a wood trailer for the Black Sea region, achieving improvements in load handling, ergonomics, and environmental performance. Other studies have expanded this integrative methodology to broader contexts. Rong et al.(2024)26 incorporated Kano, Axiomatic Design, and TRIZ to create a full-cycle design workflow validated through an electric vehicle case study. Tandiono et al.(2023)27 used Kano, environmentally oriented QFD (QFDE), and TRIZ to support sustainable system-level innovation. In the furniture domain, Chen et al. (2024)28 demonstrated a practical application of KANO–AHP–TRIZ in designing willow-woven furniture, illustrating how the three methods work together to form a complete loop from user requirements to engineering solutions.

Design research paradigm and theoretical foundation based on multiple method integration

The single design method has limitations when addressing complex innovation problems. The KANO model provides valuable qualitative insights into requirements but cannot quantify priorities. AHP excels in multi-criteria decision-making but lacks a mechanism for generating innovations. TRIZ offers tools for resolving contradictions but requires precise identification of key inputs. FCE is useful for scheme evaluation and relies on a scientific indicator system. Consequently, systematically integrating multiple methods and constructing a closed-loop process—from “problem discovery” to “scheme generation” and then to “effect evaluation”—has become a significant paradigm in complex product design. This integration is not merely an accumulation of methods; it is grounded in design science and system innovation theory, creating a seamless conversion chain that transforms the output of one method into a high-quality input for the next. This approach effectively overcomes the limitations of single methods. Previous studies have explored and validated similar integration frameworks for their effectiveness.

Tao Wang et al. (2024)29 adopted the GT-AHP-QFD integrated method to design a pollination drone for greenhouse pear trees. This approach achieved efficient pollination and provided a reference for agricultural machinery design. YanXiao Zhao et al. (2024)30 proposed a method that combines GT-AHP-FCE. This method led to the design of tea sets inspired by the “Shan Hai Jing,” uncovered new demands, enhanced user experience, and verified the feasibility of the approach. Tao Wang et al. (2025)31 developed the GT-AHP-FCE integration method. Using the Zen aesthetic coffee table as an example, they demonstrated the effectiveness of this method in furniture design. Tao Wang et al. (2023)32 utilized the GT-AHP-FCE method, using the integration of Qatari culture into the creative design of the World Cup as a case study. They extracted requirements through user interviews, calculated weights, and selected the best solutions, thereby verifying the feasibility of merging national culture with design. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that the model of conducting demand analysis using Kano, transforming and weighting with AHP/QFD, and resolving contradictions through TRIZ is methodologically transferable and effective across various product domains. The theoretical contribution of this study is threefold: (a) it systematically applies this integrated framework for the first time to the emerging health management-oriented home product domain of intelligent shoe cabinets, addressing unique multiple contradictions such as sterilization, space, and interaction; (b) it explicitly introduces FCE fuzzy comprehensive evaluation as the final verification step of the process and directly links its weight system with the front-end AHP, thereby strengthening the theoretical rigor of the “design-evaluation” closed loop; (c) it demonstrates how, under resource constraints, the hierarchical design strategy of “prioritizing essential functions for guarantee and innovating for charm functions” can be formulated based on the output of KANO-AHP-TRIZ, offering clear guidance for engineering practice.

Overall, prior research confirms that combining Kano for requirement identification, AHP or QFD for weighting and mapping, and TRIZ for resolving contradictions creates a robust methodology applicable across various product categories, including consumer electronics, vehicles, and furniture. Despite these advancements, there is a significant lack of research applying this integrated approach to smart shoe cabinets. Existing studies often focus on individual functions or technologies, lacking a comprehensive framework that incorporates user experience, health-related features such as disinfection and mildew prevention, automated identification, and space optimization. To date, no published work has employed the full KANO–AHP–TRIZ methodology to guide the development of intelligent shoe cabinets.

Research analysis

Overview of the analytical method

A systematic analysis and structured discussion by the design team resulted in the development of a comprehensive design process for an intelligent shoe cabinet. This process was created to meet the diverse needs of urban households, commercial environments, and medical facilities. To support this initiative, a detailed research and design framework was established, consisting of three primary components. This study proposes the integrated KANO-AHP-TRIZ-FCE framework, which aims to systematically address three core challenges in intelligent product design: the ambiguity and conflict of demands, the need to shift decision-making from qualitative descriptions to quantitative parameters, and the requirement for objective evaluation of innovative solutions.

The first phase focuses on extracting fundamental user requirements for the intelligent shoe cabinet. This was accomplished through a thorough review of existing literature, expert interviews, rational analysis, and market research. The identified requirements were classified using the Kano model questionnaire into attractive, expected, essential, and non-differentiated functions, providing a qualitative foundation to guide the overall design direction. In the second phase, the AHP was applied to construct a user demand index system. Expert evaluations were incorporated, and consistency checks were performed to ensure reliability. This approach enabled the quantification of the relative importance of various requirements and facilitated the prioritization of design factors, thereby supporting informed decision-making in subsequent design stages. The third phase integrates TRIZ theory with the outcomes from the Kano and AHP analyses to systematically address contradictions within high-priority design elements. By applying inventive principles and the laws of technological evolution, innovative solutions were developed to achieve an optimal balance between functionality and user experience. The resulting design demonstrates a harmonious alignment of user requirements, logical coherence, and innovation-driven improvements, providing both methodological and theoretical guidance for future product development, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

The necessity and logical framework of the integrated methodology

In addressing a key challenge in the design of intelligent products—how to systematically convert ambiguous, diverse, and often conflicting user demands into practical and assessable innovative engineering solutions—it is clear that no single existing method can independently complete this closed loop. Therefore, it is essential to construct an integrated framework comprising KANO, AHP, TRIZ, and FCE. Each of these four methods fulfills irreplaceable and logically progressive functions.The KANO model identifies and classifies user requirement attributes, preventing uniform resource allocation and providing a qualitative basis for determining priorities. The AHP method, based on KANO classifications, constructs judgment matrices and performs consistency checks to convert experts’ assessments of the relative importance of requirement categories into precise weights, thereby addressing the decision-making challenge of prioritization after classification. TRIZ theory focuses on resolving contradictions among high-weight requirements, mapping abstract engineering conflicts into specific solutions through a systematic library of inventive principles, thus bridging the gap between “what are the requirements” and “how to achieve the technology.” Finally, the FCE integrates multi-level fuzzy evaluation information and calculates a comprehensive score based on AHP weights, offering an objective and transparent quantitative basis for scheme selection. In conclusion, these four methods create a complete logical loop from “demand insight → priority decision-making → contradiction resolution → solution verification,” with each step being closely connected and complementary in function. Removing any one of these steps would disrupt the decision-making chain or diminish the credibility of the results. Therefore, this integrated framework serves as a necessary and powerful methodological response to the systematic innovation design challenges of complex product systems.

This study posed minimal risk to participants and was exempt from formal ethical review by the Department of Global Convergence at Kangwon National University, following institutional guidelines. All procedures adhered to relevant regulations and principles of research ethics, including academic integrity and responsible research conduct. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Kano analysis

Preliminary demand analysis based on the Kano model

Acquisition and classification of user requirements for the Kano model intelligent shoe cabinet

The current shoe cabinet market features a coexistence of traditional and emerging smart products. Traditional shoe cabinets have historically competed based on material, design, and price. Materials such as solid wood, laminate, and metal cater to diverse consumer preferences and budgets, but their designs primarily focus on storage functionality, lacking innovative features. The rigid internal structures of these cabinets often fail to accommodate various shoe shapes, resulting in inefficient space utilization. Additionally, the enclosed environment can promote bacterial growth and unpleasant odors, compromising hygiene and the overall user experience. Consequently, traditional shoe cabinets exhibit significant limitations in health management and intelligent control, revealing a functional gap that presents opportunities for innovation in smart product design.

Smart shoe cabinets have emerged to address this gap, representing a significant shift from basic storage to intelligence-enhanced functionality. Current innovations in the market primarily emphasize aesthetics or isolated features but often lack a systematic design logic grounded in user behavior and health management needs. Functional offerings are frequently limited to deodorization, drying, and UV sterilization, without sufficient adaptation to various usage scenarios, such as households, medical facilities, or fitness centers. Moreover, smart control systems often suffer from limited interaction quality, low algorithmic accuracy, and poor module compatibility, restricting the overall intelligence and usability of these products.

To tackle these challenges, this study adopts a user-centric research approach. Specialized surveys, field visits, and expert interviews were conducted across diverse user groups to provide a scientific basis for optimizing smart shoe cabinet functionality and guiding design innovation. The study categorized users into three primary segments. The first segment comprised core users, including multi-generational households with elderly members or children, single working professionals, and operators of gyms and postpartum care centers. The second segment included potential users, such as individuals renovating homes, pet owners, fitness enthusiasts, and those with allergies. The third segment encompassed “lost users,” defined as individuals who had previously purchased or used smart shoe cabinets but discontinued their use due to dissatisfaction with functionality or poor user experience.

Qualitative interviews were conducted to explore user needs, following the principle of information saturation. Five users from each category (core, potential, and churned) were interviewed to gain a deep understanding of their behavioral motivations and experience patterns. This stage aims to provide a conceptual framework and item basis for the subsequent KANO questionnaire design, rather than for statistical inference. The preliminary findings will be verified and prioritized through large-sample quantitative research. In total, 15 participants were interviewed, with five from each user segment, and the collected data were analyzed to identify 16 initial user design requirements. Through multi-channel targeted recruitment and pre-screening, we ensured that the sample encompassed various backgrounds, including multi-generational families, allergy groups, and those experiencing functional failures. The goal is to maximize the diversity of user needs and experiences, ultimately building a comprehensive list of requirements for subsequent quantitative research.

By integrating principles of affective design, the requirements were organized into four hierarchical levels, summarized in Table 1. Through in-depth interviews and satisfaction analyses across three user groups, a comprehensive smart shoe cabinet design system was developed to address diverse usage scenarios. This approach signifies a shift from feature-driven design to a needs-oriented paradigm, offering both theoretical and methodological support for the advancement of intelligent home products.

Kano model questionnaire analysis

Based on the five-point scale of positive and negative questions for the 16 initial user requirement design elements, the Kano questionnaire was designed, See Appendix Table 5. This study employed a combined strategy of purposive and snowball sampling. Initially, based on prior interviews, three typical user profiles were identified as criteria for selecting the initial respondents. Subsequently, the sample size of this user group was expanded through recommendations from these initial respondents. An electronic questionnaire was created using the “Questionnaire Star” platform and distributed via a link shared in the target users’ WeChat group, industry community forum, and email. To prevent duplicate submissions, the online questionnaire restricted responses to one submission per IP address. Offline, paper versions of the questionnaire were placed at partner venues, such as gyms and design studios, where they were distributed and collected by on-site supervisors. After collection, all questionnaires were manually reviewed, and invalid ones—those exhibiting obvious patterns in responses or completed in a short time—were excluded. Ultimately, all 250 questionnaires were deemed valid.

The survey questionnaire targeted the three user groups mentioned above. A total of 250 questionnaires were distributed online and offline, yielding 250 responses, resulting in a 100% response rate. The distribution of the questionnaire was strictly limited to the three types of target user groups identified through in-depth interviews conducted in the previous stage. Targeted invitations were sent via the researcher’s network and cooperation channels to ensure a highly relevant sample. A combination of an “online link and offline paper questionnaire” was utilized. Throughout the data collection period, researchers conducted one-on-one follow-up reminders for those who did not respond to the invitations. All respondents had clear interests or experience using smart home or health management products, resulting in high cooperation rates. Additionally, the researchers promised to provide a summary of the results to participants as an intangible incentive.

SPSS statistical software was used to process and analyze the questionnaire data. Attribute values for each category were calculated and categorized using the Kano model classification evaluation table, See Appendix Table 6.

One of the core objectives of this study is to classify the attributes of 16 user requirements using the KANO model. To ensure the stability of the classification results, it is recommended that each requirement item receives a sufficient number of responses. With 250 samples, the average number of responses for each requirement item exceeds 15, meeting the basic requirements for KANO classification analysis and providing a reliable percentage basis for the subsequent calculation of the Better-Worse coefficient. In design studies employing integrated methods such as KANO-AHP-TRIZ, the sample size typically ranges from 200 to 40033,34. Our sample size (N = 250) falls within this conventional range and has been demonstrated to be adequate to support the entire process from requirement identification to weight analysis.

Calculation of the Better-worse coefficient and quadrant diagram analysis

Following the Kano questionnaire survey, which was conducted based on the needs of different user groups, the levels of user satisfaction and dissatisfaction for each functional requirement were calculated. The Better coefficient reflects the extent to which user satisfaction increases when a specific feature is present. Higher Better values indicate a stronger positive effect of that feature on overall user satisfaction35. Conversely, the Worse coefficient represents the extent to which user satisfaction decreases when a feature is absent, with higher values indicating a more pronounced negative impact36. Based on these coefficients, priority should be given to fulfilling Must-be requirements with high Worse values, while also addressing Attractive requirements with high Better values to maximize overall user satisfaction. For each user requirement, the initial attribute category is determined based on the frequency distribution of positive and negative response combinations. The final classification follows these decision rules: If the proportion of a specific attribute (such as A) exceeds 50%, it is classified directly as that attribute. If no single attribute exceeds 50%, an auxiliary determination is made using the Better-Worse coefficient. Requirements with high |Worse| coefficients are prioritized to ensure basic satisfaction, while those with high Better coefficients are considered to enhance satisfaction potential. This study specifies that when the absolute value of the |Worse| coefficient for a requirement exceeds 0.5, its attribute tendency is strengthened to M or O. Conversely, when the Better coefficient exceeds 0.6, its attribute tendency is strengthened to A or O. All boundary cases are independently evaluated by two researchers, followed by a discussion to reach a consensus. Typically, “Better” is denoted as S, representing a positive effect, while “Worse” is denoted as D, representing a negative effect. The calculation formulas for the Better-Worse coefficients are provided in Eqs. (1) and (2). This systematic approach allows for the quantitative evaluation of functional requirements, ensuring that design efforts prioritize features that significantly enhance user satisfaction while minimizing dissatisfaction.

Among these, (A) represents the proportion of users who perceive the feature as “Attractive Attributes,” (O) represents the proportion of “One-dimensional Attributes,” (M) represents the proportion of “Must-be Attributes,” and (I) represents the proportion of “Indifferent Attributes.” Through analysis and calculation, the Kano attribute categories were identified for 16 demand indicators. The Kano model categorizes functional requirements into four quadrants, each representing a distinct type of user-driven attribute. The first quadrant includes Attractive attributes, which exceed user expectations and generate delight when present but do not cause dissatisfaction if absent. In this study, this quadrant features approximately six attributes, such as Automatic Sterilization and Deodorization (Better = 78.54%, Worse = − 45.49%) and Assisted Shoe On/Off Function (Better = 70.51%, Worse = − 32.05%). These attributes are crucial for creating differentiated product value and enhancing the overall user experience.

The second quadrant consists of One-dimensional attributes, which primarily reflect user expectations regarding innovation and intelligent interaction. Notable features in this category include Voice Control and Smart App Remote Control. These features directly influence perceived core value, making them central to market competition and necessitating continuous optimization to ensure high performance and reliability. The third quadrant contains Indifferent attributes, which have minimal impact on user satisfaction or dissatisfaction. While these features may be retained as optional modules in future product iterations, they should not be emphasized in core design decisions or primary resource allocation to avoid inefficient investment. The fourth quadrant comprises Must-be attributes, representing fundamental capabilities that users naturally expect. This category includes approximately four features, such as Quiet and Energy-Saving Operation, Security Protection Design, and Space Optimization with Modular Structure. Although their presence does not significantly increase satisfaction, their absence can lead to considerable dissatisfaction, making them essential for baseline product acceptance.

Reliability and validity analyses of the Kano questionnaire were conducted using SPSS. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.657, exceeding the 0.6 threshold and indicating acceptable internal consistency. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.623, suggesting that the dataset was appropriate for factor analysis. Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded a significance level of 0.000 (< 0.05), confirming the data’s adequacy for validity assessment.

Based on the Kano classification, the recommended development priority for product features generally follows the order: Must-be (M) > One-dimensional (O) > Attractive (A) > Indifferent (I). In the present study, the classification results included four Must-be attributes, three One-dimensional attributes, six Attractive attributes, and three Indifferent attributes (Fig. 3). Resolution of contradictions and design approaches based on TRIZ theory

During the design and development process, the team identified several conflicting situations. The factors A, O, and M, along with the weighting of requirements within the target hierarchy, can lead to various issues in practical application. First, in accordance with the General Technical Requirements for Smart Shoe Cabinets issued by China’s State Administration for Market Regulation in 2023, key specifications include: sterilization efficacy testing methods (referencing GB 21551.3–2010), electrical safety standards (equivalent to IEC 60335-2-80), and IoT communication protocols (mandatory support for Bluetooth 5.0 and above). These standards ensure compliance with market development policies.

Second, systematic mapping and resolution of three technical conflicts were conducted: physical conflict (“Automatic shoe retrieval recognition vs. space optimization reliability”), technical conflicts (“High-efficiency sterilization/deodorization vs. energy efficiency and quiet operation” and “Voice/gesture interaction vs. accidental touch safety”). This process yielded implementable innovative design solutions.

In this study, a typical technical contradiction arises between high-efficiency sterilization and deodorization, on one hand, and energy-saving and quiet operation, on the other. According to the Kano model analysis, user demand for automatic sterilization, deodorization, and drying/dehumidification corresponds to Attractive Attribute A2 and One-dimensional Attribute O1. Achieving these functions requires the coordinated operation of heating elements, air-circulation systems, and ozone-assisted purification modules to ensure microbial reduction and optimal air quality performance. However, these processes inevitably increase energy consumption and noise levels, which directly conflict with Must-have Attribute M3, associated with energy efficiency and quiet operation.

Based on the TRIZ contradiction matrix mapping, the related engineering parameters include Improved Parameter 16 (Temperature) and 9 (Speed). In contrast, the Deteriorated Parameters involve 21 (Energy waste), 26 (Reliability), and 27 (Harmful factors), such as ozone or ultraviolet exposure. Correspondingly, the recommended TRIZ inventive principles derived from parameter mapping include: 35 (Parameter variation), 36 (Phase transition), 19 (Periodic action), 24 (Mediator), 22 (Turning disadvantage into advantage), 30 (Soft shell), and 40 (Composite material).

See Appendix Table 8. Feature-rich user needs—such as automatic shoe retrieval and gesture-based interaction—introduce additional complexity to the system’s mechanical layout and sensing mechanisms. While these capabilities enhance user convenience, they can also conflict with fundamental expectations for reliability and safety, including protective safeguards and clear status feedback. To address these contradictions, the TRIZ analysis identified several relevant inventive principles: Pre-reaction (No. 9), Feedback (No. 23), Mediation (No. 24), Homogenization (No. 33), and Pre-buffering (No. 11). The improvement parameters (the aspects to be enhanced) mainly include: (32) Operability, which refers to the ease with which users control the device through voice, gesture, etc.; and (38) Complexity of control, which refers to the intelligence level and processing capacity of the system for recognizing various interaction instructions. The deterioration parameters (the aspects that deteriorate) mainly include: (26) Reliability, which refers to the stability of the interaction system in accurately identifying user intentions and avoiding misjudgments; and (27) Harmful factors, which specifically refer to the personal safety risks (such as injuries caused by accidental movements of the mechanical arm) that may result from incorrect operation. Mapping “enhancing the natural interaction experience” to improving “operability” and “complexity of control,” and the possible resulting “false triggering” and “accidents” are mapped to “reduced reliability” and “increased harmful factors.” Guided by these principles, the design integrates multimodal recognition with a two-stage safety verification process. As a result, the system forms a unified interaction framework that transitions smoothly from natural user input to accidental touch prevention and ultimately to stable operational reliability. This method has optimized and enhanced the security and reliability of human-computer interaction. See Appendix Table 9.

A second key contradiction arises between automatic shoe recognition and retrieval and the structural reliability needed for space-efficient storage. Users increasingly expect shoe cabinets to hold a large number of footwear items—including irregular shapes like boots and high heels—while maintaining a compact footprint suitable for limited entryways. However, incorporating mechanical components for automated recognition and retrieval increases spatial and structural demands, heightening the conflict between functional integration and physical constraints.

The improvement parameters—specifically, (7) the volume of moving objects and (8) the volume of stationary objects—refer to the physical space occupied by the automatic shoe-taking mechanism (the moving object) and the entire shoe cabinet (the stationary object). Additionally, (31) harmful factors generated by the system/object can include the potential failure risks associated with the complex mechanical system itself. (26) Reliability pertains to the stability and maintenance-free operation of the complex electromechanical system over the long term. (34) Adaptability describes the shoe cabinet’s ability to accommodate shoes of various sizes and types. Finally, (30) the sensitivity to external harmful factors reflects the system’s resistance to disturbances, such as user errors.

By applying systematic contradiction-solving methods, the design shifts from traditional static storage to a dynamic, vertically oriented configuration. This transformation replaces fixed partitions with adaptive, multi-level access pathways, thereby increasing effective storage density. Consequently, the shoe cabinet maintains a compact external footprint while significantly increasing its internal capacity. This solution directly addresses the conflict between limited volume and the demand for increased storage capacity. See Appendix Table 10. Modular design for sterilization and deodorization

The design of the sterilization module in this intelligent shoe cabinet faces multiple challenges, including broad-spectrum inactivation, comprehensive coverage, and human-machine safety. A single technology cannot meet all these requirements. To address this issue, the solution adopts a localized modular sterilization strategy, integrating UV-C LEDs and low-concentration ozone within a sealed chamber. This approach leverages gas-mediated flow channels and safety interlock structures (based on TRIZ principles 24 and 22) to achieve efficient disinfection while effectively preventing leakage and risks of human exposure.

The UV-C sterilization module utilizes LEDs with a peak wavelength of 253.7 nm, which effectively disrupts the DNA/RNA of bacteria and viruses, rendering them incapable of reproduction46,47. Advantages of this approach include direct microbial inactivation, rapid activation, and the absence of chemical residues. To ensure safety, the UV-C source is fully enclosed within the cabinet, positioned either beneath load-bearing partitions or within sidewall air ducts. Door-opening power-cut detection and proximity sensors automatically deactivate the UV-C module if the chamber is improperly sealed or if a user approaches, effectively preventing accidental exposure. The ozone-assisted module complements UV sterilization by targeting shadowed areas that are otherwise difficult to disinfect. Ozone’s strong oxidizing properties enable comprehensive, dead-zone-free sterilization, making it particularly effective against mold spores that are resistant to UV light48,49. Because high ozone concentrations can adversely affect human respiratory health50, the system integrates a solid-state ozone generator with a high-precision ozone sensor to maintain a closed-loop control, keeping ozone levels below the safe threshold of 0.05 ppm. After sterilization, activated carbon adsorption and negative ion neutralization processes rapidly decompose residual ozone, with the system notifying users of the “safe unlock” time to ensure operational safety.

For drying and deodorization, positive temperature coefficient (PTC) ceramic heaters provide self-limiting temperature control, automatically preventing overheating51. To reduce energy consumption, a hybrid system combining heat-pump low-temperature drying and phase-change moisture absorption (TRIZ Principle 36) is implemented. Phase-change materials embedded in the air channels store and release heat, stabilizing low-temperature drying, while intermittent pulse operation (TRIZ Principle 19) maintains sterilization efficiency and reduces both energy use and noise. The deodorizing filter module uses replaceable adsorption cartridges, allowing users to maintain performance according to usage frequency. Built-in temperature and humidity sensors automatically adjust fan speed, operating quietly at low speeds during daytime (TRIZ Principles 1 and 27) and executing short high-power purification cycles at night, achieving continuous air purification alongside energy efficiency. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations optimize airflow through flexible soundproof ducts (TRIZ Principle 30) and large-diameter, low-speed fans (TRIZ Principle 35), achieving high airflow with minimal noise52,53.

Finally, the “Photocatalytic Passive Sterilization System” represents a significant innovation. The interior surfaces are coated with TiO2 nano-photocatalytic material (TRIZ Principle 40) and are periodically irradiated with short-pulse UV light. This design offers three key advantages: high-efficiency sterilization, low energy consumption, and silent operation, ensuring a safe, hygienic, and user-friendly environment within the smart shoe cabinet.

Scheme evaluation based on fuzzy comprehensive evaluation

To verify the effectiveness of the design scheme, this section utilizes the weights determined by the AHP method within the KANO-AHP-TRIZ framework from Chap. 3. It employs the fuzzy comprehensive evaluation method to conduct a quantitative assessment of the intelligent shoe cabinet concept presented in Chap. 4. By aggregating the perceptions of experts and users, the analysis examines the comprehensive acceptance and satisfaction of the design across multiple dimensions, including function, interaction, safety, and aesthetics, thereby providing empirical support for design decisions.

Based on the AHP model, a judgment matrix was constructed to determine the weight of each requirement. These requirements were then categorized into three quasi-levels: A, O, and M. These categories serve as first-level evaluation criteria, while the 13 user-requirement items represent the second-level indicators. The corresponding evaluation matrices are denoted as R1 for Must-be Attributes, R2 for One-dimensional Attributes, and R3 for Attractive Attributes.

To develop the evaluation content, the study identified key product features and included them in the questionnaire as indicator items. These features encompassed intelligent prompts and status feedback, safety protection, automatic sterilization and drying, gesture-based interaction, app-based intelligent control, environmental health monitoring, modular structural configuration, energy-efficient and quiet operation, and automatic shoe recognition. They were selected through an analysis of existing systems and a systematic review of user needs. As a result, a Likert scale questionnaire was developed, featuring five rating levels: Very Welcome, Welcome, Neutral, Not Welcome, and Very Unwelcome, assigned numerical values of 90, 75, 60, 45, and 30, respectively. To ensure validity, 30 questionnaires were distributed. The reviewers consist of experienced users, designers, and scholars, aiming to achieve a relatively balanced perspective. However, it is important to acknowledge that their evaluations reflect the subjective judgments of a specific professional group.

Discussion

The core output of this research is a conceptual design scheme for an intelligent shoe cabinet based on the integrated KANO-AHP-TRIZ framework. The comprehensive score of 80.814, reported in Sect. 4.3, represents the satisfaction assessment conducted by experts and potential users regarding the concept, functional configuration, and user experience expectations of this scheme. This assessment effectively validates the acceptance and appeal of the design scheme at both theoretical and subjective cognitive levels. However, this evaluation, based on subjective scoring, is fundamentally different from the experimental performance verification of physical prototypes. The subjective scoring scheme assesses whether the target group considers the design concept valuable and satisfactory, while the experimental performance of the physical prototype requires rigorous testing to determine if hard indicators such as sterilization efficiency, energy consumption, and noise levels meet the claimed standards. This research has successfully completed the entire process from demand analysis to scheme generation and satisfaction evaluation. However, it has only reached the stage of conceptual design and scheme evaluation. The physical effectiveness of the engineering strategies derived from TRIZ, such as phase change heat storage and modular UV-ozone disinfection, has not yet been verified through prototype manufacturing and laboratory testing. The integrated framework of KANO-AHP-TRIZ-FCE, developed in this research, demonstrates its effectiveness through a systematic approach that guides the closed-loop innovation process from vague requirements to specific solutions and their evaluation. The final design solution, along with a high FCE evaluation score (80.814), further validates the logical coherence and practical value of this framework. Firstly, this framework systematically drives design innovation. The KANO model identifies essential requirements such as “safety protection” (M2) and “space optimization” (M4), as well as attractive requirements like “automatic sterilization and deodorization” (A2) and “automatic shoe recognition and retrieval” (A1). The AHP then converts these demand attributes into quantifiable weights, clarifying design priorities. When high-priority essential requirements conflict with attractive ones, TRIZ employs the contradiction matrix to correlate parameters like “sterilization efficiency” with “energy consumption” and “noise.” This analysis leads to technical strategies such as “phase change material heat pump drying” and “modular UV-ozonization disinfection,” transforming emotional expectations into quantifiable engineering parameters. The FCE score of 80.814 verifies that the design scheme has received overall recognition in functionality, aesthetics, and interaction. This supports the effectiveness of the pathway: “KANO demand identification → AHP weight ranking → TRIZ contradiction resolution → FCE closed-loop evaluation,” achieving a complete logical closed loop from user insight to satisfaction verification. There are three theoretical contributions: First, the KANO attribute classification is integrated into the AHP criterion layer, establishing a unified quantitative evaluation framework for demand. Second, it is confirmed that the TRIZ contradiction matrix can map user experience requirements to engineering parameters and derive specific invention principles. Third, a hierarchical strategy of “prioritizing basic functions and systematically adding value functions” is proposed, providing practical guidance for product iteration under resource constraints. Practically, the AHP demand ranking can directly inform the product roadmap and version definition, optimizing R&D resource allocation. The TRIZ engineering strategy offers a viable path for the technical team, shortening the development cycle and balancing performance and cost. This integrated approach is applicable not only to intelligent shoe cabinets but also transferable to other smart home devices and hygiene management systems in medical and fitness contexts, supporting standardized management at entry points.

However, there are limitations to consider. First, the subjectivity of expert judgment: the AHP weights are based on the subjective assessments of 15 domain experts. While the consistency test (CR < 0.1) and the aggregation process ensure logical consistency and group convergence, the expert backgrounds (all from design and academic fields) may bias the weights towards “innovation” and “user experience,” potentially downplaying factors such as “production cost” or “supply chain feasibility” from an industrial perspective. Second, the inherent boundaries of the method type: this study focuses on method application and validation, aiming to demonstrate the feasibility and output of an integrated process rather than conducting a comparative experimental study. Therefore, this article did not establish a control group for comparison with the experimental group, nor did it conduct a direct performance parameter comparison with existing market products. Although the FCE score indicates acceptance of the scheme, it cannot be claimed that this method is absolutely superior to others in terms of “innovation efficiency” or “optimality of the output solution.” Fourth, and most importantly, the conceptual plan has not been verified. As mentioned earlier, all engineering strategies and design schemes proposed in the research remain at the conceptual stage. Their actual performance—such as sterilization efficiency, energy consumption, and noise level—along with reliability and long-term durability, has not undergone rigorous testing and verification through prototype production and laboratory environments. Given these limitations, future work should delve deeper along the following two paths:

Path 1: Methodological Expansion. Expand the sample scope (across regions, generations, and scenarios) to test the universality of the demand model and develop a decision support tool integrating the KANO-AHP-TRIZ workflow to enhance industrial usability. Quantify the advantages of this method through controlled experiments and compare the differences in demand coverage, innovation solution quality, and development cycle between teams using this integrated method and traditional processes.

Path 2: Empirical Verification. Advance the prototype implementation and experimental validation of the conceptual scheme, including the development of functional prototypes, conducting quantitative tests on core indicators such as sterilization rate, drying efficiency, power consumption, and noise in accordance with national standards, and conducting small-scale user field trials to collect usability and long-term reliability data. This will extend the research value from the methodological level to the engineering application level. Ultimately, only by comparing the actual performance of the prototype with that of mainstream commercial products as a benchmark can objective evidence of the performance advantages of this solution be obtained, thus completing the closed loop from a “valuable theoretical solution” to a “verified feasible solution through experiments.”

Conclusions

This study addresses the disconnection between user demands and innovative solutions in the development of smart home products. To achieve this, we constructed and verified a structured design framework that integrates the KANO model, the AHP, and TRIZ inventive principles. Using the KANO model, we classified the user demands for smart shoe cabinets, quantified their priorities with AHP, and transformed conflicts among high-weight demands into engineering contradictions to generate solution strategies. Ultimately, we derived a specific conceptual design scheme that received a comprehensive score of 80.814 through the FCE, indicating high recognition for the scheme. The study reached three main conclusions: First, we successfully established a complete methodological pathway that moves from qualitative classification and quantitative ranking of demands to engineering contradiction resolution, providing an operational process for related product innovation. Second, essential demands in the smart shoe cabinet field—such as safety and space—serve as foundational elements, while attractive demands like automatic sterilization and intelligent shoe retrieval are key to differentiation, offering a clear basis for research decisions. Third, the scheme generated from this framework was recognized, demonstrating that the integrated method possesses both theoretical rigor and practical feasibility.

In summary, this study progressed from methodological exploration to scheme generation and preliminary verification. Its fundamental value lies in providing a verified, reusable structured design tool and reference process, effectively bridging user demands and technological innovation.
