Mapping Rare Earth Element Distribution in MSWI Ash Using Physical Separation and Sequential Extraction
Authors: Mihret Melese, Adrian Javier Sing Jethmal, Mikyung Kim, Quang Tuan Lai, Mohd Danish Khan, Kwangsuk You, Kyung-Ho Park, Ji Whan Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The escalating global demand for rare earth elements (REE) has highlighted the potential of anthropogenic waste streams as secondary resources. This study characterizes the distribution and speciation of REE in Korean municipal solid waste incineration (MSWI) bottom ash (BA) and fly ash (FA). The ashes were subjected to particle size fractionation and magnetic separation to examine REE distribution across different particle sizes and magnetic fractions. In addition, raw BA and FA samples were analyzed using sequential extraction to assess REE speciation and leachability. Elemental composition and mineral phases were determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The results show that REEs are enriched in the magnetic fractions of both BA and FA and exhibit slightly higher concentrations in coarser particle fractions. Light REE (LREE) predominate across all particle sizes, including the nonmagnetic fractions. Sequential extraction results show that REEs are predominantly hosted in stable mineral phases, with only a limited proportion present in labile fractions. Overall, these findings reveal the distribution and chemical associations of REE in MSWI ash and provide a basis for future studies on their speciation and potential mobilization.

Introduction

Municipal solid waste (MSW) comprises a wide range of everyday discarded items, including food scraps, textiles, furniture, paper, plastics, batteries, and electronic appliances. − With over 2 billion tons generated globally each year and projections indicating a 70% increase by 2050, efficient waste management has become a pressing environmental and economic issue. Common disposal methods include landfilling, open dumping, open burning, composting, anaerobic digestion, and incineration. , Among these, incineration is popular due to its ability to reduce waste volume by up to 90% and mass by around 70%, offering not only significant volume reduction but also potential energy recovery.

Notably, around 90% of the residual ash produced through incineration is BA, which, along with FA, must be properly managed to mitigate environmental risks. Over the period from 1997 to 2022, Korea saw a significant shift in its waste management practices. Landfilling rates dropped dramatically from 63.85% to just 10.23%, a reduction to less than one-sixth of the original level. Meanwhile, the incineration rate more than tripled, rising from 7.12 to 24.00%, and the recycling rate more than doubled, increasing from 29.04 to 65.77%. These trends highlight the increasing reliance on incineration and, in turn, underscore the importance of effectively managing the resulting ash.

Unmanaged MSW can lead to severe issues, such as land scarcity, groundwater and air pollution, greenhouse gas emissions, spontaneous landfill fires, and even disasters like landfill collapses. , In addition, MSW contains valuable materials, and if not recovered, these resources especially REE are lost. MSWI Ash can be identified as a potential secondary source for REE, as REE become concentrated in the ash during incineration, often reaching 100–400 ppm. − The concentration can be higher when electronic waste (e-waste) is present in the feedstock. While e-waste accounts for over 5% of global total solid waste, its contribution to incineration feed depends on the effectiveness of waste segregation. In many countries, waste separation is inconsistently applied, increasing the likelihood of electronic components entering the incinerator stream.

REE are considered critical materials due to their essential role in a wide range of technologies, from consumer electronics such as mobile phones, televisions, cameras, LEDs, and rechargeable batteries, to manufacturing applications including high-strength magnets, metal alloys, pigments, and polishing agents. They are also vital for renewable energy technologies, including hybrid and electric vehicles, wind turbines, and solar panels, as well as advanced systems such as lasers, radars, night-vision equipment, and precision weapons, and medical devices like MRI contrast agents, portable X-ray machines, and medical or dental lasers. , Recovering REE from MSWI ash is therefore essential, as substantial amounts, potentially hundreds of grams per ton of ash, would otherwise be lost, reducing the supply of these critical elements essential for high-tech industries and renewable energy technologies.

Although REE occur relatively abundantly in the Earth’s crust, with an average concentration of 169 ppm, their distribution is uneven. They are categorized into LREE (137.8 ppm) and HREE (31.3 ppm), , and are found in various mineral forms such as carbonates, oxides, phosphates and silicates, ,− each requiring different extraction processes. China dominates the global REE market, accounting for nearly 70% of production in 2024, followed by the U.S. (11.6%) and Myanmar (7.97%). , Global output has risen from 133.6 thousand metric tons in 2010 to 390 thousand metric tons in 2024, but this geographic concentration, combined with rapidly increasing demand, highlights the vulnerability of the worldwide REE distribution system. The urgency of alternative sources is further reinforced by international climate goals. Achieving net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050, as targeted under the Paris Agreement, requires a major energy transition, with forecasts suggesting that mineral demand for clean energy technologies could quadruple by 2040. , By that time, these technologies are projected to account for over 40% of total REE demand, underscoring the need to explore secondary sources such as MSWI ash. Given the growing importance of REE and their uneven global distribution, coupled with increasing demand across strategic sectors, the exploration of secondary recovery methods has become essential.

In this study, FA and BA samples were analyzed to evaluate their potential as secondary sources of REE. Since the composition of MSW and the resulting ash varies according to regional characteristics such as urbanization, industrial activity, and waste management practices, localized studies are necessary. Magnetic separation, particle size distribution, and chemical speciation assessments were performed to determine the concentration and distribution of REE across magnetic and nonmagnetic fractions, different particle size ranges, and various chemical speciation forms, including ion exchangeable, carbonate bound, Fe/Mn oxide associated, organic bound, and residual fractions.

Materials and Methods

Sample Description and Collections

FA and BA were obtained from the Daejeon City Corporation’s household waste incineration facility. Due to its heterogeneous nature and wide particle size distribution, BA was reduced to a subsample using the coning and quartering method. The bulk sample was formed into a cone, flattened, and divided into four quarters, with two diagonally opposite quarters discarded; the procedure was repeated until the required fraction was obtained (Figure S1). Hand picking was additionally conducted to remove large unburned residues, with particles larger than 5.6 mm discarded (Figure S2).

Both FA and BA were oven-dried at 100 ± 5 °C for 4 h prior to analysis. The prepared samples were then used for particle size distribution, magnetic separation, and subsequent experimental procedures. A schematic of the overall assessment process is presented in Figures S3 and S4.

Sample Preparation and Pretreatment

Particle Size Separation

The particle size distribution of FA and BA was determined using a Malvern Mastersizer 3000 particle size analyzer and a RO-TAP sieve shaker through a series of sieves with varying mesh diameters, respectively (Figure and Tables S1,S2). FA, being predominantly fine, has a tendency to agglomerate. The Malvern Mastersizer 3000 enables rapid agglomerate dispersion and measures particle sizes from 10 nm to 3.5 mm, making it suitable for characterizing the fine particles of FA. In contrast, BA is coarser, with some particles exceeding 3.5 mm, and was therefore analyzed using sieve analysis. Based on the resulting data, each material was divided into three particle size fractions for further analyses.

Particle size distribution of oven dry (a) FA and (b) BA.

Magnetic Separation

For magnetic separation, 1 kg of oven-dried sample was mixed with 5 L of water to form a slurry, stirred at 400 rpm for 3 hours. Wet magnetic separation was then carried out using a 5000-gauss wet drum magnetic separator. Following separation, filtration was performed, and the solids were collected and oven-dried prior to analysis.

Chemicals

The reagents used in the sequential extraction and digestion procedure were magnesium chloride (MgCl2, 98.0%, anhydrous, extra pure; Samchun Chemicals), sodium acetate (NaOAc, extra pure grade; Duksan Pure Chemicals), acetic acid (HOAc, extra pure; DC Chemicals), hydroxylamine hydrochloride (NH2OH·HCl, extra pure grade; Duksan Pure Chemicals), nitric acid (HNO3, 60%, guaranteed reagent chemicals; Daejung Chemicals & Metals), and sodium peroxide (Na2O2, 96%; Thermo Scientific). All these chemicals are of analytical grade.

REE Analysis

Sample Characterization and Elemental Analysis

The FA and BA samples were ground using a vibrating cup mill (FRITSCH) and characterized to determine their mineralogical and chemical composition. Mineralogical characterization was carried out using an XRD (D8 ADVANCE, Bruker), and chemical composition was determined with an X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (XRF) (MXF-2400, Shimadzu). The results of XRF analysis for both ashes are presented in Table . Sulfur content was measured using a carbon–sulfur analyzer (CS230, LECO), and ignition loss was determined using a muffle furnace (Ajeon Heating Industrial Co., Ltd.). Fractions obtained from sieve analysis and magnetic separation, as well as the leachates from the sequential extraction, were measured for elemental concentrations using ICP-OES (Optima 5300DV, PerkinElmer; 720-ES, Varian).

For REE quantification, ground samples were calcined in a crucible at 550 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min for 3 h using a digital muffle furnace (FHX-12, Daihan Scientific Co., Ltd.). The mass loss during calcination is summarized in Table S4. After cooling, 1 g of the calcined sample was mixed with 6 g of Na2O2 and homogenized for about 2 min, then roasted at 500 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min for 1 h. The cooled roasted mass was dissolved in 100 mL of boiling deionized (DI) water in two steps and left overnight for dissolution. Residual solids on the crucible walls were treated with 25% HNO3 under ultrasonication for approximately 3 min (Powersonic 405, Hwashin Technology Co., Ltd.). Additional 25% HNO3 was added to reach a total acid volume of 100 mL. The combined solution was stirred for at least 5 h, diluted with DI water to a final volume of 250 mL, filtered, and 50 mL aliquots were collected for ICP-OES analysis.

Quality Assurance and Quality Control

Analytical reliability was ensured through multipoint external calibration, procedural blanks, and replicate measurements. Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were calculated as 3.143 × SD and 10 × SD of blank signals, respectively, where SD is the standard deviation of replicate blank measurements. Calibration curves for all REE exhibited excellent linearity, with correlation coefficients (R 2) ≥ 0.998 (Tables S5,S6). Analytical precision was evaluated using replicate measurements and reported as relative standard deviation (RSD, %). Only concentrations above the LOD are reported, while values below the LOD are considered not detected (ND). Sample measurement uncertainty was calculated based on three replicate measurements. Instrument stability was verified through periodic calibration checks and quality control standards. Detailed analytical performance parameters, including LOD, LOQ, R 2, RSD, calibration slopes, and intercepts for sequential extraction and fractionated samples, are provided in Tables S5–S8.

Sequential Extraction Procedure

REE speciation and mobility were examined using a sequential extraction procedure consisting of five steps. Extractions were performed on an open-air shaker (OS-2000, JEIO TECH, Korea) at 350 rpm and room temperature. Centrifugation was conducted at 3000 rpm for 10 min during steps 1–3 using a Hanil MF550 centrifuge and at 4000 rpm for 15 min during steps 4–5 using a Hanil Combi 408 centrifuge. After each extraction and centrifugation, the supernatant was separated, and the residue was rinsed with the corresponding volume of distilled water specified in Table , followed by a second centrifugation. Different volumes were applied for BA and FA during the reducible fraction step because the two materials were processed as separate experiments. These adjustments were made for practical handling, and the rinse volumes are accounted for in the calculation of REE concentrations. The supernatants from the extraction and rinse steps were combined for subsequent ICP-OES analysis. The residue obtained after each step was used for the next extraction.

Sequential Extraction of MSWI Ash Fractions

The sequential extraction procedure was carried out to fractionate REE into water-soluble, ion exchangeable, carbonate bound, reducible, and acid soluble forms, as detailed in the following steps.

Step 1. Leaching of water-soluble REE from 2 g of pulverized FA or BA using 20 mL distilled water for 5 h 40 min.

Step 2. The step 1 residue was treated with 20 mL of 0.1 M MgCl2 for 3 h to extract the exchangeable fraction.

Step 3. Carbonate bound REE were removed by suspending the residue from step 2 in 10 mL of 0.1 N NaOAc/HOAc and agitating for 4 h.

Step 4. Reducible REE associated with Mn, and Fe oxides were extracted from the residue of step 3 using 400 mL of 0.5 M NH2OH·HCl with mechanical shaking for 24 h.

Step 5. The final extraction targeted REE bound in poorly soluble phosphate phases, like apatite. The residue from step 4 was resuspended in 400 mL of 6.5% HNO3 and mechanically shaken for 24 h.

Results and Discussion

Particle Size Distribution of FA and BA

Sieve analysis of BA indicated that particles greater than 5.6 mm were unburned residues, such as metal scraps and other debris, and were therefore discarded (Figure S2). A total of 72.14% of the BA was below 5.6 mm and considered representative for subsequent analyses. This fraction was further divided into three subfractions using sieving. Particle size, an important parameter for BA characterization, was categorized as <425 μm, 425 μm–1.7 mm, and 1.7 mm–5.6 mm for BA. FA particle size distribution was first determined using a Malvern analyzer, confirming the predominantly fine nature of the FA, with over 90% of particles under 150 μm (Table S1). FA was then divided into three size fractions by sieving, less than 25 μm, 25 μm to 53 μm, and greater than 53 μm (Table S3). These fractions were used for all subsequent analyses.

Chemical Compositions of FA and BA

The chemical compositions of BA and FA were determined using XRF, and the results are summarized in Table . The main constituents of both ashes are SiO2 and CaO. In BA, SiO2 (37.20 wt %) and CaO (25.34 wt %) dominate the composition, which is due to their low volatility and high boiling points. These are followed by Al2O3 (10.86 wt %) and Fe2O3 (6.37 wt %). In contrast, FA is characterized by a much higher CaO content (50.41 wt %). This enrichment is primarily associated with the injection of Ca­(OH)2 into the flue gas for the neutralization of acidic gases such as HCl and SO2 during air pollution control operations. , Other oxides, including SiO2 (5.35 wt %), Al2O3 (2.46 wt %), and Fe2O3 (1.62 wt %), occur at lower concentrations in the FA. Additional constituents, including MgO, K2O, Na2O, TiO2, MnO, P2O5, and SO3, are present in both ash types in varying proportions.

REE Speciation in FA and BA

Sequential extraction revealed distinct differences in the chemical forms of REE in FA and BA. The total REE concentrations were independently measured at 62.79 ± 3.57 ppm in raw FA and 144.96 ± 10.11 ppm in raw BA (Table ), showing that BA contains more REE than FA. In both ashes, REE were distributed across five fractions, water-soluble (step 1), ion exchangeable (step 2), carbonate bound (step 3), reducible (step 4), and acid soluble (step 5). All fraction values are expressed as percentages of these total REE concentrations (Table ). The remaining proportion was considered as the residual fraction and was estimated by subtracting the REE recovered in steps 1–5 from the independently measured total REE concentration. Because the sequential extraction procedure used in this study is not exhaustive, direct determination of the residual fraction typically requires an additional digestion step such as aqua regia, which was not applied here.

In FA, 5.92% of total REE were water-soluble and 11.50% were ion exchangeable, indicating that a portion of REE is loosely bound and potentially extractable under mild conditions. ,, In contrast, BA showed only 0.95 and 2.02% in these two fractions, respectively, reflecting stronger retention of REE within the BA matrix.

Carbonate bound REE were minimal in both materials, accounting for 2.12% in FA and 0.67% in BA, suggesting that carbonate dissolution contributes little to REE mobility. The largest proportion of REE occurred in the reducible fraction in FA at 45.66%, whereas in BA they were mainly distributed between the reducible and residual fractions, comprising 45.92 and 39.35%, respectively. XRD analysis revealed the presence of refractory silicate phases in BA, including anorthite (CaAl2Si2O8), melilite group minerals such as gehlenite (Ca2Al2SiO7), and akermanite (Ca2MgSi2O7). These Ca–Al silicate minerals can structurally incorporate REE (e.g., La3+, Ce3+) by substituting for Ca2+ within their crystal lattices, with charge balance maintained through stoichiometry. , The strong Si–O and Al–O bonds in these aluminosilicate minerals require high activation energy to break. Additionally, during acid treatment, silicate surfaces can develop a passivating amorphous silica layer, further limiting dissolution. These structural and chemical factors together hinder the release of REE, and some REE are physically encapsulated within the mineral matrices, explaining why a significant fraction (39.35%) remains in the residual fraction. This underscores the need for more intensive recovery strategies.

The acid soluble fraction accounted for 29.30% of total REE in FA and 11.10% in BA. In addition, the undissolved residual fraction indicates that BA contains a much larger proportion of stable REE bearing phases (39.35%) compared with FA (5.49%). , As summarized in Table , REE content (REE*) represents the total REE recovered in steps 1–5, while the remaining REE reside in the undissolved residual fraction. The results reflect the higher mineralogical stability of BA, indicating a greater abundance of silicate phases. These observations are consistent with the XRF and XRD results, which also show higher silicate content in BA than in FA.

The concentrations of individual REE in each extraction step are presented in Figure and Table S9, which provides the element specific distribution of REE across the sequential extraction fractions for FA and BA. In FA, LREE such as Ce, La and Pr, along with the HREE Gd and Y, dominate the extractable fractions, whereas most other HREE were not detected. Conversely, in BA, LREE including Ce, La, Nd, and Pr, as well as the heavy REE Gd, Tb and Y, were detected across multiple extraction steps.

The sequential extraction results indicate clear differences in how REE are retained in FA and BA. FA contains a larger proportion of REE in more accessible fractions, whereas BA has most REE bound within stable mineral phases. , Several elements, including Dy, Er, Eu, Lu, Tm, Yb, and Sc, were not detected in any sequential extraction step for both ashes. In addition, Ho and Sm were not detected in the BA, and Nd was not detected in the FA.

However, total digestion ICP-OES analysis showed that Ho and Sm were present in BA, and Nd, Er, Tm, and Yb were detected in both BA and FA (Table ). This indicates that elements not mobilized during the sequential extraction but detected by total digestion predominantly remain in the residual fraction and cannot be extracted by mild chemical treatments. ,

These speciation patterns have direct implications for recovery strategies. In FA, the more loosely bound REE can be targeted using mild chemical treatments, which mobilize a small portion of the REE. In contrast, the REE bearing phases in BA are more stable and therefore require more aggressive recovery approaches, such as stronger chemical or thermal treatments, reflecting the higher abundance of stable REE in BA. Understanding the REE distribution across mineralogical fractions is essential for developing efficient and selective extraction strategies from MSWI residues. These findings provide a basis for designing targeted extraction processes tailored to the specific mineralogical characteristics of FA and BA.

Effect of Particle Size and Magnetic Separation on REE Concentrations in MSWI Ashes

MSWI ashes contain measurable concentrations of REE in both BA and FA with their distribution influenced by particle size and magnetic properties. Magnetic separation can effectively isolate Fe bearing phases, such as hematite and magnetite as confirmed by XRD analysis (Figure and ), which are often enriched in REE. Additionally, coarser fractions tend to contain higher proportions of silicate rich particles, which incorporate elevated REE concentration.

REE concentrations in both FA and BA show enrichment in the magnetic fractions relative to the raw ash. Among the particle size fractions, the coarser fractions exhibit slightly higher REE concentrations than the raw ash, although the increase is minimal. In FA, the magnetic fraction exhibits a total REE (∑REE) concentration of 219.33 ± 8.01 ppm while representing only 1.66% of the total FA mass, which is more than three times higher than the 62.79 ± 3.57 ppm measfured in the raw FA. Elements such as Ce, Gd, La, Nd, Pr, Tb, and Y are particularly enriched in the magnetic fraction. For instance, Ce increases from 15.31 ± 0.82 ppm in the raw ash to 39.93 ± 0.98 ppm in the magnetic fraction, and La from 8.00 ± 0.46 ppm to 22.04 ± 0.53 ppm. The nonmagnetic fraction of FA accounts for 65.51% of the material and contains 68.60 ± 4.04 ppm ∑REE, thereby retaining most of the REE inventory due to its large mass proportion. The total loss of FA during wet separation was 32.83 %. Elements such as Dy, Eu, Ho, Lu, and Sc remained below detection limits in all fractions Figure .

A similar pattern is observed in BA. The magnetic fraction contains 232.22 ± 10.6 ppm ∑REE while representing 16.09 % of the ash, which is higher than the 144.96 ± 10.11 ppm measured in the raw BA. The nonmagnetic fraction, accounting for 78.69 % of the total mass, contains 110.82 ± 8.49 ppm and therefore holds the largest share of the overall REE inventory. As with FA, the fractions do not total 100%, showing that 5.22% of the BA was lost during wet separation.

The enrichment of REE in the magnetic fraction reflects association with Fe-rich spinel phases, such as magnetite. , Coprecipitation studies have demonstrated that REE (Dy, Nd, La) can occupy the magnetite lattice, implying that REE are capable of incorporating into the spinel structure; however, this occurs under controlled chemical synthesis conditions that differ from the ash formation environments. In the present case, Fe-spinel (magnetite) is more likely associated with amorphous or graphitic carbon containing REE, contributing to their enrichment in magnetite-rich ash fractions. ,, REE in the magnetic fractions can be encapsulated within carbon surrounding magnetite grains, rather than being incorporated into the Fe-spinel lattice. REE such as La, Ce, Nd, Pr, and Gd have been reported to occur as nanoscale particles within these carbon coatings, which allow their comigration with magnetite during magnetic separation. These observations suggest that REE can migrate with magnetite during beneficiation and magnetic separation processes.

The enrichment observed in the magnetic and nonmagnetic fractions of FA may be partly influenced by the wet treatment process. During wet magnetic separation, dissolution of salts such as CaClOH, KCl, and NaCl reduces the bulk mass of the ash, which may lead to passive enrichment of REE due to the removal of material with lower REE content. In FA, where 32.83% of the mass was lost during wet separation, the REE concentration would theoretically increase from 62.79 ppm to approximately 93.48 ppm solely due to mass reduction. However, the measured REE concentration in the magnetic fraction of FA (219.33 ppm) is much higher than this theoretical value, indicating that magnetic separation selectively concentrates REE-bearing phases, in addition to enrichment caused by mass reduction.

In addition, particle size fractionation indicates that in FA, the coarser fraction (>53 μm) exhibits a higher ∑REE concentration of 77.36 ± 3.14 ppm relative to raw FA (62.79 ± 3.57 ppm). In BA, ∑REE concentrations increase from 144.96 ± 10.11 ppm in the raw BA to 164.68 ± 1.27 ppm in the 1.7–5.6 mm fraction. Individual REEs such as Ce, Er, La, and Y display similar distribution patterns, with slightly elevated concentrations observed in coarser fractions. Dy, Eu, Ho, and Lu remain below detection limits across all fractions, while Sc, Sm, and Tm remain below detection limits in FA. Particle size fractionation does not result in significant REE enrichment across size classes, although a tendency toward greater concentrations in coarser fractions is observed.

These findings demonstrate that MSWI ashes contain appreciable REE concentrations, suggesting their potential as a secondary source of REE. The REE concentrations measured in BA and FA in this study are generally consistent with previously reported values (Table S10). As observed in other studies, ,, BA exhibits higher REE concentrations than FA, reflecting the preferential retention of REEs in BA during incineration, while a portion is transferred to FA via flue gas. Subsequent treatment processes, such as magnetic separation and particle size fractionation, can further concentrate REE in specific ash fractions.

While the present study focuses on characterization, the distribution of REE across magnetic and particle size fractions provides insight into their occurrence in different ash fractions and may inform future processing considerations. Although the magnetic fractions exhibit elevated REE concentrations, their low mass proportion limits their overall contribution to the total REE inventory. In contrast, the nonmagnetic fractions retain the majority of REE due to their dominant mass share. Variations in REE concentrations among particle size fractions are relatively small. Furthermore, sequential extraction results indicate that most REE are associated with Fe/Mn oxides and the residual fraction. REE in the reducible fraction can be released through reductive dissolution, followed by acid leaching. In contrast, REE in the residual fraction are incorporated within more stable mineral phases, such as the aluminosilicate matrix, and therefore require more intensive treatments, including alkali leaching or alkali fusion to break down the mineral framework and release the contained REE, allowing subsequent extraction using acids, or chemical roasting followed by acid leaching.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrate that MSWI ash contains measurable concentrations of REE and may serve as a secondary source. Among the investigated fractions, the coarse fraction showed slightly higher total REE concentrations compared with the raw ash, indicating no strong enrichment.In addition, magnetic fractions of both BA and FA were enriched in REE compared with their nonmagnetic counterparts. However, due to their larger mass proportions, the nonmagnetic fractions retain a substantial share of the total REE inventory. Sequential extraction revealed differences in REE speciation between ash types. In FA, a greater fraction of REE occurred in water-soluble and ion-exchangeable phases compared with BA, reflecting relatively weaker binding within the ash matrix. Both ash types contained minor REE fractions associated with carbonate phases. However, most REE in both BA and FA were associated with Fe/Mn oxide phases. FA contained only a small residual REE fraction, whereas BA retained a considerable residual fraction, suggesting that a significant share of REE in BA remains incorporated within stable mineral matrices such as silicates or refractory oxides. In summary, these findings indicate that MSWI ash shows distinct REE distribution patterns across fractions, with magnetic fractions exhibiting higher concentrations but nonmagnetic fractions dominating the overall REE inventory due to their mass contribution, while FA contains comparatively more labile REE associations. These results provide a characterization of REE distribution and speciation in MSWI ash. Future work should focus on developing improved enrichment approaches and exploring extraction methods tailored to ash mineralogy and REE speciation.
