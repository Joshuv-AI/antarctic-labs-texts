An improved sinh cosh optimizer for optimal scheduling of a microgrid with multi-energy resources and storage considering different weather conditions
Authors: Junjie Ma, Huao Shen, Saber Arabi Nowdeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growing integration of renewable and distributed energy resources has increased the complexity of microgrid (MG) operation due to inherent uncertainties and the trade-off between economic and environmental objectives. Developing an optimization framework that simultaneously addresses renewable intermittency, electric vehicle (EV) participation, and stochastic variations in weather-dependent generation and demand remains a significant challenge. This study presents a multi-objective and stochastic optimization framework for MG scheduling, including photovoltaic (PV), microturbines (MT), fuel cells (FC), battery energy storage systems (BESS), and EVs under demand response (DR) based on real data. The MG is optimized to meet daily demand under three weather conditions—sunny, cloudy, and rainy—while minimizing operating cost and emissions. An Improved Sine Cosine Optimizer (ISCHO), enhanced with chaos theory, is developed to prevent premature convergence. Furthermore, an Adaptive Weighted Unscented Transformation (AWUT-MG) is introduced to efficiently capture uncertainties in PV generation and load demand while maintaining computational efficiency. Three operational cases are examined: (i) MG without DR, (ii) MG with DR and EV integration, and (iii) stochastic MG optimization under uncertainty. The results demonstrate that EV integration substantially improves flexibility, reducing both operating cost and emissions across different weather conditions. ISCHO consistently outperforms SCHO, PSO, and SCA in terms of convergence speed and solution quality. Incorporating stochastic modeling through AWUT-MG provides increases of 13.81%, 14.84%, and 16.01% in operating cost and 6.17%, 6.41%, and 6.63% in emissions across sunny, cloudy, and rainy conditions, respectively—highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed AWUT-MG framework for realistic, uncertainty-aware MG scheduling.

Introduction

Motivation and background

The accelerated global transition toward decarbonization integrates EVs and RES into the power system at unprecedented rates1,2. In return for reduced emissions and transformed grid infrastructure—most specifically at the MG level3—the unique operational challenges are introduced. The widespread adoption of EVs increases peak load demand4, whereas the intermittency of RES threatens to compromise supply stability5. MGs, however, present a decentralized solution by integrating EVs, RES, and energy storage to enhance local balancing and resilience. Nevertheless, the stochastic nature of EVs and renewables complicates MG operation. Emerging technologies of V2G and DR enhance flexibility and performance through bidirectional energy exchange and adaptive load management, respectively6,7. Dynamic electricity price, load patterns, and renewable generation require sophisticated energy management. Stochastic optimization is able to handle these uncertainties through probabilistic modeling8 and allows making reliable and cost-effective decisions9. DR, EV flexibility combined with uncertainty-aware mechanisms and advanced optimization techniques: indeed, all critical enablers of sustainable, resilient, and efficient operation of MGs.

Related works and research gaps

Recent studies have addressed MG energy management from the points of view of objective functions, limitations, and resolution methods, including DR, cost and emission reduction, and system flexibility. In10, an Energy Management System (EMS) using the Bald Eagle Search (BES) algorithm was developed for MGs integrating RESs and PHEVs, minimizing operational costs and emissions through multi-objective optimization. A hybrid Beluga Whale Optimization (BWO)–Dandelion Optimizer further improved convergence and solution quality. Reference11 developed an improved day-ahead scheduling framework for microgrids incorporating wind power, where coordinated use of energy storage and a hybrid demand response approach manages real-time price fluctuations by coupling incentive-driven load shifting with price elasticity analysis. In12, a virtual power plant (VPP) integrates renewables and CHP units for optimal scheduling, where renewable uncertainty is managed using the Red Fox Optimization (RFO) algorithm to improve system reliability and efficiency. A hierarchical energy management system (EMS) for multi-microgrid coordination was proposed in13, where each MG performs local optimization while a higher-level Distribution System Operator (DSO) oversees system-wide decisions, thereby improving scalability and flexibility. In14, a hybrid Gravitational Search Algorithm (GSA) and Pattern Search (PS) approach was developed for MG scheduling with EV integration, aiming to minimize operational costs through efficient energy dispatch. Similarly15, investigated MG planning with multiple PHEVs and RESs to achieve economic, technical, and environmental advantages, employing Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS) to model uncertainties and a Modified Sparrow Search (MSS) algorithm for optimization. In16, the coordination of DR programs with MG operation planning is investigated to enhance flexibility and cost savings. Distributed RESs, BESS devices, and EVs’ capacity and scheduling are optimally estimated by the work, adapting to dynamic system requirements. To address uncertainties in RES generation, load patterns, and energy prices, Hong’s two-point estimation method (2 m + 1 PEM) was employed by the authors, offering a computationally efficient alternative to full probabilistic modeling. In17, an EV- and battery-based hybrid renewable energy system was proposed to minimize maintenance, construction, and battery degradation costs, using a hybrid metaheuristic strategy based on the Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) algorithm for improved convergence in complex MG scheduling problems. Reference18 proposed a fuzzy-based optimal scheduling model for hydrogen-integrated energy systems, accounting for the operational characteristics of electrolyzers and hydrogen fuel cells under various scenarios. The model employs the CEPLEX solver and utilizes fuzzy chance-constrained programming to effectively represent and manage system uncertainties. Reference19 introduced an innovative commercial framework that facilitates shared rental energy storage among several industrial users. The study designed a two-layer iterative planning model grounded in robust optimization and demand protection concepts, aiming to enhance the economic return on shared storage investments while accounting for uncertainties in net load variations. In20, a transactive multi-energy microgrid framework is proposed that incorporates various energy conversion technologies to address both environmental and economic objectives, aiming to minimize operational expenses and reduce greenhouse gas emissions. An Improved Gradient-Based Optimization (IGBO) algorithm for MG energy management was proposed in21 to enhance renewable utilization and minimize operating costs, showing strong performance in dynamic, multi-objective scheduling. In22, an EMS for isolated, meshed MGs was designed to ensure autonomous operation and economic feasibility during grid outages, combining DR programs with a novel marginal pricing scheme and deep learning–based flexibility for improved convergence and optimal scheduling. Based on networked MG operation23, introduced a multifunctional EMS for RES-based MG clusters with energy storage, supporting fully isolated, interconnected, and hybrid modes. Furthermore24, presented a residential MG planning framework integrating PV, BESS, and ACWH to improve energy efficiency, reliability, and sustainability using climate data and simulation-based control. Lastly25, proposed a stochastic EMS for a hybrid grid-connected MG comprising MTs, PVs, WTs, and BESS, employing a Modified Zebra Optimization Algorithm (MZOA) to enhance performance under uncertainty. Reference26 introduces a bi-level risk-constrained EMS for a multi-MG system with CHP capacity to maximize the overall system profit for competitive electricity and heat markets via a Value-at-Risk (CVaR) methodology.

Summary of the literature review.

In27, a reverse incentive-based DR strategy was proposed for industrial shared energy storage systems to enhance profitability and optimize electricity revenues. In28, a bi-level optimization model based on Stackelberg game theory was developed for multi-MG thermal energy storage systems (MMG-TES) to minimize emissions and operating costs through coordinated interaction between MGs and DSOs. In29, an enhanced arithmetic optimization technique, referred to as the Lévy Arithmetic Algorithm, is employed for optimal off-grid MG dispatch with a combination of solar, wind, and diesel generators. The method proposed optimizes economic efficiency and reduction of emissions in comparison to traditional methods of optimization in terms of cost savings and environmental gains. Reference30 examined the integration of DR strategies in MG operations to address RES uncertainty, fluctuating demand, and volatile electricity prices, thereby reducing costs and enhancing reliability. In31, a rule-based EMS employing the Levy Flight Algorithm was proposed to manage a hybrid hydrogen–battery storage system for improved efficiency and lower operational expenses. The study in32 developed a hydrogen-based EMS for CHP MGs using a multi-objective Salp Swarm Algorithm (SSA) to minimize costs and optimize hydrogen utilization from renewables. In33, an EMS based on the Multi-Verse Optimizer (MVO) was applied to multi-MG systems to achieve balanced trade-offs among cost, reliability, and renewable utilization. A policy gradient model for real-time MG energy balancing with PHEV optimization in interconnected networks was introduced in34. Finally35, presented a stochastic optimization framework for grid-connected MGs employing Beta and Weibull distributions to model renewable variability, effectively reducing both operational costs and emissions. Recent advancements in MG energy management, as reflected in studies10–34–35, have focused on improving cost efficiency, emission reduction, and system reliability through hybrid optimization and intelligent control. Various algorithms have been developed to enhance convergence and scheduling precision, while probabilistic approaches like MCS have addressed renewable uncertainty36. Several works have also introduced hierarchical EMS architectures and coordinated DR and EV participation to strengthen grid flexibility. Despite these achievements, most existing methods still face challenges related to computational complexity, oversimplified uncertainty modeling, and limited adaptability to variable weather and market conditions. These limitations motivate the present study to propose a more robust, uncertainty-aware, and practically implementable optimization framework for sustainable MG operation. However, many of these approaches still face critical shortcomings that hinder their applicability to realistic and dynamic energy environments.

Altough several studies10,14,19,25,29,30 have optimized MG operation for cost and efficiency, most neglect the influence of diverse weather patterns—such as sunny, cloudy, and rainy conditions—on emission minimization from non-renewable sources and storage systems. Contributions

This research advances the state of the art in microgrid (MG) energy management and stochastic optimization through the following key contributions:

A comprehensive multi-objective stochastic optimization framework is developed for optimal scheduling and energy management of a grid-connected MG comprising PV systems, MT, FC, BESS, and electric vehicles (EVs) under DR programs. The fuzzy multi-objective framework simultaneously minimizes operational cost and emissions while explicitly considering three distinct weather conditions—sunny, cloudy, and rainy—to reflect realistic and variable operating environments.An Improved Sinh Cosh Optimizer (ISCHO) is proposed by embedding chaos theory into the conventional Sinh–Cosh Optimizer (SCHO)37 to find the decision variables of the problem. Chaos theory can be applied advantageously to overcome some SCHO limitations, such as premature convergence or reduced diversity due to inherent ergodicity, non-repetitiveness, and dynamic sensitivity, enhancing global exploration and enabling effective escapes from local optima.A novel stochastic optimization technique, termed Adaptive Weighted Unscented Transformation for MG Optimization (AWUT-MG), is proposed to efficiently model uncertainties in PV generation, load demand, and electricity prices. Unlike Monte Carlo simulation, which needs a very large number of random samples, AWUT-MG uses the unscented transformation to select a minimal set of intelligently chosen sigma points, reaching comparable accuracy with orders of magnitude lower computational cost, while adaptively weighting scenarios based on their operational impact.Three comparative case studies—deterministic optimization, DR with EV integration, and stochastic optimization under uncertainty—are conducted to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed methods in handling uncertainty and improving economic and environmental performance.A detailed performance analysis demonstrates that ISCHO consistently outperforms benchmark algorithms such as SCHO, PSO, and SCA in terms of convergence speed, solution quality, and robustness, confirming its superiority for complex MG scheduling tasks.

Paper structure

Section 3 proposes a new stochastic approach, named AWUT-MG, that allows managing uncertainties in crucial MG variables. Sections 4 and 5 present the results of simulations, practical implications, and discussion in detail on the performance of the proposed framework. Finally, Sect. 6 summarizes the findings of the research.

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\dot{m}}_{{H}_{2}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} is hydrogen mass flow rate (kg/h), ηFC is FC electrical efficiency (typically 0.4–0.6), LHVH2 is lower heating value of hydrogen.

The operating cost of a FC at time t is normally represented by a quadratic cost function10,32:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{FC}}\left(t\right)={\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{FC}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{FC}}^{2}\left(t\right)+{\beta\:}_{\mathrm{FC}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{FC}}\left(t\right)+{\gamma\:}_{\mathrm{FC}}$$\end{document}

Where, CFC(t) is the overall operating cost of the FC at time t, αFC is the coefficient of the nonlinear (quadratic) cost of output power and loss of efficiency, βFC is linear coefficient for fuel and maintenance-cost, and γFC is fixed operating cost.

MT model

MT model

MT model

Microturbines (MTs) are compact, reliable generators that can use various fuels. They enhance microgrids by providing agile, controllable power, improving resilience, stability, and cost-effectiveness10,12,25. The fuel consumption rate of an MT, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\dot{f}}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, typically is a piecewise-linear or linear function of power output10,12,25:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\dot{f}}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)={a}_{\mathrm{MT}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)+{b}_{\mathrm{MT}}$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\dot{f}}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} is the fuel consumption rate (e.g., m³/h or kg/h), and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${a_{{\mathrm{MT}}}},{b_{{\mathrm{MT}}}}$$\end{document}are empirical coefficients derived from manufacturer data or experimental results.

Otherwise, efficiency-based modeling is used10,12,25:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\eta\:}_{\mathrm{MT}}=\frac{{P}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)}{{\dot{f}}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)\cdot\:LH{V}_{\mathrm{fuel}}}$$\end{document}

Where, LHVfuel ​ is the lower heating value of the fuel (e.g., for natural gas: ~10 kWh/m³).

The MT operating cost is defined via a quadratic cost relation taking into account fuel consumption, maintenance, and wear10,12,25:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)={\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{MT}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{MT}}^{2}\left(t\right)+{\beta\:}_{\mathrm{MT}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)+{\gamma\:}_{\mathrm{MT}}$$\end{document}

Where, CMT​(t) operating cost at time t (e.g., $/h), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha _{{\mathrm{MT}}}},{\beta _{{\mathrm{MT}}}},{\gamma _{{\mathrm{MT}}}}$$\end{document}are cost coefficients taking into account fuel consumption, maintenance, and fixed costs. The cost function is integrated in MG optimization models so that the total generation cost is minimized while ensuring reliable operation.

Objective function

Costs of operating

The operating cost is the total expense of running a microgrid, covering power generation, storage, grid transactions, and EV management. An accurate cost model is crucial for optimizing energy management under economic and environmental constraints. The total cost of operation at time t, represented as Ctotal(t), includes the following14–17:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{total}}\left(t\right)={C}_{PV}\left(t\right)+{C}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)+{C}_{\mathrm{FC}}\left(t\right)+{C}_{\mathrm{Grid}}\left(t\right)+{C}_{\mathrm{BESS}}\left(t\right)+{C}_{\mathrm{EV}}\left(t\right)+{C}_{\mathrm{DR}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{PV}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{MT}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{FC}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{Grid}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{BESS}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{EV}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{DR}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} denote operating cost of PV, MT, FC, Grid, BESS, EV, and DR at time t, respectively.

To obtain cumulative operation cost within a scheduling period T, total cost is added across all time instants:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{total}}={\sum\:}_{t=1}^{T}{C}_{\mathrm{total}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

This objective function may be optimized in the MG’s EMS optimization algorithm, subject to power balance, resource availability, and reliability requirements.

Operation cost of PV is represented by14–1712\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{PV}}={\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{PV}}.{P}_{PV}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{PV}}$$\end{document} is per kW PV power price.

The cost of BESS operation taking into account charging/discharging activity is as follows14–17:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{BESS}}\left(t\right)={\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{ch,BESS}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{ch,\:BESS}}\left(t\right)-{\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{disch,BESS}}.{P}_{\mathrm{disch,\:BESS}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha _{{\mathrm{ch,BESS}}}}$$\end{document}and ​\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha _{{\mathrm{disch,BESS}}}}$$\end{document}are the cost for each unit of charged and discharged battery energy.

The selling or buying price of electricity from/to the main grid is a function of the market price at each time step14–17:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{Grid}}\left(t\right)={\rho\:}_{purch}\left(t\right)\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{grid}}^{purch}\left(t\right)-{\rho\:}_{\mathrm{sell}}\left(t\right)\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{grid}}^{sell}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rho _{purch}}(t)$$\end{document}is the cost of purchased power from the grid for each unit ($/kWh), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rho _{{\mathrm{sell}}}}(t)$$\end{document}is cost of sold power to the grid for each unit ($/kWh), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{{{\mathrm{grid}}}}^{{purch}}(t)$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{{{\mathrm{grid}}}}^{{sell}}(t)$$\end{document}are purchased and sold grid power power (kW).

When EVs participate in energy management through charging or discharging, their operating cost is also developed14–17:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{EV}}\left(t\right)={\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{ch,EV}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{ch,\:EV}}\left(t\right)+{\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{disch,EV}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{disch,\:EV}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha _{{\mathrm{ch,EV}}}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha _{{\mathrm{disch,EV}}}}$$\end{document}are the cost for each unit of energy charged and discharged of the EV.

DR is stimulated by monetary compensation for voluntary load reduction or load shifting14–17:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{DR}={\lambda\:}_{i}\left(t\right).D{R}_{i}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, DRi(t) is customer i demand reduction (kW), and λi(t) is DR incentive price for each unit of reduced power ($/kWh) for customer i.

Emissions

Environmental management in a MG is as important as cost management and system reliability10,29. Total emissions at time t, Etotal(t), are the total emissions from all fossil-based generation units and grid imports, and are calculated as10,29:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{total}}\left(t\right)={E}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)+{E}_{\mathrm{FC}}\left(t\right)+{E}_{\mathrm{grid}}^{purch}\left(t\right)-{E}_{\mathrm{grid}}^{sell}\left(t\right)+{E}_{\mathrm{ch,BESS}}\left(t\right)-{E}_{\mathrm{dis,BESS}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, EMT​(t) denotes the emissions of the MT at time t, EFC​(t) denotes emissions of the FC at time t, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{{{\mathrm{grid}}}}^{{purch}}(t)$$\end{document}denotes emissions from electricity purchased from the grid, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{{{\mathrm{grid}}}}^{{sell}}(t)$$\end{document}denotes emission from electricity fed to the grid, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{ch,BESS}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{dis,BESS}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} denote the emission of charge and discharge of the BESS at time t.

It is important to note that the BESS itself produces no direct emissions during operation. The terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{dis,BESS}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{dis,BESS}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} represent indirect, system-level emission factors. The charging term allocates emissions from the generation source used to charge the BESS, while the discharging term represents the emissions offset by displacing generation from other units, thus appearing as a negative value in the total emission balance.

For calculation of cumulative emissions over the entire scheduling period T, emission function is summed for each time step10,29:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{total}}={\sum\:}_{t=1}^{T}{E}_{\mathrm{total}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

MT emission is represented linearly as a function of output power by19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)={\epsilon\:}_{\mathrm{MT}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{MT}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, εMT is MT emission coefficient, PMT(t) is actual-time power output of the MT (kW).

FC produces electricity from hydrogen, and their emissions are different depending on the source of hydrogen. The model of emission is defined by14–17:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{FC}}\left(t\right)={\epsilon\:}_{\mathrm{FC}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{FC}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon _{{\mathrm{FC}}}}$$\end{document}refers to the hydrogen effective emission factor.

The BESS discharging emissions are represented as follows14–17:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{dis,BESS}}\left(t\right)={\epsilon\:}_{BESS}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{dis,BESS}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P_{{\mathrm{dis,BESS}}}}(t)$$\end{document}is the BESS discharging power at time t (kW), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon _{BESS}}$$\end{document}is the BESS discharging emission factor at time t.

The BESS charging emissions are represented as follows14–17:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{ch,BESS}}\left(t\right)={\epsilon\:}_{BESS}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{ch,BESS}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P_{{\mathrm{ch,BESS}}}}(t)$$\end{document}is the BESS power charged at time t (kW).

Grid import emissions depend on the energy mix in the grid, which is location and time dependent. The purchased electricity emission is expressed as14–17:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{grid}}^{purch}\left(t\right)={\epsilon\:}_{\mathrm{grid,purch}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{grid}}^{purch}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon _{{\mathrm{grid,purch}}}}$$\end{document}is grid purchased power emission factor at time t, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{{{\mathrm{grid}}}}^{{purch}}(t)$$\end{document}is power sold to the grid (kW).

When selling electricity to the grid, emissions are likely to be offset. Emissions of selling electricity are expressed as14–17:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{\mathrm{grid}}^{sell}\left(t\right)={\epsilon\:}_{\mathrm{grid,sell}}\cdot\:{P}_{\mathrm{grid}}^{sell}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon _{{\mathrm{grid,sell}}}}$$\end{document}is grid emission factor of power sold at time t, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{{{\mathrm{grid}}}}^{{sell}}(t)$$\end{document}is power sold to the grid (kW).

Power balance constraint

PV constraint

BESS constraint

Proposed optimizer

In this study, the new improved Sinh Cosh optimizer ISCHO that is founded on chaos theory has been employed for the optimal sizing and efficient energy management of the MG. Through the integration of chaotic maps into optimization, the algorithm enhances convergence rate and solution diversity, enabling good performance to search for the optimum capacities of MG devices for various wether conditions.

The conventional Sinh Cosh optimizer (SCHO) is a metaheuristic algorithm inspired by the mathematical properties of hyperbolic functions (sinh and cosh), which are leveraged to effectively balance the exploration and exploitation phases of the optimization process. The algorithm operates through three main phases: an initial exploration phase, an intensified search within promising regions, and a bounded search strategy to refine the solution space boundaries. During exploration, candidate solutions are updated using stochastic and hyperbolic weight coefficients to encourage diversity and avoid premature convergence. In the exploitation phase, the search focuses locally around the best-found solutions, utilizing hyperbolic functions to guide the population toward more precise optima. A dynamic switching mechanism, governed by a parameter that adapts over iterations, regulates the transition between these phases. The complete mathematical formulation and detailed mechanics of the SCHO algorithm are thoroughly described in the original reference37.

Multi-objective optimization

In multi-objective optimization, there are inherent conflicts among objectives operating cost and emissions, for instance, and improvement in one metric often involves the deterioration of another. A fuzzy decision-making approach is thus applied to convert quantitative objective values into normalized satisfaction degrees (µ) that range from 0 (total dissatisfaction) to 1 (complete satisfaction). Such membership functions allow for smooth trade-off analysis and provide a built-in ability to make decisions using human-like reasoning. By aggregating the satisfaction degrees through the application of fuzzy operators, such as the weighted average, this approach recognizes optimal compromise solutions from the Pareto front and offers flexibility and robustness in making any decision. For a particular objective OFδ​, the corresponding membership function µk(fk) is generally defined in a piecewise manner as38:42\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{z}\left({OF}_{z}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}1,&\:if\:{OF}_{z}\le\:{OF}_{z}^{\mathrm{min}}\\\:\frac{{OF}_{z}^{\mathrm{max}}-{OF}_{z}}{{OF}_{z}^{\mathrm{max}}-{OF}_{z}^{\mathrm{min}}},&\:if\:{OF}_{z}^{\mathrm{min}}<{OF}_{z}<{OF}_{z}^{\mathrm{max}}\\\:0,&\:if\:{OF}_{z}\ge\:{OF}_{z}^{\mathrm{max}}\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

Where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{OF}_{z}^{\mathrm{min}}$$\end{document}​, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{OF}_{z}^{\mathrm{max}}$$\end{document} represent the desirable and undesirable bounds, respectively, for the z-th objective. This function smoothly scales the satisfaction level depending on how close the objective value is to the optimal region.

Once individual satisfaction degrees for all objectives are obtained from membership functions, they are combined into a total fuzzy satisfaction level (µT) by a weighted sum approach. In this approach, predefined weights WIz are assigned to each objective according to the relative importance of the objectives, which reflects system priorities or decision-maker preferences. The result, µT, is a scalar measure that allows quantification of overall solution performance, hence enabling the ranking and selection of optimum solutions with an indication of the trade-offs between conflicting objectives38.43\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{T}=\sum\:_{z=1}^{Z}{WI}_{z}\cdot\:{\mu\:}_{z}\left({OF}_{z}\right)$$\end{document}

Fuzzy decision-making aggregates objective satisfaction levels to choose balanced compromises, hence offering robust and flexible solutions to real-world multi-objective problems with conflicting goals.

Proposed stochastic approach: AWUT-MG

Microgrid energy management is confronted with significant uncertainties in PV generation, market prices, and load demand. Traditional approaches, such as Monte Carlo simulation, handle uncertainties but are computationally expensive. Unscented Transformation, on the other hand, offers a much more efficient alternative with better accuracy over Taylor expansion and fewer samples. However, UT suffers from limitations regarding fixed weighting and poor adaptability. In order to overcome these deficiencies, this paper proposes AWUT-MG, Adaptive Weighted Unscented Transformation for Microgrid Optimization, an improved UT with dynamic weight assignment, scenario reduction, and integration with multi-objective optimizer ISCHO.

Case I involves the multi-objective optimization and energy management of a microgrid with various energy sources and storage, without using a DR strategy. The goal is to minimize both operating costs and emissions. The performance of a new algorithm, ISCHO (an improved version of SCHO based on chaos theory), was tested against the traditional SCHO, PSO, and SCA algorithms under three different weather conditions: Sunny, Cloudy, and Rainy. The best results for this case are presented in Table 4, while a comparative analysis of the objectives and computational costs is illustrated in Fig. 7. The ISCHO algorithm delivers the best performance across all tested weather conditions. In sunny weather, it achieves the lowest operating cost ($955.76), emissions (1177.42 kg), and computation time (25.84 s), primarily due to high solar power generation reducing reliance on polluting sources. Under cloudy weather, with reduced solar power, ISCHO maintains its superior performance with the lowest cost ($1034.46), emissions (1243.75 kg), and fastest computation. It effectively manages the increased need for dispatchable sources like MT and FC by optimizing their use alongside storage (BESS and EV), resulting in only a slight, expected increase in cost and emissions compared to sunny conditions. The conclusion is that ISCHO consistently outperforms all other algorithms in all weather conditions. Its success is attributed to its hybrid design, which incorporates chaos theory to effectively escape local optima and find better solutions more quickly and efficiently.

The analysis confirms that the microgrid performance is directly related to the availability of solar energy, and high PV generation under sunny conditions reduces both the operational cost and emissions. In cloudy conditions, the performance degrades a bit, and under rainy conditions, there is a sharp increase in thermal sources involvement, leading to higher costs and emissions. This again establishes the importance of maximum utilization of available renewable energy, particularly solar PV, in an optimal microgrid energy management strategy. Numerical results from Table 4 demonstrate that the ISCHO algorithm outperforms SCHO, PSO, and SCA in all kinds of weather conditions by reducing the operating costs, emissions, and computational time in concert. In particular, ISCHO has yielded the best optimal solutions for sunny ($955.76, 1177.42 kg, 25.84 s), cloudy ($1034.46, 1243.75 kg, 22.74 s), and rainy weather conditions ($1089.39, 1275.01 kg, 25.44 s). This justifies the enhanced global search capability, proper balance between exploration and exploitation activities, and precise derivation of optimality by the proposed ISCHO. All these pieces of evidence establish ISCHO as a promising approach for multi-objective optimization in modern microgrid energy management.

Pareto solution set of different algorithms in sunny weather conditions for Case II for different algorithms are presented in Fig. 8. The maximum hypervolume (often called Pareto front hypervolume) is a performance indicator used in multi-objective optimization. It measures the total volume in the objective space that is dominated by the obtained Pareto front and bounded by a reference point. In simpler terms, it quantifies how well the set of solutions covers the trade-off region between objectives—larger hypervolume means the algorithm has found more diverse and higher-quality trade-off solutions (closer to the true optimal front). So, when the proposed ISCHO achieves a higher maximum hypervolume, it means it finds better and more balanced solutions between cost and emissions compared to other algorithms.

Results of case III

In Case III, the microgrid optimization is performed for uncertainties in PV generation, load demand, and electricity prices using the proposed AWUT-MG method combined with ISCHO. Unlike the deterministic approaches, AWUT-MG utilizes the Unscented Transformation to generate 2n + 1 (seven for n = 3 uncertain parameters) representative sigma points that capture the statistical properties of input uncertainties. Each sigma point scenario is optimized separately using ISCHO to determine the optimal dispatch of all the distributed energy resources. The ISCHO algorithm solves all seven sigma-point cases separately to get the best operation and dispatch schedule of all DERs, including photovoltaic systems, FCs, MTs, batteries, EVs, and DR programs. Afterwards, aggregation is done through an adaptive weighting scheme based on each scenario’s sensitivity impact on operational objectives, cost, and emissions, in such a way that higher-weighted scenarios have a greater contribution towards the final decision. Further, filtering based on the KL divergence removes duplicate scenarios, maintaining modeling accuracy with a reduced computational burden.

According to numerical results indicated in Table 7; Fig. 13, for sunny conditions, operating cost increased from $733.53 to $834.84 (13.81%), and emissions from 1066.90 kg to 1132.73 kg (6.17%) with consideration of uncertainty. For cloudy conditions, operating cost increased from $750.90 to $862.34 (14.84%), and emissions from 1140.07 kg to 1213.14 kg (6.41%). In rainy weather, operation cost rose from $800.05 to $928.21 (16.01%), and emissions rose from 1156.11 kg to 1232.76 kg (6.63%). The evidence is clear as daylight that volatilities in the output of the renewables, demand, and market price can play a crucial role in determining MG performance, leading to increased cost and emissions compared to deterministic models.

A main advantage of the proposed AWUT-MG framework is its computational efficiency. Whereas the stochastic model presented, Case III in Table 7, required around 62–70 s, a comparable Monte Carlo Simulation, using 1000 scenarios based on the same uncertainty distributions, would call for an estimated 7.2–8.3 h. This dramatic reduction, achieved using only 7 intelligently selected sigma-points instead of thousands of random samples, underlines AWUT-MG’s suitability for practical day-ahead microgrid scheduling for which computational time is a crucial issue.

Discussion

This section analyzes the results of the simulations and their practical implications for real-world microgrid operations, based on five main aspects: demand response strategies, electric vehicle integration, stochastic modeling, weather impacts, and time resolution effects.

Effect of DR Program

Demand Response significantly improves microgrid flexibility and economic performance by shifting loads from peak periods. It maximizes the usage of renewables in sunny weather, stabilizes the operations during cloudy weather, and mitigates performance loss during rainy periods by optimizing the dispatch of resources. In fact, DR is quite essential for reliable and sustainable operation when there are fluctuations in weather and generation.

EV Integration Effect

Electric Vehicles act as mobile storage, which can take up and give out energy to and from the grid in both directions. It absorbs surplus renewable energy when demands are low and gives power at peak demands, providing better load balancing and lowering usage of conventional generators. EV integration enhances system reliability and efficiency in any weather, facilitating higher renewable utilization.

Effect of Stochastic Modeling and Uncertainty Representation

The AWUT-MG framework provides a realistic representation of uncertainties in solar generation, load demand, and electricity prices. While stochastic modeling provides moderately higher costs and emissions due to the inclusion of built-in risk buffers, it ensures operational feasibility under unforeseeable conditions. Adaptive weighting and sigma-point reduction enable robust decision-making using the proposed approach with low computational effort.

Impact of Weather Fluctuations

The weather conditions consequently affect the performance of a microgrid system; the sunny days result in the lowest costs and emissions because of high PV output. Cloudy periods exhibit the highest decline in performance, while rainy conditions increase reliance on thermal units, that then raises costs and emissions. The ISCHO optimizer thus adapts to weather fluctuations by optimally dispatching the resources for PVs, storage, and EVs.

Effect of Time Resolution on Optimization Performance

The AWUT-MG/ISCHO framework scales well to finer time resolutions, such as 15–5 min, by better decision precision and capturing short-term dynamics. While increasing the resolution increases computational complexity and faces challenges with regard to data availability, the approach remains scalable for applications involving high-resolution data when available and maintains robust performance under dynamic conditions.

Conclusion

In this study, a new multi-objective and stochastic framework was developed for the optimal scheduling and energy management of an MG across three common weather types, sunny, cloudy, and rainy, to minimize operation cost and emissions. The problem was solved by the ISCHO, which was enhanced by chaos theory to improve convergence behavior. To provide room for uncertainty in PV generation, load demand, and energy prices, a stochastic approach named AWUT-MG was proposed, which enables robust and efficient energy scheduling. The proposed framework effectiveness was verified via three case studies: Case I – MG optimization without DR; Case II – MG with DR and EV integration; and Case III – stochastic MG optimization under uncertainty with DR. Results comparison with SCHO, PSO, and SCA confirmed the superior performance of ISCHO concerning operating cost, emission, and computational effort minimization in any weather condition.
