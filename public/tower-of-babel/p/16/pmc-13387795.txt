Balanced cable bone transport of the tibia using automated motorized struts
Authors: Elizabeth P. Wellings, James A. Blair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Determine if automated struts can successfully power a balanced cable transport (BCT) during tibial bone transport and thus benefit from the advantages of automation.Design:Retrospective observational case series.Setting:Level 1 trauma center.Patients/Participants:A retrospective analysis of 7 tibial BCTs using automated struts was performed with an average defect size of 102 mm.Outcomes:Union rate, complications, external fixation index, bone healing index, and mechanical alignment.Results:The average bone healing index was found to be 21.9 days/cm in bifocal BCT and 13.7 days/cm in trifocal BCT. Automated struts successfully powered 7 BCTs but were not without complications. Four patients had strut malfunctions necessitating unscheduled return to clinic. Other complications not secondary to the struts included pin tract infections (n = 4) and unplanned return to the operating room (n = 2, docking site nonunion and revision ankle arthrodesis).Conclusions:Automated struts powering BCT is feasible but not without complications. This treatment strategy combines the advantages of automation with the advantages of BCT. Incorporating automation into these complex constructs will allow for ease of use for patient and caregiver during the long process of bone transport, theoretically decrease noncompliance, which otherwise can cause severe complications, and increased segmentation, which may improve quality of regenerate bone formation.Level of Evidence:Level 4.

1. Introduction

Treating segmental bone defects with bone transport via distraction osteogenesis has been described since the 1950s and has evolved to include both internal and external techniques.1 Although demonstrated to be a successful and reliable method of treating bone defects, the speed at which transport can occur is limited by the rate of new bone formation and the healing of the skin around the circular external fixator's half-pins and wires. The concept of bidirectional and tandem multifocal transport was first described by Borzunov in 2007 in an effort to decrease total treatment time for large defects and thus decrease time spent in external fixation.2,3 This technique was further popularized by Quinnan in 2017 with the development of balanced cable transport to decrease soft tissue complications from traveling pins and wires and aid in conversion to nail.4 Multifocal cable transport techniques have also been described to include trifocal bidirectional transport and trifocal tandem transport.4 Bone transport can also be performed internally with telescoping rods and plates. All-internal bone transport via the plate-assisted bone segment transport technique and the internal bone-transport nails are an attractive option for patients but necessitate prolonged weight-bearing restrictions with limited capabilities for multifocal transport.5–8 External circular frames allow for immediate weight bearing at the cost of bulky external fixation, risk of pin site infections, and cosmesis concerns of pin tracts.

Subsequently in 1989, Ilizarov theorized that increased distraction frequency produced better quality regenerate bone.9,10 With traditional hexapod struts, the patient is required to manually adjust multiple times a day, with some struts allowing for 0.25-mm increments. Manual adjustments may be cumbersome for patients and introduce potential for human error. Complications from erroneous strut adjustments can lead to premature consolidation requiring revision osteotomy, poor regenerate quality and thus nonunion, and iatrogenic malalignment or deformity. Originally described by Ilizarov, the use of automated motorized struts relieves the patient of this burden, decreases human error, and allows for more control by the surgeon.9,10 Current automated strut designs work via a battery-operated control box (head unit) attached to 6 individual motors attached via 6 individual cables. The motors attach to threaded telescoping struts similar to manual struts. The motors then turn based on the uploaded program to the head unit. In addition to addressing the limitations of manual distraction osteogenesis, automated struts may also theoretically produce better quality regenerate bone, as demonstrated by Ilizarov, and may also decrease any pain experienced by the patient with each adjustment by allowing for more frequent, smaller adjustments.

The introduction of automated bone transport helps to overcome the current limitations of manual distraction osteogenesis. In this paper, a novel technique is described incorporating automated motorized struts to power cable-assisted bone transport. Proof of concept is demonstrated through a case series of bifocal and trifocal transports along with outcomes.

2. Materials and methods

A retrospective analysis of all tibial bone transports performed by the senior author (J.A.B.) between September 2023 and March 2024 was performed. Inclusion criteria included patients undergoing balanced cable transport of the tibia powered by motorized automated struts. Patients undergoing bone transport without balanced cables, without automated motorized struts, or with all-internal techniques were excluded. All patients were followed until radiographic union. All cases were performed at a single, urban level-one trauma center. The study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the local institutional review board. This study was approved by the committee on research ethics at the institution in which the research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of the World Medical Association (www.wma.net) and that any informed consent from human subjects was obtained as required.

Preoperative planning consisted of assessment of soft tissues to determine if soft tissue reconstruction was required. All patients requiring acute soft tissue reconstruction waited until free-flap maturation (approximately 6 weeks) until application of bone transport frame. Once the patient was ready for frame application, the total bone defect was measured using calibrated postoperative imaging and/or intraoperative rulers. Anticipated points of fixation, ring placement, strut lengths, corticotomy sites, and dynamic ring travel distance to avoid impingement were planned.

Although each frame construct was unique, each frame consisted of the following key components: a proximal ring block consisting of an open aluminum ring attached to a full ring (aluminum or carbon fiber) connected by four 65-mm posts (Fig. 1A) and long threaded rods to connect to the distal ring/s (J&J MedTech, Depuy Synthes, Raynham, Massachusetts). The carbon fiber ring does not have any offset tabs, therefore, can be placed in any orientation and allows for better radiographic osseous visualization. When using an aluminum ring for the distal ring in the proximal ring block, the tabs were orientated in a way where the tabs from the proximal ring did not align with the tabs of the distal ring to allow the struts to not impinge on the rings (Fig. 1B). The 65-mm posts allow for enough clearance to fit the automated strut head unit between the 2 rings. Of note, the head unit can be placed anywhere on the frame, but we found it easiest to place it on the anterior aspect of the frame between the 2 proximal rings of the ring block. The head unit requires 7 consecutive ring holes to attach to the frame when using the recommended head unit clip attachment. After assembling the proximal ring block, the dynamic ring for the pushing struts when constructing a hybrid balanced cable transport frame was assembled using a titanium ring with the tabs in the same orientation as the most proximal ring (Fig. 1B and C). This was attached using threaded Ilizarov rods, which allowed for increased stability, maintenance of alignment with the traveling rings, and assessment of alignment intraoperatively. Distally, a static ring was attached using an aluminum ring with tabs in the same orientation as the proximal and dynamic rings. Unlike with a standard hexapod ring, the ring tabs are placed in the same orientation to allow for symmetric placement of transport struts. When additional fixation was needed distally, a foot ring was incorporated into the frame.

In general, the construct includes a proximal ring block (A) consisting of a titanium 3/4 ring and a carbon fiber full ring. A carbon fiber ring is optional but does have the added benefit of radiolucency while monitoring regenerate formation. A ring block provides increased frame stability and versatility at the cost of increased bulkiness. The 3/4 ring is chosen to avoid posterior impingement of the ring behind the knee. The ring block should be oriented with the center tab of the 3/4 ring facing anterior (B). Rings are attached by 4 Ilizarov threaded rods to increase stability and allow for smooth travel of the dynamic ring. The dynamic ring needs to be a titanium ring with the center tab facing anterior so that transport struts can be connected without issue (C). As described by Quinnan et al, an Ilizarov cable 1.8 × 1200-mm cable (Smith and Nephew, Watford, England) is used as it has adequate length and no ball tip. The cable is passed proximally creating a loop around the anterior cortex. The ends of the cable are then left intramedullary. Make sure the cables are not crossing or entangled to allow for straight line of pull in a balanced fashion (D). The cable ends are passed out the distal segment, making sure that each cable end is exiting the correct side of the distal tibia (E). Fluoroscopy is used to verify that cables are not twisted. A fulcrum is then placed in between the cables distally (F–H).

Before placing the assembled frame around the patient's leg, the cables were passed using the technique described by Quinnan, et al.4 An incision was made to expose both proximal and distal bone ends if needed. A 4.3-mm hole was created from medial to lateral 15 to 20 mm proximal to the bone end of the transporting segment (Fig. 1D). A Hewson suture passer was used to pass each end of the cable. Moving to the opposite bone segment, an anterior to posterior fulcrum (5.0 bicortical screw) was placed 15 to 20 mm distal to the proximal aspect of the static bone segment (Fig. 1E). Of note, multiple different fulcrums can be used depending on the frame construct and surgeon preference including fully threaded cortex screws, smooth pegs, tensioned thin wires, or half pins. A 4.3-mm drill was then used from medial to lateral just distal to the fulcrum to create a 90° cable turn around the fulcrum. A Hewson suture passer was then used to pass the appropriate cable ends from one side to the other around the fulcrum such that the medial cable exited laterally and vice versa. Care was taken to avoid inappropriate crossing of the cables (Fig. 1F–H). After successful passage of the cable, the incision(s) were closed before sliding the frame over the leg. Proximal and distal frame fixation was obtained with tensioned wires and half-pins as appropriate. Coronal and sagittal alignment was verified throughout the process. The proximal and distal syndesmosis was stabilized if there was a fibular gap.

In bifocal transports, the cable ends exiting medially and laterally were wrapped around a pulley system attached to the distal static ring (Fig. 2A). Different pulley constructs are shown in Figure 2B. The cable was then attached directly to the pulling transport struts proximally (Fig. 2C). The pulling transport struts allowed for an in-line pull parallel to the frame (Fig. 2D and E). Automated struts are not designed to directly attach to the cable; therefore, to avoid needing additional floating rings that may cause early impingement, the automated struts were modified to perform like transport struts allowing for direct attachment to the cable ends (Fig. 3F–J). Before attaching the cable ends to the struts, an appropriate corticotomy site was chosen and performed using a percutaneous multiple drill hole technique and completed with an osteotome.11 Completion of the corticotomy was verified with fluoroscopic translation between the 2 segments. Cables were then attached with minimal tension to avoid premature gapping of the corticotomy site.

In the tandem trifocal transports, an additional dynamic ring was between the proximal and distal static rings, which drove the proximal transport segment while the cable drove the distal transport segment in the same direction. Appropriate corticotomy sites were chosen for the proximal and distal transport segments followed by fixation of the dynamic ring. Fixation of the dynamic ring was performed before corticotomy. Note that fixation with one half-pin before corticotomy in the floating segment makes it difficult to maintain coronal and sagittal alignment once the segment is free, whereas placing 2 half pins before corticotomy helps maintain desired alignment but does make it difficult to verify completeness of the corticotomy. Corticotomies were then performed using the same percutaneous multiple drill hole technique. In addition to the pulling transport struts attached to the cable ends, 3 additional transport struts were attached from the proximal ring block to the dynamic ring. The dynamic ring struts pushed, whereas the cable struts pulled. Care was taken to verify that the travel distance required of the dynamic ring would not cause impingement on the distal static ring. Most often, medium automated struts were used for both pulling and pushing struts.

In the bidirectional trifocal transport, cables were passed both proximally and distally and then passed around a fulcrum centered at the middle of the bone defect. The fulcrum was an anterior to posterior directed partially threaded half-pin, which was secured to a static ring. The proximal cable was passed under the fulcrum, around medial and lateral pulleys and then attached proximally to pulling struts (as described above). The distal cable was passed over the fulcrum, around the same medial and lateral pulleys and then attached distally to pushing struts.

After frame completion, intraoperative flat plate X-rays were obtained. The head unit was then attached to the frame and the 6 motors (J&J MedTech, Depuy Synthes, Raynham, Massachusetts). Each motor was secured into the struts with available clips and additionally secured with zip ties (Fig. 3A). If there were unused motors, they were secured to the frame with zip ties (Fig. 3A). The authors recommend avoiding any dressings at the motor/threaded rod interface to avoid impingement of the motor, which can cause the head unit to signal an error (Fig. 3B and C).

After frame application, the bone transport program was developed. All transports had a latency period of 7 to 10 days. Each segment was transported between 0.7 and 1.0 mm per day.

Each program automatically began at the end of the programmed latency period at the programmed rate. Patients were made non–weight bearing until surgical incisions were healed at which point they were allowed to weight bear as tolerated. Patients returned to clinic every 2 to 3 weeks for clinical assessment and radiographs to assess transport progress. Patients then returned to the operating room as soon as the transport was completed for docking site debridement and conversion to intramedullary nail.12 Patients then returned to clinic in 2 to 3 weeks for a wound check. Patients returned every 4 to 6 weeks to monitor maturation of the regenerate and healing of the docking site. Patients were allowed to begin weight bearing at 6 weeks after conversion to internal fixation. Patients were then seen every 6 to 12 weeks to verify maintained alignment and complete regenerate maturation after incorporating weight bearing. Note that although monitoring regenerate and transport progress, the automated struts are not radiolucent and can impede visualization on radiographs. When needed, the struts and head unit may be removed for radiographs and then replaced and secured.

Outcome measures included union rate, complication profile, external fixation index (EFI), bone healing index (BHI), and final mechanical alignment. EFI was defined as days spent in the circular external fixator divided by the total defect size (days per cm). BHI was defined as the time to bone healing from placement of the circular external fixator divided by the total defect size (days per cm). Bone healing was defined as the time at which the docking site showed healing on at least 3 of 4 cortices, serial radiographs show progressive regenerate maturation, and painless weight bearing. Outcomes were compared with current literature on balanced cable transport with manual struts.

Continuous variables were presented as means with ranges. All statistical analyses were performed with Microsoft Excel version 16.89.1.

3. Results

Seven patients met inclusion criteria and were included in the study. The mean patient age at the time of injury was 34 years (range, 17–56 years) with a mean body mass index (BMI) of 30 kg/m2 (range, 25–35 kg/m2). The frame designs included bifocal antegrade balanced cable transport (n = 3), tandem hybrid trifocal balanced cable transport (proximal transporting segment powered by a dynamic ring with half-pins and distal transporting segment powered by cables) (n = 3), and bidirectional trifocal balanced cable transport (n = 1). The mean bone defect size was 102 mm (range, 42–140 mm) with a majority located in the distal third of the tibia (n = 5). Defects were secondary to acute trauma (n = 3), infected nonunion (n = 3), and chronic osteomyelitis (n = 1). All patients had initially sustained a Gustilo–Anderson type III open tibia fracture (57% type IIIA, 43% type IIIB). The mean latency period between frame application and initiation of transport was 9 days (range, 7–14 days) running at a mean rate of 0.9 mm/day per transported segment (range, 0.67–1.2 mm/day). Mean follow-up was 20 months (range, 12–25 months) (Table 1).

The average BHI and EFI was 21.80 days/cm and 15.72 days/cm for bifocal balanced cable transport, respectively, and 18.80 and 8.84 days/cm for trifocal balanced cable transport, respectively. There was one outlier in the BHI for trifocal balanced cable transport secondary to docking site nonunion. With this outlier removed, the average BHI for trifocal balanced cable transport was found to be 13.67 days/cm. All patients maintained mechanical alignment within an average of 1° to 4° throughout transport (Table 1) and were converted to intramedullary nail within 1 week of program completion.

Regarding complications, 4 patients (57%) had malfunctions of the automated struts necessitating unscheduled return to clinic. One of the 4 malfunctions led to soft tissue compromise around transporting half pins as the program significantly increased the transport rate but was able to be treated conservatively. Four patients (57%) developed pin tract infections, which were effectively treated with a short course of oral antibiotics. Two patients (29%) required unplanned return to the operating room: one patient required precautionary removal of intramedullary nail placed after transport completion because of development of infection adjacent to free flap, resulting in development of docking site malunion secondary to incomplete docking site union. This was treated with a return to the operative room for revision intramedullary fixation (Fig. 4), and one patient required revision ankle arthrodesis to correct malalignment after initial fixation. Neither of these complications requiring an unplanned return to the operating room were a direct result of the automated struts.

4. Discussion

The reintroduction of automated motorized struts has affected the limb reconstruction and lengthening community by relieving patients and caregivers of the responsibility of manual strut adjustments multiple times a day, increasing surgeon control, and allowing for a more continuous distraction. Although automated struts are most commonly used in a hexapod frame for deformity correction, recent studies have demonstrated its use in distraction osteogenesis.13–15 The current paper describes a novel technique of adapting this technology for external bone transport and through a case series provides proof of concept. This technique combines the advantages of automation (ease of use, decreased noncompliance, increased segmentation) with the advantages of balanced cable transport (easier conversion to intramedullary nail, less scarring, decreased potential for pin tract infections).

Several studies have reported improved regenerate bone quality with increased segmentation, whereas other studies have refuted this claim.9,10,16–19 Ilizarov, et al reported that 60 segmentations a day (using an automated strut) compared with 4 segmentations a day improved regenerate quality,9,10 although Bright, et al16 compared motorized distraction (n = 27) at a rate of 1400 segmentations a day (1/1400 mm) vs manual distraction (n = 43) of the tibia and found no significant difference in time to union or complication rates. Kreitz, et al18 reported denser regenerate bone with increased segmentation, but no biomechanical difference compared with standard manual segmentation. In the current study, the BHI was slightly improved relative to what was reported by Quinnan (bifocal: 21.80 vs 24.49 days/cm; trifocal: 13.67 vs 17.01 days/cm), but these cannot be directly compared.4 The EFI was similar in both studies (bifocal: 15.72 vs 11.27 days/cm; trifocal: 8.84 vs 9.98 days/cm), but again direct comparison is difficult. Increased segmentations may improve regenerate quality and allow for decreased EFI, decreased BHI, and, therefore, earlier time to frame-free weight bearing especially when combined with early conversion to intramedullary nail, but larger comparison studies would be required to support this theory.

The addition of automated motorized struts comes with many potential benefits at the expense of increased cost, frame bulk and weight, and programming complexity. There is substantial benefit in relieving the patient or caregiver of having to perform manual strut adjustments multiple times a day especially in the setting of polytrauma or individuals with decreased manual dexterity. Certain patient factors may increase the risk of error when using manual struts including obesity, low socioeconomic status and education level, and unreliable return for follow-up. These errors may lead to premature consolidation, poor regenerate formation, or malalignment. The cost of the motorized struts is less expensive than traditional hexapod struts, but the head unit itself increases the frame cost by approximately $7000. When combining the costs of the cheaper motorized struts, the cost difference is minimal compared with standard frames with manual hexapod struts. The addition of the head unit adds 600 g (1.32 lbs) to the weight of the frame. There was initial concern that the activation sounds of the motorized struts would bother or wake up patients, but per patient feedback, this did not appear to be an issue.

The introduction of new technology does not come without complications.20–22 Feng, et al retrospectively reviewed 199 tibial bone transports using the Ilizarov technique and identified the following complications rates: pin tract infections (60%), induced malalignment (43%), and delayed union (25%). After multivariate analysis, they identified older patients with larger defects, higher EFI, increased operations, and middle and distal third defects to have a higher incidence of complications.21 Hwang, et al20 described an all-internal tandem trifocal cable transport of the tibia using a magnetic telescoping nail but encountered multiple complications including malalignment (which required unplanned return to the operating room), poor regenerate consolidation, and prolonged weight-bearing restrictions. In the current series, complications related to the automated struts were because of programming issues resulting in unplanned clinic visits. Programming complications were more common in the more complex frames (trifocal), as expected. Compared with programming for deformity correction using a hexapod strut formation, the current software had to be manipulated to allow the struts to function as transport struts at different rates. Software updates may allow the option to program the struts for bone transport, therefore, decreasing the programming complexity and decreasing complications secondary to programming errors. Further advances in automated bone transport struts will decrease frame complexity.

The study is limited in its power and generalizability because of it being a retrospective review of a small case series performed by a single surgeon. Each frame was unique, therefore, difficult to replicate, although a general framework has been provided for the overall design and thought process of the technique.

In our experience, limitations of current automated struts include lack of real-time monitoring of frame progress and errors, added weight and bulkiness increasing frame traffic especially when performing multiple corrections requiring more than one head unit, and difficulty monitoring regenerate and deformity correction on radiographs because of radiopaque nature of the motors, cables, and head unit (although these can be temporarily removed and then replaced if needed for imaging/MRI purposes). Future advances in automated struts are focusing on wireless Bluetooth technology, patient and surgeon real time feedback, and decreased weight.

5. Conclusion

The addition of motorized automated struts to power balanced cable bone transports is feasible and comes with multiple potential benefits including decreased human error and noncompliance, increased segmentation allowing for more biologically friendly distraction osteogenesis with and potentially improved regenerated formation. Further research is necessary to better understand and characterize the differences in regenerate quality based on segmentation, rate, and rhythm.

6. Clinical significance

The results are clinically relevant as they show the feasibility of automated struts when driving balanced cable transport. Incorporating automation into these complex constructs will allow for ease of use for patient and caregiver during the long process of bone transport, theoretically decrease noncompliance, which otherwise can cause severe complications, and increased segmentation, which may improve quality of regenerate bone formation.
