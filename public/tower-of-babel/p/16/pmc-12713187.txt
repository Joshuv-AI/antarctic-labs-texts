D-dimer drives immune dysregulation in COVID-19 via chemokine modulation and inflammatory signaling
Authors: Jingwei Hu, Min Liu, Binbin Qin, Bin Shen, Yingjun Huang, Jiayan Zhu, Qi Zheng, Xingdong Zheng, Jingyi Cai, Ying Xie, Yajun Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•D-dimer is not merely a bystander biomarker, but an active participant in immune regulation during SARS-CoV-2 infection.•D-dimer modulate the immune microenvironment by influencing chemokine-mediated cell recruitment.•D-dimer functions not only as a clinical biomarker but also as a potential driver of thromboinflammatory responses.Keywords: D-dimer, COVID-19, IL-6, Chemokines, Immune response, THP-1, scRNA-seq, PI3K-Akt, Cytokine storm

Introduction

COVID-19, caused by SARS-CoV-2, presents a spectrum of clinical manifestations influenced by various biomarkers. These biomarkers play crucial roles in assessing disease severity and prognosis. Studies have underscored the significance of d-dimer levels in COVID-19 patients, demonstrating their association with disease severity and mortality. Elevated d-dimer levels reflect a hypercoagulable state and indicate an increased risk of thrombosis, a prominent feature in severe COVID-19 cases (Watanabe et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2020). This hypercoagulability contributes to complications such as pulmonary embolism and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), significantly worsening patient outcomes. Furthermore, a meta-analysis confirmed the correlation between elevated d-dimer and increased rates of intensive care unit (ICU) admission (Bárbara Gomes et al., 2022). Beyond infectious diseases, studies have consistently shown that high d-dimer levels predict poor outcomes in cancer patients. In solid tumors, elevated d-dimer levels are associated with reduced overall survival. Similarly, gastric cancer patients with higher plasma d-dimer levels exhibit shorter long-term survival. These findings highlight the prognostic relevance of d-dimer across various malignancies (Ay et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2014).

Aging is accompanied by a pro-inflammatory state and increased coagulability, contributing to higher baseline d-dimer levels in elderly populations. This observation is crucial for distinguishing pathological elevations from age-associated baseline levels (Annalisa et al., 2021; Cancro, 2020). d-dimer, a fibrin degradation product, serves as a key biomarker of clot formation and fibrinolysis. It has garnered considerable attention in recent years, particularly in the context of cancer and COVID-19 (Chao et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2022). d-dimer plays a critical role in the systemic inflammatory response characteristic of severe COVID-19, contributing to cytokine storms and subsequent multi-organ dysfunction (Zhao et al., 2021).

In conclusion, elevated d-dimer levels, alongside inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP and IL-6, are pivotal indicators of severe COVID-19 (Sherman et al., 2021). While the diagnostic and prognostic value of d-dimer in COVID-19 is well established (Sharma et al., 2024), its potential role in modulating the immune response remains largely unexplored. In our clinical cohort, we observed significantly higher d-dimer levels in severe COVID-19 patients compared to those with mild disease, and correlation analyses revealed a strong positive relationship between d-dimer and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels.

To further investigate the immunological implications of elevated d-dimer, we analyzed published single-cell RNA sequencing data and identified monocytes, along with monocyte-derived dendritic cells and macrophages, as the primary immune cell types expressing IL-6 in COVID-19 patients. To examine how varying d-dimer levels influence immune cell behavior, THP-1 cells were treated in vitro with clinically relevant concentrations mimicking mild and severe disease states. Transcriptomic analysis following d-dimer stimulation revealed that, although IL-6 expression remained unchanged, high d-dimer concentrations selectively upregulated chemokines such as CXCL6 and CXCL5. These chemokines are known mediators of immune cell recruitment and migration, suggesting that d-dimer may contribute to immune dysregulation in severe COVID-19 by modulating the chemotactic landscape rather than directly enhancing cytokine production. Our findings provide novel insights into the immunomodulatory effects of d-dimer and its potential role in shaping the inflammatory milieu of severe SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Methods and materials

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Shanghai Second People’s Hospital (Approval No [023-KY-16]). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient recruitment and clinical data collection

A total of 356 participants were recruited from the Shanghai region Shanghai Second People’s Hospital, including 200 patients with severe COVID-19, 115 with mild disease, and 41 SARS-CoV-2-negative controls. SARS-CoV-2–negative status was confirmed using RT-PCR assays for viral RNA, with all negative controls tested at least twice during the same clinical visit. The participant classification was based on the clinical diagnostic and treatment guidelines for COVID-19 issued by the National Health Commission of the PRC. In addition, the controls (n = 41) was also tested using serological assays (IgM/IgG) to further exclude prior infection. Clinical data were collected for each participant, including: d-dimer, peripheral blood absolute lymphocyte count, eosinophil count, IL-6, procalcitonin, creatine kinase isoenzyme, myoglobin, troponin I, B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), alveolar oxygen partial pressure, lactate, glucose, creatinine, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and prognosis. Correlation analyses were performed to investigate the relationships between d-dimer levels and other clinical indicators. All patients with comorbidities were excluded at the time of recruitment, ensuring that the enrolled participants did not have underlying health conditions. The majority of participants were over 60 years old, with only 4 individuals younger than 60. Previous studies have reported there was minimal influence of gender on d-dimer levels in COVID-19 patients. Given these considerations, the correlation analyses were not adjusted for age, gender, or comorbidities. The details of the patients are showed in Table S1.

Identification of d-dimer-Associated genes

Transcriptomic data from public datasets, including GSE157859 from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), as well as previously published literature, were analyzed to identify genes associated with d-dimer levels in severe, mild, and recovering COVID-19 patients. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database, and gene enrichment analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database.

Single-Cell expression analysis of IL-6

To investigate the cellular sources and expression characteristics of IL-6, single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets were analyzed using publicly available platforms (Bernardes et al., 2020). Comparisons were made between COVID-19 positive and negative patients, with particular focus on IL-6 expression across various cell types in lung and liver tissues. All parameters were configured in accordance with the respective online tool guidelines.

d-dimer stimulation model in THP-1 cells

Previous studies have identified monocytes, including monocyte-derived macrophages and dendritic cells, as key innate immune cell subsets expressing IL-6 during COVID-19. To investigate the transcriptional response of monocytes to d-dimer, THP-1 cells were stimulated with concentrations representing median d-dimer levels in severe (2.03 mg/L) and mild (1.04 mg/L) COVID-19 patients. Two concentrations of d-dimer were selected based on (i) physiologically relevant levels observed in patients with mild versus severe COVID-19 (Gungor et al., 2021), and (ii) preliminary dose-response experiments showing that these concentrations exerted measurable but non-cytotoxic effects on THP1 cells. Each experiment was conducted using three independent biological replicates to ensure reproducibility. Total RNA was extracted 24 h post-stimulation and subjected to RNA sequencing. Differential gene expression and downstream enrichment analyses were conducted to explore the immunomodulatory effects of d-dimer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and R software. Group comparisons between mild, severe, and control groups were conducted using Student’s t-test. Spearman correlation was used to assess the relationship between d-dimer and clinical indicators such as IL-6. Transcriptomic data were analyzed using DESeq2, with differentially expressed genes defined by an adjusted p-value 〈 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| 〉 1. Functional enrichment and protein interaction analyses were conducted using KEGG and STRING databases. Single-cell RNA-seq data were analyzed using publicly available platform (Single Cell Portal), and statistical comparisons were performed using the default methods provided by the database.

Results

Higher d-dimer levels were observed in severe COVID-19 patients and were associated with poorer prognosis

Although the global COVID-19 pandemic has been effectively controlled, the virus continues to exhibit seasonal endemic patterns in various countries and regions (Alejandro, 2021; Alessandra et al., 2021). As such, it remains important to monitor emerging clinical characteristics. We analyzed 315 patients diagnosed with SARS-CoV-2 infection who presented to our hospital after the lifting of pandemic restrictions, along with 41 SARS-CoV-2 molecular and antibody test-negative individuals as controls.

Based on clinical criteria, patients were categorized into severe and mild groups. We compared d-dimer concentrations across these groups and assessed their association with clinical outcomes. The results showed no significant difference in d-dimer levels between mild cases and the control group. However, patients with malignancies exhibited significantly higher d-dimer levels than those with mild disease (Fig. 1A). Moreover, patients with elevated d-dimer levels had a worse prognosis (Fig. 1B). We further evaluated whether other clinical indicators differed between the mild and severe groups. Significant differences were observed in peripheral blood absolute lymphocyte count, creatine kinase isoenzyme, myoglobin, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and lactate levels (Table S1).

(B) Comparison of d-dimer concentrations between the survival and death groups following COVID-19 infection. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, as determined by Student’s t-test. Asterisks indicate significance levels: * p < 0.05, **** p < 0.0001.

These findings indicate distinct clinical profiles between mild and severe COVID-19 cases. Among them, d-dimer serves as a reliable blood biomarker for distinguishing disease severity and predicting patient prognosis.

The correlation between d-dimer concentration and other clinical features

To further investigate the clinical parameters associated with d-dimer levels, we analyzed the correlation between d-dimer and various laboratory indicators in both severe and mild COVID-19 patients. Our results showed a significant positive correlation between IL-6 and d-dimer levels in severe cases (correlation coefficient > 0.3, p < 0.05), whereas no such correlation was observed in mild cases (Fig. 2A). Similarly, troponin I levels were positively correlated with d-dimer in severe patients (correlation coefficient > 0.3, p < 0.05) but not in mild cases (Fig. 2B). In contrast, glomerular filtration rate (GFR) exhibited a significant negative correlation with d-dimer in severe cases (correlation coefficient = –0.3, p < 0.05), while no correlation was observed in the mild group.

(B) The association between d-dimer levels and Troponin I concentrations in patients with severe and mild conditions, respectively. (C) The correlation between d-dimer levels and Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) in patients with severe and mild conditions, respectively.

Other clinical indicators did not show significant correlations with d-dimer levels in either patient group (Figure S1A–S1C). These results showed that a statistical correlation between d-dimer and IL-6 levels in patients with severe COVID-19, while the potential regulatory relationship need to be further explored.

Integrated analysis reveals immune-related gene signatures and interaction network in COVID-19

To characterize the gene signatures associated with severe COVID-19, we integrated data from a published GEO dataset (GSE157859) (Bernardes et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022), differentially expressed genes reported in the literature, and DEGs identified from our clinical cohort. DEGs were identified using the DESeq2 pipeline (adjusted p < 0.05, |log2FC| > 1). The DEGs from our clinical data were identified by using cytokine detection. An UpSet plot was generated to visualize the overlap among these gene sets (Fig. 3A). The overlapping genes were then used to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING database (Fig. 3B). The PPI network revealed distinct clustering of immune-related cytokines and chemokines, indicating that severe COVID-19 primarily involves dysregulation of immune system pathways. Among these, IL-6 emerged as a central node, suggesting its pivotal role in the inflammatory response associated with disease severity. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using g:GOSt, within the top 20 GO terms, those primarily associated with immune processes and COVID-19 comprised Cytokine Signaling in immune system, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Interleukin-4 and Interleukin-13 signaling, Th17 cell differentiation, Inflammatory bowel disease, Network map of SARS CoV 2 signaling, Interleukin-10 signaling, T cell activation SARSCoV2 (Fig. 3C).

Network of d-dimer Related Genes. (A) UpSet plots depicting d-dimer related genes identified from the literature, GEO dataset, and laboratory data. (B) Network analysis of d-dimer related genes generated using STRING. (C) Enrichment analysis of d-dimer related genes conducted using KOBAS.

Single-Cell expression profile of IL-6 across tissues in COVID-19

To characterize IL-6 expression patterns before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection, we utilized publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing datasets via the Single Cell Portal. Based on the data quality, completeness, and relevance to our study, specified datasets obtained from the Single Cell Portal (SCP1289, SCP1213, SCP1219, and SCP1216) were used for our further analysis. First, we analyzed nasopharyngeal swab samples from PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 positive and negative patients. IL-6 expression was significantly elevated in COVID-19-positive samples compared to negatives and was enriched in specific immune cell subsets (Fig. 4A).

(B) UMAP visualization of IL-6 expression in lung cells, stratified by cell type. (C) UMAP visualization of IL-6 expression in liver cells, stratified by cell type. (D) Analysis of IL-6 expression in heart cells, categorized by cell type.

We further extended the analysis to single-cell transcriptomic datasets from lung (Fig 4B), liver (Fig. 4C), and heart tissues (Fig 4D). The results revealed tissue-specific differences in the cellular sources of IL-6. Notably, IL-6 expression was predominantly localized to monocytes, granulocytes, and T lymphocytes, with varying contributions across different organs. These findings suggest that IL-6 expression in COVID-19 is both cell-type- and tissue-specific, reflecting distinct local immune responses to SARS-CoV-2 infection.

d-dimer triggers immune activation and enriches inflammatory signaling pathways in THP-1 cells

THP-1 monocytic cells were stimulated with d-dimer concentrations reflecting mild and severe COVID-19. After 24 h, RNA-seq identified 150 differentially expressed genes (126 upregulated, 24 downregulated) (Fig. 5A). GO enrichment analysis showed the top 20 enriched categories included inflammatory response, immune response, response to external stimulus, response to cytokine, and cellular response to cytokine stimulus. Several additional processes, such as defense response, cytokine production, cell surface receptor signaling pathway, and myeloid leukocyte activation, were also highly enriched in after d-dimer treated (Fig. 5B). KEGG analysis further highlighted enrichment of immune-related and disease-associated pathways. The most prominent pathways included hematopoietic cell lineage, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, Staphylococcus aureus infection, IL-17 signaling pathway, and Coronavirus disease (COVID-19). Additional enriched pathways were related to immune regulation and inflammation, such as neutrophil extracellular trap formation, rheumatoid arthritis, phagosome, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway. Several disease-associated and signaling pathways, including tuberculosis, amoebiasis, transcriptional misregulation in cancer, and Rap1 signaling pathway, were also identified (Fig. 5C). These results suggested that the DEGs were strongly enriched in immune and inflammatory pathways, highlighting d-dimer potential involvement in immune regulation and response. Heatmap clustering confirmed enhanced expression of immune-related genes under high d-dimer conditions (Fig. 5D). GSEA revealed that d-dimer stimulation significantly activated several signaling pathways. Notably, the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathway showed strong enrichment, underscoring d-dimer’s potential role in reshaping the cytokine environment (Fig. 5E). The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway was also upregulated, implying effects on immune regulation and cell survival (Fig. 5F). The IL-17 signaling pathway was enriched, suggesting a pro-inflammatory response (Fig. 5G). The Herpes simplex virus 1 infection pathway indicated induction of antiviral and inflammatory gene programs (Fig. 5H). Together, these findings indicate that d-dimer may drive immune dysregulation through multiple key signaling axes.

Discussion

D-dimer, a fibrin degradation product and classical marker of thrombosis, has gained increasing attention during the COVID-19 pandemic not only as a prognostic indicator but also as a potential immunomodulatory factor (Alba et al., 2020; Clarisse Martins et al., 2020; David et al., 2020). Numerous clinical studies have consistently reported elevated d-dimer levels in patients with severe COVID-19 and their association with poor prognosis (Fatemah et al., 2020; Fernando et al., 2020; Grigoris et al., 2020). Our findings corroborate this, demonstrating significantly higher d-dimer concentrations in severe cases, particularly among patients with comorbidities such as malignancy, and their correlation with worse clinical outcomes.

Importantly, our correlation analyses revealed a significant positive relationship between d-dimer and IL-6 levels in severe patients, suggesting a potential regulatory interaction. This observation aligns with previous reports indicating that elevated d-dimer may be linked to hyperinflammatory states, including cytokine storm (Park et al., 2022; Schafer et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). Notably, this correlation was absent in mild cases, highlighting the disease stage–dependent nature of d-dimer–IL-6 interplay. In addition, troponin I and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) also correlated with d-dimer in severe cases, suggesting its association with multi-organ involvement.

To explore the molecular underpinnings of d-dimer–associated immune responses, we performed integrated transcriptomic analyses combining public datasets, literature-curated genes, and our own clinical results (Bernardes et al., 2020; Gupta et al., 2024). This revealed a convergent immune-related gene signature, with IL-6 as a central node in the protein–protein interaction network. Single-cell RNA sequencing further supported these findings, demonstrating tissue- and cell-specific IL-6 expression in COVID-19–positive patients, particularly in monocytes, granulocytes, and T cells across respiratory and extrapulmonary tissues. These results suggest that IL-6–producing innate immune cells may be critical mediators of d-dimer–associated inflammation.

To experimentally test d-dimer’s immunomodulatory role, we established an in vitro stimulation model using THP-1 monocytic cells and d-dimer concentrations reflective of clinical values in mild and severe cases. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that high d-dimer concentrations induced a broad immune activation program, including upregulation of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and key immune pathways. GO and KEGG analyses identified enrichment in processes such as cytokine response and inflammatory signaling. GSEA further revealed that d-dimer stimulation activated canonical pathways including IL-17 signaling, PI3K-Akt, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and herpes simplex virus 1 infection, all of which are associated with innate immune activation and inflammation.

Interestingly, despite clinical correlations with IL-6, while our present study found that d-dimer stimulation had a significant impact on THP-1 immune-related genes, its regulation of IL-6 was not significant in vitro. A possible explanation is that the influence of d-dimer on IL-6 may depend on multiple immune cell subsets or specific subpopulations, rather than solely on THP-1 cells. Another possibility is that the presence of IL-6 promotes d-dimer production, thereby creating a reciprocal regulatory loop that underlies their clinical correlation. Hasan A et al. directly stimulated PBMCs with d-dimer and observed an increase in IL-6 secretion from the mixed cell populations, which was further enhanced when combined with SARS-CoV-2 protein stimulation (Hasan et al., 2018). However, the cytokine profiling was limited. In our present study, we used specific immune cell subpopulations for stimulation and constructed a more comprehensive immune regulatory network. Future studies could employ fluorescently labeled d-dimer to co-incubated with PBMC or tissue-derived (e.g., lung) immune cells to identify the directly affected subpopulations. Subsequent mapping of immune regulatory networks involving T cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and other subsets would be of great significance for elucidating the underlying mechanisms of d-dimer–IL-6 regulation. These results indicated that d-dimer may not be a direct cytokine inducer, but rather acts through modulating chemokine networks such as CXCL5 and CXCL6, which regulate immune cell recruitment. This highlights a potentially underappreciated role of d-dimer in shaping the immune microenvironment, rather than initiating inflammation parse.

In clinical pathological samples, the levels of chemokines CCL2, CXCL10, CXCL5, and CXCL6 are significantly elevated in both pulmonary lavage fluid and peripheral blood. CXCL5 and CXCL6 promote the recruitment of neutrophils, and excessive infiltration further amplifies local inflammation and contributes to the production of cytokine storms. However, chemokines play crucial roles in both antiviral immunity and inflammatory injury. Blocking these factors may alleviate inflammation but could also impair viral clearance, which partly explains why clinical reports on targeting chemokines or their receptors remain limited. To date, only the CCR5 antagonist, named maraviroc, has been approved for anti-HIV treatment. CCR5 functions as a core receptor for HIV cellular entry, and maraviroc's primary mechanism of action is to alter the conformation of CCR5, thus blocking HIV from invading CCR5-positive cells (Van Der Ryst, 2015).

Our present study highlights a potential immunoregulatory function of d-dimer within specific immune cell subsets, with implications for prognosis after viral infection, particularly in cytokine storm–associated lung injury. While our findings suggest an immunoregulatory role of d-dimer, we cannot yet fully determine whether it acts as a causal driver or merely reflects underlying immune dysregulation. Establishing causality will require additional experimental validation, such as testing the effects of exogenous d-dimer or its blockade in relevant models. Similarly, the proposed link between d-dimer, monocyte-derived chemokines, and CXCL5/6 in COVID-19 remains a working hypothesis that warrants further mechanistic investigation. Additionally, it is essential to verify whether CXCL5/CXCL6 levels can serve as clinical indicators of viral clearance and inflammatory damage, requiring a substantial amount of data and samples for validation.

Taken together, our study supports the emerging view that d-dimer is not merely a bystander biomarker, but may also participate in immune regulation during SARS-CoV-2 infection. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into how d-dimer may contribute to disease severity through immune modulation. Importantly, while our results suggest a possible link between d-dimer–induced chemokines and immune dysregulation, the therapeutic feasibility of targeting these pathways remains highly speculative. Further mechanistic investigations and clinical validation are required before considering any therapeutic applications.
