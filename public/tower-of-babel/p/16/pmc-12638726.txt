Activated CaMKIIδ translocates to the RyR nanodomain in cardiomyocytes
Authors: Anna Bergan-Dahl, Cathrine R Carlson, Martin Laasmaa, Hariharan Subramanian, Adelle Basson, Jia Li, Almira Hasic, Marianne Lunde, Hege Ugland, Ornella Manfra, Enno Klussmann, Julie Bossuyt, Donald M Bers, Viacheslav O Nikolaev, William E Louch, Xin Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe heartbeat is triggered by the coordinated release of Ca2+ from the ryanodine receptor type-2 (RyR) in cardiomyocytes. Phosphorylation of RyR by Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent kinase IIδ (CaMKIIδ) fine-tunes this process in health, while hyperphosphorylation causes excessive, pathological Ca2+ release. We investigated how CaMKIIδ is spatially recruited and anchored to RyRs to achieve this functional regulation.Methods and resultsWe employed confocal and dSTORM microscopy to investigate the macro- and nanoscale distribution of CaMKIIδ across cardiomyocytes, respectively. We linked positional rearrangement of the kinase during β-adrenergic stimulation (isoproterenol, Iso) to alterations in RyR phosphorylation and function (Ca2+ sparks), and the requirement of the CaMKIIδ anchoring protein AKAP18δ by knockdown/knockout. Confocal microscopy revealed that macroscale CaMKIIδ localization was not markedly altered during Iso-treatment, although a narrowing of its distribution around the Z-lines occurred, where the RyR reside. Higher resolution dSTORM imaging confirmed that local mobilization of CaMKIIδ by Iso decreased the distance from Z-lines and RyRs to the nearest CaMKIIδ by 28 and 12%, respectively. Functionally, kinase translocation into the RyR nanodomain was accompanied by increased channel phosphorylation and Ca2+ spark frequency. These actions were dependent on CaMKIIδ activity, since kinase translocation, RyR phosphorylation, and activation were all mimicked by the upstream activator of CaMKIIδ (8-CPT) and prevented by direct CaMKIIδ inhibitors (AIP, N1 peptide). A critical role of AKAP18δ in this mechanism was supported by immunoprecipitation experiments, which showed greater kinase binding to AKAP18δ during Iso-stimulation. Furthermore, loss of AKAP18δ by viral-mediated AKAP18δ knockdown or knockout prevented CaMKIIδ translocation to Z-lines. Microtubular disruption also blocked CaMKIIδ translocation.ConclusionCollectively, our results indicate that nanoscale movement of CaMKIIδ is closely associated with RyR activation following β-adrenergic stimulation. This translocation depends on an intact microtubular network and kinase binding to AKAP18δ.

Introduction

In cardiomyocytes, the opening of voltage-gated L-type calcium channels (LTCCs) during the action potential enables Ca2+ entry, which in turn triggers a larger release of Ca2+ via ryanodine receptors type-2 (RyRs) in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) membrane. The binding of released Ca2+ to troponin C in the myofilaments elicits myocyte contraction. Relaxation is mediated mainly by the SR Ca2+ ATPase 2 (SERCA2), which recycles Ca2+ back into the SR; an action that is regulated by SERCA2’s endogenous inhibitor, phospholamban (PLN).1,2

Beat-to-beat Ca2+ cycling is fine-tuned by auxiliary proteins, such as Ca2+/ calmodulin-dependent protein kinase IIδ (CaMKIIδ).3 Each CaMKIIδ monomer consists of a C-terminal association domain, a regulatory domain, and an N-terminal catalytic domain. When the kinase is inactive, the regulatory domain binds directly to the T-site and the S-site in the catalytic unit, keeping the kinase in a ‘closed’ autoinhibited conformation.4,5 During activation, Ca2+/CaM binds to the regulatory domain and displaces it from the T- and S-site. The kinase then takes on an ‘open’ conformation, which allows it to phosphorylate target proteins, including LTCCs, RyRs, and PLN, and modify these proteins’ activities.3,5,6 For example, CaMKIIδ phosphorylates RyR at Serine 2814; a modification that results in increased Ca2+ sensitivity and a lowered activation threshold.7,8 In cardiovascular diseases such as heart failure, CaMKIIδ overactivity drives excessive RyR Ca2+ leak9,10 and dispersion of channel clusters.11,12 These changes are, in turn, linked to the generation of cardiac arrhythmias13,14 and weakened contraction.11,15

While the importance of CaMKIIδ activity in healthy and diseased hearts is well characterized,3,6 less is known about how the kinase reaches its targets. We recently discovered that AKAP18δ, an A-kinase anchoring protein, anchors CaMKIIδ to nearby PLN and RyR to enable the phosphorylation of these proteins.16 Importantly, we found that CaMKIIδ activity could be regulated by its binding to distinct domains within AKAP18δ.16 This raises the question of how CaMKIIδ is spatially recruited to these sites. Previous work performed in neuronal cells has shown that activated CaMKII undergoes translocation to phosphorylate effector proteins.17–19 Work by Wood et al. revealed that CaMKIIδ also exhibits a high degree of mobility in cardiomyocytes.20 In particular, the authors observed that electrical pacing–induced CaMKIIδ translocation away from Z-lines, while reducing intracellular [Ca2+] by chelators, stabilized CaMKIIδ at Z-lines. Thus, it remains unclear how activated CaMKIIδ reaches targets such as RyRs, since the majority of these channels are located near Z-lines.21

We presently hypothesized that activation of CaMKIIδ promotes its translocation to RyRs in an AKAP18δ-dependent manner. Employing dSTORM super-resolution microscopy, we found that CaMKIIδ exhibited preferential Z-line localization at baseline and exhibited a nanoscale translocation towards the RyR nanodomain upon β-adrenergic stimulation. This translocation was accompanied by an increase in RyR phosphorylation and Ca2+ spark frequency and was blocked by CaMKIIδ inhibitors. Moreover, CaMKIIδ translocation was observed to require an intact microtubule network and AKAP18δ, suggesting a mechanism by which physical displacement of the activated kinase enables RyR phosphorylation.

Results

β-adrenergic stimulation causes CaMKIIδ translocation to RyRs at the Z-lines

We first investigated the relative positioning of CaMKIIδ and RyRs under baseline conditions in left ventricular rat cardiomyocytes using confocal microscopy (Figure 1A). We observed marked colocalization of these proteins, with a striated pattern consistent with preferential localization at the Z-lines. Indeed, co-staining of α-actinin and CaMKIIδ confirmed this arrangement (Figure 1B). Negative controls performed with secondary antibodies alone showed no such staining pattern (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1).

We next investigated whether the cellular distribution of the kinase is affected by acute treatment with the β-adrenergic agonist Isoproterenol (Iso, 100 nM, 10 min). Confocal imaging revealed no marked changes in the macroscale CaMKIIδ signal relative to Z-lines (Figure 1B and C). However, closer examination of CaMKIIδ distribution revealed a narrowing of fluorescent peaks at the Z-lines following Iso-treatment, as indicated by fluorescence-intensity profiles (Figure 1D) and measurements of the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of these signals (Figure 1E). Thus, despite the limited resolution of confocal imaging, these observations suggest that CaMKIIδ may translocate closer to Z-lines following Iso-treatment.

We used two-colour dSTORM super-resolution microscopy for greater detailed resolution of CaMKIIδ movement in response to Iso. In agreement with our confocal imaging, dSTORM imaging revealed a shift of CaMKIIδ positions towards α-actinin following Iso-treatment (Figure 1F). Specifically, quantification of the nearest-neighbour distance (NND) from α-actinin to the closest CaMKIIδ confirmed a significant decrease in these values by 28% (± SEM 6%) following 10 min of Iso-treatment (Figure 1G). On the other hand, large-scale, cell-wide movements of CaMKIIδ were not apparent (Figure 1H) in both the presence and absence of Iso. Here, we observed that ∼27% of all CaMKIIδ clusters remained within 50 nm of α-actinin, while remaining clusters were found at relatively random longitudinal distances along the half-sarcomere. The same was true for overall CaMKIIδ area (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2A), except that roughly half of the total CaMKIIδ area was within 50 nm of α-actinin, indicating that the Z-line CaMKIIδ clusters are larger on average than those elsewhere. These results were also confirmed in rabbit cardiomyocytes, where a strong tendency of translocation towards the Z-lines was observed following Iso-treatment, without alterations to the macroscale distribution or area of CaMKIIδ (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3A–D).

Similar results were obtained for two-colour dSTORM imaging of RyR and CaMKIIδ (Figure 1I) as Iso application reduced the distance from the average RyR cluster to the closest CaMKIIδ by 12% (±SEM 2%) (Figure 1J). The overall macroscale distribution of CaMKIIδ clusters again remained largely unchanged (Figure 1K), with over half of the CaMKIIδ within 150 nm of an RyR cluster. Furthermore, when we assessed the colocalization of CaMKIIδ-RyR by a proximity ligation assay, we found a 36% (±SEM 5%) increase in the colocalization signal following Iso-treatment (Figure 1L). Note that this increase in colocalization is unlikely to result from changing RyR localization, since we found no alterations in the nearest α-actinin-RyR distance after Iso-treatment (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2B). Notably, we observed that the nanoscale effects of Iso on CaMKIIδ positioning at Z-lines were partially reversible with wash-out (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4A), while the overall distribution of CaMKIIδ across the sarcomere remained unaltered by either Iso or wash-out (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4B).

Taken together, these data support that acute β-adrenergic receptor (β-AR) stimulation promotes nanoscale translocation of CaMKIIδ towards RyRs located near the Z-lines, without affecting the broader arrangement of the kinase across the sarcomere. Notably, this Iso-induced coalescence of CaMKIIδ at Z-lines in these quiescent cells is opposite to effects induced by electrical pacing of adult ventricular myocytes (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5A and B). This finding is in agreement with a previous confocal study in adult rabbit ventricular myocytes, where pacing induced CaMKIIδ mobility and distance from Z-lines (Wood et al.).20 However, when Iso was applied together with pacing, the CaMKIIδ localization was intermediate (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5A), indicating a competition of influence of Iso vs. pacing on CaMKIIδ localization to the Z-line.

CaMKIIδ movement is dependent on kinase activation

Next, we tested whether CaMKIIδ translocation is directly dependent on its activation state. In cardiomyocytes pre-treated with the selective CaMKII inhibitor AIP (autocamtide-2-related inhibitory peptide, myristoylated, 2 μM, 45 min), acute application of Iso no longer resulted in a shift in CaMKIIδ localization towards Z-lines (Figure 2A and B) or RyRs (Figure 2C and D). Application of another peptide inhibitor of CaMKIIδ called N1 (AKAP18-N (55–74)16; with a cell-permeable transactivator of transcription (TAT)-tag, 5 μM, and 45 min), similarly inhibited Iso-induced translocation of the kinase (see Supplementary material online, Figure S6A–C). Importantly, neither AIP nor N1 had any effect on the overall baseline NND from α-actinin to CaMKIIδ in the absence of Iso-stimulation (see Supplementary material online, Figure S6E). On the other hand, activation of CaMKIIδ via the exchange protein activated by cAMP 2 (Epac2) pathway22 using 8-CPT-cAMP (8-CPT, 10 μM, and 10 min)22 reproduced effects similar to Iso, inducing nanoscale movement of CaMKIIδ towards the Z-lines (Figure 2A and B) and RyRs (Figure 2C and D). At the macroscale, neither AIP, N1, nor 8-CPT altered the overall CaMKIIδ distribution (Figure 2E, Supplementary material online, Figure S6D). These results support that nanoscale CaMKIIδ movement towards the RyRs at the Z-lines is dependent on the activity of the kinase.

It is well established that CaMKIIδ activation during β-AR stimulation causes RyR phosphorylation at Ser2814,23 and that the resulting channel activation augments spontaneous Ca2+ release events called Ca2+ sparks.24 In our hands, CaMKIIδ translocation towards RyRs coincided with expected alterations in RyR phosphorylation status and activity. Indeed, application of either Iso or 8-CPT significantly increased RyR phosphorylation at Ser2814, whereas AIP (tendency) or N1 treatment attenuated the effect of Iso (Figure 2F, Supplementary material online, Figure S6F). Functional measurements of Ca2+ sparks (Figure 2G, Supplementary material online, Figure S6G) were closely aligned with these effects, as Iso and 8-CPT treatments led to similar increases in spark frequency and size, while AIP and N1 reversed the effect of Iso on spark frequency back to control levels and attenuated spark size (Figure 2H and I, Supplementary material online, Figure S6H and I). These data raise the possibility that activation of RyRs may be induced by nanoscale translocation of CaMKIIδ to the channels.

CaMKIIδ can also be activated by oxidation.25 To test whether this means of kinase activation has similar effects on its translocation, we exposed cardiomyocytes to H2O2 (100μM) for 15 min. In these cells, no translocation towards Z-lines was observed (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7A), and the broader distribution of the kinase across the sarcomere remained unchanged (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7B). These findings suggest that control of CaMKIIδ positioning is linked to downstream effects of β-AR stimulation, but not necessarily other mechanisms of kinase activation.

CaMKIIδ anchoring and translocation to the RyR nanodomain are dependent on AKAP18δ

Figure 3A–D shows potential mechanisms by which CaMKIIδ may translocate and functionally regulate RyRs. These mechanisms are likely facilitated by AKAP18δ, which we have previously shown to anchor CaMKIIδ at RyR.16 We thus hypothesized that the observed CaMKIIδ shift towards RyRs following β-AR or 8-CPT activation is mediated by either the movement of AKAP18δ itself (Figure 3A) or by the release of CaMKIIδ from AKAP18δ to RyR (Figure 3B) as CaMKIIδ is known to co-immunoprecipitate with RyR.7,26 Alternatively, CaMKIIδ could be recruited to RyR-associated AKAP18δ (Figure 3C), or the CaMKIIδ position on the AKAP18δ protein could be shifted closer to the RyR (Figure 3D), given that there are distinct binding sites between CaMKIIδ and AKAP18δ.16

We began by ruling out the possibility of movement of AKAP18δ itself driving kinase translocation (Figure 3A) since two-colour dSTORM imaging revealed no shift in the position of AKAP18δ towards the Z-line during Iso-treatment (Figure 3E and F). We next tested whether CaMKIIδ is released from AKAP18δ following activation, bringing it closer to its site of action at RyRs (Figure 3B). To test this possibility, we compared the degree of interaction between CaMKIIδ and AKAP18δ before and after β-adrenergic stimulation. Immunoprecipitation analysis revealed that more CaMKIIδ co-immunoprecipitated with AKAP18δ following Iso-stimulation (Figure 3G), ruling out the release of CaMKIIδ from AKAP18δ (Figure 3B).

An alternative explanation is that activated CaMKIIδ is critically recruited to AKAP18δ (Figure 3C) or that it moves within AKAP18δ (Figure 3D) to allow RyR phosphorylation, both in line with the increased co-immunoprecipitation. To investigate these possibilities, we experimentally reduced AKAP18δ expression by culturing LV cardiomyocytes in the presence of an adenovirus carrying a short hairpin sequence for AKAP18. In comparison with cells treated with adenovirus expressing a scrambled control sequence (SCRM), AKAP18-knockdown (AKAP18-KD) significantly reduced AKAP18 mRNA from 72 h following transduction (Figure 4A), with further reduction after 96 h post transduction (Figure 4B and C), where we observed an ∼60% decrease in AKAP18δ expression. Antibody specificity was confirmed by a blocking peptide (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8A).

Based on the above results, we employed cardiomyocytes cultured for 96 h to study effects on CaMKIIδ movement using two-colour dSTORM. Iso-treatment still induced a marked translocation of CaMKIIδ towards the Z-lines, indicating that the mechanism controlling CaMKIIδ positioning remains intact following cell culture (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8B). We also found no alteration in CaMKIIδ location when comparing scrambled virus treatment to the non-viral control (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8C). However, we observed a markedly smaller α-actinin to CaMKIIδ distance in basal conditions for AKAP18-KD (vs. SCRM control; Figure 4D and E), which may reflect a population of CaMKIIδ that has become associated with an alternative binding partner when AKAP18δ levels are reduced. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the kinase translocation mechanism remained intact in our SCRM cells treated with Iso but was lost in AKAP18-KD cells (Figure 4E). At the macroscale, reduction of AKAP18 did not disrupt the distribution of CaMKIIδ clusters, in the presence or absence of Iso (Figure 4F).

Further evidence for a key role of AKAP18δ in controlling CaMKIIδ translocation was provided by experiments employing AKAP18-knockout mice (AKAP18-KO). As in viral knockdown experiments, two-colour dSTORM revealed that Iso-induced CaMKIIδ translocation towards Z-lines was prevented in AKAP18-KO cardiomyocytes (Figure 4G and H). Similarly, neither AKAP18-KO nor Iso had any notable effect on the macroscale distribution of CaMKIIδ clusters (Figure 4I), indicating that AKAP18δ is not the sole anchor for CaMKIIδ along the Z-lines.

Taken together, these findings support a mechanism consistent with those schematically illustrated in Figure 3C and D, where CaMKIIδ movement towards the RyR nanodomain is effected by kinase translocation to AKAP18δ, or switching of its binding sites within AKAP18δ. How can we distinguish between these two possible mechanisms? We hypothesized that directed CaMKIIδ translocation to AKAP18δ would reduce the variability in RyR-to-CaMKIIδ distances by recruiting CaMKIIδ to a common anchoring point close to RyR, whereas site-switching within AKAP18δ would rather maintain NND variability (depicted in Supplementary material online, Figure S9A and B, respectively). We found that Iso-treatment reduced the distance variability (see Supplementary material online, Figure S9C), supporting the translocation mechanism displayed in Figure 3C as the most likely explanation.

AKAP18δ Contributes to regulation of basal phosphorylation of RyRs at Ser2814

To examine potential functional consequences of CaMKIIδ translocation, we investigated whether AKAP18-KD would affect CaMKIIδ activity at the RyRs, as indicated by Ser2814 phosphorylation. Under baseline conditions, we observed that reduced AKAP18δ level tended to lower Ser2814 phosphorylation (Figure 4J). However, AKAP18-KD cells still exhibited a significant increase in Ser2814 phosphorylation during Iso-treatment, which resembled the response observed in cells treated with the scrambled control virus. This finding suggests that while AKAP18δ anchors CaMKIIδ near RyRs, to functionally regulate the channels, alternative mechanisms also facilitate CaMKIIδ-mediated RyR activity during β-adrenergic stimulation.

Microtubular integrity is necessary for CaMKIIδ translocation

Our results thus far suggest an important anchoring role of AKAP18δ, although the mechanism by which CaMKIIδ reaches/is transported to these anchor sites remains unclear. Previous work in neuronal cells has shown that activated CaMKII may bind to microtubules.27 To address whether CaMKIIδ movement at the nanoscale could be dependent on the cellular network of microtubules, we disrupted the network using a 2-h treatment with either colchicine (Col, 10 μM, 2 h) or nocodazole (Noc, 10 μM, 2 h). Next, we used two-colour dSTORM imaging to assess effects on CaMKIIδ placement and movement. Neither colchicine (Figure 5A and B) nor nocodazole (Figure 5C and D) altered the basal α-actinin to CaMKIIδ NND compared to untreated control cells. However, the usual Iso-induced reduction in NND from α-actinin to CaMKIIδ was not present when microtubules were destabilized (Figure 5B and D). Analysis of the macroscale distribution of CaMKIIδ revealed that the overall distribution of CaMKIIδ clusters also remained largely unchanged (Figure 5E and F), as did the broader organization of RyRs and AKAP18δ (see Supplementary material online, Figure S10).

Taken together, these findings suggest that a stable microtubule network is required for nanoscale translocation of activated CaMKIIδ to the Z-lines, although it is unclear whether this happens through direct transport of CaMKIIδ along the microtubules, through binding to microtubule-associated proteins, or via regulatory microtubule effects at dyadic microdomains.

Discussion

In this work, we have employed advanced light microscopy techniques to reveal CaMKIIδ translocation within the RyR nanodomain. Specifically, we showed that CaMKIIδ activation via the β-AR or Epac signalling pathways induced kinase movement towards RyRs at the Z-lines. This translocation was prevented by employing CaMKIIδ inhibitors. Functionally, we observed that CaMKIIδ activation and movement were accompanied by augmented RyR phosphorylation at Ser2814 and increased Ca2+ spark frequency. Mechanistically, we observed that CaMKIIδ translocation, and likely activity, within the RyR nanodomain was dependent on both its anchoring by AKAP18δ and an intact microtubule network. These data provide a new, nanoscale understanding of the dynamic role that CaMKIIδ plays in fine-tuning cardiomyocyte Ca2+ release and contractility.

CaMKIIδ localization at baseline and following activation

CaMKIIδ phosphorylation of RyR at Ser28147,26 is expected to require close proximity between the kinase and receptor. Our confocal and dSTORM analysis revealed that the majority of CaMKIIδ is near Z-lines, where RyR localization is most prominent (Figure 1A–C), and that Iso-stimulation leads to CaMKIIδ translocation towards the Z-lines and RyR (Figure 1D, E, G and J). A similar reduction in NND was observed following cellular treatment with the Epac activator 8-CPT-cAMP (Figure 2A–D). Although 8-CPT-cAMP activates Protein Kinase A (PKA) and Protein Kinase G (PKG) in addition to CaMKIIδ, we observed that treatment with the CaMKIIδ inhibitors AIP and N1 prior to Iso-stimulation prevented the CaMKIIδ translocation (Figure 2A–D, Supplementary material online, Figure S6A–C). These findings support that CaMKIIδ translocation is linked to the kinase’s activation state. Interestingly, additional data indicated that the PKA inhibitor H89 (which may also affect other kinases such as PKG) also prevented the effect of Iso on CaMKIIδ translocation (see Supplementary material online, Figure S11A and B). Since PKA is crucial for mediating an augmentation of Ca2+ homeostasis during β-adrenergic stimulation, H89’s effects likely reflect decreased activation of CaMKIIδ by Ca2+. However, we noted that direct activation of CaMKIIδ through oxidation with H2O2 did not induce translocation (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7), which supports that the manner by which CaMKIIδ is activated is critical. Given that PKA and CaMKIIδ may have overlapping roles in regulating RyR phosphorylation,28 future work should be aimed at unravelling these coordinated functions.

While our findings are novel in cardiomyocytes, previous work in other cells has shown that CaMKIIδ is translocated to its target proteins in an activity-dependent manner, and that this translocation is essential for the targets’ phosphorylation.17,29 Investigations of other kinases have similarly demonstrated that their translocation to membranous compartments precedes phosphorylation of target proteins at these locations.30,31 Thus, physical recruitment to effector proteins may be a paradigm that is shared among diverse kinases. Notably, while previous work has reported macroscale CaMKII displacement,17,29–31 our findings in cardiomyocytes have shown nanoscale translocation, which may reflect the very tight geometry of the dyad.

The role of AKAP18δ

The preferential localization of CaMKIIδ near Z-lines, both at baseline and following Iso-stimulation (Figure 1A–C) is consistent with its anchoring at these sites. Based on our recent work,16 we hypothesized that AKAP18δ might provide such a role. Although Shannon et al. reported mobility of AKAP18γ,32 we did not observe alteration in the NND from Z-lines (α-actinin) to AKAP18δ (Figure 3F) following Iso. These results, alongside increased CaMKIIδ co-immunoprecipitation with AKAP18δ after Iso-stimulation (Figure 3G), indicated that AKAP18δ serves as a CaMKIIδ anchor at rest, and as a hub that can accommodate additional CaMKIIδ molecules during β-AR stimulation. This mechanism is in line with the mechanism proposed in Figure 3C. Such a role of AKAPs is already well established for the recruitment and anchoring of PKA.33 Further support for the mechanistic model described in Figure 3C was provided by AKAP18-KD in cultured rat cardiomyocytes. With the caveat that cell culture induces an inevitable rounding of cellular edges and partial loss of t-tubules (see Supplementary material online, Figure S12), we nevertheless observed that AKAP18-KD prevented CaMKIIδ translocation (Figure 4E), as did AKAP18γ/δ-KO (Figure 4H). However, we still noted preferential localization of the kinase at the Z-lines despite AKAP18δ removal in both KD and KO models (Figure 4F and I). This finding suggests that AKAP18δ is not the only anchoring point for CaMKIIδ at the Z-lines, in agreement with previous work showing that CaMKIIδ can be anchored at LTCCs,34 α-kinase anchoring protein,35 and at the RyR complex itself.26 Thus, a secondary anchoring protein, potentially with a weaker CaMKIIδ affinity than AKAP18δ, may serve as a kinase anchor closer to Z-lines following AKAP18δ reduction. Such a binding site may provide redundancy in the machinery controlling RyR phosphorylation.

Interestingly, the KD and KO models displayed differences in baseline CaMKIIδ positioning (Figure 4E and I), which may reflect the presence of a compensatory mechanism in the KO mouse, while there was less time for such adaptation following acute AKAP18-KD. Species differences should also be considered. Notably, the rat heart only expresses the δ variant of AKAP18,36 while the mouse heart expresses the γ and δ isoforms. Both AKAP18γ and δ variants contain an anchoring domain for CaMKIIδ16, and both were deleted in our knockout mouse model, arguing against an important role of isoform differences between species.

Alternative mechanisms

The above findings point towards AKAP18δ being important for accepting translocating CaMKIIδ, as illustrated in Figure 3C. However, since previous work by Carlson et al. has shown that AKAP18δ harbours two distinct binding sites for CaMKIIδ,16 an alternative mechanism could be envisioned where CaMKIIδ moves between these two sites, bringing it closer to RyRs (see Figure 3D). We expect that such site-switching within AKAP18δ would maintain distance variability between CaMKIIδ and RyR (see Supplementary material online, Figure S9A and B). Instead, our measurements showed a decrease in the variability of this NND (see Supplementary material online, Figure S9C), suggesting that there is in fact kinase movement to AKAP18δ binding close to RyRs (Figure 3C, Supplementary material online, Figure S9A). Prior to its translocation, this population of CaMKIIδ may either be unbound or bound to other anchors more distal from the Z-lines/RyRs. These results are further corroborated by the predicted distance between the two AKAP18δ-binding sites for CaMKIIδ. Using AlphaFold37,38 and NGL viewer,39,40 we found that the binding sites were separated by less than 10 nm: a distance that is shorter than the one observed for our NND shift in CaMKIIδ (Figure 1G and J).

It's also possible that upon CaMKIIδ activation, the kinase does not bind differently to AKAP18δ, but rather changes its conformation to bring it closer to RyRs. A similar mechanism has been reported for AKAP-anchored PKA, as flexibility within the AKAP holoenzyme allows substrate phosphorylation while the kinase remains affixed.41 In the case of CaMKII, plasticity of the kinase’s structure can include a dynamic transition from a compact to an extended conformation.4 For CaMKIIα, kinase plasticity can include a transition from a compact to an extended conformation in the range of 15–35 nm.42 Although such a transition is in a similar scale as our measured reduction in RyR-CaMKIIδ NND following Iso-stimulation, a very low proportion of CaMKIIα has been found to exist in the compact conformation, hence arguing against an importance in our experiments. Furthermore, we expect that alteration of the CaMKIIδ diameter would not alter the variability in RyR-to-CaMKIIδ NND to the extent observed (see Supplementary material online, Figure S9C). The observed reduction in this distance is consistent with greater trafficking of CaMKIIδ to AKAP18δ (Figure 3C), further supported by the increase in CaMKIIδ and AKAP18δ binding following Iso-stimulation (Figure 3F).

Functional implications

Regardless of the precise underlying mechanism, it is highly likely that the provided proximity between AKAP18δ-bound CaMKIIδ and RyRs enables channel phosphorylation. However, our previous work showed that binding of CaMKIIδ to AKAP18δ also lowers the kinase’s threshold for Ca2+ activation.16 Thus, AKAP18δ may facilitate RyR phosphorylation not only by positioning CaMKIIδ nearby, but by allowing activation at lower local [Ca2+]i. Although we cannot determine a causative relationship, we certainly observed that Iso-induced CaMKIIδ translocation was paired with marked increases in RyR phosphorylation (Figure 2F) and Ca2+ spark frequency (Figure 2G and H). An intriguing possibility is that RyR activity itself, which is a key regulator of CaMKIIδ activation, may contribute to control of kinase positioning, perhaps on a beat-to-beat basis. For example, a positive feedback loop could be envisioned where activated CaMKIIδ is translocated towards Z-line RyRs, and then maintained there following RyR activation, where additional Ca2+ release can increase CaMKIIδ activation. Indeed, our preliminary experiments performed with PKA inhibition described above are consistent with such a mechanism of positive feedback from the RyRs (see Supplementary material online, Figure S11). Under more physiological conditions, such as during continuous pacing, Ca2+ entry via LTCCs and release by RyRs may additionally contribute to CaMKIIδ activation34 and positioning. These possibilities merit investigation in future work.

As described above, our AKAP18-knockdown and knockout findings (Figure 4E and H) suggest that there is an alternative anchor that maintains CaMKIIδ near Z-lines, from which CaMKIIδ may phosphorylate RyRs. Such redundancy is supported by previous work showing that cardiomyocytes from another murine AKAP18 (AKAP7) KO model responded normally to β-AR stimulation,43 although AKAP18δ has been shown to anchor PKA at PLN and coordinate PKA-mediated PLN phosphorylation.44 While AKAP18δ could also serve as a PKA regulator at RyR, our preliminary work showed that Ser2808 phosphorylation during Iso-treatment is maintained following AKAP18-KD (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8E), further supporting the possible existence of alternative CaMKIIδ/PKA anchors. Future work will be aimed at untangling what appears to be multiple mechanisms by which CaMKIIδ and PKA regulate RyR activity.

Although the present work was focused on CaMKIIδ regulation of RyRs, it should be noted that AKAP18δ-bound CaMKIIδ additionally regulates PLN, and thus Ca2+ reuptake by SERCA2.16 Since a pool of PLN protein is localized near the Z-lines,45 it is plausible that CaMKIIδ translocation during β-AR stimulation may also facilitate PLN phosphorylation at Thr17. In supplementary work, we investigated the effects of AKAP18-KD (96 h, ∼60% reduction) on pThr17 and did not observe significant changes following Iso-treatment in comparison with scrambled controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8D). This lack of effect could reflect the remaining amount of AKAP18δ (∼40%) following KD. Notably, AKAP18-KD did not diminish PKA-dependent phosphorylation of Ser16 either (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8F). Thus, it remains as yet unclear whether CaMKIIδ translocation is important or not for PLN regulation.

Comparison with pacing-induced CaMKIIδ translocation, and the role of microtubules

Interestingly, we found that the directionality of Iso- and 8-CPT-induced CaMKIIδ translocation was opposite from the effect of electrical pacing observed by Wood et al.20 We presently confirmed this movement away from Z-lines during pacing using two-colour dSTORM imaging, but also observed that Iso-stimulation partly prevented this translocation (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4B, Supplementary material online, Table S4). How do these two different stimuli have distinct effects on CaMKIIδ positioning? Wood et al. proposed that the pacing effects resulted from the kinase being released from binding sites at the Z-lines, allowing it to reach target sites further into the sarcomere. Could Iso-stimulation counter these effects by ‘trapping’ CaMKIIδ at the Z-lines? Support for this view comes from previous work conducted in neuronal cells, which has shown that CaMKII translocation holds the kinase at target sites following autophosphorylation.19 In cardiomyocytes, such stickiness might reflect the larger size of the open, autophosphorylated configuration of the kinase, which could impede its drift from the tight confines of the dyad. Since CaMKIIδ autophosphorylation occurs mainly during β-AR stimulation, but not during the weaker stimulus of electrical pacing,46 we expect that during the physiological fight-or-flight response, CaMKIIδ ‘trapping’ at the Z-line might mitigate the effect of pacing on its movement. The true fight-or-flight response, however, includes an increase in the pacing frequency, which has not been investigated in this study, but should be examined in future investigations.

Some caution should be taken in interpreting our microtubule disruption experiments. While it is well established that microtubules facilitate cellular transportation,47 microtubule disruption has previously been shown to affect both the structural integrity of the SR48 and Ca2+ signalling in cardiomyocytes.49 Such changes could affect CaMKIIδ translocation following Iso-stimulation, such as observed in Figure 5A–D, or potentially affect the β-adrenergic signalling pathway at the dyad. Although assessment of the placement of RyR and AKAP18δ with confocal microscopy following microtubule disruption did not reveal macroscale repositioning of these proteins (see Supplementary material online, Figure S10), we cannot exclude that nanoscale alterations take place near the dyad, which inhibit CaMKIIδ translocation.

Regardless of the precise underlying mechanism, distinct CaMKIIδ translocation during pacing and Iso may reflect the need to prioritize phosphorylation of Z-line RyRs during the high physiological demand associated with β-AR stimulation.

Comparison of the effects of acute and prolonged β-adrenergic stimulation

While our study has focused on the acute effects of β-AR signalling, it is well established that this signalling becomes attenuated following prolonged stimulation.11,50 Our preliminary data indicate that changes in CaMKIIδ localization may contribute to this attenuation. In cardiomyocytes exposed to a long duration (60 min) of β-AR stimulation, we observed that the NND from α-actinin to CaMKIIδ was no longer reduced (see Supplementary material online, Figure S13A). In fact, these values tended to be higher than those observed under basal conditions. Since β-AR desensitization involves internalization and downregulation of G protein-coupled receptors,50 this retreat of CaMKIIδ from the Z-line may reflect diminishing activity of the kinase. Functionally, we have previously observed declining RyR phosphorylation during this same timeline, supporting that there is a tightly regulated system in place to counter the pro-arrhythmic effects of prolonged β-AR stimulation.11

CaMKIIδ-driven phosphorylation of RyRs has emerged as an important driver of aberrant Ca2+ homeostasis linked to heart failure and triggered arrhythmias. Understanding and countering the channel’s overactivity will require greater insight into how CaMKIIδ is anchored and regulated at the RyR nanodomain. In this work, we demonstrate that RyR phosphorylation during CaMKIIδ activation is accompanied by nanoscale translocation of CaMKIIδ towards the RyRs. We further show that this translocation is dependent on an intact microtubular network and AKAP18δ, a critical CaMKIIδ anchor. These data thus identify AKAP18δ and nanoscale positioning of CaMKIIδ as potential novel therapeutic targets in cardiac disease.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings support the view that while CaMKIIδ is highly mobile in cardiomyocytes, kinase positioning is under tight, nanoscale control. In the case of β-AR stimulation, we show that CaMKIIδ precisely translocates to the RyR nanodomain in a manner which is dependent on kinase activation, the CaMKIIδ anchor AKAP18δ, and an intact microtubular network. While further work is required to unravel the array of signalling pathways that appears to control RyR phosphorylation and CaMKIIδ, our work suggests that spatially sensitive regulation of channel activity may fine-tune cardiomyocyte contractility to meet physiological demand. Based on our data, we suggest that targeting CaMKIIδ localization within the RyR nanodomain could provide a novel approach to correcting the channel’s aberrant activity in cardiac disease.
