Nephroprotective effects of Zanthoxylum armatum DC. extract via modulation of oxidative and nitrosative stress in acute kidney injury in rats
Authors: Neeraj Thakur, S. K. Shukla, Mahesh Kumar, G. E. Chethan, Alok Singh, K. C. Nikhil, M. Saminathan, Priyanka Choudhary, J. L. Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Zanthoxylum armatum DC. has anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant, and antipyretic properties, which are widely recognized. In this study, phytochemical components, antioxidant efficacy, in vivo nephroprotective activity, and immunomodulatory potential of Z. armatum DC. hydroethanolic extract have been investigated in a gentamicin-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) rat model.Methods:Rats treated with gentamicin (100 mg/kg) for 8 days were administered Zanthoxylum armatum DC. extract. Based on biochemistry, oxidative stress indices, cytokine levels, kidney injury biomarkers, and histopathology, the nephroprotective and immunomodulatory efficacy of Z. armatum DC. extract was evaluated.Results:Catalase, glutathione reductase, superoxide dismutase, and interleukin-10 levels in blood and tissue homogenates decreased due to gentamicin toxicity, whereas serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, lipid peroxide, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, cystatin C, kidney injury molecule-1, and gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase levels elevated. Zanthoxylum armatum DC. treatment reduced kidney damage, cytokine imbalance, and oxidative stress; however, results were nearly similar to standard drug. The organoprotective efficacy of Z. armatum DC. was further validated by histopathology of kidney, liver, and heart tissues.Conclusion:The present research shows that Z. armatum DC. can ameliorate gentamicin-induced AKI, which may be attributed to its antioxidant properties, phenolic and flavonoid phytoconstituents, and ability to suppress inflammatory cytokines. Further studies may therefore be developed to assess its safety and efficacy for clinical trials.Statement of Novelty:Z. armatum DC. hydroethanolic extract has various properties like antioxidant, organoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and potential to inhibit biochemical parameters involved in renal impairment via blocking p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase/nuclear factor kappa B p65 signaling pathway.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a global public health concern that is estimated to cause millions of fatalities every year[1]. AKI, formerly known as acute renal failure, is a term used to denote clinical symptoms associated with intrinsic renal malfunction. A rise in serum creatinine within 2–7 days and a decline in renal function are indicators of AKI[2]. Numerous factors, such as oxidative stress, immunological dysregulation, apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory response, have been linked to AKI development[3]. Uncertainty underlies the processes via which AKI raises the risk of adverse impacts. Animal models are therefore essential for testing novel treatments and elucidating physiological processes[4]. Understanding the pathophysiology of kidney ailments and developing robust preclinical testing methods for novel treatments depend primarily on animal models. Rodents are the most commonly employed experimental model for AKI and chronic kidney disease (CKD), while several others have been developed[5]. The reversible recovery is one of the intriguing characteristics of pre-renal AKI. AKI is caused by a variety of complex mechanisms, and oxidative stress and inflammatory response are crucial variables in its progression[6]. Reactive oxygen species/reactive nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) and the ensuing oxidative stress play a major role in the development of AKI. The antioxidant system’s main components are glutathione reductase (GSH), glutathione peroxidase, catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD). Via lipid peroxidation and the oxidation of the polyunsaturated fatty acids in the red blood cell (RBC) membrane, oxidative stress is caused by an increase in the activity of enzymes that generate free radicals, a decrease in the activity of enzymes that eliminate them, and an inadequacy of antioxidants. Meanwhile, mitochondria are also a vital target for the deleterious effects of ROS[7–9]. In the pathogenesis of AKI, inflammation plays a crucial role. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), a pro-inflammatory cytokine, is produced in AKI and is linked to the etiology of kidney damage. TNF-α has the ability to trigger a pro-inflammatory cascade and itself has cytotoxic effects[10]. Although its role in kidney injury is less well established, interleukin-10 (IL-10) is recognized as a quintessential anti-inflammatory cytokine that performs the vital task of regulating inflammation. IL-10 protects against renal ischemia through the stimulation of neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin[11,12]. Serum creatinine (SCr) has some limitations as a kidney injury marker, so several kidney injury markers have been developed to enhance risk assessment, early detection, differential diagnosis, and prognosis for AKI[13]. Members of the cysteine proteinase inhibitor protein superfamily, such as cystatin C (CysC), are freely filtered by the glomerulus and nearly wholly reabsorbed in the proximal tubule, where they undergo nearly total catabolism. CysC is not secreted by the renal tubules, in contrast to creatinine[14]. Kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), a glycoprotein found in cell membranes, has a number of characteristics that make it a good biomarker for kidney injury, which includes low or absent KIM-1 expression in healthy kidneys, significant up-regulation and insertion into the proximal tubule’s apical membrane following ischemia-reperfusion injury, and persistence in epithelial cells until recovery[15]. The metabolically active proximal tubule is home to the brush border enzyme gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT). Due to its large molecular weight, GGT is unable to get through the glomerular barrier. Its urine level is therefore mostly attributed to tubular injury rather than glomerular injury[16]. The goal of managing AKI is to preserve renal function, which should quickly return to normal following aggressive and successful treatment. For individuals with AKI, optimizing the window of time for treatment is critical. It has been seen that certain natural anti-inflammatory or antioxidant pharmaceuticals can speed up the recovery process[17]. Several herbal remedies may be useful in the management and prevention of AKI like silymarin, a lipophilic extract of the seeds of Silybum marianum (L.) The aromatic perennial shrub or small tree Zanthoxylum armatum DC. (family: Rutaceae), also known as Timur, can grow up to 6 m tall and has dense glabrous foliage with straight prickles. It is spread from Kashmir to the northeastern part of India, Bhutan, Pakistan, China, Japan, North and South Korea, North Vietnam, Laos, Myanmar, and Taiwan[19]. Bioactive compounds found in the plant include lignans, alkaloids, sterols, coumarins, phenolics, terpenoids, flavonoids, benzenoids, glycosides, alkenic acids, amino acids, fatty acids, armamide, L-sesamin, L-asarin, L-planinin, limonene, methyl cinnamate, linalool, linayl acetate, geraniol, methyl cinnamate, citral, linalool, and sabines[20]. Many traditional medical practices have used various portions of Z. armatum DC., including the fruits, stem, leaves, and bark, as appetizers, antipyretics, diuretics, and anti-inflammatory agents. Zanthoxylum armatum DC. stem bark aqueous extract has been utilized for studying the antidiabetic and antioxidant effects, and found to be non-toxic at different dose rates[21]. Because of its pharmacological traits, Z. armatum DC. is in high demand both nationally and internationally[22].

Several components of herbal remedies include nephroprotective and antioxidant properties that assist in preventing kidney damage[23]. The present study was undertaken to evaluate the organoprotective efficacy of hydroethanolic extract of Z. armatum DC. in gentamicin-induced AKI Wistar albino rat model. The main outcome measures were considered to be the amelioration of oxidative stress, cytokine imbalance, and kidney damage. We hypothesized that supplementation of Z. armatum DC. will alter the levels of ROS/RNS, cytokines, urine and serum biochemical profiles, and concentrations of kidney injury biomarkers like KIM-1, CysC, and GGT in urine and serum.

Methods

Plant material and extraction

Zanthoxylum armatum DC. plant bark was collected from the Mandi district of Himachal Pradesh, a hilly state of Northern India. The bark was dried and then cut into small pieces, washed in distilled water to eliminate dirt, air-dried, and then pulverized uniformly with an electric grinder. The powdered material of bark was soaked overnight in hydroethanolic (1:1) solvent and then extracted using a magnetic stirrer for 6 h at room temperature. Whatman filter paper (No. 40) was used to filter, and solvent was extracted using a rotary evaporator. The extract was vacuum sealed and refrigerated until used. The yield of Z. armatum DC. from extract was 18.7%, brownish, thick, and sticky residue.

Phytochemical studies

Total phenol, total flavonoid, and total flavanol contents

The concentrations of phenolics, total flavonoid, and total flavanol in plant extract were assessed by procedures given by Biglari et al, Zou et al, and Kumaran and Karunakaran, respectively[24–26].

In vitro antioxidant activity

DPPH scavenging assay and total antioxidant capacity

The antioxidant activity of Z. armatum DC. plant extract was evaluated by the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was estimated according to the phosphomolybdenum method given by Asnaashari et al[27] and Prieto et al[28], respectively.

High-performance liquid chromatography

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of Z. armatum DC. hydroethanolic extract and powder was carried out at the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research-Central Institute of Medicinal and Aromatic Plants (CSIR-CIMAP), Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh, India, after outsourcing.

In vivo nephroprotective activity

Study animals

Twelve to thirteen-week-old Wistar albino male rats (IVRI2CQ), weighing approximately 150–200 g, were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Section of the Indian Council for Agricultural Research-Indian Veterinary Research Institute (ICAR-IVRI), Izatnagar. The animals in the institute’s laboratory animal house were given a 2-week acclimatization period. The rats were kept in the institute’s laboratory animal house in hygienic polypropylene cages with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle at a room temperature of 25 ± 2°C for the duration of the study. The animals were given a standard diet and unlimited access to water.

Experimental methodology

Using the previously established gentamicin-induced AKI rat model, the nephroprotective and antioxidant properties of the plant material were assessed[29]. A total of 24 male rats had been divided into four groups of six at random. Group I (Gp. I) was considered as healthy control and was not given any treatment. Group II (Gp. II) was disease control and received gentamicin at 100 mg/kg body weight (BW) intra-peritoneally (IP) for 8 days. Group III (Gp. III) rats received gentamicin along with standard herbal drug Cystone® (Himalaya Pharmaceutical Company, India) at 100 mg/kg BW by oral gastric gavage for 8 days. Group IV (Gp. IV) received gentamicin along with hydroethanolic (1:1) extract of Z. armatum DC. [effective concentration50 (EC50)] at 400 mg/kg BW orally by gastric gavage for 8 days (the EC50 was calculated from an earlier study: Neeraj Thakur[30]).

Sample collection

On days 0, 4, and 8 of the trial, individual animals were housed in metabolic cages, and 24-hour urine samples were taken. 5 M hydrochloric acid was used to acidify part of each urine sample to a pH of 2. After 10 min of centrifuging urine samples at 1500 g to remove debris, the supernatants were kept at −20°C until analysis. Blood samples were obtained by venipuncturing the tail vein of rats on days 0, 4, and 8 of the trial and placing them in vials with heparin and clot activator, respectively. Blood samples in vials containing a clot activator were allowed to clot before being centrifuged for 20 min at 4°C at 3000 revolutions per minute (rpm) to separate the serum. The serum samples were then stored at −20°C until analysis. At the end of the study (day 8), the animals were sacrificed, and samples of kidney, heart, and liver tissue were taken for histopathology and the assessment of oxidative stress indicators. For electron microscopy, one half of the kidney was preserved in 2.5% glutaraldehyde.

Urine and serum biochemical study

Urine urea nitrogen (UUN), creatinine, total protein, albumin, sodium and potassium levels in urine, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, total protein, albumin, and sodium and potassium concentrations in serum were assessed spectrophotometrically using commercially available kits as per the manufacturer’s guidelines (Tulip Diagnostics Private Limited, Goa, India), with a Thermo Scientific Spectro 20D+ spectrophotometer.

Oxidative/nitrosative stress indices (blood)

RBC suspension and RBC hemolysate

About 3 mL of blood was collected from the rats in vacutainers containing heparin (10 IU/mL) as an anticoagulant. After centrifugation for 10 min at 3000 rpm, the plasma and buffy coat were removed. An ice-cold isotonic normal saline solution (NSS) was used to wash RBCs three times. To make a 10% hemolysate, the RBC pellet was diluted in a 1:10 ratio with ice-cold distilled water. The remaining RBC pellet was then diluted in a 1:1 ratio with ice-cold NSS to create an RBC suspension.

Estimation of GSH, SOD, lipid peroxide, and CAT

GSH and SOD in blood were estimated by the 5-5ʹdithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) method in RBC suspension as described by Prins and Loos[31] and the method given by Madesh and Balasubramanian[32], respectively. Lipid peroxide (LPO) and CAT levels in RBC hemolysate were determined by the methods given by Placer et al[33] and Bergmayer[34], respectively.

Oxidative/nitrosative stress indices (tissue homogenate)

Tissue homogenates

200 mg of tissue sections from the kidney, heart, and liver were collected in ice-cold phosphate buffer saline (PBS) (pH 7.4), and tissue homogenates were prepared under cold conditions by using a tissue homogenizer. The tissue homogenates were centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm, and the resulting supernatant was used to estimate nitric oxide (NO) and LPO/malondialdehyde (MDA). Organ samples were collected in a different solution containing 0.02 M EDTA in order to estimate GSH.

Estimation of GSH, MDA, and NO

GSH and MDA concentrations in homogenates were determined using the DTNB method suggested by Sedlak and Lindsay[35] and the thiobarbituric acid method given by Rehman[36], respectively. NO level in tissue homogenate was estimated by using Griess reagent as per the method given by Green et al[37].

Immunomodulatory activity

Estimation of TNF-α (catalog no. KB3145) levels in urine and serum, and IL-10 (catalog no. k11-0109) levels in serum and tissue homogenate were done using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Krishgen BioSystems, Mumbai, India).

Estimation of kidney injury biomarkers

Biomarkers of kidney injury like CysC (catalog no. k11-0146), KIM-1(catalog no. k11-0550) (Krishgen BioSystems, Mumbai, India), and GGT (Aspen Laboratories, Pvt. Ltd., Delhi) were estimated by utilizing commercially available kits as per the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Histopathology

Tissue samples (5 mm) from kidney, liver, and heart were collected and fixed in 10% formalin. Further, these samples were processed for histopathological examination as per the standard protocol.

Scanning electron microscopy

Collected tissue samples (1–1.5 mm) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 12 h at 4°C for ultrastructural studies by electron microscope. After fixation, tissues were washed at 4°C in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.2) three times for 15 min duration. Washed tissues were then dehydrated for 20 min each at 4°C in ascending grades of alcohol, followed by washing in absolute alcohol. In a liquid carbon dioxide chamber, dehydrated tissues were dried at 31.5°C at 1100 psi. Then, dried tissues were mounted on aluminum stubs with adhesive tape, and gold coating was done using a JFC-1600 auto fine sputter gold coater. Coated tissues were viewed at 80 kV under the scanning electron microscope JSM-6610 LV (JEOL Ltd., Japan).

Data analysis

The data analysis was done by one-way analysis of variance using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 27. Data (n =6 in each group) were analyzed for calculating mean, standard error of mean (SEM), and significance of difference (P < 0.05), if any, between mean values of different groups and days. Graphs were drawn in GraphPad Prism version 9.

Discussion

Ionic drugs are secreted by tubular organic ion transporters across the tubule’s luminal membranes, and filtered toxins are reabsorbed into the tubule lumen, making kidney vulnerable to a greater percentage and concentration of drugs and toxins than other organs. Due to this reason, renal tubular epithelial cells are more vulnerable to harm[7,38]. Gentamicin belongs to the aminoglycoside class of drugs with the main bactericidal mechanism as inhibition of protein synthesis. Major disadvantages of gentamicin use include risks for ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity[39]. Nephron destruction in the kidneys causes AKI in animal models. Gentamicin antibiotics are frequently utilized in the development of kidney damage animal models. There are substantial variations in the antibiotic induction dosages used to induce kidney damage. Gentamicin toxicity is directly linked to phospholipidosis[40]. Antioxidants, inflammatory mediators, and genetic modifiers are among the many treatments that have been suggested to address this problem[41]. Numerous antioxidants have been researched to safeguard the physiological characteristics and function of the kidneys, but none have been shown to be clinically effective as of yet. Considering this worry, attention has been focused on using natural antioxidant sources as substitutes for synthetic antioxidants to aid in alleviating kidney damage[42]. According to some recent research, 38 natural product-derived drugs have been approved in a decade for various conditions like infectious diseases, oncology, neurological diseases, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, and diabetes[43].

India has long been recognized for its abundance of medicinal plants.

Many ethnic groups in India and Nepal have made considerable use of Z. armatum DC. in their traditional indigenous medical practices. The seeds and bark of Z. armatum DC. are used as a tonic for fever and dyspepsia, as well as an aromatic and carminative[44]. Total phenol and flavonoid contents in hydroethanolic (50%) extract of Z. armatum DC. bark were calculated as mg GAE/g and were found to be slightly higher than previously reported concentrations in hydroethanolic (70%) extract[19]. It proves that 50% ethanolic is a better solvent for fractionation of polyphenols and flavonoids as compared to 70% ethanol. When the hydroethanolic extract was subjected to phytochemical analysis using HPLC, berberine was identified and measured as a physiologically active substance. This result is consistent with the findings of previous researchers who reported 1.78 mg berberine/g bark in 70% ethanolic extract of Z. armatum DC[45]. The quaternary benzylisoquinoline plant alkaloid berberine is nonbasic and has a documented medical history in both Chinese and Ayurvedic medicine. Berberine is an active ingredient found in the stem bark, rhizome, and roots of numerous medicinally significant plants[46]. Plants are the primary source of alkaloids, a class of basic nitrogen-containing chemical compounds that are also occasionally found in fungi and mammals. Alkaloids, one of the secondary metabolites of plants, have a wide range of physiological and pharmacological properties, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, analgesic, and anti-tumor effects. These properties make them a wealth of resources for drug discovery[47]. Berberine shows its antioxidant effects by reducing the radical to the corresponding hydrazine on reacting with the hydrogen donors in berberine[48]. Zanthoxylum armatum DC. extract has active ingredients that can give free-radical hydrogen and neutralize the potentially harmful entity, as demonstrated by the radical scavenging activity. Overall antioxidant activity of Z. armatum DC. extract suggested the existence of potent reducing agents. Numerous medicinal plant extracts have been demonstrated to have a positive correlation between their polyphenol and flavonoid content and their capacity to reduce phosphomolybdate[49].

On days 4 and 8, the erythrocytes of the disease control group showed a significant increase in LPO levels and a significant decrease in SOD, CAT, and GSH activity when compared to the healthy control group. The abundance of mitochondria in renal tubular cells aids in meeting the high energy requirements of the reabsorption process. The renal tubules are particularly susceptible to oxidative injury because mitochondria are the primary location for intracellular free-radical generation via the respiratory chain[50]. The oxidant–antioxidant equilibrium is altered by the overproduction of ROS brought on by gentamicin nephrotoxicity. Being a non-enzymatic antioxidant, GSH protects cells from the damaging effects of ROS by acting as a free-radical scavenger. The depletion of GSH levels caused by gentamicin may be the result of increased formation of free radicals, increased consumption in the protection of –SH group-containing proteins, and decreased SOD antioxidant enzymes linked to an excess of hydrogen peroxide and superoxide anions. Lipid peroxidation also disrupts the lipid content of the membrane, which raises MDA, a byproduct of lipid peroxidation[51–53]. Superoxide anion is capable of being converted into hydrogen peroxide and water owing to the catalytic activity of CAT and SOD. Through enzymatic processes, GSH can efficiently neutralize free radicals directly or indirectly[54]. In contrast to disease control, Z. armatum DC. treatment resulted in a significant (P < 0.05) decrease in LPO levels and a significant (P < 0.05) increase in GSH, SOD, and CAT levels. In the overall antioxidant defense strategy, the function and efficacy of the first-line defense antioxidants – which primarily consist of SOD, CAT, and glutathione peroxidase – are crucial and essential, particularly with regard to the superoxide anion radical (O2•−), which is continuously produced during regular bodily metabolism[55]. Polyphenols work as reducing agents, metal chelators, and free-radical absorbers, quenchers, and neutralizers to efficiently stop biological systems from deteriorating under high levels of oxidative stress. The ability of plant polyphenols and flavonoids to reduce metal ions could render them important in regulating the redox status of the cell and affecting the activities of metallo-enzymes[56].

The administration of gentamicin caused AKI, which was indicated by elevated levels of urine total protein, albumin, sodium, SCr, BUN, and TNF-α, and decreased levels of urine creatinine, UUN, serum total protein, albumin, sodium, and IL-10 in the disease control group compared to the healthy control group because of renal function impairment. A decrease in urine creatinine and UUN levels indicates incorrect urea removal and reduced tubular excretion[57–59]. BUN and SCr concentrations rise as a result of renal function deteriorating due to the inability of the body to eliminate nitrogenous waste products in urine[60]. Pro-inflammatory cytokine like TNF-α is up-regulated in ischemic renal injuries, leading to an influx of neutrophils. TNF-α has been known to increase ROS/RNS formation in kidney diseases, leading to urinary albumin excretion, which we can correlate with the findings of the present study[61]. IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, plays an integral role in both AKI renal protection and the inhibition of the systemic or local inflammatory response spurred by ischemic AKI[62]. Through the inhibition of cyclooxygenase-2 expression, IL-1, and TNF-α release, berberine exerts anti-inflammatory actions. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that berberine can mitigate ischemia-reperfusion injury by reducing ROS formation and modifying the effects of oxidative stress[63,64]. There is a complex relationship between TNF-α and oxidative damage. In renal disorders, TNF-α has been shown to enhance ROS and RNS production, which increases the excretion of urine albumin, and this can be correlated with the findings of the present study[65]. In contrast to the disease control group, the Z. armatum DC.-treated group exhibited higher levels of urine creatinine, UUN, serum total protein, serum albumin, serum sodium, and IL-10, and lower levels of urine total protein, urine albumin, urine sodium, SCr, BUN, and TNF-α. Nephroprotective activity of Z. armatum DC. can be attributed to the presence of berberine and kaempferol. Berberine present in Z. armatum DC. inhibits production of anti-inflammatory cytokines and enhances expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, and also shows anti-oxidant properties by scavenging the free radicals[66]. Berberine may reduce oxidative stress by increasing SOD and decreasing the expression of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP) oxidase, which may be mediated by the adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway. For the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, berberine activates the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway in kidney[67,68]. By blocking the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38-MAPK)/nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) p65 signaling pathway, berberine reduces the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β[69]. Kaempferol reduces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and improves nephrotoxicity by reducing inflammation and oxidative stress[70]. To mitigate renal tubular damage, kaempferol prevents ROS/apoptosis signal-regulating kinase-1 from activating the MAPK signaling pathway[71]. Following gentamicin administration, kidney, liver, and cardiac tissue homogenates demonstrated reduced levels of GSH and IL-10, while MDA and NO levels increased, indicating the production of oxidative stress and inflammation. Gentamicin can lead to overproduction of toxic free radicals and increased cellular lipid peroxidation in renal tubules, liver, and cardiomyocytes[72–74]. AKI-induced oxidative stress, urine toxins, activated leukocytes, and inflammatory cytokines may all be involved in disorders of the liver and heart[75]. Compared to disease control (Gp. II), animals treated with Z. armatum DC. (Gp. IV) had greater GSH levels and lower MDA and NO levels; these values were comparable to those of the cystone treatment group (Gp. III). Berberine increased GSH, SOD, CAT, and TAC levels and decreased MDA levels in lead-induced renal injury[76].

Renal histopathology revealed severe tubular degeneration, tubular epithelial vacuolation, tubular dilatation, presence of eosinophilic proteinaceous material, and basophilic cast within the tubular lumen of PCT and DCT. Severe vacuolar degeneration and necrosis of hepatocytes, vasculitis with infiltration of mononuclear cells, and mild degeneration and separation of cardiac myocytes were also seen in the disease control. Disarrangement of hepatic strands, necrosis, sinusoid enlargement, vacuole formations, leukocytic infiltrations, dilation, and congestion of blood vessels with hemorrhage have been observed in gentamicin-induced hepatotoxicity[77]. The gentamicin-treated group showed signs of myocardial fiber hypertrophy, larger intracellular gaps between myocardial fibers, blood vessel congestion and thrombosis, and severe bleeding when compared to the control groups[78]. The production of ROS and RNS may also contribute to the extent of the damage[79]. Lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress may result from gentamicin accumulation, which is mostly possible in the renal cortex. Damage to the kidneys can result from their propensity to mediate the generation of ROS in mitochondria, including superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals. Gentamicin buildup in the renal cortex causes alterations in the morphology of the kidneys, and the overall condition is quite similar in experimental animals and humans. The gentamicin-induced nephropathy model has been employed in order to investigate the pathophysiological and molecular mechanisms that underlie the nephrotoxicity of gentamicin[80]. Other organs may sustain harm as a result of gentamicin-induced AKI adverse effects[4,81]. In the case of AKI, the accumulation of uremic toxins may potentially lead to cardiovascular damage. In addition to preventing renal damage, treatment with Z. armatum DC. also reduced the effects of gentamicin on liver tissue and the myocardium, as evidenced by mild tubular degeneration, mild sinusoidal space dilatation, and mild cardiac myocyte degeneration.

Patients in need of more intensive therapy could be identified with the use of early kidney injury diagnosis. To diagnose kidney damage early, various kidney injury markers have been used. Serum and urine CysC and KIM-1 levels, as well as urine GGT levels, were higher in disease control than in healthy control. Compared to creatinine, CysC is a reliable indicator of glomerular filtration rate since it is only eliminated by the kidney. Furthermore, because CysC can predict AKI 24–48 h before any increase in SCr levels, it can identify subclinical AKI and tubular damage[82]. Due to its function in controlling extracellular proteolysis, a rise in CysC content in bodily fluids and tissues signifies disease. The diagnosis and management of many illnesses, including CKD, depend on the measurement of CysC levels in bodily fluids[83]. It has been noted that during the regeneration phase following an injury, KIM-1 is expressed in greater quantities in the apical aspect of PCT cells, and urine KIM-1 levels increase more quickly than SCr[84]. Urine containing KIM-1 is very specific for kidney damage. There is no evidence of KIM-1 expression in any other organs that could affect kidney excretion. It has been demonstrated in numerous preclinical experiments with a wide range of renal insults, including different toxins, to be a marker for injury that is far more sensitive than BUN and SCr[85]. GGT and other urine enzymes are unable to pass through the glomerular barrier due to their high molecular weight. Consequently, tubular injury rather than glomerular injury is the main cause of its urine level[86]. Animals treated with Z. armatum DC. had significantly lower levels of KIM-1, CysC, and urine GGT than those in the disease control group. This could be attributed to the renoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties of kaempferol and berberine[45,67,87]. If more rats with gentamicin-induced AKI had been included in the study, the statistical significance would have been higher, and inclusion of dose-response data could have benefited the study. Despite this limitation, the trustworthiness of the results seems to have been unaffected by the multivariate approach used to comprehend the pathophysiology of gentamicin-induced AKI.

Conclusion

The results of the current study show that Z. armatum DC. is effective in curing gentamicin-induced AKI in a rat model. This may be due to a number of characteristics, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, organoprotective, and potential to inhibit biochemical parameters via blocking p38-MAPK/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway that contribute to renal impairment. In brief, Z. armatum DC. extract raised the levels of antioxidant enzymes (GSH and CAT) and IL-10 in urine, serum, and tissue homogenates, while reducing the levels of LPO, TNF-α, and kidney damage biomarkers (KIM-1, CysC, and GGT). Since many nephroprotective remedies are not available, alternative therapeutic approaches like ethno-veterinary medicine are crucial in preventing kidney injury. The effectiveness and safety of Z. armatum DC. in human clinical trials may be assessed by further thorough investigation in a broader population.
