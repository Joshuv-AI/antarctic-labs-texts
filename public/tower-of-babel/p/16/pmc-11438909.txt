Visible-light induced effective and sustainable remediation of nitro organics pollutants using Pd-doped ZnO nanocatalyst
Authors: Sagar Vikal, Savita Meena, Yogendra K Gautam, Ashwani Kumar, Mukul Sethi, Swati Meena, Durvesh Gautam, Beer Pal Singh, Prakash Chandra Agarwal, Mohan Lal Meena, Vijay Parewa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitroaromatic compounds represent a class of highly toxic pollutants discharged into aquatic environments by various industrial activities, posing significant threats to ecological integrity and human health due to their persistent and hazardous nature. In this study, Pd-doped ZnO nanoparticles were investigated as a potential solution for the degradation of nitro organics, offering heightened photocatalytic efficacy and prolonged stability. The synthesis of Pd-doped ZnO NPs was achieved via the hydrothermal method, with subsequent analysis through XRD spectra and XPS confirming successful Pd doping within the ZnO matrix. Characterization through FESEM and HRTEM unveiled the heterogeneous morphologies of both undoped and Pd-doped ZnO nanoparticles. Additionally, UV–vis and PL spectroscopy provided insights into the optical properties, chemical bonding, and defect structures of the synthesized Pd-doped ZnO NPs. Pd doping induces a redshift in ZnO’s absorption spectra, reducing the bandgap from 3.12 to 2.94 eV as Pd concentration rises from 0 to 0.2 wt.%. The photocatalytic degradation, following pseudo-first-order kinetics, achieved 90% nitrobenzene abatement (200 µg/L, pH 7) under visible light within 320 min with a catalyst loading of 16 µg/mL. The photocatalytic efficacy of 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO (k = 0.058 min⁻1) exhibited a 25-fold enhancement compared to bare ZnO (k = 3.1 × 10–4 min-1). Subsequent quenching and ESR experiments identified hydroxyl radicals (OH•) as the predominant active species in the degradation mechanism. Mass spectrometry analysis unveiled potential breakdown intermediates, illuminating a plausible degradation pathway. The investigated Pd-doped ZnO nanoparticles demonstrated reusability for up to five successive treatment cycles, offering a sustainable solution to nitro organics contamination challenges.Keywords: Visible light, Nitro organics, Photocatalyst, ZnO Nanoparticles, PalladiumSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Nitroaromatics are a ubiquitous class of organic compounds featuring at least one nitro functional group (NO₂) directly attached to a conjugated aromatic ring system1. The electron-withdrawing properties of these nitro groups confer unique reactivity and stability to the compounds, facilitating their extensive application in the production of various industrial materials, including explosives, pesticides, and dyes2. However, the same nitro functionalities that render these compounds industrially valuable also confer significant resistance to biodegradation, leading to their persistent accumulation in the environment3. The widespread and prolonged use of nitroaromatic compounds have resulted in substantial contamination of both groundwater and soil, directly impacting drinking water quality and posing significant risks to human health4–7. The widespread application and subsequent environmental persistence of nitroaromatic compounds underscore the critical need for effective remediation strategies to mitigate their impact on ecosystem and human health8. Conventional remediation approaches, adsorption, ozonation, coagulation, electrochemical9,10, encompassing classical, biological, and physicochemical methodologies11,12, encounter significant challenges in effectively mitigating this pollutant owing to its inherent stability and enduring nature.

In the realm of environmental remediation, Advanced Oxidation Processes (AOPs) have emerged as a compelling strategy13, distinguished by their capacity to generate highly reactive oxidative species (ROS). Highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH) possess an appropriate oxidation potential (E⁰ = + 3.06 V)14,15 and an exceptionally short lifetime (~ 0.1 ns) within the realm of ROS. This unique combination translates to their exceptional efficacy in degrading harmful organic compounds16. Advanced AOP (Advanced Oxidation Processes) techniques17 have been explored for degradation, including sonolysis, UV irradiation combined with either O3 or H2O2, pulsed plasma discharge, supercritical water oxidation and radiolysis. Iron redox couples (Fe(II)/Fe(III)) coupled with hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) have gained prominence as Fenton’s reagent18, effectively catalysing the degradation of organic pollutants via hydroxyl radical (•OH) generation. Fenton’s reagent suffers from limitations like the need for highly acidic conditions (pH < 3) and the formation of significant iron sludge, raising concerns about secondary environmental pollution.

An alternative strategy for harnessing the oxidative potential of H2O2 to degrade organic substrates involved its coupling with a suitable photocatalyst. The integration of H2O2 with Zinc oxide (ZnO) photocatalyst enhances the overall efficiency of the degradation protocol targeting organic compounds19–21. However, owing to its high bandgap energy, ZnO exhibited photocatalytic activity primarily under ultraviolet (UV) illumination (λ < 400 nm)22. Elemental doping to ZnO is one of the approaches to resolving this concern23,24. Nonetheless, it is generally challenging to substantially improve visible light absorption of ZnO by elemental doping. Besides, the stability and the requirement of the environmentally demanding method for the fabrication of doped ZnO photocatalyst hamper their utility. Moreover, the energy conversion efficiency is constrained by suboptimal charge separation, which is attributed to high electron–hole (e−/h+) recombination rates and sluggish charge transport, as well as the slow kinetics of interfacial reactions. The presence of dopant levels can further exacerbate recombination by acting as recombination centres.

Strategic manipulation of surface defects has emerged as an auspicious method to advance the photocatalytic performance of doped semiconductor photocatalysts25–27. The interfacial defects, such as oxygen vacancies, play a crucial role in dictating photocatalytic processes. Surface oxygen vacancies act as bifunctional centres, suppressing charge carrier recombination through electron/hole trapping and promoting pollutant adsorption for efficient charge transfer. ZnO active sites are locally influenced by these defects, investigating the impact of oxygen vacancies alongside simultaneous element doping to modulate ZnO’s photocatalytic properties remains a formidable frontier in research28–31.

Hence, building upon our ongoing investigation into the synthesis and photocatalytic evaluation of nanomaterials32–36, this work reports a rapid hydrothermal route to synthesize oxygen-vacancy-rich Pd-doped ZnO nanoparticles, followed by an annealing step. This method significantly enhances photocatalytic degradation of nitroaromatic pollutants under visible light with hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) activation. Previous research has not explored the engineering of Pd-doped ZnO surface defects to enhance the photocatalytic degradation of nitroaromatic pollutants. The present investigation, including morphological, structural, and optical analyses, reveals the interplay between oxygen vacancies and Pd dopants in the photocatalytic mechanism. Additionally, we conducted EPR and radical scavenging experiments to delineate the role and nature of reactive oxygen species (ROS) involved in the degradation pathway. Based on our experimental findings, we propose a mechanistic framework that elucidates the enhanced photocatalytic activity observed in this study.

Experimental

Preparation of Pd-doped ZnO nanophotocatalyst

Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and zinc chloride (ZnCl2) (Sigma Aldrich) are used as precursors in the hydrothermal technique of nanoparticle formation. Zinc chloride solutions of varying concentrations were made in 100 ml distilled water and stirred for 90 min. In the meantime, 100 ml distilled water were mixed with weighted sodium hydroxide pellets while being stirred for the same duration to prepare 0.5 M NaOH solutions. A NaOH solution is was added drop by drop to the previous solution while being constantly stirred. In addition, the aqueous solution was placed in an autoclave (a sealed Teflon-lined container) and boiled to 200 °C for six hours continuously under autogenous pressure. Afterwards, it was cool down to atmospheric temperature. Following the procedure, the white precipitate was cleaned with distilled water and dried further for two hours at 120 °C in an oven. After achieving a fine powder of bare ZnO nanoparticles by grinding, Pd-doped ZnO nanostructure was obtained by using palladium chloride (PdCl2) (Sigma Aldrich) as a product. Zn1-xPdxO nanostructured powder samples (where x = 0.02, 0.04, 0.08, and 0.2 weight percent) were synthesized using the same method as ZnO NPs in their bare form. In a muffle furnace, samples of ZnO and Pd-doped ZnO nanostructure were annealed for two hours at 700 °C.

Characterization

Synthesized nanoparticles were examined for crystallinity, structure, and phases by using an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker AXS, D8 Advance). Bare and Pd-doped ZnO NP’s surface characteristics and composition were investigated using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) (FEI, Quanta 200F) and energy dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX) , respectively. The composition and oxidation states of Pd-doped ZnO NPs were investigated using XPS analysis. Structural properties were determined using HRTEM/SAED. Optical characteristics and chemical bonding of the synthesized Pd-doped ZnO NP’s were estimated by using UV–vis spectroscopy. The optical properties of the synthesized bare and Pd-doped ZnO NPs were assessed using photoluminescence (PL) (FLS 980, Edinburgh Instrument), respectively. GC analyses were carried out by using a Shimadzu GC-FID system. GC–MS analyses were carried out on the Shimadzu GCMS-QP2020 system. At room temperature, all ESR measurements were performed on a Bruker EMX ESR spectrometer (Billerica, MA).

Photocatalytic experiment

The investigation focused on the photodegradation efficacy of Pd-doped ZnO concerning the photocatalytic degradation of nitroaromatic compounds. A solution of nitrobenzene (10 mL, 200 µg/L) was prepared, to which photocatalyst (16 µg/mL) and hydrogen peroxide (0.3 mL) were added. For half an hour, this mixture was left in the dark to achieve adsorption–desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, the mixture was transferred to a photoreactor equipped with double-walled quartz and water chiller, having a volume of 100 mL. The reaction was conducted at ambient temperature, with constant stirring, utilizing a tungsten lamp of 35W as the source of light. At regular intervals of 40 min, a small sample was extracted from the solution. The employed photocatalyst was subsequently separated and reused for subsequent reactions through centrifugation (10,000 rpm for 10 min). The photocatalytic efficiency of the prepared samples was evaluated and analysed using gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC-FID) instrument. Moreover, the deterioration efficiency’s progression was assessed by employing Eq. 1.

The initial concentration of the compound denoted as C0, and its concentration after photoirradiation at a given time t, denoted as Ct, were investigated. Furthermore, the photocatalytic activity of the prepared catalysts was also examined on other nitroaromatic compounds, namely p-nitrophenol, p-nitroaniline, and m-dinitrobenzene in analogous trials.

Recyclability test

Recycling investigations were conducted wherein a nitrobenzene solution of 100 ml was selected as the representative pollutant, coupled with a 16 µg/ml of 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO. Upon the completion of the reaction, the catalyst underwent recuperation through centrifugation at a rate of 10,000 revolutions per minute for a duration of 10 min. Subsequently, the retrieved catalyst underwent thorough washing procedures involving water and ethanol, enabling its reuse across five consecutive reaction cycles.

Figure 2 shows SEM images of the samples. The morphological changed from nanorods (zinc oxide nanoparticles) to irregular nanorods & sphere (palladium doped zinc oxide nanoparticles) could be owing to the change of Pd into Zn2+ ZnO matrix. However, the particle’s surface free energy reduces and moulds to a certain shape by thermodynamic mechanism. During synthesis, shape control procedures might make use of kinetics, thermodynamics or an amalgam of the both41. Numerous benefits result from being able to alter the particle’s shape, including enhanced catalytic activity, plasmonic absorbance and self-assembly42,43. UV–Vis spectroscopic analysis

Figure 5 illustrates the ultraviolet–visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–vis DRS) spectra obtained for both bare and Pd doped ZnO samples. Figure 5 inset shows the band gap estimated using the Kubelka–Munk plot.

UV- Vis DRS spectra of bare & Pd-doped ZnO NPs and Kubelka–Munk plot (inset).

Bare ZnO exhibits a maximum absorption around 350–450 nm, consistent to a band gap of ~ 3.12 eV50. In contrast, the Pd doping induces a redshift in the absorption spectra of ZnO, signifying a narrowed bandgap. The calculated band gaps for the Pd-doped samples are determined as 3.01 eV (0.04 wt%), 2.95 eV (0.08 wt%), and 2.94 eV (0.2 wt%), respectively. This reduction in band gap is attributed to both the quantum confinement effect and the introduction of metal dopants in ZnO, which can induce oxygen vacancies. Doping in ZnO introduces impurity bands within the band gap arising from s-d or p-d orbital interactions between host and the dopant51,52. Dopant-induced electronic state localization lowers bandgap energy, leading to a red-shifted absorption spectrum towards the visible region53,54. Furthermore, oxygen vacancies create localized mid-gap states, facilitating photoexcitation of electrons from the VB to the CB, thereby increasing visible light absorption55. The observed redshift in the absorption wavelength range, coupled with the heightened absorption intensity, signifies a substantial increase in the rate of electron–hole pair formation on the catalyst surface, consequently enhancing the photocatalytic efficiency of the catalyst.

Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy analysis

The photoluminescence (PL) spectra of nanostructured materials provide critical insights into the electron–hole pair separation dynamics. Figure 6 illustrates the PL spectra of ZnO and Pd-doped ZnO (0.08 wt%) when excited at a wavelength of 380 nm. The bare ZnO sample exhibits prominent ultraviolet (UV) emission peaks at 421 nm and 404 nm. Additionally, two distinct bands are observed in the visible region at 499 nm and 537 nm. The UV emission peaks correspond to the recombination of free excitons in ZnO56. The visible emissions are primarily associated with deep-level defect states within ZnO57. The green emission bands, situated in the 500–550 nm range, are ascribed to transitions initiated by oxygen vacancies58.

PL analysis for bare and Pd-doped ZnO NPs.

The primary mechanism underlying this emission process involves radiative recombination between photo-generated holes and electrons in oxygen vacancy states. The introduction of palladium induces a significant alteration in the PL emission characteristics of ZnO. Specifically, Pd doping results in a pronounced decrease in the intensity of UV emission, accompanied by an increase in the intensity of visible emission. The suppression of UV emission suggests a reduced rate of radiative recombination of band-edge electron–hole pairs from the conduction band (CB) minima to the valence band (VB) maxima, facilitated by Pd doping. This phenomenon can be attributed to the migration of electrons from the CB to various electron trapping centres introduced by Pd atoms59. The visible emission peaks in ZnO are significantly altered after Pd doping, which can be attributed to an increased formation of oxygen vacancies. These vacancies also act as trapping centres for photo-generated electrons, which in turn can form either free or bound excitons. The presence of these oxygen vacancy defects results in an enhanced PL intensity60,61 in the visible region for Pd-doped ZnO. During the synthesis of these samples, oxygen defects are stochastically distributed within the growing particles, leading to their presence predominantly at the particle surfaces. Consequently, Pd doping, alongside the creation of oxygen defects, impedes the recombination of electron–hole pairs and accelerates electron transfer, ultimately augmenting the photocatalytic activity of the material62,63.

Photocatalytic assessment

The photocatalytic efficacy of the samples prepared in their pristine state was examined by subjecting them to visible light irradiation to facilitate the degradation of nitroaromatic compounds. Preliminary control trials were conducted employing nitrobenzene (200 µg/L) as a representative environmental pollutant. The experimental procedures involved the utilization of a 10 ml nitrobenzene solution, a catalyst concentration of 16 µg/ml, and the addition of 0.3 ml hydrogen peroxide, adhering to precise measurements. The degradation efficiency of the system was assessed by means of gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis, and the resulting outcomes were documented at multiple time intervals. All experimental procedures were conducted under conditions of a neutral pH. Prior to exposure to visible light, the analytes were subjected to a dark incubation period in the presence of catalyst for an initial duration of 30 min, in order to establish an equilibrium state of adsorption and desorption. The degradation efficiency was quantified using the equation presented in Eq. 1. A minimal alteration was observed in the nitrobenzene solution lacking a photocatalyst under the influence of visible light exposure and under dark conditions. Figure 7(a) depicts the time-dependent gas chromatography (GC) spectra of the nitrobenzene solution containing 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO. The GC analysis revealed a prominent peak corresponding to nitrobenzene, appearing at a retention time of 10.4 minutes64. As the duration of light exposure increased, the intensity of the GC peak consistently declined. Ultimately, after a period of 320 min, the intensity of the GC peak significantly decreased, indicating the near-complete degradation (90%) of nitrobenzene.

The degradation kinetics of the system was evaluated using a pseudo-first-order kinetics model65, represented by Eq. 3.

The reaction rate constant (k), expressed in units of min−1, governs the slope of the system under investigation. The concentrations of nitrobenzene solution at the irradiation time (Ct) and the initial time (C0) are denoted by the corresponding variables. The visible-light-induced degradation of nitrobenzene exhibited a kinetic behaviour that can be approximated by a pseudo-first-order reaction model, as evidenced by the observed linear relationship between the logarithm of the concentration ratio (log Ct/C0) and time (t). The investigation yielded optimal results in terms of degradation efficiency when utilizing 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO. A comparison of rate constants (k) for different compositions, namely bare ZnO, 0.04%, 0.08 wt%, and 0.2 wt% Pd-doped ZnO, revealed values of 3.1 × 10–4 min-1, 3.4 × 10–4 min-1, 0.0073 min-1 and 0.0019 min-1, respectively (Fig. 7(b-c)).The rate constants associated with 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO exhibited a significant 25-fold increase compared to bare ZnO. Conversely, an examination of the photocatalytic activity of ZnO doped with 0.2 wt% Pd indicated a noticeable decline in performance. This decline can be attributed to a combination of factors, including alterations in the material’s phase structure, changes in charge carrier transport, and modifications in the redox potential resulting from excessive doping66. Collectively, these factors create an unfavourable environment for efficient photocatalysis, leading to diminished overall system performance.

The degradation rate of nitrobenzene is significantly influenced by the quantity of photocatalyst employed, as evidenced through multiple experiments where the photocatalyst amount was varied under optimal conditions of pH 7 and a nitrobenzene concentration of 200 µg/L. The degradation of nitrobenzene demonstrates a positive correlation with an increase in the amount of catalyst, attributable to the augmented availability of active sites for photodegradation67. However, an excessive quantity of photocatalyst has been observed to induce substantial aggregation, which subsequently hampers the accessibility of active sites, leading to a decrease in the degradation rate68. In a meticulous analysis, it was determined that an optimal loading of 16 µg/mL of 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO yielded the highest efficiency of degradation, as depicted in Fig. 8(a, d). Additionally, it was established that the introduction of 0.3 mL of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into the system sufficed for the desired conversion process. Interestingly, any surplus of H2O2 demonstrated no detectable impact on the procedure, implying a saturation of hydroxyl (•OH) radicals69, as illustrated in Fig. 8(b, e).

Nitrobenzene remediation with (a & d) effect of catalyst concentration, (b & e) effect of H2O2 amount, (c & f) effect of nitrobenzene concentrations. (The experimental errors are below 5% in all cases with three repeating experiments.).

The present study aimed to explore the influence of the initial concentration of nitrobenzene on the efficiency of photocatalytic degradation through a comprehensive investigation spanning a spectrum of concentrations ranging from 100 to 250 µg/L (Fig. 8c, f). The outcomes unveiled a remarkable outcome, whereby the optimal rate of nitrobenzene degradation was attained at a concentration of 200 µg/L solution. However, surpassing this critical threshold led to a notable decline in the degradation rate. This decline can be primarily ascribed to the restricted availability of •OH radicals and the manifestation of competitive reactions involving intermediates70.

The effluent discharged by diverse industrial sectors manifests a wide spectrum of pH values, thereby rendering the pH parameter a pivotal determinant in photocatalytic reactions (Fig. 9a-b). Consequently, an investigation was undertaken to assess the degradation of nitrobenzene across the pH span of 3 to 9 by judiciously employing NaOH and HCl solutions in appropriate quantities. Remarkably, an intriguing observation emerged, wherein an elevation in pH value corresponded to a decline in degradation efficacy. Specifically, the degradation extent dwindled from 90 to 55% as the pH escalated from 3.0 to 9.0. This regression can be attributed to the following underlying factors. Firstly, at elevated pH levels, H2O2 undergoes spontaneous decomposition, thereby resulting in the predominant formation of molecular oxygen (O2) as the principal by-product71. Importantly, O2 exhibits diminished oxidative potential when compared to the highly reactive hydroxyl radical (•OH). Secondly, with increasing pH, the dissociation of H2O2 generates hydroperoxide anions (HO2-), which can either react with H2O2 or act as scavengers for •OH. Consequently, the reaction rate is attenuated within this pH range72.

(a-b) Nitrobenzene remediation with effect of pH, (c) pHpzc of 0.08 wt% Pd doped ZnO NPs. (d) effect of scavengers, (The experimental errors are below 5% in all cases with three repeating experiments.).

Photocatalysis occurs predominantly at the surface of nanoparticles, where the efficiency of the photocatalyst is profoundly influenced by multiple factors including solution pH, pollutant type, and the nanoparticles’ ability to adsorb pollutants73,74. Therefore, determining the point of zero charge (pHPZC) is essential for predicting the surface charge dynamics during the photodegradation process75 (Fig. 9c) . For instance, the pHPZC of Pd-doped ZnO (0.08 wt%) was quantified using the pH drift method76,77, revealing a value of 7.51. Below this pH, Pd-doped ZnO exhibits a positive charge due to the protonation of surface functional groups, whereas above this pH, the ZnO NPs surface carries a negative charge78. The removal efficiency is notably higher under acidic pH conditions and moderate under alkaline conditions, likely attributable to the strong Lewis acid behaviour of Pd-doped ZnO at low pH levels, facilitated by its positively charged surface. Consequently, nitrobenzene (NB), a strong Lewis base79, readily adsorbs onto the positively charged catalyst surface under acidic conditions.

In the supplementary investigations, a variety of scavengers were employed, encompassing MeOH, which functioned as a scavenger for hydroxyl radicals, silver nitrate, utilized for capturing electrons, KI, employed for trapping holes, and benzoquinone, serving as a scavenger for superoxide radicals (Fig. 9d)80. The experimental findings unambiguously demonstrated a notable reduction in the degradation rate across all instances, with the exception of the scenario where benzoquinone was utilized as the scavenger. This unequivocal observation firmly established the active participation of the electron (e-), hole (h+), and hydroxyl radical (•OH) species within the degradation process.

In order to ascertain the universality of the improved photocatalytic efficiency for the degradation of nitro organic compounds, we conducted additional investigations on the photocatalytic behaviour of bare ZnO and Pd-doped ZnO NPs in the context of degradation of various aromatic nitro compounds, including p-nitrophenol, p-nitroaniline, and m-dinitrobenzene. The corresponding experimental findings, as presented in Fig. 10(a-f), unequivocally demonstrate a comparably remarkable enhancement in activity, analogous to that observed in the nitrobenzene degradation process. All reactions exhibited apparent first-order kinetics, as confirmed by the linear relationship in the plot of ln(C0/C) versus time (t), depicted in the Fig. 10. Among the tested catalysts, Pd-doped ZnO nanoparticles demonstrated the highest photodegradation activity for various model nitro organic pollutants under optimal reaction conditions. Specifically, the Pd-doped ZnO NPs achieved removal efficiencies of 88%, 87%, and 91% for p-nitrophenol, p-nitroaniline, and m-dinitrobenzene, respectively.

The photocatalytic reaction entails the involvement of a multitude of dynamic species81, comprising the superoxide radical (O2•-) and hydroxyl radical (•OH), along with the photo-induced e-/h+. In order to ascertain the precise categories of liberated radicals produced throughout this reaction, the technique of electron spin resonance (ESR) was employed as a means of detection (Fig. 11). To effectively capture and identify these radicals, the spin-trap agent known as 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) was judiciously utilized82.

EPR spectra of radical (a) DMPO/O2•- (b) DMPO/•OH with various times, the effect of dopant concentration on the production of (c) DMPO/O2•- (d) DMPO/•OH adducts.

Under the conditions of illumination and in the presence of a 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO catalyst, the ESR analysis elucidated the existence of hydroxyl radicals (•OH). This was evident from the distinctive peak83 corresponding to the DMPO/•OH adduct in a ratio of 1:2:2:1, while the characteristic peaks of superoxide radicals (O2•-) were conspicuously absent. The gradual augmentation of DMPO/•OH adduct intensity with the progression of reaction time indicated the stepwise generation of hydroxyl radicals. This ESR experiment provided empirical evidence for the formation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) during the degradation process of nitrobenzene. Significantly, the 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO catalyst exhibited a higher redox potential compared to other tested materials, resulting in the generation of a higher concentration of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) during the degradation process. This observation was further substantiated by the enhanced peak intensity of DMPO/•OH adducts, specifically observed in the case of the 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO catalyst.

Based on the aforementioned findings, we can propose a mechanistic pathway for the degradation of nitrobenzene by Pd-doped ZnO (Fig. 12a). Under visible light irradiation, the valence band (VB) of ZnO is excited, causing the transfer of electrons to the conduction band (CB). Simultaneously, the excitation generates holes in the VB. However, due to their propensity for recombination, these charged carriers tend to diminish the photocatalytic activity of ZnO84. Owing to oxygen vacancy defect and Pd doping, new trapping states were generated and the photoexcited electrons are effectively trapped by the energetically favourable trapping states85, thus promoting enhanced separation of electron–hole pairs in Pd-doped ZnO. Consequently, this facilitates the efficient reduction of H2O2 by the electrons in the CB, leading to the generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and hydroxide ions (-OH)86. Furthermore, the accumulated holes in the VB effectively oxidize the hydroxide ions (-OH) to form additional hydroxyl radicals (•OH)86. Ultimately, the nitrobenzene molecules undergo oxidation through the action of photogenerated active species such as •OH, e-, and h+.

GC–MS was employed to investigate the formation of intermediate compounds during the degradation of nitrobenzene (Fig. 12b). The identification of intermediates through GC–MS analysis, coupled with the presence of a key reactive species (•OH), allowed for the proposal of a plausible degradation pathway for nitrobenzene (Fig. 12c). Initially, nitrobenzene underwent free radical hydroxylation, resulting in the production of isomeric nitrophenols. Simultaneously, the nitro group, possessing strong electron-withdrawing characteristics, displayed electrophilic behaviour and was susceptible to attack by •OH, leading to the formation of phenol. Subsequent oxidation of these phenolic compounds by •OH facilitated the formation of hydroquinone. The hydroquinone underwent dehydrogenation to yield benzoquinone. Further oxidation of these compounds via a ring-opening mechanism resulted in the generation of lower-molecular-weight species. Ultimately, these light molecules underwent mineralization into carbon dioxide and water. The findings of this study align with previous research on the degradation of nitrobenzene81. It is important to emphasize that the complete mineralization of the nitrobenzene solution was observed, as supported by the generation of GC–MS intermediates, which are considered non-hazardous in comparison to the initial compound.

Recycle assessments were performed to evaluate the reusability of a photodegradation system employing 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO and its effectiveness in degrading nitroaromatic compounds. The obtained outcomes, as illustrated in Fig. 13, convincingly reveal the outstanding reusability of product, even after undergoing five successive cycles, thereby indicating the notable stability of the catalyst. Spectroscopical investigation of both the fresh and reused photocatalysts reveals strikingly similar characteristics, implying the preservation of structure and morphology of the photocatalyst following repeated utilization. To validate the heterogeneous nature of the 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO catalyst in the reaction, the model reaction was repeated using the photocatalyst from a previous cycle, employing optimized conditions. Subsequently, the photocatalyst was separated from the reaction mixture after a reaction time of 60 min. The resulting filtrate was further stirred for an additional 12 h, and the rate of degradation was monitored at intervals of 30 min. Particularly, no further increases in the degradation rate were observed even after 12 h, indicating that the reaction was solely driven by the presence of 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO. This comprehensive experimentation effectively confirms the heterogeneous nature of the current photocatalytic system.

Conclusion

In this study, bare and Pd-doped ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) were successfully synthesized via a hydrothermal method. The results demonstrated that the synthesized nanomaterials exhibited distinct surface morphologies, with bare ZnO particles displaying nanorod-like structures, while Pd-doped ZnO particles (0.04 wt%, 0.08 wt%, and 0.2 wt%) formed irregular nanorods and spherical shapes. Notably, UV–VIS and PL analyses confirmed that Pd doping significantly suppressed the recombination of photo-induced charge carriers and effectively narrowing the optical band gap. Among the photocatalysts tested, 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO nanoparticles (16 µg/mL) demonstrated superior photocatalytic performance, achieving 90% degradation of a 200 µg/L NB solution at neutral pH under visible-light irradiation, adhering to pseudo-first-order kinetics. Radical quenching experiments, coupled with EPR studies, validated the generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) as the primary reactive species during the degradation process. Mass spectrometry further identified key intermediates, elucidating a plausible degradation pathway for NB. This method demonstrates broad applicability, efficiently extending its photocatalytic degradation capabilities to a range of aromatic nitro compounds. Additionally, reusability studies confirmed the excellent stability of the 0.08 wt% Pd-doped ZnO nanoparticles, with consistent performance observed over five successive treatment cycles, underscoring their potential for sustainable environmental remediation.
