Transforming nursing students’ attitudes toward death through the “caring for life in death” program: a quasi-experimental, longitudinal, mixed-methods study
Authors: Alexandra Neto, Félix Neto, Patrício Costa, Maria José Gomes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPalliative care education may be insufficient, resulting in gaps in healthcare students’ knowledge and emotional management skills when caring for palliative patients and their families. Training programs addressing personal and professional limitations while promoting self-awareness and growth could help close these gaps. This quasi-experimental study investigated the effects of the “Caring for Life in Death” Program on perceptions of death, communication skills, and emotional self-regulation in healthcare students.MethodsTwo hundred nine nursing students from the School of Health Sciences, Polytechnic Institute of Bragança were allocatd to a training group (n = 115) or control group (n = 94). Students in their first and second years completed a twelve-session “Caring for Life in Death” program. Data were collected through a semi-structured questionnaire and various scales, including the Abbreviated Death Fear Scale, Death Anxiety Questionnaire, and Brief Scales on Various Perspectives of Death.ResultsParticipants in the training group demonstrated significant reductions in death-related fear and anxiety, alongside improved coping and reflective abilities, underscoring the importance of enhancing palliative care training to strengthen emotional resilience.ConclusionThe findings have significant implications for palliative care education and the well-being of healthcare students.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04760-3.Keywords: Palliative care, Training, Death attitudes, Healthcare students

Although death is a universal and inevitable aspect of the human condition, transcending cultural, historical, and situational boundaries, modern Western society often approaches it with avoidance and denial. Contemporary values emphasizing individualism, competitiveness, consumerism, technological advancement, and profit orientation contribute to the marginalization of death as a topic of reflection [1, 2]. Within this sociocultural context, the psychological implications of death and dying have garnered increasing scholarly attention, particularly concerning anxiety and fear among healthcare students [3–5]. In healthcare education and practice, students and professionals are frequently confronted with patient death, which often elicits emotional distress that remains insufficiently acknowledged and inadequately addressed. Recurrent exposure to dying patients and bereaved families, coupled with limited formal education in death, dying, and bereavement, contributes to substantial psychological strain and emotional burden within this population [6–10].

Despite growing recognition of the psychological and educational challenges associated with death and dying [6, 7, 11–13], existing research indicates persistent gaps in healthcare curricula, including inconsistent integration of palliative care, limited training in emotional regulation, and insufficient opportunities for experiential learning with patient death [14–16]. Many students and early-career professionals report feeling unprepared to manage end-of-life care, experience significant emotional distress, and lack structured support to develop coping strategies, communication skills, and empathy [14, 17–19]. These limitations underscore the need for targeted interventions, such as the “Caring for Life in Death” program, to transform nursing students’ attitudes toward death and enhance their preparedness for clinical practice.

The present study

The present study investigates the impact of the Caring for Life in Death program on nursing students. This study is embedded within a broader educational intervention targeting both healthcare students and professionals, which was completed in May 2025 (see Supplementary Figures 1S and 2S). The primary research question examines the extent to which participation in the Caring for Life in Death program influences nursing students´ perceptions of death, as well as their levels of death-related anxiety and fear.

Accordingly, this study examines the impact of the Caring for Life in Death program on nursing students. The primary outcomes are changes in perceptions of death and levels of death-related fear and anxiety. Secondary outcomes include self-awareness, understanding of life context, and personal and professional development. Based on the existing literature and the stated objectives, the following hypotheses and qualitative expectation were formulated:

H1: Participation in the Caring for Life in Death program will lead to statistically significant improvements in nursing students’ perceptions of death.

H2: Participation in the Caring for Life in Death program will lead to a statistically significant reduction in death-related fear and anxiety among nursing students.

Qualitative expectation: Post-intervention reflections are expected to reveal themes related to: (a) enhanced emotional awareness regarding death and dying; (b) meaning-making processes concerning life, death, and mortality; and (c) personal and professional development, including communication competence and professional identity formation.

The quantitative hypotheses and qualitative expectations were examined using validated quantitative instruments and qualitative analytic procedures specifically selected to operationalize the constructs of interest.

Compared with existing educational interventions in end-of-life care, which often focus on discrete competencies and short-term training formats [20, 21], this program adopts a holistic and multidimensional approach. It explicitly addresses not only knowledge and skills but also students’ perceptions of death, death-related anxiety and fear, and processes of self-awareness and personal development.

In addition, the program is grounded in a reflective framework that encourages critical engagement with personal beliefs and experiences related to death, an aspect that has been underrepresented in prior interventions. It is also embedded within a broader interprofessional initiative, although the present study focuses on nursing students.

Finally, the program addresses a contextual gap: there is a lack of published studies in Portugal examining structured educational interventions that target both the emotional and developmental dimensions of end-of-life care.

Method

Participants

This study included a sample of 209 undergraduate nursing students and employed a quasi-experimental design with two groups. Participants were enrolled in the early stages of the first and second years of the undergraduate nursing programme at the School of Health Sciences, Polytechnic Institute of Bragança. This sampling strategy was adopted to ensure that participants had not received prior education or training in palliative or end-of-life care.

Inclusion criteria were: (a) enrolment in the first or second year of the undergraduate nursing programme; (b) absence of previous formal education or training in palliative or end-of-life care; and (c) provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included prior training or professional experience in palliative or end-of-life care.

Following institutional approval, the programme was advertised in classrooms, and interested students were invited to participate. Of the 214 students invited, 209 provided informed consent and were enrolled in the study. Participants were allocated to either a training group or a control group using a non-randomized procedure consistent with the quasi-experimental design. Specifically, allocation was based on pre-existing class groupings, with entire classes assigned to one of the study conditions. This approach was adopted to preserve the natural classroom structure and to minimise the risk of contamination between participants that could arise from interaction among students within the same class. The control group was assured of the opportunity to participate in the programme at a later stage. Upon completion of the study, all participants received a certificate of participation. No monetary compensation was provided.

The final sample comprised 209 undergraduate nursing students, including 94 students at the end of the first academic year and 115 students at the beginning of the second academic year. The program was delivered on-site, using facilities made available by the School of Health Sciences. The principal researcher moderated the data collection and seminars and had no teaching, evaluative, or supervisory relationship with the participating students, thereby minimising potential conflicts of interest or power imbalances. All participants responded to the European Portuguese protocol scales individually and voluntarily on paper, ensuring anonymity and confidentiality, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the institution’s ethical guidelines. The Program protocol was approved by the Faculty of Psychology and Education Sciences Ethics Committee, University of Porto (Ref. No 2018_11-02b). To ensure participant anonymity and enable accurate data linkage across study activities, each participant generated a unique personal identification code following standardized instructions. The code consisted of six alphanumeric characters, including at least one special character. Participants were instructed to memorize the code and record it in the designated field on all study documents. This procedure allowed longitudinal tracking of responses while maintaining confidentiality. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment in the study.

This study employed a convergent mixed-methods design, in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, analysed separately, and then integrated during interpretation.

Instruments

The program was evaluated at three time points: pre-training (Time 1, [T1]), post-training (Time 2, [T2]), and a three-month follow-up (Time 3, [T3]).

Four instruments were utilised:

Abbreviated Death Fear Scale by Collett-Lester (AB-CLFODS; for the Portuguese Adaptation) [22]. Was used to test Hypothesis 2. Contains 16 items divided into four subscales (Fear of Own Death, Fear of Own Dying Process, Fear of Death of Others, and Fear of the Dying Process of Others), each consisting of four items. The scale used a five-point response format ranging from 1 (none) to 5 (a lot). Each subscale score ranged from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating greater fear of death and the dying process. This instrument demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s ω values of 0.89 and 0.90, respectively. Measurement invariance was supported, with Comparative Fit Index (CFI) values consistently above 0.94, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) values below 0.065, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) values under 0.055.

Death Anxiety Questionnaire (DAQ; for the Portuguese adaptation) [23]. Consisted of 15 statements with three response options: none, a little, and a lot. These statements related to the concepts of loss, suffering, and loneliness, where higher scores (indicating “a lot”) reflected greater anxiety about death. The internal consistency was acceptable, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.83, and test–retest reliability was high (r = 0.87). Was employed to assess general death anxiety and complemented the AB‑CLFODS in evaluating changes in death‑related fear and anxiety associated with Hypothesis 2.

Brief Scales on Various Perspectives of Death (BSVPD; for the Portuguese adaptation) [24]. Was used to assess nursing students’ perceptions of death and dying, corresponding to Hypothesis 1. This scale consisted of eight subscales with responses on a Likert scale from 1 to 6, assessing perspectives on death such as suffering/loneliness, belief in an afterlife, indifference, death as the unknown, abandonment with guilt, courage in death, death as failure, and death as a natural end. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient yielded the following values for the first through eighth factors, respectively: 0.83, 0.94, 0.84, 0.86, 0.83, 0.83, 0.87, and 0.78 [24].

The Caring for Life Program Evaluation Healthcare Students Questionnaire (CLPEQ) was developed as a programme evaluation instrument to assess healthcare students’ perceptions of the impact, relevance, and adequacy of the Caring for Life in Death intervention, rather than as a standardised psychometric measure of the study’s primary outcomes. The Questionnaire was developed for the present study to assess students’ perceived impact and satisfaction with the program. Item content was informed by established literature and previously validated instruments in end-of-life and palliative care education, particularly measures assessing attitudes toward care of the dying, end-of-life educational needs, communication, and emotional aspects of care [25–28]. To support content validity, the instrument was reviewed by a panel of experts in palliative care, nursing education, and psychology, who evaluated the items for clarity, relevance, and alignment with the intended domains. Minor revisions were implemented based on their feedback.

Responses are recorded on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater perceived impact, relevance, adequacy, or satisfaction. No reverse-scored items were included in the instrument.

An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted to examine the instrument’s underlying structure. The results supported a two-factor solution: program relevance and training adequacy. The program relevance factor comprised nine items with factor loadings ranging from 0.31 to 0.71, indicating acceptable to strong associations with the latent construct (see Table 4). The highest loading items reflected the development of communication skills and emotional self-regulation (λ = 0.71) and increased awareness of beliefs and emotions related to death and dying (λ = 0.69), whereas lower loadings were observed for perceived future professional impact (λ = 0.31). The training adequacy factor included four items with stronger and more consistent loadings, ranging from 0.57 to 0.71, supporting the coherence of this dimension.

Consistent with its evaluative purpose, questionnaire data were analysed using descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations), and findings were interpreted as indicators of participants’ perceived experiences, perceived impact, and satisfaction with the programme.

The internal consistency reliability of the AB-CLFODS, BSVPD, and DAQ scales was evaluated for both the healthcare student training group (TGp) and the control group (CGp). The AB-CLFODS (16 items), BSVPD, and DAS scales showed moderate to excellent reliability across both groups. Specifically, the AB-CLFODS demonstrated α = .82 and ω = .84 for the TGp, and α = .89 and ω = .89 for the CGp. For the BSVPD, reliability coefficients were α = .86 and ω = .86 in the TGp, and α = .84 and ω = .85 in the CGp. The DAS scale showed α = .76 and ω = .77 in the TGp, and α = .84 and ω = .85 in the CGp (see Supplementary Table 1S).

These findings support the robustness of the global scales while highlighting potential refinements in the subscales, particularly within the training group [29, 30] (see Supplementary Table 1S).

The reliability of the CLPEQ was examined in the healthcare student training group (TGp), yielding good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha (α) and McDonald’s omega (ω) both equal to 0.85. At the subscale level, the Program Relevance dimension demonstrated satisfactory reliability (α = 0.81; ω = 0.81), while the Training Adequacy dimension also showed acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.75; ω = 0.76) [31, 32].

Additionally, qualitative data were collected through structured, written, reflective, coded activities completed as part of the study protocol.

Procedure

The present study incorporates the second and third stages of a broader research project (see Supplementary Figure 2S). The first moment occurred between 2017 and 2018, with a follow-up in 2024 to replicate the construct validity obtained in 2018 [22, 33]. The second occurred between September 2024 and April 2025, while the third occurred three months after the program’s implementation. Participants in the control group were assured the opportunity to participate in the program at a later stage; however, this has not yet been implemented because the students in the control group are currently unavailable due to their internship schedules.

“Caring for life in death” program (A program for promoting and developing communication and emotional self-regulation skills in end-of-life care)

The program took place from late September 2024 to late July 2025. This study is part of a broader educational intervention involving nursing students and healthcare professionals (studies 2 and 3). The program aimed to link theoretical knowledge with practical skills through 12 seminars that included literary reflections, clinical case discussions, short videos, and simulations focused on communication and emotional regulation strategies. The program emphasised the importance of understanding core communication principles, engaging in reflective practice regarding communication processes, and acknowledging the psychological dimensions of the dying experience. Furthermore, it addressed the stages of grief and incorporated experiential learning opportunities to practice essential communication skills to improve care delivery [34] (see supplementary Figures 1S and 2S). The design aimed to boost participant engagement and enhance learning outcomes through a simulation-based approach, including role-playing activities.

The seminars were delivered by the principal author, who holds formal qualifications in clinical psychology, professional experience in palliative care, and clinical nursing experience. This combination of expertise ensured that the programme content was both theoretically grounded and clinically relevant. During implementation, the principal author facilitated all seminar sessions, while co-authors assisted with observational notes, participant guidance, and data collection. Expert consultation was also sought during programme development to ensure the content was valid, pedagogically sound, and aligned with best practices in palliative care education.

The Caring for Life in Death program commenced with two introductory sessions, twelve structured seminars, and a final follow-up session, establishing a cohesive framework to maximise its educational impact (see Supplementary Table 2S). The introductory sessions marked the pre-test phase and contextualised the subsequent seminars, which focused on core themes in palliative care (see Supplementary Table 3S). Upon completion of the seminar series, an evaluation session was conducted as part of the post-test phase. A follow-up session was held three months later to reassess participants’ progress and retention of knowledge and skills.

Each seminar was carefully designed to address a wide range of critical topics essential for the understanding and practice of palliative care. Seminars one and two introduced foundational concepts, including communication principles, the six core tenets and assumptions of palliative care, and the primary challenges and accomplishments within the field. These sessions integrated literary reflections and clinical case discussions to promote deeper comprehension.

Seminars three and four focused on emotional literacy in the context of life and death, alongside techniques for emotional regulation. Instructional strategies included the use of short, illustrative videos and simulated exercises to strengthen participants’ emotional awareness and regulation skills.

Seminars five and six emphasised the development of empathetic relationships and therapeutic alliances, underscoring their importance in palliative care practice.

Seminars seven and eight examined the psychological dimensions of life and death, exploring both personal and interpersonal perceptions, psychological responses to death, and processes leading to acceptance. These sessions engaged students with literary works, visual media, and case-based discussions to foster critical reflection.

Seminars nine and ten addressed the ethical dimensions of care, emphasising the interconnection between dignity, ethical decision-making, and human connection, through reflective activities grounded in literary texts and clinical scenarios.

The final sessions, seminars eleven and twelve, focused on the complex task of delivering bad news. These sessions employed simulation-based techniques to enhance communication competencies, followed by structured discussions and reflective exercises to consolidate learning.

Taken together, the Caring for Life in Death program provided a comprehensive, practice-oriented curriculum designed to equip students with the theoretical foundations and practical competencies necessary for delivering compassionate and effective palliative care. A follow-up session, conducted three months after the intervention, was designed to replicate the evaluation procedures.

Data analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were conducted to examine the data. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics, with categorical variables reported as absolute (N) and relative (%) frequencies, and continuous variables presented as means and standard deviations (SD). Prior to conducting parametric analyses, the assumption of normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test [35]. This test was selected due to its robustness and sensitivity in detecting deviations from a normal distribution, particularly in small to moderate sample sizes. A p-value less than 0.05 was interpreted as a significant departure from normality. The internal consistency reliability of the AB-CLFODS, BSVPD, and DAQ scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α) and McDonald’s omega (ω) for both the healthcare student training group (TGp) and the control group (CGp). The normality tests for the AB-CLFODS, BSVPD, and DAQ scales yielded statistically significant results for most items, suggesting violations of the univariate normality assumption. However, descriptive analyses showed skewness values ranging from −2 to 2 and kurtosis values between −8 and 8 for these instruments, indicating approximate normality [36, 37].

Inferential analyses included a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess the effect of group membership (training vs. control) on participants’ age. A mixed factorial ANOVA was employed to evaluate within-subject changes across three time points (pre-test, post-test, and follow-up) and between-group differences. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were tested prior to analysis. When the assumption of homogeneity of variances was violated, Welch’s ANOVA was applied. In the case of significant main effects or interactions, post hoc comparisons were conducted using either Tukey’s HSD or the Games–Howell procedure, depending on the results of Levene’s test for equality of variances [38, 39].

For the mixed factorial ANOVA, the assumption of sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test. When sphericity was violated, degrees of freedom were adjusted using the Greenhouse–Geisser correction [39–41].

Significant interaction effects were further explored through simple effects analyses to clarify the nature and direction of the differences across time points and groups. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta-squared (η2 p) to assess the magnitude of group effects. The interpretation of η2 p values followed the conventional thresholds: small effect (η2 p ≈ 0.01), medium effect (η2 p ≈ 0.06), and large effect (η2 p ≥ 0.14) [42, 43]. Confidence coefficients were calculated at the 95% confidence level for all statistical estimates. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All descriptive and inferential analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 30), and McDonald’s ω was calculated using JAMOVI (Version 2.3.28).

The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1 (version 3.1.4.9), to detect the group × time interaction in a design with two groups and three measurement points (repeated measures ANOVA, within–between interaction). The analysis assumed α = .05, power = .80, a correlation among repeated measures of ρ = .50, and ε = 1. For a medium effect size (f = 0.25), the required sample size was approximately 28 participants (≈14 per group). A thematic content analysis was carried out by the authors following the procedures described by Braun and Clarke [44]. The analysis followed a systematic, iterative process involving data familiarisation, initial coding, theme development and refinement, and the interpretation of findings in relation to the study objectives (coding framework encompassing emotional awareness, meaning-making regarding life and death, communication competence, and professional identity development). To enhance analytical rigour, two researchers independently coded the data, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Themes were generated inductively, allowing patterns to emerge from participants’ narratives rather than being imposed a priori. Given the integrative and reflective nature of the narratives, the analysis yielded overarching themes that captured the core patterns of meaning across participants’ accounts, without further differentiation into subthemes. This approach enabled a nuanced understanding of participants’ experiences and of how they engaged with and integrated the programme’s reflective and emotional learning components. These reflective narratives were collected post-intervention and analysed thematically to explore the study’s qualitative expectation regarding students’ emotional awareness, meaning-making processes, and personal and professional development.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The participants’ ages ranged from 17 to 51 years (N = 209). In the training group (n = 115), the mean age was 21.30 years (SD = 4.43). In the control group (n = 94), the mean age was slightly lower at 20.57 years (SD = 5.72). The overall mean age for the total sample was 20.97 years (SD = 5.05), 95% CI [20.28, 21.66].

While both groups were predominantly female overall, gender distribution within each group was nearly balanced. The two groups were comparable on several demographic variables, with no significant differences observed (see Table 1). Most participants were unmarried (TGp = 93.9%, CGp = 95.7%) and identified as believers (TGp = 82.6%, CGp = 84%), actively engaging in religious practices. Additionally, most healthcare students had not made arrangements for their death. Additional sociodemographic details are presented in Table 1.

Fear of death and dying process

At baseline (T1), both the TGp and CGp exhibited moderate to high levels of fear related to death and dying (TGp: M = 56.2, SD = 9.49; CGp: M = 57.3, SD = 12.7). Within the TGp, the highest mean scores were observed on the Fear of dying of others subscale (M = 15.1, SD = 2.89), followed by Fear of dying of self (M = 14.9, SD = 2.84), Fear of death of others (M = 14.3, SD = 3.50), and Fear of death of self (M = 11.9, SD = 3.75; see Supplementary Table S4). Similarly, the CGp reported the highest levels for Fear of dying of others (M = 15.5, SD = 3.68), followed by Fear of death of others (M = 15.2, SD = 3.46), Fear of dying of self (M = 14.9, SD = 4.05), and Fear of death of self (M = 11.7, SD = 4.78; see Supplementary Table 4S).

Post-training assessments (T2) indicated a reduction in overall fear of death and dying in both groups (TGp: M = 51.2, SD = 11.5; CGp: M = 55.6, SD = 12.3; see Supplementary Table S5). At follow-up (T3), the TGp continued to show a further decrease (M = 50.1, SD = 11.6), while the CGp exhibited a slight increase (M = 56.2, SD = 12.8; see Supplementary Table 6S).

Analyses of the AB-CLFODS subdimensions (fear of death and dying of self and others) revealed statistically significant differences over time (p < 0.05).

Perspectives on death

In T1 both the TGp and CGp reported moderate to high scores on the “Death as the Unknown” dimension (TGp: M = 28.2, SD = 5.30; CGp: M = 29.1, SD = 6.34), as well as on the “Death as a Natural End” dimension (TGp: M = 21.6, SD = 2.24; CGp: M = 21.2, SD = 2.96; see Supplementary Table S5). At post-training (T2), the TGp showed a decrease in perceptions of “Death as the Unknown” (M = 26.9, SD = 5.43), followed by an increase at follow-up (T3: M = 27.2, SD = 7.71). In contrast, perceptions of “Death as a Natural End” in the TGp increased slightly at T2 (M = 22.5, SD = 2.00) and remained slightly decreased at T3 (M = 22.1, SD = 2.13).

Overall, analyses of the BSVPD subscales (Suffering/Loneliness and Death as a natural end) revealed statistically significant differences across time points (p < 0.05; see Table 2), indicating a reduction in perceptions of suffering and loneliness, while fostering a more natural acceptance of death over time. In contrast, the control group demonstrated stable or less adaptive trajectories. In the training group, perceptions of death as failure and abandonment with guilt decreased across measurements, whereas courage in facing death showed an increase. These patterns were not observed in the control group.

Anxiety in the face of death

Pre-training results indicated that both the TGp and CGp exhibited moderate-to-high levels of death anxiety (TGp: M = 33.3, SD = 4.88; CGp: M = 33.6, SD = 5.97). The highest levels of anxiety were observed in the subdimensions of death as loss and death as suffering: TGp - Loss (M = 17.3, SD = 3.21), Suffering (M = 8.8, SD = 1.71); CGp - Loss (M = 16.8, SD = 4.91), Suffering (M = 9.4, SD = 1.72; see Supplementary Table 4S).

In follow-up, a slight reduction in overall death anxiety levels was noted (TGp: M = 31.1, SD = 5.22; CGp: M = 33.2, SD = 6.19; see Supplementary Table 6S).

Analysis of the DAQ total revealed significant results (p < 0.05), indicating that TGp showed a significant reduction in anxiety levels compared to the CGp (see Table 2).

Although the observed effect sizes were in the small-to-moderate range (η2 ₚ = 0.021–0.098), such magnitudes may still be considered educationally meaningful in interventions targeting complex psychosocial constructs (such as fear of death), where change is typically gradual and influenced by multiple contextual factors [42].

Emotional development

Participants reported that the program made a significant contribution to their emotional literacy, enhancing their ability to identify, understand, and regulate their own emotions. Reflections revealed increased awareness of the emotional labour inherent in palliative care, as well as a heightened capacity for empathy and compassion. Students reported feeling more prepared to engage with complex emotions such as grief, loss, and sadness - both their own and that of others. This domain highlights the emotional resilience cultivated through structured reflection and experiential learning, emphasizing the importance of emotional competence in healthcare contexts (see Table 3).

Communicating with people at the end of life

Effective communication emerged as a central concern, particularly in contexts involving end-of-life care. Students identified verbal, non-verbal, and paraverbal communication as key skills necessary for delivering difficult news and providing emotional support. The importance of active listening, silence, and presence was also underscored, illustrating a sophisticated understanding of therapeutic communication. This theme reflects the recognition that communication in palliative care is not merely about conveying information but about fostering connection, empathy, and trust in moments of profound vulnerability (see Table 3).

Impact on identity and professional attitude

The reflective process helped solidify students’ professional identity, particularly in relation to the humanistic values inherent in nursing. Participants emphasised the significance of being present, empathetic, and ethically grounded in care delivery, especially when technological interventions no longer serve therapeutic purposes. Many described a shift from task-oriented thinking to a more holistic, person-centred approach. This theme emphasises the development of a professional posture that prioritises dignity, compassion, and moral responsibility in caring for individuals at the end of life (see Table 3).

Reflection on the role of the student and future professional vocation

The program prompted students to reflect deeply on their roles as future healthcare professionals and the vocational nature of nursing. Many described a sense of confirmation regarding their career choice and expressed increased readiness to face the emotional demands of clinical practice. The program served as a preparatory platform, equipping them with both technical and emotional tools to engage with dying patients. This theme reflects the internalization of professional values and a commitment to providing ethical, compassionate care in real-world clinical settings (see Table 3).

Humanising care

A recurring theme in the narratives was the emphasis on humanising care, where the patient is seen not merely as a clinical case but as a whole person with history, emotions, and dignity. Students emphasised the importance of presence, listening, and respecting the patient’s individuality, particularly when curative measures are no longer viable. This theme reinforces the idea that relational and affective dimensions of care are essential, especially in palliative settings, and that these aspects must coexist with technical proficiency to achieve truly holistic care.

Personal growth and spirituality

Finally, many students described personal growth that extended beyond their academic or professional development. The program encouraged introspection, fostering a deeper sense of meaning and purpose in both personal and professional life. Spirituality, broadly defined as connection, transcendence, and meaning-making, emerged as a critical element in how students made sense of death and suffering. This theme suggests that encounters with mortality can serve as catalysts for personal transformation, offering students a more integrated understanding of life, death, and the human experience (see Table 3).

All students reported that the program had a positive influence on their perspectives on life and death. Furthermore, healthcare students reported beneficial effects on their anticipated professional development, including improved understanding of emotional experiences and emotion regulation strategies, as well as deeper reflection on communication challenges in palliative care. These qualitative findings were corroborated by the responses obtained from the program evaluation questionnaire. Mean scores ranged from 4.25 to 4.71, with median values of 5, indicating highly positive evaluations.

The program also increased participants’ awareness of their personal beliefs, emotions, and emotional reactions concerning death and dying.

Descriptive statistics indicated high mean scores across both factors, with program relevance (M = 4.56, SD = .35) and training adequacy (M = 4.55, SD = .42), suggesting a highly positive evaluation of the program. At the item level, mean scores ranged from 4.25 to 4.71 for program relevance and from 4.47 to 4.62 for training adequacy, with relatively low standard deviations, indicating limited variability in responses. The highest-rated items included its anticipated positive effect on future professional life (M = 4.68, SD = .47), its contribution to reflection on communication challenges in palliative care (M = 4.65, SD = .50), and its enhancement of knowledge and skills for emotional management (M = 4.63, SD = .54). Participants also endorsed increased awareness of their emotions to facilitate better emotional regulation and communication in end-of-life care (M = 4.57, SD = 0.58) and reported moderate to high agreement regarding its influence on perceptions of life and death processes (M = 4.42, SD = .64) and awareness of beliefs and emotions related to death (M = 4.25, SD = .67).

Satisfaction with the program was similarly high, particularly for alignment of content with real-world palliative care issues (M = 4.71, SD = .48), its contribution to communication skills and emotional self-regulation (M = 4.64, SD = .54), and the overall methodology (M = 4.62, SD = .56). Ratings for program objectives (M = 4.50, SD = .52), development of critical thinking and emotional self-awareness (M = 4.50, SD = .57), and workload appropriateness (M = 4.47, SD = .63) were also consistently high. Overall, these results indicate that participants perceived the program as impactful, relevant, and well-designed.

Overall, the program appears to contribute meaningfully to the development of emotional regulation and communication skills essential for end-of-life care (see Table 4).

Discussion and conclusions

This study examines the outcomes of a training program designed to evaluate the impact of the “Caring for Life in Death” Program on perceptions of death, improving communication skills, and emotional self-regulation in healthcare students within the context of end-of-life care.

We hypothesised that the program would positively influence participants’ perceptions of death and dying, reduce associated fear and anxiety, and enhance self-awareness, understanding of life context, and both personal and professional development.

Quantitative findings were consistent with Hypotheses 1 and 2, indicating reductions in fear of death and anxiety related to the dying process, as well as shifts toward more constructive perspectives on death among participants in the training group. Specifically, participants reported lower perceptions of death as suffering, failure, and abandonment, alongside greater acceptance of death as a natural process and increased perceived courage in confronting end‑of‑life issues. These patterns were observed across demographic groups, suggesting that the observed changes were not substantially moderated by participant characteristics. These findings were consistent with Chu et al. [17] study, which reported that the intervention group scored significantly higher in “Approach Acceptance of Death” than the control group following training.

The qualitative findings further contextualize these results by illuminating potential mechanisms underlying these changes. Participants described enhanced emotional literacy, improved emotional regulation, and greater resilience when engaging with themes of death, dying, and bereavement. In particular, communication in end‑of‑life care emerged as a central area of learning, with students reporting a shift from a predominantly technical orientation toward a more relational and person-centered approach characterized by presence, empathy, and compassionate communication. The qualitative findings can be interpreted within established theoretical frameworks. Emotional development aligns with models of emotional intelligence [48], while the prominence of reflection in professional identity formation is consistent with experiential learning theory [49]. Themes related to communication and humanizing care reflect person-centered care principles [50], and the reported personal growth and meaning-making processes are in line with meaning-centered and existential approaches [51].

Together, these perspectives suggest that the program fostered emotional competence, reflective capacity, and meaning-making, which may explain the observed improvements in attitudes toward end-of-life care.

The qualitative findings were consistent with the study’s expectations, suggesting that participation in the Caring for Life in Death program was associated with positive changes in participants’ emotional awareness, reflective capacity, and approach to end‑of‑life communication. Participants’ narratives indicated greater emotional regulation and a shift toward more relational and person‑centred care. Importantly, the qualitative findings provide contextual insight into the quantitative changes, illustrating possible processes through which the intervention may have contributed to participants’ outcomes. For instance, the observed reductions in fear of death and dying and in death-related anxiety among the training group are consistent with participants’ reports of enhanced emotional literacy, improved emotion regulation, and increased preparedness for grief, loss, and end-of-life situations. Similarly, the quantitative decrease in perceptions of suffering and loneliness, alongside increased acceptance of death as a natural process, is reflected in qualitative themes emphasising the humanisation of care, the development of empathy, and a shift toward more holistic, person-centred perspectives.

Furthermore, increases in courage in facing death and reductions in maladaptive perceptions, such as abandonment with guilt, align with participants’ reflections on professional identity formation, ethical commitment, and strengthened communication skills in palliative care contexts. These convergent findings suggest that the intervention was associated not only with measurable changes in death-related attitudes but also fostered deeper emotional and professional development, as evidenced through participants’ reflective narratives.

Together, the integration of quantitative and qualitative findings supports an explanatory interpretation of the program’s impact, complementing statistically significant changes with participants’ subjective experiences and providing a more comprehensive understanding of the possible mechanisms underlying the observed effects.

Given the quasi‑experimental design, these quantitative and qualitative findings should be interpreted cautiously and are best understood as indicative of associations rather than definitive causal effects.

Statistically significant differences were identified between the control group and participants who completed the Caring for Life in Death program. The intervention produced statistically significant improvements over time, particularly in the TGp, which showed greater reductions in AB-CLFODS, MMP, and MPMO scores compared to the CGp. These improvements were most pronounced from the first to the third time point, suggesting sustained benefits. Interaction effects across multiple subscales indicated that the TGp responded differently over time than CGp. While both groups began with similar baseline scores, group differences became more evident as the study progressed. No meaningful change was observed in the control group across time, highlighting the intervention’s impact on psychological outcomes. These results align with existing evidence indicating that palliative care training can substantially reduce fear and anxiety related to death and dying and promote effective thinking about death and their coping skills [17, 52–56].

Participation in the intervention was associated with a reduction in death anxiety over time, with participants reporting lower anxiety levels following the programme and at follow‑up compared with baseline. Notably, this pattern was observed in TGp, which showed a decrease in death anxiety after the intervention, whereas the CGp exhibited relatively stable levels across assessment points. Overall, participants who did not receive the intervention consistently reported higher levels of death anxiety. Although causal inferences cannot be drawn, these findings are consistent with a potential role of the intervention in reducing death anxiety. Moreover, the results align with previous research indicating that palliative care education and training programmes are associated with reductions in fear and anxiety related to death and dying [17, 52, 55, 56].

Quantitative and qualitative responses from the program evaluation questionnaire indicated high levels of agreement and satisfaction among healthcare students. Participants emphasised the program’s effectiveness in deepening their understanding of life and death, improving emotional regulation, and strengthening communication skills within palliative care settings. These results were similar to those of other studies on educational programs in this field [56–60]. The program was particularly appreciated for its relevance to real-world clinical challenges and its contribution to participants’ emotional and professional growth. These findings are consistent with those of Romão et al. [61], who conducted a scoping review of 45 studies published between 2014 and 2024. Their review mapped the global landscape of death education interventions, analysing various educational strategies and various delivery methods, including online, in-person, and hybrid formats. The review concluded that death education programs effectively enhanced end-of-life communication, increased emotional resilience, and reduced death-related anxiety and avoidance. While the outcomes were largely positive, the authors emphasised the need for further research and standardisation to optimise program implementation and impact.

Additionally, students recommended that palliative care training should be mandatory for all undergraduate students [58]. Salmani et al. [57] reported that a palliative care training intervention, consisting of ten theoretical and three practical sessions, significantly improved students’ perceived self-efficacy, psychosocial support, and symptom management.

Research indicates that longer programs emphasising communication skills and self-care domains lead to greater improvements [21] and highlight end-of-life simulation as a particularly effective pedagogical approach, serving as a powerful strategy for teaching and learning in death education [62].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings, particularly regarding causal inference. First, the intervention’s duration was constrained by logistical constraints related to participant access, which may have limited both the depth and durability of the observed effects. Future research should explore interventions of varying lengths to determine the optimal timeframe for achieving robust and sustained outcomes in palliative care education [63]. Second, the exclusive reliance on self-report measures may have restricted the assessment of implicit or unconscious attitudes toward death, particularly those that are avoided, suppressed, or denied [64]. Social desirability bias may also represent a significant limitation, as participants may have responded in ways they perceived as more acceptable or appropriate, rather than reflecting their genuine attitudes or emotions [65]. Incorporating behavioral, observational, or physiological measures in future studies could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of emotional and attitudinal changes [66]. Future research would benefit from examining the impact of interventions of varying durations to identify the optimal timeframe for achieving robust, sustained outcomes in palliative care education.

The study sample was relatively small and drawn from a single academic institution, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the use of non-random sampling procedures may have introduced selection bias and reduced external validity [63, 66]. However, this approach enhanced ecological validity and reduced the likelihood of intervention contamination. Participants’ awareness of their involvement in the study may also have influenced their responses, further confounding effects. To strengthen external validity, future research should employ larger, more diverse, and multicentre samples, alongside randomized designs, to ensure that findings are representative and generalizable across different educational and clinical contexts [63, 66]. Nevertheless, the use of a convenience sample is common and often unavoidable in educational and healthcare interventions due to practical and ethical constraints, including class composition and curricular requirements [67]. Participants’ awareness of their involvement in the study (i.e., Hawthorne effect) may also have influenced their responses, potentially confounding intervention effects. Although participants in the CGp were assured access to the program at a later stage, this has not yet been implemented due to their current unavailability related to internship commitments. Future research could address these limitations by including larger, more diverse samples, involving faculty members or practicing nurses, comparing different pedagogical models, and evaluating the program’s impact in real clinical settings. Such studies would provide more generalizable evidence and help determine the intervention’s practical effectiveness.

Taken together, these limitations suggest that the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and indicative rather than definitive evidence of the intervention’s effectiveness. Future studies employing larger, more diverse, and multicentre samples, randomized designs, and validated multimethod assessment strategies will be essential to strengthen causal inference and enhance the generalizability of results across educational and clinical contexts.

In conclusion, despite these constraints, the findings provide valuable insights for both educational practice and future research. As human services professionals transition into the workforce, they will increasingly encounter end-of-life challenges within settings such as hospitals, hospices, assisted living facilities, and long-term care environments. These professionals must cultivate not only a foundational knowledge of the dying process but also emotional preparedness, including the ability to manage their own responses to death and dying. Enhancing emotional self-awareness is critical to providing effective, empathetic, and ethically sound palliative care.

Overall, the findings indicate that the program positively influenced students’ perspectives on life and death while supporting their professional development. It improved emotional awareness and regulation, encouraged reflection on communication challenges in palliative care, and increased recognition of personal beliefs and emotions related to death and dying. These outcomes underscore the program’s educational and clinical value. The study further suggests that death education can effectively reduce fear and anxiety associated with caring for dying individuals, with important implications for clinical practice. By decreasing death-related distress among human service providers, such training may enhance their capacity to deliver compassionate, high-quality care to patients and their families at the end of life [17, 21, 49, 51].

This study underscores the importance of integrating emotional competency into death education curricula. Seminars that address life and death issues can foster a sense of shared experience, promote peer support, and equip students with strategies for emotional regulation. These educational experiences may reduce fear and anxiety related to death, thereby improving students’ readiness to engage with palliative care patients and their families. In terms of implementation, such programs could be integrated into undergraduate curricula as modular components combining short theoretical lectures with facilitated reflective seminars, case-based learning, and guided group discussions. Repeated exposure across the curriculum, rather than a single standalone intervention, may enhance impact. The involvement of trained facilitators is recommended to support emotional processing and ensure a safe, reflective environment for students. The results have practical implications for improving healthcare education and student well-being. Hopefully, this work will inform the development and implementation of similar programs in healthcare and educational settings.
