Mitigating diffusion-length limitations in back-contact CZTSSe solar cells via synergistic field-effect engineering: a two-dimensional TCAD simulation study
Authors: Chao Chen, Shufeng Zhuo, Jingyi Zheng, Kaihong Chen, Xiong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interdigitated back-contact (IBC) architectures can eliminate front-surface parasitic absorption, yet their application to kesterite Cu2ZnSn(S,Se)4 (CZTSSe) absorbers is strongly constrained by the mismatch between the minority-carrier diffusion length typically on the order of 1 µm and industrially relevant electrode pitches of 15–25 µm. Here, we use two-dimensional TCAD simulations, calibrated against a certified 14.9%-efficiency front-contact CZTSSe device, to numerically investigate a synergistic field-effect engineering strategy for quasi-interdigitated back-contact (QIBC) CZTSSe solar cells. Negative fixed charges of −3 × 1012 cm−2 at the front Al2O3/CZTSSe interface induce a hole accumulation layer for lateral majority-carrier transport. Highly asymmetric electrode geometry with 95% n-electrode coverage minimizes minority-electron diffusion distances. Full-thickness bandgap grading from 1.45 to 1.11 eV generates a quasi-electric field of approximately 2.4 kV cm−1 that drives vertical electron drift. The simulated optimized quasi-IBC device achieves a power conversion efficiency of 15.75% with short-circuit current density of 38.48 mA cm−2, open-circuit voltage of 606 mV, and fill factor of 67.5%, surpassing the calibrated front-contact reference of 14.9%. Sensitivity analysis establishes threshold material parameters (bulk defect density below 1014 cm−3 and electron mobility above 40 cm2 V−1 s−1) that are within reach of current fabrication technology. These simulation results suggest that synergistic field-effect engineering offers a promising design route toward back-contact kesterite solar cells.

Introduction

The development of low-cost, high-efficiency, and environmentally benign photovoltaic technologies has become increasingly urgent in the context of the global energy transition. Among emerging thin-film absorbers, copper zinc tin sulfide-selenide (CZTSSe, Cu2ZnSn(S,Se)4) has attracted sustained attention because of its earth-abundant and non-toxic constituents, tunable bandgap (1.0–1.5 eV), and high absorption coefficient exceeding 104 cm−1.1–3 These attributes make CZTSSe an attractive alternative to CdTe and CIGS technologies, which are constrained by toxicity or elemental scarcity. Despite these advantages, the certified efficiency of CZTSSe solar cells has stagnated near 14–15%, far below the Shockley–Queisser (SQ) limit.4,5 This deficit is dominated by a large open-circuit voltage loss (>400 mV), which is commonly attributed to the high density of intrinsic defects, particularly CuZn and ZnSn antisite pairs, that act as non-radiative recombination centers.3,6–8 Consequently, the minority-carrier diffusion length (Le) in most CZTSSe absorbers remains on the order of 1 µm or below.9,10 A recent study reported a 14.9% device efficiency by introducing additional vacancy defects at the CZTSSe surface via magnesium doping, thereby reducing the impact of CuZn defects and decreasing charge loss in the device.5

Even with continued advances in absorber quality, the conventional front/back-contact (FBC) architecture imposes intrinsic optical and electrical penalties. The front transparent conductive stack (i-ZnO/ITO) absorbs and reflects a fraction of incident photons, particularly near the band edge, while the CdS/CZTSSe heterointerface introduces defect-assisted recombination.11–14 Together, these losses impose an efficiency ceiling that is difficult to overcome within the FBC framework. To address these architectural limitations, the interdigitated back-contact (IBC) architecture offers an attractive alternative. By relocating both the electron- and hole-selective contacts to the rear side of the absorber, the IBC design eliminates parasitic absorption and reflection associated with front-surface transport layers and transparent electrodes.15–19

Owing to these advantages, back-contact designs have defined the efficiency frontier of crystalline-silicon photovoltaics, with hybrid IBC silicon cells reported at 27.81% efficiency, representing the state of the art for single-junction c-Si devices.20 Beyond silicon, the IBC concept has also begun to extend to perovskite photovoltaics, where experimental all-back-contact devices have exceeded 10% efficiency and recent groove-based flexible back-contact modules fabricated by scalable roll-to-roll slot-die coating have achieved stabilized efficiencies up to 12.8%.19,21,22 By contrast, its application to chalcopyrite thin-film absorbers remains at an early stage. For CIGS, Rezaei et al. reported an opto-electrical modeling study in which an ultrathin IBC architecture, assisted by front-side Al2O3-induced field-effect passivation, was predicted to increase the efficiency from 11.9% for an FBC reference to 17% after geometry and bandgap optimization.15

The practical realization of IBC architectures, however, depends critically on absorber-specific carrier-transport properties. For CZTSSe, translating IBC architectures faces a fundamental challenge: the severe mismatch between the minority-carrier diffusion length (Le, typically on the order of 1 µm) and the electrode pitch achievable by low-cost patterning methods (15–25 µm). When the electrode pitch greatly exceeds Le, photogenerated carriers must travel lateral distances far beyond their effective diffusion length before reaching the collecting junction, resulting in extensive electrically inactive regions and severe recombination losses. This geometric constraint constitutes the primary obstacle to realizing efficient CZTSSe back-contact solar cells.

In this work, we numerically investigate a strategy to mitigate this diffusion-length mismatch through synergistic field-effect engineering, rather than claiming an experimentally realized QIBC device. Specifically, negative fixed charges at the front Al2O3/CZTSSe interface (Qf = −3 × 1012 cm−2) are introduced to induce a lateral hole-accumulation layer, while full-thickness bandgap grading (ΔEg = 0.34 eV) is employed to generate a quasi-electric field that assists vertical electron collection. Within the calibrated two-dimensional TCAD framework, the simulated optimized 20 µm-pitch quasi-IBC (QIBC) device reaches a PCE of 15.75%, compared with 14.9% for the calibrated front-contact reference.

Simulation method

Silvaco technology computer-aided design (TCAD) was employed to investigate the CZTSSe QIBC solar cells. The two-dimensional (2D) framework is essential for back-contact architectures, as the lateral carrier transport across electrode periods cannot be captured by one-dimensional models. The simulation comprises two coupled steps: optical modeling to calculate the photogeneration profile, followed by electrical simulation to evaluate device performance.

In the optical step, the Transfer Matrix Method (TMM) is used to compute the wavelength-resolved generation rate under AM1.5G illumination (100 mW cm−2, 300–1500 nm). Because the electrode patterning is at the rear and the front surface is unpatterned, the one-dimensional TMM generation profile is a reasonable first-order approximation for the illuminated side; local optical effects near the rear contacts are expected to be secondary. For the bandgap-graded absorber (Section 3.4), the optical constants of the Se-rich endpoint composition are used throughout; the resulting generation profile therefore does not capture the composition-dependent absorption near the S-rich front surface, which may lead to a slight overestimation of short-wavelength absorption. The complex refractive indices for CZTSSe follow Choi et al., while optical constants for other layers are taken from the literature.23–25 In the electrical step, the Silvaco Atlas module self-consistently solves the Poisson equation coupled with the drift-diffusion and continuity equations. Carrier recombination is modeled via radiative, Auger, and Shockley–Read–Hall mechanisms, with interface recombination characterized by surface recombination velocities. For bandgap-graded regions, the quasi-electric field is incorporated via the energy-shift model.

To ensure predictive reliability for the novel QIBC architecture, we first calibrated the model against the certified 14.9%-efficiency CZTSSe device reported by Wang et al., which employed a glass/Mo/MoSe2/CZTSSe/CdS/i-ZnO/ITO structure (inset of Fig. 1a).5 Three key parameters were iteratively adjusted to fit the experimental data: acceptor concentration (NA), bulk defect density (Nt), and interface defect density. As shown in Fig. 1, the calibrated model reproduces both the J–V curve (Fig. 1a) and EQE spectrum (Fig. 1b) with excellent agreement. It should be noted that the reference FBC device achieves a PCE of 14.9% with an open-circuit voltage (VOC) of 576 mV, a short-circuit current density (JSC) of 37.26 mA cm−2, and a FF of 70.0%. The calibrated minority carrier diffusion length is Le ≈ 1.2 µm, consistent with literature values. Leveraging these validated parameters, the QIBC model was constructed as shown in Fig. 2a. Throughout this work, we refer to the proposed architecture as QIBC (quasi-interdigitated back-contact); the unoptimized QIBC with calibrated parameters is termed the baseline QIBC, while BC-model and BC-optimized denote the unoptimized and optimized QIBC devices simulated with the saturation-onset material parameters in Section 3.5, respectively. The baseline structure comprises a CZTSSe absorber (700 nm) with patterned rear contacts, featuring alternating n-electrodes (CdS/i-ZnO/ITO) and p-electrodes (Mo). The n-contact retains the CdS buffer and i-ZnO/ITO window stack from the FBC reference (Table 1), serving as the electron-selective contact with an ITO back electrode. The p-contact uses Mo, forming a quasi-ohmic contact through a thin MoSe2 interfacial layer consistent with the FBC reference. The electrode geometry is defined by the pitch (pitch = WN + WP + WGap), with a fixed gap of 0.5 µm for electrode isolation. Baseline simulations employ symmetric electrodes (WN = WP). The pitch is varied over a wide range (0.2–1000 µm in Section 3) to fully map the lateral transport limitation, with particular focus on the range from 5 µm (pitch ≈ 4Le, near-ideal) to 20 µm (pitch ≈ 17Le, industrially-relevant). Field-engineering strategies, including Al2O3 passivation with fixed negative charges and front-surface bandgap grading, are subsequently introduced to mitigate this limitation, as detailed in Section 3.

(a) Schematic cross-section of the QIBC device structure showing the electrode geometry parameters. (b) Simulated J–V characteristics comparing the FBC reference and baseline QIBC device (20 µm pitch, no field engineering).

The CZTSSe absorber parameters in the QIBC model are identical to those listed in Table 1. The n-contact reuses the FBC buffer/window parameters (CdS/i-ZnO/ITO, Table 1); the Mo/MoSe2 rear p-contact uses the same interface recombination parameters as the FBC reference; and the electrode isolation gap is fixed at 0.5 µm. In the baseline QIBC, a rear Al2O3 dielectric layer is present between adjacent electrodes for electrical isolation (visible in Fig. 2a); this layer is part of the fixed geometry and carries no net charge in the baseline case. A second, functionally distinct Al2O3 layer at the front CZTSSe surface, carrying negative fixed charges, is introduced as a field-effect engineering element in Section 3.2. Bandgap grading is introduced separately in Section 3.4. The simulated trends should therefore be interpreted as design-window guidance within this calibrated framework, rather than as a process-specific device recipe.

Results and discussion

Effect of electrode pitch

To establish the baseline performance of the QIBC architecture, we first compared a 20 µm-pitch device (symmetric electrodes, no field-effect engineering) with the calibrated FBC reference. Note that the QIBC baseline uses a 700 nm absorber whereas the FBC reference uses 1500 nm; the thickness optimization for QIBC is discussed separately in Section 3.4. This initial comparison should therefore be viewed as a baseline design comparison rather than an isolated architecture-only test. As shown in Fig. 2b, the baseline QIBC device exhibits substantially degraded performance: JSC decreases from 36 to 14.9 mA cm−2, and the J–V curve displays pronounced bending characteristic of poor carrier collection. The resulting PCE of 4.6% with FF of only 57.2% represents a large loss compared to the FBC reference (PCE = 14.9%, FF = 70%).

To systematically quantify the impact of electrode pitch on device performance, we conducted a parametric sweep of the pitch from 0.2 to 1000 µm, with all other parameters held constant at their calibrated baseline values. Fig. 3 presents the evolution of the short-circuit current density (JSC), open-circuit voltage (VOC), fill factor (FF), and PCE as a function of the electrode pitch (Fig. 3a–d). The PCE reaches a maximum of ∼7.2% at a pitch of ∼1 µm, where the lateral transport distance remains closely matched to the minority-carrier diffusion length (Le ≈ 1.2 µm). Even at this optimal pitch the baseline QIBC PCE remains far below the FBC reference (14.9%), indicating that lateral transport is not the only loss mechanism; the symmetric geometry, thinner absorber (700 nm vs. 1500 nm), and absence of a vertical built-in field also limit carrier collection. As the pitch increases beyond 1 µm, a continuous performance degradation is observed. At a 20 µm pitch, the PCE falls to ∼4.5% with the FF decreasing to ∼57%. As the pitch expands further beyond 30 µm, a sharp non-linear performance collapse occurs, with the FF plummeting below 40%. In addition, VOC exhibits a gradual but continuous decrease from its peak of ∼0.549 V at sub-micron pitches to a plateau of ∼0.537 V at pitches beyond 30 µm. This reduction in VOC reflects the exacerbation of non-radiative bulk recombination, as carriers are forced to traverse increasingly longer lateral distances before reaching the junction. The substantial decline in JSC and the severe collapse of the FF at larger pitches confirm that the efficiency loss is predominantly driven by this transport-limited recombination rather than a generation deficit.

Dependence of QIBC device performance on electrode pitch (baseline structure, no field engineering): (a) short-circuit current density (JSC), (b) open-circuit voltage (VOC), (c) fill factor (FF), and (d) power conversion efficiency (PCE).

To elucidate the microscopic origin of this performance degradation, we examined the spatial distribution of the recombination rate within the device. Fig. 4 compares the two-dimensional recombination profiles for the FBC reference (left) and the baseline QIBC device with a 20 µm pitch (right). In the FBC structure, the recombination distribution is relatively uniform across the absorber. By contrast, the QIBC device exhibits a highly non-uniform distribution, featuring a pronounced high-recombination region (indicated in red, >1020 cm−3 s−1) located directly above the wide electrode.

Two-dimensional distribution of the recombination rate for (left) the FBC reference device and (right) the baseline QIBC device with a 20 µm pitch. The color scale represents the recombination rate in logarithmic units (10 × cm−3 s−1).

This spatial pattern directly reflects a severe lateral transport bottleneck. Carriers photogenerated in this region must diffuse laterally over ∼10 µm to reach the collecting junction at the electrode gap, a distance far exceeding the intrinsic ∼1.2 µm diffusion length. Unable to traverse this distance before recombining, these carriers are lost to bulk recombination, creating an extensive “electrical dead zone.” The existence of this dead zone effectively severs the photocurrent collection pathway, accounting for the pronounced deterioration in FF and JSC. This analysis confirms that insufficient lateral transport capability is the dominant efficiency loss mechanism in large-pitch QIBC architectures.

Effect of fixed charges

To overcome the diffusion-length limitation for lateral transport, we introduce a high-density negative fixed charge (Qf) at the Al2O3/CZTSSe front interface. This strategy is inspired by the well-established use of Al2O3 fixed charges for field-effect passivation in c-Si and CIGS solar cells. Although direct experimental characterization of Al2O3/CZTSSe fixed charges remains limited, the negative charge polarity of Al2O3 on p-type absorbers is well documented in analogous chalcogenide systems. Here, we simulate whether charge-induced band bending can create a low-resistance lateral conduction channel for majority carriers. This mechanism is supported by the experimental work of Vermang et al., who demonstrated that negative Al2O3 fixed charges induce field-effect passivation in ultrathin CIGS devices, significantly reducing front-surface recombination.26

Fig. 5 presents the effect of Qf on device performance for a 20 µm-pitch QIBC with symmetric electrodes. As Qf increases from 1010 to 1013 cm−2, JSC improves from ∼16.5 to ∼18.0 mA cm−2, the FF increases from ∼57% to ∼60%, and the PCE rises from ∼5% to ∼6%. While these trends confirm that the hole accumulation layer enhances lateral majority-carrier transport, the overall improvement is modest, with an absolute gain of only ∼1% in PCE. This limited enhancement clearly indicates that minority-carrier (electron) collection remains the dominant efficiency bottleneck.

Effect of fixed charge density (Qf) on the performance of the symmetric QIBC device (20 µm pitch). Evolution of (a) short-circuit current density (JSC), (b) open-circuit voltage (VOC), (c) fill factor (FF), and (d) power conversion efficiency (PCE) as a function of Qf.

The physical origin of this limitation is straightforward: although the Qf-induced hole accumulation layer provides a low-resistance pathway for majority carriers, minority electrons must still diffuse through the highly resistive bulk to reach the n-electrode. In a symmetric electrode geometry, electrons generated above the p-electrode region (constituting approximately half of the total absorber area) must travel lateral distances of ∼10 µm, far exceeding the ∼1.2 µm intrinsic electron diffusion length. These electrons recombine prior to collection, strictly limiting the achievable JSC regardless of how efficiently holes are transported.

Effect of electrode asymmetry

To address this fundamental asymmetry between majority and minority carrier transport, we introduce an asymmetric electrode geometry. In this design, the n-electrode width (WN) is deliberately expanded while the p-electrode width (WP) is minimized. The rationale exploits the decoupled transport kinetics: holes can efficiently traverse long lateral distances via the low-resistance surface accumulation layer to reach even a narrow p-electrode, whereas short-lived electrons benefit from a widened n-electrode that significantly reduces their required transport distance.

Fig. 6 shows the device performance as a function of the n-electrode coverage fraction at a fixed 20 µm pitch and Qf = −3 × 1012 cm−2. JSC increases almost linearly from ∼3 mA cm−2 at 5% coverage to ∼32 mA cm−2 at 95% coverage (Fig. 6a), directly reflecting improved minority electron collection as the average diffusion distance to the n-electrode decreases. The PCE follows a similar near-linear trend, rising from ∼1% to over 10% (Fig. 6d). Meanwhile, VOC decreases slightly from ∼0.551 V to ∼0.544 V (Fig. 6b), attributable to the increased junction area and the associated recombination current. The FF exhibits a more complex behavior, peaking at ∼60% around 20–30% coverage before declining at higher n-electrode fractions (Fig. 6c), likely due to increasing series resistance as the p-electrode becomes excessively narrow.

Effect of n-electrode coverage fraction on QIBC device performance at a 20 µm pitch with Qf = −3 × 1012 cm−2: (a) JSC, (b) VOC, (c) FF, and (d) PCE.

Even at 95% n-electrode coverage (leaving only 5% of the pitch for the p-electrode), efficient hole collection is maintained. This confirms that the Qf-induced hole accumulation layer provides sufficient lateral conductivity to transport holes over distances approaching 20 µm without significant resistive loss. Fixed charge passivation combined with electrode asymmetry thus enables a large performance improvement: the PCE increases from ∼6% with symmetric electrodes (Fig. 5d) to >10% with highly asymmetric electrodes.

Effect of bandgap grading

Having mitigated the lateral transport limitations through fixed charge passivation and electrode asymmetry, we now examine the effect of absorber thickness. Fig. 7 compares device performance as a function of thickness for uniform and graded bandgap profiles.

Effect of absorber thickness on QIBC device performance comparing uniform bandgap (Eg = 1.11 eV) and graded bandgap (Eg = 1.45 → 1.11 eV, full-thickness linear gradient): (a) JSC, (b) VOC, (c) FF, and (d) PCE.

For the uniform bandgap case (Eg = 1.11 eV), increasing the absorber thickness beyond an optimal point leads to a significant degradation in overall performance. While enhanced optical absorption causes the JSC to initially rise to a peak of ∼39 mA cm−2 at 800 nm, it subsequently decreases to ∼32 mA cm−2 at 1500 nm (Fig. 7a). This counterintuitive decline at greater thicknesses indicates that additional photogenerated carriers in the deeper regions of the absorber are lost to recombination before collection. Similarly, the VOC drops from ∼0.57 to ∼0.54 V (Fig. 7b), reflecting increased bulk recombination. Most critically, the FF collapses from ∼68% to ∼58% (Fig. 7c), a clear signature of transport-limited collection efficiency. Consequently, the PCE peaks at ∼15% for a thickness of ∼600 nm and steadily declines to ∼10% at 1500 nm (Fig. 7d). These results indicate that, even with optimized lateral transport, the short minority-carrier diffusion length imposes a severe constraint on the usable absorber thickness.

To overcome this vertical transport bottleneck, we introduce a full-thickness bandgap grading. The bandgap varies linearly from Eg = 1.45 eV at the front surface to 1.11 eV at the rear, corresponding to a S/(S + Se) ratio gradient from ∼0.4 to ∼0.1. Such a composition profile is experimentally achievable: Yang et al. demonstrated a band-gap-graded CZTSSe device with 12.3% efficiency using controlled S/(S + Se) grading, confirming that such composition profiles can be realized through surface sulfurization and sequential deposition of Se-rich and S-rich precursor layers.26,27 The conduction band gradient creates a quasi-electric field that drives minority electrons toward the rear n-electrode via drift rather than diffusion.28

The physical mechanism of the bandgap grading is illustrated in Fig. 8, which compares the energy band diagrams for uniform and graded absorbers. For the uniform case, the conduction band edge (Ec) remains essentially flat throughout the absorber, and minority electrons must rely solely on diffusion to reach the rear n-electrode. In contrast, the graded bandgap creates a pronounced downward slope in Ec from the front surface toward the rear. Since the S/Se anion substitution in CZTSSe primarily shifts the conduction band minimum while the valence band is largely pinned by the Cu-d and Se/S-p hybridization, the bandgap variation is predominantly accommodated by the conduction band, and the valence band edges nearly overlap between the two cases.29 The conduction band gradient establishes a built-in quasi-electric field of approximately F = ΔEc/d ≈ 0.24 eV/1.0 µm ≈ 2.4 kV cm−1 throughout the absorber, which accelerates photogenerated electrons toward the collecting junction via drift rather than diffusion. Additionally, the elevated Ec at the front surface creates an electron-reflecting barrier that suppresses front-surface recombination.

Energy band diagrams comparing uniform bandgap (Eg = 1.11 eV, red) and graded bandgap (Eg = 1.45 → 1.11 eV, blue) QIBC devices under AM1.5G illumination.

Bandgap grading improves all four performance metrics and reduces the thickness dependence. At 500 nm thickness, the VOC increases from 0.57 to 0.61 V (+40 mV), and the FF improves from 68% to 71% (+3%), reflecting reduced front-surface recombination due to the widened surface bandgap. More importantly, the thickness-dependent degradation is strongly suppressed. As thickness increases to 1500 nm, the JSC remains essentially constant at ∼37 mA cm−2, the VOC decreases by only 10 mV (compared to 30 mV for uniform), and the FF declines by only 4% (compared to 10% for uniform). The quasi-electric field effectively extends the collection depth far beyond the intrinsic diffusion length, enabling efficient extraction of carriers generated throughout the absorber thickness.

As a result of these improvements, the graded device achieves a peak PCE of ∼16% at an optimal thickness of 800–900 nm, compared to ∼15% at ∼600 nm for the uniform case. Furthermore, the graded device maintains a PCE >15% even at 1500 nm thickness, indicating improved tolerance to thickness variations, which may ease thickness control requirements in fabrication.

Effect of material parameters

Having established the optimized QIBC device architecture, we now examine how device performance depends on key material parameters, with the goal of identifying practical thresholds for experimental realization.

Fig. 9 shows the impact of bulk defect density (Nt) on device performance. The defect density directly determines the minority carrier lifetime (τ ∝ 1/Nt) and, consequently, the diffusion length Le = √(Dτ). For Nt < 1014 cm−3, all performance metrics remain nearly constant: JSC ≈ 39 mA cm−2, VOC ≈ 0.61 V, FF ≈ 70%, and PCE ≈ 17% (Fig. 9a–d). This saturation indicates that the field-effect engineering (fixed charge passivation, electrode asymmetry, and bandgap grading) effectively facilitates carrier collection even when the diffusion length exceeds the device dimensions.

Effect of bulk defect density (Nt) on QIBC device performance: (a) JSC, (b) VOC, (c) FF, and (d) PCE.

When Nt > 1014 cm−3, device performance degrades sharply. The FF decreases most sharply, dropping from 70% to below 40% as Nt approaches 1016 cm−3 (Fig. 9c), indicating severe transport limitations. Concurrently, JSC decreases from 39 to ∼10 mA cm−2 (Fig. 9a), and VOC falls from 0.61 to 0.45 V (Fig. 9b) due to increased non-radiative recombination. Consequently, the PCE collapses from ∼17% to ∼2% (Fig. 9d).

These results identify Nt ≈ 1014 cm−3 as a critical threshold for QIBC devices. At this level, the minority carrier lifetime is ∼10 ns and the diffusion length is ∼1 µm, consistent with state-of-the-art CZTSSe absorbers reported in the literature. Importantly, reducing Nt below this threshold yields diminishing returns, suggesting that once moderate material quality is achieved, device engineering dominates the efficiency outcome. It should be noted that the PCE ≈ 17% observed at Nt < 1013 cm−3 is an upper-bound result under idealized conditions not yet achievable in current CZTSSe absorbers.

The effect of carrier mobility is presented in Fig. 10a. Both electron mobility (µn) and hole mobility (µp) exhibit saturation behavior, but with distinctly different thresholds. For µn, PCE increases sharply from ∼4% at 1 cm2 V−1 s−1 to ∼16% at 40 cm2 V−1 s−1, beyond which further improvement yields diminishing returns. In contrast, µp saturates much earlier at ∼10 cm2 V−1 s−1, indicating that hole transport is less critical than electron transport in the QIBC architecture. This asymmetry is physically reasonable: electrons must traverse the entire absorber thickness to reach the rear n-electrode, while holes benefit from the accumulation layer induced by the fixed charges and need only travel laterally to the nearby p-electrode.

Sensitivity of QIBC device performance to material parameters: (a) carrier mobility (electron mobility µn and hole mobility µp), (b) doping concentration (Na), (c) rear interface defect density (Dit), and (d) simulated J–V characteristics comparing FC-model (front-contact reference), BC-model (baseline back-contact), and BC-optimized (back-contact with field-effect engineering).

The doping concentration (Na) shows weak influence on device performance (Fig. 10b). PCE varies by only ∼0.15% absolute across three orders of magnitude (1014–1017 cm−3), with a shallow optimum around 3–5 × 1014 cm−3. This insensitivity suggests that the field-effect engineering decouples device performance from precise doping control, providing flexibility for experimental fabrication.

Similarly, the rear p-contact interface defect density at the CZTSSe/MoSe2 interface (Dit) has minimal impact on device efficiency (Fig. 10c). PCE remains stable at ∼15.9% for Dit up to 1012 cm−2, decreasing only slightly to ∼14.7% at 1015 cm−2. This tolerance to interface quality can be attributed to two factors: (i) the bandgap grading creates a quasi-electric field that rapidly sweeps electrons away from the interface region, reducing the time available for interface recombination; and (ii) the high n-electrode coverage (95%) means that most carriers are collected at the CZTSSe/CdS junction rather than passing through the CZTSSe/MoSe2 interface.

Based on these sensitivity analyses, we identify the following threshold parameters at which individual performance metrics enter the saturation regime: Nt ≤ 1014 cm−3, µn ≥ 40 cm2 V−1 s−1, µp ≥ 10 cm2 V−1 s−1, and Dit ≤ 1012 cm−2. Fig. 10d compares the J–V characteristics of three device configurations, all simulated using the above saturation-onset parameters (note that these differ from the calibrated values in Table 1; hence the BC-model results here differ from the baseline QIBC in Section 3.1): the front-contact reference (FC-model), the baseline back-contact device without field engineering (BC-model), and the optimized back-contact device (BC-optimized). The BC-model exhibits severely degraded performance with JSC ≈15 mA cm−2, VOC = 530 mV, and FF ≈ 57%, yielding a PCE of only 4.5%. By contrast, the BC-optimized device achieves JSC = 38.48 mA cm−2, VOC = 606 mV, FF = 67.5%, and PCE = 15.75%, exceeding the FC-model reference (PCE = 14.9%). The superior JSC results from relocating the CdS/i-ZnO/ITO stack from the illuminated front surface to the rear, which eliminates front-side parasitic absorption; the net optical gain remains positive despite the change in rear reflectance from Mo to ITO. The higher VOC compared to the FBC reference is attributed to the combined effects of reduced front-surface recombination, fixed-charge passivation, and conduction-band grading, rather than to back-contact geometry alone. It should be noted that the ∼16% PCE values observed in the individual parameter sweeps (Fig. 10a–c) reflect single-parameter optimization with all other parameters held at their calibrated baseline values. The BC-optimized result of 15.75% represents the self-consistent device in which all four threshold parameters are simultaneously applied, and is therefore adopted as the flagship efficiency for direct comparison with the calibrated front-contact reference.

Conclusion

In this work, we numerically investigate a strategy to mitigate the fundamental challenge limiting back-contact CZTSSe solar cells: the severe mismatch between the minority-carrier diffusion length and the industrially achievable electrode pitch. Through comprehensive TCAD simulations, we show that synergistic field-effect engineering can mitigate this diffusion-length barrier without requiring improvements in bulk material quality.

The proposed strategy comprises three elements that address lateral hole transport, vertical electron collection, and minority-carrier diffusion distance, respectively. First, negative fixed charges at the front Al2O3/CZTSSe interface (Qf = −3 × 1012 cm−2) induce a hole accumulation layer that provides a low-resistance pathway for lateral majority-carrier transport. Second, highly asymmetric electrode geometry (95% n-electrode coverage) minimizes the average diffusion distance for minority electrons while exploiting the enhanced hole conductivity. Third, full-thickness bandgap grading (1.45 → 1.11 eV) establishes a quasi-electric field (∼2.4 kV cm−1) that transforms vertical electron transport from diffusion-limited to drift-assisted.

The combined effect of these three modifications raises the simulated PCE from 4.5% (unoptimized) to 15.75%. Without field-effect engineering, the unoptimized back-contact device (BC-model) at 20 µm pitch exhibits severely degraded efficiency (PCE ≈ 4.5%, FF ≈ 57%) due to extensive electrical dead zones. With field-effect engineering, the optimized device achieves JSC = 38.48 mA cm−2, VOC = 606 mV, FF = 67.5%, and PCE = 15.75%, exceeding the front-contact reference (14.9%). The superior JSC results from relocating the CdS/i-ZnO/ITO stack to the rear, eliminating front-side parasitic absorption, while the higher VOC compared to the FBC reference is attributed to the combined effects of reduced front-surface recombination, fixed-charge passivation, and conduction-band grading, rather than to back-contact geometry alone.

Sensitivity analysis identifies threshold material parameters beyond which further improvement yields diminishing returns: bulk defect density Nt ≤ 1014 cm−3, electron mobility µn ≥ 40 cm2 V−1 s−1, hole mobility µp ≥ 10 cm2 V−1 s−1, and rear interface defect density Dit ≤ 1012 cm−2. Device performance shows weak sensitivity to doping concentration and rear interface quality, indicating that the field-effect engineering provides tolerance to fabrication variations.

These results indicate that field-effect engineering may extend the applicability of back-contact architectures to thin-film absorbers with short diffusion lengths, though experimental verification is still needed, particularly regarding rear-contact patterning at sub-micron resolution, the stability of Al2O3 fixed charges during CZTSSe processing, and the realization of controlled full-thickness S/(S + Se) grading profiles.
