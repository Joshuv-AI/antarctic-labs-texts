Soft tissue changes in Dementia: A comprehensive systematic review
Authors: Francesco Pipitone, Caterina Bernetti, Gianfranco Di Gennaro, Federico Greco, Luca Pugliese, Bruno Beomonte Zobel, Carlo A Mallio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•TMT is a viable biomarker for sarcopenia in dementia imaging.•Craniofacial muscle loss correlates with cognitive impairment.•Muscle fat infiltration links to brain atrophy in AD and DLB.•Sarcopenia and dementia share metabolic and inflammatory pathways.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a syndrome characterized by a gradual loss of muscle mass, strength, and physical performance, often contributing to physical disability and higher all-cause mortality, independent of comorbid medical conditions [1], [2], [3], [4], [5].

Sarcopenia affects a significant proportion of older adults, typically aged 65 years and older, with prevalence estimates ranging from 10% to 40% depending on the studied population and diagnostic criteria. In patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD), the prevalence of sarcopenia appears to be even higher, reaching 60–70% in some cases [1], [2], [3].

In fact, recent literature has highlighted a significant association between sarcopenia and neurodegenerative processes and cognitive impairment, which appear to be accelerated in sarcopenic elderly patients compared to healthy, age-mached ones [1], [6], [7], [8].

Some imaging-focused studies also revealed correlation between structural brain changes and specific muscle parameters, such as lower total and regional brain volumes in individuals exhibiting components of sarcopenia (reduced muscle mass and function). For instance, Camargo et al. demonstrated that slow walking and weak handgrip strength were associated with lower total brain volume and poorer performance on functional tests, such as visual memory and language [9], [10], [11]. Kim et al. also demonstrated that hippocampal atrophy mediates the link between sarcopenia and cognitive decline [7]. In these clinical assessments, muscle mass and body composition were typically determined using specialized techniques, such as Dual-energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) or MRI to ensure objective data collection.

Therefore, an early diagnosis of sarcopenia and the management of its risk factors is warranted to limit the progression of its negative outcomes [1]. According to the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP), sarcopenia is diagnosed by considering muscle strength, muscle mass, and physical performance [12]. The determination of muscle strength and physical performance, via grip strength and gait speed respectively, offers a simple and accessible diagnostic approach; however, it requires active patient cooperation, which can be limited in patients with dementia due to cognitive and behavioral deficits [2]. By contrast, assessing muscle mass represents more objective data, even though it often requires specialized imaging techniques that are more expensive and sometimes impractical given the underlying co-morbidities of patients 13. In clinical practice, it is common practice to estimate muscle quantity by using the appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) index obtained by Dual-energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) or bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), or lumbar muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) obtained using computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [1], [2]. However, performing these assessments in patients with dementia can be challenging due to the presence of comorbidities that may limit reliable measurement of skeletal muscle mass [1].

Given the practical limitations of these traditional methods to assess sarcopenia in dementia patients, temporal muscle thickness (TMT) measurement has emerged as a promising alternative [14]. Although the work by Ranganathan et al. did not meet the specific inclusion criteria for this systematic review—as it did not focus on patients with cognitive impairment—it remains a seminal work in demonstrating the diagnostic utility of TMT [15]. The authors demonstrated that TMT correlates strongly with psoas muscle area, a validated predictor of frailty. This finding suggests that TMT assessment on existing brain imaging (CT or MRI) may offer a valuable surrogate marker of systemic muscle loss, particularly in patients where whole-body assessments are unfeasible. More recently, Maskos et al. demonstrated that the TMT can be used to assess general muscle wasting in patients with intensive care unit acquired weakness (ICU-AW) [16]. They correlated the TMT measured by ultrasound (US) and CT, with the rectus femoris thickness (RFT) measured on US, which is considered a parameter of evolving sarcopenia, demonstrating a positive correlation between the decline of both structures. In parallel, with growing interest in the temporal muscle as a biomarker of sarcopenia, other studies have begun exploring soft tissue structures captured in routine head and neck imaging [16]. For instance, Borda et al. highlighted the potential use of the tongue and masseter muscles as surrogate markers of muscle quantity [13]. Despite the established correlations between TMT and linear markers like rectus femoris thickness, there is a relative scarcity of studies directly comparing TMT to the 3D volumetric assessment of limb muscles. Future research utilizing automated 3D segmentation of both cranial and systemic musculature is warranted to further validate the volumetric accuracy of TMT as a global biomarker.

Beyond quantitative muscle loss, qualitative deterioration—particularly through fat infiltration (FI) — has emerged as an independent factor contributing to neurodegeneration [2], [10]. Increased muscle adiposity has been linked with cortical thinning and reduced gray matter in both cerebrum and cerebellum, suggesting a systemic interplay between muscular and neural degeneration [10], [17]. Muscle FI can be quantified non-invasively using MRI techniques such as Dixon sequences, which allow for the separation of water and fat signals, or proton spectroscopy (1H-MRS) [10], [17].While other imaging modalities such as CT and ultrasound are frequently used for sarcopenia assessment, this study focuses on MRI to evaluate craniofacial soft tissues as opportunistic surrogate biomarkers. This approach leverages routine brain MRI scans—already required for the diagnostic workup of neurodegenerative diseases—to provide additional information on systemic frailty without the need for further examinations or radiation exposure.

This systematic review aims to explore the association between sarcopenia and dementia, in particular AD, with an emphasis on muscle atrophy and altered muscle composition as potential imaging biomarkers of cognitive decline. By critically synthesizing current evidence, this review evaluates the feasibility of using soft tissue measurements—particularly from routine brain imaging—to predict dementia progression and identify future research directions.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

The systematic literature research was carried out in March 2025 across three electronic databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library and Scopus. The search strategy included two primary Boolean combinations of keywords:

1.(“dementia” OR “Alzheimer’s disease”) AND (“atrophy” OR “fat infiltration” OR “sarcopenia”) AND (“MRI biomarkers”)

(“dementia” OR “Alzheimer’s disease”) AND (“atrophy” OR “fat infiltration” OR “sarcopenia”) AND (“MRI biomarkers”)

2.(“dementia” OR “Alzheimer’s disease”) AND (“temporal muscle” OR “soft tissue”) AND (“atrophy” OR “fat infiltration” OR “sarcopenia”)”

(“dementia” OR “Alzheimer’s disease”) AND (“temporal muscle” OR “soft tissue”) AND (“atrophy” OR “fat infiltration” OR “sarcopenia”)”

In addition, a hand search was conducted by using Google Scholar and by screening the reference lists of all the eligible articles found with the database search. There were no restrictions on the date range for the literature search.

Following PRISMA guidelines, the literature search and study screening were conducted independently by two authors (F.P. and C.B.) to minimize selection bias. Titles and abstracts were screened first, followed by a full-text review of potentially eligible studies. Any discrepancies regarding study inclusion or data extraction were resolved through consensus or, if necessary, by consultation with a third senior reviewer (C.A.M.).

Study selection

After removing duplicates, the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles were screened to determine if they met the selection criteria. To further confirm eligibility, the full texts of the remaining articles were screened using including standards. The inclusion criteria for this review were: 1) Studies investigating alterations in soft tissue structures in patients with cognitive dysfunction, 2) studies evaluating the association between sarcopenia and dementia, in particular AD, 3) full-text articles available in English. On the other hand, the Exclusion criteria were: 1) Review articles, preclinical studies, and case reports, 2) studies investigating only brain MRI biomarkers without muscular assessment. Results

The systematic search resulted in 128 articles, with an additional 4 studies identified through manual searching. After removing duplicates, the remaining articles were screened for inclusion based on title and abstract, and where necessary, full-text evaluation. Following application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 8 studies were deemed eligible for final inclusion. Thickness and volume of craniofacial muscles as biomarkers of sarcopenia

Four studies investigated the possibility of using craniofacial muscles as a surrogate for systemic muscle loss. Cho et al. found that temporal muscle thickness (TMT) was related to appendicular skeletal mass (ASM) (r = 0.379, p = 0.001) as well as to a reduced Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (r = 0.350, p = 0.003) and Global Deterioration Scale (GDS) (r = − 0.267, p = 0.039) [18]. Interestingly, Borda et al. found an association between TMT and MMSE only in patients with dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and not in the AD group [14]. The specificity of the association between TMT and MMSE in the DLB group might reflect the different underlying pathophysiologies of the two dementia forms, with a potentially earlier involvement of musculature in one of the conditions. Nevertheless, significant associations were found between TMT and mortality in the whole sample (HR = 0.815, p-value 0.002), in the AD group (HR = 0.834p-value = 0.031), and in the DLB group (HR = 0.767p-value = 0.019) [14]. Maskos et al. found that the decline in TMT, measured by CT and US, was proportional to the decline in RFT during ICU stay (TMT to RFT: r = 0.746, p < 0.001; CT-based TMT to ultrasound-based RFT: r = 0.609, p < 0.001) [16]. Borda et al. found a significant correlation of the masseter muscle and tongue volumes –determined by MRI- with the appendicular lean soft tissue index (ALSTI), determined via DXA: masseter volume (Rho 0.33, P-value < 0.001), tongue volume (Rho 0.26, P-value < 0.001) [13]. Concordance between sarcopenia with masseter and tongue versus sarcopenia with ALSTI as reference has a Kappa of 0.989 (P < 0.001) for masseter and a Kappa of 1 for the tongue muscle (P < 0.001) [13].

Muscle atrophy and dementia

Two studies evaluated how the presence of sarcopenia plays a role in cognitive decline. Kim et al. found that, by comparing two groups, one with sarcopenia and the other without, in the former there was a significant thinner mean cortical thickness especially in the right occipital pole [7]. The same research group, in another study, found that the link between sarcopenia and poor cognitive function was mediated by a decreased hippocampal volume (−0.197, p = 0.022), which was further associated with low MMSE (indirect pathway β = − 0.438, p = 0.046)[7]. However, sarcopenia was not associated with MMSE (direct pathway β = − 0.892, p = 0.115), without mediation of hippocampal volume.

Fat infiltration in muscles and brain atrophy

The presence of muscle FI, used as a proxy for poor muscle quality (or integrity), was associated with widespread brain volume loss. In the included studies, this qualitative assessment was performed using MRI fat-signal quantification or CT attenuation values. Two studies investigated how muscle FI, an indicator of poor muscle quality, relates to neurodegeneration. Gurholt et al. compared different groups depending on the degree of sarcopenia [17]. They found that the probable sarcopenia group had higher muscle FI and lower appendicular lean mass relative to the non-sarcopenia group. Regarding brain volumes, the sarcopenia group had thinner cortex in the temporal, frontal and parietal lobes as well as smaller brain volumes in the lower brainstem (r = − 0.05, P = 2.3e − 13), cerebellum white matter (WM) (left/right r = − 0.04, P = 2.7e − 15/ 2.6e − 12) and cerebellum cortex (left/right r = − 0.04, P = 2.5e − 10/1.2e − 13).

Uchida et al. found that a decrease in free-fat mass index (FFMI) and a rapid decline in muscle mass index (MMI) caused a faster decline of the MMSE score, especially in men than in women [19]. Changes in the body mass index (BMI) and fat mass index (FMI) did not have a significant association with changes in cognition.

Results from the studies in aging individuals

The included studies spanned from 2022 to 2024, with sample sizes ranging from 40 to over 33.000 participants. Age distribution ranged from 60 to 85 years, with a predominance of AD and DLB in most cohorts [7], [13], [14], [18], [19]. Two studies did not make a definite diagnosis of dementia, and no specific memory tests and neurological scores to assess cognition were applied [10], [16].A consistent relationship was observed between sarcopenia-related imaging biomarkers and cognitive decline. Craniofacial muscle measurements such as TMT, masseter, and tongue volume emerged as promising non-invasive surrogates for systemic muscle loss, showing strong correlations with both sarcopenia indices and cognitive performance scores [13], [14], [16], [18]. Structural brain changes—including cortical thinning and hippocampal atrophy—were more pronounced in individuals with muscle atrophy or low muscle quality, particularly in those with confirmed sarcopenia [7]. Furthermore, the presence of muscle FI, as a proxy of poor muscle integrity, was associated with widespread brain volume loss, especially in subcortical and cerebellar regions [10], [19]. Together, these findings suggest that cranial muscle-based biomarkers, derived from commonly available imaging techniques, may provide clinically relevant insights into both the systemic and neurological aspects of dementia progression.

Discussion

In this systematic review, we examined the relationship between cranial muscle-based imaging biomarkers and cognitive decline in aging and dementia. The findings revealed consistent evidence that TMT, and to a lesser extent the masseter and tongue muscles, are associated with systemic sarcopenia. These craniofacial muscle measurements may serve as accessible, incidental biomarkers of muscle mass, particularly useful for individuals with dementia who already undergo routine neuroimaging. Importantly, sarcopenia and muscle quality indicators were also linked to neurodegenerative changes, including cortical thinning and subcortical atrophy, especially in AD and DLB. Together, these results highlight the emerging role of muscle imaging in characterizing systemic frailty and neurodegenerative vulnerability. These findings emphasize the emerging utility of muscle imaging as a non-invasive adjunct in neurodegenerative risk stratification.

The strength of this approach lies not in the introduction of a new standalone diagnostic tool, but in the 'opportunistic' utilization of standard clinical MR neuroimaging. By extracting quantitative masseter and temporal muscle parameters from routine 3 T MRI, we provide a dual-purpose assessment of both central neurodegeneration and peripheral sarcopenia markers. This strategy maximizes the diagnostic yield of a single imaging session, effectively identifying systemic frailty without the need for additional specialized scans, extra costs, or further patient burden.

Integrated Conceptual Framework: The Muscle-Brain axis

The observed associations between craniofacial muscle changes and cognitive decline suggest an integrated “muscle-brain axis” driven by shared systemic pathways. Moreover, Kim et al. (2024a) also propose that sarcopenia and AD may share genetic risk factors [7], [20], [21].

We have found the following hypotheses to explain this link:

•Metabolic and endocrine dysregulation: Skeletal muscles, acting as endocrine organs, secrete various factors such as insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I), which has anti-inflammatory properties [22]. Sarcopenia-related reductions in muscle mass may therefore lead to decreased IGF-I levels, contributing to systemic inflammation and oxidative stress − conditions that exacerbate Aβ accumulation in the AD spectrum [23], [24], [25]. This is further supported by animal models suggesting that mutations in the amyloid precursor protein gene not only drive Aβ pathology but are also associated with reduced muscle mass and strength [26], [27].

•Systemic Inflammation and oxidative stress: Literature findings also support a bidirectional relationship between muscular and neuronal degeneration. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress represent key pathways where muscle-derived factors influence systemic inflammatory status, potentially triggering or worsening neuroinflammation − though aging remains an independent factor influencing both systems [7]. Gurholt et al. expanded on this by demonstrating that individuals with sarcopenia and increased muscle FI exhibit widespread structural brain changes, including cortical thinning, reduced WM fractional anisotropy, and lower brain volumes [17]. Their analysis revealed that sensorimotor brain regions mediated the relationship between sarcopenic traits and cognitive performance, suggesting a shared anatomical-functional pathway. They hypothesized that inflammatory and metabolic dysregulation may exacerbate both sarcopenia and cognitive impairment [28], [29], [30]. Furthermore, Kim et al. found that different sarcopenia domains corresponded to distinct brain alterations [7]. Reductions in skeletal muscle mass were associated with higher Aβ deposition, especially in the parietal, precuneus, and cingulate cortices. Meanwhile, muscle strength was more closely linked to WM hyperintensities, and physical function to global brain atrophy.

Temporal muscle thickness and Neurocognitive correlates

The hypothesized biological link between Temporal Muscle Thickness (TMT) and AD-related pathologies is rooted in the muscle-brain axis. Reduced TMT serves as a cross-sectional indicator of systemic muscle depletion, which is closely tied to amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition.

TMT, which can be assessed incidentally during brain imaging procedures, has emerged as a practical, non-invasive, surrogate biomarker for the muscle-brain axis (Fig. 2). Multiple studies have demonstrated its correlation with global sarcopenia markers and cognitive outcomes.

The measurements of the temporal muscles thickness (TMT) are shown using the Silvian fissure and the orbit roof as points of reference, due to their high visibility on routine MRI and their stability, to ensures high inter-rater reproducibility and standardizes the measurement at the muscle's maximum thickness, following established protocols in literature. A slight asymmetry may be observed between the right and left temporal muscles. Such variations are common and can result from individual masticatory dominance, dental alignment, or minor head tilting during MRI acquisition. To minimize the impact of these factors and improve the validity of TMT as a biomarker for systemic sarcopenia, clinical protocols generally recommend calculating the mean thickness of both sides.

Borda et al. found that TMT was significantly associated with both mortality and cognitive decline in individuals with AD and DLB [14]. Specifically, lower TMT values were linked to reduced MMSE scores, particularly in DLB patients. Given its sensitivity to malnutrition and general muscle mass, TMT could serve as a valuable marker of nutritional status and general muscle mass [33]. While this association could represent a confounding factor when linking TMT directly to dementia, malnutrition, sarcopenia, and cognitive decline often coexist in a synergistic 'frailty' cycle, where each condition exacerbates the progression of the others [33].

Similarly, Cho et al. reported that TMT was a better predictor of cognitive function in dementia patients than ASM measured via DXA [18]. This supports previous findings that TMT correlates well with whole-body muscle mass and may be particularly relevant in geriatric and neurodegenerative populations [15], [34], [35], [35], [36], [37]. As noted by Furtner et al., TMT not only reflects chronological aging but also captures biological vulnerability, thereby offering added value in the management of patients [38].

Regarding healthy populations, studies have shown that craniofacial muscles undergo physiological changes with age. For instance, Mikel González-Fernández et al, and Uysal et al. demonstrated a significant decrease in masseter and temporalis muscle thickness in healthy older adults compared to younger individuals [39], [40]. Furthermore, research by Kilgour et al. confirmed that even in healthy aging, neck and cranial muscle cross-sectional areas correlate with age-related brain volume changes [37]. However, the studies analyzed here indicate that this decline is pathologically accelerated in patients with Alzheimer’s Disease and Dementia with Lewy Bodies.

In the context of neurocritical care, Maskos et al. compared TMT to rectus femoris measurements and found parallel patterns of muscle wasting. This suggests that cranial muscle measurements can serve as reliable proxies for systemic sarcopenia, even in acute care settings [16].

Expanding this perspective, Borda et al. demonstrated that both masseter and tongue muscle measurements also predicted systemic sarcopenia. Like TMT, these measurements can be derived from routine head and neck imaging, allowing for faster sarcopenia screening and improved clinical management [14].

However, the use of TMT as a biomarker may have limitations, including potential sensitivity to local factors unrelated to systemic sarcopenia and the need for standardized measurement protocols across different imaging platforms.

Muscle fat infiltration and hormonal modulation

It is important to note that while craniofacial muscle measurements provide valuable insights into muscle quantity, the studies on FI, primarily focusing on thigh muscle and systemic indices, highlight the significance of muscle quality in the context of neurodegeneration.

Muscle FI, a marker of poor muscle quality, in fact, is increasingly recognized as a surrogate indicator of biological aging. It may mirror cerebral degeneration through common degenerative pathways such as lipid dysregulation, oxidative stress, and inflammation.

Uchida et al. demonstrated that older adults with a > 5% reduction in free-fat mass index (FFMI) and muscle mass index (MMI) over six years experienced greater cognitive decline, particularly in MMSE performance [19]. Interestingly, this association was more pronounced in men, likely due to sex-specific hormonal changes [41]. Testosterone decline in aging males is a known risk factor for both sarcopenia and dementia [24], [42], [43], [44]. In contrast, although estrogen has neuroprotective effects in women, its relationship with muscle mass remains inconsistent, underlining the need for sex-specific interventions [45], [46].

Nakao et al. and Borda et al. also utilized MRI to assess FI in the tongue, identifying it as a key indicator of muscle quality [47], [48], [49]. Furthermore, Borda et al. established a significant association between malnutrition and both reduced muscle volume and increased intramuscular fat in the masseter and tongue muscles of older individuals with mild dementia [50]. These findings are complemented by the work of Kroon et al., which further supports the relevance of muscle quality markers in neurodegenerative contexts [51].

These studies underline how increased muscle adiposity appears to be associated with widespread brain structural changes, suggesting that both the amount and the composition of muscle tissue are critical factors in the interplay between sarcopenia and dementia.

As a prospect for further investigation, functional assessments may complement imaging findings. Nakao et al. reported a significant correlation between tongue pressure waveform analysis, performed using a JMS tongue pressure measurement device, and tongue muscle composition assessed by MRI [47]. This suggests that the qualitative deterioration of craniofacial muscles directly impacts functional outcomes, such as tongue pressure, which could serve as a clinical indicator of sarcopenia progression in dementia patients.

Literature Gaps and future directions

Despite the compelling associations identified in this review, current evidence remains largely correlational. A critical gap exists in understanding the transition from association to causation; therefore, future research must prioritize longitudinal studies that simultaneously track changes in craniofacial muscle composition (via MRI-derived MFI) and neurodegenerative markers (such as amyloid/tau PET or fluid biomarkers) over time. Such studies would clarify whether muscle loss precedes or merely mirrors neural decline. Furthermore, the role of sex-specific hormonal modulation remains an open question: while testosterone decline in men is a known driver of sarcopenia, the distinct role of estrogen in muscle-brain preservation and its impact on dementia risk in women remains inconsistent and warrants rigorous investigation. Finally, establishing standardized, age-adjusted cut-off values for TMT and masseter volume across diverse populations will be essential for their implementation as reliable clinical screening tools.

Conclusions

This review synthesizes current evidence on muscle-based imaging biomarkers for assessing sarcopenia and its neurological associations in individuals with cognitive impairment. Specifically, the incidental visualization of craniofacial muscle quantity through TMT, masseter, and tongue measurements, on routine cranial imaging offers a practical, non-invasive opportunity to assess systemic frailty, especially in individuals with cognitive impairment.

Importantly, the consistent associations observed extend beyond muscle quantity; studies demonstrating a link between increased general muscle FI (a measure of muscle quality) and adverse brain changes alongside cognitive decline highlight the significance of muscle composition. These findings support the potential integration of musculoskeletal and neuroimaging markers into comprehensive risk assessment models for dementia, integrating both quantitative craniofacial muscle markers and general muscle quality indicators. On particular, these readily measurable changes in the facial features on routine brain MRI could serve as novel, non-invasive opportunistic peripheral biomarkers, complementing traditional neurodegenerative risk stratification. Further investigation into shared biological mechanisms—such as inflammation, hormonal imbalance, and metabolic dysfunction—is warranted to understand these relationships better. Future longitudinal studies are needed to validate the clinical utility of these biomarkers, with attention to sex-specific factors, underlying pathophysiology, and real-world applicability. A key direction for future research should be to further explore the distinct and combined roles of both craniofacial muscle trophism (quantity) and FI in various muscle groups (quality) as promising indicators of sarcopenia and neurodegenerative disease progression, potentially investigating the specific diagnostic or prognostic value of FI even within the craniofacial muscles themselves.
