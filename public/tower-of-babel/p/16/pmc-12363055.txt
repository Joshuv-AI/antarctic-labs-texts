The impact of digital storytelling on psychosocial well-being in individuals with type 1 diabetes: a randomized clinical trial study in Iran
Authors: Sheyda Zamani, Samereh Abdoli, Fataneh Ghadirian, Leila Mardanian Dehkordi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiving with diabetes presents psychosocial challenges, including distress, burnout, and depressive symptoms. Peer-created digital storytelling (DST) videos offer a platform for sharing personal experiences and management strategies, potentially improving psychological well-being and supporting better diabetes management. DST, grounded in Narrative and Social Cognitive Theory, provides an innovative, culturally adaptable, and low-cost intervention that addresses limitations of conventional psychosocial approaches. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of DST in improving psychological outcomes in individuals with Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) in Iran.MethodsThis was a two-group, parallel, single-blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT) study conducted from July 2023 to May 2024. Sixty individuals with T1D were recruited from comprehensive health centers in Iran and randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (n = 30) or the control group (n = 30) using MinimPy software. The intervention group participated in a 4-week program, where they viewed and discussed peer-created DST videos depicting successful T1D management, while the control group received routine care. Study outcomes, including diabetes distress, diabetes burnout, and depressive symptoms, were measured at baseline, immediately post-intervention, and one month post-intervention using validated scales. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24.0, employing both descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultsThe intervention group demonstrated significant improvement in mean scores for diabetes distress, diabetes burnout, and depressive symptoms at two time points (immediately post-intervention, and one-month post-intervention), with all p-values < 0.001. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in these scores at any time point within the control group (p > 0.05).ConclusionReflecting on peer-created DST videos appears to be an effective intervention for reducing diabetes distress, diabetes burnout, and depressive symptoms in individuals with T1D. This study addresses a gap in the literature by evaluating a theory-driven, peer-led digital intervention in a low-resource setting. This approach could serve as a valuable model for mental health promotion initiatives in this population. Further research is needed to assess the long-term effects and sustainability of DST interventions.Trial registrationThis study was registered as a RCT at the Iranian Clinical Trial Center. Registration Date: 2023/07/05, Registration Number: IRCT20170625034743N2.Keywords: Diabetes burnout, Diabetes distress, Digital storytelling, Depressive symptoms, Diabetes

Background

Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) is a chronic condition that demands continuous and complex management, affecting nearly every aspect of an individual’s life [1]. Unlike their peers without diabetes, individuals with T1D are at a higher risk of psychological challenges such as diabetes burnout, diabetes distress, and depressive symptoms [2], which can significantly impact self-care and overall well-being.

Diabetes burnout refers to emotional exhaustion, frustration, and feeling overwhelmed by the ongoing demands of diabetes management [3, 4]. This often manifests as a lack of motivation to engage in necessary diabetes self-care activities [5]. Similarly, diabetes distress encompasses the negative emotional and psychological responses individuals experience as they manage their diabetes, regardless of its type. It highlights how emotional distress often arises from an individual’s perception of health threats and their evaluation of available coping resources [5].

In addition to burnout and distress, depressive symptoms are another prevalent mental health issue among those with T1D. They are characterized by persistent low mood, a loss of interest in daily activities, and significant impairment in an individual’s ability to function [6]. This condition can also lead to suicidal thoughts, compounding the difficulties faced by individuals with diabetes [7]. Studies indicate that 38.3% of individuals with T1D report clinically significant diabetes distress, while 27.8% experience depressive symptoms above the diagnostic threshold [8]. Furthermore, half of this population reports experiencing diabetes burnout [4, 9]. These psychological factors interfere with effective diabetes management, contributing to poorer treatment adherence, decreased self-care, and worse clinical outcomes [4, 10–13].

The impact of psychological challenges underscores the need for accessible, tailored psychological interventions for individuals with T1D [14]. A variety of psychosocial interventions have been explored, including education and skills training, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), motivational interviewing, family therapy, support groups, and digital interventions [15, 16].

While these interventions show promise, they often face limitations in terms of accessibility, sustained engagement, and relevance to the emotional, cognitive, and motivational complexities of living with T1D. A Cochrane systematic review found no significant effect of psychological interventions on diabetes distress compared to usual care in T1D populations [16], highlighting the ongoing need for innovative, scalable, and effective approaches.

Digital storytelling (DST) was chosen for this study due to its narrative-based approach, which fosters emotional processing, peer support, and self-efficacy—particularly in settings with limited access to traditional psychosocial resources [17, 18]. DST is rooted in Narrative Theory, which posits that storytelling is a fundamental mechanism through which individuals process emotions, construct meaning, and build social connections [19]. It is also guided by Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which suggests that individuals learn behaviors by observing role models [20].

DST allows participants to share personal diabetes experiences using multimedia (videos, audio, graphics), and it has been increasingly used in healthcare. This approach promotes learning from others’ lived experiences, creating a sense of community and support that enhances engagement and self-management [19, 21–29]. DST has demonstrated efficacy in diabetes education and support, particularly when culturally tailored and co-created with patient input [30, 31].

Several studies report positive outcomes associated with DST, including increased self-efficacy, improved mental and physical health, reduced depressive symptoms, and enhanced disease management [22, 32]. A review by Wilson et al. found that DST helped children with cancer better understand and cope with their illness [33], while Houston et al. noted limited effectiveness in blood pressure control among adults with hypertension [34], suggesting DST’s impact may vary across conditions and outcomes.

Despite growing interest, few intervention studies have targeted psychological distress in people with diabetes, and there is limited evidence on DST’s effects on self-efficacy, confidence, motivation, and emotional well-being in this population [35, 36]. Furthermore, no systematic reviews have comprehensively assessed these outcomes, and group-based DST interventions for chronic disease remain scarce [37]. Variability in target populations, designs, and implementation strategies also limit generalizability.

DST integrates theoretical insights from Narrative Theory and SCT to support psychological well-being in T1D. Narrative Theory facilitates meaning-making and emotional processing, while SCT fosters behavior change through modeled experiences. Practically, DST is a culturally adaptable, low-cost, and scalable intervention that promotes peer support and emotional resilience—especially critical in resource-limited settings.

Therefore, the specific impact of reflecting on peer-created DST videos on key psychosocial outcomes—such as diabetes distress, burnout, and depressive symptoms—among individuals with T1D remains largely unexplored. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of DST in improving psychological outcomes in individuals with T1D in Iran.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a two-group, parallel, single-blind randomized controlled trial (RCT) with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The trial was conducted between July 2023 and May 2024 in Iran. To maintain objectivity in the analysis, data analysts were blinded to group allocations and were provided only coded data.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Isfahan, one of Iran’s largest metropolitan areas, recognized for its diverse urban population and comprehensive primary healthcare infrastructure. This metropolitan area encompasses various urban districts, which may present differences in socio-economic characteristics.

To ensure representativeness across these diverse urban areas and to select the ten study centers, a multi-stage random sampling approach was employed. Comprehensive health service centers in Isfahan are administered under two main networks: Health Center No. 1 (comprising 30 centers serving northern and northwestern districts) and Health Center No. 2 (comprising 31 centers serving southern and southeastern districts). These two networks generally correspond to distinct geographical regions within the city, encompassing a range of socio-economic strata.

The selection of the ten study centers proceeded as follows: Initially, a random choice was made between Health Center No. 1 and Health Center No. 2 using a coin toss. Once a main Health Center network was selected (e.g., Health Center No. 1), one of its affiliated comprehensive health service centers was chosen by drawing lots from a list of all centers under that network. This process continued, alternating the initial coin toss for the main Health Center network, until a total of ten centers were selected, comprising five centers from Health Center No. 1’s network and five from Health Center No. 2’s network. This multi-stage random selection process aimed to achieve a balanced representation of centers across the city’s diverse areas, thereby helping to distribute potential unmeasured confounding factors, including socio-economic status, across the study groups.

Participants

The study involved two groups of individuals with T1D: study participants and study storytellers.

Study Participants were individuals diagnosed with T1D who had an electronic health record at selected health service centers of a city in Iran. Inclusion criteria included being at least 18 years old and having a T1D diagnosis for a minimum of six months. Participants were required to have access to and basic familiarity with the Telegram messenger platform, as it was used for study communication. If participants were not familiar with Telegram Messenger, they were provided with training to ensure they could effectively use the platform. Additionally, participants were required to be free of cognitive, visual, or motor sensory impairments, including severe depressive symptoms (as indicated by the use of mental health medications), intellectual disabilities, or other significant health conditions, as documented in their medical records. Exclusion criteria included unwillingness to continue participation, hospitalization, experiencing a stressful event during the trial, missing more than two DST sessions, or failing to respond to telephone follow-ups more than twice.

Study storytellers were volunteers who were not part of the control or intervention groups and were not involved in the study’s data collection or intervention process. Their sole responsibility was creating the DST videos used in the study. Study storytellers met the same inclusion criteria as the participants, with additional requirements of a history of well-managed HbA1c levels (defined as < 7% for at least one year), confirmed by clinical records. These records were reviewed to confirm well-managed HbA1c levels (defined as < 7%) for at least one year prior to selection, along with self-reports of effective diabetes self-management (see Table 1).

Sampling and recruitment

To select study participants, a systematic approach was employed. In collaboration with the Center Manager, a list of 643 individuals with T1D was compiled from the integrated electronic Health System database (SIB). Among these, 356 individuals met the initial eligibility criteria. The list was stratified to reflect the proportional allocation of individuals with T1D at each health center, ensuring that the sample represented the total number of individuals with T1D across the selected centers. Eligible participants were identified through a review of SIB charts. Phone calls were then made to inform individuals of the study, including details on confidentiality and their right to withdraw at any time. A total of 356 phone calls were made. Individuals who verbally agreed to participate and demonstrated access to and basic familiarity with Telegram Messenger were included. The final target sample of 60 participants was achieved through this process. Following recruitment, in-person meetings were arranged at locations preferred by the participants, such as healthcare centers or other convenient sites. During these meetings, informed written consent was obtained prior to proceeding with data collection.

Storytellers for the study were initially drawn from participants in previous studies who had expressed interest in participating in future research. They were all members of the Gabric Diabetes Association and had been designated as Diabetes Ambassadors due to their successful disease control. These potential storytellers were reviewed based on inclusion criteria and conducted interviews to explain the study’s objectives, the process of creating DST videos, and the commitment required. In total, eight potential storytellers were identified, of whom four agreed to share their experiences through DST. These four storytellers contributed stories on four distinct themes related to their lived experience with T1D, ensuring a range of perspectives on diabetes self-management.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was determined using G*Power software. The calculation was based on findings from a previous relevant study, which reported an effect size of 1.06 [38], with a 95% confidence level and a desired statistical power of at least 80%. According to these parameters, a minimum of 25 participants per group was required. To account for potential participant dropout and to maintain adequate power, an anticipated attrition rate of 20% was included in the calculation. Consequently, the final sample size was increased to 30 participants per group, resulting in a total of 60 participants for the study.

Random allocation

For random allocation, the minimization method was employed using MinimPy software, an open-source tool specifically designed for implementing this advanced randomization technique in clinical trials [39]. The minimization method is an adaptive allocation strategy that ensures treatment groups remain balanced across key prognostic factors, such as age, sex, and disease severity. This method dynamically adjusts allocation to minimize imbalances between groups, enhancing the reliability and validity of the study’s results. The MinimPy program calculates the optimal group assignment for each new participant to maintain balance across key prognostic factors, thereby reducing biases and enhancing the internal validity of trial results. This approach is particularly beneficial in smaller studies, where random imbalances in group allocation could otherwise skew outcomes, ensuring that the treatment groups remain comparable throughout the trial [40].

In this study, MinimPy was used to ensure that both the intervention and control groups were as similar as possible in terms of key characteristics, thereby improving the rigor and reliability of the findings. Variables such as age and sex were entered into the software, and participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group, with 30 participants in each. However, two participants from each group withdrew due to lack of interest, resulting in a final sample of 56 participants, with 28 in the intervention group and 28 in the control group. This methodical approach to randomization was critical in minimizing potential confounding variables, ensuring that the observed effects could be more confidently attributed to the intervention itself (See Fig. 1).

Intervention details

Process of creating DST videos

The development of the DST intervention was guided by Narrative Theory, which emphasizes storytelling as a means of meaning-making, emotional processing, and fostering social connections [19], and SCT, which posits that individuals learn behaviors by observing role models [20]. Narrative Theory informed the selection of four core themes—resilience, managing emotions, interpersonal relationships, and burnout—drawn from phenomenological studies on T1D experiences [41, 42], ensuring alignment with participants’ emotional and psychosocial challenges.

SCT guided the recruitment of storytellers with well-managed T1D (HbA1c < 7%) as role models to demonstrate effective coping strategies and promote self-efficacy and motivation.

The intervention was developed using a participatory design approach, involving consultations with T1D patients and diabetes educators to ensure cultural relevance and feasibility in Iran’s resource-limited context. Storytellers received training on video creation, including guidance on content (e.g., personal experiences, management strategies), format (3–5-minute videos), and use of technical tools (e.g., InShot application).

A psychiatric nurse and the research team provided ongoing support to ensure narrative clarity and emotional safety. Each video underwent three rounds of iterative review and revision by the research team, focusing on technical quality, relevance, and ethical compliance. This process also functioned as a form of preliminary testing and refinement to enhance the intervention’s effectiveness.

Intervention implementation

Both intervention and control groups received standard routine diabetes care, including regular monthly physician visits and referrals to specialists (e.g., nutritionists, nurses, mental health professionals) as needed. The intervention group also received the DST intervention, which consisted of four two-hour virtual group sessions. To manage the meeting duration and ensure active participation, the intervention group was divided into two subgroups, each with 15 participants. This division allowed sufficient time for all participants to share their reflections without feeling rushed. The virtual group sessions were held online via the Triple-B academic platform over a four-week period, with sessions scheduled at times based on participants’ preferences (morning and afternoon). Participants were informed in advance that the meetings would be recorded, with confidentiality and safety assured.

The diverse participant groups, in terms of age and sex, contributed to a wide range of shared experiences. To accommodate their schedules, sessions were offered in both morning and afternoon, and participants were informed that meetings were recorded to ensure confidentiality. Prior to the intervention, participants received training on using the Triple-B academic platform and navigating the online environment. To address any potential technical difficulties, a designated research assistant was available throughout the intervention period to provide technical support via phone, ensuring that no participant was excluded due to technical issues.

Despite the 15-participant group size (due to resource and logistical constraints), every effort was made to maximize session effectiveness. Each participant was allocated approximately 10 min to share their reflections during meeting. However, individual sharing times varied, with many participants speaking for shorter durations, which allowed ample time for other session components. To encourage engagement and manage the discussion effectively, short breaks (5–10 min) were incorporated into the session schedule, allowing participants to rest and stay focused. Each session has longed near 2-hour.

At the start of each session, the study team replayed the videos to ensure that all participants had reviewed the content. During the sessions, participants engaged in discussions and exchanged opinions. A research team psychiatric nurse moderated these discussions, using guided questions (e.g., ‘Did you have similar experiences?’ and ‘How did you address these issues?‘) to encourage reflection, personal sharing, and discussion of management strategies.

Following the group discussions, the study team provided a summary of the session’s content and offered scientific insights related to the topic. These insights, drawn from reliable sources and approved by experts, reinforced the practical applications of the stories and helped participants integrate the information into their diabetes management strategies.

The control group received only standard routine T1D care, including continuous monitoring, personalized treatment adjustments, and access to professional support for blood glucose control and emerging diabetes-related issues.

Feasibility and acceptability

The feasibility of the DST intervention was supported by several key factors. The participatory design approach ensured cultural relevance and contextual appropriateness. Comprehensive training and ongoing support enabled storytellers to produce meaningful and resonant video content. The use of accessible and familiar technologies (InShot, Telegram, Triple-B) facilitated seamless implementation.

Participant engagement further supported feasibility, with a high retention rate in the intervention group—93.3% of participants completed the intervention, with only 2 out of 30 withdrawing. Virtual group sessions were delivered without significant technical issues, reinforcing the practicality of the remote delivery format. Additionally, participants expressed a desire to continue meeting after the study concluded, indicating strong acceptability of the DST intervention.

Intervention fidelity

To ensure fidelity, several measures were implemented. All moderators, including the psychiatric nurse, completed a two-day structured training program covering session protocols, guided discussion techniques, and methods for fostering a safe, supportive environment. A standardized session manual was developed to maintain uniformity across both subgroups, outlining the flow of each session: video playback, participant reflection, guided discussion, and summary insights. Fidelity was monitored by an external ethics committee observer who attended two randomly selected sessions. Additionally, participant engagement with the videos was reinforced through regular reminders sent via Telegram. These strategies ensured adherence to the intervention protocol and supported consistent delivery across groups.

Monitoring and safety

A comprehensive monitoring system ensured the safety of participants and storytellers. During the informed consent process, participants were informed about potential risks, including psychological impacts of DST. They were encouraged to report discomfort to a designated liaison (a master’s level nurse), who consulted with a psychiatric nurse for support. Group sessions were moderated to minimize distress, and no serious adverse events were reported, indicating the intervention was well-tolerated.

Data collection

The study survey, incorporating valid and reliable measures, was developed using Google Forms, which is user-friendly and accessible in Iran. Google Forms uses encrypted data storage and secure access controls, limiting access to authorized individuals, to protect sensitive health information. This platform streamlined survey creation, distribution, and data management and ensured participant confidentiality [43]. On average, participants took 30 min to complete the questionnaires.

Study measures

The research team developed a socio-demographic and clinical questionnaire to collect data on participants’ sex, age, occupation, marital status, education level, HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and duration of diabetes. Diabetes distress was assessed using the Diabetes Distress Scale-17 (DDS-17), developed by Polonsky and colleagues. This scale includes 17 items that evaluate emotional distress related to diabetes, with responses scored on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (no problem) to 6 (serious problem). The DDS-17 encompasses four subscales: (1) emotional burden, (2) physician-related distress, (3) regimen-related distress and (4) interpersonal distress. The Persian version of the DDS-17 has demonstrated excellent internal reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.924 [44]. In this study, the reliability of the DDS-17 was also assessed, yielding Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92.

Diabetes burnout was assessed using the Diabetes Burnout Scale (DBS) developed by Abdoli and colleagues in 2021. The DBS includes 12 items measuring exhaustion, detachment, and loss of control. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree) [45]. The Persian version of the DBS demonstrated strong internal reliability in Iran, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.813 [46]. In this study, the reliability of the questionnaire was assessed as 0.85.

The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), widely used to assess the severity of depression and screen for depressive symptoms, was used in this study. BDI consists of 21 items, with responses scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3 [47]. The BDI has demonstrated sufficient reliability across various populations, with psychometric properties commonly reported as acceptable (r = 0.93). The Persian version of the BDI has shown high reliability and is considered a robust instrument for measuring depression in the Iranian population (r = 0.91) [48]. In this study, the reliability of the BDI was assessed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86.

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 was used for data entry and statistical analysis. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test confirmed that all continuous variables followed a normal distribution within each group (p > 0.05). Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, were calculated. Independent Samples T-Test and Chi-square test were used to compare numerical and categorical socio-demographic variables between the two groups. To assess differences in diabetes distress, diabetes burnout, and depressive symptoms between the two groups at baseline, immediately post-intervention, and one-month post-intervention, Independent Samples T-Test was used. Repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to examine changes in mean scores for diabetes distress, diabetes burnout, and depressive symptoms within each group across the study’s duration—before, immediately post, and one-month post- intervention.

Results

This study initially included 60 participants who were randomly assigned to either the control or intervention group, with 30 participants in each group. During the study, four participants withdrew: two from the control group and two from the intervention group, due to personal reasons, leading to post-test and follow-up losses. This resulted in a dropout rate of 6.6%. Consequently, the final analysis included 28 participants in each group (see Fig. 1).

Descriptive socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

Participant ages ranged from 18 to 52 years. The majority were female (67.9%), and 55.4% were single. There were no significant differences between the groups in age, sex, education, diabetes duration, FGP level, or HbA1c level (see Table 2).

Effects of DST on depressive symptoms

At baseline, there were no significant differences in depressive symptoms scores between DST and control groups (P > 0.05 ) (Table 7). However, significant differences were observed in mean depressive symptoms scores between the DST and control groups immediately post-intervention (P < 0.001, effect size = 0.72) and one-month post-intervention (P < 0.001) with a large effect size (2.6) (Table 7).

Discussion

The current study investigated the impact of peer-created DST videos on diabetes distress, diabetes burnout, and depressive symptoms among individuals with T1D. The results indicate that peer-created DST videos have a significant positive effect on these psychological outcomes. This highlights their potential as an effective intervention for improving mental health in diabetes management.

Impact on diabetes distress

Participants engaging in DST showed significant reductions in total diabetes distress and its subscales. Notably, a substantial reduction was observed in emotional burden subscale [49]. This finding suggests that DST facilitates an environment where individuals can process and express their emotional experiences, allowing for greater self-reflection and deeper understanding of their emotions. The cathartic nature of storytelling appears to alleviate the psychological burden associated with managing a chronic condition like diabetes. This process enables participants to confront their distress and better navigate the emotional challenges of diabetes. This supports the claim that storytelling effectively manages psychological well-being in this context [27, 30, 50].

The study also highlighted a significant decrease in regimen distress, which relates to feelings of failure and frustration in managing diabetes [49]. This is a common challenge for individuals with chronic conditions, as complex treatment regimens often lead to emotional strain and diminished self-efficacy [1]. The observed reduction in regimen distress among DST participants implies that storytelling interventions offer a meaningful outlet for participants to share their experiences, reflect on their struggles, and develop more effective coping strategies. By learning from others’ experiences, participants are likely able to gain new perspectives on managing their condition, which fosters resilience and a sense of shared experience [50, 51]. This aligns with the notion that peer-supported interventions, such as DST, can empower individuals to navigate the complexities of diabetes self-management by cultivating a supportive environment of shared knowledge and solidarity.

The findings also demonstrated a notable decrease in physician distress among DST participants, which is characterized by concerns about receiving adequate healthcare and quality support from the healthcare system [49]. The reduction in physician-related distress observed in this study suggests that DST provides a unique platform for individuals to articulate their healthcare-related concerns and experiences. This narrative exchange may improve the communication between patients and healthcare providers, enhancing mutual understanding and fostering a more empathetic, supportive therapeutic relationship. By sharing their stories, participants may feel empowered to engage more actively with healthcare providers, which in turn could lead to improved healthcare outcomes [28, 52].

Moreover, DST participants reported a decrease in interpersonal distress, which refers to a lack of support and understanding from family and friends regarding the challenges of living with diabetes [53]. The reduction in interpersonal distress highlights that DST facilitates improved communication and empathy within interpersonal relationships [54]. Through storytelling, individuals can provide insight into the psychological and emotional burdens of diabetes, encouraging better support and understanding from their social circles. By fostering emotional support from peers and loved ones, DST not only addresses the individual’s psychological well-being but also strengthens the social support networks that are crucial for coping with the mental, emotional, and physical challenges of diabetes [54, 55].

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research examining the acceptability and perceived effectiveness of DST interventions. For instance, a study evaluating a group-based, facilitated digital storytelling intervention for type 2 diabetes (T2D) self-management among rural Latino patients found that participants reported significant improvements in confidence, motivation, and behavioral intentions for T2D self-management. This study demonstrated that DST can have a powerful impact on individuals’ sense of agency in managing their condition, reinforcing the notion that peer-driven interventions can significantly enhance diabetes self-management [56].

Impact on diabetes burnout

The study revealed a significant reduction in burnout and its dimensions among participants engaged in DST. Our findings demonstrate a notable decrease in exhaustion resulting from the demands of diabetes care [45, 57]. This reduction in exhaustion suggests that DST provides an important platform for individuals to share their experiences, challenges, and emotions related to diabetes, helping alleviate the overwhelming fatigue often associated with burnout [58].

Additionally, the study observed a significant decrease in detachment among participants in the DST group. The reduction in detachment suggests that DST interventions help individuals reconnect with their diabetes management routines. By providing a space for sharing and reflecting on personal experiences, DST helps restore a sense of control and motivation, reducing feelings of apathy and avoidance of self-care activities [50, 59–61].

A scoping review by Chang et al. explored the psychosocial benefits of DST interventions for older adults and people with dementia, highlighting DST’s potential to improve psychosocial well-being. By allowing individuals to share their life stories through digital media, DST can foster social connection, combat isolation, and promote mental health [62].

Impact on depressive symptoms

Participants engaged in DST experienced significantly greater reductions in depressive symptom scores compared to the control group. This suggests that DST can be an effective intervention for alleviating psychological symptoms in individuals with diabetes. DST has been shown to enhance social connectedness [63], offering a platform for individuals to create and share personal narratives through digital media. This process of storytelling facilitates catharsis and empowerment [27, 64], which can act as a protective factor against depressive symptoms [65]. By enabling participants to articulate their experiences and connect with others, DST helps mitigate the emotional challenges associated with diabetes management [21].

Furthermore, the study by Yuan et al. (2024) highlighted how digital storytelling supports young adults with diabetes by addressing diabetes-related stigma and fostering resilience. Yuan et al.‘s findings suggest that DST allows participants to reinterpret their experiences of diabetes in a more positive light, promoting better emotional well-being and coping strategies, which aligns with the benefits observed in our study [66].

Strengths and limitations

This study provides valuable insights into the impact of digital storytelling (DST) on psychological well-being, specifically addressing diabetes distress, burnout, and depressive symptoms. The use of a standardized 4-week, 2-hour session intervention with peer-created DST videos allowed for a well-structured and replicable approach. By evaluating outcomes at multiple time points—before, immediately post-intervention, and one month post-intervention—the study offers a comprehensive assessment of both short-term and potential longer-term effects. Furthermore, the focus on a real-world context in Iran highlights the potential of DST as a cost-effective intervention for creating social support in populations with limited access to traditional resources such as support groups and diabetes education programs.

Several limitations must be considered when interpreting the findings. The small sample size of 56 participants limits the generalizability of the results and may affect the detection of smaller but potentially meaningful effects. Also this size increases the risk of both Type I and Type II errors. Additionally, the 16 DST videos created by only four storytellers may not capture the full diversity of experiences among individuals with diabetes, potentially reducing the intervention’s relevance to a broader population. A larger and more varied pool of videos could enhance the intervention’s effectiveness.

The study’s one-month follow-up period limits understanding of the long-term sustainability of the observed improvements. This short duration means that while we observed promising immediate effects, we cannot make strong claims about the persistence or durability of these benefits over an extended period. Therefore, the long-term efficacy and sustained impact of the intervention remain to be determined. Future research with extended follow-up periods is essential to comprehensively assess the lasting effects of this intervention.

The single-blind design, in which participants were aware of their group allocation, introduces the potential for bias in self-reported outcomes. The study’s focus on individuals with T1D limits its applicability to other types of diabetes. Furthermore, potential confounding factors, such as participant demographics or diabetes management practices, were not controlled for. Additionally, the study was conducted in Iran, where access to social support resources and diabetes education programs is limited compared to developed countries, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to populations in regions with more readily available resources.

Cultural, socioeconomic, and contextual factors may influence the success of DST interventions, and tailoring DST to the unique needs of diverse communities could increase its effectiveness. Lastly, the risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons should be acknowledged. Future research should consider adjusting for multiple comparisons to enhance the reliability of findings.

Implications for research, practice, and policy

Despite the study’s limitations, its findings have important implications for research, practice, and policy. The study contributes to the growing body of literature on narrative-based interventions, particularly peer-created DST, highlighting its potential to enhance emotional well-being and reduce psychological distress. DST provides a platform for individuals to share personal experiences, fostering emotional processing, resilience, and self-reflection. This study adds a new dimension to our understanding of how digital media can support mental health in chronic illness care, offering a novel approach to managing the psychological challenges faced by individuals with diabetes.

The results suggest that integrating DST into diabetes care programs could offer substantial benefits, including enhancing emotional resilience and reduced isolation. Healthcare providers should consider incorporating DST into patient education and support strategies to improve emotional well-being and self-management. Integrating DST into routine care could address both the physical and psychological aspects of diabetes management.

The study also advocates for the inclusion of psychosocial support services in diabetes care policies. Policymakers should invest in innovative interventions like DST to meet the mental health needs of individuals with chronic conditions. Resources should be allocated to develop and support programs that incorporate DST, making them accessible to diverse populations. Community organizations can also play a critical role by hosting storytelling workshops and creating spaces for sharing experiences. This fosters a sense of community and enhances quality of life for individuals with diabetes.

Conclusion

The results of this study highlight the promise of DST as an effective intervention for improving psychological well-being in individuals with T1D. The findings demonstrate that DST can significantly alleviate distress, burnout, and depressive symptoms by providing a platform for individuals to express and process their diabetes-related experiences and challenges. This innovative approach not only supports emotional expression but also promotes self-reflection and resilience, contributing to improved mental health outcomes.

DST represents a promising model for future mental health promotion, particularly in chronic illness management. By engaging individuals in meaningful narratives and facilitating shared experiences, DST offers valuable complements to traditional diabetes care strategies. However, further research with larger sample sizes, diverse populations, and longer follow-up periods is essential to fully assess the long-term effects and broader applicability of DST interventions. Continued investigation will be crucial in validating and expanding these findings, ultimately enhancing the mental health and well-being of individuals living with T1D.
