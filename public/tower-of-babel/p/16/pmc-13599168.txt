Assessing effectiveness of HOSPITAL score and LACE index for predicting 30-day readmissions in a tertiary care hospital in Pakistan: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Manzar Abbas, Hajra Arshad, Muhammad Umar Mahar, Javerya Hassan, Izza Tahir, Namra Aziz, Lena Jafri, Mehmood Riaz, Aysha Almas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnplanned hospital readmissions not only negatively impact patients’ quality of life but also pose a substantial burden on both the hospital and the individuals. Hence, there is a growing need to identify and prevent avoidable readmissions, aiming to reduce mortality and enhance the quality of care. Background

Unplanned hospital readmissions adversely affect the quality of life of patients along with being a burden on the hospital and the patient. Studies conducted in the United States (US) recruiting Medicare beneficiaries reported a readmission rate of 19.6% after 30 days and 34.0% after 90 days, with higher rates in teaching hospitals and those with < 100 beds [1, 2]. Readmissions are increasingly being used as an indicator of quality of care. According to a one-year observational study of 1000 general care patients who were readmitted within 30 days of their discharge, 25% of readmissions are potentially avoidable [3].

In an LMIC like Pakistan, chronic diseases, both communicable and non-communicable, significantly contribute to the double burden of diseases. Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) make up 21%, and respiratory diseases have a contribution of 7% of all the diseases in Pakistan [4, 5]. Unlike in LMICs, the statistics from the US suggest that hospital readmission rates for targeted conditions, such as pneumonia, heart failure, and acute myocardial infarction, have been decreasing over the years and only accounted for 17.8% of all 30-day readmissions in 2015 [6]. The literature further showed that the percentage of patients readmitted to Internal Medicine departments within 30 days after discharge ranges from 8.5% to 14% [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, in Pakistan, only one study reported a 30-day readmission rate of 15.3% due to advanced age, multi-morbidities, persistence of symptoms, and nosocomial infections [11].

There is an unmet need for basic health requirements in an LMIC like Pakistan, which is often due to a lack of healthcare facilities. Studies have shown several factors influencing hospital readmissions including demographics, characteristics of index admission, discharge day (Friday/weekend), recurrence of symptoms, decision-making regarding readmission, early discharge, lack of discussions about care goals among patients with serious illnesses, inability to follow-up, and gender of the treating physician [3, 12, 13]. There is a lack of readmission prediction tools that are validated on our population in Pakistan; thus, we aim to validate the LACE index and HOSPITAL score, which takes into account these factors, in LMICs [14, 15]. LACE refers to Length of stay, Acuity of the admission, Comorbidity of the patient (measured with the Charlson comorbidity index score), and Emergency department visits (measured as the number of visits in the six months before admission). HOSPITAL score refers to Hemoglobin at discharge, discharge from an Oncology service, Sodium level at discharge, Procedure during index admission, Index Type of admission, number of Admissions during the last 12 months, and Length of stay. Preventing these readmissions can lead to decreased mortality and improvement in the quality of care [16].

This study was conducted to determine if the HOSPITAL score and LACE index are fair predictors of 30-day readmission in adult patients admitted to the Internal Medicine Service at a tertiary care center in Pakistan, to validate the score in an LMIC.

Methods

Construction and content

This is a retrospective cohort study. This study was conducted on all patients aged 18 and above who were admitted to the internal medicine service through the emergency room or electively over 5 years (2016–2020) at the Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH), Karachi, Pakistan. Patients who had died during the index admission, patients admitted outside the IM department; outlier > 100 days in LOS were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of data acquisition and the total number of patients at each stage of data cleaning/analysis

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical clearance (2021-7023-20010) was received from the Aga Khan University Ethics Review Committee (Institutional Review Board) before conducting the study. Informed consent was not required for the study as the study involved retrospective analysis using de-identified patient data from medical records (only the principal investigator could identify the patients for validation purposes).

Primary outcome variable was readmission to the hospital within 30 days (of discharge or LAMA [Leave against medical advice]).

Thirty-day readmission was defined as ‘readmissions within 30 days of discharge from the index (i.e., initial) admission’ [5].

Independent variables were the LACE index and HOSPITAL score.

LACE index refers to Length of stay, Acuity of the admission, Comorbidity of the patient (measured with the Charlson comorbidity index score), and Emergency department visits (measured as the number of visits in the six months before admission). LACE indexes range from 0 to 19, with an expected probability for readmission of 0 to 43.7% based on the initial validation study of the LACE score. It was classified as a score of 0–4 = Low; 5–9 = Moderate; and a score of ≥ 10 = High risk of readmission [14].

CCI predicts the mortality for a patient who may have a range of concurrent conditions, such as heart disease, AIDS, or cancer. A score of zero means that no comorbidities were found: the higher the score, the higher the predicted mortality rate [17, 18].

HOSPITAL score refers to Hemoglobin at discharge, discharge from an Oncology service, Sodium level at discharge, Procedure during index admission, Index Type of admission, number of Admissions during the last 12 months, and Length of stay. HOSPITAL scores of 0–4 points are classified as low risk for readmission (5%), 5–6 points as intermediate risk (10%), and 7 or more points as high risk (20%) based on the initial validation study of the HOSPITAL score [15].

Data acquisition

Electronic data was extracted from the following sources: (i) Health Information Management System (HIMS) for diagnosis, demographics, health coverage plan, length of stay, procedures, and mortality (on May 6, 2022). (ii) Laboratory data was acquired from information technology and through the MyPatient (internal servers of AKU health record system) (on June 14, 2022). (iii) Patient Business and Services Department (PBSD) for health coverage plan, cost of bed, and acuity level (on September 13, 2022). Data from internal medicine, infectious diseases, and endocrinology departments were combined into a single master sheet. The patient admission number was used as a unique factor to combine all this data.

Disease categorization

Patient co-morbid and primary diagnosis for each admission were documented in the original dataset using ICD-9-CM coding up to 2020 and ICD-10 coding thereafter. We organized related ICD codes into broader disease categories based on ICD coding, which were used in the subsequent analyses. A list of the ICD codes and their corresponding categories can be found in Supplement 1.

CCI calculations

The CCI includes a set of 19 disease categories in its scoring criteria. Thus, to incorporate the CCI into the analysis, the ICD Codes were further categorized to create separate columns for the 19 comorbidities included in the CCI. The Enhanced ICD-9-CM codes were used in the calculation of the CCI instead of Deyo’s ICD-9-CM [20]. A patient’s CCI score was determined by adding up points assigned to each of their comorbid conditions, with each condition’s points based on the CCI’s specific weighting.

30-day readmission

A Python script was made to identify the first occurrence of admission as an index admission (Supplement 2 Information Snippet 1). Then, the script tried to find the second occurrence of admission (if it was available in the dataset) and calculated the duration of readmission (from the date of discharge of the index admission to the date of admission of the second occurrence). If that duration was within 30-days, the script made a new column labeling it as “Readmitted”; otherwise, it labeled it as “Not Readmitted”. Those who were labeled as “Readmitted” were excluded in the next cycle of lookups for index admission. All those patients who died during the index admission (n = 3106) were excluded from the analysis.

Laboratory values

The laboratory dataset included all investigations performed during the hospital stay. Wherever possible, the most recent value on the day of discharge for each parameter was included in the analysis. If a particular lab parameter was not available for the discharge date, a value, up to a maximum of 5 days before the discharge date, was used instead in the subsequent analysis. Patients with missing laboratory data while calculating only the HOSPITAL score (Hemoglobin and Sodium) (about 21%) were excluded from the analyses, and no imputation process was applied in such cases. Details of data extraction and stepwise exclusion of cases are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data analysis

Python scripts were written to perform the above-mentioned data aggregation, transformations, filtering, and groupings needed to prepare the dataset for the analysis. All subsequent descriptive and inferential statistics were then performed using R Statistical Software (v4.3.2; R Core Team 2023) [21].

The normality of the continuous variables was checked with the Anderson-Darling test as the total dataset was beyond the limitation of the Shapiro-Wilk test [5000 cases). Most of the variables in our dataset were normally distributed (p > 0.05). To reduce selection bias, only the first eligible hospitalization per patient was considered as the index admission, and subsequent admissions were not reused as new index events. Patients who died during the index admission were excluded to ensure adequate follow-up for assessing 30-day readmissions.

Mean and standard deviation are reported for quantitative variables. Frequency and percentages are for qualitative variables. T-test and chi-square were conducted to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively, in readmitted vs. not readmitted patients within 30 days.

Patients with HOSPITAL score > 5 and LACE score > 10 were considered high risk for readmission [15, 22]. ORs (95% CI) were reported using binary logistic regression. We used adjustments and created three different models to examine the independent effect of the LACE index or HOSPITAL score on predicting readmission. The area under the curve (AUC) for HOSPITAL score > 5 and LACE score > 10 was calculated using the ROC method. Brier score and C-statistic were also calculated for the discriminatory power (predictive probability) of the ROC curve. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Z-test was used to compare the discriminative abilities of the LACE index and the HOSPITAL score.

Discussion

This study on 35,496 adult patients reports that 8.0% (2,822) were readmitted within 30 days of index admission. We found that the LACE score and HOSPITAL score in our population showed comparable results with higher income countries. Interestingly, urinary tract infections and pyelonephritis emerged as significant causes of readmission, underscoring the importance of targeted interventions for this condition to avoid preventable readmission, especially in older men. This finding could be associated with the hot temperature of Karachi, Pakistan.

The readmission rate in Internal Medicine departments within 30 days after discharge ranges from 8.5% to 14% in European countries [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, a higher number of readmissions, i.e., 15.3% (75 out of 491 patients), has been reported in a single center study from Pakistan [23]. Our study showed a lower rate of readmission compared to all other studies, i.e., 8.0% (2822 out of 35,496). Factors contributing to lower readmission could be a multidisciplinary approach to patient management and strict compliance with medical management protocols at this center. Alternatively, our estimates could be low as we did not follow patients readmitted to other hospitals after having index admission at AKUH or those who died after initial discharge.

The LACE index was derived and validated by Carl et al. to predict early death or unplanned readmission within 30 days after discharge [14]. LACE index is a good discriminator for 30-day readmission in our population with a C-statistics of 0.657, comparable to the initial validation study by Carl et al., which reported a C-statistics of 0.684 and high accuracy [14]. Similar results have been reported by Vasuki et al. with the LACE index in predicting 30-day readmission in patients with MI [24]. A systematic review reported that the LACE index showed favourable performance overall in predicting 30-day readmission, especially in patients with chronic conditions [25]. On the contrary, some studies showed that heart failure patients showed poor performance of the LACE index with a C-statistics of 0.551 in predicting 30-day readmission [26]. The reason for this could be that these figures are specific to heart failure patients who might be at higher risk of dying and have multiple comorbidities and might not get readmitted.

HOSPITAL score, developed and validated by Donze et al. at an academic medical center in Boston, predicts potentially avoidable 30-day readmission with a good discrimination power with a C-statistics of 0.64 [15]. Our results also show C-statistics of 0.64, indicating that the model performed well in predicting 30-day readmission. Another single-center study conducted in the US reported a C-statistic of 0.75 in their patient population, indicating good discrimination for hospital readmission [22]. However, other studies conducted on heart failure and geriatric patients predict 30-day readmission showed C-statistics of 0.59, indicating poor discrimination ability of the score [26, 27]. Hence, our findings are similar to what is reported in the literature [15].

Another important factor is that the HOSPITAL score does not include data regarding patient comorbidities, which is a significant factor in readmission, especially in LMICs where there is a double burden of diseases. LACE index and HOSPITAL score performed well in our population. The results were comparable to the initial validation study, but the latter still had higher C-statistics [14]. However, LACE is easier to calculate but requires CCI, which is notably challenging in retrospective studies. LACE also does not require laboratory data and is a quick and efficient way of predicting avoidable readmissions. The mean CCI in our readmitted population was 2.5, significantly higher than that in the validation study (Mean CCI = 0.5) [14], emphasizing the high number of comorbid and double burden of diseases.

Interestingly, socioeconomic factors, like the ability to pay hospital bills out of pocket or being admitted to a low-cost bed, seem to impact the risk of readmission. Although commonly used tools like LACE and HOSPITAL scores do not account for income, our findings suggest that lower-income patients exhibit a different pattern in their healthcare utilization. These patients are often less likely to be readmitted because of financial constraints and limited access to follow-up care. This is particularly relevant from an LMIC perspective, where a significant portion of the population faces economic challenges. Therefore, it is crucial to consider socioeconomic status when assessing patient readmission risks and to tailor post-discharge support to address the unique challenges faced by populations in LMICs. These findings may be particularly applicable to underserved regions of Pakistan or any other LMIC, where economic barriers and limited healthcare infrastructure may further exacerbate disparities in access to post-discharge care. Implementation of targeted interventions like the LACE index or the HOSPITAL score in these settings could help reduce readmissions.

Limitations

This is a single-center study, conducted in only one department (internal medicine) at a tertiary care hospital, and hence, findings cannot be generalized to other hospitals. Patients were not followed up for the readmissions within 30-days in other hospitals, other clinics, or those who died after index admission (this was a large dataset, and following up with such a large number of patients through phone calls is not cost-effective). Disease categorization was based on ICD-9-CM or ICD-10 codes, which can be subject to coding inaccuracies and variation among coders and over time.

While calculating the CCI, some of the comorbidities might have been disregarded during initial data entry into the HIMS, although our HIMS and ICD coding are very systematic and less likely to miss comorbid conditions. Unaccounted variables, such as frailty, functional status, or social support, were not available and could confound the relationship between risk scores and readmission. Patients with missing key laboratory data necessary for the HOSPITAL score were excluded, and no imputation was performed. This could introduce bias if patients with missing data differed systematically from those with complete data. Also, some patients might have died before potentially getting readmitted.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study contributes valuable insights into the performance of readmission prediction tools within the specific context of Pakistan, an LMIC with limited medical resources. LACE index and HOSPITAL score show fair prediction for readmission and hold potential for enhancing early detection and intervention strategies to mitigate readmissions. This study sheds light on the need for validating and applying predictive score calculations across other hospitals that consider the complex healthcare landscape of LMICs using a prospective cohort design.
