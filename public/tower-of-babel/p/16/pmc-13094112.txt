Comparison of clinical performance among the I-Gel Proseal Lma and Tulip Oropharyngeal Airway
Authors: O Çimen, F Gültop, O Kale, E Arık, B Yarsan, Z Baykal, J Ergil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSupraglottic airway (SGA) devices are widely used to facilitate ventilation during general anesthesia. However, comparative clinical data on different SGA designs remain limited. This study aimed to compare the placement characteristics, clinical performance, and complication profiles of three SGA devices: I-Gel™, the ProSeal™ LMA, and Tulip® Oropharyngeal Airway.MethodsThis prospective, observational study included 135 adult patients (ASA I–III) scheduled for elective surgery. Three patients were excluded before device placement, leaving 132 patients for analysis. Device selection was based on routine clinical judgment. Failed placements (n = 17) were included in the analysis of placement success and failure rates but excluded from performance-related outcomes such as insertion time and ease of insertion. The primary outcome was insertion time. Secondary outcomes included number of attempts, need for additional maneuvers, hemodynamic parameters, and perioperative complications.ResultsAmong 132 evaluated placements, the Tulip device demonstrated a significantly higher failure rate compared with I-Gel and ProSeal (25% vs. 5% and 5%, respectively; p = 0.004). Consequently, performance outcomes were analyzed in 115 successfully ventilated patients (I-Gel n = 38, ProSeal n = 38, Tulip n = 39). The Tulip group required a greater number of insertion attempts (p = 0.002). No significant differences were observed between the groups regarding insertion time, ease of insertion, need for maneuvers, hemodynamic variables, or adverse events (p > 0.05).ConclusionWhile ProSeal and I-Gel showed comparable clinical performance, the Tulip airway was associated with a higher rate of unsuccessful placement and required more insertion attempts. These findings suggest that device design and operator familiarity may influence placement success. Larger randomized studies are warranted to confirm these results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03728-9.Keywords: Supraglottic airway, I-Gel, ProSeal LMA, Tulip airway, Insertion success, Anesthesia

Introduction

A wide variety of supraglottic airway (SGA) devices are used in clinical practice to secure the airway. These devices are preferred for providing a secure airway in both spontaneously breathing and mechanically ventilated patients. Several airway management guidelines recommend supraglottic airway devices as rescue tools to facilitate ventilation and tracheal intubation in adults with difficult airways, including those from the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA), the Difficult Airway Society (DAS-UK), and Spanish multidisciplinary guidelines [1–3].

Various supraglottic airway devices have been developed to improve airway safety and reduce the risk of aspiration and trauma. However, comprehensive clinical studies comparing the safety and efficacy profiles of different SGA types are limited [4]. Differences in the characteristics and structures of SGA types can lead to variations in their clinical performance. Factors such as patient and surgical suitability, fasting status, ease of insertion, and risk of airway trauma influence the device selection and placement.

This study aimed to compare the clinical performance of three commonly used SGAs –I-Gel™, the ProSeal™ LMA, and Tulip® Oropharyngeal Airway – in terms of insertion time, ease of insertion, hemodynamic responses, and perioperative complications. We hypothesized that the insertion times and clinical performance of the three SGAs would be similar.

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Anesthesiology, Ankara Etlik City Hospital, Turkey, between June and October 2025. Approval was obtained from the Ankara Etlik City Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee on June 11, 2025 (protocol number: AEŞH-EK-2025-156). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE checklist. This observational study was not registered in any clinical research database. After obtaining ethics committee approval, all patients provided written informed consent.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Patients included in the study were ASA I, II, or III, aged 18 to 80 years, with a body mass index between 18 and 30, no known difficult airway anatomy, no history of difficult airway, scheduled for elective surgery with an SGA under general anesthesia, and expected to have an operating time of less than 4 h. All participants provided informed consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients requiring intubation due to surgical duration, lack of mental capacity, risk of aspiration, advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, history of head and neck surgery, cervical instability, pregnancy, mouth opening less than 2.5 cm, and those in whom SGA placement failed after the third attempt and an alternative method was used. These cases were recorded for placement success and failure analysis but were excluded from performance-related outcomes.

Patient grouping

The attending anesthesiologist selected the supraglottic airway device according to routine clinical practice, independent of the study protocol. Patients were assigned to groups based on the device used. Device selection was not randomized.

SGA placement procedure

Three different supraglottic airway devices were evaluated: the I-Gel®, which has a gel structure instead of an inflatable cuff; the ProSeal Tuoren® Double-Lumen LMA, which allows the s passage of a gastric tube to decompress the stomach; and the Tulip® Oropharyngeal Airway, which inserts a high-volume balloon into the oropharynx (Fig. 1). The attending anesthesiologist selected the supraglottic airway device based on routine clinical practice and independent of the study protocol. The SGA size was determined based on the patient’s weight according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The cuffed devices were inflated with the recommended volume of air.

I-Gel, ProSeal Double Lumen LMA, Tulip Oropharyngeal Airway, respectively

All supraglottic airway devices were inserted by anesthesiologists with a minimum of two years of clinical experience in airway management. Any airway adjustment maneuvers (adjusting the head and neck position, changing the insertion depth, jaw thrust, changing the device size, and repositioning the device) were recorded.

Anesthesia protocol

Anesthesia was administered and standardized according to the standard operating procedures of Ankara Etlik City Hospital. After preoxygenation, anesthesia was induced using intravenous propofol (3 mg/kg), rocuronium (0.5 mg/kg), and fentanyl (1 µg/kg). Adequate anesthesia depth was confirmed by the loss of the eyelash reflex and BIS monitoring. Balloon mask ventilation was maintained for 2 min, and the selected SGA was lubricated with a water-based lubricant. A standard insertion technique was used, with the device blindly advanced along the hard palate into the hypopharynx by an experienced anesthesiologist until resistance was encountered. Patients who did not achieve effective ventilation after a maximum of three insertion attempts were recorded, switched to alternative ventilation methods, and included in the analysis of placement success and failure rates but excluded from performance-related outcomes. The SGA cuff was inflated with the volume of air recommended by the manufacturer. The effectiveness of positive pressure ventilation was assessed using a composite clinical evaluation, including continuous end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂) monitoring, bilateral chest auscultation, appropriate cuff inflation according to manufacturer recommendations, and the absence of audible mouth leak sounds during ventilation.

Once effective ventilation was observed, the SGA was secured to the lip using adhesive tape. All patients received pressure-controlled and volume-assured ventilation. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane inhalation, maintaining MAC at 1. After device insertion, the attending anesthesiologist who inserted the supraglottic airway device to assess the ease of insertion (easy, moderate, difficult), and to indicate whether any additional manoeuvres were required. In accordance with standard clinical practice, the supraglottic airway device was removed at the end of surgery after reversal of neuromuscular blockade with sugammadex (2 mg/kg), once adequate spontaneous breathing was established. The timing of SGA removal was determined by the attending anesthesiologist based on the patient’s ability to maintain effective ventilation and adequate alertness. All adverse events were identified from intraoperative records and postoperative recovery room reports, and any device-related complications observed after SGA removal and in the recovery room were recorded.

Primary and secondary outcomes

All data were recorded by a member of the research team who was not involved in the clinical care. Demographic data included sex, age, body mass index, ASA class, and duration of anesthesia.

The primary outcome was the insertion time of the supraglottic airway devices. Secondary outcomes included ease of insertion, number of attempts at effective ventilation, overall success rate, postoperative hemodynamic variables, and airway device–related complications.

The primary outcome, time to insertion, was defined as the interval from the initial contact of the supraglottic airway device with the patient’s mouth to confirmation of effective ventilation. Insertion time was measured by an independent observer using a stopwatch according to a predefined standardized protocol.

Ease of insertion was assessed by the anesthesiologist performing the procedure and classified as easy, moderate, or difficult based on clinical practice. Easy insertion was defined as rapid and smooth placement on the first attempt without the need for additional maneuvers. Moderate insertion was defined as successful placement on the first attempt with limited additional maneuvers or prolonged insertion time. Difficult insertion was defined as placement requiring multiple maneuvers or repositioning of the device. The number of insertion attempts was recorded separately as an independent variable. No validated or standardized scale was used for the assessment.

Adverse events were predefined before the study. The recorded adverse events included cough, bronchospasm, hypoxia, gagging, sore throat, aspiration, hiccups, hoarseness, dental injury, dysphagia, dysphonia, soft tissue injury, and intraoral bleeding. Any additional adverse events were recorded under the category “other.” All adverse events were identified from intraoperative records and postoperative recovery room reports and were assessed by the anesthesiologist responsible for the patient’s anesthesia care.

Sample size calculation

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.2) based on pilot data from 10 patients per group.

Assuming mean insertion times of 9.0 ± 6.0 s, 10.0 ± 6.8 s, and 14.0 ± 7.6 s, the calculated effect size was Cohen’s f = 0.32 for the primary outcome (insertion time). A minimum of 99 evaluable patients was required to achieve 80% power at an α-level of 0.05. To account for anticipated exclusions and failed placements, recruitment was extended to 135 patients to ensure an adequate number of successfully ventilated cases for performance analysis.

Statistical analysis

The research data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). In descriptive statistics, categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, while continuous variables are presented as medians (IQR) or means ± standard deviations. Continuous variables were assessed for normal distribution using visual (histograms and probability plots) and analytical (Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests) methods.

The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons among three independent groups. Following a significant Kruskal–Wallis test, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction. A corrected significance threshold of p < 0.017 (0.05/3) was used for the three pairwise comparisons. In this study, a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were verified by an independent biostatistician.

Results

One hundred thirty-five patients who met the inclusion criteria were evaluated. In one patient, mask ventilation was inadequate, necessitating tracheal intubation. One patient was intubated due to surgical complications, and another was excluded from the study due to a prolonged operative time. In total, 132 patients were included in the performance analysis. Discussion

In this study, three different SGAs were compared in terms of insertion time and clinical performance. The failure rate (p = 0.004) and number of attempts required for effective ventilation (p = 0.002) were significantly higher in the Tulip group than in the I-Gel and ProSeal groups. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of insertion time, ease of insertion, manoeuvrability, injury, or adverse events.

The Tulip airway device differed significantly from the other SGAs in terms of design, materials, and insertion technique. This may be due to the Tulip cuff being inflated with 40–60 ml of air and being shorter than the more commonly used SGAs, which prevents adjustment of the insertion depth. The higher number of attempts in the Tulip group in this study may indicate the need for more precise positioning, manoeuvrability, and repositioning of the device.

In addition, the learning curve associated with the less frequent clinical use of the Tulip airway compared to other supraglottic airway devices may have contributed to the high failure rate observed in this study.

Studies on the Tulip airway device or similar Tulip brand supraglottic devices are limited, and there is no clear consensus. Robinson et al. [5] reported that the Tulip oropharyngeal airway provides highly effective ventilation immediately after mask ventilation following anesthesia induction. However, the results of our study do not align with this finding.

In a study by Zhang et al. [6], the GMA-Tulip device was tested in elderly patients in the lateral position, and its success rate was found to be high. However, the design differences between this device and the classic Tulip, as well as the absence of an inflatable cuff, make direct comparisons difficult.

In Robinson et al. [5], 80% of the cases required an additional jaw-thrust maneuver. In our study, no difference was found between the groups regarding the maneuver requirements among patients who were successfully ventilated.

Although no difference was found in insertion times among the patients who were successful in our study, the average insertion time for Tulip was longer (15 s), unlike the pilot study conducted by Imashuku et al. [7].

Several randomized trials and prospective reviews have reported shorter insertion times, higher insertion success rates, and easier insertion compared to ProSeal, while ProSeal sometimes provides better stability due to its structural properties (Singh et al. [8]; Sule et al. [9]). A meta-analysis by Maitra et al. [10] compared I-Gel and ProSeal, finding no significant difference in insertion time, initial success, or ease of insertion, though a trend toward easier insertion with I-Gel was noted. In our study, no difference was found between the clinical use of I-Gel and ProSeal.

The absence of a cuff balloon that requires inflation with the I-Gel makes it more practical for clinical use. In a study by Park et al. [11], I-Gel was found to be quicker and caused fewer complications (bleeding, sore throat, dysphagia) than LMA-P, providing advantages over LMA-P in adults under general anesthesia. In our study, no differences were observed between the devices in terms of adverse events.

No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of age, body mass index, or duration of anesthesia. However, the higher proportion of male patients and ASA II patients in the ProSeal group suggests that patients in this group may differ in airway anatomy, and that clinicians may select SGAs with which they have greater clinical experience for patients they perceive as challenging. The generally wider oropharyngeal structure in male patients may facilitate the placement of SGA. Controlled studies are needed to assess the impact of SGA clinical use by sex.

Comorbidities related to the ASA surgical risk score, such as obesity and obstructive sleep apnea, may be associated with difficult SGA placement. However, in this study, no differences were found in clinical practice in our ProSeal group, where only five (13.2%) patients were ASA I, and the remaining patients were ASA II or III. It is plausible that the ASA classification does not affect the SGA placement characteristics. The lack of a significant difference in postoperative hemodynamic parameters suggests that all devices provide similar cardiovascular stability.

Variations in SGA placement techniques, operator experience, and the need for additional maneuvers limit their clinical application. Because devices such as the I-Gel and ProSeal have been widely used for a long time, anesthesiologists are highly familiar with them. However, Tulip has been evaluated in only a few centers, generally in pilot and small studies. The lack of user experience may prevent the technical advantages of the device from being fully realized in clinical practice. Operator experience is a significant variable affecting outcomes, and standardizing this experience is difficult because of individual preferences and variations.

The prospectively collected data in our study provide useful information for device selection and support the notion that SGAs are safe and applicable options in anesthesia practice.

Previous studies have reported favorable ventilation outcomes with the Tulip airway, particularly when used by providers with limited experience in airway management. In a randomized crossover study, Shaikh et al. [12] demonstrated that the Tulip GT® airway provided more effective ventilation than a facemask and Guedel airway in anesthetized patients. However, in that study, a direct comparison with other second-generation supraglottic airway devices was not performed, and airway management was undertaken by providers with limited training in advanced airway techniques. In contrast, in the present study, the Tulip airway was found to be comparable to I-Gel™ and ProSeal™ LMA with respect to ease of insertion in successfully placed cases. Nevertheless, the Tulip airway was associated with a higher rate of unsuccessful insertion, underscoring the importance of distinguishing between insertion feasibility and overall placement success.

Previous prospective studies [13] in geriatric patients have demonstrated that established second-generation supraglottic airway devices, such as the I-Gel and LMA Supreme, provide reliable ventilation with high insertion success rates when used by experienced anesthesiologists.

Simulation studies have demonstrated that the ease of insertion and placement success of supraglottic airway devices may differ depending on the device design and user experience. In a manikin study by Bollucuoğlu [14] et al., medical students showed variable success rates and insertion times across different supraglottic airway devices, highlighting that the perceived ease of insertion does not necessarily translate into consistent placement success.

Recent perspectives [15] on the evolution of supraglottic airway devices emphasize that future airway management will increasingly rely on devices that combine ease of use, consistent placement success, and predictable clinical performance. In this context, the variability in insertion success observed with newer or alternative devices underscores the importance of rigorous clinical evaluation against established second-generation supraglottic airway benchmarks.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, patients in whom effective ventilation could not be achieved using the Tulip airway were excluded from the performance analysis. Although the clinical performance appeared comparable among successfully ventilated patients, the relatively high exclusion rate in the Tulip group suggests that the success of device placement —and consequently the overall clinical performance—may be limited for this device. The unexpectedly high failure rate of the Tulip airway emerged during the conduct of the study and was not anticipated at the time of planning.

Orogastric leak pressure is a well-established parameter for assessing the functional performance and successful placement of supraglottic airway devices [16]; however, it is not the only evaluation criterion.In the present study ventilatory assessment relied on composite clinical indicators rather than quantitative measures such as oropharyngeal leak pressure. Although ventilation adequacy was evaluated using clinically relevant criteria, including ETCO₂ monitoring, chest auscultation, appropriate cuff inflation, and the absence of audible mouth leaks, this approach remains somewhat subjective.

The assessment of ease of insertion is subjective. Although all device insertions were performed by experienced anesthesiologists and categorized according to predefined clinical criteria, this subjective assessment may have limited the objectivity of the comparisons between the supraglottic airway devices.

As the device selection was based on clinical judgment, residual confounding cannot be excluded. However, this design also provides insights into the real-world clinical use of supraglottic airway devices. In addition, differences between the groups, including sex distribution and ASA classification, may reflect the observational design of the study. These imbalances may have influenced device selection and clinical outcomes.

These limitations are consistent with the reporting considerations outlined in the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Finally, the single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This prospective observational study found no significant differences in the insertion time, ease of insertion among successfully placed cases, or adverse events among the I-Gel™, ProSeal™, and Tulip supraglottic airway devices. However, the failed insertion rate and number of insertion attempts were significantly higher for the Tulip device than for the other two devices. These findings suggest a multifactorial outcome, potentially related to the device design and operator-related factors. Further randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols are required to comprehensively assess the performance of supraglottic airway devices.
