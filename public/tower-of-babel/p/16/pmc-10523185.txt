Introduction

Brown-McLean Syndrome (BMS) was first documented by Brown and McLean in 1969 in eight cases that presented findings of peripheral corneal edema at least six years following intracapsular cataract surgery. The cases all presented with circumferential corneal edema with or without endothelial staining and a characteristically clear central cornea.1 To date, BMS is typically described in primarily cataract-related complications, and it is inextricably associated with long term aphakia.2 However, BMS has also rarely been reported in atypical surgical history like keratoplasty and pars plana vitrectomy or anterior chamber intraocular lens implantation.3

This paper is a case-documentation of BMS in a phakic patient with glaucoma following congenital glaucoma surgery in phakic eyes.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old male with unremarkable past social or family history and a known case of congenital glaucoma who underwent trabeculectomy on both eyes, presented to our clinic with a long history of mild progressive blurriness of vision for years. Upon examination, the patient was bilaterally phakic, with bilateral 360-degree of peripheral corneal edema sparing the center of the cornea with endothelial pigmentation and bilateral patent peripheral iridectomy in an otherwise stable ophthalmic examination. His uncorrected visual acuity was 20/20 in the right eye and 20/30 in the left eye with normal intraocular pressure. Both eyes are shown (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). We utilized anterior segment optical coherence tomography using the MS-39 (Costruzione Strumenti Oftalmici, Florence, Italy). In the right eye, a high-resolution scan of the right eye showed increased corneal thickness in the periphery and normal central corneal thickness (Fig. 3). While the left eye showed similar OCT findings in addition to a superficial corneal scar (Fig. 4). A diagnosis of Brown-McLean Syndrome was established. Our plan was to prescribe topical steroids and topical 5% hypertonic sodium chloride and schedule him for regular follow-up. Discussion

Brown-McLean syndrome is described as a cataract surgery-related complication that is inextricably linked to aphakic patients. Persistent corneal edema can be associated with foreign body sensations, redness, pain, tearing, and photophobia, aside from reduced visual acuity. Corneal edema as a surgical complication typically starts at the center of the cornea, then spreads peripherally, usually affecting the visual acuity of the patient, ranging from mild blurring of vision to more severe blinding corneal edema. In contrast to BMS, in which the corneal edema spares the center of the cornea.3 After conducting a literature review utilizing PubMed, Google Scholar, the Web of Sciences, MEDLINE, and EBSCO Using the key words (Brown-McLean syndrome, BMS, corneal edema, Corneal edema after glaucoma surgery, aphakic corneal edema, psudophakic corneal edema, and similar words), we did not find any prior reports of BMS similar to our case. There are multiple associations with BMS in the published literature. For instance, in 2015, Tourkmani and colleagues reported a case of BMS in a 12-year-old patient who underwent cataract extraction with posterior chamber intraocular lens insertion. The patient presented for routine follow-up with classical peripheral corneal edema with endothelial staining.4 In 2016, Mohebbi and colleagues documented a case of BMS in a patient with Hallermann-Streiff syndrome (HSS). The patient had the classical presentation of BMS in the setting of aphakia associated with HSS.5 Moreover, in the same year, Suwan reviewed the charts of BMS patients in a single center and documented that the disease is associated with high myopia in 40% of the documented cases, which can be related to our case, as congenital glaucoma patients who are at risk of axial myopia.6 Moreover, BMS developed in patients who underwent different types of ocular surgery, like extracapsular lens extraction (ECCE), phacoemulsification, pars plana vitrectomy, or penetrating keratoplasty. The author also documented the development of the BMS in patients without any previous intraocular surgery, such as those with keratoconus and myotonic dystrophy,6 intensifying the point that the BMS is not entirely a lens-related phenomenon. In 2019, Mallikarjun and colleagues also reported a case of BMS. The patient presented with a foreign body sensation and a peripheral corneal ring of edema, sparing the corneal center. He underwent cataract extraction eight years ago, and he had a diagnosis of BMS.7 Chatterjee and colleagues in 2020 also documented a typical case of BMS in a patient with aphakia and no other associated ocular disease. The authors explained that the disease is such a rare entity with the development of modern phacoemulsification techniques that many training ophthalmologists are unaware of its existence.8 In 2021, Alenaze and colleagues reported a case of BMS in an aphakic patient with homocystinuria.9 Two cases were reported by Lee and colleagues in 2021 where patients had undergone cataract extraction. The first was a Marfan syndrome case who was left aphakic and developed BMS four years later, with characteristics confirmed by specular microscopy. The second case underwent cataract extraction 11 years ago and developed BMS symptoms two months after undergoing macular hole surgery in both eyes.10 Due to its rarity and the fact that it spares the central cornea, making most of the patients asymptomatic, major interventions like endothelial keratoplasty have never been reported as a treatment for such a condition. The treatment of Brown-McLean syndrome began with prevention, as previous research suggested that superior iridectomy could be beneficial.6 However, our case did not support this hypothesis. Topical treatments like sodium chloride and topical steroids have been used effectively in some cases, while we tried them in our case with no favorable outcome up to the last follow-up. The rationale behind the use of topical steroids is their possible effect on improving the endothelium's pump function, which may improve the edema. While the purpose of using hypertonic medications is to increase the tear film's tonicity in order to subsequently draw fluid osmotically from the cornea into the tears, they also have a positive impact on stromal edema, although their maximal impact is on epithelial edema.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Others have used anterior stromal puncture, amniotic membrane transplantation, Burr-Assisted Peripheral Superficial Keratectomy, and penetrating keratoplasty.16 Indeed, penetrating keratoplasty has been reported as a subsequent intervention for an infected ulcer in a BMS case.2 Conclusion: Brown-Mclean syndrome is an exceedingly rare finding in cases following cataract surgery. The literature has multiple case reports and case series documenting the different associations with this disease; such associations are variable in nature, and as discussed herein, we demonstrate the importance of the potential of such a disease to develop, albeit rarely, in phakic patients who are undergoing intraocular surgery. The preventive role of PI is also questionable.
