Self-reported physical activity intensity and training volume are not related to menstrual health among recreationally active Swiss females
Authors: Claudia Kubica, Selina Zimmermann, Claudio R. Nigg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMenstrual cycle (MC) symptoms and disorders are common among females, with higher prevalence rates observed in competitive athletes. However, many women are physically active at recreational levels and might be also affected by MC health issues. To date, little is known about how physical activity intensity and training volume impact MC health in this broader, under-researched group. Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the association of physical activity and menstrual health among active Swiss females of reproductive age.MethodsBetween May and June 2023, we conducted a cross-sectional online survey among females (≥ 18 years) who were neither pregnant nor using hormonal contraceptives. Self-reported data on MC health, including premenstrual syndrome (PMS), oligomenorrhea, and secondary amenorrhea, were collected. Binary logistic regressions were used to assess the associations between leisure-time physical activity, overall training volume, and MC health.ResultsA total of 719 females were included (mean age = 29.2 ± 8.2 years; BMI = 22.5 ± 3 kg/m2; moderate to vigorous physical activity = 367 ± 296 min/week; mean training volume = 4.8 ± 4 h/week; average MC length = 29.4 ± 8.9 days). The prevalence rates were 15.9% for PMS, 17.8% for oligomenorrhea, and 2.9% for secondary amenorrhea. No associations of physical activity and training volume were observed for PMS and secondary amenorrhea. Higher levels of light physical activity were associated with a greater prevalence of oligomenorrhea (OR = 1.028, 95% CI: 1.005 - 1.053). The association was small in magnitude and model fit (R2 < 0.2).ConclusionOur findings suggest no clinically relevant association between MC health and physical activity as well as training volume in recreationally active females. Longitudinal research is needed to verify these results and inform guidelines to support menstrual health among active females.

Background

The menstrual cycle (MC) is a recurring biological phenomenon within the female reproductive system, commencing at menarche and persisting until postmenopause [1]. It is marked by hormonal fluctuations of ovarian steroids crucial for fertilization and pregnancy [1]. However, the MC extends its influence beyond the reproductive system, impacting various systems and tissues within the female body [2]. This influence encompasses essential biological processes, including metabolism [3, 4], ventilation [5], and immunity [6], profoundly affecting female physiology [7].

Although a healthy MC is essential for various biological processes, epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 80% of women of reproductive age experience symptoms associated with their MC at some point during their reproductive life [8]. These symptoms can range from single premenstrual symptoms, such as mood swings, bloating, cramping, or headaches, to the premenstrual syndrome (PMS) [9], which refers to regular, recurring, psychological and/or somatic complaints, occurring specifically in the luteal phase of the MC [10].

Additionally, other severe menstrual disorders affect both active and inactive females, including oligomenorrhea (MC length > 35 days) and secondary amenorrhea (absence of menstrual bleeding for three or more consecutive months) [11–14].

Beyond its implications for fertility and other biological processes, menstrual symptoms and disorders also have an impact on women’s overall health. Disruptions in the MC can contribute to endothelial dysfunction and reduced bone mineral density [15]. This, over the long term, could elevate the risk of osteoporosis and premenopausal coronary artery disease [16, 17]. Additionally, menstrual symptoms and PMS can lower quality of life [18, 19] and interfere with social life, work, and education [20]. Overall, MC disorders are an obvious warning sign and can lead to severe health issues [21, 22].

Physical activity (PA) offers the chance to alleviate MC disorders, as well as the risk of increasing MC disorders [23]. While MC-related symptoms and disorders are prevalent across the female population, highly active females appear to have an even higher risk [11]. Although the mechanisms underlying MC disorders are multifaceted, such as the potential suppression of ovarian sex hormone production [24] caused through disturbances of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, all contributing factors are not fully understood yet [25]. Notably, evidence suggests that exercise intensity and volume may independently increase the risk of menstrual dysfunction [26–28]. Most studies focus on elite athletes, overlooking recreationally active females—a large population group who may also be at elevated risk of menstrual disorders. Recreationally active females also often have high amounts of training volume [29], but due to different environments and structures, they tend to have a reduced sleep duration and quality, higher stress and injuries and overall impaired health and wellbeing [30]. On the other hand, PA might alleviate MC disorders, such as PMS, in recreationally active females. Understanding the prevalence and influence of the specific intensity domains of PA and training volume on MC disorders within this broader population is essential to identify actionable areas for improving menstrual health [16, 31].

Consequently, this study seeks to address this gap and assess the prevalence of MC disorders and the relationship between PA intensity, overall training volume, and MC health among active females in Switzerland.

Methods

Study design and participants

All participants completed a cross-sectional online survey. The survey was based on a sample of Swiss women aged 18 until late menopausal transition (occurrence of amenorrhea of 60 days and longer) [31]. Participants were recruited via announcements at the universities of Bern and Basel, and social media (Facebook, Instagram). Additionally, sports clubs and sports associations in Switzerland were contacted to distribute the survey among their members. Due to the varied recruitment strategies, including requests to forward the recruitment post, the response rate is unknown. Overall, 2376 participants started the survey, and 1294 (54.5%) completed it. Only complete responses were included in the data analysis. Participants were excluded from the analysis if they were younger than 18 years (n = 11), had not reached the menarche yet (n = 6), were currently pregnant or had reached menopause or experience MC irregularities postpartum or premenopausal (n = 28), considered themselves as “professional athlete” (n = 12) or “inactive” (n = 11), reported less than 30 min PA and less than 30 min of training volume/week (n = 123), or took hormonal contraceptives (n = 394). These exclusion criteria reduced the total sample for these analyses to 719 participants. Table 1 provides a detailed description of the participants’ demographics. The survey protocol was approved by the Human Science Ethics Committee of the University Bern (Ethics approval Nr. 2023-03-01) and informed consent to participate was obtained from all of the participants.

Questionnaire

The data were collected through an online survey (LimeSurvey, LimeSurvey GmbH, Hamburg, Germany), which was open from May to June 2023. The survey comprised four sections: (1) informed consent, (2) demographics, including self-reported weight and height, (3) engagement in PA and sports, and (4) menstrual history, including menstrual symptoms and disorders and contraceptive use.

For demographics, age, body weight, body height, and relationship status were assessed. To assess leisure-time PA, the adapted validated version of the Godin-Shephard leisure-time physical activity questionnaire was used [32–35]. The questionnaire identifies the number of days and number of minutes in which light (LPA; activity with minimal effort such as yoga, archery, bowling, easy walking, etc.), moderate (MPA, not exhausting activity such as fast walking, baseball, tennis, easy bicycling, easy swimming etc. ), and vigorous PA (VPA, heart beats rapidly, such as during running, jogging, soccer, squash, vigorous swimming or bicycling, etc.) were performed. Additionally, the overall retrospective sport-specific training volume was queried by: “How many hours did you train on average per week in the past four weeks?“. This dual approach was chosen to differentiate between general PA behavior across intensity domains and structured, exercise-related training load. While intensity-based PA categories reflect physiological exertion and align with public health recommendations [36], training volume provides complementary information on cumulative exercise exposure and chronic training stress, which is particularly relevant for menstrual health and energy availability [37, 38].

To collect data concerning the menstrual history, participants were asked at what age they reached menarche. Further, they had to retrospectively recall the average length of their past three menses, as well as the average duration of menstruation [1]. Oligomenorrhea was defined as reporting an average MC length of > 35 days [13]. Two distinct questions analysed secondary amenorrhea. First, participants were asked whether they had menstrual bleeding in the past 90 days (except due to pregnancy) and if they currently experience an absence of three or more consecutive menstruations (except due to pregnancy). Participants were characterized as “secondary amenorrheic” if they haven’t had menstruation within the past three months and they currently experience the absence of three or more periods in a row (except due to pregnancy) [13].

To assess PMS, the German version of the screening instrument for premenstrual symptoms (SIPS) was used [39]. The symptoms asked for in the questionnaire were: depressed mood/hopelessness, anxiety/tension, tearful/increased sensitivity to rejection, anger/irritability, decreased interest in home activities/social activities, difficulty concentrating fatigue/lack of energy, overeating/food craving, insomnia, hypersomnia, feeling overwhelmed or out of control, and physical symptoms. Further, as listed above, participants were asked whether their symptoms interfered with any of the five domains: work efficiency or productivity, relationships with coworkers, relationships with family, social life activities and/or home responsibilities. The participants were asked to rate their symptoms and impairment as “not at all”, “mild”, “moderate” or “severe”. To be characterized as having PMS, the females had to report at least one of the four core symptoms (irritability, dysphoria, tension, lability of mood) as severe and at least four additional symptoms as moderate to severe. They also had to report that their symptoms interfered severely with their ability to function in at least one of five domains (work efficiency, social life, home responsibilities, and relationships at work or at home) [39, 40]. The SIPS is a validated screening tool [41, 42] to assess the premenstrual syndrome among females. However, all self-report data, such as used in this study to assess PA and PMS has a potential for an under- or overreporting [41, 43].

Additionally, several possible confounders were assessed in the questionnaire, such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, drug use, number of children, sleep duration, sleep quality, current diagnosed eating disorder, sedentary behavior, and age at menarche. The complete questionnaire set can be provided upon request.

Statistics

Data was analyzed using RStudio 2025.09.2 + 418 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). The results are reported as means ± standard deviation. Differences in sociodemographic characteristics, current PA levels, overall training volume, and menstrual cycle (MC) characteristics between participants with and without PMS, oligomenorrhea, and secondary amenorrhea were examined using non-parametric Mann–Whitney U tests, as most variables were not normally distributed. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set a priori at p < .05. To account for multiple testing in the group comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.

Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between three dichotomous outcomes PMS, oligomenorrhea, and secondary amenorrhea (0 = no, 1 = yes) and PA intensities, including LPA, MPA, VPA, the combined measure of moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA), and overall weekly training volume.

All logistic regression models were a priori adjusted for age and body mass index (BMI). Additional potential confounding variables (smoking status, alcohol consumption, drug use, number of children, sleep duration, sleep quality, current eating disorder, sedentary behavior, and age at menarche) were evaluated using a structured screening approach. Each potential confounder was added individually to the base model (PA variable + age + BMI). Confounders were retained in the final models if they showed statistical relevance (p < .10) and/or meaningfully improved model fit (ΔNagelkerke R² ≥ 0.01).

Multicollinearity among predictor variables was assessed by examining bivariate correlations, which were low to moderate (r < .70), indicating that multicollinearity did not bias the regression estimates. Associations are presented as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke’s R², calculated manually from the model log-likelihoods to ensure consistency across models. Nagelkerke’s R² values were interpreted according to Backhaus et al. as small (R² > 0.20), moderate (R² > 0.40), and large (R² > 0.50) model fits [44].

Meaningful ORs were classified in this study according to the classifications proposed by Chen et al. (2010) above an OR of 1.5 as a moderate effect, and a large effects for OR > 5 [45], and effect sizes are calculated according to Cohen (2013) as f2 = (R2/1- R2) with f2 = 0.02 small effect, f2 = 0.15 moderate effect and f2 = 0.35 strong effect [46]. In consideration of the exploratory nature of the study and the existing limited evidence in this research field, multiple statistical comparisons were performed and results should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Demographic information, along with the prevalence of PMS, oligomenorrhea, and secondary amenorrhea, is provided in Table 1. Based on the BMI, 4.5% of the participants were underweight, 79.4% normal weight, 13.1% overweight, and 2.5% obese [47]. Among all participants, 15.9% were characterized as having PMS [39], 17.8% as having oligomenorrhea, and 2.9% as having secondary amenorrhea [13]. The most frequently mentioned sports in which the participants were engaged were: running (13.9%), strength training (12.5%), handball (8.1%), gymnastics (7.6%), cycling (7.1%) and volleyball (6.4%). MVPA ranged from 2 min/week up to 1920 min/week and training volume from 1 to 50 h/week.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the association between PA intensity, training volume, and MC disorders among recreationally active females. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine MC disorders in Swiss recreationally active females, filling a gap in research on this population. Understanding the prevalence and influence of the specific intensity domains of PA and training volume on MC disorders within this broader population is necessary to identify actionable areas for improving menstrual health.

Although MC disorders vary across sports disciplines [14], our findings align with studies on recreationally active females in other countries. For example, studies have reported prevalence rates of 11.3% for oligomenorrhea and 2.6% for secondary amenorrhea in U.S. college students [12], and 16% for combined amenorrhea/oligomenorrhea across multiple countries, including the USA, Brazil, France, Spain, Germany, and the UK [49–51]. The prevalence of oligomenorrhea (17.8%) and amenorrhea (2.9%) in our sample were lower than rates observed in international athletes [49–51] and Swiss athletes [52] or recreationally active females when oligomenorrhea and secondary amenorrhea was combined [53] This difference may reflect variations in training volume (14 h/week [50] vs. 5 h/week in our study), nutritional status (BMI 20,7 kg/m2 [49] vs. 22,5 kg/m2 in our study), or other lifestyle factors between recreational and elite athletes such as weight or psychological stress [54].

The prevalence of PMS in our study (15.9%) is consistent with findings from the Swiss National Health Survey 2007, which reported a rate of 15.7% among participants not using oral contraceptives [55]. Also to other studies investigating PA and PMS among recreationally active females, prevalence rates were comparable with 19.3% [56] and 12% among the broad population in France [57].

Premenstrual syndrome

No relevant differences in demographic characteristics were observed among participants with PMS compared to their counterparts without PMS, oligomenorrhea or secondary amenorrhea in our study. These findings are consistent with previous research on recreationally active women [56]. Across studies in recreationally active/exercising women, PMS cases and controls are typically drawn from comparable populations and show broadly similar baseline demographics (e.g., age and BMI) [56]. Likewise, in exercising women with menstrual disturbances, groups are often age-/BMI-matched or do not differ in key baseline characteristics [58, 59].

In the present study, no significant association was observed between self-reported PA intensity or overall training volume and the odds of PMS odds. Accordingly, PA explained only a negligible proportion of variance in the PMS status. These findings contrast with several intervention studies suggesting that moderate to vigorous intensity training may alleviate PMS symptoms [60], as well as with the review by Buchanan-Smith and colleagues, which reported strong evidence for an inverse association between PA and PMS prevalence based on observational studies [50, 51].

This discrepancy may be partly explained by differences in study design. While intervention studies allow for causal inference, the cross-sectional analysis in the present study precludes conclusions regarding causality [61]. In addition, differences in the operationalization of PA, such as continuous vs. categorial comparison of PA, may further limit the comparability across studies [62]. Moreover, recent reviews and meta-analyses highlighted that while exercise may be beneficial for PMS, inconsistencies in training protocols, PMS assessment methods and overall study quality resulting in considerable uncertainty regarding the magnitude of PA effects on PMS outcomes [62, 63].

Consistent with our findings, other cross-sectional studies have also failed to detect an association between PA and PMS [56]. However, prior research suggests a distinction between prevalence and symptom burden. Overall PMS prevalence may remain largely unchanged, whereas PA may reduce the severity of specific symptoms among those affected [62, 64, 65].

Oligomenorrhea

In our study, participants with oligomenorrhea were younger and experienced an increased bleeding length compared to those without PMS, oligomenorrhea and secondary amenorrhea. These findings are consistent with previous research showing higher prevalence rates of oligomenorrhea among younger females [66]. Although earlier studies have linked higher BMI values with oligomenorrhea, this association was mainly observed in overweight or obese populations with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 [67]. Notably, only 15.6% of ur sample was classified as overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), which may explain why we did not observe higher BMI values in the oligomenorrhea group.

Research on BMI differences among healthy participants is inconclusive. For instance, one study reported lower BMI values in the oligomenorrhea group within a healthy BMI range compared to low or high BMI values [68], whereas another detected higher BMI values in females with oligomenorrhea [69] or a higher prevalence of menstrual cycle irregularities among those with a low BMI [70]. These conflicting findings may be related to different underlying mechanisms - such as hypothalamic inhibition or hyperandrogenism - that lead to distinct physiological responses, including divergent effects on BMI [69]. However, our data does not allow us to draw definitive conclusions regarding these mechanisms.

Considering OR for oligomenorrhea, we identified a slightly increased risk for LPA (OR = 1.028, 95%CI = 1.005-1.053) but not for the other PA intensity categories or overall training volume. For interpretation it should be kept in mind that although statistically significant, the association between LPA and oligomenorrhea was very small in magnitude in our study, and explained only a small proportion of variance (R² < 0.20) [48]. This finding aligns with previous research [53, 70]. In the study from Baranauska and colleagues which combined oligomenorrhea and secondary amenorrhea in their analysis [53], higher ORs were observed with increased amounts of light intensity training. Also, in the study from Sternfeld et al. the minutes of daily VPA were positively associated with MC length [71]. Baranauska and colleagues suggested that excessive volumes of light intensity training may reflect unspecific exercise sessions, such as “junk miles” or cross-training sessions, mainly increasing energy expenditure rather than sport-specific skill development [53], thereby predisposing females to hypothalamic inhibition due to low energy availability, leading to a reduced gonadotrophin-releasing hormone secretion [72]. In contrast to the study from Baranauska and colleagues, we did not observe significantly increased ORs for MPA, VPA, or overall training volume. The divergence from the findings of Baranauska and colleagues may be due to their combined analysis of oligomenorrhea and secondary amenorrhea [53], whereas we analyzed them separately. Moreover, although both studies relied on self-reported data, Baranauska and colleagues inquired about training intensities [53], while in our study overall PA and training volume were assessed.

Secondary amenorrhea

When comparing participants with secondary amenorrhea and participants without PMS, oligomenorrhea and secondary amenorrhea in our study, those with secondary amenorrhea had a lower body mass and BMI and showed a higher training volume. These findings are consistent with previous research in physically active female populations [49, 73, 74]. However, the limited number of participants with secondary amenorrhea in the present sample (n = 21) substantially limits the robustness and generalizability of these findings and warrants cautious interpretation.

Considering the OR, no significant associations were found for LPA, MVP, VPA, MVPA or training volume. One plausible explanation is that a high amount of PA particularly when dominated by a high proportion of MVPA, may substantially increase energy expenditure and may therefore, contribute to the risk of low energy availability [75]. Given that low energy availability is considered as a key mechanism underlying secondary amenorrhea among active females [74], these findings highlight the potential role of training intensity in influencing menstrual function. Nevertheless, definitive conclusions regarding low energy availability cannot be drawn from the present data, as dietary intake and direct markers of energy availability were not assessed.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the findings are based on self-report data. Although validated questionnaires were used to assess PA levels and PMS [33, 39], self-report measures are prone to bias, including overestimation of PA [76] and underreporting of PMS [41]. In addition, the prevalence rates for oligomenorrhea and secondary amenorrhea were determined based on participants’ retrospective recall of their MC patterns. While this approach is common in the literature [49, 74], it carries a risk of misclassification. Furthermore, as with all self-reported measures, recall bias and social desirability bias cannot be excluded [77]. We therefore recommend that future studies verify these results using device-based measures for PA (e.g., accelerometry) [62], robust assessment of PMS [42] and standardized hormonal or clinical evaluations to verify menstrual disturbances such as oligomenorrhea and secondary amenorrhea [65].

Second, this study employed a cross-sectional design, it is not possible to infer causality. High-qualitative longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify the temporal and causal relationships between PA intensity and overall training volume and PMS, oligomenorrhea, and secondary amenorrhea among Swiss recreationally active females.

Third, even though several relevant potential confounders were accounted in our analysis, such as age, smoking status, alcohol consumption and number of children, residual confounding related to low energy availability cannot be excluded as energy intake and overall energy availability were not assessed in this study [78].

Fourth, given the exploratory nature of this investigation and the relatively early stage of research in this field, multiple statistical tests were conducted, which increases the risk of type I error [65]. While this approach was chosen to comprehensively explore potential associations, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in independent samples.

Finally, although the sample size was large, the demographic profile of our large sample may not fully represent all recreationally active women of reproductive age. It is possible that individuals with a specific interest in menstrual health were more likely to participate, introducing potential bias into our findings.

Conclusions

This study provides novel evidence on the associations between PA intensity, overall training volume, and MC health among recreationally active women in Switzerland. While PA explained only a small proportion of variance in premenstrual syndrome, higher levels of LPA were associated with oligomenorrhea. No consistent associations were observed for training volume across menstrual health outcomes. Longitudinal studies employing objective, device-based measures are necessary to further clarify how PA’s volume, intensity, modality, and other associated training variables affect menstrual health in recreationally active women.
