Comprehensive assessment of whole-genome sequencing-based variant calling pipelines in monozygotic twins
Authors: Chengyue Tan, Weifen Sun, Jiaxin Yu, Jiajing Fu, Chen Ming, Xiling Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhole-genome sequencing (WGS) has emerged as a cornerstone technology in precision medicine, genetic diagnostics, and forensic identification. Compared to traditional capillary electrophoresis, WGS captures the entire genomic landscape and thus support high-resolution individual identification and even the differentiation of monozygotic (MZ) twins with nearly identical DNA contents. The minute genetic variation between MZ twins offer a rigorous paradigm for assessing the accuracy and sensitivity of different variant calling algorithms. Therefore, we benchmarked eight state-of-the-art variant calling pipelines using MZ twin genomes sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at 30× and 60× coverage. The precision, robustness, efficiency, and concordance of these pipelines were systematically evaluated against a polymerase chain reaction-validated gold standard set of small variants for MZ twin discrimination. Our results indicated that DRAGEN read aligner combined with BCFtools achieved the optimal balance between accuracy and computationally efficiency, albeit with minor specificity and robustness tradeoffs. GATK HaplotypeCaller exhibited the highest accuracy and sensitivity among all tested pipelines, but required exceptionally high computational resources. DRAGEN and Strelka2 pipeline showcased superior robustness with the highest true-positive ratio and the lowest false-positive ratio. Collectively, these insights gained from the detection of subtle variations within highly similar genomes provide critical guidelines applicable to scenarios demanding high-precision variant calling.

Introduction

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) provides an unparallel and comprehensive insight into the content of human genomes at single-base resolution. Variant calling, the process of identifying genetic variations from sequencing reads, has gained increasingly importance in crucial domains, including cancer research, therapeutic innovation, and organ-specific studies [1–4]. The advent of WGS in forensic science further extends its significance to specialized applications such as individual identification in criminal investigation [5]. Therefore, precise variant detection becomes paramount for analytical outcomes.

Currently, the development of high-throughput sequencing technology has spawned a variety of genomic analysis tools that serve different scenarios to uncover mutations presented in sequencing data. This process typically comprises three structured stages: raw sequencing data preprocessing and quality control, read alignment, and variant calling. Initially, low-quality bases (commonly located near the 3′ end of the reads) and exogenous sequences, such as sequencing adapters, are trimmed using read processing tools like Cutadapt [6], FastQC [7], fastp [8], and FASTX-Toolkit [9]. Subsequently, the cleaned reads are mapped onto the human reference genome. Widely used mapping tools include BWA [10, 11], DRAGEN [12], and Bowtie2 [13]. Post-alignment, PCR deduplication [14] and base quality recalibration [15, 16] can be executed, as recommended by the GATK best practices [16, 17]. The final stage, variant calling, distinguishes genuine variants from artifacts generated during library preparation, sample enrichment, and sequencing. Several variant callers have been developed for this purpose, such as GATK HaplotypeCaller [17], VarScan [18], and Strelka2 [19]. However, the rapid expansion of variant detectors has introduced significant challenges: (i) algorithmic heterogeneity: divergent statistical models resulted in performance variability across genomic contexts; (ii) sensitivity-specificity trade-offs: rare variant detection was still confounded by background noise and false positives; (iii) computational scalability: achieving a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency in large-scale studies continued to pose practical constraints; and (iv) standardization deficits: no consensus pipelines exists across diverse study designs and sample types.

In previous research, performance comparisons of different variant calling pipelines have been conducted, and best practices for accurate variant detection have been proposed. Liu et al. sequenced the exomes of 20 individuals and compared the performance of four prevalent callers coupled with the BWA aligner, employing both single-sample and multiple-sample variant-calling strategies [20]. Roberts et al. applied four single-nucleotide variant (SNV) calling algorithms to matched cancer-normal exomes of a chronic myeloid leukemia patient, sequenced with the Illumina HiSeq platform in paired 100 bp reads. Their study revealed significant differences in the number and characteristics of detected SNVs [21]. Sandmann et al. evaluated eight variant callers in identifying SNVs and short INDELs using two sets of real Illumina targeted sequencing data from patients with myelodysplastic syndrome [22]. Moreover, a spectrum of benchmarking studies have utilized either the well-characterized NA12878 DNA reference sample [23–33] or synthetic datasets [23, 27, 30, 33, 34] to evaluate the strengths and limitations of diverse variant calling pipelines. The NA12878 sample, originating from an individual of European ancestry and released by the Genome in a Bottle (GIAB) Consortium (https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/genome-bottle), has undergone multiplatform sequencing across global laboratories. A major concern with GIAB is that the same sequencing technologies and variant calling algorithms employed in benchmark construction were also used for algorithm testing [34]. This dependency potentially compromises the evidentiary robustness of such assessments. Besides, sequence technologies and variant calling methods have advanced rapidly, it is imperative to remain abreast of emerging best practices and tailor them to the context-specific investigation. Critically, some real variants may occur at ultralow allelic frequencies in cases of impure samples, rare tumor subclones, or circulating DNA scenarios. Within forensic science, monozygotic (MZ) twins could act as a representative example. MZ twins share identical nuclear DNA, as they come from a single zygote. Nevertheless, tiny genetic discrepancies emerge due to stochastic mutational events and environmental factors during ontogeny. The detection of such ultralow-frequency variants from sequencing data presents formidable challenges, thus underscores the crucial demand for more sensitive statistical modelling and advanced error correction approaches.

In this study, we focused on identifying discordant within-twin variants against PCR-validated golden variant calls from our previous work [5], and systematically benchmarked eight state-of-the-art variant detection pipelines in this context. The benchmark encompassed the principal algorithmic paradigms represented by Bayesian (BCFtools [35], Strelka2, SpeedSeq [36]), Hidden Markov Model (GATK and DRAGEN HaplotypeCaller), deep learning (DeepVariant [37]), and Fisher’s exact test (VarScan2), together with the Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA)-accelerated DRAGEN-OS, thereby providing a thorough assessment of current variant detection methodologies widely used in clinical and forensic genomes. We further delineated the core algorithms of each variant caller and, where applicable, highlighted their strengths and caveats. These insights established an evidence-based framework for optimal pipeline selection, laying the groundwork for more precise and reliable genetic analyses across various fields, particularly within forensic practices.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A pair of female MZ twins A and B, aged 27 and a 6-year follow-up at age 33 years (referred to A_27y, A_33y, B_27y, B_33y) were enrolled. Peripheral blood samples were anticoagulated with EDTA-Na2, and genomic DNA was next extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini-Kit according to the recommendation (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and analyzed using the Qubit™ dsDNA High Sensitivity (HS) Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Carlsbad, USA) to quantify DNA concentration. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Academy of Forensic Science, Ministry of Justice, People’s Republic of China (accession number: 2024–15).

Whole-genome sequencing

A total of 100 ng of genomic DNA was constructed as WGS library using an Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, genomic DNA was fragmented to an average size of 350 bp using a Covaris ultrasound unit (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA) followed by bead-based size selection (SPRIselect, Beckman Coulter). DNA fragments were then end-repaired, adenylated, and ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters, and PCR amplification was subsequently conducted. PCR products were evaluated using Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for concentration measurement and an Agilent 2100 analyzer for purity analysis. Finally, WGS libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 flow cell to achieve 60× and 30× coverage with 2 × 150 bp cycles, respectively. Of which, the 60× coverage WGS datasets were dedicated to downstream analysis, whereas the 30× coverage WGS datasets were restricted to sequencing depth analysis.

Sequence alignment and preprocessing

In the GATK4 preprocessed pipeline, the raw sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using BWA-MEM. Duplicate reads were identified and tagged using Picard (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard) MarkDuplicates and then removed with the designated parameter REMOVE_DUPLICATES = true. The base quality recalibration was performed using the GATK BaseRecalibrator with three known variant datasets: dbsnp_138.hg38.vcf.gz, 1000G_phase1.snps.high_confidence.hg38.vcf.gz, Mills_and_1000G_gold_standard.indels.hg38.vcf.gz involved, followed by GATK ApplyBQSR. Picard AddOrReplaceReadGroups were used to add group information.

In the DRAGEN preprocessed pipeline, a new hash table was first constructed using the human reference genome GRCh38. Subsequently, the DRAGEN-OS tool was employed to align the raw sequencing reads to the GRCh38 genome, utilizing the prebuilt reference hash table. The aligned sequences were output in SAM/BAM format.

Variant calling

GATK HaplotypeCaller was designated as caller1 (G-HaplotypeCaller) for variant detection, followed by GATK GenotypeGVCFs, which was utilized to preserve the gvcf records of 24 chromosomes (22 autosomes, X, and Y) for each sample. Four well-established variant datasets, including hapmap_3.3.hg38.vcf.gz, 1000G_omni2.5.hg38.vcf.gz, 1000G_phase1.snps.high_confidence.hg38.vcf.gz, and dbsnp_138.hg38.vcf.gz were employed for raw variant recalibration. DeepVariant, referred to as caller2 (G-DeepVariant) was implemented using GATK preprocessed bam files as input. This approach leverages deep learning algorithms and deep neural networks to predict variant likelihoods and identify variant calls. Among DRAGEN-based variant calling pipelines, two distinct functions within BCFtools, namely mpileup and call, were integrated as caller3 (D-BCFtools) for the identification of variants. DeepVariant, SpeedSeq, and Strelka2 were designated as caller4 (D-DeepVariant), caller5 (D-SpeedSeq), and caller6 (D-Strelka2), respectively, and were executed with their recommended parameters. Caller7 (D-VarScan2) utilized SAMtools [38] mpileup to generate genotype likelihoods for each genomic position, followed by variant calling utilizing VarScan2 [39]. For caller8 (D-HaplotypeCaller), a short tandem repeat (STR) table file for the reference genome was initially constructed by GATK ComposeSTRTableFile. Subsequently, the GATK CalibrateDragstrModel utilized this STR table file to estimate parameters for the STR model, which were then incorporated into variant calling with HaplotypeCaller in DRAGEN mode.

Variant filtering

To enhance variant calling accuracy and reduce background noise, we initially employed RepeatMasker [40] to remove variants within repetitive genomic regions, which are prone to false-positive variant calls due to mapping and sequencing errors. Subsequently, we excluded variants located in gap and centromere regions, identified using genomic coordinates downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser database (https://hgdownload.soe.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/hg38/database/). Assembly gaps represent incompletely sequenced or assembled segments, while centromeres present challenges for precise variant calling due to their highly repetitive architecture. Finally, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) exhibiting an allele depth below 5 reads and quality score (QUAL) less than 20 were excluded, and high-confidence variants were thereby obtained.

Ground truth callsets

Six variants derived from paired MZ twin genomes sequenced at 30× coverage and validated via both amplicon-based targeted next-generation sequencing and Sanger sequencing in our previous work were used as the ground truth reference (Supplementary Table S1) [5]. Besides, a panel of 12, 15, 11, and 22 stringently filtered loci within the A_27y, B_27y, A_33y, and B_33y genomes that failed experimental validation were designated as true negatives [5]. We further downloaded the HG007 sample from the GIAB Consortium, a well-characterized WGS dataset from a female individual in a trio family, including her husband (HG006) and her son (HG005). Variant calling was conducted on the HG007 dataset using the D-BCFtools pipeline and its accuracy was assessed by comparing the results to the GIAB benchmark variant set.

Robustness assessment

We employed the Next-generation Sequencing Analysis Toolkit (NEAT; version 3.0) [41] on twin A genome aged at 27 years to generate a large amount of in silico synthetic reads simulating real sequencing data across different pipelines. A repertoire of 100 randomly selected SNPs on chromosome 20 (chr20) identified by each pipeline was utilized to construct pipeline-specific ground truth VCF files, wherein a single PCR-validated variant, chr20:38763028, was included (Supplementary Table S1). gen_reads.py (https://github.com/zstephens/neat-genreads) was executed with a read length of 150 bp, an average mutation rate and error rate of 0 (−M 0, −E 0), and a coverage of 60× based on GRCh38 chr20 reference genome. The simulations were repeated 100 times for each of the eight pipelines with distinct random seeds. Finally, the simulated paired-end reads were analyzed using corresponding variant calling pipelines, thus in silico variants were obtained. Robustness was assessed by comparing these variants to the ground truth set with vcf_compare_OLD.py (https://github.com/zstephens/neat-genreads) script, yielding counts of PerfectMatches, FN_variants, FP_variants, and TotalGoldenVariants. Sequencing depth

To investigate the effect of sequencing depth on variant calling accuracy, we first merged the original 30× and 60× datasets, and a high-depth sequencing output of 90× paired-end reads was obtained. Downsampling from 90× were then implemented by BBNorm (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap) algorithm with designated depths and default parameter settings. The process was repeated three times per depth, yielding a triplicate of gradient coverage depths: 30×, 40×, 50×, 60×, 70×, and 80×. Variants were subsequently called utilizing G-HaplotypeCaller and D-BCFtools, independently. TPR was calculated as the proportion of the six PCR-validated loci identified at each sequencing depth.

Results

Multiple variant calling pipelines summary

In this study, eight distinct variant calling pipelines were employed on a pair of female MZ twin genomes sequenced at ~30× and 60× coverage, respectively (Fig. 1). The pipelines combined two widely used alignment tools, BWA-MEM and DRAGEN-OS, with six state-of-the-art variant callers. A comparison of the underlying probabilistic models, alignment strategies, and variant-detection algorithms is presented (Table 1; Supplementary Tables S2–S3). As a result, both BWA-MEM and DRAGEN-OS successfully mapped over 99% of raw reads onto the reference genome across all eight samples (Supplementary Table S4). Candidate variant callsets were subsequently produced, fluctuating from 3.30 to 3.97 million SNPs derived from 60× datasets across the eight analytic pipelines (Supplementary Table S5). Following rigorous quality filtration, an average of 987,459 ± 1,763 (mean ± SD), 887,532 ± 19,280, 929,659 ± 1,619, 938,806 ± 573, 1,012,908 ± 2,230, 945,851 ± 286, 744,410 ± 145,820, and 920,078 ± 3,842 SNPs were obtained per calling pipelines, indicating broad concordance despite methodological heterogeneity.

Performance comparison of sequence aligners and variant callers

To systematically compare the performance of the eight distinct pipelines, we first evaluated their runtime during two critical stages: sequence alignment (FASTQ to BAM) and variant calling (BAM to VCF) across four MZ twin genomes. In the sequence alignment and preprocessing stage, the GATK (BWA) pipeline exhibited an average processing time of 89.10 ± 5.64 (mean ± SD) hours, while the DRAGEN pipeline demonstrated a significantly reduced runtime of 10.53 ± 1.52 h (Fig. 2A). During the variant calling stage, D-HaplotypeCaller displayed the longest processing time (551.07 ± 44.34 min), followed by D-VarScan2 (72.29 ± 5.63 min) and D-SpeedSeq (64.70 ± 5.58 min) across the DRAGEN-based pipelines (Fig. 2B). Notably, D-BCFtools, D-DeepVariant, and D-Strelka2 showcased superior computational efficiency with substantially reduced runtime requirements of 42.34 ± 3.52, 38.10 ± 2.84, and 29.43 ± 3.29 min, respectively. Within the GATK framework, the average runtime of G-HaplotypeCaller was 7.45 times higher than that of G-DeepVariant, reaching 310.95 ± 11.72 min (Fig. 2B). These findings collectively demonstrate that the DRAGEN-based pipeline consistently exhibited superior computational efficiency for both sequence alignment and variant calling on WGS data. However, the combination with HaplotypeCaller, regardless of the underlying framework, resulted in relatively inefficient processing speeds.

We next estimated the accuracy of the eight distinct pipelines against those six loci confirmed by PCR in our previous work [5] (Supplementary Table S1). The results indicated that G-HaplotypeCaller and D-BCFtools demonstrated the highest TPR values, achieving 100% across all analyzed samples under the criteria of an allele depth higher than 5 reads and a quality score larger than 20 (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Table S6). Given the remarkable computational speed and superior accuracy of D-BCFtools, we further validated its performance using the well-characterized HG007 dataset from the GIAB Consortium. As expected, D-BCFtools effectively identified 3,784,668 (98.06%) of the 3,859,701 golden standard variants in the HG007 dataset. Moreover, an additional callset of 60 stringently filtered loci that failed experimental validation revealed that G-HaplotypeCaller reached the highest specificity, exhibiting 100% true negative ratio (TNR) values across four MZ twin genomes (Fig. 2D). In contrast, D-BCFtools showcased compromised specificity, with an average TNR value of 93.79 ± 4.30% observed. Overall, these results highlighted the superior sensitivity of D-BCFtools in variant detection, albeit with reduced specificity. G-HaplotypeCaller, as the most wildly used variant calling tool, consistently maintained strong performance.

Variant calling robustness of multiple pipelines

To systematically evaluated the robustness of each variant calling pipeline, we simulated 100 paired-end chr20 sequencing datasets, respectively, and in silico variants were therefore obtained. Ground truth call sets were constructed by randomly sampling 100 SNPs on chr20 identified by each pipeline on A_27y WGS datasets with the inclusion of a single PCR-validated variant, chr20:38763028. As a result, D-Strelka2 emerged as the top performer with the highest TPR of 97.65%–98.38%, the lowest FDR of 0.08%–0.14%, and the lowest FNR of 1.62%–2.35% (Fig. 3). G-HaplotypeCaller also exhibited robust performance with TPR values between 92.63% and 96.37%, FDR values between 0.10% and 0.17%, and FNR values between 3.63% and 7.37%. In contrast, D-DeepVariant demonstrated the lowest TPR, fluctuating from 88.30% to 88.86%, and D-VarScan2 had the highest FDR (1.34%–1.62%) and FNR (8.13%–8.57%) among the tested pipelines.

The precision, recall and F-measure metrics provided additional insight into the robustness assessment. D-Strelka2 reached the highest precision (99.86%–99.92%), recall (97.65%–98.38%), and F-measure (98.75%–99.13%). G-HaplotypeCaller and G-DeepVariant also performed admirably, with precision values of 99.83%–99.90% and 99.82%–99.85%; recall values of 92.63%–96.37% and 94.38%–94.62%; and F-measure values of 96.10%–98.11% and 97.03%–97.16%, respectively.

Overall, D-Strelka2 outperformed other variant calling pipelines, demonstrating exceptional robustness in variant detection. G-HaplotypeCaller also manifested robust performance across all evaluated metrics.

Variant calling accuracy at different sequencing depth

To thoroughly evaluate the impact of sequencing depth on variant detection, we first pooled the original 30× and 60× libraries to generate 90× paired-end reads. Downsampling from 90× WGS datasets were then performed in triplicates to obtain in-silico coverages of 30×, 40×, 50×, 60×, 70×, and 80×. At each depth, three technical replicates of samples A_27y, A_33y, B_27y, and B_33y were independently analyzed using both D-BCFtools and G-HaplotypeCaller. As expected, the total number of high-confidence SNPs increased with depth for both pipelines (Fig. 5A), and G-HaplotypeCaller produced significantly larger callsets than D-BCFtools across all samples overall (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001). Remarkably, all benchmark variants were successfully detected across depth gradients in four simulated genomes higher than 40× coverage (Supplementary Fig. S1). After stringent filtering (allele depth ≥ 5 reads and QUAL ≥20), D-BCFtools exhibited marked performance decline at 50× coverage in sample B_33y, as evidenced by diminished TPR values (Fig. 5B). This finding confirmed that low-depth data are insufficient to support stringent filtering for those ultralow-frequency variants. Furthermore, minor fluctuations were observed at 70× and 80× coverage in the sample B_27y, indicating that depth-dependent stochasticity can persist even in high-coverage regimes. Collectively, these results suggested that both G-HaplotypeCaller and D-BCFtools exhibit excellent sensitivity, and optimal variant-calling performance is depended on depth-aware filtering strategy.

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively evaluated the performance of the eight different variant calling pipelines that integrated two emerging aligners (BWA-MEM and DRAGEN-OS) and six state-of-the-art variant callers (BCFtools, DeepVariant, SpeedSeq, Strelka2, VarScan2, and HaplotypeCaller). Compared to previous benchmarking efforts relied on the most wildly used GIAB reference data for human sample NA12878, which was constructed from the consensus of multiple short-read variant callers, we sequenced a pair of female MZ twins aged 27 years and 6-year follow up when they were 33 years at an ~60× coverage. Thus, our benchmarking approach overcomes potential overfitting inherent in the reuse of tested variant callers in NA12878, providing an unbiased assessment of variant detection performance.

In order to assess the accuracy and efficiency of the eight distinct pipelines, we employed a golden standard set of PCR-validated variants detected in the same MZ twin genomes at 30× coverage. The ultralow-frequency alleles that distinguish this twin pairs provide a rigorous evaluate regime. GATK best practice is recognized as the most wildly used variant calling pipeline, as it applies local de novo assembly for haplotype reconstruction and a variety of advanced statistical methods (e.g. logistic regression, hidden Markov model and naïve Bayes classification) to accurately distinguish genuine genetic variants from sequencing artifacts. Indeed, G-HaplotypeCaller demonstrated excellent accuracy and robustness in this study. It also incorporates advanced base quality score recalibration, enabling excellent specificity even in low-depth dataset. This observation could be substantiated by relatively reduced read depths but significantly elevated quality scores than alternative pipelines (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Table S6). However, the computational demands of local reassembly and probabilistic modeling resulted in suboptimal runtime and memory efficiency. D-BCFtools as another top-performing variant caller, gained superior sensitivity from increased read depths attributed to simplified base recalibration and higher quality score. Furthermore, D-BCFtools demonstrated substantially enhanced running efficiency over GATK through hardware-based accelerations. It is therefore recommended that either the DRAGEON-OS aligner is used alone, or in combination, where BCFtools is used for downstream variant calling, both could provide an accurate and ultra-rapid solution for WGS data analysis. Notably, D-BCFtools performed compromising robustness with an average TPR, precision, recall, and F-measure value of 93.75%, 98.55%, 93.75%, and 96.09% based on 100 simulations, whereas G-HaplotypeCaller averaged to 95.30%, 99.88%, 95.30%, and 97.53%, respectively. Furthermore, we observed that both pipelines exhibited a minor decline in sensitivity after stringent quality control in the case of MZ genomes sequenced below 40× coverage. Therefore, a good post hoc filtering is extremely context specific and require consideration of each tool’s quality metrics. It should be note that the present study utilizes a benchmark dataset restricted to SNPs, as small INDELs failed experimental validation. We recognize that the inclusion of high-confidence INDELs would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of variant calling methodologies. Consequently, we plan to integrate INDELs into our benchmark once rigorously validated datasets become available, thereby enabling a more systematic evaluation of variant calling performance across diverse genomic alterations. Moreover, to ensure an unbiased comparison, all pipelines were run with their default parameters, except GATK VariantRecalibrator, where --max-gaussians was fixed at 4 to prevent overfitting by constraining model complexity and enhance model stability by promoting convergence. Systematic fine-tuning of the extensive parameters may yield additional gains for specific scenarios and represents a direction for our future work.

In conclusion, our benchmarking on real WGS datasets revealed that the DRAGEN and BCFtools pipelines have high accuracy and ultra-rapid efficiency for small variant detection, and therefore has significant potential for implementation in routine clinical and forensic WGS analysis, where speed may be of essence. G-HaplotypeCaller as the mainstay of germline variant analysis, it consistently achieved superior sensitivity and specificity among all evaluated pipelines, albeit with the cost of markedly decreased computational efficiency. Overall, these results provide empirically grounded guidance for selecting optimal variant detection strategies that best align with the accuracy, speed, and resource constraints inherent to specific analytical context.
