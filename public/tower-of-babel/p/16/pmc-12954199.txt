Microbial crosstalk along the oral–gut axis: organ-specific oncogenic adaptations of Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum
Authors: Shiwang Wu, Xinyi Ye, Jingsi Wang, Mei Li, Yage Fu, Shan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe oral-gut microbiota axis is increasingly recognized as a critical bridge linking local infection to systemic diseases. Growing evidence demonstrates that oral microbes not only enter the gut via saliva, bloodstream, and direct migration but also successfully colonize under specific microenvironmental conditions, reshaping the local microbial ecosystem. Periodontal pathogens such as P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum drive gastrointestinal tumorigenesis through chronic inflammation, metabolite production, and signaling pathway reprogramming. Moreover, oral–gut microbial interactions reveal cancer-specific risk patterns, with distinct roles in esophageal, gastric, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers. This review not only highlights the key mechanisms by which oral microbes promote tumor development but also emphasizes their clinical potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets, offering novel directions for translational research on the oral-gut axis.Key words: Oral microorganisms, Gut microbiota, Transmission and colonization, Carcinogenic substances, Signaling pathways, Gastrointestinal cancer

Introduction

The microbiota plays an increasingly pivotal role in the intricate network linking human health and disease. Recent studies confirm that microbiota inhabiting different body sites do not exist in isolation but interact through multiple pathways to collectively shape the host’s physiological state. As the two largest microbial habitats in the human body, the oral cavity and gut are linked by the continuous passage of the digestive tract, forming the so-called “oral–gut axis.” This axis serves not only as a key site for nutrient digestion and absorption but also as a critical interface for microbiota interactions that influence host health. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have elucidated the roles of oral and gut microbiota in the onset and progression of various diseases, including inflammatory disorders and cancers. Among these, gastrointestinal cancers are malignancies with high morbidity and mortality worldwide, and their association with microbiota has drawn considerable attention.

Historically, cancer research has largely focused on host genetic factors, lifestyle, and environmental exposures. However, advances in microbiome technologies have increasingly highlighted the long-overlooked role of microbiota—particularly bacteria—in tumor initiation and progression. As one of the most microbiologically diverse sites in the human body, the oral cavity is not only a source of pathogens causing various local diseases but may also influence distal organs, including the gastrointestinal tract, via the “oral–gut axis.” Periodontal pathogens may cause local inflammation in the oral cavity and disseminate to the intestine via saliva, swallowing, hematogenous spread, and other routes, thereby altering the composition and function of the gut microbiota and potentially contributing—directly or indirectly—to gastrointestinal tumorigenesis.

Although the role of oral microbes in gastrointestinal carcinogenesis has garnered increasing attention in recent years, most existing reviews focus on individual bacterial species or specific types of cancer, lacking a systematic overview of the entire carcinogenic pathway along the "oral–gut axis." In addition, how oral microorganisms cross the physiological barrier to colonize the gut, how they affect the host immune and inflammatory environment through multiple signaling pathways, and whether there is a common or specific mechanism in different cancers are still poorly understood. In this review, the whole process of transmission, colonization and signal regulation of P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum are systematically summarized, and the mechanism of action of P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum in esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, colorectal cancer and pancreatic cancer is compared. The aim of this study is to provide a theoretical basis and intervention strategies for the microecological diagnosis and treatment of gastrointestinal tumors.

The oral–gut microbiota axis: a novel perspective in gastrointestinal cancer

The role of oral microbes in gastrointestinal diseases and cancers

The oral microbiome is among the most complex and diverse microbial ecosystems in the human body, comprising over 700 bacterial species along with fungi, viruses, and archaea [1,2]. The oral cavity serves not only as the entry point for food into the digestive tract but also as a site of continuous interaction between microorganisms and the host immune system [3]. A healthy oral microbiota maintains a dynamic balance with the host and contributes to local physiological processes, including nutrient metabolism and immune system development [2,4]. However, disruption of this balance, leading to dysbiosis, can cause oral diseases such as dental caries and periodontitis, and may even impact systemic health [5].

In recent years, growing evidence has linked the oral microbiota to a range of systemic diseases, with gastrointestinal disorders and cancers attracting particular attention. Studies indicate that oral microbiota imbalance is closely associated with gastrointestinal inflammatory diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and plays a key role in the initiation and progression of gastrointestinal cancer [6,7]. Oral microorganisms can influence gastrointestinal health through multiple mechanisms. For example, they may directly reach the gut and alter its microbial composition and function [[8], [9], [10]].In addition, oral inflammation—particularly periodontitis—can trigger systemic inflammatory responses that indirectly modify the gastrointestinal microenvironment [11]. Moreover, metabolic products of oral microbes—such as acetaldehyde, hydrogen sulfide, butyric acid, indole, and nitrosamines—may exert carcinogenic or cancer promoting effects, enter the gastrointestinal tract, and influence tumor initiation and progression.

Epidemiological studies have shown that periodontitis is linked to an elevated risk of several gastrointestinal cancers, including esophageal, gastric, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers [[12], [13], [14]]. For instance, a prospective cohort study found that high P. gingivalis abundance was associated with an increased risk of pancreatic cancer [15], while another study reported F. nucleatum enrichment in colorectal cancer tissues, correlating with lymph node metastasis [16]. These findings suggest that oral microorganisms, particularly periodontal pathogens, may contribute to gastrointestinal cancer development via the “oral–gut axis.”

Concept and significance of the oral–gut axis in cancer research

The “oral–gut axis” concept underscores the interconnections between the oral and gut microbiota and its role in maintaining host health and influencing disease onset [17]. Although anatomically continuous, the oral cavity and gut harbor markedly different microbial communities, largely due to the oral–gut barrier [18,19]. This barrier comprises physical (e.g., esophageal and gastric anatomy), chemical (e.g., gastric acid and bile), and immunological (e.g., mucosal immune system) components that limit—but do not entirely prevent—the spread of oral microbes to the gut [20]. Under certain conditions—such as impaired barrier function or reduced host immunity—oral microorganisms can breach the barrier, colonize the gut, and alter its microenvironment [21,22].

The concept of the "oral–gut axis" holds significant theoretical and practical value in gastrointestinal cancer research, offering a novel perspective for understanding the initiation and progression of these malignancies. Traditional research on gastrointestinal cancer has primarily focused on local intestinal factors. However, the "oral–gut axis" concept highlights the need to incorporate the oral microbiota into etiological investigations and consider tumorigenesis from a broader, systemic perspective. The "oral–gut axis" also provides a theoretical foundation for developing novel strategies for the prevention and treatment of gastrointestinal cancer. For instance, limiting the transmission of oral pathogens through improved oral hygiene, or restoring a balanced microbial ecosystem by modulating the composition of oral and gut microbiota, may reduce the risk of gastrointestinal cancer and enhance treatment efficacy.

Moreover, the "oral–gut axis" offers a novel approach to the early diagnosis of gastrointestinal cancer. Huang’s study revealed that the salivary microbiota of patients with gastric cancer differs significantly from that of healthy individuals, and that changes in the abundance of certain oral bacteria may be associated with the initiation and progression of the disease [23]. Thus, the salivary microbiota holds potential as a non-invasive biomarker for the early detection of gastrointestinal cancer. Analyzing salivary microbiota composition could enable early risk assessment of gastrointestinal tumors, potentially facilitating timely intervention and improved patient outcomes.

Overall, the "oral–gut axis" represents a multi-level ecological interaction system that highlights how oral microecology may remotely influence the development of gastrointestinal tumors. However, a deeper understanding of this long-distance carcinogenic process requires elucidating the biological mechanisms by which oral microbes cross physiological barriers, migrate, and successfully colonize the gut. The following section will focus on the transmission routes and colonization mechanisms of oral microorganisms as critical prerequisites for their carcinogenic potential.

Transmission and colonization of oral microbes along the oral–gut axis

Routes of microbial dissemination: saliva, bloodstream, and direct migration

Transmission of oral microbiota to the gut is a complex process involving multiple routes, with saliva swallowing being the primary pathway [24]. The human body produces large volumes of saliva daily, rich in oral microorganisms. Swallowed saliva continuously delivers oral microorganisms into the digestive tract, eventually reaching the intestine [24,25]. F. nucleatum, abundant in the oral cavity of periodontitis patients, enters the gastrointestinal tract via saliva (0.75–1.5 L/day, containing 10^8–10^10 bacteria). Disruption of gastric acidity—such as from antibiotics or proton pump inhibitors—facilitates colonization of oral pathogens in the gut, impairing intestinal barrier function and promoting inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [26]. Although most oral bacteria cannot survive gastric acid and bile, some can withstand the harsh gastrointestinal environment and successfully colonize the intestine [27]. Jinuk Jeong et al. first demonstrated the dominance of Streptococcus and Haemophilus in the oral microbiome—centered on the “oral–gut” axis—using saliva and stool samples from 112 healthy Koreans. Co-occurrence network analysis suggested that this pathway may shape the gut microbiota structure via swallowing. This finding provides direct evidence for “ecological transmission” between oral and intestinal sites, but methodological limitations should be noted: saliva collection time and dietary status were not standardized, and the resolution of 16S amplicon sequencing was limited to the genus level, making it difficult to distinguish pathogenic from commensal strains. This may mask the potential pathogenic role of certain Streptococcus species, such as S. mutans, in intestinal inflammation [28]. In contrast, Karine Clement obtained duodenal fluid samples via endoscopy, combined oral and fecal microbiome analyses, and found that the duodenal microbiome more closely resembled the oral than the intestinal microbiome. Oral Streptococcus and Veillonella were enriched in the duodenum of obese individuals, with their abundance directly associated with dietary intake and metabolic disorders [29]. Notably, these two studies were limited to a healthy Korean cohort and a French cohort with morbid obesity.The generalizability of these findings across multiple centers, BMI ranges, and dietary patterns requires verification.

In addition to saliva swallowing, hematogenous dissemination represents another potential route for oral microorganisms to reach the gut [8]. Oral inflammation—particularly periodontitis—can cause vascular rupture in gingival tissue, allowing oral microorganisms and their metabolites to enter the bloodstream [30,31]. Certain pathogens, such as P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum, can disseminate to the intestinal tract via the bloodstream. These bacteria exploit host cells as “Trojan horses” to reach the gut via the bloodstream and colonize the intestinal mucosa [32,33]. In addition, invasive procedures such as oral surgery and dental treatments can introduce oral bacteria into the bloodstream, thereby increasing the risk of their dissemination to the gut [34]. Studies have shown that the incidence of bacteremia increases significantly after dental surgery, with oral bacteria such as Streptococcus and Actinomyces frequently detected in the bloodstream [[35], [36], [37]].

Direct migration represents another potential route for the transmission of oral microorganisms [38].Since the oral cavity and gut are anatomically connected via the digestive tract, oral microorganisms may directly migrate to the gut through the esophagus [39]. The esophagus serves as a conduit between the mouth and stomach, and during swallowing, saliva and food particles may carry oral microorganisms that colonize the esophageal mucosa. The esophageal pH (∼7.0) in healthy individuals creates a favorable environment for the survival of oral microorganisms [40,41]. Current data indicate that operational taxonomic units (OTUs) are highly similar among the tongue coating, saliva, and esophageal mucosa in healthy individuals [40,42]. In patients with esophageal diseases, the esophageal microbiota composition closely resembles that of the oral cavity, suggesting that oral microbes can migrate directly to the lower gastrointestinal tract via the esophagus [[43], [44], [45]]. In addition, decreased gastric acid secretion, impaired gastrointestinal motility, or gastroesophageal reflux can compromise the esophageal mucosal barrier, increasing the likelihood of oral microbial colonization [46,47].The use of proton pump inhibitors reduces gastric acid secretion, which enhances the survival of oral microorganisms in the esophagus and stomach and facilitates their further migration to the lower gastrointestinal tract [48] (Fig. 1).

Factors influencing gut colonization: host immunity and intestinal microenvironment

Once oral microorganisms reach the intestine, their colonization and activity are influenced by multiple factors.The host immune system serves as a key barrier against colonization by foreign microorganisms. Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) in the intestinal mucosal immune system detect oral microbiota-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), activating innate immune cells—such as macrophages and dendritic cells—to secrete interleukin 23 (IL 23). This, in turn, drives the differentiation of T helper 17 (Th17) cells and the release of effector molecules—such as interleukin 17A (IL-17A) and interleukin 22 (IL-22)—while inducing intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) to produce antimicrobial peptides and regenerating family member 3 (Reg3) to combat intestinal bacterial infection [49,50]. In addition, secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) is critical for mucosal immune defense. sIgA is synthesized by local mucosal immune tissues in response to intestinal antigens and secreted by epithelial cells onto the mucosal surface, where it specifically eliminates pathogens [51,52]. Under homeostatic conditions, CD4⁺ regulatory T (Treg) cells maintain immune tolerance by secreting interleukin 10 (IL 10), thereby eliminating invading ectopic bacteria and preventing their overgrowth and colonization in the intestine [53,54]. However, during impaired host immunity or intestinal inflammation—such as in IBD—the expression of the tight junction protein occludin (OCLN) is downregulated, compromising intestinal barrier integrity and reducing immune surveillance. Concurrently, Treg cell numbers in the intestinal lamina propria decrease, and the balance between pro inflammatory and anti inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6/IL-10) is disrupted.This creates opportunities for oral microorganisms to breach the “ecological isolation” barrier [55,56].

Physical and chemical features of the intestinal microenvironment—such as pH, oxygen tension, and bile acid concentration—also influence oral microbial colonization. Streptococcus maintains intracellular pH homeostasis under acidic conditions by upregulating the arginine deiminase (Arc) system and using arginine metabolism to generate ATP and release ammonia [57,58]. P. gingivalis resists bile acid toxicity by overexpressing an efflux pump; its genome encodes a bile salt hydrolase (BSH) homolog that modifies bile acid structure and reduces antimicrobial activity [59]. The gut is a complex ecosystem inhabited by a large community of resident microbiota [60]. Resident gut microbiota exert competitive exclusion through “nutritional priority” mechanisms. Butyrate-producing bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, consume short-chain fatty acid precursors via cross feeding, limiting energy availability to foreign bacteria [61,62].Consequently, oral bacteria must compete with resident gut flora for space and nutrients to establish successful colonization.

Moreover, intrinsic traits of oral bacteria determine their colonization capacity in the gut.The colonization advantage of F. nucleatum arises from its multimodal adhesion system: Fap2 achieves initial attachment by recognizing galactose-N-acetylgalactosamine glycans (Gal-GalNAc) on intestinal epithelial cells. The FadA protein disrupts cell junctions via the β-catenin/E-cadherin signaling pathway, facilitating trans-epithelial migration and thereby promoting easier colonization in the intestine [63,64].In contrast, P. gingivalis overcomes host defenses through synergistic virulence mechanisms: gingipains cleave immunoglobulin heavy chains, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) activates the NF-κB pathway via the Toll like receptor 2/4–MyD88 signaling axis. Additionally, secreted outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) act as “Trojan horses,” delivering virulence factors to distant organs and facilitating colonization [59].

Once oral microorganisms breach multiple physiological barriers and colonize the gut, they cease to be passive bystanders and instead act as active “microecological participants” influencing host physiology and disease development. They can actively contribute to the initiation and progression of gastrointestinal tumors through multiple mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, secretion of carcinogenic metabolites, activation of signaling pathways, and modulation of the immune microenvironment.The following sections will systematically examine these mechanisms, detailing their specific pathways and carcinogenic potential.

Mechanistic pathways linking oral microbes to gastrointestinal tumorigenesis

Chronic inflammation as a driver of oral microbe–mediated carcinogenesis

Periodontitis and systemic inflammatory responses

Chronic inflammation is a key driver in the development of numerous diseases, including cancer [65].As a prevalent chronic inflammatory condition, periodontitis extends beyond the periodontal tissues, exerting systemic biological effects—including on the gastrointestinal tract—via the “oral–systemic axis” [66]. Periodontitis results from dysbiosis of the oral microbiota and is characterized by gingival inflammation and destruction of periodontal tissues [67]. In periodontitis, the oral microbiota undergoes marked compositional shifts, including niche expansion of P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum and the formation of pathogenic biofilms dominated by the “red complex” [68]. These pathogens not only directly damage periodontal tissues but also trigger excessive host inflammatory responses, releasing large quantities of mediators such as tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin 1β (IL-1β), and interleukin 6 (IL-6) [69]. The inflammation induced by periodontitis is not confined to the oral cavity but can disseminate systemically, triggering a body-wide inflammatory response [70].Mukherjee et al. reported significantly elevated serum inflammatory mediator levels in patients with periodontitis, indicating that the disease can induce a systemic inflammatory state [71]. Systemic inflammation is recognized as a common pathological basis for many chronic diseases, including cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disorders, impaired glucose metabolism, rheumatoid arthritis, and malignancies [72].

Chronic inflammation plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of gastrointestinal cancers [73]. First, systemic inflammation driven by chronic oral diseases such as periodontitis can amplify inflammatory activity in gastrointestinal tissues, creating a microenvironment conducive to tumorigenesis [25,74]. Second, infiltrating immune cells—including macrophages and neutrophils—within the tumor microenvironment (TME) establish a pro-tumor inflammatory network via secretion of mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6, directly promoting tumor cell proliferation and metastasis [75,76]. Studies indicate that TNF-α enhances SNAIL transcription factor stability by activating the protein kinase B/glycogen synthase kinase 3β (AKT/GSK-3β) pathway, while also driving epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and increasing cancer cell invasiveness [77]. TNF-α can further upregulate SNAIL and TWIST expression via the NF-κB signaling axis and act synergistically with the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathways to promote tumor cell migration and dissemination [78,79]. IL-6 upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) via the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling axis, thereby promoting angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and subsequent tumor growth and invasion [80,81].

Pro-inflammatory mechanisms of P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum

P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum are two of the most clinically significant pathogens in periodontitis and are major drivers of periodontal tissue destruction. Both exhibit potent pro inflammatory activity, inducing host inflammatory responses via multiple mechanisms and thereby contributing to the initiation and progression of gastrointestinal cancers.

P. gingivalis is a Gram-negative anaerobe that activates host inflammatory responses through multiple pathways:

1.TLR signaling pathway: P. gingivalis differentially activates Toll-like receptor (TLR) family members via lipid A structural heterogeneity in its LPS, establishing a dynamic balance between pro- inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses. Pentacylated lipid A (LPS1690) activates the NF-κB pathway via the TLR4/MD-2 complex, markedly increasing IL-6, IL-8, and CXCL10, whereas tetracylated lipid A (LPS1435) acts as a TLR4 antagonist and suppresses anti-inflammatory IL-10 production [[82], [83], [84], [85]]. In addition, gingipains degrade complement components C3 and C5a, thereby preventing formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) [86,87]. Concurrently, P. gingivalis exploits the C5a receptor (C5aR) to reverse-activate TLR2 signaling. This complement–TLR cross-regulation decouples pro-inflammatory responses from pathogen clearance, fostering an “inflammatory tolerance” state [86,88]. Moreover, the C5aR–TLR2 pathway induces degradation of the autophagy-related protein LC3-II and blocks lysosomal fusion, thereby facilitating bacterial persistence within host cells [[89], [90], [91]]. During chronic infection, P. gingivalis suppresses TLR2 and NOD2 promoter activity by inducing DNA methylation reprogramming in host cells. For instance, in gingival epithelial cells, P. gingivalis infection induces CpG island hypermethylation in the TLR2 promoter and reduces chromatin accessibility via histone deacetylase (HDAC) recruitment, ultimately impairing initial inflammatory signal recognition [[92], [93], [94]]. Furthermore, OMVs carrying miRNA-584 target lactoferrin receptor (LfR) mRNA, suppress its translation, and reduce anti-inflammatory factor secretion, thereby reinforcing a pro-inflammatory phenotype through positive feedback [95,96].

2.NLRP3 inflammasome: P. gingivalis activates the NLRP3 inflammasome through multiple mechanisms, including ATP release and P2X7 receptor activation. These mechanisms collectively promote the secretion of IL-1β and interleukin-18 (IL-18), thereby initiating the inflammatory response. Studies have shown that P. gingivalis triggers the NF-κB pathway through TLR2/TLR4-dependent signaling, upregulates pro-IL-1β expression, and subsequently activates NLRP3 inflammasome assembly via the ATP–P2X7receptor axis and potassium efflux caused by lysosomal damage. Caspase-1 is ultimately responsible for the maturation and secretion of IL-1β [97,98]. P. gingivalis can also enhance P2X7 receptor signaling by releasing gingipains, which cleave host complement C5 to generate C5a, thereby amplifying NLRP3 inflammasome activation [[99], [100], [101]]. Additionally, deficiency of the melanoma 2 (AIM2) inflammasome contributes to IL-18 secretion by recognizing bacterial DNA, resulting in a synergistic pro-inflammatory effect [102,103].

3.Regulation of proinflammatory factors: P. gingivalis promotes inflammation by modulating the host chemokine network through multiple virulence factors. The capsular polysaccharide of P. gingivalis serotype K1 directly induces macrophages to secrete CXCL8, thereby attracting neutrophils and monocytes to the infection site and exacerbating local inflammation [104]. Mfa1 stimulates IL-8 and CXCL1 secretion in gingival fibroblasts and endothelial cells by activating the TLR2/4 signaling pathway, which in turn triggers the NF-κB and MAPK pathways [105]. Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) containing LPS and proteases upregulate ICAM-1 and IL-8 via the PAR-2 receptor, promoting transendothelial migration of neutrophils and monocytes [106]. In addition to K1 capsular polysaccharide, Mfa1, and OMVs, P. gingivalis also degrades VE-cadherin junctions between vascular endothelial cells via gingipains, activating the NF-κB pathway. This stimulation leads endothelial cells to secrete CXCL8 and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), thereby recruiting monocytes and promoting their differentiation into pro-inflammatory macrophages [107]. Notably, P. gingivalis can also target host signaling pathways by secreting specific phosphatase SerB, which inhibits the activities of NF-κB and STAT1, leading to a significant reduction in the expression of chemokines CXCL8 and CXCL10 [108,109]. This "chemokine paralysis" effect not only hinders the recruitment of neutrophils but also impairs local immune surveillance, allowing persistent bacterial colonization and chronic inflammation in tumor tissue [110]. However, this immunosuppressive effect is temporally and spatially limited, and may indirectly trigger systemic inflammation through periodic tissue damage [108]. In addition, P. gingivalis induces N2-type polarization of tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) by upregulating neutrophil chemokines CXCL1 and CXCL2. These TANs secrete neutrophil elastase (NE) and myeloperoxidase (MPO), thereby promoting tumor angiogenesis and immune evasion [[111], [112], [113]].

Fusobacterium nucleatum is a Gram-negative anaerobe that serves as a bridging bacterium in oral biofilms, contributing to the formation of complex biofilm structures. It possesses strong pro-inflammatory capabilities and can induce inflammatory responses through multiple mechanisms.

1.Adhesion and invasion mechanisms: F. nucleatum facilitates adhesion and invasion by binding to host cell receptors via its surface adhesin, FadA. FadA binds to E-cadherin, activates the β-catenin signaling pathway, upregulates oncogenes including cyclin D1 and c-Myc, and induces the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-8 and CXCL1 [114,115]. Fap2 enhances bacterial colonization in the tumor microenvironment by binding to Gal-GalNAc glycans on the surface of tumor cells [116]. F. nucleatum also evades host immune responses and facilitates the co-aggregation of other pathogenic bacteria through biofilm formation [114].

2.Pro-inflammatory effects of outer membrane vesicles (OMVs): OMVs released by F. nucleatum carry lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which activates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and triggers downstream NF-κB signaling, thereby promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-8, TNF-α, and IL-6, contributing to an inflammatory microenvironment [117]. OMVs also induce apoptosis of epithelial cells via the RIPK1/RIPK3 pathway, reduce the expression of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and occludin, thereby disrupting the intestinal barrier, increasing intestinal permeability, and promoting the translocation of bacteria and toxins [55,118].

3.Activation of the TLR/NF-κB signaling pathway: F. nucleatum activates the NF-κB signaling cascade via the TLR4-MyD88 pathway and upregulates miR-21 expression, which inhibits the RAS GTPase RASA1, thereby promoting cell proliferation and invasion [119]. It also activates TLR2 and TLR9, whose synergistic effects enhance the secretion of IL-1β and IL-6, contributing to a tumor-promoting microenvironment [120]. Both P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum induce inflammatory responses via TLR signaling pathways, but their regulatory mechanisms differ significantly. P. gingivalis achieves bidirectional regulation of TLR4 through polymorphisms in its lipid A structure and exploits complement-TLR2 interactions. It also employs multiple regulatory mechanisms, including epigenetic modifications and autophagy, to establish an inflammation-tolerant state that favors its survival. In contrast, F. nucleatum activates a simpler signaling route via the combined stimulation of TLR4, TLR2, and TLR9. This leads to sustained activation of the NF-κB pathway and promotes the formation of a tumor-supportive microenvironment.

4.Immune cell regulation and chronic inflammation: F. nucleatum promotes M1 macrophage polarization and stimulates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, while simultaneously inhibiting the anti-inflammatory phenotype of M2 macrophages [121,122]. It also induces the differentiation of Th1 and Th17 cells by upregulating IL-12 and IL-23, resulting in sustained inflammatory damage to intestinal tissues [121,123,124]. Finally, the Fap2 protein binds to the TIGIT receptor, suppressing the cytotoxic function of natural killer (NK) cells and protecting tumor cells from immune attack [125,126].

5.Dysbiosis of the intestinal microbiota: After colonizing the gut, F. nucleatum undergoes excessive proliferation, competing with commensal bacteria such as Enterococcus faecalis for ecological niches and disrupting microbial colonization balance. This leads to a reduction in beneficial bacteria like Bifidobacterium, and an increase in opportunistic pathogens such as Escherichia coli-Shigella, thereby altering the overall composition of the intestinal microbiota [127,128]. The resulting dysbiosis increases pro-inflammatory bacterial abundance, reduces anti-inflammatory species, and disrupts microbial metabolite homeostasis, ultimately fostering a pro-inflammatory microenvironment that exacerbates intestinal inflammation [128].

In addition to promoting tumor microenvironment remodeling through chronic inflammation, oral pathogens also contribute to carcinogenesis via their secreted metabolites. Growing evidence indicates that these metabolites possess direct genotoxicity and immunomodulatory properties, and synergize with host metabolic imbalances to drive carcinogenic events, including DNA damage and activation of oncogenic signaling pathways. The following section provides a detailed analysis of the types and mechanisms of these metabolites, emphasizing their pivotal roles in tumor initiation and progression.

Microbial metabolites as carcinogenic mediators

Acetaldehyde and alcohol metabolism synergy

Microbial metabolites serve as key mediators in microorganism–host interactions. Certain microbial metabolites possess carcinogenic or tumor-promoting properties. Acetaldehyde, a metabolite generated by the oral microbiota, is a carcinogen of considerable concern. It is an intermediate product of ethanol metabolism with pronounced toxicity and carcinogenic potential [129]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies acetaldehyde as a Group 1 carcinogen, confirming its carcinogenicity in humans [130]. In humans, ethanol is primarily metabolized in the liver via enzymes such as alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH), and cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1), producing the highly reactive metabolite acetaldehyde [131,132]. Acetaldehyde exhibits high affinity for DNA, inducing multiple carcinogenic effects and genetic damage. Within the oral microbiota, genera including Neisseria, Rothia, Streptococcus, and Prevotella can metabolize ethanol to acetaldehyde. Neisseria exhibits exceptionally high acetaldehyde-producing capacity in vitro, whereas Streptococcus mitis, Prevotella histicola, and Rothia mucilaginosa also display notable metabolic activity [133]. Saliva samples from type I communities (dominated by Streptococcus and Rothia) showed significantly greater acetaldehyde-producing potential than type II communities (dominated by Neisseria), despite the latter’s lower relative abundance in type I communities. This finding derives from the clinical study by Yokoyama et al., who collected stimulated saliva from 100 healthy men, analyzed community composition using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and measured acetaldehyde-producing capacity under natural oral conditions [134]. Concerning the primary source of acetaldehyde, notable discrepancies exist between Moritani’s and Yokoyama’s studies: Moritani's in vitro experiments (controlled culture system) clearly indicated that Neisseria is an efficient producer of acetaldehyde, while Yokoyama's clinical research (in vivo complex community environment) found a significant negative correlation between the relative abundance of Neisseria and acetaldehyde production capacity in the in vivo environment, suggesting that intra-community interactions (e.g., metabolic inhibition or gene-expression regulation) may account for the differing metabolic profiles of Neisseria in vitro versus in vivo. Furthermore, although Neisseria abundance is low in type I communities, overall acetaldehyde-producing capacity is higher, indicating that the functional characteristics of the oral microbiota community (such as the synergistic metabolism of multiple genera) may be more important than the abundance of a single genus in determining the acetaldehyde production level and, consequently, the risk of carcinogenesis. This functional difference at the community level is precisely the characteristic of the complex in vivo ecosystem that is difficult to capture in in vitro single-strain experiments.

Acetaldehyde, a highly reactive compound, can specifically bind to DNA molecules, forming mutagenic DNA adducts. These adducts alter the three-dimensional structure of DNA and markedly inhibit its replication and repair [135]. More importantly, they exert strong genotoxic effects by inducing DNA damage mechanisms, including base substitutions and double-strand breaks, as well as cytogenetic alterations such as sister chromatid exchanges and chromosomal rearrangements [[136], [137], [138]]. Acetaldehyde also affects DNA methylation, leading to altered expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. Mechanistically, acetaldehyde inhibits the enzymatic activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and reduces DNMT mRNA levels, thereby decreasing DNMT protein synthesis. Additionally, acetaldehyde may interfere with the synthesis of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), thereby disrupting normal DNA methylation processes [139]. During ethanol metabolism, the accumulation of reactive oxygen species and electrophilic compounds—particularly acetaldehyde and lipid peroxidation products such as 4-hydroxynonenal—can damage DNA structure and ultimately promote malignant cellular transformation [140].

Oral diseases, including poor oral hygiene and periodontitis, can disrupt the balance of the oral microbiota, leading to an increased abundance of acetaldehyde-producing bacteria and fungi and, consequently, elevated acetaldehyde levels in the oral cavity [141]. Acetaldehyde in the oral cavity directly contacts the oral mucosa, exerting toxic effects such as DNA damage, abnormal cell proliferation, and increased risk of oral cancer [142,143]. In addition, acetaldehyde produced orally can enter the digestive tract through swallowed saliva, exerting toxic effects on the esophageal and gastric mucosa and elevating the risk of esophageal and gastric cancers [[144], [145], [146]] (Table 1).

Hydrogen sulfide and other microbial metabolites

In addition to acetaldehyde, the oral microbiota produces various metabolites—such as hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), butyrate, indole, and nitrosamines—that may exert carcinogenic or tumor-promoting effects. Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) is a volatile, pungent gas and a bioactive molecule generated during the metabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids [147]. It is synthesized by various bacteria, notably P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum [147]. At physiological concentrations, H₂S exerts protective effects on multiple organ systems, including the cardiovascular, renal, hepatic, nervous, and skeletal systems [[148], [149], [150], [151], [152]]. These effects involve mechanisms such as antioxidant activity, inflammation modulation, and regulation of cell signaling. However, excessive H₂S becomes cytotoxic, primarily by inhibiting cytochrome c oxidase—a key enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain—thereby disrupting cellular energy metabolism and ultimately inducing apoptosis or necroptosis [153,154]. Excess H₂S may also damage DNA structure by inducing oxidative stress or directly interacting with genetic material [155]. In the tumor microenvironment, H₂S promotes cancer cell proliferation via signaling pathways including EGFR/ERK/MMP-2 and PTEN/AKT [156]. It also upregulates VEGF expression to stimulate angiogenesis and enhances tumor-cell invasion and metastasis by modulating extracellular-matrix remodeling and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [157,158].

Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid, is produced by intestinal bacteria through fermentation of dietary fiber [159]. It exhibits diverse biological activities, including anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor effects [159,160]. However, the oral environment differs substantially from that of the gut. In the oral cavity, P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, and Treponema denticola can also produce butyrate during metabolism [161,162]. In chronic inflammatory settings such as periodontitis, bacterial butyrate production in the oral cavity may exert carcinogenic effects. Studies indicate that oral-bacterial butyrate may promote cancer-cell proliferation and metastasis by inducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), upregulating matrix metalloproteinases, altering immune responses, and modulating cellular metabolism and acid–base balance [[163], [164], [165], [166]]. Evidence suggests that indole may exert carcinogenic or tumor-promoting effects. In the oral microecosystem, P. gingivalis secretes indole compounds [167]. These microbiota-derived indoles may influence the intestinal environment through two mechanisms: on the one hand, as aryl hydrocarbon receptor ligands, they participate in immune regulation and maintain intestinal homeostasis at physiological concentrations, whereas abnormal concentrations may elevate tumor risk [[168], [169], [170]]. On the other hand, it can directly act on intestinal epithelial cells to regulate their proliferation, differentiation and apoptosis, and participate in the occurrence and development of cancer [171,172].

Nitrosamines are potent carcinogens formed when nitrate-reducing bacteria convert dietary nitrate to nitrite, which subsequently reacts with amines [173,174]. Oral microorganisms such as Streptococcus and Veillonella convert dietary nitrate to nitrite, which reacts with amines to form nitrosamines, which may increase the risk of gastric cancers [23]. In terms of molecular mechanism, the carcinogenic mechanism of nitrosamines mainly involves two key links: Firstly, nitrosamine molecules undergo hydroxylation reaction under the catalysis of cytochrome P450 enzymes to generate highly reactive alkylation intermediates (such as alkyl diazonium ions, etc.). These active metabolites can form covalent binding with DNA molecules, thereby compromising the structural integrity of genetic material [175]. Secondly, after metabolic activation of nitrosamine derivatives such as N-nitrophenylamine, their nitrobenzene groups can form specific adducts with DNA bases, leading to abnormal changes in genetic information [175,176]. Additionally, nitrosamines promote ROS generation [177], leading to oxidative-stress–induced DNA damage and elevating the risk of malignant transformation [178] (Fig. 2).

Oral microorganisms can produce various metabolic products such as acetaldehyde, hydrogen sulfide, butyric acid, indole and nitrosamines, which have potential carcinogenic or cancer-promoting effects.

Signaling pathways modulated by oral pathogens

NF-κB signaling: inflammation and tumor progression

Signaling pathways are essential intracellular networks that transmit and regulate information, governing processes such as cell growth, differentiation, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses. The oral microbiota can modulate host cell signaling via multiple mechanisms, thereby influencing the onset and progression of diseases, including cancer. The nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway is a central regulator of inflammation and cell proliferation, playing a pivotal role in tumor initiation and progression.

1.NF-κB activation and inflammation: As a central hub of inflammatory regulation, the NF-κB pathway is crucial in oral microbiota-mediated chronic inflammation. P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum activate NF-κB via TLR-dependent mechanisms, markedly upregulating inflammatory mediators (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and causing tissue inflammation [[179], [180], [181]]. Additionally, Treponema denticola activates both NF-κB and MAPK pathways via TLR2, promoting secretion of IL-36γ and exacerbating gingival inflammation [182]. Clinically, P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum act as “initiators” of lesions, whereas T. denticola functions as an “amplifier,” intensifying disease progression. This division of labor forms a critical link in the inflammatory cascade.It also identifies molecular targets for stratified, targeted interventions.

These findings indicate that oral-microbe–regulated pathways interact extensively, forming a complex network that drives both inflammation and tumorigenesis. On one hand, P. gingivalis activates the IL-6→JAK2/STAT3 cascade via NF-κB, amplifying IL-6–mediated inflammation and promoting tumor proliferation and metastasis—linking inflammation to carcinogenesis. Additionally, T. denticola co-activates NF-κB and MAPK, upregulating IL-36γ and producing a synergistic “amplification effect” on inflammation. This synergy may result from shared upstream receptors (e.g., TLR2) or downstream transcription factors (e.g., AP-1, NF-κB, MAPK), co-regulating target genes to intensify pathology. On the other hand, microbial metabolites like LPS and H₂O₂ initiate multi-pathway responses via distinct receptors: LPS from P. gingivalis activates NF-κB (and potentially MAPK via TLR4), while H₂O₂ from S. sanguinis triggers NF-κB and may indirectly modulate JAK/STAT through oxidative stress. This “one-cause–multiple-effects” pattern enables a single microbe or its products to influence host cells through an interconnected multi-pathway network.

MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and Wnt signaling pathways in cancer development

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway is a central signaling cascade that regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, programmed cell death, and inflammatory responses [192]. Oral microorganisms can activate the MAPK pathway via TLR2 and TLR4, thereby enhancing tumor-cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [193,194]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), MAPK activation further accelerates tumor-cell proliferation and metastasis [195]. The phosphoinositide 3 kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) pathway is another critical regulator of cell growth, metabolic homeostasis, and survival [[196], [197], [198]]. P. gingivalis promotes tumor-cell survival by activating the PI3K/Akt pathway and suppressing apoptosis [199]. Aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway—critical for embryonic development and tissue homeostasis—is strongly linked to multiple tumor types [200]. F. nucleatum promotes CRC progression via two mechanisms: (1) upregulating cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (Cdk5) to activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling, enhancing cell proliferation and migration [201]; and (2) using its FadA adhesin to bind E-cadherin on CRC cells, triggering β-catenin signaling to regulate inflammatory and oncogenic responses [202]. The cancer-promoting effect of F. nucleatum in CRC is largely Wnt/β-catenin-dependent, reflecting the intrinsic biology of CRC cells, where Wnt pathway activation is a common molecular hallmark. By exploiting “weak links” in this pathway—such as E-cadherin expression or Cdk5 regulation—F. nucleatum exerts a targeted pro-tumor effect in CRC.In contrast, NF-κB activation by F. nucleatum in CRC is primarily linked to shaping the inflammatory microenvironment rather than serving as a core oncogenic driver. Beyond these classical pathways, oral microorganisms can also modulate JAK/STAT, TGF-β, and Hippo signaling, contributing to the initiation and progression of gastrointestinal malignancies. Nouri et al. reported that alterations in the oral microbiota can activate the IL-6/STAT3 pathway, intensifying inflammation and increasing carcinogenic risk [203].

Regulation of signaling pathways by the oral microbiota is complex, often involving multiple bacterial species and intersecting pathways. Different oral bacterial species may modulate distinct pathways, while the same species can influence multiple pathways via diverse mechanisms. Host-cell genetic background, immune status, and other factors further modulate how oral microbiota regulate signaling pathways.

Interactions between oral and gut microbiota ecosystems

Oral microbes shaping gut microbiota composition

The oral and gut microbiota are interconnected via the “oral–gut axis,” interacting to jointly influence host health [55]. Oral pathogens reach the intestine via saliva, swallowing, hematogenous spread, and other routes, where they compete and interact with resident gut microbial communities [8,58].These interactions manifest as competition for nutrients and ecological niches, or as mutual inhibition or facilitation among microorganisms [58,204]. One study reported that oral-derived Streptococcus and Veillonella can overcome physical and microbial barriers to colonize the gut, with operational taxonomic unit (OTU) counts declining markedly from the stomach and small intestine to the distal gut [27,205]. Once established, these oral bacteria compete for nutrients and niches with dominant native gut genera such as Bacteroides and Firmicutes [27,205]. P. gingivalis, a key species, shows positive correlations with dominant oral bacteria and negative correlations with the gut-dominant genus Turicibacter, suggesting that oral bacteria can reshape intestinal communities, particularly in the small intestine [27].

However, oral dysbiosis can exert broader impacts on gut microbiota composition. Oral dysbiosis is frequently linked to diseases such as periodontitis, characterized by overgrowth of pathogenic species and depletion of beneficial taxa [206,207]. When dysbiotic oral communities migrate to the gut, they often exhibit non-homeostatic states dominated by pathogenic taxa. Such aberrantly colonizing oral microbes can disrupt intestinal microecological balance, induce dysbiosis, exacerbate inflammation, and increase the risk of intestinal tumors [7].

Abdelbary et al. [208]demonstrated a close association between oral and intestinal dysbiosis. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) microbiome analyses reveal imbalances in both gut flora and the oral microecosystem. Salivary microbial communities in IBD patients differ markedly from those of healthy individuals. Veillonella and Prevotella exhibit significant overgrowth. Neisseria, Streptococcus, Haemophilus, and Fusobacterium are closely linked to intestinal health. Stool analyses revealed increased colonization by pathogenic taxa such as Clostridium sensu stricto 1 and Escherichia–Shigella in IBD patients; these organisms promote inflammation, stimulate pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and damage the intestinal mucosal barrier. Notably, Streptococcus was the only genus detected in both oral and gut environments, suggesting the oral cavity may act as an important reservoir for enteric strains. Using microbial culture and phylogenetic analysis, researchers isolated numerous Streptococcus strains from IBD patients and healthy controls, confirming high genetic homology between oral- and gut-derived isolates from the same individual, thereby reinforcing the oral cavity as a potential source of enteric Streptococcus. These findings indicate that oral dysbiosis may contribute to IBD pathogenesis by altering gut microbial composition, providing molecular epidemiological evidence for “oral microbiota–driven intestinal inflammation.” However, the small sample size (n = 14) limits the generalizability of strain-traceability conclusions. Chen [209] examined interactions between oral–gut axis microbiota and Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori). H. pylori is a major gastric pathogen linked to multiple diseases, including gastric cancer [210]. Chen’s [209]findings indicate that H. pylori infection disrupts the gastric environment, reshaping oral and gut microbiota and turning the stomach into a conduit for cross-site microbial interactions. H. pylori is detectable in both oral cavity and gut, and its interactions with oral–gut axis microbiota influence colonization, infection, and pathogenicity. Infection or eradication of H. pylori, along with its interplay with oral–intestinal axis microbiota, can alter microecological balance and influence disease onset and progression. However, Chen did not define the time frame for H. pylori migration from oral cavity to stomach, and spatiotemporal dynamics remain unmonitored. Future studies should integrate multi-omics analyses (metatranscriptomics, metabolomics, and single-cell immunomics) to elucidate microbial functional activity, and employ organoid models to validate pathogenic mechanisms.

Reciprocal effects of gut dysbiosis on the oral microenvironment

Interactions between the oral and gut microbiota are bidirectional, and evidence indicates that gut dysbiosis can, in turn, influence the oral microenvironment. Mechanistically, gut microbiota imbalance can modulate the oral microenvironment through multiple pathways. Damage to the intestinal barrier allows bacteria and inflammatory mediators to enter the systemic circulation. Cytokines released during the intestinal inflammatory response can trigger systemic inflammation, disrupt oral homeostasis, and promote the proliferation of oral pathogens [211,212]. Additionally, alterations in gut microbiota can influence oral microbial activity via specific metabolic pathways, such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) production [213,214]. Several clinical studies have substantiated this reverse effect. Patients with liver cirrhosis—a chronic liver disease frequently associated with gut dysbiosis—often exhibit marked oral microbiota alterations, including increased pathogenic bacteria and reduced health-associated taxa. These findings suggest a close relationship between oral and gut microbiota alterations during disease progression [215,216]. At the basic research level, a genome-wide association study (GWAS) further revealed potential links between gut microbiota composition and oral diseases. The data indicated that Enterobacteriales, Bacteroidales S24.7, Lachnospiraceae UCG-008, Prevotella 7, and Pasteurellales may increase periodontitis risk, whereas Ruminiclostridium 6 may reduce it. Reverse analysis revealed significant alterations in six gut microbial taxa in periodontitis patients, directly linking gut microbial shifts to the onset and progression of oral inflammatory diseases [217].

Mechanistic basis of oral–gut microbiota interactions

Oral dysbiosis can influence the intestinal microenvironment via multiple mechanisms, altering gut microbiota composition and function, thereby promoting gastrointestinal cancer development. Conversely, gut dysbiosis may affect the oral microenvironment, exacerbate inflammation, and create a vicious cycle.

The mechanisms underlying oral–gut microbiota interactions can be summarized as follows:

1. Bidirectional cross-habitat migration: Oral bacteria may reach the gut via enteral routes (saliva, food) or through hematogenous spread from periodontal inflammatory sites, whereas gut microbes may migrate retrogradely to the oral cavity via the fecal–oral route. This bidirectional migration can reshape the microbial ecology of both sites, thereby impacting host physiological homeostasis [218,219]. 2. Immunomodulatory network: Oral microbiota can modulate host immunity by activating pattern recognition receptors (e.g., TLRs), inducing antimicrobial peptide secretion, and enhancing secretory immunoglobulin A (SIgA) production. In periodontal disease, oral pathogen-reactive Th17 cells can migrate to the gut via lymphatic circulation and exacerbate inflammation, such as colitis, once the intestinal mucosa is activated by homologous pathogens [220,221]. 3. Metabolic cross-regulation: Oral microbiota can influence gut microbiota composition through metabolite production or ecological competition. For instance, periodontitis-associated oral bacteria may reshape gut microbial structure and modify metabolic potential via saliva transmission [222,223]. 4. Disease association spectrum: Dysbiosis of oral or gut microbiota contributes to various diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal and pancreatic cancers, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, neurodegenerative disorders, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular diseases. For example, F. nucleatum is significantly more abundant in the tumor tissues and saliva of colorectal cancer patients than in healthy individuals, making it a typical disease-association marker [222,224]. 5. Biofilm-mediated pathology: Oral and intestinal microbes can form biofilms as a means to evade host immune defenses. Such structured communities are closely linked to the progression of dental caries, periodontal disease, and intestinal inflammation. For instance, pathogens within oral biofilms can exacerbate distal intestinal injury by enhancing drug resistance and virulence factor secretion [225]. 6. Specific microbiota interactions: Certain bacteria colonize both oral and intestinal niches, and their interactions may contribute to disease development. For example, Bacteroides fragilis and Clostridium perfringens, as well as Bifidobacterium adolescentis and C. perfringens, exhibit significant interaction effects. Such interactions may influence host health by modulating the local microenvironment [226]. 7. Cross-boundary regulation of H. pylori: H. pylori infection disrupts gastric acid homeostasis, weakens the oral–gut barrier, and enhances cross-boundary interactions between the two microbiota, ultimately reshaping their composition and functional characteristics [209].

The above section summarizes how oral pathogens contribute to gastrointestinal tumors through mechanisms including immune regulation, metabolite release, signaling pathway activation, and microbiota interactions. These mechanisms form common pathways through which the “oral–gut axis” influences host tumor susceptibility. However, gastrointestinal cancers differ markedly in anatomy, microecological background, and carcinogenic mechanisms. This review focuses on four major gastrointestinal cancers—esophageal, gastric, colorectal, and pancreatic—to explore the specific mechanisms and clinical relevance of oral microbiota in each.

Cancer-specific implications of the oral–gut microbiota axis

Esophageal cancer: microbial contributions to tumor risk

Esophageal cancer is a malignant tumor of the esophagus that poses a serious threat to human health and ranks among the leading cancers worldwide in incidence and mortality [227]. Although esophageal cancer can occur at any age, its incidence increases markedly with advancing age. Most cases occur in individuals over 50 years old, with men affected more frequently than women [228,229]. Two main histological types of esophageal cancer are recognized. Esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) arises from glandular cells in the lower esophagus, near the gastroesophageal junction [230]. EAC is the predominant form of esophageal cancer in Western populations [231]. Its primary risk factor is chronic gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). Persistent acid reflux damages the esophageal mucosa, thereby increasing the risk of EAC [232,233]. Obesity, through increased intra-abdominal pressure from fat accumulation, promotes acid reflux and elevates EAC risk [234,235]. The second major type, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), originates from the thin, flat squamous epithelial cells lining the upper esophagus. Numerous risk factors are associated with ESCC, with tobacco smoking being a major contributor [236]. Harmful constituents of tobacco, including nicotine and tar, directly damage the esophageal mucosa, disrupt its structure and function, and increase cancer risk [236,237]. Heavy alcohol consumption also irritates and damages the esophageal mucosa. Acetaldehyde, a carcinogenic metabolite of ethanol, contributes to ESCC risk, particularly with long-term heavy drinking [236,237]. Dietary habits such as frequent consumption of very hot, coarse, or spicy foods, combined with deficiencies in vitamins and trace elements, impair mucosal metabolism and repair, thereby increasing cancer risk [238]. Genetic susceptibility also contributes to esophageal cancer risk. Individuals with a family history of esophageal cancer have an elevated risk. Environmental exposures, including long-term contact with polluted air or water, and infections such as human papillomavirus (HPV), may also contribute to esophageal carcinogenesis [[237], [238], [239]]. Despite advances in surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies, the prognosis for esophageal cancer remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate below 20% [240]. A recent meta-analysis reported a 39% higher risk of esophageal cancer (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.15–1.68) in patients with periodontal disease, indicating an epidemiological link between periodontitis and esophageal cancer [241].

Recent studies suggest that the oral microbiota may influence the initiation and progression of esophageal cancer via the “oral-esophageal axis,” potentially through multiple pathogenic mechanisms. P. gingivalis has been recognized as a potential oncogenic bacterium. It contributes to the development of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) through multiple molecular mechanisms. For instance, its fimbrial protein FimA activates CD4⁺ T cells, leading to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. At the same time, LPS activates Toll-like receptors on macrophages to jointly construct a sustained inflammatory response. Gingipains degrade key components of the host immune system—including immunoglobulins, complement proteins, and antimicrobial peptides—thereby impairing immune defense. Pilus-mediated adhesion and cellular invasion allow P. gingivalis to evade immune surveillance. Activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway upregulates matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9), which degrade the extracellular matrix and facilitate tumor invasion. TNF-α and reactive oxygen species (ROS) upregulate glucose transporters GLUT-1 and GLUT-4, enhancing glycolysis to support tumor growth. Additionally, secreted nucleoside diphosphate kinase (NDK) inhibits ATP-dependent caspase-1 activation, thereby reducing apoptosis. These mechanisms synergistically promote esophageal cancer progression [242].Chen et al. [243] demonstrated that P. gingivalis infection triggers epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in esophageal epithelial cells via IL-6/STAT3 signaling, promoting CD44 and ALDH1 expression in cancer stem cells and enhancing their invasive capacity. Additionally, autophagy is induced to elevate IL-6 secretion and recruit myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), contributing to an immunosuppressive microenvironment that promotes ESCC progression. Liang et al. [244] further reported that P. gingivalis inhibits the expression of the GRHL3 transcription factor and its downstream tumor suppressor PTEN by upregulating miR-194, thereby activating the Akt pathway and promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion in ESCC.

On the other hand, recent studies have confirmed that F. nucleatum promotes the growth, migration, and invasion of ESCC cells through multiple pathways. The initial mechanism involves the direct infection of ESCC cells by F. nucleatum, which significantly increases the expression of NOD1 and its signaling adaptor RIPK2. This activation triggers the NF-κB pathway, promoting nuclear translocation of the p65 subunit and enhancing the transcription of TNF-α, IL-8, and cell cycle-related genes. Consequently, tumor cell growth and invasion are enhanced [245]. Additionally, F. nucleatum increases the expression of the methyltransferase METTL3, which modifies the GGACU motif in the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of c-Myc mRNA via m⁶A methylation. This modification enhances mRNA stability, activates downstream oncogenic pathways, and accelerates tumor cell growth and metastasis [246]. Simultaneously, F. nucleatum infection activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), promoting its nuclear translocation and binding to xenobiotic response elements (XREs) within the CYP1A1 promoter region, thereby significantly increasing CYP1A1 expression. CYP1A1 activation via the PI3K/AKT pathway enhances ESCC cell proliferation and resistance to apoptosis. Knockdown of CYP1A1 or inhibition of AKT can block the carcinogenic effect of F. nucleatum, whereas the AHR antagonist CH-223191 suppresses CYP1A1 expression and the activation of its downstream AKT, thereby exerting an anticancer effect [247]. Furthermore, Lei et al. [248] observed that F. nucleatum infection markedly increases interleukin-32 (IL-32) expression in ESCC cells, which subsequently upregulates protease 3 (PRTN3), establishing an IL-32/PRTN3 signaling axis. This signaling axis promotes ESCC cell proliferation and invasion by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway and upregulating cell cycle proteins, including Cyclin D1 and c-Myc. These findings highlight the multifactorial oncogenic roles of P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum in ESCC progression and suggest potential targets for therapeutic intervention (Fig. 3).

Gastric cancer: synergistic roles of oral pathogens and H. pylori

Gastric carcinoma is a malignant tumor with high global incidence and mortality, characterized by complex and multifactorial pathogenic mechanisms [249]. Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is the primary etiological factor [250]; however, not all infected individuals develop gastric cancer, indicating that additional factors beyond H. pylori contribute to carcinogenesis [119]. In recent years, the potential role of the oral microbiota in gastric cancer initiation and progression has gained increasing attention.

Comparative analysis by Huang et al. revealed characteristic shifts in the salivary microbiota of gastric cancer patients: pro-inflammatory genera such as Corynebacterium and Streptococcus were markedly enriched, whereas nitrate-reducing genera such as Haemophilus and Neisseria were significantly depleted. These microbial alterations may be closely linked to the pathogenesis of gastric cancer [23]. Notably, H. pylori colonization and pathogenicity are modulated by the gastric microenvironment and host immune status. Oral microbiota dysbiosis may alter the gastric microenvironment, thereby influencing H. pylori colonization and pathogenic potential [209]. In particular, reduced abundance of nitrate-reducing oral bacteria may disrupt nitrite metabolism in the stomach, promoting the formation of N-nitroso compounds—potent carcinogens—and ultimately elevating gastric cancer risk [23].

Studies have shown that P. gingivalis serves as a critical link between oral microbiota dysbiosis and tumor immune escape in gastric cancer patients [251]. On one hand, the gingipains secreted by P. gingivalis specifically degrade tight junction proteins between epithelial cells and synergize with Helicobacter pylori toxins, impairing gastric mucosal barrier integrity. This increases the permeability to pathogens and virulence factors, ultimately inducing chronic inflammation such as gastritis and systemic inflammatory responses [252,253]. These pathological changes not only create a tumor-promoting microenvironment but also facilitate cancer cell metastasis by disrupting intercellular junctions [254]. On the other hand, gingipains also impair cellular stress responses and accelerate tumor progression by cleaving and phosphorylating the 27-kDa heat shock protein (HSP27) [[255], [256], [257]]. P. gingivalis may upregulate Bcl-2 expression through its fimbriae and hemagglutinin-like proteins, thereby inhibiting the programmed cell death of infected host cells [90]. This anti-apoptotic effect has been observed in both dendritic cells and CD3⁺ T cells infected with P. gingivalis, representing a key mechanism of immune evasion [90]. The fimbriae Mfa1 and FimA further enhance anti-apoptotic activity by modulating the expression of C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) and FOXO1/3 transcription factors [258]. Notably, P. gingivalis also activates protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2) and the ERK1/2–Ets1/3–p38 signaling cascade, which induces MMP9 expression and facilitates tumor cell invasion and metastasis [259,260] (Fig. 4).

The mechanism by which P. gingivalis causes gastric cancer: The secreted Gingipains degrade epithelial junction proteins, working in conjunction with H. pylori to disrupt the gastric mucosal barrier, promoting inflammation and tumor formation. It can inhibit cell apoptosis, reduce stress responses, and achieve immune evasion through fimbriae. Additionally, this bacterium activates the PAR2 and ERK1/2-Ets1/3-p38 pathways, upregulates MMP9 expression, and enhances the invasive and metastatic capabilities of tumors.

F. nucleatum, a common Gram-negative anaerobic bacterium in the oral cavity, has recently been implicated in the development and progression of gastric cancer (GC). Clinical studies have shown that elevated levels of F. nucleatum in gastric cancer tissues are significantly associated with poor patient prognosis. F. nucleatum has potential as a diagnostic biomarker and is also closely associated with the clinicopathological features of gastric cancer [261]. In a study involving 120 gastric cancer patients, 31 with atrophic gastritis, 35 with non-atrophic gastritis, 26 with gastric polyps, and 20 healthy controls, salivary levels of F. nucleatum were significantly higher in gastric cancer patients than in other groups. These levels were positively correlated with TNM stage and lymph node metastasis. This method demonstrated a diagnostic sensitivity of 73.33%, specificity of 82.14%, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.813. Its diagnostic performance was superior to that of traditional serum markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9). These findings suggest that F. nucleatum may serve as a valuable biomarker for the early detection and clinical assessment of gastric cancer [262].

Mechanistically, Fusobacterium nucleatum drives the malignant transformation and progression of gastric cancer through multiple dimensions and pathways: 1) From the perspective of genomic stability, the genotoxic metabolite H₂S secreted by F. nucleatum induces DNA oxidative stress, significantly increasing the frequency of somatic mutations across the genome. This process induces gain-of-function mutations in proto-oncogenes such as ERBB2 and PIK3CA, and inactivates tumor suppressor genes like TP53, thereby persistently activating the ERBB2-PIK3CA-AKT-mTOR oncogenic signaling network and impairing DNA repair and apoptotic regulation. Additionally, F. nucleatum frequently forms a symbiotic relationship with Helicobacter pylori, aggravating inflammation and microecological imbalance by remodeling the gastric mucosal microenvironment. This fosters the proliferation and clonal expansion of mutant cells, ultimately accelerating gastric cancer progression [263]. 2) Regarding phenotypic transformation, F. nucleatum induces epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in gastric cancer cells. Specifically, downregulation of E-cadherin and upregulation of N-cadherin, vimentin, and Snail—a key EMT transcription factor—promote epithelial cell depolarization and acquisition of mesenchymal traits, enhancing tumor metastatic potential [262]. 3) Extracellular vesicles (Fn-EVs) derived from F. nucleatum contribute to treatment resistance and maintenance of cancer stem cell properties. Fn-EVs promote the expression of DNA repair proteins such as γH2AX, 53BP1, and RAD51, enhancing resistance to oxaliplatin. They also upregulate stemness markers like CD44, CD47, and Nanog, thereby maintaining cancer stem cell properties. Furthermore, Fn-EVs regulate signaling pathways involved in proliferation, migration, and invasion, promote abnormal cell proliferation, enhance colony formation, induce EMT, and increase metastatic potential [264]. 4) In the non-coding RNA network, F. nucleatum infection induces gastric cancer cells to release exosomes enriched with the long non-coding RNA HOTTIP (Fn-GCEx). Upon uptake by uninfected gastric cancer cells, HOTTIP acts as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA), binding to miR-885-3p via complementary base pairing and relieving its post-transcriptional repression of the target gene EphB2. Overexpression of EphB2 activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway via phosphorylation, ultimately promoting gastric cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and facilitating tumor growth and metastasis [265]. 5) In immune microenvironment regulation, F. nucleatum invades gastric cancer cells via endocytosis, survives intracellularly, and activates the IL-17/NF-κB/RelB pathway. This induces secretion of cytokines such as IL-17A and CXCL8. This recruits tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) and promotes their differentiation into pro-tumor subtypes with high PD-L1 expression. These TANs bind to PD-1 on CD8⁺ T cells via PD-L1, leading to T cell exhaustion and the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. TANs also synergistically promote tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis, thereby facilitating malignant progression and immune evasion in gastric cancer [190]. Additionally, F. nucleatum activates the NOD1/RIPK2 signaling pathway in gastric adenocarcinoma cells, induces nuclear translocation of NF-κB (p65 subunit), and enhances its transcriptional activity, significantly upregulating downstream genes such as MMP-9. As a result, the proliferation, migration, and invasion capacities of tumor cells are enhanced [266] (Fig. 5).

F. nucleatum's mechanism of causing gastric cancer: The H₂S metabolites cause DNA oxidative damage, triggering oncogenic mutations and inactivation of tumor suppressor genes, and enhancing the transfer ability through ATP conversion. Fn-EVs promote abnormal DNA repair and stem cell characteristics. Exosomes carry lncRNA HOTTIP to activate the PI3K/AKT pathway, while the activation of IL-17/NF-κB and NOD1/RIPK2 pathways synergistically enhances immunosuppression and invasion and metastasis.

In summary, P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum play pivotal roles in the carcinogenesis, progression, metastasis, and prognosis of gastric cancer. Their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets warrants further investigation.

The surface protein CbpF binds with nanomolar affinity to the IgV domain of CEACAM1 on T cells and NK cells, and through coordination with a histidine-mediated hydrogen bond network, inhibits immune cell activity [285]. Additionally, Fap2 binds to TIGIT, further suppressing anti-tumor immunity [286]. On the other hand, F. nucleatum activates the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, upregulating S100A9 in both macrophages and CRC cells. This promotes M2 polarization and enhances MMP7 transcription via the MAPK (JNK)-AP1 pathway, facilitating extracellular matrix degradation and increased invasive capacity [287,288]. Furthermore, F. nucleatum stimulates neutrophils to release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), promotes angiogenesis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through VEGF, and upregulates MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression to degrade the basement membrane, collectively promoting tumor cell migration, invasion, and capture by circulating tumor cells [289].

Finally, F. nucleatum binds specifically to LY6A on regenerative stem cells (RSCs) located in the intestinal crypt base, activating the LY6A signaling pathway, upregulating RPS14 expression, driving abnormal RSC proliferation, and inducing cancer stem cell-like properties [292].

Pancreatic cancer: oral microbiota and tumor microenvironment remodeling

Pancreatic cancer is a highly lethal malignancy of the digestive tract, characterized by complex and multifactorial pathogenic mechanisms [296]. Increasing attention has been directed toward the role of the oral microbiota in pancreatic cancer development.

In periodontitis, dysbiosis of the oral microbiota facilitates the translocation of oral pathogens to the gut, alters intestinal microbial composition, and subsequently influences the pancreas, promoting pancreatic inflammation and tumorigenesis. P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum, key periodontal pathogens, have emerged as oral bacteria of particular interest in pancreatic cancer research. Epidemiological studies have linked P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum infections to an increased risk of pancreatic cancer. A recent meta-analysis by Ma, encompassing three case–control studies (n=2029; 888 pancreatic cancer patients and 1141 controls), reported that P. gingivalis was more frequently detected in cases than in controls (OR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.09–1.74; P = 0.007). No significant differences were observed for other bacterial species [297]. Hiraki et al. reported that intraperitoneal injection of Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (PG-LPS) in mice markedly upregulated pancreatic cancer–related genes Reg3G (73-fold) and Reg3A (11-fold). In the PG-LPS group, Reg3A/G-positive cells increased within the α-cell region of the pancreatic islets, without signs of pancreatic inflammation, suggesting that periodontal disease or PG-LPS may induce Reg3A/G upregulation and serve as a potential risk factor for pancreatic cancer [298]. Hayashi et al. further observed that 15.5% of pancreatic cancer patients harboring F. nucleatum infection exhibited larger tumors, increased metastasis, and poorer prognosis [299].

P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum synergistically drive the progression of pancreatic cancer through molecular crosstalk and modulation of the tumor microenvironment. 1) At the cellular and molecular levels, chronic infection with P. gingivalis induces acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM) in pancreatic acinar cells, characterized by the upregulation of SOX9 and CK19, thereby establishing a phenotypic foundation for malignant transformation. In KRAS-mutant cells, P. gingivalis evades host clearance via CXCR4-mediated internalization, suppresses KRAS-induced ROS accumulation, and mitigates oxidative stress-induced senescence. Simultaneously, it increases glutathione (GSH) levels by upregulating SLC1A5 expression and activating the GOT1/GOT2 pathway, thereby enhancing antioxidant defenses. This metabolic adaptation provides cancer cells with a survival advantage under nutrient-deprived conditions, accelerating the progression from pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [300]. 2) At the tumor microenvironment level: Following colonization via the oral–pancreatic axis, P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum activate the chemokine network involving CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCR2, and S100A8/A9, promoting the recruitment and activation of tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs). TANs enhance neutrophil elastase (NE) secretion, which, in synergy with bacterial factors, promotes tumor cell proliferation, fosters a pro-inflammatory milieu, and suppresses CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cell infiltration, ultimately establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment [111]. Additionally, under hypoxic conditions, the bacteria activate the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway via a TLR2-independent mechanism, leading to upregulation of oncogenes such as Cyclin D1 and Heparanase. This establishes a positive feedback loop of “hypoxia–bacterial survival–oncogenic signaling” that further drives tumor proliferation [301].

F. nucleatum promotes pancreatic cancer through dual mechanisms involving unique adhesion and cytokine signaling. Its Fap2 adhesins bind to Gal/GalNAc glycans on pancreatic cell surfaces, facilitating bacterial invasion and inducing host cell secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as GM-CSF and CXCL1. Among them, GM-CSF activates pro-cancer signaling pathways through autocrine/paracrine pathways, and cooperate with other cytokines to repopulate the microenvironment to strengthen the malignant phenotype of cancer cells [302]. Meanwhile, bacterial colonization promotes CXCL1 secretion, which enhances tumor cell migration and invasion via autocrine activation of the CXCR2 signaling pathway. It also recruits myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), suppressing CD8⁺T cell infiltration and establishing a dual-layered immunosuppressive microenvironment, ultimately contributing to a cytokine-dependent oncogenic effect [299] (Fig. 8).

P. gingivalis promotes the transformation of pancreatic acinar cells and the development of PanIN into PDAC. It alleviates oxidative stress through CXCR4 internalization and upregulates SLC1A5 to enhance antioxidant capacity. In the tumor microenvironment, the activation of the chemokine network drives the recruitment of TANs, enhancing tumor proliferation and immunosuppression. Under hypoxic conditions, the PI3K/Akt pathway is activated, upregulating oncogenes, and enhancing tumor proliferation. F. nucleatum targets the glycosylated surface of pancreatic cells through Fap2, inducing the release of GM-CSF and CXCL1. GM-CSF activates oncogenic signals, while CXCL1 promotes cancer cell migration and invasion, recruits MDSCs, reduces CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and constructs an immune microenvironment supportive of tumor growth.

The concept of the oral–gut–pancreas axis underscores the interconnectedness of the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and pancreas, emphasizing its role in maintaining host homeostasis and contributing to disease pathogenesis. Therefore, dysbiosis of the oral microbiota may impact gut and pancreatic health via the oral–gut–pancreas axis, thereby increasing the risk of gastrointestinal and pancreatic cancers.4.5 P. gingivalis across gastrointestinal cancers: shared and tumor-specific mechanisms

Cross-cancer core strategies of P. gingivalis-driven tumorigenesis

Although the molecular mechanisms of P. gingivalis vary across the four cancer types, involving distinct signaling pathways and effector molecules, its core regulatory network remains highly conserved and can be summarized at four levels: 1) Sustained construction of a chronic inflammatory microenvironment: In ESCC, FimA activates CD4⁺ T cells to release IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [242]; in GC, P. gingivalis cooperates with H. pylori to induce gastritis and systemic inflammation [253]; in CRC, it triggers an NLRP3 inflammasome–mediated pro-inflammatory cytokine storm [269]; and in PC, it recruits TANs to form a pro-tumor inflammatory network [111]. Collectively, these processes converge on the classical axis of “chronic inflammation → tissue damage → aberrant repair → cancer.” In this context, IL-6 and TNF-α act as central inflammatory mediators—serving not only as markers of persistent inflammation but also as key drivers of cell proliferation and apoptosis inhibition—thus functioning as the “universal currency” of P. gingivalis across cancer types. 2) Multi-faceted immune-evasion strategies: P. gingivalis circumvents host immune clearance via complementary mechanisms. First, it directly impairs immune defenses by degrading immunoglobulins and complement via gingipains (ESCC) [242]or by upregulating Bcl-2 to inhibit immune-cell apoptosis (GC) [90]. Second, it suppresses adaptive immunity by inducing PD-L1 expression via the NOD1/2–RIP2 pathway (CRC) [270]and by reducing CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration (PC) [111], thereby creating an “immune-desert” microenvironment. This dual immune-evasion mode—combining active degradation with passive inhibition—underpins its persistent colonization across diverse tumor microenvironments and its pro-tumorigenic activity. 3) Targeted activation of invasion- and metastasis-associated molecules: The matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) family constitutes key effectors of P. gingivalis–driven tumor dissemination. In GC, MMP-9 is induced via the PAR2–ERK1/2 pathway [242]; in CRC, Gingipains directly activate MMP-9 [259]; and in PC, TAN-derived neutrophil elastase (NE) synergistically promotes metastasis [111]. Moreover, cross-cancer activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway—via PTEN inhibition in ESCC, pilus-mediated signaling in CRC, and hypoxia-dependent activation in PC—enhances cytoskeletal remodeling and migratory capacity, underscoring the pathogen’s precise targeting of core metastatic machinery. 4) Recurrent deployment of virulence factors: Fimbriae (FimA, Mfa1) and Gingipains—core virulence determinants of P. gingivalis—play pivotal roles across all four cancers. FimA demonstrates remarkable versatility, functioning in “adhesion → signal activation → phenotype modulation” by mediating cell invasion (ESCC), regulating CXCR4/FOXO1/3-dependent anti-apoptosis (GC), and activating the PI3K/Akt pathway (CRC) [242,258,271]. Gingipains serve as a critical bridge between bacterial virulence and host malignant transformation through protein degradation (tight junction proteins in GC, MMP-9 precursors in CRC), protein modification (HSP27 phosphorylation in GC), and signaling activation (MAPK/ERK pathway in CRC). The conserved expression and functional versatility of P. gingivalis constitute the molecular foundation for its tumor-promoting effects across multiple organ systems [195,252,259,303].

Fusobacterium nucleatum in GI tumors: common architecture and organ-specific programs

Conserved oncogenic logic across GI cancers

Fusobacterium nucleatum exhibits a highly conserved oncogenic framework across four gastrointestinal cancers, reflecting a common strategy of hijacking host signaling networks and remodeling the tumor microenvironment to drive malignant progression.

1) Central role of NF-κB signaling: NF-κB serves as a pivotal hub for F. nucleatum–mediated tumor promotion in ESCC (NOD1/RIPK2-driven p65 nuclear translocation) [245], GC (NOD1/RIPK2- and IL-17/RelB-dependent) [190,266], and CRC (TLR4/MYD88-and ALPK1-mediated) [277,279]. Its activation sustains chronic inflammation by upregulating TNF-α, IL-8, and other mediators, while inducing cell-cycle regulators such as Cyclin D1, c-Myc, and MMPs to promote proliferation, invasion, and EMT. This conserved reliance on NF-κB underpins F. nucleatum’s capacity to drive inflammation-to-cancer transition via innate immune pathway activation. 2)Multi-node activation of PI3K/AKT: This survival-, metabolism-, and invasion-regulating pathway is activated by F. nucleatum through diverse mechanisms—CYP1A1 and the IL-32/PRTN3 axis in ESCC [247,248], EphB2 phosphorylation in GC [265], β-catenin nuclear translocation and RadD/CD147 interaction in CRC [278,283].PI3K/AKT activation enhances anti-apoptotic capacity, drives metabolic reprogramming via mTOR, and fuels rapid tumor proliferation, making it a central effector axis in F. nucleatum–mediated malignancy. 3) Remodeling the immunosuppressive niche: F. nucleatum elevates TAN-derived PD-L1 to deplete CD8⁺ T cells in GC [190], downregulates MSH6 while upregulating PD-L1 in CRC to enable immune escape [279], and recruits MDSCs via CXCL1 in PC [299]. This dual strategy—upregulating checkpoint molecules and recruiting suppressive immune cells—constitutes a conserved mechanism for evading immune surveillance. 4) Synergistic phenotypic transformation: EMT induction is a recurrent strategy by which F. nucleatum augments metastatic potential—via IL-32/PRTN3-driven mesenchymal shift in ESCC [248], E-cadherin downregulation with Snail upregulation in GC [262], FadA-mediated disruption of the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex in CRC [280,281]. In parallel, F. nucleatum convergently targets c-Myc in all four tumors, enhancing proliferation by stabilizing its mRNA, activating transcription (β-catenin nuclear translocation in CRC), or increasing expression via pathway activation (in GC and PC).

Owing to the distinct anatomical structures, physiological functions, and microecological profiles of different gastrointestinal organs, the carcinogenic mechanisms of Fusobacterium nucleatum exhibit marked tissue specificity.

1)Esophageal cancer: RNA epigenetics and metabolic enzyme pathways: In ESCC, F. nucleatum uniquely promotes carcinogenesis via epigenetic mechanisms, inducing high METTL3 expression to catalyze m6A modification at the GGACU sequence within the 3′-UTR of c-Myc mRNA, thereby enhancing its stability—an alteration absent in the other three tumor types [246].In addition, through an ESCC-specific AHR/CYP1A1/AKT axis, F. nucleatum activates AHR, promotes its binding to xenobiotic response elements (XREs) within the CYP1A1 promoter, and enhances anti-apoptotic capacity via CYP1A1-mediated PI3K/AKT activation. Pharmacological blockade of this axis by CH-223191 offers a potential therapeutic target specific to ESCC [247]. The IL-32/PRTN3 signaling axis, also unique to ESCC, drives cell-cycle progression through PI3K/AKT regulation [248].

Oral microbiota in GI cancer: diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets

Oral/salivary microbiota as non-invasive biomarkers for early GI cancer detection

Early detection of gastrointestinal (GI) cancer is crucial for improving patient survival rates [304].

Conventional diagnostic methods for GI cancer, such as endoscopy and tissue biopsy, are invasive and costly, limiting their feasibility for large-scale screening [305]. In recent years, non-invasive biomarkers have attracted increasing attention for their potential in the early diagnosis of GI cancer.

The oral microbiota, as an easily accessible biological sample, holds promise as a biomarker for the early detection of GI cancer [306].

Comparative analyses revealed significant heterogeneity between the salivary microbiota of gastric cancer (GC) patients and healthy individuals, with changes in the relative abundance of specific bacterial taxa potentially linked to tumor initiation and progression [23,262]. Data indicated increased abundance of Streptococcus and Corynebacterium, alongside a marked decrease in Haemophilus and Neisseria, in the saliva of GC patients [23]. These salivary microbiota profiles could be used to construct a diagnostic model for GC with high accuracy. The fecal microbiota of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients also differs significantly from that of healthy individuals. The abundance of oral-derived Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum is elevated in CRC patient feces and correlates with patient prognosis [307,308]. Fecal microbiota profiles can likewise support the development of CRC diagnostic models with potential clinical value. However, fecal microbiota composition is influenced by various factors—such as diet and lifestyle—potentially limiting its specificity and sensitivity as a biomarker. Compared with fecal microbiota, salivary microbiota offers convenient, non-invasive sampling and is less affected by dietary and lifestyle factors, making it more suitable as a biomarker for early GI cancer detection. Therefore, further studies are warranted to validate the clinical utility of salivary microbiota for early GI cancer detection and to develop a saliva-based early diagnostic kit.

Targeting the oral–gut axis in therapy: probiotics and oral interventions

Probiotics as adjuncts to cancer therapy

Targeting the microbiota has emerged in recent years as a promising strategy in cancer therapy. Given the role of the oral microbiota in the initiation and progression of gastrointestinal (GI) cancer, modulating it may represent a novel therapeutic approach. Probiotics are live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host by modulating the composition and function of the intestinal microbiota [309]. Advances in biomedical research have increasingly highlighted the potential of probiotics in cancer prevention and therapy. Multiple recent studies have demonstrated that probiotics (such as Lacticaseibacillus casei) can directly inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells and induce tumor cells to undergo apoptosis.These effects are characterized by increased activities of apoptosis-related enzymes (caspase-3 and caspase-2), DNA fragmentation, and altered cell morphology [310,311]. Probiotics exert anti-tumor effects through multiple mechanisms, such as modulating the gut microbiota [309], enhancing barrier integrity [312], regulating immune responses [313], and producing anti-tumor metabolites [314]. In GI cancer management, probiotics can serve as adjuvant therapy alongside conventional treatments to enhance efficacy and mitigate adverse effects.

Oral hygiene and periodontal therapy as systemic modulators

Oral interventions, including improved oral hygiene and periodontal therapy, can positively influence the prevention and management of GI cancer. A nationwide Korean cohort study showed that better oral hygiene reduced oral pathogen transmission and inflammation, thereby lowering GI cancer risk [315]. Another large cohort study suggested that poor oral hygiene is associated with increased risk of upper GI cancers, whereas good oral hygiene may help prevent them [316]. Zhang et al. [317] reported that poor oral hygiene was linked to higher incidence and mortality from cancers—particularly those of the digestive system—with stronger associations in rural than urban populations. Periodontal therapy effectively controls inflammation, restores oral microbiota balance, reduces systemic inflammatory responses, and contributes to GI cancer prevention and management [207,318]. Kamata et al. demonstrated that periodontal treatment significantly improved anti-P. gingivalis IgG titers, reduced liver fat content, normalized liver enzymes (AST, GGT) and endotoxin levels, and promoted liver function recovery [319]. These findings suggest that periodontal therapy may enhance liver health via the “oral–gut–liver axis.”Future studies should validate the clinical value of oral interventions for GI cancer prevention and explore novel therapeutic strategies targeting the oral microbiota.

Summary and Prospect

This review systematically examines the role of the oral microbiota—particularly P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum—in the development and progression of GI cancer, with emphasis on pathogenic mechanisms. Existing evidence confirms that the “oral–gut axis” is a critical pathway influencing GI health. Dysbiosis of the oral microbiota may contribute to GI cancer initiation and progression through multiple mechanisms. Oral pathogens can disseminate and colonize the gut, trigger chronic inflammation, generate carcinogens, modulate host signaling pathways, and ultimately drive GI carcinogenesis. Complex interactions between oral and gut microbiota collectively shape the gastrointestinal tumor microenvironment.

Future research directions can be carried out from the following aspects:

1.Elucidating the specific mechanisms by which oral pathogens contribute to GI cancer: research should clarify how these pathogens spread via the “oral–gut axis,” colonize, and act within the intestinal microenvironment. Further work is needed to delineate the molecular mechanisms by which oral pathogens induce GI inflammation, generate carcinogens, and modulate host signaling, and to identify key targets and pathways.
