Unveiling meropenem-induced thrombocytopenia: Insight from a positive rechallenge case
Authors: Mahdi Aleid, Fatimah Alsaif, Ahmed Alkudaimi, Zahra Hussain Almousa, Bashayer Al Shatti, Zahra Alqaysum, Zahra Noor Alqassab, Jood Ali, Fatimah Aldalouj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeropenem is a broad-spectrum carbapenem antibiotic widely used to treat serious infections.Although generally safe, it can rarely cause drug-induced thrombocytopenia (DITP), a potentially life-threatening condition. We report a case of a 43-year-old male who developed progressive thrombocytopenia after starting meropenem within 3 days, with platelet count recovering upon drug discontinuation. A positive rechallenge with meropenem led to rapid recurrence of thrombocytopenia, giving a probable association. Platelet count improved after permanent cessation of the drug. This case underscores the need for clinicians to be aware of meropenem-induced thrombocytopenia, emphasizing prompt recognition and discontinuation to prevent complications. Regular platelet monitoring during therapy is advised for early detection.Keywords: Meropenem, Thrombocytopenia, Rechallenge, Infections

Introduction

Meropenem is a widely utilized broad-spectrum antimicrobial that belongs to the carbapenem class. It is eﬀective against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative microorganisms, as well as anaerobes and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing microbes [1]. Meropenem is indicated for use against susceptible organisms in various conditions, including sepsis, bacterial meningitis, febrile neutropenia, complicated urinary tract infections, severe community-acquired pneumonia, and intra-abdominal infections [2], [3]. The most frequently observed adverse eﬀects of meropenem include diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, headache, rash, and thrombophlebitis [4]. Rare adverse eﬀects associated with meropenem, occurring in less than 1% of cases, include leucopenia, neutropenia, and pancytopenia [3]. The literature has reported a few instances of thrombocytopenia induced by meropenem, which have demonstrated the immune mechanisms responsible for this condition. Drug-induced thrombocytopenia (DITP) can be a life-threatening condition if not diagnosed and managed appropriately. As meropenem is increasingly used in hospitals today, it is important to be aware of the rare but serious adverse eﬀects it may cause.

Case report

A 43-year-old male with a complex medical history, including diabetes mellitus managed with an insulin pump, panhypopituitarism on long-term hormone replacement therapy like hydrocortisone and levothyroxine, epilepsy on levetiracetam, and a previously treated intracranial germinoma, presented to the emergency department with a one-day history of altered level of consciousness and recurrent seizures. On evaluation, he was found to have severe hypoglycemia, necessitating endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation for airway protection. He was subsequently admitted to the intensive care unit for close monitoring and comprehensive management.Baseline laboratory investigations revealed a platelet count of 217 × 10⁹/L. Two days after, and due to suspected infection, microbiological cultures were taken and the patient was started on Meropenem at a dose of 1000 mg intravenously every 8 h.

Following initiation of meropenem, platelet count demonstrated a progressive decline: 192 × 10⁹/L on day 3, 166 × 10⁹/L on day 4, 108 × 10⁹/L on day 5, 62 × 10⁹/L by day 6,and 47 × 10⁹/L by day 7, consistent with clinically significant thrombocytopenia without any obvious complications like bleeding. Microbiological Cultures revealed no growth. Meropenem was discontinued on day 7 of initiation due to suspected drug-induced thrombocytopenia and shifted to Ertapenem 1 g IV once daily and levofloxacin 750 mg IV once daily with continuing daily monitoring of platelet count for possible Cross-reactivity between Carbapenem. Early recovery of platelet count was observed after discontinuation of meropenem, with an increase to 56 × 10⁹/L on day 1, followed by further improvement to 108 × 10⁹/L by day 5 of discontinuation.

After 5 days on Ertapenem and owing to a recurrence of clinical symptoms, increasing in white blood cells, and inflammatory markers including C-reactive protein and Procalcitonin suggestive of infection, a clinical decision was made by Health care team to take new microbiological cultures and re-initiate meropenem 1000 mg intravenously every 8 h as the thrombocytopenia was not proven to be induced by Meropenem previously. Multiple factors affecting the decision to re-initiate meropenem for this case like sepsis related, other medication coexisting, burden of hematological disorders in the area the patient was living. The platelets count was 209 × 10⁹/L with normal coagulation profile and no bleeding symptoms at the time or re-initiation. However, a rapid decline in platelet count to 79 × 10⁹/L within 2 days of re-initiation prompted permanent discontinuation of the drug. At that time and upon investigation, a blood culture revealed Carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii which was treated with Ampicillin/sulbactam and Colistimethate Sodium. Following cessation of meropenem, platelet count demonstrated progressive recovery day by day, reaching 88 × 10⁹/L, 106 × 10⁹/L, 131 × 10⁹/L, and 157 × 10⁹/L, approaching baseline levels. This temporal pattern highlights a strong association between meropenem exposure and thrombocytopenia, with clear evidence of dechallenge and positive rechallenge.

Discussion

Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP) should be suspected in patients who develop a reduction in platelet count within approximately one week after exposure to a medication, and in certain cases, it may occur within hours of drug administration [4].

DITP can occur through diﬀerent mechanisms, broadly categorized into non-immune and immune-mediated pathways [5].The non-immune mechanism involves suppression of megakaryocyte production and decreased platelet synthesis. However, the more common mechanism is immune-mediated, in which drug-dependent antibodies bind to platelet surface glycoproteins such as GPIIb/IIIa and GPIb/IX in the presence of the drug, leading to accelerated peripheral platelet destruction [6]. Diﬀerent drugs are associated with distinct immune mechanisms; for example, heparin induces thrombocytopenia via immune complex formation, beta-lactam antibiotics such as penicillins and cephalosporins act through hapten-dependent antibody formation, while other agents including sulfonamides,nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), anticonvulsants, and rifampicin are associated with drug-dependent antibody-mediated platelet destruction [4].

Several published case reports describe meropenem-associated thrombocytopenia. Cachia et al.reported an 11-year-old boy who developed severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count declining from 322 × 10⁹/L to 14 × 10⁹/L) and a pruritic maculopapular rash with petechiae after seven days of intravenous meropenem administered for intra-abdominal sepsis following appendectomy; platelet count recovered after drug discontinuation, consistent with an immune-mediated mechanism [7]. Dawaiwala and Pawar reported a 52-year-old male who developed severe thrombocytopenia with oral bleeding within two days of meropenem initiation, with platelet recovery after cessation [8].In a systematic review literature analysis of platelet abnormality caused by carbapenems, Meropenem was the most frequently implicated carbapenem in drug‑induced thrombocytopenia, accounting for approximately 64.3% (27/42) of reported cases. The condition developed rapidly, with a median onset of 2.5 days after initiation, and the majority of cases occurring within the first week of therapy. The platelet count typically reached its nadir around day 5 (range 1–10), with a median nadir of ∼21.5 × 10⁹/L, often reflecting severe (grade 3–4) thrombocytopenia associated with clinically significant bleeding complications. Management primarily involved immediate discontinuation of meropenem, with supportive therapy such as platelet transfusion or corticosteroids when indicated, resulting in recovery of platelet count in most patients within approximately one week. Notably, rechallenge data, although limited, demonstrated recurrence of thrombocytopenia in a small number of cases (≈4.8%, 2 cases), with a similar median onset (∼3–4 days) upon re-exposure, thereby strengthening the causal association. Causality assessment using the Naranjo scale generally classified these reactions as “probable” (scores 5–8), further supporting a strong relationship between meropenem exposure and thrombocytopenia [9].

Our case extends this literature by providing a positive rechallenge. In the present case, a clear temporal relationship was observed. Baseline platelet count were normal prior to meropenem initiation but progressively declined during the first treatment course, reaching 47 × 10⁹/L as nadir. Discontinuation of meropenem resulted in gradual platelet recovery, with the most pronounced improvement during the drug-free interval. Importantly, rechallenge led to a rapid decline in platelet count within 48 h, followed by recovery upon drug withdrawal. This pattern strongly supports an immune-mediated mechanism of thrombocytopenia.

The case fulfilled three out of four George et al. criteria for DITP (temporal association, recovery after drug withdrawal, and recurrence on rechallenge) with the exclusion-of-alternatives criterion partially limited by the concurrent sepsis (Table 1) [6]. On the Naranjo adverse drug reaction probability scale, the case scored 7 (Table 2), corresponding to a probable adverse drug reaction, and the WHO-UMC system classified the relationship as “certain“.

Laboratory testing for drug-dependent platelet antibodies can be performed using enzyme immunoassays, flow cytometry, and monoclonal antibody-specific immobilization assays. While these methods are highly specific, their limited sensitivity and restricted availability reduce their clinical utility. Therefore, diagnosis of DITP, including meropenem-associated cases, relies primarily on clinical evaluation and exclusion of alternatives, with serological testing reserved for confirmation when available [8].In our case, drug-dependent platelet antibody test is not available, and results are not immediately available, requiring specialized laboratories.

Management of DITP primarily involves prompt discontinuation of the oﬀending drug, which is usually suﬃcient for platelet recovery and is associated with a favorable prognosis [5].Corticosteroids are not routinely recommended unless immune thrombocytopenia cannot be excluded [10]. Drug-dependent antibodies may persist for months to years, creating a sustained risk of recurrence if the patient is re-exposed [11].

In our case, this is directly demonstrated by the rapid platelet decline on rechallenge within two days. Platelet transfusion is reserved for patients with severe thrombocytopenia or active bleeding and may be ineﬀective if the drug has not been completely cleared [6]. In our case, platelet transfusion was not required, and discontinuation of meropenem alone was suﬃcient to restore platelet count to baseline levels.

Conclusion

Immune thrombocytopenia caused by meropenem is a rare but serious side eﬀect where the Body’s immune system mistakenly attacks its own platelets, leading to a drop in their numbers and increasing the risk of bleeding. If a patient on meropenem suddenly develops low platelet Count without an obvious reason, clinicians should consider this possibility. Stopping meropenem quickly usually helps the platelets recover fast. It’s important to note this reaction in the patient’s medical records to prevent them from taking the drug again in the future. Regularly checking platelet levels while on meropenem can also help catch this issue early.
