Selective embolization for recurrent gingiva bleeding associated with aneurysmal bone cyst and hemophilia: A case report

Introduction

Spontaneous and massive gingival bleeding is a rare clinical symptom. It hassevere morbidity if it is combined with systemic disease like hemophilia, since the intraoral region is the active region. It carries a significantly increased risk of life-threatening bleeding. Intraoral injuries can result from accidental tooth bites, sharp edges, excessive brushing, and hard food or hot food. Spontaneous and massive gingival bleeding is usually caused by vascular lesions, especially tumor, both malignant and benign [1]. Aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) is one of the vascular lesions that can cause gingival bleeding. It is a benign type tumor that has an expandable bony lesion. Although ABC is a non-neoplastic condition, it has the potential for rapid growth, considerable bony destruction and cause several emergency conditions in some structures. ABC is commonly found in long bones and only 2 % of cases are found in the head and neck region, especially in the facial bones. The rarity of this disease in the head and neck region and the possibility of misdiagnosis caused by mimicking a vascular tumor make this lesion more challenging [2].

Systemic disease can also cause spontaneous massive gingival bleeding, eg hemophilia. A patient with coagulopathy in this area carries a significantly increased risk of life-threatening bleeding. Therefore, we present a patient with spontaneous, massive and recurrence gingival bleeding associated with ABC and hemophilia. This case report has been reported in line with the Surgical Case Report (SCARE) guidelines [3].

Case presentation

A 2-years-old male patient was brought to the clinic complaining of a lump on the left cheek. The lump initially appeared 5 months ago, the size of a marble seed. The lump had gradually increased in size to its present state, and there was active bleeding from the edge of the lump. Based on medical and family history anamnesis, there was no history of trauma, but the patient had history of hemophilia B and had undergone treatment. However, one month ago, the patient was misdiagnosed with right and left mandibular adenoblastoma and underwent two surgeries. Based on extraoral examination, facial asymmetry was apparent, with a lump in the left side of the lower jaw, spongy consistency, firmness, and a size of approximately 4 × 7 cm (Fig. 1). On intraoral examination, the right gingival region showed a smooth-surfaced mass; on the left gingiva, a smooth-surfaced mass was found with active bleeding. There was no neurological deficit. During the treatment, it was observed that the patient's ABC received its vascular supply from the inferior facial artery, a branch of the external carotid artery (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Video). Then, we performed selective embolization of the inferior facial artery. This was chosen because the inferior facial artery is an important vascular supply to the facial area (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

ABC is one of the vascular lesions that can cause gingival bleeding. Primary ABCs may be initiated due to posttraumatic subperiosteal or intramedullary hemorrhage or reactive bodily conditions, such as increased venous pressure due to vascular malformations or hemodynamic compromise, leading to high intraosseous venous pressures, bone expansion, and destruction of the vascular bed. Secondary ABCs can originate from a previous bony lesion such as hemangioma, fibrous dysplasia, ossifying fibroma, central giant cell granuloma, or chondroblastoma. The higher recurrence rate in mandibular ABC secondary may be due to various histopathologies of the primary lesion. In this case, the patient was diagnosed with right and left mandibular adenoblastoma and underwent two surgeries. The clinical presentation is variable and nonspecific and may include pain, local swelling, and pathological fracture [4]. In this case, a smooth-surfaced mass was shown; on the left gingiva, a smooth-surfaced mass was found with active bleeding. Active bleeding can be caused by a high vascular supply, known as hypervascularized ABC. It is in the mandible and can be derived from the vascular supply of blood vessels originating from the facial artery. The facial artery is a branch of the external carotid artery that enters the face from the lower border of the mandible at the anterior border of the masseter muscle. It has a tortuous path and runs up and forward, lateral to the oral commissure. Near the mandible, arteries and veins run very close together and diverge from each other as they travel towards the nose. This high vascular supply can cause the bleeding to become more massive [5].

Systemic disease can also cause spontaneous massive gingival bleeding, eg hemophilia. Hemophilia A, transmitted as an X-linked recessive trait, is the most common hereditary bleeding disorder caused by various gene encoding factor VIII (FVIII) mutations. Meanwhile, hemophilia B occurs due to a defect in the F9 gene, resulting in a lack of production of blood clotting factor IX. A patient with coagulopathy in this area carries a significantly increased risk of life-threatening bleeding. In hemophiliac patients (Factors VIII and IX), 64 % of gingival bleeding occurred compared with other oral sites. Hypervascularized ABC and a history of hemophilia suffered by this patient made the patient's gingival spontaneous bleeding massive and recurrent [6]. Massive bleeding as complications of hemophilia is a serious problem that might cause life-threatening complications [7]. To control bleeding in hemophilia patients, several alternatives are available, including conservative treatment, clotting factor replacement and surgery. The patient was also treated with a coagulant agent such as vitamin K, tranexamic acid and an antihaemophilic agent for coagulant factor replacement. Surgery in patients with hemophilia is a real challenge, as it can be associated with massive intraoperative bleeding [8]. Angiography and embolization of bleeding episodes or pre-operative embolization have rarely been performed as principal therapy. However, selective angiographic embolization should be considered especially for patients who require large amounts of blood transfusion to maintain a life-sustaining cardiovascular function [8,9]. Before getting treatment, we should identify the source of bleeding and its extension through selective carotid angiography.

In this patient, selective embolization was used to control massive bleeding successfully. This method effectively reduced bleeding episodes and minimized transfusion requirements. Selective embolization of the terminal branches can be highly effective, but patient selection, pre-procedural imaging, and choice of embolic material are essential for success. Potential risks include tissue necrosis, recurrence due to incomplete embolization, and other procedure-related complications. Comparative options such as surgical resection or conservative therapy are often associated with higher risk of intraoperative bleeding and morbidity in hemophilia patients, making embolization an appealing minimally invasive alternative.

Conclusion

Spontaneous recurrent gingival bleeding associated with ABC and hemophilia is a rare and challenging clinical condition. Selective embolization represents an effective, minimally invasive option that can prevent severe complications and reduce the need for extensive surgical intervention in complex vascular regions.
