Daratumumab in high-risk MGUS and low-risk smoldering myeloma: results of the Phase II D-PRISM study
Authors: Omar Nadeem, Michelle P Aranha, Robert A Redd, Michael Koontz, Jeffrey V Matous, Andrew J Yee, Jeffrey A Zonder, Andrew Kin, Sophie Magidson, Elizabeth D Lightbody, Ting Wu, Floris Chabrun, Jean-Baptiste Alberge, Ankit K Dutta, Jacqueline Perry, Ashlee Sturtevant, Mahshid Rahmat, Junko Tsuji, Christine Davie, Caroline Ricciardi, Frances Arters, Marjorie Marto, Amy Bergeron, Jacalyn Rosenblatt, Elizabeth K O’Donnell, Tarek H Mouhieddine, Lorena Pantano, Jacob P Laubach, Paul G Richardson, Gad Getz, Lorenzo Trippa, Romanos Sklavenitis-Pistofidis, Irene M Ghobrial
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDaratumumab is approved for patients with multiple myeloma (MM) and high-risk smoldering MM (HR-SMM). However, HR-SMM is often as genomically complex as MM, suggesting it may be too advanced for single-agent intervention. We report on a Phase II trial of single-agent daratumumab in patients with earlier-stage disease, including high-risk monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance and low-risk SMM, to test if earlier treatment can induce deep responses and prevent progression to MM (D-PRISM/NCT03236428, n = 41). As primary outcome, the rate of Very Good Partial Response or better is 17% (95% CI: 7–32), which is comparable to what was observed in HR-SMM and does not meet the study’s primary endpoint. The overall response rate is 54%, with two patients developing MM and 51% biochemical progression. Grade 3 or higher toxicities include hypertension (7%), diarrhea (2%), flu-like symptoms (2%), and headache (2%). Genomic and immune variables associated with biochemical progression are identified in exploratory analyses leveraging whole-genome and single-cell RNA-sequencing. This study demonstrates that, although less effective than expected, daratumumab is safe and can induce deep responses in certain early-stage patients, highlighting the importance of adopting genomic and immune profiling to improve patient selection and maximize the benefit/risk ratio in trials of early intervention.Subject terms: Myeloma, Biomarkers

Introduction

Precursor plasma cell disorders, including monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) and smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM), exhibit a variable risk of progression to symptomatic multiple myeloma (MM), ranging from 1% per year for MGUS to up to ~50% in 2 years for patients with high-risk SMM (HR-SMM)1–5. MGUS is present in approximately 4% of people over the age of 50, and has higher prevalence in older individuals, Black individuals, and individuals with a family history of blood cancer1,2,4,6–11. Features associated with high-risk MGUS (HR-MGUS) include non-IgG paraprotein, serum M protein concentration of >1.5 g/dl, and an abnormal free light chain (FLC) ratio, and these patients carry a lifetime risk of progression to MM estimated at 37–58% at 20 years12,13. On the other hand, high-risk SMM can be defined by various criteria, including the 2/20/20 model and the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) risk score tool14. While low risk SMM (LR-SMM) patients may not be at immediate risk of progression, approximately 50% will progress to overt MM at 10 years15. Additional high-risk features that reflect disease biology, including cytogenetics, are currently under investigation with promising results16–23.

Currently, the standard approach for patients with MGUS/SMM is observation until progression24–26. However, early treatment with lenalidomide has shown benefit compared to observation in two Phase III trials in patients with HR-SMM27–29. More recently, the Phase III AQUILA trial demonstrated significant improvement in progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) with single-agent daratumumab compared to observation in patients with HR-SMM30,31. On the basis of these results, daratumumab monotherapy was recently approved for patients with HR-SMM in Europe and the US.

A key question remains whether the HR-SMM stage is already too advanced for a single-drug intervention, as these patients exhibit genomic features that resemble those seen in MM32–34. Several trials using intensive regimens in HR-SMM have failed to show significantly different response rates compared to those observed in newly diagnosed MM (NDMM). Examples of this include the GEM-CESAR trial, which studied the regimen of carfilzomib, lenalidomide and dexamethasone (KRD) and autologous stem cell transplantation in HR-SMM, and demonstrated a complete response rate (CR) of 64%35. Similarly, a single-arm trial of KRD demonstrated a CR rate of 75.9% and a trial of ixazomib, lenalidomide and dexamethasone demonstrated a CR rate of 31% in HR-SMM36,37. This leads to the hypothesis that perhaps even earlier interventions at a lower-risk disease state may be necessary to induce deeper responses and long-term remission in certain patients with precursor conditions35,36. Another key challenge for the optimal treatment of early-stage patients remains the identification of patients who are more likely to benefit32–34,38. While the risk of progression of LR-SMM to overt myeloma is estimated at 6.2% at 2 years per the 2/20/20 model, all patients with SMM carry a lifetime risk of MM14. While there is general agreement that there is a need for tools that measure disease biology and the state of the patient’s immune system in order to more accurately predict who is likely to progress, it is not necessarily the case that those who are most likely to progress when untreated are also the ones who are most likely to respond to therapy, thus maximizing the benefit-risk ratio. For example, genomic variables that predict a higher risk of progression to MM in patients with SMM, such as KRAS mutations, are not predictors of response to therapy in patients with overt MM18,33. Therefore, biomarkers to predict response to drugs, such as daratumumab, that may be used for interception of progression in patients with SMM are needed to better drive clinical decisions.

In this study, we aimed to assess whether single-agent daratumumab could induce deep responses (i.e., Very Good Partial Response (VGPR) or better) and improve long-term outcomes in patients with HR-MGUS and low-risk SMM (LR-SMM). Additionally, by performing whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) of tumor and immune cells, we aimed to identify predictive biomarkers of response to daratumumab monotherapy in early-stage patients, which following the recent FDA approval may be critical for optimizing patient selection.

Results

Efficacy and safety of single-agent daratumumab in patients with HR-MGUS and LR-SMM

Between January 2018 and February 2020, 41 patients were enrolled on this study and received therapy (Fig. 1A). Patient baseline characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1 and Fig. 1B. Most patients (n = 36/41; 88%) had LR-SMM, and 5 patients (12%) had HR-MGUS. Per the 2/20/20 criteria for patients with SMM (n = 36), 22 patients had LR-SMM (54%), 12 had IR-SMM (29%), and 2 (5%) had HR-SMM. In total, 39/41 patients (95%) completed 20 cycles of therapy as per the protocol. Two patients discontinued therapy early due to withdrawal of consent (one after 6 cycles and one after 16 cycles).

When considering FISH results obtained at baseline, primary cytogenetic abnormalities were detected in only 16 out of 41 patients (39%). In contrast, all 19 patients (100%, 95% CI: 82–100) profiled at baseline with scRNAseq of tumor cells (n = 17) or WGS (n = 14) were successfully classified. Furthermore, scRNAseq and WGS detected additional secondary high-risk abnormalities (1q + , n = 1; del13q, n = 4). To enable cytogenetic analyses, we allowed extrapolation of FISH results from any point during the course of disease for primary cytogenetic abnormalities and any point prior to treatment administration for secondary abnormalities, and successfully classified a total of 38 patients (93%, 95% CI: 80-98). By combining all results, we classified a total of 39/41 patients (95%, 95% CI: 83-99), whom we could assess for high-risk cytogenetics (Fig. 1B). High-risk cytogenetics, as defined by the IMWG (i.e., considering t(4;14), t(14;16), 1q + , and del13q), were present in 14/39 patients (36%, 95% CI: 22–53)14.

Adverse events are detailed in Table 1. The most common toxicities of any grade were fatigue (n = 24/41; 59%), cough (n = 19/41; 46%), nasal congestion (n = 17/41; 41%), headache (n = 14/41; 34%), nausea (n = 13/41; 32%), and leukopenia (n = 13/41; 32%). Grade 3 or higher toxicities included hypertension (n = 3/41; 7%), diarrhea (n = 1/41; 2%), flu-like symptoms (n = 1/41; 2%), and headache (n = 1/41; 2%). Upper respiratory infections (URI) occurred in 9 patients (22%), with 10% of patients developing grade 2 URIs. Neutropenia was seen in 12 patients (29%), with 7 (17%) experiencing grade 2 neutropenia. There were no grade 3 neutropenias or grade 3 infections. No patients developed secondary hematologic malignancies at a median follow-up of ~5 years. One patient developed a new primary cancer (breast cancer).

Progression-free and overall survival post-treatment with daratumumab in patients with HR-MGUS and LR-SMM

The median follow-up for all 41 patients was 63.5 months (range, 6.7–79.8). Median OS was not reached (NR), and OS was 100% at 5 years of follow-up (Fig. 2A). As of December 2024, 25 patients remained on follow-up. Overall, two patients progressed to MM and in both cases, progression was diagnosed based on the SLiM criteria (Fig. 2B)5. One of the 2 patients was noted to have 60% plasmacytosis on their end of treatment (EOT) BM biopsy and subsequently began induction therapy. The other patient was noted to have a FLC ratio of >100 at follow-up visit 3 and began induction therapy. No patients developed symptomatic MM.

Twenty-one patients (51%, 95% CI: 35–67) developed biochemical progression based on the IMWG criteria39. The median biochemical PFS was 59.5 (range: 5.5–76.3) months (Fig. 2C). Six patients developed biochemical progression while on therapy, while 15 patients developed it after completing therapy, with four patients developing it within 1 year of completing therapy. No patients discontinued trial therapy due to progression. Biochemical progressors demonstrated continuous gradual increase in M-spike levels and involved:uninvolved FLC ratios over time (Fig. 2D, E).

The median duration of response for patients who achieved PR or better was 64.8 months (Fig. 2F). Out of the 21 patients who developed biochemical progression, one patient started therapy on an HR-SMM protocol as they subsequently met 2/20/20 criteria for HR-SMM, while another patient met the SLiM criteria for progression, as described above, and began induction therapy. Nineteen patients had not started subsequent therapy at the time of their last follow-up.

Depth of response and high-risk cytogenetics associated with biochemical PFS in response to daratumumab

Next, we searched for variables that can predict biochemical PFS in our patients. There was no difference in biochemical PFS by stage (HR-MGUS vs LR-SMM, two-sided log-rank, p = 0.66; 2/20/20 HR-SMM vs LR-SMM, p = 0.45; 2/20/20 HR-SMM vs IR-SMM, p = 0.48) and neither M-spike levels nor BM infiltration were associated with biochemical PFS on univariate analysis (p = 0.22 and p = 0.39, respectively)14. While there was no difference in SLiM-CRAB PFS, biochemical PFS was significantly longer in patients who achieved VGPR or better (NR vs 52.2 months; two-sided log-rank, p = 0.014) and duration of response was also longer, although this comparison did not reach statistical significance (two-sided log-rank, p = 0.075) (Fig. 3A–C).

While the impact of high-risk cytogenetics, as defined by the IMWG, on SLiM-CRAB PFS could not be assessed due to low power, biochemical PFS was significantly longer in patients with SMM and standard vs high-risk cytogenetics by IMWG (NR vs 49.2 months; two-sided log-rank, p = 0.037) (Fig. 3A, B). To determine if other cytogenetic abnormalities may be more predictive in the setting of daratumumab monotherapy, we tested recurrent abnormalities that were present in at least 2 patients (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, we found that the presence of t(11;14) was associated with significantly longer biochemical PFS (two-sided Wald p = 0.016 and two-sided log-rank p = 0.0029) (Fig. 4D). In contrast, the presence of t(4;14) was associated with shorter biochemical PFS (two-sided Wald p = 0.0046 and two-sided log-rank p < 0.001) (Fig. 4E). The presence of t(4;14) is considered a high-risk factor in both treatment-naïve patients with SMM, as well as patients with overt MM who receive therapy. On the other hand, the presence of t(11;14) is not associated with superior outcomes in treatment-naïve patients with SMM or patients with overt disease who receive therapy. Thus, the observed association of t(11;14) with response to daratumumab may be related to its administration as monotherapy instead of in combination with other agents or its administration earlier on in the course of disease.

Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals immune biomarkers of response to daratumumab monotherapy in patients with HR-MGUS and LR-SMM

To explore how the immune system may influence response to daratumumab monotherapy in this patient population, we performed scRNAseq on 48 CD138-negative and 18 CD138-positive BM mononuclear cell samples from 36 patients (biochemical progressors (P), n = 18; non-biochemical progressors (NP), n = 16; patients who discontinued treatment, n = 2). Another 6 patients (P, n = 3; NP, n = 3) also had samples drawn at EOT to monitor immune cell dynamics post-therapy (Supplementary Table 2). Notably, among progressors, 8 patients progressed within ~24 months from treatment initiation (early progressors), while the remaining patients (n = 10) progressed after ~45 months (late progressors). In total, we profiled 71,771 BM immune cells, including 37,734 T cells, 10,338 NK cells, 5422 B cells, 16,277 monocytes, and 2,000 dendritic cells (Fig. 5A).

First, we compared the proportions of all immune cell types (n = 20) between progressors (n = 18) and non-progressors (n = 16). Interestingly, biochemical progressors had significantly higher proportions of CD56dim NK cells at baseline compared to non-progressors (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum, q = 0.0364) (Fig. 5B). No such difference was observed between early vs late progressors (p = 0.275). To ensure our results were not confounded by differences in target antigen expression by tumor cells, we compared the proportion of CD38-expressing ( > 0.1) tumor cells between groups and observed no association with biochemical progression (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.613) (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, no significant difference in tumor burden was observed between progressors and non-progressors (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.644 and p = 0.581 for BM infiltration and M-spike, respectively). Lastly, and in line with prior observations, the proportion of CD38-expressing ( > 0.1) CD56dim NK cells decreased significantly post-treatment (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank, p = 0.00216), but with no difference between progressors (n = 3) and non-progressors (n = 3), suggesting that NK cell fratricide due to CD38 expression may not be associated with response in this setting (Fig. 5D)40.

To understand how the baseline phenotype of CD56dim NK cells differs between progressors and non-progressors, we performed differential expression analysis after downsampling to ensure equal numbers of cells were contributed by each patient within each group and equal numbers of cells were compared across groups (P, n = 18; NP, n = 11; 396 cells per group). We observed significantly higher levels of FCER1G in CD56dim NK cells of non-progressors compared to progressors and in late compared to early progressors (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum, q < 0.05) (Fig. 5E, F).

Discussion

This study evaluated the safety and efficacy of single-agent daratumumab in patients with HR-MGUS and LR-SMM (n = 41). The ORR was 54% (95% CI: 37–69) in the entire study cohort, the rate of VGPR or better was 17% (95% CI: 7–32%), and 4 patients (10%; 95% CI: 3–23) achieved a CR. The median biochemical PFS was 59.5 months. Twenty-one patients developed biochemical progression, two patients developed SLiM progression (one at EOT and one during follow-up), and no patients developed symptomatic progression by CRAB criteria5.

Although the rate of VGPR or better was expected to be higher at the time this trial was designed and the primary end point was not achieved, it is in line with the response rates reported since in the Phase III AQUILA trial of daratumumab monotherapy in patients with HR-SMM (ORR of 63.5%, VGPR or better of 29.9%) and the Phase II CENTAURUS trial of daratumumab monotherapy in patients with IR-SMM/HR-SMM (ORR of 56.1%, VGPR or better of 29.3%)31,41,42. This suggests that achieving deep responses may require more complex treatment, therefore underscoring the need for improved patient selection to maximize the benefit/risk ratio in patients with early-stage disease where overtreatment is a critical concern.

Interestingly, M-spike levels and the degree of BM infiltration were not associated with biochemical PFS in our trial and no difference in biochemical PFS was observed between patients of different stage. This suggests that biological factors, such as tumor biology or the state of the patient’s immune system, may play a key role in this early setting. Indeed, high-risk cytogenetics, as defined by the IMWG in 2020, were associated with shorter biochemical PFS in our trial. Moreover, t(4;14) was associated with a higher risk of progression in our cohort, while t(11;14) was associated with a lower risk of progression. These observations are particularly pertinent now, given the need to better identify patients who may benefit the most from daratumumab monotherapy, following its recent approval in Europe and the US in patients with HR-SMM on the basis of the AQUILA trial. While the AQUILA trial targeted patients with HR-SMM, 62% of patients enrolled had either LR-SMM or IR-SMM based on the 2/20/20 criteria, compared to 83% of patients in D-PRISM, suggesting that the biomarkers discovered in D-PRISM could potentially be relevant for the AQUILA population, too31.

Importantly, these correlative analyses would not have been possible based on clinical-grade FISH testing conducted at baseline. Instead, they were enabled by conducting research-level WGS and scRNAseq of tumor cells at baseline to obtain accurate cytogenetic profiling information on a larger number of patients, a lot of whom were unclassified by clinical FISH testing at baseline37. Additionally, they were enabled by allowing the extrapolation of FISH results from multiple timepoints during the course of disease, highlighting the importance of repeated testing when clinical-grade FISH is the only method available. Altogether, these observations demonstrate the critical role of genomic alterations in predicting outcome and the need for more sensitive genomic surveillance tools that can contextualize the results of clinical trials22,32,37.

In addition to genomic profiling, we have previously shown that immune profiling can be leveraged for predicting outcome post-treatment in patients with SMM37,43. To explore whether immune profiling could be useful for predicting response to daratumumab monotherapy in patients with HR-MGUS and LR-SMM, we performed scRNAseq of BM immune cells from 36 patients. We found that a higher baseline frequency of CD56dim NK cells was associated with biochemical progression. This is in contrast to results from the CASSIOPEIA trial of daratumumab, bortezomib, thalidomide, and dexamethasone in MM patients, where CD56dim NK cells at diagnosis were associated with a higher probability of achieving minimal residual disease (MRD)-negativity post-treatment44. This discrepancy could be due to the earlier stage of our patients or testing daratumumab as monotherapy instead of in combination, but it also suggests that CD56dim NK cell abundance alone may not predict better outcomes post-treatment with daratumumab40,45,46. Notably, we also noted that lower FCER1G expression levels in CD56dim NK cells were associated with biochemical progression. Lower FCER1G levels are typically associated with an adaptive-like NK cell phenotype and have been shown to enhance antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, but reduce the capacity of NK cells to respond to tumor cells or control viral infection47–49. Additional validation of these results is needed in this patient population, but collectively, they suggest that immune biomarkers may indeed matter for prediction of response and correlative immune profiling should be considered when designing trials32,43.

Another key challenge in designing clinical trials in patients with precancerous conditions and a lower risk of progression, such as HR-MGUS and LR-SMM, is selecting appropriate endpoints that can be read out relatively quickly. We have previously shown that depth of response predicts progression in patients with HR-SMM treated with ixazomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone37. Similarly, MRD-negativity was associated with longer biochemical PFS in the GEM-CESAR trial in patients with HR-SMM35. Here, we demonstrated that deeper responses can predict longer biochemical PFS in patients with early-stage disease and it remains to be seen whether IMWG response criteria can also predict SLiM-CRAB PFS in this setting.

In conclusion, we have examined the activity of a therapeutic agent in early-stage precursor MM preceding HR-SMM. Given the recent approval in Europe and the US of single-agent daratumumab in patients with HR-SMM, there is an urgent need to improve the identification of the patient population that may benefit the most from early treatment. This study demonstrates that daratumumab is safe and may be efficacious in certain patients with HR-MGUS and LR-SMM, despite a lower than expected rate of deep responses (i.e., VGPR or better), with only 2 patients progressing to MM on the basis of the SLiM criteria and without anyone developing frank symptomatic disease within 5 years of follow-up. While longer follow-up is needed to evaluate the impact of this intervention on SLiM-CRAB progression, these results are reassuring and in line with results obtained in patients with HR-SMM, where an overall survival benefit was also observed. Additionally, this study demonstrates that depth of response is a meaningful predictor of biochemical progression in this setting. Importantly, this study nominates genomic and immune biomarkers that can be used to optimize patient selection and the benefit-risk ratio for daratumumab monotherapy, which is a key unmet clinical need. In doing so, this study highlights the importance of conducting correlative studies of genomic and immune profiling to identify biological biomarkers of response that can help guide the design of future trials for interception of progression.

Methods

D-PRISM is a single-arm, Phase II study using single-agent daratumumab in patients with HR-MGUS and LR-SMM (D-PRISM; NCT03236428, posted 1 August 2017). The trial protocol was approved by the Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center Institutional Review Board (#17-212) and is provided in the Supplementary Information file. Patient samples were banked under this protocol for correlative studies. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient selection was blind to the participant’s sex and gender.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility criteria for patients with HR-MGUS included <10% plasmacytosis and <3 g/dL M-spike in the serum along with at least 2 of the following 3 criteria: abnormal serum FLC ratio ( < 0.26 or >1.65), M protein ≥1.5 g/dL or non-IgG M protein (including IgA). Low-risk SMM was defined as having only 1 of the following criteria: monoclonal protein ≥ 3 g/dL, ≥10% BM plasmacytosis, or sFLC ratio <0.125 or >8. Patients who met SLiM-CRAB criteria were excluded5.

Treatment Regimen

A treatment cycle was defined as 4 weeks in length. All patients received daratumumab dosed at 16 mg/kg intravenously for each four-week cycle for up to 20 cycles. During Cycles 1 and 2, patients received daratumumab weekly. During cycles 3 through 6, daratumumab was administered every 2 weeks. During cycles 7 through 20, daratumumab was administered every 4 weeks. Assessments of disease and progression were performed at the beginning of each cycle, at the end of treatment, and every 3 or 6 months thereafter. Patients were to be followed for up to five years after the discontinuation of therapy.

Objectives and end points

The primary objective of this trial was to determine the proportion of patients who achieve VGPR or better after 20 cycles of therapy. Response was determined according to the IMWG criteria50. Secondary objectives included the following: 1) assess the duration of response; 2) assess the safety of the agent in this patient population; 3) assess if baseline immune features can predict response or progression, 4) assess changes in the immune microenvironment in HR-MGUS and LR-SMM patients during the study; 5) assess the association between immune changes and progression to SMM or MM; and 6) assess the rate of secondary hematologic malignancies.

Statistics and reproducibility

The study aimed to enroll 50 patients, however due to the COVID-19 pandemic that occurred during the trial enrollment period, enrollment was ended early with 41 patients enrolled. The study was not randomized and thus, the investigators were not blinded. According to the study protocol, the criterion to determine whether the treatment is promising was for the lower limit of the two-sided 90% binomial confidence interval for the deep response rate (i.e., VGPR or better) to be at least 50%. In practice, both 90% confidence intervals and 95% confidence intervals were reported for ORR and the rate of VGPR or better. Patients who received any amount of study treatment were evaluable for safety and toxicity analyses from the time of their first dose. Patients who had measurable disease, received at least one cycle of therapy, and had their disease re-evaluated were considered evaluable for response. Patients who showed objective disease progression prior to removal from the study were also considered evaluable.

No data was excluded from analysis, unless explicitly stated in the manuscript and figure legend. For example, analyses on genomic biomarkers of progression were conducted in patients with SMM who had cytogenetic data available, thus excluding patients with MGUS and a single patient with SMM and no cytogenetic information.

Progression-free survival was measured from the date of registration to the date of documentation of progressive disease (SLiM-CRAB or biochemical progression) or death from any cause and censored for those alive and free from progression. According to the SLiM-CRAB criteria, a diagnosis of MM was made if ≥ 60% clonal plasma cells were detected in the BM, the involved:uninvolved FLC ratio was ≥ 100, >1 focal lesion was detected on MRI, or patients developed CRAB symptoms5. Time-to-event endpoints were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Median follow-up was calculated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Clinical responses to study treatment were reported as proportions with 95% exact binomial confidence intervals. Continuous measures were summarized by their median and range, and categorical variables were summarized as proportions. Response outcomes and other categorical variables were tested for association with continuous variables using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests or to other categorical variables using two-sided Fisher’s exact tests. Statistical analyses of clinical data were performed using R (v4.4.2; 2024-10-31).

No sex-based or gender-based analyses were conducted in this study, as such analyses were not part of the study protocol. Self-reported sex information is available for participants in the scRNAseq cohort in the study’s dbGaP site (for access to dbGaP, please, see Data Availability).

Non-preplanned exploratory outcomes

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) data and analysis

Bone marrow (BM) aspirates from 36 patients enrolled on the trial were collected at baseline (n = 36) and EOT (n = 6). Bone marrow mononuclear cells were isolated using Ficoll and subjected to CD138 selection using AutoMACS (Miltenyi), followed by cryopreservation in Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), supplemented with 10% Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO). Cryopreserved CD138-negative cells (n = 48) from 36 individuals and CD138-positive cells (n = 18) from 17 individuals were thawed and encapsulated on a Chromium Controller, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (10X Genomics). Gene expression libraries were prepared using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ Reagent Kits (v2, Dual Index) and Library Construction Kits (10X Genomics). For tumor samples, B cell receptor (BCR) libraries were also constructed using Chromium Single Cell Human BCR Amplification Kits and Library Construction Kits, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (10X Genomics). Libraries were sequenced on NovaSeq S6000 instruments at the Genomics Platform of the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard (Cambridge, MA).

FASTQ files were demultiplexed, and count matrices were produced using CellRanger (v6.0.1), and downstream analyses were conducted using R (v4.2.2) and Seurat (v5.1.0)51,52. Immune cells were annotated using established immune cell expression markers and differential expression analysis, as is standard in the field37,43,53,54. Red blood cells and plasma cells identified in the CD138-negative samples were discarded, resulting in a total of 20 immune cell types used for downstream analyses. Tumor cells were identified on the basis of expressing markers of malignancy (e.g., NCAM1, CCND1, etc.) and exhibiting monoclonal expansion in the BCR data37,54–56.

Immune cell proportions were computed by dividing the cell count of a cell type in a sample by the sample’s total immune cell count. Two patients who discontinued treatment were excluded from downstream analyses related to progression. The remaining patients had at least 200 immune cells detected and were included in downstream analyses (n = 34). Immune cell proportions for all cell types (n = 20) were compared between biochemical progressors (n = 18) and non-progressors (n = 16), or early (n = 8) and late biochemical progressors (n = 10), using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (a = 0.05). The proportion of CD38-expressing tumor cells and CD56dim NK cells was determined by computing the proportion of cells with normalized CD38 expression >0.1. For 6 patients with EOT samples available, the proportion of CD38-expressing CD56dim NK cells was compared between baseline and EOT timepoints by a two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Differential expression analysis was conducted using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to compare the baseline expression profile of CD56dim NK cells between biochemical progressors (n = 18) and non-progressors (n = 11), or early (n = 8) and late biochemical progressors (n = 10). Only patients with at least 20 CD56dim NK cells at baseline were considered for these analyses. To balance the number of cells compared between groups as well as the number of cells contributed by each individual, we downsampled 396 cells per group for biochemical progressors (18 patients, 22 cells each) versus non-progressors (11 patients, 36 cells each) analysis and 280 cells per group for early (8 patients, 35 cells each) versus late biochemical progressors (10 patients, 28 cells each) analysis. Lastly, we filtered for genes with more than 3 counts in at least 10 cells. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (a = 0.05).

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of tumor samples

Whole-genome sequencing was performed on bone marrow CD138+ cells (tumor) and matched peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC; normal). Genomic DNA was extracted using the Monarch Genomic DNA Purification Kit (New England Biolabs), and libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II FS DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) with unique dual-index adapters (NEBNext Multiplex Oligos) and sequenced at the Genomics Platform of the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard (Cambridge, MA). Sequencing reads were aligned to GRCh38, and the Getz Lab WGS processing and analysis pipeline was deployed, as previously described33,57.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and cytogenetic classification analyses

Results from clinical-grade FISH testing were extracted from medical records and reviewed for accuracy by three independent data collectors. To maximize the contribution of FISH to our cytogenetic classification, we allowed extrapolation of FISH results for primary cytogenetic abnormalities only (i.e., patients were considered positive for primary IgH translocations and hyperdiploidy, if they were positive by FISH at any point during the course of disease). In contrast, patients were considered positive for secondary cytogenetic abnormalities (gain or amplification of chr1q/1q + , del13q, and del17p) if a positive FISH result was obtained at baseline or prior to that. Positive results for secondary abnormalities that were obtained after treatment administration were not considered to account for the possibility of a newly acquired secondary abnormality.

To identify cytogenetic biomarkers of biochemical progression, we combined extrapolated FISH results with results from scRNAseq and WGS (which were conducted on tumor cells from a total of 19 patients who had tumor cells available for testing). We restricted this analysis to patients with SMM and cytogenetic classification (n = 35) and tested only abnormalities that were present in at least 2 patients (primary: t(11;14), t(4;14), t(14;16), hyperdiploidy; secondary: 1q + , del13q, del17p) using Cox regression and two-sided Wald tests. Kaplan-Meier analyses with two-sided log-rank tests were also conducted for cytogenetic abnormalities with a two-sided Wald p < 0.05.
