Sarcopenia (defined by low muscle mass) as a predictor of disease progression and mortality in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome
Authors: Diar Zooravar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objectivesCardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome reflects the interconnected nature of cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic dysfunction. Sarcopenia, or the loss of skeletal muscle mass, is linked to adverse outcomes in chronic diseases, but its role within the CKM framework remains poorly defined. This study aimed to investigate the association between sarcopenia, CKM syndrome severity, and mortality risk.MethodsThis study analyzed data from 5,925 adults (aged ≥ 20 years) across eight NHANES cycles (1999–2006, 2011–2018). Sarcopenia was defined using FNIH criteria based on DXA-derived muscle mass. CKM syndrome staging followed the AHA classification. Multinomial and ordinal logistic regression examined the relationship between sarcopenia and CKM stage. Cox proportional hazards models evaluated the impact of sarcopenia on all-cause, cardiovascular (CVD), and non-CVD mortality. Sensitivity analysis was performed to confirm robustness.ResultsDuring a mean follow-up of 125.47 (0.94) months, 851 deaths occurred. Sarcopenia was significantly associated with increased odds of advanced CKM stages: (OR: 2.236, 95% CI: 1.594–3.135), stage 3 (OR: 3.332, 95% CI: 2.001–5.548) and stage 4 (OR: 3.495, 95% CI: 2.157–5.662), compared to Stage 1. Sarcopenia also predicted higher mortality risks in patients with CKM stage 1–4: all-cause mortality (HR: 1.527, 95% CI: 1.274–1.831), CVD mortality (HR: 1.903, 95% CI: 1.299–2.788) and non-CVD mortality (HR: 1.407, 95% CI: 1.124–1.762).ConclusionSarcopenia is strongly linked to CKM syndrome severity and is a predictor of mortality across CKM stages. These findings suggest that assessing and managing sarcopenia could offer a valuable strategy to mitigate disease progression and improve survival in individuals with CKM syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01943-x.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Muscle mass, Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome

Introduction

The interplay between cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) has been broadly researched. About half of the patients with advanced CKD have CVD, and CVD is the second common cause of death among patients with CKD, accounting for 30% of all deaths in these patients [1, 2]. Moreover, the role of metabolic disorders like type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is recognized in the progression of CKD and CVD, so that the prevalence of heart failure and CKD in patients with T2DM has been reported up to 10% and 31.5% in nation-wide cross-sectional studies [3, 4]. Recognizing this interplay, the American Heart Association (AHA) recently introduced the concept of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome. This integrated model reflects a shift in understanding, emphasizing the need for holistic strategies that address the multisystem nature of chronic disease rather than isolated components [5].

As the global burden of CKM syndrome grows, it is crucial to identify factors that may accelerate its progression or influence long-term outcomes. In this context, some metabolic indices and depression severity are associated with the risk of mortality in patients with CKM syndrome [6, 7]. One such factor gaining attention is sarcopenia, the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength, which is particularly prevalent among older adults and individuals with chronic illness [8]. Muscle tissue secretes myokines, such as irisin, myostatin, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), which regulate energy balance, glucose homeostasis, and systemic inflammation [9]. Sarcopenia is associated with a low-grade systemic inflammatory state, characterized by increased inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [10]. This inflammatory state contributes to insulin resistance (IR) and vascular remodeling, which are key features of both CVD and metabolic disease [11, 12]. Moreover, IL-6 enhances basal glucose uptake and translocation of GLUT4 [13]. Thus, muscle loss reduces insulin-stimulated glucose disposal, exacerbating IR and increasing the risk of T2DM [9]. In addition, muscle loss contributes to decreased capacity for the oxidation of fatty acids, leading to the accumulation of lipid metabolism mediators, including ceramides and diacylglycerol, which lead to both metabolic and cardiovascular damage [14].

Despite these connections, the potential role of sarcopenia in the field of CKM syndrome remains largely unexplored. It is also unclear whether sarcopenia independently contributes to mortality risk in individuals with varying stages of CKM. Addressing these questions is not only relevant for understanding disease mechanisms but may also inform preventive and therapeutic strategies for high-risk populations. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between sarcopenia and the severity of CKM syndrome, as well as its association with mortality in affected individuals. The goal is to determine whether sarcopenia may serve as a clinically meaningful marker of disease progression and prognosis within the context of CKM syndrome.

Methods and materials

Study design and participants

This study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a continuous program conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). NHANES employs a complex, multistage probability sampling design to obtain a nationally representative sample of the civilian U.S. population. The survey integrates interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory testing to provide comprehensive Health and nutritional data. This analysis included participants from the NHANES 8 cycles conducted between 1999 and 2006 and 2011–2018. A total of 101,316 individuals were enrolled in the screening process. Definition of covariates

Patients were categorized by gender (male or female) and marital status (married or unmarried). Racial/ethnic groups were classified into five categories: Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Hispanic and Other races. Educational level was categorized into three groups: less than 9th grade, 9th − 11th grade, 12th grade and above. Alcohol consumption was categorized into three groups: individuals who had consumed no more than 12 alcoholic beverages over their lifetime were classified as never drinkers; those who had consumed more than 12 drinks in their lifetime but none in the past year were identified as former drinkers; and those reporting consumption of 12 or more drinks within the previous year were designated as current drinkers [15]. Similarly, smoking status was divided into three categories: never smokers (fewer than 100 cigarettes smoked in total), former smokers (had smoked over 100 cigarettes in their lifetime but had abstained for at least one year before the survey), and current smokers (smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and were either still smoking or had quit within the past year at the time of assessment) [15]. Body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) were measured in kilograms per square meter (kg/m²) and centimeter (cm), respectively. All clinical and biochemical measurements, including blood pressure and laboratory parameters, were obtained during one referral in the same examination cycle for each participant.

The history of CVD was determined based on self-reported physician diagnoses obtained through a standardized medical condition questionnaire during an individual interview. Participants were asked, “Has a doctor or other health expert ever informed you that you have congestive heart failure (CHF), coronary heart disease (CHD), angina pectoris, myocardial infarction (MI), or stroke?” A participant was classified as having CVD if the response to any of these conditions was “yes.” Hypertension (HTN) was defined by mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) measurement ≥ 140 mmHg or mean diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, the prescription of antihypertensive medication, or a self-reported physician diagnosis [16]. Three consecutive blood pressure measurements were obtained after resting quietly in a sitting position for 5 min. If a blood pressure measurement was interrupted or incomplete, a fourth measurement was obtained. Mean SBP and mean DBP were calculated using the average of at least two reported blood pressure measurements. DM was defined according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) diagnostic criteria: self-reported diagnosis, use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dl, or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% [17]. CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g based on the latest guideline of the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes 2024 (KDIGO) [18]. eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [19]. The history of cancer was obtained through self-reported diagnosis. Metabolic syndrome was defined according to AHA/NHLBI update to the criteria established by the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) [20]. Participants were classified as having metabolic syndrome if they met three or more of the following five criteria:

Abdominal obesity, defined as a WC greater than 102 cm in men or greater than 88 cm in women.

Definition of exposure

In this study, sarcopenia was defined solely based on low muscle mass according to the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) criteria using appendicular lean mass adjusted for body mass index (ALM/BMI). Measures of muscle strength and physical performance were not included due to data limitations [21]. As part of the diagnostic criteria, muscle mass was measured using DXA, a validated and widely accepted technique for assessing body composition [22]. Specifically, low muscle mass was determined using the ALM/BMI ratio. Appendicular lean mass (ALM) was calculated as the sum of lean tissue in both arms and both legs. Individuals were considered to have low lean mass if their ALM/BMI ratio was less than 0.789 for men and less than 0.512 for women [21].

Between 1999 and 2006, DXA scans were conducted using the Hologic QDR-4500 A fanbeam densitometer (Hologic, Inc., Bedford, Massachusetts). For data collected from 2011 to 2018, the Hologic Discovery model A densitometers (Hologic, Inc., Bedford, Massachusetts) were applied, using software version Apex 3.2. To ensure consistency in muscle mass measurements across NHANES cycles, DXA scans were performed under strict quality control protocols despite the use of different machines. Daily and weekly phantom scans, site-specific calibration checks, and air scans were used to monitor equipment performance. A statistical method was applied to detect calibration shifts over time, and correction factors were developed when needed. However, differences in participant data before and after adjustments were minimal, and corrections were not necessary. These procedures ensured the reliability and comparability of DXA measurements across all survey years.

Definition of outcomes

In this study, the primary outcome was to assess the relationship between sarcopenia and higher stages of CKM syndrome. CKM syndrome is a recently defined clinical concept by the AHA that demonstrates the interconnected and compounding relationship between CVD, CKD, and metabolic disorders such as DM and obesity [5]. The staging of CKM syndrome followed the 2023 framework established by the AHA, which outlines a progressive model of multisystem dysfunction [5]. However, due to the limited availability of certain clinical data within NHANES, a modified version of this classification was adopted as operationalized by Tu et al. (2025), who developed a validated approach tailored specifically to the NHANES dataset [23] (Supplementary Table 3).

The secondary outcome was to assess the relationship between sarcopenia and the risk of all-cause, CVD mortality, and non-CVD mortality in patients with CKM syndrome. Data from the NHANES public-use linked mortality file, updated through December 31, 2019, were used to determine mortality. This data was matched to the National Death Index (NDI) by the NCHS. Disease-specific causes of death were identified according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). CVD mortality was defined as deaths attributed to either diseases of the heart (ICD-10 codes I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51; recoded as UCOD_LEADING = 001) or cerebrovascular diseases (ICD-10 code I60–I69; recoded as UCOD_LEADING = 005). Non-CVD mortality encompassed all other causes of death represented in the linked file.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3 and incorporated the complex, multistage probability sampling design of the NHANES. Sample weights, strata, and primary sampling units were applied following NHANES analytic guidelines to ensure nationally representative estimates. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were explained as a number (percentage) while continuous variables were described as mean (standard deviation). One-way ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare parametric and non-parametric variables in different stages of CKM, respectively.

Survey-weighted multinomial and ordinal logistic regressions were used to examine the association between sarcopenia and higher stages of CKM syndrome. CKM stage 1 was defined as the reference group in multinomial regression, and sarcopenia was used as a binary categorical variable. Moreover, both regression models were used to assess the association between ALM/BMI as a continuous variable and higher stages of CKM syndrome. CKM was defined as a nominal variable in multinomial regression analyses and as an ordinal variable in ordinal regression analyses, respectively.

Additionally, survey-weighted Cox proportional hazard regression was used to evaluate the association between sarcopenia and all-cause and cause-specific mortality. The variance inflation factor (VIF) of the covariates was checked, and covariates with VIF > 5 were excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Table 1). We included 101,316 participants in the screening process. A total of 56,131 participants were excluded due to missing data to calculate ALM. Moreover, 15,305 participants with missing data of CKM syndrome or CKM stage 1 were excluded, leaving 29,880 patients. Further participants were removed due to missing data covariates (n = 23,995). Association between sarcopenia and progression of CKM syndrome

In a multinomial logistic regression, sarcopenia was associated with increased risk of being in more advanced CKM stages. Compared to patients with stage 1, the presence of sarcopenia was associated with an increased risk of CKM syndrome stage 2 (OR: 2.236, 95% CI: 1.594–3.135, p < 0.001), stage 3 (OR: 3.332, 95% CI: 2.001–5.548, p < 0.001) and stage 4 (OR: 3.495, 95% CI: 2.157–5.662, p < 0.001). Ordinal logistic regression also showed that the presence of sarcopenia was associated with significantly higher risk of severe CKM stages (OR: 1.954, 95% CI: 1.596–2.393, p < 0.001). Moreover, increasing the ALM/BMI ratio was associated with a lower risk of more advanced stages of CKM syndrome in both models. (Tables 2 and 3).

Association between sarcopenia and mortality in patients with CKM syndrome

During a mean follow-up of 125.47 (0.94) months, 851 deaths, including 260 CVD-related and 591 non-CVD deaths, occurred. Proportional hazard regression was performed to assess the role of sarcopenia in predicting all-cause and CVD mortality in patients with CKM syndrome. In fully adjusted model, presence of sarcopenia was associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.527, 95% CI: 1.274–1.831, p < 0.001), CVD mortality (HR: 1.903, 95% CI: 1.299–2.788, p < 0.001) and non-CVD mortality (HR: 1.407, 95% CI: 1.124–1.762, p = 0.0029) in patients with CKM syndrome stage 1–4 (Table 4).

In patients with non-advanced CKM syndrome (stages 1 and 2), sarcopenia was associated with a meaningful increase in all-cause (HR: 1.689, 95% CI: 1.111–2.567, p = 0.0141) and CVD (HR: 3.574, 95% CI: 1.241–10.291, p = 0.0182) mortality. However, the risk of non-CVD (HR: 1.352, 95% CI: 0.816–2.241, p = 0.242) mortality did not significantly increase.

In patients with advanced CKM syndrome (stages 3 and 4), sarcopenia significantly elevated the risk of all-cause (HR: 1.467, 95% CI: 1.193–1.804, p < 0.001), CVD mortality (HR: 1.544, 95% CI: 1.104–2.16, p = 0.011) and non-CVD mortality (HR: 1.443, 95% CI: 1.11–1.878, p = 0.006.

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analysis, patients who were followed up for less than 24 months were excluded (Supplementary Table 2). Findings of this analysis confirmed the robustness of the Cox regression findings.

Discussion

This study found that presence of sarcopenia (defined by low muscle mass) was significantly associated with more advanced stages of CKM syndrome and increased risk of all-cause, CVD, and non-CVD mortality. These associations remained significant after adjusting for a wide range of demographic, laboratory, and clinical variables and were supported by robust sensitivity analysis for mortality outcomes. The findings suggest that sarcopenia may play an important role in the progression and prognosis of CKM syndrome and highlight the potential value of assessing muscle mass as part of an approach to this complex multisystem condition.

These findings are consistent with the previous publications that reported sarcopenia as an independent risk factor of CVD, CKD, and metabolic syndrome [24–26]. The similar role of sarcopenia has been shown for the risk of mortality among the mentioned patients [27, 28]. This supports and extends prior observations that loss of muscle mass is closely tied to systemic inflammation, IR, and metabolic dysregulation, which are hallmarks of CKM syndrome.

IR and muscle loss are closely interconnected through several biological mechanisms. Hyperglycemia, a hallmark of IR, stimulates the activation of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, a key pathway involved in protein degradation within muscle tissue [29]. At the same time, IR impairs muscle protein synthesis by inhibiting the activity of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism, ultimately contributing to muscle atrophy [30]. Skeletal muscle, in Turn, serves as the primary site for insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, normally responsible for clearing as much as 80% of glucose postprandial [31]. However, in sarcopenic individuals, this glucose-disposing capacity is significantly impaired due to reduced muscle mass and diminished expression of glucose transporter proteins like GLUT4 [32]. A similar bidirectional relationship exists between systemic inflammation and sarcopenia. In sarcopenic individuals, this imbalance often shifts toward a pro-inflammatory state, characterized by elevated cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [10]. In sarcopenia, mitochondrial dysfunction results in the accumulation of mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mDAMPs), including mitochondrial DNA. These mDAMPs activate the Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathway and trigger Nuclear Factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, thereby increasing the expression of IL-6 and TNF-α [33].

The remarkable result is that the association between sarcopenia and CVD mortality was most pronounced in patients at CKM stages 1 and 2. This suggests that muscle loss in the earlier phases of the syndrome could amplify cardiovascular risk before advanced kidney or metabolic complications have fully developed. For clinicians, this means that early-stage patients may require more proactive cardiovascular protection and targeted lifestyle support.

The findings of this study highlight the importance of paying closer attention to muscle health in people with CKM syndrome. Sarcopenia was common at every stage of CKM and was linked to poorer outcomes, making it more than just a secondary observation. In clinical practice, a decline in muscle mass may be an early signal that a patient’s overall physiological reserve is waning and that their care plan should be adjusted to address this vulnerability. Therefore, routine incorporation of muscle health assessments into CKM care could help identify those who would benefit from early intervention. These assessments need not always rely on advanced imaging; even basic measures such as strength testing or body composition estimates can be valuable when combined with the broader clinical picture.

The implications extend beyond individual interventions. Coordinated care between primary care providers, cardiologists, nephrologists, endocrinologists, and rehabilitation specialists can ensure that muscle health is addressed alongside cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic management. By embedding muscle preservation into routine CKM care, especially in the early stages, clinicians have the opportunity to slow disease progression, reduce CVD mortality, and improve patients’ quality of life.

Strength and limitaions

This study’s strengths include its use of NHANES data, which provides a large and nationally representative sample, allowing for generalizable conclusions across diverse U.S. populations. Multivariable adjustments and sensitivity analyses enhances the robustness of the findings. Moreover, stratified analyses allowed us to explore relationships between sarcopenia and mortality outcomes in early and advanced stages of CKM.

A key limitation of this study is the use of muscle mass alone to define sarcopenia, without inclusion of muscle strength or physical performance metrics. Current diagnostic guidelines, such as those from the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP2) and the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), emphasize a more comprehensive approach that includes measures like handgrip strength and gait speed [34, 35]. Future studies should integrate these functional assessments to better capture the full clinical spectrum of sarcopenia and to refine its prognostic value within the context of CKM syndrome. Moreover, while the adapted CKM staging framework was validated for use with NHANES data, it may not fully capture the clinical complexities observed in patient populations. A further limitation is that this was an observational study. While our results suggest a meaningful link between sarcopenia, CKM stage, and mortality, we cannot rule out the influence of residual confounding or reverse causation. Finally, despite extensive covariate adjustments, unmeasured confounding, including physical activity, cannot be completely ruled out.

Further studies are needed to determine whether improving muscle mass or function can alter the trajectory of CKM syndrome. Interventional trials that combine resistance exercise, nutritional strategies, and possibly anti-inflammatory therapies may offer insight into whether muscle-targeted approaches can prevent progression or reduce mortality in this population. Moreover, integrating muscle health metrics into risk prediction models for CKM could refine patient stratification and support earlier intervention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights sarcopenia as a key determinant of disease severity and mortality outcomes in individuals with CKM syndrome. The consistent associations observed across CKM stages suggest that sarcopenia is not merely a comorbidity but a central feature of disease progression. Early identification and management of sarcopenia may offer a valuable strategy to mitigate risk and improve outcomes in this complex, high-risk population.
