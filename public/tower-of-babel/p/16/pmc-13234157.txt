A deep representation learning model to predict response to vagus nerve stimulation

ABSTRACT
Implantable neurotechnologies are increasingly used to reduce seizure burden in pediatric epilepsy. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), the most common option, is effective for only half of patients, with no means to predict outcome prior to surgery. As a result, many children undergo invasive and costly procedures without benefit. Although T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (T1w) is routinely acquired presurgically and may capture structural brain differences relevant to treatment outcome, its high dimensionality relative to sample sizes has limited its utility in predictive modelling. To address this challenge, we present VQ-VNS, a deep representation learning model to predict VNS outcome based on preoperative T1w (n = 263). First, we present data from the largest paediatric VNS cohort (n = 1046), wherein presurgical clinical data could not predict response (AUC 0.54,p > 0.99). Next, VQ-VNS was pretrained on 7433 T1w images to learn compact anatomical representations enabling its classifier to predict VNS response (AUC = 0.73,p = 0.007). Model predictions localized to serotonin-rich brain regions and inferred large-scale disruptions in network connectivity among non-responders. This biologically interpretable predictor based on routine structural imaging improves upon current clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Epilepsy is the most common serious neurological disorder in children1. The medical and psychosocial burden of drug-resistant epilepsy2 (DRE) has driven the increased usage of implantable neurotechnologies to improve seizure control. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is the most commonly recommended device3, supported by decades of clinical experience in paediatric populations. VNS has been shown to reduce seizure frequency4, improve quality of life5, and decrease mortality2. However, only half of patients realize a benefit after treatment, and clinicians are currently unable to identify responders to VNS prior to surgery6–8, leading to unnecessary, invasive, and costly procedures. Given that there is little variability in implantation technique9, heterogenous outcomes are likely related to intrinsic differences among patients – particularly in brain structure and function. There is an unmet need to presurgically identify responders to VNS to personalize treatment options.

T1-weighted (T1w) MRI, a routine component of presurgical evaluation in paediatric epilepsy10, represents a high-dimensional feature space (>6 million voxels per image) that may contain complex, prognostically-relevant biomarkers. However, it remains underutilized in predictive modelling, as prognostic signatures are likely embedded in subtle, distributed, and nonlinear anatomical patterns11–13 – challenges compounded by the modest sample sizes typical of paediatric imaging datasets. Approaches to identify VNS responders based on coarse imaging features such as the presence of lesions14,15 or structural etiologies of epilepsy15–17 have not been informative and reported inconsistent associations. Region-based morphometry18, is constrained by atlas-based parcellations that fail to capture diffuse, whole-brain patterns and yield weak associations with outcome. Crucially, there are no studies that have developed predictive models for VNS response on an individual patient level that are suitable for clinical use8. Consequently, structural imaging has yet to be adopted as a viable predictor of VNS response.

Recent advances in generative modelling have enabled self-supervised learning of compact whole-brain embeddings from high-dimensional imaging data, capturing subtle and distributed anatomical patterns without reliance on predefined features. Architectures such as vector-quantized variational autoencoders19 (VQ-VAEs) and latent diffusion models20 have leveraged these lower-dimensional representations to synthesize realistic brain images while modelling interindividual variability21,22. While primarily developed for image synthesis, these models’ principal utility in clinical contexts lies in their ability to learn compressed, semantically rich representations of whole-brain anatomy. Yet, their application to clinical prediction tasks remains underexplored23. Moreover, most prior work has focused on adult datasets – such as the UK BioBank24 – with limited attention to paediatric populations despite their increased neurodevelopmental variability25.

In addition to technical barriers, clinical deployment of deep learning in neuroimaging is often limited by clinician apprehension about model opacity. Interpretability is increasingly viewed as a prerequisite for clinical adoption26. Consequently, there is growing interest in addressing this requirement by either developing inherently interpretable models or applying post hoc analyses to explain the outputs of models not explicitly designed for interpretability27. In the context of implantable neurotechnologies, where treatment decisions often involve surgical risk and uncertainty, predictive models must offer both accuracy and mechanistic transparency, grounded in neurobiological plausibility.

In this work, we present VQ-VNS, a clinically focused deep learning framework for predicting response to VNS directly from presurgical T1w MRI. Our approach combines a VQ-VAE, trained to learn discrete whole-brain anatomical representations, followed by a linear support vector machine28 (SVM) classifier for outcome prediction. To provide interpretability, we integrate gradient-weighted class activation mapping29 (Grad-CAM) and Shapley additive explanations30 (SHAP) to generate voxel-level predictive salience maps. To examine the neurobiological mechanisms underlying model performance, we also related the salience maps to normative neurochemical brain architectures as well as large-scale brain networks disrupted in non-responders using normative connectomic data. Our results demonstrate that interpretable deep representation learning can be leveraged to predict VNS outcomes while offering insight into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying treatment response.

Results

Clinical data do not predict response after VNS

We compiled the largest database of children to undergo VNS (n = 1046) from 11 North American centres through the Connectomics and Vagus Nerve Stimulation Outcome Study (CONNECTiVOS, Fig. 1a)28. Participants were deemed responders if seizure frequency was reduced by ≥80% and non-responders if ≤20% (n = 694, Fig. 1b) to reflect the bimodal distribution of seizure outcomes and mitigate the effects of ambiguous intermediate outcomes29. The mean [SD; range] age was 10.6 [4.7; 0.8–24.2] years, 54% male and included 51% responders (Fig. 1b, c). We first examined the association of each presurgical clinical variable with response by applying ordinary least squares regression (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. 1). No clinical features were significantly associated with VNS outcome after false discovery rate correction (Supplementary Table 1). Nonlinear embedding of the clinical data using uniform manifold approximation and projection31 (UMAP) also failed to separate responders and non-responders (Fig. 1e; s = –2.7 × 10−4, p = 0.71). This was corroborated by principal component analysis which revealed low explained variance (<12% for PC1) and no systematic response associated differences (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2). Labelling the UMAP embeddings by treatment site revealed visually apparent but objectively weak (s = –0.04, p > 0.99) site-specific groupings (Fig. 1f), indicative of subtle inter-site variability in patient selection criteria without strong site-level effects.

Finally, consistent with findings in smaller cohorts6,8, an SVM model trained on presurgical clinical features – including demographics, epilepsy history, and electroencephalography – was unable to distinguish responders to VNS from non-responders beyond chance-level performance (AUC = 0.54, p > 0.99; Fig. 1g). Furthermore, there was no difference in output current (1.9 ± 0.7 vs. 1.7 ± 0.7; t(101) = −1.4; p = 0.17; n = 103) or duty cycle (21 ± 10% vs. 19 ± 10%; t(101) = −1.3, p = 0.2, n = 103) between responders and non-responders.

Self-supervised representation learning captures anatomical patterns associated with VNS outcome

To study whether structural brain differences could predict outcome, we developed VQ-VNS, a deep representation learning model to identify VNS responders based on standard T1w sequences (Fig. 2). Because each T1w MRI scan contains over six million voxels – far exceeding the sample sizes of most paediatric epilepsy datasets (typically <100 patients per centre)32 – dimensionality reduction is critical to mitigate overfitting and enable effective model training. However, conventional approaches to dimensionality reduction, such as region-based parcellation, are insufficient to model the relationship between structural alterations and response following VNS (Supplementary Tables 3-4). Thus, the VQ-VNS model consists of two stages: (1) a VQ-VAE19 architecture compresses the high-dimensional MRI data into a compact latent representation, and (2) a support vector machine (SVM) classifies patients based on these latent features. The VQ-VAE architecture is well-suited to compressing complex anatomical data into compact latent representations21. We trained this model on large datasets of healthy subjects and persons with epilepsy who had not received VNS (n = 7,433; Fig. 2a–c). These datasets included participants across the lifespan (age 1.9–88.9 years) and contained a wide range of anatomical and acquisition-related variability to support the VQ-VAE’s ability to learn robust, generalizable representations. We optimized the VQ-VAE model with a composite loss term – comprising mean absolute error, perceptual loss33, spectral loss34, commitment loss19 and an adversarial term derived from a discriminator35 model – to generate high-quality reconstructions of the input T1w image. Finally, we assessed whether this learned latent space captured features associated with VNS response without additional training. We encoded each subject’s image from the training subset of children who underwent VNS in the CONNECTiVOS cohort (199 of 263 images, Supplementary Fig. 5), computed the median latent representations separately for responders and non-responders, quantized these representations, and decoded them to generate synthetic archetypal brains (Fig. 3c). Compared to the archetypal responder, the resulting archetypal non-responder showed reduced grey matter, white matter and subcortical volumes, together with ventricular enlargement (Fig. 3d). These differences suggest that the latent representations captured structural features relevant to treatment outcome without direct optimization for VNS outcome prediction and that VNS non-responders exhibit lower brain volumes (i.e. atrophy) compared to responders.

Latent representations support accurate prediction and spatial interpretation of VNS outcome

To evaluate the predictive utility of the latent space for response following VNS, we trained a linear SVM classifier (Fig. 2e) using quantized latent vectors from the frozen VQ-VAE encoder. To reduce dimensionality, each four-dimensional latent tensor (x, y, and z spatial dimensions × feature channels) was spatially averaged across the x, y, and z axes, yielding a single 32-dimensional21 feature vector per subject. Each element of this vector therefore captures the global magnitude of the respective latent feature learned from the underlying T1w image. We then applied two-sided t-test-based feature selection on the training data (n = 199) to retain 27 of the 32 latent features (Supplementary Table 7), which were used to train several classifiers for comparison. The SVM demonstrated the strongest predictive performance (Supplementary Fig. 6). To assess the stability and significance of the learned features, we first trained 1000 SVM classifiers on randomly partitioned subsets of the VNS cohort. The mean AUC across models was significantly greater than that expected under the null distribution (mean AUC = 0.61; t(1998) = 28.6, p = 1e−150; Fig. 3e), suggesting that the latent representations carried measurable predictive signal.

The final SVM classifier was trained using the training (n = 199) and validation (n = 25) subsets of the VNS cohort and tested on an independent held-out set (n = 39). The resulting model achieved an AUC of 0.73 in predicting response to VNS (permutation p = 0.007; Fig. 3f; Supplementary Fig. 7; Supplementary Table 8). The VQ-VNS model maintained its predictive capacity across diverse age groups and treatment centres (Supplementary Fig. 7C). Model performance remained robust with the inclusion of subjects with ambiguous outcomes binarized at the 50% seizure reduction threshold (n = 53; AUC = 0.67, permutation p = 0.02; Supplementary Fig. 8). To evaluate the classifier’s ability to generalize across acquisition sites, we performed site hold-out validation, in which all images from one site were excluded during training and used exclusively for testing (Supplementary Table 9). Sites contributing small sample sizes were combined to create a single evaluation set. The classifiers achieved above-chance accuracy (0.61 to 0.67), suggesting site-invariant structural features predictive of VNS response.

To evaluate the specificity of the SVM in predicting VNS outcomes, we tested its performance on an independent cohort of children who underwent temporal lobectomy for temporal lobe epilepsy39 (n = 102; mean age [SD] = 12.8 [3.9] years; 69.6% seizure freedom). The SVM model did not predict outcomes above chance level after temporal lobectomy (AUC = 0.57, permutation p = 0.12; Fig. 3f), suggesting that its decision boundary did not generalize to this population.

To visualize brain regions contributing most to model predictions, we implemented a hybrid salience attribution method integrating SHAP30 values from the SVM with Grad-CAM29 from the VQ-VAE encoder. Subject-specific SHAP values corresponding to the 27 selected latent features were expanded to a full 32-dimensional vector by assigning zeros to the unselected features. We computed the dot product between this SHAP vector and each subject’s latent representation, and backpropagated the resulting scalar through the VQ-VAE encoder. Gradients and activations were then extracted from the final convolutional layer to produce voxel-wise salience maps. This approach enabled SVM-derived feature importance to guide spatial salience attribution in the original image space. Model salience localizes to serotonergic networks disrupted in VNS non-responders

To better understand how model predictions relate to the chemoarchitecture of the brain, we correlated the VQ-VAE model’s salience map to the topology of brain receptor and transporter densities across nine different neurotransmitter systems from over 1200 healthy individuals40. Neurotransmitter receptors are heterogeneously distributed across the neocortex and may be modulated by implanted technologies to modify neural states and reshape network-level communication41. Each correlation was assessed against a null distribution generated via spin permutation testing, in which a single rotational affine was applied across all subjects’ latent salience maps for each permutation, prior to upscaling and averaging (Fig. 4b). We generated 1000 such null maps per comparison to compute empirical p-values and account for spatial autocorrelation. The VQ-VAE’s attention was concentrated within brain regions enriched in serotonin transporter (5-HTT) expression (r = 0.26, p = 0.04; Fig. 4c).

The anatomically distributed pattern of salient regions also suggested involvement of broader brain networks in VNS response. To further interpret model predictions, we leveraged lesion network mapping, an approach which links focal structural abnormalities to disruptions in large-scale brain circuitry using normative connectomic data42. This allowed us to test whether brain atrophy in non-responders, relative to 2382 typically developing children (age 1.9 to 25.0 years, 63% male), converged upon a common network aligned with the VQ-VNS model’s saliency profile. We first demonstrated that cortical thickness measurements did not contain site-specific batch effects (Supplementary Fig. 9) and that group-level spatial patterns of atrophy alone did not clearly distinguish responders from non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 10). Regional brain atrophy maps converged upon large-scale structural (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 11) and functional (Fig. 4e) networks that were preferentially disrupted in non-responders to VNS. The VQ-VNS model’s saliency map showed significant spatial concordance (Supplementary Fig. 12a) with these structural (p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 12b) and functional (p = 0.04, Supplementary Fig. 12c) networks following spin permutation tests. Network findings were also concordant with brain regions to which vagus afferent fibres are known to project through the nucleus tractus solitarius32, including areas that are activated by VNS43.

Discussion

The inability to predict VNS response before surgery leads half of treated children to undergo invasive treatment without benefit, while exposing them to unnecessary risks. Current methods for identifying candidates rely solely on presurgical clinical features, which perform no better than chance. In this study, we introduce VQ-VNS, a deep learning model that predicts VNS response from a single, presurgical T1w MRI. Moreover, this model extends the utility of T1w MRI beyond structural assessment by capturing features indicative of network-level dysfunction. By combining widely available structural imaging with an interpretable, representation-based architecture, VQ-VNS offers a new framework for both individualized outcome prediction and deriving mechanistic insights into response to neuromodulation.

VQ-VNS addresses a central limitation in applying deep learning to clinical neuroimaging: the mismatch between high data dimensionality and limited sample sizes. Traditional approaches typically address this imbalance in one of two ways. Some extract coarse regional features, which allows modelling of inter-regional interactions but sacrifices spatial precision44. Voxel- or vertex-wise analyses preserve high spatial resolution but treat each location independently, making them sensitive to spatially clustered group-level differences while overlooking subtle, distributed patterns spread across the brain45. This dichotomy forces a trade-off between spatial resolution and global context, limiting the ability to capture the distributed, nonlinear interactions that likely drive complex clinical outcomes. In contrast, VQ-VNS leverages a vector-quantized variational autoencoder to learn compact whole-brain representations that retain globally relevant features. By exploiting the spatial inductive biases of convolutional architectures46, the VQ-VAE model encodes distributed structural patterns into a low-dimensional latent space amenable to interpretable classification. Through a repurposing of vector-quantized models – typically used in generative tasks – VQ-VNS bridges the gap between detail and integration, offering a new framework for using structural imaging in clinical modelling.

The VQ-VNS model demonstrated predictive accuracy above chance levels across the paediatric age span. In the CONNECTiVOS cohort, we demonstrate that a child’s chronological age at implantation is not associated with VNS outcomes. Rather, a pattern of brain atrophy characterized by the VQ-VNS salience map predicts response across development (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Convergence of the salience map with the LNM analysis – wherein brain atrophy is parametrized relative to normative age- and sex-matched developmental trajectories – supports a framework where structural brain changes drive VNS response.

By linking latent representations derived from routine T1-weighted MRI to large-scale network disruptions, our findings provide evidence that deep learning models can infer clinically relevant alterations in brain network architecture using only structural imaging. Prior studies in animals and humans have shown that VNS modulates activity in distributed brain regions32,47–49 – including the nucleus tractus solitarius, thalami, insula, somatosensory cortex, and the frontal, temporal, and occipital cortices. Thalamocortical connectivity11–13 and somatosensory activation50 are also associated with treatment response. However, such insights have typically required dynamic modalities such as functional MRI, electrophysiology, or neurovascular imaging, alongside complex time series analysis. Our findings suggest that deep learning models can infer these network-level alterations directly from structural imaging. Consequently, this work also provides a framework to understand and predict putative responses to an increasing number of implantable neurotechnologies in epilepsy beyond VNS.

Taken together, these findings suggest that large-scale network disruptions – centred on serotonin-rich brain regions – may underlie differential VNS response in epilepsy. Serotonergic signalling has been implicated in VNS-induced neuroplasticity51 and is also thought to exert antiepileptic effects, as pharmacological depletion of serotonin levels has been shown to increase seizure susceptibility across multiple models52. This convergence implicates serotonergic circuits as putative mediators of VNS efficacy in epilepsy and raises the possibility that similar mechanisms may contribute to therapeutic benefit in other conditions treated with VNS, such as drug-resistant major depressive disorder53. In support of this, preclinical studies have demonstrated that targeted disruption of serotonergic neurons abolishes the antidepressant effects of VNS54. The consistent involvement of serotonergic pathways across both human neuroimaging and animal models points to a common neurobiological substrate underlying VNS response across distinct neurological and psychiatric disorders.

This study has several limitations. First, while VNS implantation is highly standardized with minimal inter-subject variability, stimulation parameters were not standardized across sites and were adjusted at the discretion of the treating clinician, leading to potential differences in the charge density delivered. However, among participants with available data (n = 103), mean stimulation parameters (output current 1.8 mA; duty cycle 20%) were consistent with optimally programmed populations55. Second, although our models did not account for variable follow-up durations, prior analyses on a subset of these data have shown that long-term response to VNS is predicated upon early clinical benefit within the first 3–6 months of programming56. Therefore, it is unlikely that additional follow-up would change response classification, particularly at the thresholds for response in this work. Third, images with significant anomalies or motion artefacts were excluded from response prediction because the pretraining datasets did not include images with significant anatomical abnormalities. This may limit generalizability to grossly abnormal brains, which represented <10% of our datasets (45 out of 489 images screened, Supplementary Fig. 4). Additionally, the VQ-VNS model was trained exclusively on T1w imaging. While more advanced imaging sequences could improve model performance, it would be at the expense of accessibility. Finally, while our salience maps aligned with the widespread serotonergic system, they did not overlap with the locus coeruleus-noradrenergic (LC-NE) system. This does not discount the established mechanistic role of the LC-NE pathway57,58; which may be underrepresented within our latent space due to its highly focal concentration within the brainstem (Supplementary Fig. 13). Thus, our findings highlight the role of widespread serotonergic signalling without precluding the involvement of other mechanisms. Ongoing work will focus on expanding these models to predict patient-centred outcomes such as quality of life59 and to support personalized treatment selection across a broader range of implanted neurotechnologies. Future work should incorporate probabilistic classifiers to yield confidence estimates that better support clinician decision-making.

The current study presents a specific deep representation learning model to predict response to VNS based on a widely available imaging sequence. This model, with strong neurobiological mechanistic explainability, can improve upon the current practice of determining VNS candidacy based on clinical features alone. Its application may support more effective treatment planning by identifying likely responders in advance and directing putative non-responders toward alternative neurotechnologies, thereby optimizing both patient outcomes and healthcare resource utilization.

Methods: VNS Data Collection

Data were collected through a multi-institutional registry as part of the CONNECTiVOS study (Connectomic Profiling and Vagus Nerve Stimulation Outcomes Study)59. This study involved children with epilepsy and was conducted across multiple North American sites, with local investigators engaged in design, data collection, and authorship. This study was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating sites (REB Approval Number 1000060282PID) and carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki60. Data were collected from 1046 patients who underwent VNS implantation at centres in Canada and the United States. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in the CONNECTiVOS protocol. All data were collected under local ethics approval with informed consent and assent where appropriate, and were de-identified prior to analysis. No material transfer, resource-poor settings, or elevated researcher risk were involved. We did not prespecify indications for VNS implantation, and all programming parameters were at the discretion of treating physicians.

For each participant, we collected continuous data on age at VNS implantation, duration of epilepsy, and percent seizure reduction at last follow-up based on parent-reported seizure frequency. Binary variables included: sex, presence of a genetic diagnosis, developmental delay, family history of epilepsy, history of infantile spasms, congenital or acquired epilepsy aetiology, and prior epilepsy surgery. EEG data included variables indicating focal versus generalized seizure onset and lateralization of interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs). Preoperative imaging data, including High-resolution 3D T1-weighted (T1w) MRI were collected both prospectively and retrospectively.

T1w MRI for the VNS cohort was acquired at either 1.5 T or 3 T. Scanner manufacturers include Philips, Siemens, General Electric, and Toshiba. Scanner models included Achieva, Skyra, Trio TIM, Ingenuity, Signa HDxt, Discovery MR750, Ingenia, Intera, Symphony, Prisma, Avanto, Signa Excite, Symphony TIM, Trio, Titan, Discovery MR750w, and Genesis Signa. Key parameters for T1-weighted imaging were: flip angle, 12.3° (8.0°–40.0°); echo time, 3.7 ms (1.7–9.2 ms); repetition time, 7.9 ms (1.1–25.0 ms); and mean voxel size, 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 mm.

Pretraining data compilation

We compiled data from a cohort of healthy subjects and persons with epilepsy from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE, n = 412)61, the Healthy Brain Network (HBN, n = 2081)62, the Calgary Preschool MRI (n = 265)63, the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN, n = 649)64, and the Amsterdam Open MRI Collection (AOMIC, n = 923)65, SickKids internal normative dataset (n = 1260), The Imaging Database for Epilepsy And Surgery (IDEAS, n = 542)66, Focal Cortical Dysplasia Type II (FCD-2, n = 170)67, Temporal Lobe Epilepsy – UNAM (UNAM-TLE, n = 66), Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP, n = 1065)68 datasets for a combined dataset of 7433 images. To balance retaining a sufficient training set size with sufficient subjects for validation and robust assessment on a held-out test cohort, these data were split into training, validation, and test sets in ratios of 75%, 10%, and 15%, respectively, which is commonly used in the context of clinical datasets.

The SickKids internal normative T1w images were acquired at either 1.5 T or 3 T across various scanners (Signa HDxt, GE Healthcare; Achieva, Philips Healthcare; Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthineers) with a dedicated head-coil. Images were acquired either in the sagittal or the axial plane: TR/TE, 6-9/2-5 ms; flip angle: 10-15 degrees, mean voxel size, 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.5 mm.

All T1w images were brain extracted38,69,70 resampled to 1 mm3 isotropic resolution71, bias field corrected, intensity normalized with Fuzzy C-means72, and rigidly registered73 to a 1mm MNI brain extracted template (Fig. 2a)74.

Analysis of clinical data

Participants were classified as non-responders if they had ≤20% seizure frequency reduction, and as responders for ≥80% seizure frequency reduction based on the bimodal distribution of treatment outcomes (Fig. 1b) and to mitigate misclassification risk inherent to seizure reporting from participants with ambiguous outcomes75. Based on the outcome threshold, 694 of the 1046 subjects were included. We used ordinary least squares regression to model the univariable relationship between each clinical variable and VNS response. Counts of missing values for each clinical variable are presented in Supplementary Table 10. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons.

To model multivariable and non-linear relationships, we applied Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)31. Silhouette scores76 quantified the clustering quality of UMAP embeddings by outcome and site, with significance assessed via one-sided one-sample t-tests. To further assess whether the observed manifold structure reflected biologically or methodologically meaningful variance, we additionally performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the same dataset. Loadings of the top principal components were examined to identify the metrics contributing most to overall variance within the clinical data. Scores for the leading components were then compared between responders and non-responders to evaluate whether these reduced dimensions captured differences associated with VNS outcome.

To assess the predictive value of clinical data, a support vector machine (SVM)36 was trained on 80% of the dataset (n = 550) to predict VNS outcome. The remaining 20% (n = 144) was held out as an independent test set to evaluate model performance after training. To ensure that both training and test sets contained only clearly defined responders and non-responders, subjects with ambiguous (intermediate) outcomes were excluded prior to this split. Recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV)77 was employed for feature selection. The clinical SVM model’s performance was quantified using the area under the curve (AUC) and confusion matrices. We additionally evaluated model performance with the inclusion of subjects with ambiguous outcomes (n = 352) and observed no improvement.

Cortical thickness estimation and regional-based modelling of response

Cortical thickness was estimated from T1-weighted images using FreeSurfer71 to derive individualized thickness maps used in all downstream atrophy and network-based analyses. Cortical surface reconstruction in FreeSurfer v7.4.2 began with an affine transformation aligning images to the MNI305 atlas, followed by B1 bias field estimation from white matter intensity variations and subsequent intensity inhomogeneity correction. Intensity normalization was then performed to correct for cross-site and cross-scanner fluctuations that would otherwise disrupt subsequent intensity-based segmentation steps. The skull was stripped, and white matter boundaries were automatically identified based on intensity and neighbour constraints. The white matter surface then underwent smoothing through tessellation and was expanded outwards to identify the boundaries of the pial surface. Cortical thickness was calculated as the average distance from a point on the smoothed white matter surface to its corresponding point on the pial surface and vice versa. The surfaces and parametric maps were spherically inflated and registered to a standard spherical atlas78. This was followed by region-wise regression to VNS outcome with a generalized linear model accounting for age and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV). The parcellations were derived from the Desikan-Killiany Atlas. Although this pipeline was established in adults, it has been validated for use in children79, and all surface reconstructions were visually examined for accuracy.

Vector-quantized variational autoencoder

T1-weighted MRI scans are inherently high-dimensional, making it challenging to train predictive models directly on raw voxel intensities – particularly in the context of relatively small clinical imaging datasets. This necessitates an approach to represent these data in a low-dimensional space without loss of detail necessary for downstream classification tasks. We implemented a vector-quantized variational autoencoder (VQ-VAE)19 from the MONAI library to generate these lower-dimensional latent representations. Compared to classical convolutional classifiers that require pretraining on surrogate labels such as age or diagnosis, using an autoencoder allowed the model to learn representations based solely on structural anatomy, without bias toward any specific pretraining objective.

Vector-quantized variational autoencoder implementation

The input to the VQ-VAE model was a 3D volume of size 176 × 208 × 176. The encoder comprised four convolutional downsampling blocks with 128, 128, 256, and 256 feature maps, respectively, yielding a latent representation of size 11 × 13 × 11 with 32 channels.

Quantization was performed using a codebook with 2048 entries and an embedding dimension 32. The resulting quantized latent had the same shape as the encoded latent. The decoder consisted of four transposed convolutional layers to reconstruct the final image.

The discriminator was a PatchDiscriminator from MONAI, consisting of four convolutional layers with 64 channels each, followed by a classifier head with a single output channel.

Training was performed for 140 epochs using the Adam optimizer83 with a learning rate of 1e−5. Reconstruction quality evaluation

To evaluate the fidelity of VQ-VAE reconstructions, we computed three complementary image-level metrics – mean squared error (MSE), multiscale structural similarity (MS-SSIM)36, and Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)37 between the original and the reconstructed 3D T1-weighted images. MSE and MS-SSIM were computed using MONAI (MSEMetric, MultiScaleSSIMMetric), and FID was calculated using features extracted from a pretrained ResNet-50 model84, following global average pooling across spatial dimensions. To assess the VQ-VAE model’s robustness to anatomical abnormalities, we performed a secondary evaluation on 106 subjects with abnormal imaging findings. Reconstruction fidelity was evaluated using the same three metrics – MSE, MS-SSIM, and FID.

To assess anatomical plausibility, all reconstructed and original images were segmented using SynthSeg38. Structure-wise agreement was evaluated across four tissue classes – grey matter, white matter, subcortical grey matter, and ventricles – by computing the Pearson correlation between reconstructed and original volumes across subjects.

Training the SVM classifier to predict VNS response

Response classification used the same thresholds as applied to the clinical data: ≥80% seizure reduction for responders and ≤20% for non-responders. Subjects with significant anomalies, prior neurosurgical intervention (Supplementary Fig. 4a–d), or conflicting outcome assessments, were excluded. After filtering, the final dataset comprised 263 subjects (Supplementary Fig. 5). The 263 patients included in model training did not differ significantly from the excluded cohort in terms of demographic or clinical characteristics (Supplementary Table 12).

An SVM classifier was trained on vector-quantized variational autoencoder (VQ-VAE) latent embeddings to predict binary treatment outcomes. The VQ-VAE first encoded T1-weighted MRI scans into a compressed latent representation, which was averaged across its spatial dimensions and flattened to yield a one-dimensional 32 feature vector per subject.

Latent features were evaluated via two-sided t-tests, and only those showing significant group-level differences (p < 0.05) were retained (27 of 32, Supplementary Table 7). To assess the stability and significance of the learned features, we first trained 1000 SVM classifiers with class balancing and a high regularization parameter (C = 5000) on randomly partitioned subsets of the VNS cohort. To assess generalizability and minimize data imbalance, participants were split into training (n = 199, responders = 100, non-responders = 99), validation (n = 25, responders = 13, non-responders = 12), and held-out test sets (n = 39, responders = 19, non-responders = 20) using stratified sampling to preserve approximately equal proportions of responders and non-responders in each subset. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) and confusion matrices across these sets. Robustness was assessed by permutation testing with 1,000 label shuffles to generate a null distribution of AUC scores, enabling empirical significance estimation. This procedure tests whether the VQ-VNS model’s observed performance exceeds chance expectations, thereby confirming that predictions reflect a learned mapping between input features and outcome.

To evaluate the SVM classifier’s ability to generalize across acquisition sites, we performed site-hold-out validation, in which all images from one site were excluded during training and used exclusively for testing. To enable evaluation of smaller test sizes, sites were grouped by geographic region. To further assess robustness, we tested the VQ-VNS model’s performance in subjects with intermediate outcomes - seizure reductions between 20% and 80% - without further model training. For this analysis response was binarized at the conventional >50% threshold. Similarly, model performance was evaluated using AUC and confusion matrices. The VQ-VAE encoder was frozen during SVM training. Latent embeddings were generated using an Nvidia RTX A6000 (Ampere, 48 GB VRAM) GPU, after which SVM training and inference were performed on CPU. In inference mode – including VQ-VAE latent generation and SVM prediction – the average runtime per subject was 0.19 seconds.

To evaluate the specificity of the VQ-VNS model’s performance, we tested its ability to predict seizure freedom in a separate cohort of patients with temporal lobe epilepsy39 (TLE) who underwent temporal lobectomy (n = 102; mean age [SD] = 12.8 [3.9] years; 69.6% seizure freedom). This retrospective TLE cohort was selected because high-quality preoperative T1-weighted imaging and detailed postoperative outcome data were available, enabling an assessment of whether the VQ-VNS model, training exclusively on VNS recipients, generalizes to an independent surgical population. In this cohort, approximately 70% of patients achieved seizure freedom following surgery, which enabled the evaluation of model specificity. T1-weighted images were acquired using Philips and GE scanners (Achieva, Genesis Signa, Signa HDxt, Signa Excite) with field strengths of 1.5 T or 3 T. Imaging parameters were: flip angle = 13.6° (range: 8.0°–30.0°), echo time = 4.3 ms (2.3–4.6 ms), repetition time = 8.7 ms (5.0–25.0 ms), and mean voxel size = 0.9 × 0.9 × 1.4 mm. Images with gross abnormalities were excluded, as was performed in the VNS cohort.

Model interpretation using gradient-based salience mapping

To identify brain regions relevant for outcome prediction, we generated voxel-wise heatmaps using a hybrid approach inspired by both Shapley additive explanations85 (SHAP) and gradient-weighted class activation mapping29 (Grad-CAM). The VQ-VAE encoded each subject’s image into its quantized latent representation. The feature vector for each subject was passed to the trained SVM to predict the response. SHAP values were then computed using a model-agnostic explainer with the median training feature vector as the reference. For each subject, the resulting SHAP vector was used in a dot product with their input feature vector. This scalar output was backpropagated through the encoder, and gradients were extracted from the final convolutional layer to generate latent salience maps based on gradient-weighted activations.

The latent salience maps were upsampled to the original image resolution and then spatially normalized to MNI space by applying the subject-specific nonlinear transformation from model T1 space to MNI space, previously estimated using ANTs86. Individual maps were averaged across all subjects, and the resulting group-level salience map was converted to absolute values to reflect feature importance. The signed average salience map was retained for receptor and transporter expression analyses, which are sensitive to the direction of feature contributions. Visualizations of the salience map are log transformed to aid in the identification of the most salient regions.

Mapping neurotransmitter receptor expression within salience map

To better understand the biological relevance of the regions in the VQ-VAE model’s salience map, we studied the associations to the normative chemoarchitecture of the brain using PET-derived neurotransmitter receptor and transporter expression profiles across nine different neurotransmitter systems, as compiled from previously published datasets87. These datasets were acquired from over 1200 healthy individuals. Spearman correlations were computed between the signed average salience map and each receptor expression map.

Each correlation was permutation-corrected using a latent-space spin procedure analogous to cortical surface map rotations88. Subject-level latent salience maps were rotated with random affine transformations prior to upsampling and spatial normalization. These upsampled maps were averaged to generate null group-level salience maps, which were correlated with each receptor expression map. Empirical p-values were defined as the proportion of null correlations exceeding the observed correlation. To account for multiple comparisons across all expression profiles, p-values were Bonferroni-corrected. Correlations with corrected p < 0.05 were reported.

Cortical thickness based lesion network mapping

To study whether model predictions align with large-scale brain network disruptions related to brain atrophy in non-responders to VNS, we employed a lesion network mapping (LNM) approach. Areas of cortical atrophy (relative to healthy age- and sex-matched subjects) were considered the “lesions”.

To evaluate potential site-related batch effects in the cortical thickness data, we applied t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding89 (t-SNE) to all cortical vertex values and estimated total intracranial volumes (eTIV), projecting them into two dimensions. To preserve local neighbourhood structure in high-dimensional data, t-SNE was selected. Although t-SNE does not preserve global distances well, it offers an established and effective visualization tool for our goal of exploring relative clustering and assessing potential batch effects. Embeddings were visualized by site (Supplementary Fig. 9a), outcome, and age (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c). Age showed the strongest correlation with t-SNE component 1 (Supplementary Fig. 9d). As no consistent clustering by site was observed, no further harmonization was applied.

Mapping regions of brain atrophy to a common network with normative structural connectivity

We sought to delineate whether spatially distributed atrophic regions in individuals map onto a common brain network associated with VNS response. To this end, we employed LNM90. We generated parametric maps to quantify each subject’s vertex-wise cortical thickness deviations from a normative population of 2382 children (Hospital for Sick Children, n = 393, ABIDE62, n = 338, HBN62, n = 1371, Calgary Preschool63, n = 251, Cam-CAN64, n = 29, age 1.9 to 25.0 years, 63% male). We fit first-order linear models for age and eTIV for each sex at each vertex in the normative dataset and applied them to the VNS cohort. Cohort residuals were then normalized to the normative residual distribution to derive age-, eTIV-, and sex-adjusted z-scores, which we termed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v$$\end{document}v-scores. Each subject’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v$$\end{document}v-map (full width at half maximum (FWHM) = 3 mm) was thresholded at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v\le \,-1.64$$\end{document}v≤−1.64 and binarized to generate the “lesion” map for LNM. Subjects were excluded if the atrophic vertex count exceeded 5% (Supplementary Fig. 10a) of the total brain surface (n = 5) to minimize the confounding effects of global atrophy. After applying this threshold and FreeSurfer segmentation quality-based exclusion criterion, 115 of 263 subjects were retained for lesion network mapping analyses. The remaining subjects were excluded either for failed FreeSurfer reconstruction or incomplete sex information.

To perform lesion network mapping (LNM), we used a subset of the Human Connectome Project (HCP) diffusion MRI dataset68 (n = 218, age <25). Diffusion data were preprocessed using TractoFlow91, which performs denoising, motion and eddy current correction, B0 normalization, and fibre response function (FRF) estimation. Tractography was performed using Particle Filtering Tractography (PFT)92 with interface-based seeding (60 seeds/voxel), a minimum streamline length of 5 mm, maximum of 200 mm, and spherical harmonics order 8. Only the b = 1000 s/mm² shell was used. Mean FRFs were disabled to improve multi-site harmonization.

Cortical surfaces were generated using FreeSurfer v6.0 as part of the freesurfer_flow pipeline. Surface-Enhanced Tractography93 (SET) was then applied using probabilistic fODFs from TractoFlow, with an anatomically informed stopping criterion at the grey–white junction to minimize gyral bias. Streamlines were further filtered using convex optimization modelling for microstructure informed tractography94 (COMMIT) to retain only those contributing meaningfully to the diffusion signal.

Processing was implemented via Nextflow95 workflows and Singularity96 containers, using publicly available images from the Sherbrooke Connectivity Imaging Lab (https://scil.usherbrooke.ca/).

Each VNS subject’s binarized atrophy map was used to filter HCP tractograms, retaining only streamlines intersecting the atrophic cortex. The endpoints of these streamlines were projected to MNI space (1 mm³ resolution)97. Atrophy networks were defined by combining endpoint maps across all HCP subjects and retaining voxels present in ≥20% of subjects. This thresholding approach was conceptually adapted from the disconnectome framework98, but tailored to the endpoint-based tractography analyses used in our study. Voxel-wise t-tests were performed comparing responders and non-responders across all non-zero voxels, with statistical significance determined via 3DClustSim99 (voxel-wise p < 0.05, cluster-level α = 0.05).

Mapping regions of brain atrophy to a common network with normative functional connectivity

To assess functional connectivity patterns associated with regional atrophy, we used the same atrophy maps described above as seed regions in a normative resting-state fMRI connectome. Preprocessed fMRI data were obtained from the Brain Genomics Superstruct Project (n = 1000)100. Preprocessing was conducted by the data providers and included removal of the first 4 volumes, slice-time and motion correction (MCFLIRT), exclusion of frames with framewise displacement > 0.2 mm or DVARS > 50, co-registration to anatomical images, regression of nuisance signals (motion, global signal, CSF, and white matter), bandpass filtering (0.0009–0.08 Hz), and projection to MNI-152 2 mm space.

For each VNS subject, the average BOLD timeseries from the subject’s atrophy map was extracted and used as a seed in voxel-wise linear regression across the normative dataset using AFNI’s 3dDeconvolve99. Correlation coefficients were Fisher-transformed to Z-scores, and voxelwise t-statistics were computed, yielding one statistical map per subject (n = 115). These maps were compared between responders and non-responders using a voxel-wise generalized linear model implemented in FSL’s randomise101, controlling for total atrophy volume. The resulting group-level statistical map was used in subsequent analyses assessing spatial concordance with the VQ-VAE model salience map.

Assessment of network concordance with salience map

To evaluate the spatial concordance between predictive salience maps and normative brain networks, we performed a voxel-wise overlap analysis using response-associated functional and structural connectivity networks derived from resting-state fMRI and diffusion tractography, respectively. All network maps were aligned to MNI space and resampled to 1 mm isotropic resolution.

We computed the mean of the element-wise product between the salience map and the structural and functional network maps to quantify the empirical spatial concordance. To test whether the spatial concordance was statistically significant, we leveraged the aforementioned latent-space spin permutation procedure, adapted from methods for rotating cortical surface maps to maintain spatial autocorrelation76 (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Subject-level latent salience maps were randomly rotated using affine transformations (consistent affine across all subjects per permutation), upsampled to image space via trilinear interpolation, and registered to MNI space using each subject’s pre-computed nonlinear warp. The resulting rotated maps were averaged across subjects to generate null group-level salience maps (n = 1000). For each, alignment was quantified using the same structural and functional procedures applied to the observed data, yielding a null distribution of concordance values. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 based on the proportion of null values exceeding the observed concordance, with the 95th percentile visualized for context.
