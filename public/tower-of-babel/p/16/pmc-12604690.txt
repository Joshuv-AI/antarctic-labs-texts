Frailty scoring systems in urological surgery: Current status and future perspectives
Authors: Patrick Juliebø-Jones, Arman Tsaturyan, Esteban Emiliani, Ali Talyshinskii, Bhaskar K. Somani, Wissam Khalid Kamal, Christian Arvei Moen, Eugenio Ventimiglia, Lazaros Tzelves, Christian Beisland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Frailty refers to a state of increased vulnerability and is recognised to be multifactorial. It is associated with a higher likelihood for functional dependency and a reduced life expectancy. In the surgical setting, frailty has attracted increased attention in regard to how it can impact post-operative outcomes. Many research groups have explored ways to translate frailty into a clinical marker and represent it as a quantifiable measurement. To this end, there now exist a plethora of tools (over 50 tools) available that have been applied in a clinical and/or research setting across nearly all medical and surgical specialities. However, the large number of tools can make it challenging for clinicians to know which one to adopt. This article provides an overview of the commonly used tools in urological surgery. Most tools have been studied in the context of urological cancer surgery and consistently show that frailty is associated with poorer outcomes. Of note, none of the systems described were developed exclusively for purely urological studies. Many of the abovementioned systems require a face-to-face assessment and therefore preclude retrospective data collection. Frail patients may not therefore be eligible on account of either one of these and it may account for why research in this field is lacking. Important outcomes such as the discharge location and how ADLs have changed are also challenging to collect retrospectively. Further research should focus on developing a frailty scoring system that is customised for urological surgeries.

INTRODUCTION

The term frailty refers to a state of increased vulnerability and is recognized to be multifactorial.[1] Frail individuals are at higher risk for negative health-related effects when exposed to both endogenous and/or exogenous stressors. Furthermore, it is associated with a higher likelihood of functional dependency and a reduced life expectancy.[2] While aging can contribute to frailty and it is more prevalent among older persons, ageing and frailty are not mutually exclusive. In Europe, its prevalence is estimated at 10% of the population, and this number is expected to double by 2040.[3] Of note, a larger proportion of the population is considered to be at high risk of soon becoming frail, and in a US study, 42% of community-dwelling adults were determined to be in a prefrail state.[4]

In the surgical setting, frailty has attracted increased attention in regard to how it can impact postoperative outcomes. Many research groups have explored ways to translate frailty into a clinical marker and represent it as a quantifiable measurement. To this end, there now exists a plethora of tools (over 50 tools) available that have been applied in a clinical and/or research setting across nearly all medical and surgical specialties. Given that frailty is recognized to be multidimensional, such tools usually cover several core domains such as mobility, cognitive and psychological health, chronic disease burden, functional independence (e.g., home living situation), and nutritional status. However, the large number of tools can make it challenging for clinicians to know which one to adopt. This challenge is further compounded by the fact that frailty is not specifically covered in the curricula of most urological training programs. To this end, this article serves as a primer for urologists and provides an overview of the commonly used tools in urological surgery.

METHODS

A comprehensive literature search was performed to identify articles implementing frailty scoring systems to assess outcomes associated to urological surgery. All urological surgical disciplines were eligible. Bibliographic databases searched included PubMed/Medline, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Search terms included “frailty,” “frailty assessment,” “frailty syndrome” and “geriatric assessment.” All article types were considered. The scoring systems that have been researched most in the urological literature have been presented and described in a narrative format [Figure 1].

RESULTS

Fried frailty phenotype

In 2001, Fried et al. proposed that frailty is a distinct clinical syndrome and can be considered a biological phenotype or syndrome.[5] Much of the work from that research group acted as a catalyst, driving increased attention and subsequent advancements in the field. This tool is also often referred to as the Fried Frailty Index (FI) or Fried Frailty Criteria. It is a 5-point system that allows for an individual to be classified as robust (0), prefrail (1–2), or frail (≥3). The core domains it covers are unintentional weight loss, weakness, exhaustion, slowness, and activity levels [Table 1]. Of note, cognitive function is not included. Face-to-face assessment is required, e.g., for self-reported fatigue levels. Burg et al. used this method in patients undergoing radical cystectomy (RC) and found that it could accurately predict high-grade complications.[6] It has also been employed as a decision aid for determining whether a patient is a more suitable candidate for an orthotopic neobladder or an ileal conduit when planned for RC.[7]

Clinical frailty score and frailty index

The clinical frailty score (CFS) uses nine levels to grade individuals on a range from 1 (very fit) to 9 (terminally ill). It is based on a global, one-off clinical judgment, and no calculations are required, which makes it a relatively rapid test to employ in a clinical setting. Furthermore, several studies have assessed inter-rater variability when using CFS, and the findings reveal consistently similar scores.[89] The scoring system provides descriptions of activities of daily living (ADLs) for each category, helping clinicians select the most appropriate score with greater accuracy. It is also commonly referred to as the Rockwood Frailty Scale, after the Canadian physician who developed it. Sun et al. performed a prospective study in patients undergoing radical surgeries for urological cancer. They found that a decline in ADLs at 3-month follow-up was 21.4 times higher for a CFS score over 4.[10]

Kenneth Rockwood also devised the FI. This is a more comprehensive assessment and requires more in-depth data collection in order to determine an individual’s total number of deficits (maximum 70). The sum is then divided by a predefined total and a final score can be calculated. <0.1 is consistent with a person being very fit, while >0.4 indicates severe frailty.

Modified frailty index

In order to allow for a simpler approach, a more concise 5-factor index has been devised.[11] The modified tool (mFI) covers heart failure, hypertension (requiring treatment), diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and functional dependence. A score of 0 indicates no frailty, while ≥3 indicates severe frailty. In a meta-analysis by Aceto et al., the authors concluded that a mFI score of ≥2 should be considered a “red flag” when evaluating suitability for urological surgery.[12] Voskamp et al. applied the mFI tool in patients undergoing RC and found frailty to be associated with a significantly higher risk of Clavien–Dindo ≥3 complications at both 30 and 90 days postoperatively.[13] Of note, an alternative 11-point mFI is also available.

Edmonton frail scale

The Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS) covers nine domains: Cognition, general health status, functional independence, social support, medication use, nutrition, mood, continence, and mobility/balance.[4] Total scores can range from 0 to 17, with >7 indicating frailty. There are very few studies in the urological literature that have applied this tool. Moro et al. applied EFS prospectively in patients undergoing a range of urological procedures. The authors concluded that frailty, as determined by EFS, was not a predictor for complications.[14] However, their sample size was small (n = 72) and only a few patients were ultimately classified as frail.

The timed up and go test

The EFS incorporates this tool as a basic assessment of mobility. However, it can also be applied as a stand-alone indicator.[15] Patients are tasked with standing up from a sitting position and walking 10 feet before returning to the same chair. This test is timed, with a result of <10 s categorized as normal, while a time of >20 s indicates high frailty status and an increased risk of falls. Sato et al. reported that gait speed >13 s was an independent predictor for postoperative delirium after urological surgery.[16]

Johns Hopkins adjusted clinical groups frailty indicator

The Johns Hopkins Adjusted Clinical Groups (JHACG) frailty indicator can be considered as a health analytics tool. The assessment is made up based on a group of predefined ICD 10 codes related to key domains that are related to frailty. For example, the following provide information on mobility: “R26.2 – Difficulty in walking, not elsewhere classified,” “R29.6 – Repeated falls,” “Z91.81 – History of falling.” A direct clinical assessment is not, therefore, usually required for this assessment; rather, electronic health records and claims data can be referred to. JHACG does not employ weighted scores; rather, the presence of any condition will indicate frailty. In contrast to most other tools, therefore, frailty is classified as a binary outcome rather than a continuous variable. Rosiello et al. used JHACG to assess postoperative outcomes post radical prostatectomy in the National Inpatient Sample database.[17] The authors reported that frailty as per the JHACG system was a predictor of nonhome discharge, overall complications, and higher treatment costs. Interestingly, 85% of those classified as frail had a Charlson comorbidity index <1. Of note, a separate tool is the Johns Hopkins Frailty Calculator, which is based on a direct clinical assessment. It covers five domains: weight loss, fatigue, low physical activity, slowed walking speed, and weakness. The latter is assessed based on grip strength using a dynamometer. It is scored 0–5 and also includes a “pre-frail” category. A limitation of this calculator is that it is not freely available and, to the authors’ knowledge, there are no studies to date that have used it in urological surgery.

Risk analysis index

The 14-item Risk Analysis Index (RAI) allows a score to be developed based on 14 weighted items covering the following domains: demographics, ADLs, comorbidities, nutritional status, cognitive impairment, and general health.[18] It was developed specifically for assessing frailty in surgical populations. RAI scores <20 indicate a nonfrail status. Several studies have concluded that it allows for patients at higher risk of postoperative complications to be identified before surgery.[19] A RAI revised version, has been developed for improved accuracy.[20]

Other tools

There exist a number of other tools that are not frailty-specific but often have overlapping elements. This includes the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) risk calculator. This uses data generated from the NSQIP to provide percentage estimates for parameters such as 30-day mortality and risk of serious complications. By entering the Current Procedural Terminology code, it is also specific to a particular intervention. Several studies have reported, however, that predictions with this tool often underestimate the true complication burden.[2122] Age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (ACCI) has also been applied to assess survival probability. A previous study on ureteroscopy in the elderly reported that ACCI >7 was associated with significantly worse survival.[23] Although not a formal assessment tool, bedridden status has been used as a surrogate marker for frailty to assess morbidity risk in stone surgery.[24] Sarcopenia is related to loss of muscle mass, inactivity, and poor strength.[25] As such, it is often considered a component of frailty. In a previous meta-analysis, Guo et al. found sarcopenia to be an independent predictor of mortality after urological oncology surgery.[26]

Comparing different tools – Is there a winner?

There are few published studies that have compared the available frailty scoring systems. Amin et al. obtained data on the 20 common urological procedures in a sample of 150,000 patients in the NSQIP database.[27] The authors compared the NSQIP FI, mFI, and RAI. All three indices were associated with increased odds for postoperative complications. However, they were all outperformed by the American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification. In another study comparing preoperative tools as part of the counseling for older patients with pelvic floor conditions, CFS was found to be superior to the Fried FI.[28] Beyond these examples, there remains a lack of research identifying a specific tool that consistently demonstrates superior accuracy in predicting outcomes for frail patients undergoing urological surgery.

Of note, none of the systems described were developed exclusively for purely urological studies. This could be an avenue for future research, given that there is still no one that is agreed to be the best.

Further considerations

In contrast to other areas of research, there is still a relative lack of studies performed on frailty and urological surgery. Many of the abovementioned systems require a face-to-face assessment (e.g., gait speed or grip strength) and therefore preclude retrospective data collection. Prospective studies are therefore necessary.

In general, the majority of prospective clinical studies exclude older patients as well as those with cognitive decline that prevents them from providing consent. Frail patients may not, therefore, be eligible on account of either one of these, and it may account for why research in this field is lacking.

Studies on frailty also highlight outcomes of interest that may not be traditional in the urological studies. For example, the discharge location (e.g., nursing home vs. own residence) as well as how ADLs have changed as a result of the operation. This data are also challenging to collect retrospectively. However, they represent important outcomes of interest that should be incorporated in future studies.

A further limitation in the body of research available to data is that the majority of studies have used the NSQIP database to evaluate these different frailty scoring systems. However, this is limited to 30-day follow-up only, and long-term data are not available.[1127]

CONCLUSION

A wide range of scoring systems is available to aid urologists in making a preoperative assessment of frailty status in patients. The majority has been studied in the context of urological cancer surgery and consistently shows that frailty is associated with poorer outcomes. However, at present, no single tool has been found to be consistently superior. Further research should focus on developing a frailty scoring system that is customized for urological surgeries.
