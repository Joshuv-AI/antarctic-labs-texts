Creation of the European Portuguese version of the Glasgow Composite Measure Pain Scale – short form (CMPS-SF) and initial psychometric evaluation
Authors: J C Alves, Ana Filipe, Ana Santos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPain is a complex experience, associated with several medical and surgical conditions. Median pain scores decreased with time.We presented the initial validation of the European Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF in the Portuguese language. It provides clinicians with a valid tool to assess acute pain in dogs. Background

The measurement of pain in companion animals is a complex and ongoing focus of attention for researchers and clinicians. Pain is a multi-dimensional experience with emotional, physiological, behavioural, sensory, evaluative, and affective components [1, 2]. It can be categorized as acute or chronic, depending on duration. Chronic, or maladaptive pain, can further be defined as pain that persists beyond the recovery or resolution of a primary injury, where the perception of pain continues, increasing the likelihood of developing neuropathic pain due to central and/or peripheral sensitization [3, 4]. Pain is not directly measurable, so it can be underdiagnosed and poorly managed [5, 6]. For that reason, it is crucial to have properly constructed and validated outcome measures to evaluate patients and their response to treatment [7].

Several instruments have been developed to measure acute [8, 9] and chronic pain in dogs [10, 11]. The Glasgow Composite Measure Pain Scale – Short Form (CMPS-SF) has been developed based on the original Composite Measure Pain Scale (CMPS) [12]. The validity of the CMPS in measuring pain, particularly after surgery, has been supported by different testing methods, including the evaluation of validity, reliability, and responsiveness, showing differences due to evaluator’s experience [13–15]. The CMPS-SF includes six behavioral categories: vocalization, attention to wound, mobility, response to touch, demeanor, and posture/activity, each of which contains a number of descriptor terms [12]. As the CMPS, it has been validated for assessing acute post-operative pain. It provides an important guide for veterinarians, as the obtained score is linked to the need or not for additional analgesia [16].

Validated versions of CMPS-SF translations into languages other than English can be found at www.newmetrica.com. Having an instrument translated allows for its use in a broad range of clinical settings with a large target population, backed by the scientific support of previously published works, even in different languages [17]. When an instrument is translated into a local language, several properties must be reassessed in the target population [7]. If a language is spoken in different countries, the instrument should be tested in the different populations, due to cultural, pronunciation, and vocabulary differences [18–20]. The evaluation of psychometric properties can be performed through various approaches. Instrument validity is determined to evaluate if the instrument measures what it is proposed to evaluate [21, 22]. A group of experts assesses face validity to assess if the scale looks reasonable for the determined purpose. Sometimes, the attribute to be evaluated cannot be directly observed. Under these conditions, construct validity is evaluated [23, 24]. This step is commonly performed through factor analysis and internal consistency, as assessed by Cronbach’s α [21, 25]. The ability of the instrument to deliver consistent results must also be assessed to determine its reliability [23].

Portuguese is spoken by 261 million people around the world [26]. We aimed to validate a European Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF, enabling its use in studies where the target population primarily speaks Portuguese. A previous validation of a Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF has been conducted in Brazil [27]. It is lickely that some terms, expressions, and verbs used by the two different populations are different, representing cultural and vocabulary differences [18–20]. We set three hypotheses to document the construct validity of the European Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF. First, pain scores decrease over time following surgery. Second, pain levels are associated with the severity classification of the surgical procedure. Ultimately, orthopedic procedures are often associated with higher levels of pain compared to soft tissue procedures.

Results

The sample included 68 dogs, with a mean body weight of 23.2 ± 4.2 kg and a mean age of 5.9 ± 3.1 years. Both sexes were represented, with 27 males and 41 females, and four breeds: Belgian Malinois Shepherd Dogs (n = 30), German Shepherd Dogs (n = 21), Dutch Shepherd Dogs (n = 10), and Labrador Retriever (n = 7). Two hypotheses were tested with a 2-way ANOVA. The first finding was that median scores differed between soft tissue and orthopedic cases. The second was that median scores decreased over time. After fitting the full model, no statistically significant interaction was found between time and type of surgery. This indicated that the rate of decline in pain scores over time was the same for both types of surgery. This term was therefore removed and the model re‑fit. A difference was found in the median pain score between soft tissue and orthopedic cases, as well as in the median pain score through time (p < 0.01). In all considered time points, the median score for soft tissue cases was highly likely to be lower than for orthopedic cases. No differences were found over time in soft tissue cases. A significant difference was found for orthopedic cases, where median pain scores were significantly higher at 2 and 6 h than at 24 h (p < 0.01 for both).

Discussion

The results of this study confirm the linguistic and cultural validation and construct validity of the European Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF, corroborating the findings and the comprehensive validation previously conducted in Brazilian Portuguese [27]. This translation into Portuguese is a further step in increasing the availability of this validated instrument. It allows for its use in everyday practice and research for an increasingly broader audience. This additional step will allow us to compare the results obtained with the Portuguese version to those using other validated versions [23, 27].

A previous report has validated a Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF [27]. This work was conducted in Brazil, enabling comparisons between the text of the two versions. While the meaning of the questions seems to have been preserved, several terms, expressions, and verbs are different, likely representing cultural, vocabulary, and linguistic differences and variations between European and Brazilian Portuguese [18–20]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report such differences. It would be interesting to evaluate the two versions in Portuguese and Brazilian populations. However, the Brazilian Portuguese version has been gone further extensive validation work, done through COSMIN and GRADE guidelines, encompassing criterion validity, construct validity, responsiveness, new cutoff points using ROC curve analysis, mobility of cutoff points within the scale through the inclusion or exclusion of items, sensitivity, specificity, and both intra- and interobserver reliability. This should be the way forward with the European Portuguese version.

Similar to what has been carried out in human [28] and animal [14, 29] studies, construct validity was assessed by testing if pain scores decreased over time following surgery, whether pain levels are associated with the severity classification of the surgical procedure, and whether orthopaedic procedures are associated with higher levels of pain than soft tissue procedures. Changes in pain scores during the post-operative period also allowed for the evaluation of sensitivity.

We did not observe a wide variation in pain scores for dogs within set groups or when comparing different groups. Some factors can influence these results and should be considered for future comparisons. One of those factors is the nature of the population considered. The population from which this study’s sample was obtained is very particular. Since it is a population of working dogs from the same Institution, the animals represent a set number of breeds, are subjected to a similar level of work, and are fed the same commercial food. The responses and behaviors of working dogs can differ from those of companion animals. Working dogs are known not to show overt signs of pain [30], which can make the assessment more challenging, although the team involved is very familiar with these animals. It will be interesting to evaluate if the CMPS-SF will have consistent behavior when applied to different populations [31, 32].

For the present study, the considered anaesthesia and analgesia protocol was relatively homogenous, encompassing different modalities. While contributing to patients’ comfort and low pain scores, it also plays a role in reducing score variability. For comparison, previous studies have excluded surgical cases where local blocks were used [25], as they could influence the observed results. This is particularly true in the case of cases classified as mild, where the multi-modal protocol may have provided a very good level of pain relief. Additionally, a relatively small number of different surgical procedures, with a limited representation of orthopaedic cases, were considered. Nevertheless, the instrument performed as expected, with pain scores decreasing over time. Orthopedic cases showed higher pain scores than soft tissue procedures, particularly in moderate to mild cases. Still, future studies should include a higher number of orthopaedic procedures to determine if there would be a greater difference between the orthopedic and soft tissue groups.

Although our results validate this European Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF, the study has some limitations. The properties of the Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF should also be assessed in a larger population of dogs and submitted to a broader range of surgical procedures, including more orthopedic procedures and others that would be classified as severe. It would also be interesting to evaluate the instrument’s performance with a broader range of evaluators and clinical settings, as has been performed with the original Brazilian Portuguese and Italian versions [12, 29].

Conclusions

In this study, we performed the initial validation of the European Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF and its validity in the Portuguese language. Further studies are required to determine if the present results can be replicated across samples and clinical settings.

Methods

Translation

The translation procedure was conducted in accordance with available guidelines [33]. The initial translation of the English version of CMPS-SF into Portuguese was performed by two bilingual researchers, native-speaker of the target language (forward translation). An independent researcher compared the two versions and obtained a unified translation. The unified translation was translated back into English by a bilingual translator with native-level English proficiency (backward translation). The two versions were then compared for accuracy until a consensus was reached on the final translated version. Feedback on the accuracy of the language used was obtained from a group of 5 Portuguese veterinarians, representative of the study’s target population. Any problems or issues with the Portuguese version of the CMPS-SF were addressed [34].

The CMPS-SF comprises six behavioral categories, with their associated descriptors: vocalization, attention to wound, mobility, response to touch, demeanor, and combined posture/activity [12]. For each category, the observer selects the descriptor that best fits the dog, corresponding to a score (ranging from 0 to 3, 4, or 5, depending on the item). The total score is calculated by summing the scores of all categories. A maximum score of 24 is possible if all items are considered. If the mobility category cannot be performed (e.g., due to the use of epidural anesthesia), the question of mobility is not considered. In these cases, a maximum score of 20 is possible. The original version of the CMPS-SF is freely available online (https://www.newmetrica.com/acute-pain-measurement/download-short-form-pain-questionnaire-for-dogs/). Study population and surgical procedures

Animals included in the sample were selected from the population of police working dogs of the Guarda Nacional Republicana (Portuguese Gendarmerie), constituting a convenience sample. Considered surgical procedures included castration, ovariohysterectomy, gastropexy, ovariohysterectomy in combination with gastropexy, gastrotomy, mastectomy, removal of skin masses, removal of small eyelid masses, entropion, aural hematoma, extracapsular cranial cruciate ligament repair, and traumatic lateral patella luxation repair. No restrictions were placed on the age, breed, or sex of recruited dogs.

All surgeries were performed by the same surgical team using standardized surgical, anesthetic, and analgesic protocols. Different anesthesia and analgesia protocols were used. Dogs were usually pre‑medicated with an alpha2‑adrenoceptor agonist (medetomidine) in combination with an opioid (buprenorphine or fentanyl). Induction of anesthesia was performed with propofol and maintained with sevoflurane in oxygen delivered in a semi-closed circle circuit. Sevoflurane concentration was adjusted to achieve an adequate level of anesthesia, based on the absence of palpebral reflex, ventromedial eye position, and jaw relaxation. Meloxicam (0.2 mg/kg) was administered before the surgical procedure. Constant rate infusions commonly administered combinations of fentanyl, lidocaine, and ketamine (at a dose of 0.0024 mg/kg, 1.5 mg/kg, and 0.6 mg/kg, respectively) during the procedures. For post‑operative pain relief, opioids were available as required, based on the CMPS-SF scores. For post‑operative pain relief, buprenorphine (0.02 mg/kg) was available and used for rescue analgesia. Local anesthetic blocks, using lidocaine, were used in all cases. For castrations, intratesticular and incisional blocks were used. Incisional blocks were performed for the removal of skin masses and procedures accessing the abdominal cavity. In addition, intraperitoneal blocks were used for ovariohysterectomy, and incisional in the abdominal wall were performed for gastropexy. Joint blocks were performed for joint procedures. For mastectomies, tumescent anesthesia techniques were performed.

Demographic and surgical procedural details, time of endotracheal extubation, and the need for post‑operative rescue analgesia administration were recorded for all dogs undergoing surgery.

Pain scoring

Pain scores were collected using the CMPS-SF at 2, 6, and 24 h post-endotracheal extubation for animals undergoing surgical procedures. The scoring was conducted by the same researcher, blinded to the procedure performed. Although the anatomical areas of the interventions varied according to the procedure, the researcher did not receive information on which procedure it was.

Statistical analysis

A similar approach was followed for previous validations of the CMPS-SF [29]. The surgical procedures were categorized by type – orthopedic or soft tissue – and considered in terms of surgical severity – mild or moderate. Results were evaluated in a two-stage approach. Box plots and descriptive statistics were used to make an initial evaluation of how pain scores changed through time for dogs that underwent soft tissue versus orthopaedic surgery. Pain scores of the procedures categorized as mild or moderate severity were also compared. Following this initial evaluation, a formal analysis was conducted. Different repeated measures 2-way ANOVA (random effect) models were used to explore the impact of surgery type, surgical severity, and their interaction. Follow-up comparisons were conducted when significant effects were found, using the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20, and a significance level of p < 0.05 was set.
