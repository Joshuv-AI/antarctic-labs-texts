Multispectral and anatomical assessment of chromium and nickel accumulation in urban weeds
Authors: Arlinda Cakaj, Marta Lisiak-Zielińska, Anetta Hanć, Paweł Drapikowski, Pierre Sicard, Maria Drapikowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early detection of heavy metal stress in plants is essential for effective environmental monitoring, particularly in contaminated urban areas. This study evaluated whether remote sensing combined with simplified anatomical diagnostics can provide a rapid and reliable method for detecting chromium (Cr) and nickel (Ni) stress in common urban weed species. Five species were selected: Trifolium pratense, Rumex acetosa, Alcea rosea, Amaranthus retroflexus, and Plantago lanceolata. Visible plant injuries were assessed using Evans Blue staining and image-based anatomical analysis, which enabled distinguishing between living, partially damaged, and dead cells. Multispectral observations using a MicaSense RedEdge-M camera allowed calculation of the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) to detect stress-related changes in photosynthetic apparatus. The studied species differed in their capacity to accumulate and translocate Cr and Ni. Metal bioaccumulation was low in all species (bioconcentration factor < 1), with the highest Ni accumulation observed in Plantago lanceolata. Translocation of both metals was the greatest in Trifolium pratense and Amaranthus retroflexus. Hydrogen peroxide levels increased in roots and leaves of all species, particularly in Alcea rosea. Despite the absence of visible injuries, microscopic anatomical changes were detected in T. pratense and R. acetosa, while NDVI values differed between sites. In summary, this study indicates that no simple relationship was found between physiological stress parameter values and NDVI. It is important to emphasize the need for continued research under controlled conditions with specific doses of PTEs salts. This should clearly demonstrate the relationship between plant physiological responses to stress and the results of multispectral observations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61835-6.

Introduction

Plant mineral content largely depends on species and their propensity to accumulate specific macro- and trace elements1. Minerals are usually distributed unevenly in the plant, hence their different value in roots and shoots2.

Chromium (Cr) is a natural element commonly found in rocky soils and volcanic dust3. Classified as a Group I carcinogen by International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) is highly soluble, mobile, and toxic, posing environmental risks through groundwater contamination and bioaccumulation4. Plants usually take up small amounts of Cr from soil, because it occurs mainly in the form of sparingly soluble compounds. The Cr content in plants usually does not exceed 1 µg g− 15. At low concentrations, Cr is considered essential for plants and may stimulate plant growth, possibly by participating in enzymatic metabolism or by influencing nutrients uptake6. However, some studies also suggest that Cr may affect the activity of enzymes involved in nitrogen assimilation, such as nitrogen reductase7. The phytotoxicity of Cr is largely attributed to the induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which generate oxidative stress, leading to cellular damage8.

Nickel (Ni) is widely recognized as a particularly harmful metal, belonging to the group of 23 metallic elements known for their serious impact on environmental contamination9,10. Although the type of parent rock largely determines the levels of Ni in soils, other factors, such as pollution and soil formation processes, also affect its concentration in surface layers. While Ni naturally exists in soil, its concentration in uncontaminated soils is generally low, typically below 100 mg kg⁻¹11. Although Ni is required in trace amounts, Ni is essential for plant growth and development12 and plays important roles in plant metabolism: it activates the enzyme urease, which breaks down urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide – crucial for nitrogen metabolism, it’s involved in seed metabolism and photosynthesis13. However, human activities including industrial, mining and smelting operations, as well as the intensive use of fertilizers and pesticides containing Ni have greatly increased its accumulation in the environment10,14. High levels of Ni can act as a potentially toxic element, harming plant growth, reducing crop quality, and lowering productivity, and may inhibit photosynthesis, disrupt cell membrane integrity, and interfere with nutrients uptake15. Because Ni has chemical similarities to calcium, magnesium, manganese, iron, copper and zinc, plants may absorb it in competition with these other nutrients10,16. This competition can reduce the absorption and movement of these vital nutrients, causing deficiencies. As a result, the imbalance disrupts normal physiological and biochemical functions in plants, which can ultimately lead to toxicity and cell10,17,18. Therefore, phytoremediation of Ni has become an important strategy for managing excessive Ni concentrations in soils19.

Industrial activities and sewage sludge use have also significantly increased Cr and Ni levels in soils and other matrices, raising growing environmental concerns20. In the context of increasing anthropogenic stress, the assessment of ecosystem status and pollution levels has become a priority. For bioindication in complex ecosystems, it is essential that damage detection is carried out in a fast, reliable and accurate manner. Research on physiological responses and the degree of tissue damage focuses primarily on visible plant injuries, such as chlorosis or necrosis8, for instance by analysis of anatomical damage21. Furthermore, the level of damage to the photosynthetic apparatus, using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), has become a standard method for assessing vegetation and crop health22. The method relies on the principle that the internal structure of a leaf reflects near-infrared (NIR) light strongly, as it is not absorbed by plant pigments, whereas chlorophyll pigments efficiently absorb light in the red spectrum. Consequently, healthy plants with high chlorophyll content absorb more red light and simultaneously reflect a larger portion of NIR radiation compared to less healthy or stressed plants23. The widespread use of NDVI for assessing plant health has increased with the growing availability of data – both from free platforms, such as NASA’s Landsat-8, and from unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs). These data are primarily used for NDVI measurements in agriculture and ecology, although they generally offer relatively lower spatial resolution24. Moreover, with the increasing availability of high-resolution data, researchers have begun to exploit these datasets for more advanced analyses, including the assessment of plant responses to stress before visual symptoms appear during controlled stress assays on individual specimens25–27.

The aim of this study was to investigate the effectiveness of Cr and Ni uptake and accumulation by selected weed species (Trifolium pratense, Rumex acetosa, Alcea rosea, Amaranthus retroflexus, and Plantago lanceolata) under exposure conditions located in an urban agglomeration. An integral part of the study was to estimate the degree of anatomical and physiological damage under stress and to verify whether data obtained from multispectral data, along with anatomical diagnostic imaging, are useful for assessing the state of plant damage under stress.

Materials and methods

Materials

Five widely distributed weed species were selected: Trifolium pratense, Rumex acetosa, Alcea rosea, Amaranthus retroflexus, and Plantago lanceolata. All species are well adapted to anthropogenic and disturbed habitats and are often found in urban areas, including as ornamental plant2,28. Moreover, these species have previously been used as bioindicators for the accumulation of potential toxic elements, including Ni and Cr, in environmental studies8,29–31.

Seeds of the selected plant species were collected from rural sites in western Poland, specifically within the Wielkopolska region and the Poznań agglomeration, during the natural seed maturation period between June and September 2023 depending on the species. At each site, mature, healthy plants were identified in situ by morphological characteristics according to Hultén and Fries28, and voucher specimens were collected and deposited in the collection of the Department of Ecology and Environmental Protection at the Poznań University of Life Sciences (no. 1–5/2023). Seeds were harvested manually, ensuring minimal damage to the plant and surrounding vegetation. For each species, seeds from at least 10 individual plants were pooled to capture population-level variation. Collected seeds were cleaned of debris, placed in labeled paper bags, and stored in a cool (≈ 4–10 °C), dry (< 15% relative humidity) environment until sowing.

All collection and handling procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and did not involve endangered or protected species. Prof. Zbigniew Celka from the Department of Biology Adam Mickiewicz University in Poznań preformed formal identification of the plant materials.

Organization of the experiment

The experiment was arranged according to previous research2. It began in April 2024 when seeds were planted under controlled greenhouse conditions (temperature from 16 to 18 °C, no artificial light). 5 L pots with a standard mixture of peat and sand were used (pH 6.8, N: 230 mg L− 1, P: 180 mg L− 1, K: 350 mg L− 1, Mg: 150 mg L− 1). The content of the tested elements in potting soil at the beginning of the experiment was 8.549 ± 0.054 mg kg− 1 for Cr and 7.017 ± 0.031 mg kg− 1 for Ni. Seeds of each species were sown in separate pots in equal amounts. Once germination occurred, the ten most vigorous and well-developed seedlings were retained in each pot. Throughout the germination and greenhouse cultivation stages, plants were watered once a day exclusively with deionized water, with 30 ml per pot. After 60 days of growth, the plants were relocated to three distinct exposure sites within the city of Poznań, each representing different environmental settings (Fig. S1). At each site, 3 replicates (pots) of each species were exposed (in total, 15 pots per site). The first location (Site A) was in the eastern part of the city, within a residential zone on the right bank of the Warta River (52°23′53″N; 16°57′36″E), characterized mainly by high-density multi-family housing. The second site (Site B) was located in the Botanical Garden of Adam Mickiewicz University, on the left bank of the Warta River, in the western part of Poznań (52°25′14″N; 16°52′39″E), surrounded primarily by green areas and low-density housing, with a main road located further away. The third site (Site C) was located in an area with low-density single-family housing and nearby road infrastructure (52°25′50″N; 16°54′58″E). At each site, three pots of each species were placed, giving a total of 15 pots per site (3 pots × 5 species). The plants were exposed for six weeks, from June 1 to July 16, 2024. During this time, they were watered once a day with distilled water, with 30 ml per pot. Information on air pollution at the sites during the exposure period was provided by the Regional Inspectorate for Environmental Protection and Institute of Meteorology and Water Management (Tab. S1).

Preparation sample

The plant samples were first rinsed with deionized water using Milli-Q Advantage A10 Water Purification Systems, Merck Millipore (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and separated into leaves and roots. The soil samples were sieved (2 mm). To achieve constant dry weight, the plant and soil samples were dried to a stable mass. Digestion of the powdered samples of plant (leaves and roots) and soil were carried out in the CEM Mars 5 Xpress microwave mineralization system (CEM, USA) according to a previously described procedure2. After digestion step, the samples were quantitatively transferred into polypropylene tubes and diluted 50-fold prior to analysis. The analysis of the element’s concentration in the soil was performed in accordance with the PN-EN 16,174 standards. Procedural blanks and reference materials were carried out in the same way as the samples in each digestive run2,8.

Analytical procedure and quality assurance

Elemental analysis of Cr and Ni was performed using an Agilent 7100x ICP-MS equipped with an Octopole Reaction System. Each species was represented by three replicates at each site. Instrumental parameters were optimized with the manufacturer’s tuning solution, and standard operating conditions were applied. Helium mode was used to minimize spectral interferences, while sample dilution and Rh as an internal standard reduced non-spectral and matrix effects. High-purity argon served as the plasma, auxiliary, and nebulizer gas. Calibration curves for Cr and Ni were prepared from multielement standards over the range 0.05–100 µg L⁻¹.

To evaluate trueness and establish the traceability of the measurement result, certified reference materials (CRM) were used: NIST SRM 1570a Trace Elements in Spinach Leaves (USA), NIST SRM 2711a Montana Soil. The validation parameters linearity, precision, limits of detection (LOD) and trueness were evaluated. The linearity of the calibration curve was calculated as the correlation coefficient (R), the value of which is greater than 0.9996 for all analytes. The LOD for determined elements were calculated according to LOD = 3.3 S/b, where S means standard deviation of the results obtained for the blank samples and b is the sensitivity (n = 5). The limits of detection (LOD) were 0.007 µg g⁻¹ for Cr and 0.005 µg g⁻¹ for Ni. The precision of the method, expressed as the relative standard deviation (RSD), was satisfactory, amounting to 3.0% for Cr and 3.5% for Ni. Trueness was evaluated by applying the certified reference materials and expressed as recovery (%). Recovery values ranged from 95% to 102% for plants and from 93% to 98% for soil, respectively. The results of Student’s t-test confirmed no significant differences between the measured concentration ± SD and the certified concentration ± standard uncertainty run8, .

Hydrogen peroxide content

The hydrogen peroxide content was determined using the method described by Patterson et al.32. The decrease in absorbance was measured at 508 nm using a UV–VIS spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Scientific Instruments, Japan). The reaction mixture contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.4) and reagents, 0.6 mM 4-(-2 pyridylazo) resorcinol, and 0.6 mM potassium-titanium oxalate (1:1). The corresponding concentration of H2O2 was determined against the standard curve of H2O2.

Microscopic analysis

From every pot of sample twenty-four leaf discs (20 mm in diameter) were randomly cut with a scalpel (in total, 216 leaf discs were collected in each species). Since no visible leaf damage was observed, material was collected at equal distances throughout the sample. To assess cell necrosis, a modified histocytochemical method of Faoro and Iriti33, modified by Drapikowska et al.21 was used. Leaf discs were boiled for 1 min in methanol to remove chlorophyll and rinsed with distilled water. Then, the samples were boiled for 1 min in a mixture of: glycerol, phenol, lactic acid and distilled water containing 0.02 g/100 ml Evans Blue, prepared directly before use. After the staining process, samples were refrigerated overnight. Using Evan’s blue, it was possible to observe in a light microscope individual stages of necrotic changes in all leaf tissues. This type of staining is frequently applied to identify cells which have lost integrity of cell membranes33,34. The dye is removed from live cells, but it enters and remains in cells with damaged membranes, staining them into intense blue color. Staining intensity, which is proportional to the degree of leaf cell membrane damage, is visible under a light microscope.

Image analysis

The procedure was done according to Drapikowska et al.21. The input data were the leaf surface color images recorded with the digital camera (Canon A640) mounted on the binocular microscope eyepiece. Pictures were taken of all microscopic preparations and were magnified 40 and 200 times. The analyzed surfaces covered the area of 1 cm2 for images enlarged 40 times, and the area of 0.5 cm2 for images enlarged 200 times. The aim of image processing was to extract regions (segmentation), in which the tissue was completely destroyed, partially destroyed and or still alive, and to calculate their area. The aim of image processing was also to remove noise in the form of incompletely discolored vascular bundles.

Vegetation index

MicaSense RedEdge-M multispectral camera was used to capture light reflected from the plant canopy in five spectral bands: blue (465–485 nm), green (550–570 nm), red (663–673 nm), red-edge (712–722 nm), near infrared (NIR, 820–860 nm). Image acquisition was performed at noon when there is no cloud cover. To calculate NDVI for each plant sample, the rectangular area of the plant canopy was marked manually and the mean value was calculated. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted to evaluate potential toxic elements (PTEs) concentrations in plant species across different samples and sites. Statistical tests were performed separately for leaves, roots, and soil samples for all species. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the significance of differences between and samples for Ni and Cr contents in leaves and roots, post-hoc Test Scheffe. Cluster analyses were performed using the R platform (R Core 2014) to identify similarities among sites, species, and patterns of PTEs accumulation. Data visualization was conducted using heat maps, which allowed comparison of specific element concentrations in plants and soils across sites, with two-dimensional variables (site and element) represented by color gradients. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to assess associations between elemental contents and sampling locations, as well as interactions among independent variables, including relationships between elemental concentrations, site, and physiological parameters, without any prior assumptions. All statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 13.1 and R (R Core, 2014).

Results

Potentially toxic elements content

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Scheffe Test) revealed significant variation between samples taking into account Ni and Cr content in leaves and roots (Tab. S2). The chromium concentration in leaves ranged from 0.9 mg kg− 1 in T. pratense (1B and 1 C) to 4.8 mg kg− 1 in R. acetosa (2 C). An exception is the observation for T. pratense (1 A), for which the Cr content is significantly higher (6.8 mg kg− 1). Cr content in the roots, ranged from 0.6 mg kg− 1 in A. retroflexus (4 A) to 3.0 mg kg− 1 in A. rosea (3 C) and A. retroflexus (4 C). In most samples, Cr concentrations in leaves were higher than those in roots. Exceptions were observed in a few samples (e.g., 1 C, 4 A), where Cr levels in roots were similar to or slightly exceeded those in leaves (Fig. 1).

Accumulation and translocation factor

Overall, the efficiency of Cr accumulation was relatively low, with the highest bioconcentration factors (BCF ≥ 0.3) observed in A. rosea and A. retroflexus (3 C and 4 C, respectively). In contrast, the highest Ni accumulation (BCF ≥ 0.3) was recorded in P. lanceolata (5 A and 5 C), followed by R. acetosa (2 C) and A. rosea (3B and 3 C). In terms of Cr translocation, the highest translocation factor (TF ≥ 4), the most efficient translocation was observed in R. acetosa (2 C), while T. pratense (1 A) and A. retroflexus (4 A) also demonstrated high efficient translocation. On the other hand, Ni translocation was most efficient in T. pratense (1 A) and A. retroflexus (4 A), with translocation factors ranging from 3 to 4 (Table 1).

Plant responses

An increasing level of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was detected in leaves and roots of all samples. The highest values of H2O2 were recorded in roots and leaves of A. rosea plants collected in Botanical Garden (3 A), with 5.5 nmol H2O2/min/mg protein and 4.59 nmol H2O2/min/mg protein respectively. The contents of the remaining samples are in the range of 2.55 nmol H2O2/min/mg protein in roots of A. retroflexus samples (4 A, 4B), to 4.55 nmol H2O2/min/mg protein in P. lanceolate (5 A). In leaves, the lowest activity was detected in T. pratense (1 C) 2.55 nmol H2O2/min/mg protein to 4.51 nmol H2O2/min/mg protein in P. lanceolate (5 C). In this dataset, H₂O₂ values ≥ 4.0 nmol·min⁻¹·mg⁻¹ protein indicate high oxidative stress, whereas values around 2.5–3.0 nmol·min⁻¹·mg⁻¹ reflect low to moderate stress, making relative differences among sites and species biologically meaningful2,8, (Fig. S2).

Taking into account variation of anatomical injuries, the undamaged tissue constituted the dominant part in all samples. All species have healthy leaf tissue in more than 90% of cases. Partially damaged areas showed a moderate percentage share between 0.1% (3 C and 5 A) – 50% (1 A). The necrotic area constituted the smallest part of the samples analyzed. In most cases, its share did not exceed 15%. The greatest damage was observed in T. pratense (1 A – 18.0%, 1B – 15.0%, 1 C – 18.5%). In addition, the R. acetosa sample (2 A – 24.0%) shows significant tissue damage (Fig. 4).

Higher NDVI values were observed in all examined samples from the Botanical Garden (1B − 0.84, 2B − 0.89, 3B − 0.83, 4B − 0.82, 5B − 0.86). Discussion

Species used in our experiment, proved to be good bioindicators2, so it was decided that they would also be useful in an experiment aimed at quickly identifying both anatomical damage (not visible to the naked eye), using the commonly recognized method for identifying dead tissue21,33. Next, we planned to identify physiological disturbances, such as chlorophyll production. This latter analysis was based on the integrated NDVI, commonly used for large-scale studies26; its application to individual specimens enabled precise detection of subtle physiological differences induced by Cr and Ni exposure. However, we employed a remote sensing method to detect each individual specimen. The calculated NDVI index had a completely different scale and allowed for a detailed comparison of samples in terms of their responses to potentially toxic elements stress.

By investigating Cr and Ni concentrations and bioaccumulation in different plant species, our research aimed to identify common weed species that can efficiently accumulate and transport these elements, i.e., acting as an effective hyperaccumulator. We showed that Cr concentration in examined species, were generally higher in leaves than in roots, (it was confirmed by high TF values), which may indicate the ability of the leaves to accumulate Cr37. In our results, the efficiency of Ni transport from roots to leaves was efficient, which is confirmed by high TF (values obtained in some samples of all tested weed species). Inversely, plants tend to accumulate more Ni in their roots than in their leaves, suggesting that Ni is retained in the root system and not transported in large amounts to the aboveground parts38. The variation in content between leaves and roots is also influenced by different environmental conditions, soil properties or differences between plant species39.

The results showed clear differences among plant species in Cr and Ni accumulation. Chromium levels in roots were varied but rather low in the studied plants, compared with hyperaccumulators such as sunflower (Helianthus sp.), for which concentrations of 13,700 mg kg⁻¹ in roots and 2,500 mg kg⁻¹ in shoots have been reported40. The highest Cr concentrations in roots were found in A. rosea and A. retroflexus, which also showed the strongest bioaccumulation. In T. pratense and R. acetosa, the highest Cr levels were measured in leaves (about 6.8 mg kg⁻¹ and 4.9 mg kg⁻¹, respectively). These species also showed effective Cr transport from roots to shoots, with R. acetosa being the most efficient. Unexpectedly, Amaranthus retroflexus did not show strong Cr accumulation, even though previous studies report that Amaranthus species can absorb both Cr(III) and Cr(VI). In addition, the conversion of Cr(VI) to the less toxic Cr(III) is a known detoxification process in plants37,41.

Nickel accumulation in roots proved more efficient than Cr accumulation, with the highest concentrations recorded in P. lanceolata, followed by A. rosea. In contrast, T. pratense accumulated higher amounts of Ni in leaves than the other species. The levels of Ni recorded in these plants were comparable to those reported for hyperaccumulator species such as Alyssum murale, Alyssum corsicum41, and Phragmites australis42. Nickel translocation was particularly high in T. pratense and A. retroflexus, with translocation factor (TF) values of approximately 3–4. Additionally, P. lanceolata, A. retroflexus, and A. rosea accumulated Ni at levels comparable to species from the genera Alyssum and Noccaea caerulescens43. Similar tissue-specific patterns of Cr and Ni accumulation and their oxidative consequences were described in Oxalis corniculata by Samreen et al.44, who found that both metals induce structural leaf damage and antioxidant activation, with Cr being more toxic than Ni.

Despite potentially toxic elements concentrations in plant tissues, none of the studied species showed visible leaf injury. However, earlier studies have reported that PTEs can cause chlorosis, necrosis, and reduced leaf area45, and the lack of macroscopically visible symptoms required targeted microscopic assessment to uncover early, sub-visual damage, which is a recognized indicator of stress46. This apparent discrepancy between metal load and the lack of macroscopic symptoms prompted a targeted assessment of sub-visible (microscopic) damage. Therefore, it was important to examine anatomical changes that are not visible to the naked eye, as these changes indicate plant stress45,46. In bioindication studies, precise and rapid methods for damage detection are highly desirable. This can be achieved by combining two complementary approaches: classical staining techniques, which allow the identification of dead cells47, and partially automated image analysis21. Across all studied species, varying degrees of tissue damage were detected and classified as “partially damaged” or “damaged.” The most severe microscopic damage was observed in T. pratense and R. acetosa, partially correlating with elevated metal concentrations in their tissues. In red clover (T. pratense), this result was expected, as the species efficiently accumulated Cr and Ni in leaves, whereas common sorrel (R. acetosa) exhibited a high degree of Ni bioaccumulation. For the remaining species, particularly those with high bioconcentration factors, the relatively low extent of tissue damage may be attributed to species-specific stress response rates46 or greater tolerance to potentially toxic elements stress. These patterns correspond closely with mechanisms described by Moustakas48, who demonstrated that toxic metals such as Ni, Cd and Cr trigger intensive ROS production, disrupt mitochondrial membranes, impair electron transport and activate oxidative signalling cascades leading to early cellular injury even before macroscopic symptoms become visibleand our elevated H₂O₂ levels are fully consistent with this heavy-metal-ROS framework described in ROS-focused reviews.

The main source of ROS generation in plant cells under normal light conditions is the chloroplast, but other organelles such as mitochondria and peroxisomes also contribute to ROS formation. Therefore, knowledge of the local ROS concentration in cells is a major issue, since this parameter might vary strongly to evaluate the internal effects of environmental stress on plant physiological processes, including the performance of the photosynthetic system, remote sensing methods can be used. These approaches support the assessment of vegetation vitality through indices. In our study, NDVI was applied25, to assess the physiological condition of individual plants by assessing the intensity of the photosynthesis process49. We did not find a significant correlation between NDVI and Cr and Ni concentrations in plant tissues, highlighting the complexity of plant physiological responses to environmental stress. This result may suggest tolerance of the physiological apparatus of the studied weeds to potentially toxic elements, a result that is highly reliable in plants belonging to the PTEs bioaccumulators. To investigate the possibility of remotely detecting the content of a given element in tissues, studies under controlled conditions (with known dosages of Ni and Cr salts) using a hyperspectral camera are necessary. However, disruptions in NDVI are consistent with the findings of Dobrikova et al.50, who showed that metal-induced oxidative stress decreases chlorophyll content, damages thylakoids, and impairs PSII efficiency—physiological changes that can lower NDVI even at moderate metal loads. Significant differences in NDVI among study sites suggest that additional abiotic factors - such as soil properties, water availability, and microclimatic conditions - may influence both PTEs availability and overall plant conditions. The highest NDVI, recorded in the Botanical Garden, most likely reflects more favourable growth conditions that mitigated the effects of potentially toxic elements stress. The lack of correlation found in this study between the NDVI index, which indicates plant photosynthetic activity, and the damage to plant tissues detected by image analysis indicates difficulties in thoroughly assessing the actual condition of plants. It appears that in stress conditions, reduced photosynthetic activity is the result of intense physiological changes influenced by, for example, ROS (e.g., H2O2), while tissue necrosis occurs later and is not observed concurrently with the physiological changes51. As highlighted by Tian et al.52 or Seralathan and Edward53, these limitations are particularly evident in the detection of subtle physiological and biochemical changes.

Conclusions

This study revealed pronounced interspecific differences in Cr and Ni accumulation, translocation, and anatomical responses among the plant species investigated. The capacity of plants to accumulate Cr and Ni is site- and species-specific. Amaranthus retroflexus and Alcea rosea exhibited the highest concentrations of Cr in roots, whereas Trifolium pratense, Rumex acetosa accumulated the greatest amounts of Cr in leaves. The highest Ni concentrations in roots were recorded in Plantago lanceolata and Alcea rosea, in leaves in Trifolium pratense and Alcea rosea. Among the studied species, Ni bioaccumulation was low but deserves to be emphasized the most effective nickel accumulation was detected in Plantago lanceolata, while Cr accumulation was generally low across all species. Efficient root-to-leaf metal translocation was observed primarily in Trifolium pratense and Amaranthus retroflexus for both Cr and Ni. Anatomical-level analyses provide robust indicators of metal-induced stress that may not be visible at the macroscopic level. Lower NDVI, indicating increased plant stress, were observed at sites influenced by low-intensity residential areas and major roads. Overall, the results confirm the usefulness of selected common plant species as bioindicators of Cr and Ni contamination. Their differential accumulation patterns, translocation abilities, and measurable anatomical responses highlight their potential application in environmental monitoring, although their suitability for phytoextraction remains limited. In summary, when assessing plant health under near-natural conditions, integrating chemical, anatomical, and NDVI assessments provides a broader and more reliable assessment of plant stress responses than any single indicator. The next step in future research will be to assess the response of these weed species to known Ni and Cr salt doses using image analysis combined with hyperspectral analysis under controlled conditions.
