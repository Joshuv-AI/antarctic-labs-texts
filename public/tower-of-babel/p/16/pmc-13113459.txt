Diagnosis of human cutaneous leishmaniasis: comparison between ITS1-PCR and ITS1-219 next generation sequencing
Authors: Hanan Al-Jawabreh, Suheir Ereqat, Amer Al-Jawabreh, Lina Dana, Abedelmajeed Nasereddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeishmaniasis is a vector-borne disease caused by a protozoan parasite of the genus Leishmania. The infection is transmitted by the bite of infected female sandflies of the genus Phlebotomus in the Old World or Lutzomyia in the New World. The diagnosis is made by stained smear microscopy, in vitro culture, and DNA-based detection methods.MethodsThe diagnostic methods used in the study included microscopy of Giemsa-stained touch smears, in vitro culture using NNN medium, and ITS1-PCR. The amplicon-based next-generation sequencing based on ITS1 (ITS1-219-NGS) was included in the comparison part of the study. The comparison included two groups: a CL-confirmed group that was positive by any of the tests employed, and a non-CL group that was negative by all diagnostic methods. The results of the ITS1-219-NGS were compared to ITS1-PCR followed by RFLP to identify the parasite species, which is assumed to be the gold standard owing to its superiority over microscopy and in vitro culture. The statistical tools used in the comparison were McNemar’s test, Bhapkar marginal homogeneity test, kappa statistics, and Gart and Buck equations.ResultsThe comparison showed that ITS1-219-NGS had a higher sensitivity of 94% compared to the assumed gold standard (ITS1-PCR) of 86%. Perfect agreement between the two test methods (kappa statistic = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.73–0.90) was shown. The species agreement between the two tests was fair (Kappa-0.24), with only agreement in 56% of the cases. Furthermore, the standard ITS1-PCR was unable to identify the parasite species in 29 CL cases, but was revealed by ITS1-219-NGS and confirmed by BLAST search. Unlike the ITS1-PCR, the ITS1-219-NGS identified all its positive results correctly, as confirmed by BLAST search. Moreover, 13 ITS1-PCR weak positive CL cases were found to be negative by ITS1-219-NGS, and upon BLAST search shown to be contamination of human and bacterial origin.ConclusionAmplicon-based ITS1-219-NGS is a superior, feasible, highly sensitive, and high-throughput diagnostic method with accurate species identification that can be used in clinical practice and epidemiologic surveys. Introduction

Leishmaniases is a neglected tropical disease caused by Leishmania, a kinetoplastidae parasite. The infection is transmitted by the bite of infected Phlebotomus sandfly. The disease is distributed globally in 99 countries out of 200 countries and territories are endemic for leishmaniasis. More than one billion people live in areas endemic for leishmaniasis and are at risk of infection risk [1]. Leishmaniasis is clinically manifested in three main forms; cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) caused mainly by L. major and L. tropica; fatal visceral leishmaniasis (VL) caused by L. donovani and L. infantum; and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL) caused by L. braziliansis [2]. The definition of CL adopted by WHO concluded that a case of cutaneous leishmaniasis is a person showing clinical signs (skin or mucosal lesions) with parasitological confirmation of the diagnosis (positive smear or culture) and/or, for mucocutaneous leishmaniasis only, serological diagnosis [3]. Leishmaniasis can be diagnosed depending on the form of disease, cutaneous, mucocutaneous, or visceral. Specimen of choice for cutaneous and MCL is skin scrapings from the skin or mucosal ulcer [4]. While, the specimen of choice for VL is splenic puncture, bone marrow aspirate, lymph node aspirate, and buffy coat of peripheral whole blood with a sensitivity of > 95%, 60–85%, 50–65%, and 20–70%, respectively [5–8]. The sensitivity of methods varies depending on the diagnostic method used ranging from low sensitivity by microscopy to the high sensitivity achieved by IFAT (immunofluorescence antibody test) and PCR. To attain higher sensitivity, nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) were used. The NAAT methods depend on the amplification of a certain Leishmania DNA target followed by amplicon visualization and DNA sequencing. One study identified some 51 DNA targets for the diagnosis of leishmaniasis [9]. The selection of the DNA target depends on copy numbers, increased variation to detect species level, and stability of target [9, 10]. In addition to high analytical sensitivity (62–93%) and specificity, NAATs were attractive for diagnosis and typing purposes especially because they are easiness, applicable on wide range of clinical specimen. Real time PCR (qPCR), another version of NAAT, has been additionally used to accurate diagnosis, monitor therapy by sequentially detecting parasite load and further identify asymptomatic cases with high sensitivity, specificity, and robustness [11–13]. The sensitivity of the NAAT depends on many parameters as the DNA target, type and source of specimen, quality of extracted DNA, specimen storage and transportation conditions, amplification protocols, work setup (research or clinical practice), and method of result visualization [3]. The large number of targets in NAAT methods caused result inconsistencies with varying levels of sensitivity and specificity, yet still higher than conventional diagnostic methods [14]. However, such inconsistencies have been minimized by utilizing a stringent control panel including negative and positive controls, inhibition control by spiking specimen tube with a minimum concentration of positive control to check for the presence of inhibitors if results are negative. In addition, DNA extraction control has been utilized by targeting host (not Leishmania) housekeeping genes such as β–actin or β–globin which should be positive indicating successful DNA extraction [15, 16]. Since 2020, amplicon-based next-generation sequencing (AmpNGS) technology has surfaced as a new NAAT technology for the diagnosis and species identification of Leishmania in DNA samples by high-throughput sequencing and database comparison without the need to isolate pathogens or run gel electrophoresis. Compared with traditional methods; NGS, whether amplicon-based (AmpNGS) [17] or metagenomic (mNGS) [18] is more advantageous for the identification and speciation of Leishmania. Despite the limited use of NGS method of diagnosis in the field of leishmaniasis, this study is intended to compare the ITS1-219-NGS with the conventional ITS1-PCR in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and species identification.

Methods

Study design and study population

A cross-sectional study design was used to compare the two types of PCRs. The study included specimen from patients attending or referred to the Central Public Health Laboratory (CPHL)-Ministry of Health in Ramallah-Palestine and the Leishmaniases Research Unit (LRU)-Jericho for routine laboratory diagnosis during the period between 2016 and 2024.

Sample collection, laboratory diagnosis and DNA extraction

For each patient attending LRU-Jericho, five touch smears were taken from CL-suspected lesion(s), dermal tissue aspirate with normal saline for seeding into NNN culture medium, four dermal tissue drops from CL ulcer spotted on sterile filter paper, touch smears were stained with Giemsa stain, and examined microscopically for amastigotes, and dermal tissue aspirates were cultured in rabbit enriched NNN culture media during the years 2016 and 2024, as described previously [4, 19]. DNA was extracted from spotted tissue drops using commercial extraction kits (Nucleospin Macherey-Nagel Germany). Specimen from the CPHL were DNA extracted from dermal tissue spotted on filter papers. The use of patients’ samples was approved by the IRB at Al-Quds University with only verbal informed consent was obtained from individuals or guardians. Patients’ data were not exploited, and specimen were coded for anonymity. DNA was stored at -80 °C until amplification.

DNA amplification and restriction analysis

The ribosomal internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) region separating the genes coding for ssu rRNA and L5.8 S rRNA was amplified by a PCR, using the primers LITSR and L5.8 S [20, 21], ITS1 was used as a target due to the high variability of the DNA sequence and length across Leishmania species but conserved within the genus which makes it suitable for species identification by restriction analysis. The efficiency of ITS1-PCR system was monitored by a stringent control system that included negative control with RNase free distilled water or no-sample reaction, positive control that included Leishmania turanica DNA (MRHO/MN/83/MNR-6). In addition, inhibition control was included in cases of suspected presence of inhibitors by targeting housekeeping genes as described elsewhere [15, 16]. Inhibition control involved duplicate run of the suspected sample in which the second tube was spiked with 0.4pg/µl L. turanica DNA from cultured promastigotes to ensure positive result and rule out the presence of PCR inhibitors such as hemoglobin [16]. Spiked tubes with negative results indicated the presence of PCR inhibitors. Finally, DNA extraction control was included to check the true negativity and that negative results were not due to DNA extraction failure. Two housekeeping genes were used; the β-actin gene [22] and the human β-globin gene [15]. PCR products of ITS-1 were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis and stained with ethidium bromide as described by others [20]. To identify the Leishmania species, each positive ITS1-PCR amplification product was digested at 37 °C for 2 h with the restriction endonuclease enzyme HaeIII using the conditions recommended by the supplier (New England Biolabs, 240 County Road, Ipswich, MA 01938–2723). The restriction fragments were separated on a 2% agarose gel at 100 volts for one hour in 0.5x TBE (45 mM Tris-borate, 1 mM EDTA) buffer and visualized under ultraviolet light after staining with ethidium bromide (0.5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}g/ml) as described elsewhere [15, 20].

Next generation sequencing Based on ITS1 region (ITS1-219-NGS)

The ITS1-219-NGS was developed based on the original ITS1-PCR allowing for a scientifically-sound comparison between the two methods as a major objective of this study [20, 23]. The amplification master mixes that have been used in the study were PCR-Ready™ High Yield from Syntezza-Jerusalem and GoTaq® Green Master Mix from Promega-USA depending on the availability. The PCR reaction mix was prepared in a UV sterilized PCR box with 0.2µM of each primer, 5% DMSO, 4 µl DNA template, and appropriate amount of mix and distilled water according to manufacturer’s instructions in a 25 µl reaction. The thermocycling profile using the cyclers C1000 from Biorad was 95 °C for 2 min as initial denaturation, and for 38 cycles denature, anneal, and extend were for 95 °C for 30 s, 53 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s, respectively, while ending with a final extension step at 72 °C for 6 min.

Two set of samples were selected for the comparison of the ITS1-219-NGS method. First, non-CL ulcer set (n = 94), which consisted of samples that were negative by microscopy, in-vitro culture, and conventional PCR based on ITS1 region. Secondly, CL ulcer positive set (n = 94), which is the one that consisted of samples that were positive by either of the aforementioned tests. The ITS1-219-NGS was carried out according to Nasereddin et al. [17]. using the primer pair ITS1219NGSF: TCG TCG GCA GCG TCA GAT GTG TAT AAG AGA CAG AGC TGG ATC ATT TTC CGA TG and ITS1219NGSR: GTC TCG TGG GCT CGG AGA TGT GTA TAA GAG ACA GAT CG CGA CAC GTT ATG TGA G with product size of 343 bp. The next-generation sequencing went through the following steps starting with amplicon based Library preparation, and sequencing which aimed to amplify leishmania DNA. First, ITS1-PCR was run in two 96-well plates, one for CL ulcer positive and the other for the non-CL ulcers. Twenty-five microliters of PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, X1) and eluted in 25 µl of elution buffer. Only 7.5 µl of the cleaned products underwent a secondary PCR to incorporate unique index sequences (N7XX and S5XX) for sample tagging, utilizing the Nextera XT Index Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Subsequently, 5 µl from each tagged sample was combined, thoroughly mixed, and centrifuged. Finally, the two DNA pools (100 µl each) for the CL ulcer DNA and non-CL ulcer DNA were purified with X1 AMPure XP beads and quantified using the Qubit® Fluorometer (Invitrogen). A concentration of 75ng/µl for the CL ulcers and 1.25 ng/µl for non-CL ulcers were prepared from the two pools. The target number of sequencing reads per sample was at least 10,000. Samples were subjected to high-throughput sequencing on the NextSeq500 system using the 150-cycle mid-output kit (Illumina, Inc., USA), generating reads from the forward strand.

Bioinformatics analysis

Binary Base Call (BCL) output files from a Next Seq 500 system were transformed into FASTQ format using the BCL to FASTQ conversion tool (bcl2fastq v2.20.0.422, Copyright © 2007–2017 Illumina, Inc.). The resulting files were processed through the Galaxy platform (Galaxy Version 0.7.17.1) (https://usegalaxy.eu/). Initially, the generated sequences underwent quality assessment via Fast QC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) to evaluate the integrity of the reads. The sequences were subsequently trimmed (https://usegalaxy.org/) with default settings to preserve high-quality reads, defined as those exceeding 100 base pairs in length and a minimum quality score above 20. This quality threshold corresponds to an error rate of 1 in 100, aligning with Illumina NextSeq sequencing accuracy metrics, which ensure a base call accuracy of 99%. The bioinformatics analysis involved creating virtual probes (Genus and species-specific DNA sequence) from a reference strain from the GenBank to be used in workflow pipeline for Leishmania genus and species identification by assigning the sequence read count for each species. In cases where non-Leishmania was identified, the predominant sequence was analyzed using BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) to verify species identity. A threshold of 200 reads per sample was established to confirm Leishmania positivity, following previously described criteria [24].

Statistical analysis

The WHO case definition of CL depends on demonstration of amastigotes in a stained smear taken from the ulcer or the promastigotes isolation following in-vitro culture. Both tests suffer from low sensitivity as shown previously [16, 25] making them inappropriate gold standards. In order to avoid evaluating a newly advanced method (NGS) using a relatively low-sensitivity method (ITS1-PCR), the following equations were used assuming ITS1-PCR as the gold standard [16, 26] as shown in Table 1.

The kappa statistic was used to measure the degree of agreement between the two methods, ITS1-PCR and ITS1-219-NGS in terms of results and species identification. McNemar’s test was used to detect any statistical difference between the two tests with dichotomous results (positive or negative) in a matched pairwise approach utilizing online GraphPad calculator (graphpad.com). As for the Leishmania genotyping with polytomous outcomes (L. tropica, L. major and undetermined); Bhapkar marginal homogeneity test, an extension of McNemar’s test, was utilized to detect the difference between the two diagnostic tests. The level of significance was considered as P-value < 0.05.

Results

This study is the first to compare conventional PCR with NGS utilizing the ITS1 region of CL. The vast majority of the previous studies were mainly conducted in China as case reports using metagenomics NGS of VL targeting the whole genome (Supp. Table 1).

Genus- and species-specific virtual probes and the NGS workflow

The bioinformatics analysis involved creating four virtual probes for the identification of Leishmania spp, L. donovani complex, L. major, and L. tropica based on the multiple alignments of nine reference DNA sequences (Fig. 1). The obtained sequences were analyzed by creating a workflow pipeline using the virtual probes to identify the Leishmania genus and species (supp. Figure 1). All CL ulcer cases were positive for Leishmania genus with number of sequences read count ranging from 3402 to 195,263. Based on species-specific probes, the sequence read count for each species was quantified 3109 to 95,796 reads for L. major, 781 to 179,253 reads for L. tropica (Supp. Table 2).

ITS1-219-NGS results

From a diagnostic setup, two sets of samples tested with conventional ITS1-PCR and subsequently genotyped by RFLP were randomly selected for ITS1-219-NGS test (Supplementary Fig. 2). One of the sets consisted of 94 DNA samples negative for ITS1-PCR (plate DNA bulk concentration = 1.24ng by Qubit™ fluorometer), while the other set of 94 was positive (plate DNA bulk concentration = 75.2ng by Qubit™ fluorometer). In the ITS1-PCR negative set of samples, four DNA samples (4%) were shown to be positive by the new method (ITS1-219-NGS) and further confirmed by BLAST search of the four DNA sequences which completely matched Leishmania DNA in the GenBank. While 13 weakly positive DNA samples (14%) from the positive set were shown to be negative by the ITS1-219-NGS (Table 2, Supp. Table 2). Ten out of the 13 weakly ITS1-PCR positive cases were shown to have nonspecific amplicons. The study showed that there was an almost perfect agreement between the two test methods (kappa statistic = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.73–0.90). The difference between the two diagnostic methods is a border line statistically significant (McNemar’s test, P-value = 0.049–0.052) with a difference of 4.8% (95%CI = 0.5-9.0) between the two tests.

Comparative analysis between species

Table 3 showed the results of the two diagnostic methods, ITS1-PCR and ITS1-219-NGS had fair agreement (Kappa statistic = 0.24, 93%CI = 0.12–0.37) on the species identification level and they agreed on 45 (36 + 9/81, 56%) observations only, while disagreeing on 36 results. The assumed gold standard (ITS1-PCR) was unable to identify species for 29 positive results, while they were accurately identified by ITS1-219-NGS. All 29 DNA sequences were BLAST-searched in the GenBank and found to completely (100% similarity index) match their corresponding DNA sequences in the GenBank, 28 Leishmania tropica and one L. major (Supp. Table 2). However, nine out of the 13 DNA samples that were ITS1-PCR positive but negative by the new ITS-219-NGS had their genotype incorrectly determined as L. tropica (7), L. major (2) (Supp. Table 2). Upon BLAST search of the NGS major reads for the ITS1-PCR positive, but ITS1-219-NGS negative; bacterial, viral and human origins were found (Table 4). The ITS1-219-NGS was able to genotype all its positive results. The difference between the two diagnostic tests was statistically significant (Bhapkar test; P-value < 0.001).

Validation of ITS1-219 AmpNGS test

As the gold standard (100% sensitive and 100% specific) for the diagnosis of cutaneous CL is not established so far; the most sensitive test currently available was adopted as the gold standard which is described as ‘assumed gold standard’. To overcome this dilemma, the study used Gart and Buck equations (Gart and Buck, 1966) (Table 2). Based on this, Eqs. 1 and 2 were used to calculate the sensitivity and specificity for ITS1-219-NGS test which produced higher sensitivity of 94% compared to the 87% for the assumed gold standard (ITS1-PCR) and a specificity of 100%. Without using Gart and Buck equations, the sensitivity of the ITS1-219-NGS test would have been 86% (81/94) and a specificity of 96% (90/94) which are lower than the assumed gold standard method (ITS1-PCR) (Table 3).

Discussion

This is the first study that compares the NGS technology for the detection and species identification of Leishmania in CL from suspected human patients. To date, the utilization of NGS was limited to few case reports of human VL [27–38] (Supp. table 1). We used the NGS method in the species identification and genetic diversity study of Leishmania tropica in the sand fly vector (Phlebotomus Spp.) [17, 39]. The two basic tests that depend on the demonstration of the two parasite stages, amastigote in the host and promastigotes in the in-vitro culture cannot be adopted for investigating a diagnostic test that is expected to be supersensitive. These two methods (Microscopy and in-vitro culture) adopted by the WHO case definition were shown to have extremely low sensitivity (Microscopy: 37–57%, in-vitro culture: 64%) with high false negative rate result, requiring experienced and qualified personnel, and inability to identify Leishmania species by parasite morphology [15, 40]. Therefore, as a gold standard the study adopted a similar test to new one under investigation in that it is molecular-based test that targets the same DNA sequence (ITS1) with established sensitivity and specificity in the literature that is higher than the conventional methods (microscopy and in-vitro culture), and used in similar diagnostic setting [16, 20, 41]. The ITS1-PCR assumed as gold standard used in the study has a sensitivity of 87% and 100% specificity as established earlier [16, 25]. Nonetheless, the ITS1-PCR was considered an assumed gold standard as it depends largely on agarose gel electrophoresis to visualize the ITS1-PCR and subsequent RFLP products which in considerable proportion of cases with low DNA concentration was difficult to read results and interpret restriction patterns. Even in the rare cases where Sanger sequencing was used, such low DNA-concentration samples gave erroneous DNA sequences. The study showed that the sensitivity of the ITS1-219-NGS has increased to 94% compared to 87%, the sensitivity of ITS1-PCR (assumed gold standard), while the specificity remained 100%. Despite the perfect agreement between ITS1-PCR and ITS1-219-NGS (kappa statistic = 0.82), the 6% increase in sensitivity was marginally significant (McNemar’s test, P = 0.049) which justifies the use of the new assay. The four ITS1-PCR negative, but positive by ITS1-219-NGS were confirmed by BLSAT-search. These four discrepant results may be attributed to low DNA concentration that yielded no bands on the agarose gel of the ITS1-PCR thus judged as negative, however, yielded enough number of reads (5016-190978) to confirm the presence of Leishmania DNA thus confirmed as positive. The peripheral blood is considered the least-sensitive specimen of choice compared to the most sensitive but invasive splenic puncture or bone marrow aspirate for the diagnosis of VL cases. The high sensitivity of amplicon-based NGS assay was supported by other studies which proved that NGS detected Leishmania DNA in peripheral blood but was negative by conventional PCR [33–35]. On the other hand, the 13 ITS1-PCR samples with faint bands on the agarose gel which were considered positive; yet, they yielded negligible or no reads by the ITS1-219-PCR, thus considered negative. The ITS1-PCR depends mainly on the observer’s visual reading of results compared to fully automated detection of number of DNA sequence reads. Faint traces of DNA bands on a gel agarose electrophoresis can be rated differently depending on the observer’s perception and experience. Such errors are subsequently inherited in the form of equivocal RFLP pattern.

The species identification of the Leishmania was shown to be significantly different between the studied diagnostic methods. ITS1-PCR and ITS1-219-NGS (Bhapkar test; P-value < 0.001) with high disagreement between the tests (Kappa statistic = 0.24) as ITS1-PCR was unable to determine the species of 29 positive Leishmania cases. This can be explained by the low concentration of DNA which fails to show clear restriction pattern especially when the band molecular size of the restricted pattern is small as in the case of L. tropica (24, 53, and 57 bp). Sanger sequencing does not resolve the problem as noisy uninformative DNA sequence will result in sufficient amount of the purified PCR (5-50ng) is required. Conversely, the ITS1-219-NGS was able to correctly identify species for all the positive cases including those missed by the ITS1-PCR (assumed gold standard). The advantages of the new assay test (ITS1-219-NGS) over the ITS1-PCR is the high specificity as it produces millions of DNA sequence reads, the multiple DNA sequences sequenced from the original DNA, and then giving a consensus sequence representing all for a given Leishmania sample which can then be confirmed by BLAST-search ruling out any probability for non-specific sequences to be interpreted as Leishmania. Unlike ITS1-219-NGS, ITS1-PCR followed by Sanger sequencing produces only a single sequence from the original DNA fragment leaving little chance for correct genotyping in case of partial or sequencing failure. An evident example of the superior specificity of amplicon-based NGS is the high number of reads detected by the negative control sample, which, upon BLAST-search, were shown as human and bacterial DNA contaminants. In this study, a maximum read length of 151 bp was used for sequencing, which was enough to make the correct identification and differentiation between Leishmania species. In this case, the NGS has an average error rate of less than 1/100 (Q20) leading to high accuracy, however, fragments longer than 500 bp tend to yield low quality sequences with higher error rates [42]. The low error rate and the millions of reads produced increases sensitivity and specificity of the method and thus accuracy.

Another advantage of using NGS technology is its flexibility to be utilized in targeting various genes, spacers, exome sequencing and even whole genome sequences (WGS) from different clinical specimens and from different hosts [17, 43]. Other studies have proved that amplicon-based NGS detected co-infection with multiple Leishmania spp. compared to Sanger sequencing that detected one species per sample [30]. Additionally, the assay can detect different Leishmania species coinfection mainly in opportunistic diseases in immunosuppressed patients [44]. Finally, the timeliness of the amplicon-based NGS method which takes 48 h starting from DNA extraction, amplification, sequencing and ending with bioinformatic analysis is considered short considering the high throughput sequencing power.

The main limitation of this study is the possibility of human genome contamination, host or technician, and bacterial DNA contamination from the environment which may impede the analytical sensitivity which can be mitigated by BLAST search in the GenBank and multidisciplinary interpretation of the results by microbiologists, clinicians and medical laboratory technologists. Another limitation usually raised is the high cost-per-test which could be true for a case-by-case test, but not for a bulk of samples such as in the case of epidemiologic surveys or cases in endemic areas.

Conclusions

Our study revealed the superiority of NGS-based methods over the methods currently in use. ITS1-219-NGS was shown to be feasible, highly sensitive and specific, and accurate for species identification. These findings highlight the need to incorporate it as a routine diagnostic method in clinical practice and epidemiologic surveys for cutaneous leishmaniasis.
