Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) for the treatment of epilepsy: a systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical trials (from 1990 to 2025)

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis systematic review and meta-analysis evaluates the efficacy of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) in reducing seizure frequency in patients with epilepsy, synthesising evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs).DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020 guidelines.SettingLiterature search across international (PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar) and Iranian (SID, Magiran, IranMedex) databases.ParticipantsAdult patients diagnosed with epilepsy, including those with drug-resistant focal epilepsy, from 14 included RCTs (total n=516).InterventionsActive low-frequency rTMS (0.3–1 Hz) compared with sham stimulation or active control (eg, antiepileptic drug therapy).Main outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the mean change in weekly seizure frequency analysed using standardised mean difference (SMD).ResultsFrom 1952 retrieved records, 14 RCTs were reviewed and 8 were meta-analysed (n=367). The pooled analysis showed a statistically significant reduction in seizure frequency favouring rTMS (SMD=−0.46, 95% CI −0.57 to −0.34, p=0.001, Z=−7.67), with low heterogeneity (I²=0.1%). The combined mean seizures were 6.1 (intervention) versus 7.4 (control) per week. No significant publication bias was detected (Egger’s test p=0.086).ConclusionsLow-frequency rTMS demonstrates a significant short-term reduction in seizure frequency. However, the clinical durability of this effect remains uncertain due to limited follow-up in existing trials. Definitive conclusions require larger, standardised RCTs with longer-term outcomes.Trial registrationNo human participants included.Keywords: Medical Devices

Introduction

Seizures occur due to an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory activity in the brain. Anti-seizure medications (ASMs) aim to correct this imbalance; however, approximately one-third of patients with epilepsy are resistant to these medications. Even when these medications are effective, they frequently produce undesirable side effects.1 2

In this context, several complementary and alternative methods for managing epilepsy exist, including vagus nerve stimulation, trigeminal nerve stimulation, deep brain stimulation and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), a form of non-invasive neuromodulation. These techniques are valuable, but surgery is also a very effective and safe treatment option for some patients with drug-resistant epilepsy, offering them the highest chance of seizure freedom. However, it is an invasive procedure and a thorough presurgical evaluation is necessary to determine the appropriate candidates.3 Repetitive TMS (rTMS) shows promise for treating various conditions, including epilepsy, major depression, cognitive disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, smoking cessation, schizophrenia, post-traumatic stress disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and autism spectrum disorder.4 rTMS protocols are categorised as low-frequency (≤1 Hz, inhibitory) or high-frequency (>5 Hz, excitatory). Low-frequency rTMS (<1 Hz) depresses cortical excitability, a mechanism that underpins its therapeutic application in epilepsy.5

Although rTMS is increasingly used for the treatment of epilepsy,6–9 its effectiveness in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) has been inconsistent. Some studies, including Fregni et al, 10 11 Menkes and Gruenthal12 and Wang et al,13 report that low-frequency rTMS can reduce seizure frequency. However, in rare cases, it may itself induce seizures,3 and other trials have not found significant effects compared with control groups.14 15 16 Other research suggests that rTMS may be more effective in adolescents with epilepsy.17 This inconsistency likely arises from variations in study design, including stimulation parameters such as intensity, frequency, duration, coil type, medications, treatment sessions and follow-up periods. Additionally, studies on rTMS for epilepsy vary, with some focusing on children18 or drug-resistant cases. Key issues include the prevalence of older studies, reliance on single databases (eg, PubMed),19 non-systematic reviews20 and the combination of human and animal data. Consequently, a new comprehensive systematic review is warranted. This review, conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, investigates the effectiveness of low-frequency rTMS in reducing seizure frequency in individuals with epilepsy using randomised clinical trials.

Materials and methods

Design

This study is a systematic review conducted in accordance with the PRISMA statement (2020). It includes all RCTs examining the effects of rTMS on epilepsy.

Databases and search strategy

Multiple databases (PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane, Scopus, SID, ScienceDirect, Magiran, IranMedex) and Google Scholar were searched from 1990 to October 2025 using PICO-based terms to identify rTMS epilepsy RCTs. Additionally, reference lists, citations and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform were examined for both published and unpublished data. The search strategy is detailed in online supplemental 1.

The process of study selection and data collection

A standard search methodology using English (MeSH) and Farsi keywords retrieved 1952 articles. After removing duplicates and irrelevant articles, the remaining studies were imported into EndNote, where the selection process occurred. The studies were initially screened based on their titles and abstracts by two independent and blinded reviewers. Subsequently, full-text articles were evaluated by the same independent and blinded reviewers. Any conflicts between reviewers were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted as necessary.

Data extraction and management

Two researchers independently evaluated data quality according to the Cochrane Handbook guidelines, resolving any disagreements with the assistance of a statistician and neurologist. Three researchers screened and extracted data, which included publication details, study design, participant information, specifics of the intervention (type, location, protocol), outcomes, results and complications.

Assessment of risk of bias in the extracted studies

The ROB2 checklist, a scoring system specifically designed for RCTs, was used to assess the risk of bias in the included clinical trials. This tool evaluates five domains: allocation concealment, intervention adherence, missing data, outcome measurement and outcome selection21 (online supplemental 2).

Meta-analysis

Analysis unit

The ‘study’ was regarded as an analytical unit.

Calculation of effect sizes

In this study, sample sizes below 20 per group were considered small, consistent with previous reports, with four of the eight included studies meeting this criterion (Joo et al,22 Theodore et al,23 Wang et al 24 and Melus et al 25). Using Stata V.17, the meta-analysis calculated mean differences and 95% CIs. Effect size was measured using Hedge’s adjusted g, which is similar to Cohen’s d but corrected for small sample bias. Standardised mean differences (SMDs) of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 represent small, medium and large effects, respectively.26 The SMD was selected due to the absence of other consistent data across the studies. The pooled SMD and their 95% CIs were graphically represented using forest plots (figure 1).

Evaluation of heterogeneity and publication bias

Heterogeneity in meta-analyses can result in ambiguous conclusions. This study evaluated heterogeneity using the T² and I² statistics, with I² values greater than 50% indicating high heterogeneity. Additionally, a funnel plot and Egger’s test were employed to assess publication bias.27 28

Results

Description of included studies

Search results

A total of 1952 potentially relevant articles were identified through database searches. After removing 560 duplicates and 1160 articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria, 232 articles remained. On reviewing the titles and abstracts, 90 studies remained for full-text review. The final analysis included 14 clinical trials (online supplemental 4). A manual search of Iranian databases yielded no new articles. Following a full-text review and quality assessment, eight studies were initially considered potentially eligible for meta-analysis. However, only eight of them provided sufficient and consistent quantitative data (eg, group-specific sample sizes and measures of central tendency and variability) required for effect size calculation. The remaining studies were excluded due to incomplete or non-standardised reporting, and attempts to obtain missing data from the corresponding authors were unsuccessful (online supplemental 3). Characteristics of included studies

We included 14 trial studies (516 patients) that compared rTMS with active or drug controls (Theodore et al,23 Tergau et al,29 Fregni et al,10 Cantello et al,15 Joo et al,22 Wang et al,24 Sun et al,30 Kistsen and Evstigneev,31 Seynaeve et al,16 Ashour et al,32 Sinha et al 33 and Vinod et al 34). Six studies were placebo-controlled (Theodore et al,23 Fregni et al,10 Cantello et al,15 Kistsen and Evstigneev,31 Sinha et al,33 Vinod et al,34 Hu et al 35 and Melus et al 25), and one study compared different intensities of rTMS (Sun et al 30). One study compared different intensities of rTMS as a three-arm intervention (two intervention groups against a placebo control group) (Tergau et al 29). One study compared the use of focal and non-focal rTMS (Joo et al 22). One study compared coil types of rTMS with a sham type (Seynaeve et al 16) and one study compared rTMS versus antiepileptic drug therapy (Wang et al 24).

Eight studies (60%) focused on drug-resistant epilepsy (Cantello et al 15; Fregni et al 10; Seynaeve et al 16; Sun et al 30; Tergau et al 29; Ashour et al 32 Melus et al 25). The remaining studies included all patients with epilepsy without specific restrictions.

Half of the studies employed 1 Hz stimulation, while five used 0.5 Hz and another three used 0.3 Hz. Round coils were used by Cantello et al 15 and Tergau et al,29 whereas eight-shaped coils were employed by Theodore et al,23 Sun et al,30 Wang et al,24 Vinod et al,34 Joo et al,22 Fregni et al,10 Hu et al 35 and Melus et al 25. Sinha et al 33 used both types of coils. Sham or placebo stimulation involved specialised coils designed to mimic the sensations of rTMS or the absence of coils altogether. Existing pharmacological treatments were maintained throughout the interventions. Wang et al,24 Kistsen and Evstigneev31 and Hu et al 35 implemented drug-only controls, while Sinha et al 33 and Ashour et al 32 did not specify the nature of the control intervention.

Impulse intensity varied across studies, ranging from 70% to 120% of the motor threshold. While some studies reported no side effects (Tergau et al 29 and Theodore et al 23), others noted common side effects, including headache,10 15 16 22 24 25 30 35 dizziness,15 25 30 35 sleep disturbances,10 35 discomfort, tinnitus and lack of concentration,16 lack of appetite25 35 and presyncope.25 One study reported a high incidence of side effects, affecting 97% of participants.31

All studies employed randomisation. Sun et al 30 and Wang et al 24 conducted open-label trials, while the remaining studies were double-blinded. Joo et al 22 included four arms (without a placebo group), and Tergau et al 29 had three arms (two experimental and one control). Wang et al 24 and Hu et al 35 used drug treatment as the control, whereas the other studies employed a sham control group. For further details, please refer to online supplemental 3.

Meta-analysis results

Characteristics of the included studies

Across the eight included trials, samples came from the USA (Theodore et al),23 South Korea (Joo et al),22 Italy (Cantello et al),15 China (Wang et al 24 and Sun et al 30), India (Sinha et al 33 and Hu et al)35 and Slovakia (Melus et al).25 All enrolled adults with drug-resistant focal epilepsy and used low-frequency rTMS (0.5–1 Hz) over short treatment courses (5–14 days). Targeting was individualised to the epileptogenic zone in seven studies. Sun et al 30 delivered 0.5 Hz stimulation at 90% versus 20% rMT, with 500 pulses, three times per day for 2 weeks. Wang et al 24 applied 1 Hz stimulation once daily for 7 days over the temporal focus localised by dipole source analysis. Joo et al 22 delivered 0.5 Hz stimulation at 100% rMT with 3000 and 1500 pulses per day for 5 days. Hu et al 35 delivered 0.5 Hz stimulation at 90% rMT with 1200 pulses per day for 1 week, compared with drug treatment alone in the control group. Melus et al 25 delivered 0.5 Hz stimulation at 90% rMT with 3000 pulses per day for 5 days, compared with sham stimulation in the control group. For patients whose epileptic focus was non-localised or multifocal, Joo et al 22 applied stimulation over the central vertex. Sinha et al 33 delivered 0.5 Hz stimulation at 110% rMT, with 1200 pulses per day for 10 days. Theodore et al 23 delivered 1 Hz stimulation at 120% rMT, with 900 pulses per session for 15 min two times a day over five consecutive days. Cantello et al 15 applied 0.3 Hz stimulation at 100% rMT, delivering 1000 pulses per day for five consecutive days at the vertex (online supplemental 4).

Effect of rTMS on seizure frequency

A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the effect of low-frequency rTMS on seizure frequency in patients with epilepsy. The analysis included data from eight studies, comparing the rTMS group to the control group. Descriptively, the combined mean number of seizures was 6.1 in the intervention groups and 7.4 in the control groups. SDs were reported in the original studies but could not be pooled; for study-specific SDs, refer to figure 1.

The meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant reduction in seizure frequency favouring the rTMS group. The pooled SMD was −0.46 (95% CI −0.57 to −0.34]), with a Z-score of −7.67 (p=0.001).

Heterogeneity among the studies was negligible (τ2=0.001, I2=0.1%), indicating consistent findings across the included trials. The largest contribution to this overall effect came from the study by Hu et al.35 Overall, these results suggest that the intervention has a modest but significant effect in reducing seizure frequency (figure 1).

Timeframe of outcome measurement and stimulation site

The pooled analysis showed a significant short-term reduction in weekly seizure frequency. However, all eight included trials reported outcomes over relatively short follow-up periods: The follow-up period was 8 weeks for Theodore et al 23 and Joo et al,22 6 weeks for Cantello et al,15 30 days for Wang et al,24 10 weeks for Sun et al,30 8 weeks for Sinha et al,33 4 weeks (1 month) for Hu et al 35 and 4 weeks after each treatment phase for Melus et al.25

Regarding the stimulation site, notably, studies targeting the epileptogenic zone generally report stronger short-term effects than those using non-specific stimulation. For instance, the highly weighted study by Hu et al,35 which used focal stimulation, reported a statistically significant mean reduction in seizure frequency (mean difference=−0.46; 95% CI −0.60 to −0.32). Conversely, the study by Cantello et al,15 which involved a less specific stimulation protocol, showed a negligible and statistically non-significant effect (mean difference=−0.10; 95% CI −0.88 to 0.68). The studies employing precise neuronavigation (eg, Melus et al)25 also suggest a clinically larger effect (mean difference=−6.66), further supporting the superiority of targeted stimulation. These results indicate that the observed benefits reflect short-term efficacy and suggest that precise targeting of the epileptogenic zone may enhance rTMS effect, while the durability of the effect beyond 10 weeks remains unknown.

Discussion

rTMS, which inhibits neuronal activity in the brain, is a potential treatment for epilepsy36; however, RCTs have yielded inconsistent results. A systematic review of 14 articles10 15 16 22–25 29 30 32–35 found that rTMS significantly reduces seizure frequency (Z = −7.67, p=0.001) in a diverse population of patients with epilepsy, including those with drug-resistant epilepsy, although the data used in previous meta-analyses were not directly available from the original publications. It should be noted that the data used in previous meta-analyses were not always directly available from the original publications. Among the 14 selected clinical trials, eight studies (involving 367 patients) with similar reporting methods were included in the meta-analysis.

The meta-analysis indicates a significant reduction in weekly seizure frequency following low-frequency rTMS; however, all eight included studies reported outcomes over relatively short follow-up periods, lasting up to 10 weeks. Regarding stimulation site, seven studies targeted the epileptogenic focus, whereas Cantello et al 15 applied stimulation to non-specific cortical areas. Notably, studies targeting the epileptogenic zone generally reported stronger short-term effects than those using non-specific stimulation. These findings suggest that the observed benefits reflect short-term efficacy, and the durability of the effect beyond 10 weeks remains unknown. Careful evaluation of long-term outcomes is therefore essential before considering clinical implementation.

In the study by Tergau et al,29 the number of participants in both the intervention and sham groups was not reported. Furthermore, the studies by Fregni et al,10 Vinod et al 34 and Tergau et al 29 did not fully report data before and after the intervention, rendering them ineligible for evaluation in the meta-analysis.

The included studies employed diverse methodologies and lacked sufficient design details for quality and bias assessment. Six studies10 15 23 31 33 34 included a placebo control group, while others compared the use of focal versus non-focal rTMS, rTMS applied at different intensities or durations, or the use of different coil types. Even among the placebo-controlled studies, there was considerable variation in frequency (0.3, 0.5 or 1 Hz), intervals, duration (ranging from 500 to 3000 pulses) and rTMS intensity (20–120% of rMT). Most trials administered rTMS once daily for 5–10 days, with the exception of Sun et al’s30 study, which lasted 14 days.

Seynaeve et al 16 concluded that rTMS was ineffective; however, their analysis was incomplete and potentially misleading due to a lack of comprehensive data analysis and pairwise comparisons. Two studies demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in seizures compared with baseline.10 30 However, these results should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of studies, small sample sizes and variations in methodology and design.

Previous systematic reviews, such as the study by Hsu et al in Taiwan, have demonstrated a small but significant effect of low-frequency rTMS on drug-resistant epilepsy.37 Additionally, Cooper et al 17 conducted a systematic review in the USA, analysing participant data, and found a significant reduction in seizure frequency following treatment with low-frequency rTMS.17 However, these previous systematic reviews included observational non-randomised cohort studies in which all participants received the intervention. Furthermore, in a systematic review conducted in China in 2024 by Wang et al,13 positive results regarding the efficacy of low-frequency rTMS on seizure reduction were reported.13 This review encompassed both human and non-human samples. The current research team was also unable to locate all the studies included in this review.

Although the pooled analysis showed a significant short-term reduction in seizure frequency following low-frequency rTMS, the clinical implications of these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The absence of significant publication bias (Egger’s test, p=0.086) increases confidence in the robustness of the results; however, the small number of included trials and the limited follow-up duration restrict the generalisability of the findings. Most studies focused on short-term efficacy, and none assessed sustained seizure control or cognitive outcomes beyond 10 weeks. Moreover, variability in stimulation parameters—including frequency, pulse number and coil positioning—highlights the need for protocol standardisation in future research.

Another important consideration is that while the pooled mean difference was statistically significant, the magnitude of change (mean difference = −0.46) may represent a modest clinical effect, which could vary depending on individual differences in cortical excitability and the precision of target localisation. The stronger effects observed in studies employing neuronavigated or individualised targeting support the notion that rTMS efficacy may depend on accurate identification of the epileptogenic zone. Future trials with larger, more homogeneous samples and standardised treatment protocols are needed to confirm these preliminary findings and to explore optimal stimulation parameters for durable seizure control.

Finally, given the non-invasive nature and safety profile of rTMS, its role may be best conceptualised as an adjunctive therapy rather than a stand-alone treatment for drug-resistant epilepsy. Integration of rTMS with pharmacological management or neurofeedback-based interventions could enhance treatment outcomes while minimising seizure recurrence.

Conclusions and future perspectives

This review focuses on RCTs comparing rTMS with control groups. While low-frequency rTMS shows promise in reducing seizure frequency, its efficacy may vary depending on the type of epilepsy and individual patient characteristics. Further research is necessary to fully understand its cognitive effects and to optimise its application in the treatment of epilepsy.

Limitations

This study was constrained by the limited number of eligible trials. Among the 14 selected clinical studies, only 8 with comparable reporting methods provided sufficient data for inclusion in the meta-analysis. The remaining trials were excluded because key information required for quantitative synthesis—such as group-specific sample sizes, means and SDs—was either incompletely reported or presented in non-standardised formats (eg, percentages, p values). Notably, some data cited in previous meta-analyses could not be located in the original publications, and attempts to obtain missing information from the corresponding authors were unsuccessful.

The small number of included studies and limited sample sizes also precluded meaningful subgroup or sensitivity analyses, such as comparing effects based on stimulation site (focal vs non-focal), epilepsy type or treatment parameters. Therefore, while the current findings indicate overall short-term efficacy, they do not allow robust conclusions regarding effect modifiers or the stability of results under different assumptions.

Future research should focus on longer intervention durations and follow-up periods to evaluate long-term effectiveness and potential side effects. Standardised reporting using established checklists and adequately powered sample sizes is essential. Updated meta-analyses should be conducted as additional data become available to enable more informative subgroup and sensitivity analyses.
