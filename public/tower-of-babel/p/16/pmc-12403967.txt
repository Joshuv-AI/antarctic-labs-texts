Late request for safe abortion and its determinants among reproductive age women in Ethiopia: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tirusew Nigussie Kebede, Kidist Ayalew Abebe, Tebabere Moltot Kitaw, Moges Sisay Chekole, Assalif Beyene Haile, Toyba Ebrahim Yesuf, Mesfin Tadese, Mekuanint Terefe Kassa, Mulualem Silesh Zerihun, Birhan Tsegaw Taye, Tadesse Awoke Ayele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLate requests for safe abortion during the second trimester are strongly associated with severe maternal complications, including infertility, preterm birth, low birth weight, and maternal mortality, especially in developing countries. These delays also contribute to adverse pregnancy outcomes beyond direct maternal deaths. Despite these serious concerns, data on the prevalence and contributing factors of late abortion requests in Ethiopia remain limited.ObjectiveTo estimate the pooled prevalence of late requests for safe abortion and identify the determinants among Ethiopian women of reproductive age.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive search was performed across major international databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Hinari, Scopus, Web of Science, and African Journals Online, using the CoCoPop strategy. Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated with the I2 statistic, and publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test. A random-effects model was employed to estimate the pooled prevalence and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsTen studies involving a total of 5,559 women were included. The pooled prevalence of late requests for safe abortion was 35.20% (95% CI: 26.21, 44.19). Significant determinants identified were delayed pregnancy diagnosis (AOR: 3.23; 95% CI: 2.52, 4.13), irregular menstrual cycles (AOR: 2.36; 95% CI: 1.86, 3.00), rural residence (AOR: 2.89; 95% CI: 2.34, 3.58), unmarried status (AOR: 2.86; 95% CI: 2.13, 3.86), and age under 19 years (AOR: 4.01; 95% CI: 2.41, 6.67).ConclusionLate requests for safe abortion are common in Ethiopia and are influenced by factors such as rural residence, young age, delayed recognition of pregnancy, and irregular menstrual cycles. Enhancing access to reproductive health services especially for rural and adolescent populations is essential to reduce maternal complications and promote timely care.Prospero registration numberCRD42024603467.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02559-3.

Introduction

Each year, approximately 73 million induced abortions occur worldwide, representing a significant aspect of global reproductive health [1]. Abortion is a common healthcare intervention, with 61% of all unintended pregnancies and 29% of all pregnancies resulting in induced abortion [2]. Globally, 45% of all induced abortions are unsafe; Among these, one-third are carried out under the most dangerous conditions, often by untrained individuals using hazardous and invasive methods [3]. Unsafe abortion is a significant, preventable cause of maternal deaths and morbidities, leading to physical and mental health complications as well as social and financial burdens for women, communities, and healthcare systems [4]. Worldwide, 4.7% to 13% of maternal deaths are related to abortion, with the majority of these deaths resulting from unsafe abortions [5].

In Sub-Saharan Africa, around 2 million pregnancies end in abortion each year, 77% of which are unsafe. The region has the highest abortion-related mortality rate globally, with 185 deaths per 100,000 abortions, resulting in approximately 15,000 preventable deaths annually [6]. Abortion can be safely and effectively managed by a variety of healthcare providers using either medication or surgical procedures, both of which can be administered at the primary care level [1]. Approximately 10–15% of all abortions worldwide are performed during the second trimester of pregnancy [7], which is highly related with higher risks of severe maternal outcomes [8]. Additionally, second-trimester abortions are associated with an increased risk of very preterm birth and low birth weight in future pregnancies [9]. Approximately 23% of women undergoing second-trimester abortions experience severe maternal complications [10, 11].

The lack of access to safe, affordable, timely, and respectful abortion care, combined with the stigma surrounding abortion, poses serious risks to women's physical and mental health throughout their lives. This often leads to delayed requests for induced abortion and increases the likelihood of unsafe procedures, amplifying the associated fatal risks [1]. Many Sub-Saharan African countries, including Ethiopia, have defined legal grounds for safe or induced abortion within their national reproductive health laws to prevent delays in seeking abortion care and to reduce the mortality and morbidity related with unsafe abortions [12].

In 2005, Ethiopia made a pivotal move to reduce maternal mortality and morbidity by liberalizing its abortion law, positioning itself as one of the early advocates in Africa for expanding access to safe abortion services [13]. The Ethiopian Parliament revised the penal code to permit abortion in cases of rape or incest, when the woman has physical or mental disabilities, when necessary to protect the woman’s life or physical health, or if the woman is a minor who is physically or mentally unprepared for childbirth [14]. Despite these reforms, approximately 3.2% of maternal deaths in Ethiopia are still attributed to abortion-related complications, and around 35.6% of severe maternal morbidity is linked to abortion-related issues, with second-trimester abortions contributing the largest share [15, 16]. More than 33% of women experiencing abortion complications were beyond their first trimester of pregnancy. Additionally, women residing in rural areas were more likely to seek care following a second-trimester abortion compared to their urban counterparts [17]. In Ethiopia, the incidence of late requests or delayed decisions in seeking safe abortion services remains unexpectedly high. Many women face preventable complications due to seeking care later in pregnancy. Delayed access to safe abortion is often driven by factors such as limited awareness of legal rights, stigma, lack of access to healthcare facilities, and financial constraints [17–20].

In Ethiopia, timely access to safe abortion services are influenced by several factors. The healthcare infrastructure, particularly in rural and underserved areas, often lacks adequately equipped facilities and trained healthcare providers, limiting timely abortion care [21, 22]. Additionally, sociocultural stigma around abortion and low awareness about legal provisions delay care-seeking behavior [22]. Demographic factors such as rural residence, low educational status, and limited autonomy further contribute to late presentation for abortion services [23].

In Ethiopia, numerous individual studies have been conducted across various regions to examine the prevalence and determinants of late requests for safe abortion. However, these studies have reported inconsistent and regionally variable findings, making it challenging to draw comprehensive conclusions at the national level. To address this gap and provide robust, evidence-based insights to inform policy and practice, this systematic review was undertaken. The primary objective is to synthesize existing data to estimate the pooled prevalence of late requests for safe abortion and to identify the major contributing factors among Ethiopian women.

Methods

Systematic review registration, data sources and search strategies

This systematic review was conducted to estimate the pooled prevalence of late requests for safe abortion and to identify its determinants among Ethiopian women of reproductive age. The protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42024603467 (2024). A comprehensive search was performed across major international databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Hinari, Scopus, Web of Science, and African Journals Online. The Condition, Context, Population (CoCoPop) search strategy was applied, with the condition defined as late requests for safe abortion, the context as Ethiopia, and the population as women of reproductive age [24]. The primary outcome of interest was the prevalence of late requests for safe abortion among pregnant women in Ethiopia, while the secondary outcomes were the determinants of such late requests. The research question guiding this review was: What is the magnitude of late requests for safe abortion among reproductive-age women in Ethiopia, and what are the key determinants? For each CoCoPop component, electronic databases were searched using relevant keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, combined with the Boolean operators “OR” and “AND” (see Supplementary Material S1).

Eligibility criteria and study selection

This systematic review included studies conducted in Ethiopia that reported the prevalence, magnitude, determinants, associated factors, or predictors of late requests for safe abortion among women of reproductive age. All relevant studies published in English up to the end of the search period (from September 1, 2024, to November 17, 2024) were included in this review. Studies that did not report on the outcome of interest were excluded. All identified articles from the databases and search engines were imported into EndNote 20 reference management software. This software was used to efficiently manage the large volume of references, automatically identify and remove duplicate records, and facilitate the initial screening based on titles and abstracts. The study selection process began with an initial screening of titles and abstracts. Articles deemed potentially eligible then underwent full-text screening. Three independent reviewers (TNK, BTT, and TMK) conducted the screening to ensure accuracy and reduce bias. Any disagreements among reviewers were resolved through discussion. Articles that met the eligibility criteria were included in the final review.

Quality assessment and data abstraction

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 checklist. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality assessment tool was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies. Specifically, the JBI critical appraisal checklist for analytical cross-sectional studies contains eight criteria, while the checklist for case–control studies includes ten criteria, all designed to assess the methodological rigor and overall quality of the studies [25, 26]. Each criterion offers four response options:"yes,""no,""unclear,"and"not applicable."The maximum quality score was 8 points for analytical cross-sectional studies and 10 points for case–control studies, based on the number of “yes” responses. Studies were considered good quality if they met most of the JBI criteria with minimal risk of bias, confounding, or other methodological concerns [27, 28].

To ensure the validity of the systematic review findings, only primary studies classified as good quality were included (see Supplementary Material S2). Three reviewers (TNK, TMK, and BTT) independently extracted relevant data from the selected articles using a standardized data extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel. The extracted data included the primary author, publication year, study design, study setting, study period, sample size, prevalence or magnitude of late requests for safe abortion, and selected determinants of late requests for safe abortion (crude odds ratios with confidence intervals) as reported in the original studies. The extracted data were then exported to STATA version 17 for analysis.

Publication bias and, statistical analysis

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered indicative of significant publication bias [29, 30]. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using the I2 statistic, with a p-value less than 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity. Due to the presence of heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied to estimate the pooled effect size. The effects of selected determinant variables were analyzed using separate meta-analyses for each factor. Results were presented using forest plots, showing odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Subgroup analyses were conducted based on study settings and publication years; however, these did not resolve the observed heterogeneity. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate whether pooled prevalence estimates were influenced by any individual studies.

Result

Study identification and characteristics of included studies

This systematic review and meta-analysis included published studies on late requests for safe abortion and/or their determinants in Ethiopia, identified through searches of international electronic databases. A total of 284 published articles were initially found. Of these, 106 duplicate records were removed, and 157 articles were excluded after screening titles and abstracts. Twenty-one full-text articles were then assessed based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eleven studies were excluded because they did not address the primary outcomes of interest or did not use an appropriate operational definition for late requests. For example, some studies defined a late request as seeking abortion more than one week after pregnancy diagnosis, regardless of gestational age at the time of the request (reason 1). Others investigated the magnitude and/or determinants of safe abortion in general without specifying gestational age, which was not aligned with the objectives of this review (reason 2). Ultimately, ten studies (nine cross-sectional and one case–control) met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final quantitative meta-analysis (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart of study selection for systematic and metanalysis on late request for safe abortion and its determinants among reproductive age women in Ethiopia

Characteristics of the included studies

Among the included studies, eight were cross-sectional and one was a case–control study. All the cross-sectional studies reported both the prevalence and determinants of late requests for safe abortion, while the case–control study focused solely on the determinants. The review encompassed a total of 5,559 reproductive-age women seeking safe abortion services. The largest sample size (2,198) was from a study based on the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey 2016 (EDHS 2016), while the smallest sample (246) came from a study conducted in Addis Ababa. The ten included studies were conducted across five regions of Ethiopia, in addition to the EDHS 2016 data. Specifically, four studies were from the Amhara region [31–34], one from Oromia region [35], one from Southern Nations Nationalities and Peoples region (SNNPR) [36], two from Addis Ababa [37, 38], and the remained one was from Harari [39] (Table 1). Magnitudes of late request for safe abortion

The meta-analysis of seven studies revealed that the pooled prevalence of late requests for safe abortion in Ethiopia was 35.20% (95% CI: 26.21, 44.19). A random-effects model was applied due to significant heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 98%, p < 0.001). This high level of heterogeneity suggests substantial variability in effect sizes across the included studies, which is unlikely to be due to random chance alone. Instead, it likely reflects real differences in study characteristics such as variations in population demographics, study settings, or methodologies that contribute to the observed inconsistencies (see Fig. 2).Fig. 2The pooled prevalence of late request for safe abortion in Ethiopia

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was performed to examine potential variations in the magnitude of late requests for safe abortion based on the year of publication (categorized as up to 2022 and 2023–2025) and the study area. However, this analysis did not lead to a meaningful reduction in heterogeneity. In some regions, only a single study was available, and substantial heterogeneity remained both within and between subgroups. Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis indicated that no single study exerted an undue influence on the overall estimate of late requests for safe abortion among Ethiopian women. The pooled estimate remained stable, ranging from 31.60% to 37.39%, depending on which study was omitted. While studies such as Murad et al. (2021) and Amlaku et al. (2015) slightly increased the overall estimate, others like Abrham et al. (2021), Taju et al. (2024), and Kidus et al. (2021) slightly decreased it. However, none of these variations had a significant impact on the overall effect size. Publication bias

A funnel plot and Egger’s test were used to assess the presence of publication bias. The funnel plot appeared symmetrical, suggesting no evident publication bias. This visual observation was supported by the results of Egger’s test. The slope coefficient (18.75, p = 0.154) reflects the relationship between the standard error and the effect size across studies, indicating a positive but statistically non-significant association. Similarly, the bias coefficient (6.36, p = 0.373) does not significantly differ from zero, reinforcing the conclusion that there is no substantial publication bias (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Funnel plot assessing publication bias for late request for safe abortion in Ethiopia

Determinants of late request for safe abortion

The meta-analysis identified five key determinants that are significantly associated with delayed requests for safe abortion services. These include residing in rural areas, being under the age of 19, marital status, having an irregular menstrual cycle, and experiencing a delayed recognition of pregnancy. Each of these factors demonstrates a strong statistical association with late decision-making in seeking safe abortion care. Collectively, they underscore the multifaceted barriers women face in accessing timely and appropriate abortion services. These findings highlight the critical need to strengthen reproductive health education and enhance access to comprehensive reproductive health services, particularly among vulnerable populations.

Delayed diagnosis for pregnancy

Women who experienced a delay in recognizing their pregnancy were found to be over three times more likely to request a safe abortion at a later gestational age compared to those who identified their pregnancy earlier (AOR: 3.23; 95% CI: 2.52–4.13). This finding underscores the critical importance of improving access to reproductive health services, particularly timely and accurate pregnancy detection methods. It highlights the need for targeted educational interventions to raise awareness about the early signs of pregnancy and to promote the utilization of available diagnostic resources. Strengthening these measures is essential to facilitate timely decision-making and access to safe abortion services (Fig. 6).Fig. 6The relationship between delayed in diagnosis of pregnancy and late request for safe abortion

Irregular menstrual cycle

Women with irregular menstrual cycles were nearly twice as likely to seek safe abortion services at a later stage compared to those with regular cycles (AOR: 2.36; 95% CI: 1.86–3.00). This association indicates the need for targeted reproductive health interventions for women with irregular menstruation, including access to effective family planning methods and comprehensive education on the increased risk of unintended pregnancy. Furthermore, enhanced awareness regarding the early signs and symptoms of pregnancy is essential to support timely recognition and reduce delays in seeking abortion services (Fig. 7).Fig. 7The relation between irregular menstrual cycle and late request for safe abortion in Ethiopia

Marital status

Unmarried women were nearly three times more likely to request a safe abortion at a later gestational age compared to their married counterparts (AOR: 2.86; 95% CI: 2.13–3.86). This finding highlights the necessity of promoting awareness around the consistent use of family planning methods and improving knowledge of the early signs and symptoms of pregnancy. Residence of the women

Women residing in rural areas were nearly three times more likely to seek safe abortion services at a later gestational age compared to those living in urban settings (AOR: 2.89; 95% CI: 2.34–3.58). This finding underscores significant disparities in access to reproductive health services between rural and urban populations in Ethiopia. It highlights the urgent need to improve the availability, accessibility, and quality of reproductive healthcare in rural communities to ensure timely access to safe abortion services and reduce associated delays (Fig. 9).Fig. 9The relation between rural residence and late request for safe abortion in Ethiopia

Age of the women

Women aged 19 years or younger were found to be four times more likely to seek safe abortion services at a later gestational age compared to those older than 19 years (AOR: 4.01; 95% CI: 2.41–6.67). As this age group commonly includes adolescents who are still in school, the finding emphasizes the critical need to enhance access to adolescent-friendly reproductive health services and counseling. Strengthening such services can play a vital role in reducing the incidence of unintended pregnancies, decreasing the likelihood of delayed abortion requests, and minimizing procedure-related complications among this vulnerable population (Fig. 10).Fig. 10The relation between the age of the women and late request for safe abortion

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the pooled prevalence of late requests for safe abortion in Ethiopia to be 35.20% (95% CI: 26.21–44.19). This finding is consistent with reports from other regions,, including, in Malawi (32%) [44], Kenya (39%) [45], and Chicago (32%) [46]. The alignment of results across these diverse settings suggests that delayed requests for safe abortion may represent a widespread concern in both African and Western contexts. This underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions aimed at reducing the associated maternal morbidities and improving timely access to comprehensive reproductive health services [47].

However, the findings of this review indicate a substantially higher prevalence of second-trimester abortion requests in Ethiopia compared to global estimates, which range from 10 to 15% [7]. The rate also exceeds those reported in several countries, including Canada (10.1%) [48], England (10%) [49], Netherland (6.6%) [50], Texas (14.5%) [51], and India (23%) [52]. This disparity may be explained by differences in population demographics, healthcare infrastructure, and access to reproductive health services. In Ethiopia, access to such services remains particularly limited especially in rural areas, where over 80% of the population resides [53, 54]. Inadequate healthcare infrastructure, insufficient family planning resources, and limited reproductive health education in these regions are likely contributing factors to the elevated prevalence of late abortion requests [55].

This review found that delayed recognition of pregnancy increases the likelihood of late requests for safe abortion by nearly threefold. This finding aligns with studies conducted in England, Massachusetts (USA), and the Netherlands [49, 50, 56]. One plausible explanation is that women who do not identify their pregnancy during the early trimester may consequently seek abortion services at a later gestational age. Delayed pregnancy confirmation whether due to individual circumstances, healthcare system limitations, or sociocultural factors reduces the available time for women to make informed decisions regarding abortion, thereby increasing the probability of late abortion requests [57, 58].

Women with irregular menstrual cycles are twice as likely to request second-trimester induced abortions compared to those with regular cycles, a finding consistent with reports from England [49]. This may be attributed to the fact that women with irregular menstruation are less likely to detect a missed period or associate menstrual irregularities with pregnancy. The variability of their cycles can hinder early recognition of pregnancy symptoms, leading to delayed pregnancy diagnosis and subsequently postponing decisions regarding abortion. Consequently, these women tend to seek abortion services at a later gestational age compared to their counterparts with regular menstrual cycles [59].

This review indicates that women residing in rural areas are nearly three times more likely to request second-trimester induced abortions, a finding consistent with studies conducted in Texas, Australia, and India [44, 46, 56]. This disparity primarily stems from significant barriers rural women face in accessing reproductive health services. In Ethiopia, access to sexual and reproductive health services is particularly limited in rural regions [54]. Consequently, women in these areas encounter multiple challenges, including higher rates of unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and delays in seeking safe abortion care [60–62]. Contributing factors such as inadequate healthcare infrastructure, limited transportation options, and insufficient information regarding reproductive health services exacerbate these delays, thereby increasing the likelihood of late-term abortion requests [63].

This review found that women under the age of 19 are four times more likely to seek second-trimester induced abortions compared to adult women, a finding consistent with studies conducted in the Netherlands and India [50, 52]. Adolescents, particularly those younger than 19, often lack adequate knowledge and resources to recognize pregnancy at an early stage. Additionally, they may face social stigma, fear, or legal barriers that hinder timely decision-making regarding abortion. These factors collectively contribute to delays in seeking abortion services, thereby increasing the likelihood of requesting the procedure during the second trimester [64, 65].

This review found that unmarried women are nearly three times more likely to seek second-trimester induced abortion services compared to their married counterparts, consistent with findings from studies conducted in Kenya and the United States [45, 46]. Unmarried women, particularly adolescents and young adults, frequently encounter barriers such as social stigma, fear of judgment, and limited access to contraception and reproductive health services. These challenges contribute to delayed pregnancy recognition, increased rates of unintended pregnancies, and postponements in seeking abortion care. Furthermore, social and financial pressures may impede timely decision-making, thereby increasing the likelihood of requesting abortions during the second trimester. Marital status significantly influences women’s approaches to pregnancy and abortion; married women may also experience delays, often due to the necessity of involving their partner or family in the decision-making process [66–68].

Limitation of the review

A primary limitation of this systematic review is that it includes only twelve studies conducted across five regions of the country. This limited sample size may affect the representativeness of the findings and constrain the generalizability of the conclusions at the national level. Additionally, the analysis revealed significant heterogeneity, indicating variability among findings from different regions. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, considering possible regional differences and the absence of data from some parts of the country.

Conclusion

The prevalence of late requests for safe abortion in Ethiopia were higher compared to studies conducted in other regions. Key determinants such as rural residence, unmarried marital status, younger age, delayed pregnancy diagnosis, and irregular menstrual cycles significantly contribute to this elevated rate. To effectively address this public health challenge, it is imperative to implement targeted policy interventions that focus on improving access to comprehensive sexual and reproductive health services particularly in underserved rural areas and among adolescents and young adults. Specific strategies should include expanding community-based pregnancy detection programs, enhancing family planning education tailored to women with irregular menstrual cycles, and reducing social stigma through awareness campaigns aimed at unmarried women and youth. Furthermore, strengthening healthcare infrastructure, and integrating adolescent-friendly reproductive health counseling can promote timely decision-making and reduce late-term abortion requests and associated morbidities. Future research should focus on evaluating the effectiveness of these interventions across diverse regional contexts and exploring barriers to timely abortion care to inform adaptive policy responses. Such multi-level approaches will be critical to advancing reproductive health outcomes and reducing preventable complications from delayed abortion care.
