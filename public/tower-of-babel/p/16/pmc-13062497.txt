Patterns of Discharge Analgesic Prescription in Hip Fracture Patients by Label of Cognitive Status: A Retrospective Pilot Study
Authors: Aritra Ray, Sunita Paul, Emma Wong‐She, Anagh Mane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPain relief is known to be under‐prescribed in cognitively impaired patients. Though cognitive impairment (CI) may lead to lower reported pain at times, differences disappear at higher pain levels, where nociception is similar between groups. CI research also exhibits reduced placebo responsiveness in patients. Ensuring equitable pharmacological treatment for pain after hip‐fracture surgery is thus ethically necessary. This pilot study compares discharge analgesic prescribing patterns between patients with and without documentation labels of CI in a metropolitan inpatient orthogeriatrics service.MethodsA retrospective pilot audit conducted on discharge e‐prescriptions from patients aged ≥ 65 years who underwent hip‐fracture repair in the calendar year of 2020. Fifty records with documented CI and fifty with normal cognition (NC) were studied. CI was defined by documentation labels—including dementia, mild CI, or delirium. Because this study examined prescribing behavior, the focus was on a prescriber's perception of CI evidenced by labels. This reflects real‐world clinical practice but risks misclassification bias. Multiple medication‐related outcomes were examined for potential differences between groups for this pilot study. Medication data from electronic discharge prescriptions were converted to standardized equivalents for comparison.ResultsParacetamol was prescribed more frequently on discharge to patients with documented CI than to those with normal cognition (p = 0.02). No other clinically meaningful and/or statistically robust differences in prescribing patterns were observed across analgesic or non‐analgesic medication classes. Given the small sample size and exploratory nature of the study, confidence intervals were wide and overlapping.ConclusionsParacetamol was prescribed more frequently on discharge to patients with documented CI than to those with normal cognition documented. As an exploratory pilot audit, these findings should be interpreted cautiously but may justify larger studies across broader clinical settings and timeframes.Keywords: analgesic, cognitive impairment, document labels, equity, hip fracture, orthogeriatrics, prescription

Introduction

Background

Pain management in older patients with cognitive impairment (CI) is known to be challenging compared with those who are cognitively intact [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. This is clinically important because pain is a risk factor for delirium, and delirium contributes to increased mortality and morbidity [9]. Existing literature consistently reports under‐prescription of analgesia in cognitively impaired populations [1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13].

Mechanistic and clinical studies demonstrate that pain processing varies substantially across cognitive disorders. Individuals with mild CI may endorse higher pain levels due to altered affective reactivity and sensory thresholds [14, 15, 16, 17], whereas patients with advanced Alzheimer's disease may have impaired communication despite preserved central pain pathways [18]. Additional studies highlight heterogeneity in behavioral pain expression, clinician interpretation, and disparities in analgesic delivery [4, 8, 10, 13].

Because clinicians often rely on documentation labels rather than formal cognitive testing when making prescribing decisions, this heterogeneity raises the question of how those labels influence real‐world analgesic prescribing.

This pilot study examines prescribing behavior by reviewing the discharge summaries of 50 patients with and 50 patients without documented CI following hip‐fracture repair. As the aim is to understand how clinicians respond to documentation labels of CI when prescribing analgesia, CI is treated as a single category encompassing dementia, mild CI, and delirium. This reflects real‐world documentation practices and is appropriate for an exploratory study, although it does not allow differentiation between specific cognitive syndromes.

Because CI was identified through routine documentation rather than standardized cognitive testing, generalizability is limited and misclassification is possible.

Aim

To investigate how documentation labels of CI are associated with discharge analgesia prescribing patterns in older adults admitted with hip fracture.

Methods

Study Design

The study was an observational retrospective review of discharge prescriptions for patients with hip fractures discharged from the orthopedic wards of a metropolitan hospital over a 12‐month period. Propensity score matching of key demographic and clinical variables was used to generate comparable groups of patients with normal cognition (NC) and CI. CI was defined as any documentation of dementia, mild CI, or delirium in the ANZHFR dataset or in the hospital discharge summary, reflecting how cognition is recorded in routine clinical practice.

In addition to analgesics, discharge prescription rates of anticholinergic medications and benzodiazepines were examined. These drug classes contribute substantially to sedative burden and polypharmacy in older adults and are frequently co‐prescribed in the context of CI. Their inclusion allows a broader assessment of prescribing behavior at discharge.

Study Population

Patients over 65 years of age and with a hip fracture being discharged between the 1st of January 2020 and the 1st of January 2021 from the orthopedic wards of the metropolitan hospital.

Procedures

General Data Extraction

Data on all patients discharged after hip‐fracture repair between 1 January 2020 and 1 January 2021 were obtained from the ANZ Hip Fracture Registry (ANZHFR). Only patients aged ≥ 65 years were included. Patients were categorized as cognitively impaired (CI) or normal cognition (NC) based on documentation in the discharge summary or ANZHFR dataset.

Discharge summaries without an electronic medication list were excluded, as this typically reflects a paper prescription rather than absence of prescribing. All electronically recorded discharge medication lists—including those listing no analgesics—were included.

Patient Selection and Propensity Scoring

Fifty patients with documented CI were randomly selected from the eligible CI cohort. Propensity scores were then used to identify a matched group of 50 NC patients. Propensity scoring was used to balance frailty and other demographic characteristics that could influence prescribing behavior, particularly given variability in how pre‐existing medications are documented.

Propensity scores (scaled to 100 points) were generated for each patient using the following components: age (50 points), number of discharge medications (20 points), Charlson Comorbidity Index (15 points), number of hospital admissions in the preceding year (15 points).

The 50 NC patients with the closest propensity scores to the CI group were selected as matched controls.

Because the aim of this pilot study was to describe overall prescribing intensity at the cohort level, all patients were included in dose and frequency calculations. Non‐users were assigned values of 0 mg and 0 doses/day, ensuring that averages reflected the total prescribed burden rather than dosing among users only.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared using two‐tailed t‐tests, and categorical variables using chi‐squared tests, with significance set at p < 0.05. Given the exploratory nature of this pilot study and the small sample size, analyzes were intended to be descriptive rather than inferential. Several medication variables demonstrated skewed distributions; however, to maintain consistency with the study's predefined analytic plan, non‐parametric tests were not applied. This limitation is acknowledged, and future larger studies will incorporate medians, interquartile ranges, and non‐parametric methods such as Mann–Whitney U.

Results

A total of 250 patients were discharged from the orthopedic service following hip fracture during the study period. Of these, 234 were aged ≥ 65 years, and 231 were alive at discharge. Among the 231 eligible patients, 138 had documentation of CI and 93 had normal cognition (NC). One patient was excluded due to an absent electronic discharge medication list. From the remaining cohort, 50 CI patients were randomly selected, and propensity scores were calculated for all 92 NC patients. The 50 NC patients with the closest propensity scores to the CI group were selected as matched controls, resulting in two final groups of 50 patients each for analysis.

The groups were similar in age, sex distribution, number of medications, Charlson Comorbidity Index, ethnicity, and type of hip‐fracture repair. Prior hospitalizations were higher in the NC group (mean 1.46 vs 0.82; p = 0.0499).

Opioid prescribing rates were similar between groups (CI 60% vs NC 56%), indicating that the higher paracetamol prescribing rate in the CI group did not reflect a systematic avoidance of opioids.

No other statistically significant differences were observed across opioid, NSAID, TCA, gabapentinoid, or anticholinergic prescribing. An isolated difference was noted in benzodiazepine dose frequency, which was higher in the CI group; however, total daily benzodiazepine dose did not differ significantly between groups. Pre‐admission regular medications were not available, so it is unclear whether these general prescription patterns reflect pre‐existing regimens or prescribing during the index admission.

Discussion

CI has been associated with lower dosing and less frequent prescription of analgesia in both hospital and community settings in previous studies [1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10]. In particular, an extensive review of the literature carried out in 2017 [3] showed lower amounts of analgesia being prescribed in the cognitively impaired compared to those with normal cognition. This difference is noted across studies spanning multiple settings with very few showing equivalent administration rates [11, 12, 13].

Against this backdrop, the higher rate of paracetamol prescribing observed in the CI group (p = 0.02) in this pilot study contrasts with much of the existing literature. Although the difference is modest, it suggests that prescribing behavior may be influenced by factors not fully captured in prior work.

Several explanations are possible. As highlighted in mechanistic studies, pain processing varies across cognitive disorders. Individuals with mild CI may report higher pain levels due to altered affective reactivity [14, 15, 16], while patients with Alzheimer's disease often have preserved central pain pathways despite impaired communication [18]. Clinicians therefore rely on behavioral cues and documentation labels when assessing pain [4, 8, 10, 13].

It is plausible that the presence of a cognitive‐impairment label could prompt clinicians to prescribe paracetamol more consistently as a “baseline” analgesic, particularly when communication barriers make pain assessment uncertain. This interpretation aligns with qualitative findings describing clinician caution, compensatory prescribing, and attempts to avoid undertreatment in cognitively impaired patients [4, 8].

Opioid prescribing rates were similar between groups, suggesting that the higher paracetamol use in the CI group did not reflect systematic avoidance of opioids. Prior studies have reported both reduced opioid use in CI [5, 7, 11] and no difference after adjusting for injury severity or institutional practices [6, 12]. The lack of difference in this study may reflect local prescribing norms, the matched cohort design, or the limited sample size.

We assumed that hip fractures and their healing would cause near‐equivalent levels of pain regardless of cognition. We think this is a reasonable assumption as the underlying physiology of fractures in cognitively impaired people are not known to be any different from those with normal cognition and is unlikely to be so. Mechanistic studies also show that differences in reporting of pain levels disappear at higher pain levels [14, 15, 16].

The isolated difference in benzodiazepine dose frequency, without a corresponding difference in total daily dose may reflect a greater need for medications needed for anxiety or challenging behaviors in the CI group.

In summary, with the backdrop of pre‐existing literature suggesting that patients with CI get less analgesia less frequently our study suggests that there may be sites that do prescribe simple analgesia more frequently to patients with CI. If the finding found in this pilot study was backed up by evidence in larger studies suggesting that this difference in prescription was true, then it may have implications for a number of presumptions in the literature about CI and pain relief prescription at present.

This pilot study illustrates the need to look into this further and would benefit from further work by an increase in numbers of study participants and also by repeating it in different settings.

Limitations

This pilot study has several limitations. Cognitive status was based on routine documentation rather than standardized assessment, introducing potential misclassification. The sample size was small, limiting statistical power and precluding subgroup analyzes by cognitive‐impairment type. Several medication variables were skewed, and non‐parametric methods were not applied to maintain consistency with the predefined analytic plan. Pre‐admission regular medications were unavailable, limiting interpretation of benzodiazepine and anticholinergic prescribing. Finally, findings reflect a single institution and may not generalize to other settings.

Conclusions and Future Directions

Despite these limitations, the study provides preliminary evidence that prescribing behavior may not uniformly disadvantage patients with CI. The higher rate of paracetamol prescribing suggests that in some settings, clinicians may react to assessment uncertainty by ensuring baseline analgesia. Larger and multi‐site studies incorporating standardized cognitive assessments, detailed pre‐admission medication histories, and non‐parametric analyzes are needed to clarify whether documentation labels influence prescribing and to identify where undertreatment persists.
