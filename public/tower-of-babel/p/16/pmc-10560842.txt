BioIn-Tacto: A compliant multi-modal tactile sensing module for robotic tasks
Authors: Thiago Eustaquio Alves de Oliveira, Vinicius Prado da Fonseca
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present the open-source design and fabrication of a compliant multimodal tactile sensing module. The sensing module design presented here enables robotic end-effectors to sense contact properties like pressure and vibration and estimate a quaternion that represents the deformation due to contact. We elaborated the module’s compliant structure fabrication process to use only 3D printed molds and a vacuum chamber, making it accessible to a broad range of roboticists. The process also allows production batches of up to five modules and easy deployment to robotic hands. The repository associated contains packages that support the Robot Operating System (ROS) to perform data collection and visualization. All hardware Computer-Aided Design (CAD) files and software source codes have been released and can be easily assembled and modified. The sensing module proposed in this paper uses off-the-shelf Microelectromechanical (MEMs) barometers and Magnetic, Angular Rate and Gravity (MARG) systems. Those components may be replaced by alternatives depending on the sensor’s availability, constraints related to the module’s size, or robotic application without prejudice to sensor functionality.Keywords: Tactile sensing, Compliant sensing module, Multimodal bio-inspired sensing, Touch perception, Data fusion, Haptics, Robot interaction

Hardware in context

The development of tactile sensing systems is propelled by the perceptual obstacles that robotic agents face when operating in unstructured settings. These barriers can only be overcome by sensing systems that employ not only vision but also touch sensing capabilities. The advent of collaborative robots designed to perform tasks in cooperation with workers in structured industrial sectors [3] is another scenario that motivates the adoption and development of tactile sensing systems. These sensing systems must enable touch perception at an end-effector level when performing object manipulation and exploration tasks and at a whole-body level when reacting to external stimuli from other agents, unforeseen objects, or other elements of their surroundings.

Autonomous grasp, pick-and-place, assembly of parts, and other dexterous operations can more effectively be accomplished in unstructured environments with the use of end-effectors equipped with tactile sensing systems [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10].

Most solutions in the literature for such systems sense a single contact property resulting from the interaction between a robot part and a surface of an object or the environment. Usually, these solutions consist of thin-film pressure-sensitive tactile arrays placed over rigid robot parts and do not add the compliance necessary for interacting with unknown objects or unstructured settings. If placed over thick compliant layers, these solutions would deform, and the spacial relationship between taxels would be lost, rendering the arrays unable to collect tactile images. If placed under thick flexible layers, these solutions suffer from blurring effects and lose the capacity to generate sharp tactile images.

Other authors present solutions that embed hall-effect sensors in compliant layers of silicone or polyurethane. While these solutions add compliance to the perception system, they only measure the changes in the magnetic field around the transducer. These changes can be ambiguous, either caused by tactile interactions deforming the flexible layer or by non-tactile events like the movement of ferrous materials close to the transducer.

For instance, the authors in [11] and [12] present solutions that rely on computer vision techniques or light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and phototransistor couples. In [11], the authors present a series of sensing devices that employ cameras that record the deformation of a gel-like elastomer coated with a reflective layer illuminated by LEDs. The cameras and LEDs are usually placed under an observation window that is delimited by a transparent material. In [12], the authors present a grid-like printed-circuit board populated with light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and phototransistor pairs; these pairs are covered with a rubber grid of matching dome-like structures. When contacts cause the deformation of such domes, there is a change in the light reflected by the domes and, thus, a change in the resistance of the phototransistor. These solutions can offer high resolution from cameras and array structures that lend themselves to image processing techniques. However, they have limited flexibility and compliance to unexpected contacts due to the need for rigid backings or supports. These rigid supports are necessary to maintain the image plane in the camera case or the relationship among taxels in the light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and phototransistor pair case.

In [1], the authors present the use of MEMs barometers as tactile pressure sensors. These sensors are encased in polyurethane rubber and respond to pressure stimuli on the surface of the rubber. In [13], we used a similar concept to increase sensor sensitivity by proposing a bio-inspired module that adds a compliant structure that guides surface forces to the ventilation window of a MEMs barometer. This sensor serves as a deep pressure sensor, analogous to the Pacinian mechanoreceptor found in the human skin. To estimate the compliant structure vibration and displacement due to contact, we incorporated a MARG system to perform similar functions as those of the Meissner and Merkel mechanoreceptors found in shallow levels of the human skin. The data from the MARG system can also be fused to estimate the orientation of the shallow sensors in space and thus the deformation of the compliant structure. Fig. 1, Fig. 1 show the bio-inspired multimodal tactile sensing module mounted in a robotic probe, and Fig. 1, Fig. 1 show four modules mounted on a parallel gripper.

Tactile sensing modules in a robotic probe and parallel jaw gripper.

We present the hardware and software of our open-source bio-inspired multimodal tactile sensing module. The module can be built at a low price, approximately USD$35.00, using only 3D printed molds, two-part polyurethane rubber, and commercially available PCBs for the MEMs MARG system and barometer. The sensors in these printed circuit boards (PCBs) may be replaced by others that provide the same data: i.e., magnetic field direction and intensity, angular velocity in 3 axes, gravity, and pressure. The module can also be miniaturized with custom PCBs designed to reduce its overall scale. Multiple instances of the module can be assembled on end-effectors’ fingers for in-hand object pose estimation [14] and identification [15]. The CAD files, microcontroller firmware, and Robot Operating System (ROS) [16] support the reproducibility of the module presented in this paper.

Hardware description

The present sensor quantifies touch properties beyond the perceived by most current tactile sensing technologies, such as normal pressures (stress). Our sensor borrows the human skin’s layered structure and mechanoreceptors’ functional relationships to measure micro-vibrations and skin displacement properties. Our tactile module includes a 9-DOF MARG (Magnetic, Angular Rate, and Gravity) “shallow sensor” shown in Fig. 2(a) number 1, a flexible and compliant structure indicated in Fig. 2(a) number 2, and a deep pressure sensor indicated in Fig. 2(a) number 3.

BioIn-Tacto structure and prototype.

The compliant structure connects the shallow and deep sensors (Fig. 2(a)) while performing a role analogous to the receptive field of the Pacinian Corpuscle found in the human skin. The compliant structure also defines the spatial resolution of the deep pressure sensor. In addition, it conducts the forces applied on the surface of the module to the deep pressure sensor. It also allows upper-level sensors of the module to rotate under torque or shear forces on the shallow levels of the sensor.

The following are the list of sensing modalities presented in the sensor:

1.Gravity (Field) Sensor – Accelerometer: The sensor contains an accelerometer to observe how the shallow sensors are oriented with respect to the gravity field. It is also useful to detect light touch and vibrations.

The approach for developing this multi-modal tactile sensor was based on identifying features that are lacking in other sensors. For instance, the module from [17] relies solely on magnetic sensors. It adds a compliant structure but still lacks support for a tactile array and the means to calculate the orientation of the surface in contact with objects, e.g., in twisting motions. Another work presented in [18] proposes a tactile cell that uses accelerometers to calculate the orientation of hexagonal modules.

While attractive, this approach lacks a second source of information invariant to accelerations due to impacts in the early stages of touch. For example, if the orientation of a module is estimated based only on the gravity vector, the estimate will be corrupted when external accelerations are applied to the module. Adding a 3-axis gyroscope, a 3-axis magnetometer, the support for a tactile array, and the compliant structure in the present module mitigate such disadvantages. The data acquisition architecture for the multimodal sensing module is outlined in Fig. 3.

Sensing module system overview.

The sensors from the MEMs MARG system and barometer are connected to the microcontroller unit (MCU) (Teensy ARM Cortex-M7, 600 MHz) through a Two-Wire Interface (TWI). The MCU is connected to a computer running the Robot Operating System (ROS) through a USB-Serial Interface. The MCU is responsible for sampling the sensors and publishing ROS messages containing the sensor data, timestamp, and sequence. The ROS nodes in the computer may receive these messages and forward them to specific tasks.

The sensing module presented here has been utilized in the following robotic tasks:

•Stable grasping control: The authors of [14] present a closed-loop control system for grasping unknown objects from multimodal tactile feedback data. In this system, the data from the micro-vibrations from the angular velocity sensor are fused to the pressure signals to identify grasping status and stability values by a fuzzy observer and also fed to underactuated robotic finger controllers to determine the actions to be taken to achieve and maintain a stable grasp.

•Object pose estimation: In [14], the authors also demonstrate how machine learning methods can utilize the signals from the multimodal tactile sensing modules and from a vision system to learn the relationship between tactile data and in-hand object poses. The paper explains how the data from the tactile modules can be used to establish connections between egocentric and allocentric frames of reference for object orientation.

•Tactile texture recognition: In [19] and [20], the authors present the utilization of the miniaturized multi-modal tactile sensor to classify different tactile textures. The sensor measured the variations of pressure, acceleration, angular velocity, and magnetic flux signals caused by the dynamic contact with 13 commonly used tactile textures. Six different classifiers were evaluated. The Extra Trees classifier performed best, achieving an average accuracy score of 95% and the highest accuracy of 98% using the angular velocity on the X-axis as the main feature. The articles also highlight the importance of considering the direction of exploration and the anisotropy of materials for texture recognition.

Build instructions

The module prototype is fabricated using a 3D printed molding and polyurethane casting process. The process is divided into three steps: (1) 3D printed parts and PCB preparation described in Sections 5.1, 5.2, (2) casting of the deep pressure sensor and compliant structure, detailed in Section 5.3, and (3) casting of the MARG system and bonding to the compliant structure, detailed in Section 5.4.

Operation instructions

The sensor operates connected through the Two-Wire Interface (TWI) of a microcontroller connected to a computer running ROS (Melodic) [16]. The microcontroller runs a ROS node that samples the sensors and publishes messages to the ROS nodes running in the computer through a USB-Serial connection. These messages contain a header with timestamps and sequence fields, a digital pressure signal, and signals from the 9-axis MARG system. Fig. 22 depicts the sensing module connected to the computer running ROS through a Teensy microcontroller and a wiring diagram of the connections.

The microcontroller 3 V supply and ground (GND) power the MARG and pressure sensor. The clock (SLC) and data (SDA) for both the pressure sensor and MARG system and connected to the first TWI (SLC0, SDA0) interface of microcontroller. The microcontroller is connected to the computer running ROS through a USB-Serial interface.

Validation and characterization

The proposed tactile sensing apparatus is modularized to be embedded in the fingers or jaws of robotic end-effectors [14], [15], tactile probes [21], or other robotic parts that may be susceptible to physical interactions in unstructured environments with external objects in planned or unplanned contact events. In terms of pressure, the proposed sensing module was characterized in [13]. The deep pressure sensor presented a linear response from 0.5 gf to 270 gf under compression and linear response from 4gf to 330gf under tension. The pressure was exerted over the module’s shallow sensors and guided to the deep pressure sensor in intercalated events of 10 s of stimulation followed by 10 s of no stimulation. These events represented the deformation caused by displacements incremented by multiples of 0.1 mm. They also demonstrated that the sensing module can rapidly respond to the increasing pressure levels and returns to the baseline signal irrespective of the displacement level. The sensing module was also tested to estimate the angle of deformation caused by contact with inclined surfaces with slopes varying from 2 to 45 degrees with deviations of ＋/−3 degrees. More details about the characterization and validation of the proposed sensing module can be found in [13].

Unlike rigid tactile sensors, the proposed tactile module can conform to contacts with unknown surfaces and measure acceleration, angular velocity, and changes in magnetic field direction and intensity due to physical contact events. Fig. 23 shows the sensing module complying with an inclined plane at an angle of approach not normal to the surface of the module’s shallow sensors.

In the context of the robotic hands assembled onto robotic hands, the data collected by the sensing module has been used in robotic tasks like stable grasping control [14], in-hand object pose estimation, dynamic tactile texture recognition [19], [20], and object recognition [15]. A video of the sensors on the phalanx of underactuated fingers of a hybrid robotic hand can be found on: https://youtu.be/AcfX8dV5Sao. A video displaying the sensing module in a surface estimation task can be found on: https://youtu.be/32RQCK06KM0.

Conclusions

In this paper, we present an open-source compliant multi-modal tactile sensor. The sensing module design presented here enables robotic end-effectors to sense contact properties like pressure and vibration and estimate a quaternion that represents the deformation due to contact. This tactile module is a research platform that can be built at a lower price (approximately $35, using only 3D printing and polyurethane casting) than a commercial sensor that usually lacks features such as deformation representation due to contact. Easily modifiable CAD files for all hardware and software source codes have been released, along with detailed building and operational instructions. The sensing module is sampled by the micro-controller unit (MCU) via USB serial connection to the main computer. The firmware for the microcontroller interface and Robot Operating System (ROS) packages are also provided to support data acquisition and visualization capabilities.

The sensing apparatus proposed in this paper is developed with a modular structure allowing for the connection of multiple modules with multiplexed signals that can be assembled to cover larger contact areas. For example, depending on the researcher’s purpose, it is possible to embed these modules in end-effectors such as two- or three-fingered grippers, anthropomorphic robotic hands, and other robotic surfaces. Those interested in implementing a variety of robot applications using the proposed sensing modules are strongly encouraged to contact the corresponding author to discuss potential collaborations.
