Intermediate Volume Glossectomy Defects Reconstruction: Flap Versus Primary Closure
Authors: Jidapa Pradit, Wisarut Samuckkeethum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare quality‐of‐life (QoL) outcomes between flap reconstruction and primary closure following intermediate‐volume (partial to hemi‐) glossectomy in patients with oral tongue carcinoma.Study DesignRetro‐prospective cross‐sectional study.SettingDepartment of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery, King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand.MethodsSeventy patients undergoing partial to hemi‐glossectomy were included and grouped by reconstruction method (flap reconstruction vs primary closure). QoL was assessed at 6 to 12 months posttreatment using the EORTC QLQ‐H&N35 questionnaire. Multivariable linear regression was performed adjusting for age, stage group, neck dissection, and adjuvant therapy. Effect sizes were calculated to evaluate clinical relevance.ResultsBaseline demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics were comparable between groups. Operative time was significantly longer in the flap group. Flap reconstruction was associated with significantly better QoL, including lower overall HN35 scores (40.89 ± 3.39 vs 44.74 ± 5.26; P < .01), and improved swallowing and speech domains (both P < .01). In multivariable analysis, flap reconstruction remained independently associated with better overall QoL (adjusted β, −5.23; 95% CI, −7.09 to −3.37; P < .001), swallowing (adjusted β, −2.18; P < .001), and speech (adjusted β, −1.65; P < .001). Effect size analysis demonstrated large to very large effects. Wound complications were more frequent in the flap group (14.3% vs 0%; P = .02).ConclusionFlap reconstruction was associated with improved QoL at 6 to 12 months, particularly in swallowing and speech, but with longer operative time and higher wound complication rates. Prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

Tongue carcinoma constitutes a substantial proportion of head and neck cancers globally 1 and presents significant challenges in both treatment and rehabilitation. This malignancy adversely affects not only the physical health of patients but also critical functions such as speech, swallowing, and oral hygiene, which are contingent upon the extent of surgical resection. Surgical intervention for tongue cancer typically results in 3 distinct types of volume defects: small, intermediate, and large.

Small volume defects, defined as those encompassing less than one‐third of the tongue (partial glossectomy), are generally managed effectively through techniques such as primary closure, secondary intention healing, or the application of skin and biological grafts. These methods typically yield satisfactory functional outcomes with minimal intervention. 2 Conversely, larger volume defects, particularly those involving more than half of the mobile tongue (hemi‐glossectomy), necessitate complex reconstructive strategies, often employing flap techniques. Such approaches facilitate the restoration of the tongue's volume and morphology while preserving the motility of the remaining tissues, which is crucial for maintaining essential oral functions. 3

In contrast to the well‐defined strategies for small and large volume defects, the management of intermediate volume glossectomies—defects that fall between partial and hemi‐glossectomy—or type III glossectomy 4 remains less clear. The existing literature reveals limited and conflicting evidence regarding the optimal reconstruction strategy and its impact on functional outcomes, underlining the need for further empirical investigation. A consensus has yet to be reached on whether flap reconstruction or primary closure confers superior benefits for patients with these intermediate defects.

The present study aims to evaluate the optimal reconstructive approach for partial to hemi‐glossectomy defects by comparing quality of life outcomes utilizing the EORTC QLQ‐H&N35 questionnaire. 5 This comparison will be conducted between flap reconstruction and primary closure techniques at 6 to 12 months posttreatment, thereby providing valuable insights for clinical decision‐making in reconstructive surgery.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a retro‐prospective, cross‐sectional design.

Ethical Consideration

The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Chulalongkorn University Faculty of Medicine, Bangkok, Thailand (Approval Reference Number: 0204/66). All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. Confidentiality was assured, and data were anonymized during analysis to protect participant privacy. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any stage without repercussions.

Participants

This study included 70 patients who underwent glossectomy for tongue carcinoma at the Otolaryngology Head and Neck Surgery Department of King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital. Data were collected from September 2023 to August 2024, combining retrospective electronic medical record (EMR) review with prospective patient surveys. Validated quality of life (QoL) questionnaires, specifically the EORTC QLQ‐H&N35, were administered after obtaining written informed consent from all participants.

Surgical Procedures and Reconstruction Strategy

Defects in both cohorts were predominantly located on the lateral oral tongue, with variable extension to the tongue tip. In the primary‐closure group, defects were repaired using a combined vertical and horizontal suturing technique to approximate the residual tongue (Figure 1). Selection of reconstruction modality (pedicled vs free flap) was guided by patient factors and oncologic considerations. Pedicled flap reconstruction—most commonly a submental flap—was preferentially considered for patients aged >50 years and/or those with substantial medical comorbidities or an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 1 to 2. Submental flaps were avoided in patients with level Ia nodal metastasis or level Ib disease with extranodal extension; in these cases, alternative pedicled flaps were used. Free flap reconstruction was generally performed for patients not meeting the above criteria.

Data Collection

The data collection process encompassed both a retrospective review of EMR and prospective surveys. Patients' medical records were examined to obtain demographic information (age, gender, underlying disease, smoking history), clinical characteristics (diagnosis and staging), and treatment details (type of operation, operation date, operative time, and postoperative adjuvant therapy). Furthermore, patients completed the EORTC QLQ‐H&N35 questionnaire, validated in its Thai version, to assess their quality of life (QoL). This questionnaire was administered after the patients had completed their treatment, including any postoperative adjuvant radiation or concurrent chemoradiation.

Questionnaire Score Interpretation

The EORTC QLQ‐H&N35 (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire‐Head and Neck Cancer Module) is a specialized instrument designed to assess the quality of life in patients with head and neck cancer. It is utilized in conjunction with the core EORTC QLQ‐C30 questionnaire, which evaluates general cancer‐related quality of life issues. The QLQ‐H&N35 specifically focuses on symptoms and concerns pertinent to head and neck cancer patients, including pain in the head and neck region, swallowing difficulties, sensory problems (eg, taste and smell), speech difficulties, social eating and social contact issues, sexuality, dental problems, xerostomia, sticky saliva, coughing, and felt ill. This questionnaire consists of 35 items, each of which is rated on a scale, typically ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much). Higher scores on symptom scales indicate a higher level of problems or symptoms. For instance, a higher score on the pain scale indicates more severe pain.

Sample Size

The sample size for this cross‐sectional study was calculated by using the Minimal Important Difference (MID) 5 , 6 of the EORTC QLQ‐H&N35 by the following formula: N=Z2×SD2MID2. The value of Z was determined to be 1.96. The standard deviation was ascertained to be 0.33, and the MID was established as 0.33 SD. Consequently, the calculated N was 35 for each group.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software. The significance level was set at P < .05. Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize patient demographics and baseline characteristics, with mean, median, percentage, standard deviation, and range reported for continuous variables. Independent t‐tests were employed to compare the mean QoL scores between the 2 groups. Chi‐square tests were utilized to assess the association between categorical variables and QoL outcomes.

Result

A total of 70 patients were included. Baseline demographic characteristics—including sex, age, comorbidities, and smoking status—were comparable between groups (Table 1), with a slight male predominance. The mean age was similar between the flap and primary closure groups (58.29 ± 12.49 vs 59.77 ± 16.06 years; P = .67). Clinicopathologic characteristics, including tumor site, AJCC stage, surgical procedure, and adjuvant therapy, were also comparable between groups (all P > .05). In both groups, 9 patients with early‐stage disease (stage I‐II) received adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) due to close surgical margins (<1 mm), without significant between‐group differences. Notably, operative time was significantly longer in the flap group (7.40 ± 2.15 vs 3.08 ± 0.93 hours; P < .01).

Discussion

To our knowledge, few studies have directly compared patient‐reported quality‐of‐life (QoL) outcomes after intermediate‐volume oral tongue resections (partial to hemi‐glossectomy) reconstructed with flap reconstruction versus primary closure within a defined posttreatment interval. Prior studies 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 often included heterogeneous defect sizes and reconstructive approaches, limiting applicability to intermediate defects. In contrast, we focused exclusively on partial to hemi‐glossectomy and evaluated QoL at 6 to 12 months after treatment, a time frame in which outcomes are more likely to reflect surgical and reconstructive effects rather than acute toxicity from adjuvant therapy. 11 , 12

Partial to hemi‐glossectomy can substantially impair QoL, as reductions in tongue bulk and mobility compromise articulation, bolus control, and swallowing efficiency. 13 , 14 Consistent with Riva et al, 15 even resection of 25% to 50% of the mobile tongue may result in clinically meaningful deficits. Despite this, reconstructive strategies for intermediate‐volume defects remain variable, with some surgeons favoring flap reconstruction and others opting for primary closure to reduce operative time. This variability likely reflects the limited high‐quality evidence guiding reconstruction choice in this setting. 16

Although free flap reconstruction is widely used, prior studies suggest comparable functional outcomes between commonly used free and regional flaps. 17 , 18 , 19 Accordingly, and because our primary objective was to compare reconstruction strategies rather than flap subtypes, we analyzed pedicled and free flaps as a single group.

In our cohort, baseline demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics were broadly comparable between groups, and rates of adjuvant therapy were similar, reducing the likelihood that differences in QoL were driven primarily by treatment imbalance. As expected, operative time was significantly longer in the flap group, reflecting the additional technical complexity of flap harvest and inset.

Importantly, flap reconstruction was associated with better postoperative QoL, including improvements in overall EORTC QLQ‐H&N35 scores and key functional domains such as swallowing, speech, and social eating. These findings remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders in multivariable analysis, supporting an independent association between reconstruction type and functional outcomes. The magnitude of effect was substantial, with large to very large effect sizes observed, particularly for swallowing and speech, underscoring the clinical relevance of these differences. These findings are biologically plausible, as restoration of tongue bulk and contour facilitates bolus propulsion and articulation in intermediate defects. However, these functional benefits must be balanced against increased surgical complexity. Flap reconstruction was associated with longer operative time and a higher incidence of wound‐related complications, particularly partial flap necrosis requiring debridement. These complications may reflect patient selection, flap type, and technical factors, and highlight the importance of surgical expertise and careful perioperative management.

The finding that the primary closure group reported better “feeling ill” scores despite worse overall QoL is notable. This apparent paradox may be explained by confounding related to treatment intensity, particularly differences in neck dissection extent. 20 , 21 , 22 The flap group underwent more extensive neck dissection, and this variable was independently associated with worse QoL in multivariable analysis. Therefore, this domain‐specific finding should be interpreted cautiously.

Our results are consistent with prior literature, although findings across studies remain heterogeneous due to differences in defect size, reconstructive approach, and timing of QoL assessment. For example, Canis et al 23 reported potential benefits of free flap reconstruction in pT3 tongue cancer, although differences in QoL domains were not statistically significant at longer follow‐up. Such variability highlights the importance of standardized patient selection and outcome assessment.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the nonrandomized, cross‐sectional design introduces the potential for selection bias and residual confounding. Second, the absence of baseline QoL data limits the ability to assess longitudinal change. Third, the modest sample size restricts statistical power and the number of covariates included in multivariable models. Finally, objective functional measures (eg, swallowing studies, tongue mobility, and speech intelligibility) were not included and would complement patient‐reported outcomes in future studies.

Conclusion

Flap reconstruction for intermediate‐volume (partial to hemi‐) glossectomy defects was associated with clinically meaningful improvements in quality of life at 6 to 12 months, particularly in swallowing and speech, compared with primary closure. These functional benefits were accompanied by longer operative time and higher rates of wound‐related complications. Given the nonrandomized, cross‐sectional design, absence of baseline QoL data, and potential residual confounding—including differences in neck dissection—causal inference remains limited. Prospective longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm these findings and further define optimal reconstructive strategies.
