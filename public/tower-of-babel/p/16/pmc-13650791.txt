Radiographic Screening of Wild and Semi‐Wild Cape Ground Squirrels (Geosciurus inauris) for the Presence of Peri‐Apical Masses Associated With Elodont Incisor Teeth
Authors: Quintin Norval, Adrian Tordiffe, Gerhard Steenkamp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeri‐apical masses such as elodontomas, pseudo‐odontomas, and dental dysplasia have been reported to be associated with the elodont (continuously growing) teeth of rodents. These lesions are predominantly documented in captive animals and are hypothesized to arise from environmental and dietary factors linked to captivity. This cohort study investigates the prevalence of peri‐apical masses in wild and semi‐wild Cape ground squirrels (Geosciurus inauris) and examines associated risk factors. Radiographic screening was conducted on 50 wild squirrels from Kimberley and 50 semi‐wild squirrels from Johannesburg City Park Zoo. Data on dietary habits and incisor teeth trauma were collected and analyzed. Peri‐apical masses were absent in all screened squirrels across both cohorts. Semi‐wild squirrels consumed a low‐fiber, pelleted diet, whereas wild squirrels foraged naturally. Minor incisor teeth trauma was observed, but showed no correlation with peri‐apical mass development. The study's sample size, although statistically powered, may not capture rare conditions such as elodontomas or pseudo‐odontomas. Additionally, the observational design limits causal inferences regarding potential risk factors. Peri‐apical masses appear rare in wild and semi‐wild Cape ground squirrels, contrasting with the higher incidence reported in captive rodents. The findings suggest that a low‐fiber diet did not induce peri‐apical masses associated with elodont incisor teeth in semi‐wild squirrels. Further research, including controlled trials, is recommended to clarify the etiology of elodontomas, pseudo‐odontomas, and dental dysplasia in rodents.Keywords: complex odontoma, dental dysplasia, elodontoma, pseudo‐odontoma, risk factors, rodents

Introduction

Rodents are distinguished by the presence of elodont (continuously growing) incisor teeth (Kuang‐Hsien Hu et al. 2014; Wood 1965). Continuously growing teeth are termed elodont teeth, as they never form true roots and continuously form enamel and dentine by maintaining stem cells to drive odontogenesis (Kertesz 1993; Kuang‐Hsien Hu et al. 2014). Members of the Sciuromorpha and Myomorpha rodent clades typically exhibit partial elodont dentition (i.e., the cheek teeth being anelodont), while those of the Hystricomorpha clade have a tendency towards full elodont dentition (i.e., the incisor and cheek teeth being elodont) (Capello 2010). There is a lot of debate about the nomenclature of masses at the apices of elodont teeth, that is, complex odontomas, elodontomas, dental dysplasia, and pseudo‐odontomas. Pre‐2006, some masses were referred to as complex odontomas and/or dental dysplasia. In an attempt to classify these lesions histopathologically, Boy and Steenkamp (2006) termed complex odontomas as elodontomas (Boy and Steenkamp 2006). They defined elodontomas as hamartomas that originate at the apex of elodont teeth, containing haphazard odontogenic elements of mature cementum, enamel, dentine, and bone (Boy and Steenkamp 2006). Mancinelli and Capello (2016) note that the term “elodontoma” has been used broadly in the literature; however, according to the histological framework of World Health Organization histological classification of tumors of domestic animals, lesions such as complex odontomas (hamartomas), including those affecting elodont teeth, are characterized as nondysplastic but locally expansive, compressive, and often destructive, with potential infiltrative behavior (Mancinelli and Capello 2016; Head 2003). In contrast, Crossley and Aiken (2004) originally described pseudo‐odontomas as primarily compressive and expansile lesions without locally destructive or infiltrative features (Crossley and Aiken 2004).

Most reports of these lesions cite husbandry‐related factors, such as hypovitaminosis A, diets deficient in abrasive material, and acute or chronic dental trauma (Figure 1) as causative factors for these lesions (Bulliot and Mentré 2013; Capello 2010; Thas et al. 2019). However, these associations largely reflect the pathophysiological hypothesis originally proposed by Crossley and Aiken (2004), in which trauma leads to arrested eruption, dentinal folding, apical expansion, and subsequent pseudo‐odontoma formation (Crossley and Aiken 2004). More research is needed to determine whether these two entities are related, that is, if pseudo‐odontoma could be a pre‐odontoma in the progression to elodontoma or if they are separate entities with similar causes (Mancinelli and Capello 2016; Kuijpers et al. 1996).

Proposed pathophysiological pathways of pseudo‐odontomas and elodontomas in elodont teeth. The left (A) pathway represents a multifactorial model of elodontoma development, incorporating genetic, metabolic, and environmental contributors leading to disorganized odontogenic proliferation. The left (B) pathway illustrates the trauma‐associated mechanism of pseudo‐odontoma formation (after Crossley), involving arrested eruption, dentine infolding, and apical expansion. The two entities are presented separately to emphasize their distinct biological behavior and underlying mechanisms.

Osteopetrosis, a bone disorder characterized by abnormal dense bone, has been associated with elodontomas (Ida‐Yonemochi et al. 2002). While not directly linked to captivity, it's a rare genetic mutation that can affect osteoclast function (Ida‐Yonemochi et al. 2002). In osteopetrosis, the osteoclasts are defective, resulting in a diminished periodontal ligament (PDL) space between the apical bud and the surrounding alveolar bone (Philippart et al. 1994). The PDL surrounds teeth and acts as a shock absorber, among other functions (Kuang‐Hsien Hu et al. 2014). Without a PDL, teeth can become ankylosed, or fused to the bone (Diekwisch 2004). The ability of elodont teeth to continuously grow is a result of not activating root formation and preservation of the PDL space (Tummers and Thesleff 2003). The interruption in this area of odontogenesis by failure of maintenance of the PDL space and invading alveolar bone is the recipe for the manifestation of elodontomas (Boy and Steenkamp 2006). This is characterized by daughter germ cells forming within this area, and normal growth in a linear, organized manner is changed to expanding growth in a disorganized manner (Boy and Steenkamp 2006; Ida‐Yonemochi et al. 2002; Schour et al. 1949). The osteopetrosis model explains the pathogenesis of elodontomas and serves to be a model for other posited etiologies such as trauma, Vitamin A deficiency, and low‐fiber content in diet (Figure 1).

Odontogenic masses have been frequently reported in black‐tailed prairie dogs; however, the specific classification of these lesions as “elodontomas” in the literature is inconsistent and reflects evolving terminology rather than uniform diagnostic criteria (Thas et al. 2019). Black‐tailed prairie dogs are primarily grazers with a high intake of grass (Wright and Eshar 2018). Grass contains phytoliths, which are abrasive and help to prevent malocclusion by wearing down the continuously growing incisor teeth (Solounias et al. 2019). Studies have suggested that fiber should comprise approximately 15% of the total daily diet requirements for adult rodents (Bullen 2021). In line with these findings, Thas et al. demonstrated a relationship between odontogenic lesions and low‐fiber diets in pet black‐tailed prairie dogs (Thas et al. 2019). This suggests that a diet deficient in fiber may contribute to the development of odontogenic lesions by increasing the risk of malocclusion and other dental problems (Figure 1).

Acute or chronic trauma can disrupt the root sheath and reduce the PDL space, physically inducing the inclusion of bone elements and disrupting the epithelial tissue (Figure 1) (Broom 1991; Levy 1968; Schour et al. 1949). During the history‐taking of pet rodents diagnosed with pseudo‐odontomas, some authors reported that animals were kept in barred cages (Bulliot and Mentré 2013). A study by Rodriguez‐Ramos Fernandes et al. observed that chewing on metallic tips of water dispensers was a potential source of trauma in the formation of peri‐apical masses in their study (Rodriguez‐Ramos Fernandez et al. 2010). This repetitive behavior, with no obvious purpose, can be observed in animals with limited environmental control, especially social species kept in solitary confinement (Thas et al. 2019). Chronic trauma from repetitive bar‐biting or cage‐biting is a common stereotypical behavior in captive animals, often resulting from frustration, threats, or no stimulation.

Peri‐apical lesions affecting the maxillary incisor teeth often present with signs of upper respiratory difficulty due to compression of adjacent structures; however, given inconsistencies in terminology in the literature, these clinical signs may be associated with both elodontomas and pseudo‐odontomas as space‐occupying lesions (Thas et al. 2019). Rodents are preferential nasal breathers, and the location of the maxillary teeth apices to the nasopharynx leads to grades of obstruction as these masses develop (Harkema and Morgan 1996; Norval et al. 2025). Elodontomas and pseudo‐odontomas at the mandibular incisor teeth present with less severe clinical symptoms (Thas and Garner 2012; Pelizzone et al. 2016). The tentative diagnosis of elodontomas and pseudo‐odontomas is usually confirmed through cranial radiography (Mancinelli and Capello 2016). The radiographic appearance of odontogenic lesions at elodont teeth apices differs between pseudo‐odontomas and elodontomas and should be distinguished. Pseudo‐odontomas are characterized by enlargement and deformation of the apical region, with increased radiopacity of the normally radiolucent apical bud (often described as a V‐shaped area), reduced radiolucency of the pulp cavity, and the presence of characteristic folds of dentine (Pelizzone et al. 2017; Crossley and Aiken 2004; Mancinelli and Capello 2016). These lesions are typically expansile and compressive but remain confined to the periapical region. In contrast, elodontomas present as more irregular, disorganized radiopaque masses arising from the apical bud, often with a heterogeneous internal structure and evidence of local expansion, destruction, and potential infiltration of surrounding tissue (Boy and Steenkamp 2006; Jekl et al. 2008). These features reflect their hamartomatous nature and distinguish them from pseudo‐odontomas, which do not exhibit locally destructive or infiltrative behavior. These masses can affect a single or multiple elodont teeth. A definitive diagnosis requires histological examination of a removed tooth and mass, in the case of complex odontomas, reveals a haphazard arrangement of odontogenic epithelium and stroma with invading bone trabeculae of lamellar bone (Dayan et al. 1994). The enamel is often lost during decalcification for microscopic analysis, appearing as clear spaces (Long et al. 1993). The choice of treatment for odontogenic lesions associated with elodont teeth depends on lesion type, extent of growth, and the degree of involvement of surrounding structures (Smith et al. 2013; Mancinelli and Capello 2016). For pseudo‐odontomas, management is often directed at relieving obstruction and restoring airflow, most commonly through rhinotomy followed by rhinostomy to bypass nasolacrimal or nasal obstruction. In selected cases, extraction of the affected tooth may be considered, although this can be technically challenging and associated with significant morbidity (Phalen et al. 2000; Wagner and Johnson 2001; Mancinelli and Capello 2016). In contrast, treatment of elodontomas may require more aggressive surgical intervention due to their potentially expansive and locally invasive nature. Complete surgical excision, including removal of the associated tooth, is often indicated when feasible, although prognosis depends on lesion size, location, and the extent of adjacent tissue involvement (Jekl et al. 2008; Mancinelli and Capello 2016). Overall, treatment selection should be individualized, balancing clinical severity with procedural risks and expected outcomes.

Present‐day squirrels can be categorized as either tree squirrels, flying squirrels, or ground squirrels based on their habitat preferences and physical adaptations (Nelson et al. 2005). Elodontomas have been documented in South African tree squirrels (Paraxerus cepapi) and a Cape ground squirrel (Boy and Steenkamp 2006; Steenkamp and Elliot 2026). Pseudo‐odontomas have been documented in Richardson's ground squirrels (Urocitellus richardsonii) and in black‐tailed prairie dogs (Cynomys ludovicianus) (Kubiak 2021; Takami and Une 2022; Stein and Carissimi 2008). These findings highlight the occurrence of elodontomas and pseudo‐odontomas across different squirrel taxa. In South Africa, a similar species of ground squirrel, the Cape ground squirrel (Geosciurus inauris), is found naturally in arid regions (Herzig‐Straschil 1978). Recently, the genus Xerus has been reclassified, and the subgenus Geosciurus has been elevated to replace Xerus; therefore, we use the updated genus name throughout this study (Kryštufek et al. 2016; Linchamps et al. 2023). In South Africa, the Cape ground squirrels is found in both wild, arid regions and semi‐wild conditions within the Johannesburg City Park Zoo (JCPZ). In this study, the authors test the hypothesis that elodontomas could be a consequence of captivity‐related factors. Two populations of Cape ground squirrels: a semi‐wild population from the JCPZ and a wild population, were screened radiographically for the presence of mixed radiodense/radiolucent lesions associated with elodont incisor teeth.

Materials and Methods

Ethics approval for this study was provided by the JCPZ Animal Ethics Committee as well as the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Pretoria (REC089‐21).

The semi‐wild cohort of Cape ground squirrels all live on the grounds of the JCPZ in South Africa (JCPZ, Jan Smuts Ave, Parkview, Randburg, South Africa, 2122). The JCPZ Cape ground squirrels are well habituated to humans, dig their burrows next to animal enclosures, and feed from the food set out for the zoo animals. They have minimal to no predation, and the zoo grounds are not arid or semi‐arid. The wild cohort data for the study were obtained on the following four different privately owned farms in the Kimberley district of South Africa: Brakpan farm (Sol Plaatje Local Municipality, ±30 km North of Kimberley), Kookfontein (Riet River area, ±40 km South of Kimberley), Rossland Ranch (Sol Plaatje Local Municipality, ±30 km South of Kimberley) and Langeberg (Sol Plaatje Local Municipality, ± 17 km South of Kimberley).

To detect a significant statistical difference between the two cohorts, the required sample size (n = 100; 50 per cohort) was calculated using a power value of 0.8 and an expected medium effect size (Cohen's d = 0.5). This calculation assumed a significance level of α = 0.05 and was performed using G‐Power software (latest ver. 3.1.9.7; Heinrich‐Heine‐Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany).

Live trapping was done by using stainless steel wire foot‐triggered trap cages (Tomahawk 202, 15 × 15 × 50 cm, Tomahawk Live Trap Inc., Tomahawk, Wisconsin, USA), baited with peanut butter. The traps were set out close to active burrow entrances during the morning when the squirrels are most active. Traps were inspected at hourly intervals, and captured individuals were transferred into pillowcases and transported to be processed away from the burrows.

Anesthesia was induced by a single intramuscular (IM) injection of a mixture of 0.03 mg/kg medetomidine (Domitor—Zoetis, Sandton, South Africa) with 2 mg/kg ketamine (Ketamine—Fresenius Kabi Manufacturing SA (Pty) Ltd, Midrand, South Africa). Anesthetic maintenance was delivered by gas inhalation via a nose cone with a mixture of 0.5% to 3% isoflurane (Piramal Healthcare Isofor, Safeline Pharmaceuticals (Pty) Ltd, Roodepoort, South Africa) in oxygen. After data collection, the squirrels were given a single dose of atipamezole (Antisedan—Zoetis, Sandton, South Africa) IM at five times the medetomidine dose to reverse its effects.

The Cape ground squirrels were tagged using microchip transponders (Virbac Petite Bio Glass FDX‐B Microchips (8 mm), Backhome Virbac, Centurion, South Africa), implanted subcutaneously. A microchip reader was used to check for recaptures (Backhome scanner V800, Backhome Virbac, Centurion, South Africa).

Adult and juvenile males were differentiated based on the presence or absence of fully developed testes, thus classifying juvenile males as nonscrotal and adult males being scrotal. Adult and juvenile females were differentiated by the presence of conspicuous nipples.

Extra‐oral skull radiographs were taken for each Cape ground squirrel by exposing the skull to lateral, dorsoventral, and ventrodorsal projections. A portable X‐ray generator (Port X‐II, Genoray, Republic of Korea) operating at 2.3 mA and 60 kV was used. Reusable phosphor imaging plates (CR sizes: four (5.7 × 7.5 cm) and five (5.7 × 9.4 cm), iM3, Ireland, UK) were developed by a laser scanner (CR7 Vet image plate x‐ray scanner, iM3, Ireland, UK) and images optimized and stored using the Vet Exam software (iM3, Ireland, UK) on a laptop in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. The radiographic images were converted from DICOM to 8‐bit.jpeg images and stored on Google Drive (California, USA). Stored radiographic images were assigned the same serial numbers as the Virbac microchip numbers. Identifying Virbac microchip adhesive markers were used on data sheets to record GPS data of capture sites, head‐tail lengths, weight of animals, and any relevant field notes. All the Cape ground squirrels recovered fully and were released at the point of capture.

The radiographic diagnostic criteria described were used to examine radiographs postcapture for the presence of signs of peri‐apical lesions.

Descriptive statistics were calculated using Excel (Microsoft® Excel® for Microsoft 365 MSO (Version 2405 Build 16.0.17628.20006) 64‐bit, Washington, USA).

Results

Fifty squirrels were examined in each of the two study areas. The field metadata collected from the two cohorts are summarized in Table 1. There were 34 females and 16 males in the JCPZ cohort and 32 females and 18 males in the Kimberley cohort (Table 2). The mean weight of the JCPZ cohort was 708.1 g, and for the Kimberley cohort, 644.5 g (Table 2). Both adult nonpregnant females (p = 0.2946, CI = 95%, t = 1.0602) and males (p = 0.9580, CI = 95%, t = 0.0531) had no statistical difference in their weight between the two cohorts (Table 2). The mean head‐to‐tail (H‐T) and tail (T) lengths were 51.3 and 26.1 cm for the JCPZ cohort, and the mean H‐T and T for the Kimberley cohort were 50.7 and 25.4 cm (Table 2). Squirrel 992003000113127 fractured a lower mandibular incisor tooth in the cage, which was uncomplicated. Finally, squirrel 992003000113121 had a right maxillary incisor tooth abfraction, unrelated to capture.

Discussion

From the two populations studied, one in their natural environment (Kimberley biome) and the other in an urbanized or transformed habitat, no radiographic anomalies were observed (Chapman et al. 2012). These findings add further evidence to the rarity of these lesions in wild rodents, in contrast to the cited occurrence in captive animals (Rodriguez‐Ramos Fernandez et al. 2010; Thas et al. 2019).

The ever‐growing apical bud is susceptible to the induction of disorganized growth in rodents (Jang et al. 2002). Therefore, any factor that interferes with normal cell division could have an influence on its organization (Figure 1). Possible common factors, as suggested by the cited literature, include dietary differences and increased occurrences of incisor tooth trauma in the semi‐wild group.

A diet lacking in roughage could lead to malocclusion in rodents by failing to provide the necessary abrasion to keep their continuously growing incisor teeth at a proper length (Figure 1). Malocclusion could then lead to occlusal trauma because of premature contact during mastication. Some authors suggest that trimming of overgrown incisor teeth can lead to iatrogenic trauma to the incisor teeth if done incorrectly (Losco 1995). The JCPZ cohort consumed a diet sufficient in fiber due to their access to pelleted food (antelope cubes) and mixed vegetables. Based on available nutritional data, these pellets typically contain a fiber content between 20% and 35% and a mix of vegetables 0.5%–2.5%. As a baseline, by using the fiber percentage in the JCPZ cohort diet, they did not exhibit any elodontomas or pseudo‐odontomas. This may suggest that these lesions may only occur when rodents are fed diets significantly lower in fiber than those consumed by the JCPZ group. To confirm this hypothesis, randomized clinical trials should be conducted to investigate the relationship between dietary fiber intake and the development of elodontomas in rodents. Likewise, another dietary factor, Vitamin A deficiency, has been linked to impaired dentin formation, resulting in the invasion of epithelial cords into the pulp (Kuijpers et al. 1996). Studies have suggested that dietary deficiencies of vitamin A may play a role in the formation of complex odontomas (Kuijpers et al. 1996; Orten et al. 1937). Whether Vitamin A deficiency is connected to diet in captivity has not been positively shown. The pelleted food occasionally consumed by the JCPZ cohort contains Vitamin A. It seems that if Vitamin A were a risk factor, it should be lower or absent to induce elodontomas. A supplementary diet of greens and fruits would suffice in the nutritional support for animals in captivity.

Dental trauma, either acute or chronic, has been proposed as a contributing factor in the development of odontogenic lesions affecting elodont teeth, including elodontomas and pseudo‐odontomas (Figure 1) (Ida‐Yonemochi et al. 2002; Capello 2010; Finkel et al. 1979; Thas et al. 2019; Bulliot and Mentré 2013; Rodriguez‐Ramos Fernandez et al. 2010). Incisor tooth injuries were noticed in certain individuals when they tried to escape from live traps in both groups. Although the animals in the JCPZ group are well habituated to the presence of humans, they are still afraid, and erratic behavior was observed in attempts to escape trapping. The confines of the rodents need to be more cramped than the JCPZ animals experience, if trauma is the causative factor to induce these lesions. Control studies need to be done to illustrate that traumatic injuries could be connected to elodontoma or pseudo‐odontoma formation.

One of the limitations of this study was the sample size. Since the authors are unclear about the true incidence of elodontomas and, for that matter, pseudo‐odontomas in captive, semi‐wild, or wild populations of rodents, it is difficult to estimate the sample size. Within the limitations of this study, we can conclude that elodontomas and pseudo‐odontomas are uncommon in wild and semi‐wild Cape ground squirrel populations. However, the possibility of elodontomas or pseudo‐odontomas existing in these populations cannot be entirely ruled out due to potential sampling bias. The observational design of this study, combined with the lack of positive radiological evidence for elodontomas and pseudo‐odontomas, makes it challenging to definitively identify risk factors for these lesions. While the literature suggests that elodontomas or pseudo‐odontomas are more prevalent in captive rodents, further controlled studies are needed to elucidate their etiology and whether they are entities related by progression or are separate entities.
