Nurse‐coordinated multidisciplinary comprehensive heart failure management programme: A propensity‐matched trial
Authors: Cecilia Miu‐Ching Chan, Polly Wai‐Chi Li, Derek Pok‐Him Lee, Esmond Yan‐Hang Fong, Ivy Sin‐Yee Ng, Samantha Ki‐Man Chiu, Clara Woon‐Shan Fok, Frederick Kin‐Wa Li, Shirley Ka‐Wai Lee, Karen K. Y. Ho, Wilson Y. S. Leung, Cathy Cheuk‐Sum Chan, Cindy Yiu‐Ning Tsang, Michael Kang‐Yin Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsDespite therapeutic advancements, the prognosis of heart failure (HF) remains poor, with high rates of mortality and readmission, particularly following a HF exacerbation. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of a nurse‐coordinated multidisciplinary comprehensive HF management programme on HF patients.Methods and resultsThis retrospective cohort study involved patients admitted for acute HF exacerbation at a regional hospital in Hong Kong. We established two patient cohorts: the control cohort, recruited between January and December 2021, received standard care, while the programme cohort, recruited from October 2022 to December 2023, participated in a comprehensive programme. This programme included multidisciplinary ward rounds, early initiation of guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT), discharge education, post‐discharge transitional care and cardiac rehabilitation. The primary outcome was the composite endpoint of all‐cause mortality and HF‐related readmission at 6 months. Secondary endpoints included HF‐related readmission and all‐cause mortality. We also assessed patient satisfaction and health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) in the programme cohort. The study included 732 patients, 24.0% female, 81.6% with HFrEF, mean age of 67.9 ± 13.2 years. After matching for age, sex and type of HF, 366 patients were allocated to each cohort. The programme cohort demonstrated significantly lower rates of the composite endpoint [12.0% vs. 38.0%, adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) = 0.26, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.19–0.37, P < 0.001]) and HF‐related readmissions (10.1% vs. 25.4%, aHR = 0.36, 95% CI = 0.24–0.52, P < 0.001) compared with the control cohort. All‐cause mortality was also significantly reduced (4.4% vs. 18.3%, aHR = 0.22, 95% CI = 0.13–0.38, P < 0.001). Improvements in HRQoL and high patient satisfaction were noted in the programme cohort.ConclusionsThe nurse‐coordinated comprehensive HF management programme significantly reduced readmissions and mortality, with consistent benefits across different subgroups. Further research is needed to confirm these benefits and explore mechanisms.

Introduction

Acute heart failure (HF) exacerbation is the leading cause of unplanned hospital admission in patients older than 65 years. 1 Despite significant advances in therapeutic treatments, the prognosis for HF remains poor, with in‐hospital mortality rates ranging from 4 to 10%. 2 , 3 Additionally, rehospitalization rates are extremely high, particularly during the vulnerable period between the index admission for acute HF and the subsequent 3 months. 4 Up to 31.2% of HF patients are readmitted within 3 months. 5 Hospitalization for HF itself is a strong predictor of mortality, 6 , 7 with 1‐year mortality reported to be between 15 and 30%. 8 HF patients often report poor health‐related quality of life, which is a strong and independent predictor of all‐cause death and HF hospitalization. 9 These patient outcomes underscore the need for intensive management strategies to improve their prognosis and reduce rehospitalization.

The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) recommends early initiation and up‐titration of guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT) when patients with acute HF are hospitalized, as well as early follow‐up within 2 weeks to further optimize the treatment. 10 GDMT, which combines four foundational therapies [angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI)/angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB)/angiotensin or neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), beta‐blocker (BB), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i)], is estimated to reduce the risk of all‐cause mortality by 61% compared with placebo. 11 Continuous optimization of GDMT is equally crucial for the long‐term management of chronic heart failure, ensuring sustained patient stability and improved outcomes. 10 Despite the availability of these effective treatments and evidence supporting early and ongoing titration, under‐prescription and under‐dosing are common, with over 50% of HF patients not receiving target doses due to known reasons that hinder further up‐titration to the target doses, such as patient intolerance. 12

Adherence to these medications is essential for improving prognosis and forms a key component of HF self‐care. 13 Self‐care and patient education are critical elements of HF management, empowering patients to take an active role in their own health. 14 Effective self‐care practices, including daily dry body weight monitoring, adherence to dietary recommendations and medication regimens, and recognizing early symptoms of exacerbation, can significantly reduce the likelihood of hospital readmissions and improve quality of life. 15 , 16 Unfortunately, inadequate self‐care practices among HF patients are widely reported, often resulting from insufficient structured education and support. 17 Patient education is crucial for enhancing treatment adherence, facilitating lifestyle modifications and enabling patients to respond appropriately to potential disease deterioration. 18

Nurse‐coordinated management programmes have emerged as a successful model for integrating self‐care education and support into HF treatment. 19 These programmes leverage the expertise of nurses to coordinate multidisciplinary care, provide continuous education, monitor patient progress and adjust care plans as necessary. 20 , 21 By focusing on personalized care and continuous patient engagement, nurse‐coordinated programmes address the evidence‐practice gaps in HF care and ensure that patients receive the holistic support needed to manage their condition effectively. 20 , 21 In light of this, a nurse‐coordinated multidisciplinary comprehensive HF management programme was established and commenced in a regional hospital in Hong Kong since October 2022. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of the programme on HF readmission rates and mortality, patient satisfaction and health‐related quality of life (HRQoL).

Methods

Study design and population

This was single‐centre, retrospective study conducted to evaluate the established service of the nurse‐coordinated comprehensive multidisciplinary HF management programme, random allocation of patients was not employed. Instead, a retrospective cohort of HF patients was used as the comparison group. Two patient cohorts were established: the programme cohort and control cohort. Patients aged 18 years or older were enrolled in the programme cohort if they were admitted between October 2022 and December 2023 with signs and symptoms of decompensated HF and had a positive NT‐proBNP test result (i.e., >300 pg/mL), regardless of their left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) upon admission. Patients with cognitive impairment, unable to communicate or those who refused management by the HF team were excluded. The control cohort comprised patients hospitalized due to decompensated HF requiring medical therapy, regardless of LVEF, during the period of January to December 2021. Propensity scoring 22 was performed to match patients based on age, sex and types of HF [i.e., heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmEF), and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)].

The nurse‐coordinated comprehensive HF management programme

The design of the programme adhered to the latest clinical guidelines for the treatment of acute and chronic HF published by the European Society of Cardiology in 2021. 10 The programme adopted a holistic approach, encompassing both in‐hospital and post‐discharged care to manage HF from the acute to chronic phase, using a nurse‐coordinated multidisciplinary team. This team included HF nurses, cardiologists, pharmacists, physiotherapists and occupational therapists. The programme comprised several key components: (1) multidisciplinary HF team ward rounds, (2) early initiation and titration of GDMT, (3) early post‐discharge transitional care, and (4) exercise‐based cardiac rehabilitation and occupational therapy rehabilitation.

Upon confirming the diagnosis of HF with NT‐proBNP test and/or echocardiography, the core multidisciplinary HF team members, comprising cardiologists and HF nurses, conducted daily ward rounds. The team determined the level of care and monitoring required based on the degree of haemodynamic stability and severity of HF. Patients with unstable haemodynamics were transferred to the coronary care unit for intensive monitoring and care, such as mechanical circulatory support. Stable patients continued to receive treatment and care in the general medical ward, with the HF team providing regular support for the initiation and up‐titration of GDMT and device therapy, if appropriate. The pharmacist assessed patients every 2 days to up‐titrate the GDMT initiated by the cardiologists and provided medication reconciliation and education. Additionally, in‐patient exercise rehabilitation was provided. The nurse provided comprehensive discharge education, where patients and their families received detailed information about their condition, lifestyle modifications and self‐care strategies. The nurse also provided medication counselling to ensure patients understood their prescribed medications, emphasizing the importance of adherence and potential side effects. The nurse coordinated post‐discharge appointments and arranged continued support through transition clinic services.

For post‐discharge care, patients were scheduled for early follow‐up appointments at the HF transition clinic run by HF nurses and pharmacists. The first clinic visit was arranged within 2 weeks of discharge, according to patients' individual needs. During this visit, the nurse conducted a comprehensive assessment of the patient's vital signs, electrocardiogram, symptoms and volume status, device remote monitoring (if appropriate), laboratory tests, medication tolerance and compliance, and psychosocial status. The nurse titrated GDMT according to a preset protocol and consulted a cardiologist for special circumstances. Patients with specific conditions, such as diuresis resistance or iron deficiency, were arranged to receive aquapheresis and intravenous iron supplementation in the day centre, respectively. For highly symptomatic patients who remained unresponsive to optimal treatment, levosimendan infusion was administered at the day centre with intensive monitoring and care provided by nurses. Individual health counselling on lifestyle modification and HF self‐care continued to be offered, reinforcing the guidance provided during pre‐discharge care. Additionally, the pharmacist continued to provide medication reconciliation during the first visit, ensuring it aligned with the most updated prescribed medications. All patients attending the clinic were given a hotline number for inquiries during office hours. The frequency and number of subsequent HF transition clinic visits were determined based on the patient's progress in terms of symptom status, laboratory results and the need for further drug titration. Patients exited the clinic once their HF symptoms were under control and drug treatment was optimized. Additionally, patients were offered a series of supportive care designed to enhance their physical conditioning and overall well‐being. Nurses played coordinated these care components, ensuring seamless communication and collaboration among allied health personnel. Specifically, the programme included 12 sessions of exercise training, which were supervised by physiotherapists. These sessions aimed to improve cardiovascular fitness, muscle strength and endurance through tailored exercise regimens. The physiotherapists worked closely with patients to ensure that exercises were performed safely and effectively, taking into account each patient's individual capabilities and health status. Furthermore, patients participated in four sessions focused on activities of daily living, lifestyle redesign and counselling, led by occupational therapists. These sessions were designed to empower patients by helping them develop practical skills for managing their daily routines more efficiently, such as providing guidance on optimizing daily activities, assisting patients in adapting tasks such as dressing, grooming and other personal care activities to accommodate any physical limitations they may have. The focus was on enabling patients to maintain their independence and improve their functional capacity in everyday life.

Usual care

Patients in the control cohort received standard care as typically provided by the healthcare system. This care included in‐hospital treatment within general non‐specialty wards and/or cardiac wards, without a structured framework for patient management. Patient education, rehabilitation and post‐discharge medical follow‐ups were unstructured without a standard protocol for referral and service delivery, leading to variability in access and consistency.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the composite of all‐cause mortality and HF‐related readmission at 6 months. Secondary endpoints included 6‐month HF‐related readmission and all‐cause mortality. Patients who died within 6 months post‐discharge were censored on the day of death in the readmission analyses. Information regarding hospital readmissions, causes of readmissions and death were retrieved from patient's medical charts and electronic medical records, and official death documents. HF‐related readmissions were identified based on discharge diagnosis codes consistent with HF, specifically using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐10 diagnosis codes to determine whether a readmission was related to HF. Socio‐demographic characteristics (i.e., age, sex, education level and employment status) and other clinical data (smoking status, types of heart failure, NT‐pro‐BNP and comorbidities) were retrieved from medical record. In addition, the treatment received during the index admission and upon discharge were also extracted for analysis.

The 12‐item Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ‐12) 23 was used to evaluate changes in disease‐specific health status in the programme cohort before attending the first HF transition clinic and after exiting the clinic. KCCQ‐12 comprises four domains: symptom frequency, physical and social limitations, and quality of life impairment due to HF. Scores for each domain are scaled from 0 to 100, with 0 representing the worst and 100 the best possible health status. An overall summary score is calculated by averaging the domain scores. 23 Additionally, a self‐developed survey was used to evaluate their satisfaction after attending the clinic. The survey included six items for patients to rate their level of agreement on a 4‐point Likert scale regarding their satisfaction with waiting time and their understanding of HF and its management. A higher score represents greater satisfaction. Another 11‐point item evaluated their overall satisfaction with the HF clinic, with 0 represents absolutely dissatisfied and 10 represents absolutely satisfied. All patient data were anonymized before analysis. Subject codes were used, and no identifiable information was included in the data file.

Ethical consideration

The study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study was approved by the Central Research Ethics Committee of the Hospital Authority of Hong Kong (reference number: CIRB‐2024‐163‐2). As this was a retrospective study evaluating a newly established service provided by the hospital, informed consent was not sought from patients. Approval for waiving the consent procedure was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Statistical comparisons of the clinical characteristics of the patients at baseline were performed using independent t‐tests, Fisher exact tests and chi‐square tests, as appropriate. The combined endpoints of HF readmission and mortality were plotted using Kaplan–Meier product limit estimates and compared using the log‐rank test. 24 Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate the association between the comprehensive HF management programme and usual care with outcome measures. The following covariate were adjusted in the multivariate models: age, sex, classification of HF, body mass index, lowest haemoglobin level during the index admission. In addition, subgroup analyses were performed by age and sex. Hazard ratios, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P values were computed. Paired‐samples t‐test was used to compare within‐group changes in the pre‐ and post‐programme health‐related quality of life measured by KCCQ‐12. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patients' satisfaction on the programme.

Results

The programme cohort was created by including all patients who were enrolled in the programme since October 2022, resulting in a total of 366 participants. To form the control cohort, we employed propensity score matching based on patients' age, sex and types of HF. This approach allowed us to identify an additional 366 patients who were admitted for HF exacerbation in 2021, prior to the launch of the programme. These matched patients constituted the control cohort, ensuring comparability between the two groups. The mean age of the total sample was 67.9 ± 13.2 years, and the majority being male (76.0%) and admitted for HFrEF (81.6%). The most common comorbidity was coronary artery disease, followed by diabetes mellitus. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of patients at admission in both the programme and control cohorts. The mean age of the programme cohort was 67.1 ± 12.5 years, while the control cohort was 68.6 ± 14.0 years. Male participants comprised 75.7% and 76.2% in the programme and control cohort, respectively. The socio‐demographic and clinical characteristics between the two cohorts were comparable, except for a significant higher percentage of patients in the programme cohort having coronary artery disease (61.7% vs. 48.1%) and atrial fibrillation (39.1 vs. 31.4%) compared to the control cohort. Conversely, the control cohort had a significantly higher proportion of patients with a history of stroke (9.0% vs. 15.3%) than the programme cohort. The median length of hospital stay for the index admission was comparable in both groups.

The programme cohort had a median of 3 visits (IQR: 5) to the transition clinic. Over half of the patients received exercise‐based cardiac rehabilitation supervised by physiotherapists (58.6%) and occupational therapy rehabilitation (57.1%). At the 6‐month follow‐up after the index admission, the percentage of patients receiving up‐titrated GDMT was significantly higher in the programme cohort than the control cohort: ACEI/ARB/ARNI: 80.9% vs. 32.1%, SGLT2i: 59.1% vs. 1.6%, BB: 74.9% vs. 29.3%, and MRA: 74.0% vs. 36.8%). Nearly half of the patients (47.0%) in the programme cohort were prescribed all four foundational therapies. The prescription rate for diuretics was comparable between the two cohorts.

Composite endpoint

Follow‐up data on HF‐related readmissions and all‐cause mortality within 6 months after the index admission were available for all patients. The composite of HF‐related readmission or all‐cause mortality within 6 months occurred in 44 (12.0%) and 139 (38.0) patients in the programme and control cohort, respectively. The Kaplan–Meier survival curve is shown in Figure 1 A . The log‐rank test was significant. In the multivariate Cox regression analysis, which adjusted for differences in baseline characteristics, the composite endpoint was significantly lower in the programme cohort compared with the control cohort (aHR = 0.26, 95% CI = 0.19–0.37, P < 0.001) (Table 2 ). Subgroup analysis indicated that irrespective of age groups, the composite endpoint was significantly lower in the programme cohort as compared to the control cohort, with the risk reduction more prominent among younger patients (age <60 years [aHR = 0.19, 95% CI = 0.08–0.47, P < 0.001]; 60–74 years [aHR = 0.23, 95% CI = 0.13–0.39, P < 0.001]; ≥75 years [aHR = 0.33, 95% CI = 0.20–0.54, P < 0.001]). For the subgroup analysis according to sex, the composite endpoint was significantly lower in the programme cohort than the control cohort for both male and female patients, but the risk reduction was larger in males (aHR = 0.22, 95% CI = 0.15–0.34, P < 0.001) than in females (aHR = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.21–0.72, P = 0.003).

HF‐related readmissions

Of the 732 patients in both cohort, 130 (17.8%) had at least one episode of readmission due to HF within 6 months after the index admission. Among them, 37 (10.1%) and 93 (25.4%) were from programme and control cohort, respectively. HF‐related readmission events in 1, 3 and 6 months occurred in 5 (1.4%), 20 (5.5%) and 12 (3.3%) in the programme cohort, and 36 (9.8%), 45 (12.3%) and 12 (3.3%) in the control cohort. Figure 1 B shows the Kaplan Meier curve. The log‐rank test was significant (χ 2 = 30.70, df = 1, P < 0.001). Multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated a significant 64% risk reduction in the programme cohort (aHR = 0.36; 95% CI = 0.24–0.52; P < 0.001) compared with the control cohort (Table 2 ). Subgroup analysis by age groups showed that the programme cohort had a significant risk reduction for HF‐related readmissions within 6 months in younger patients [age <60 years (aHR = 0.24, 95% CI = 0.10–0.60, P = 0.002); 60–74 years (aHR = 0.28, 95% CI = 0.16–0.51, P < 0.001)], but not the patients older than 75 years (aHR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.32–1.13, P = 0.115). Subgroup analysis by sex revealed significant risk reduction among male patients (aHR = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.19–0.47, P < 0.001), but not female patients (aHR = 0.62, 95% CI = 0.29–1.33, P = 0.220).

All‐cause mortality

A total of 83 (11.3%) patients died within 6 months after the index admission, with 16 (4.4%) in the programme cohort and 67 (18.3%) in the control cohort. Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that the programme cohort had a significantly lower risk of all‐cause mortality than the patients in the control cohort (HR: 0.22; 95% CI = 0.13–0.38; P < 0.001) (Table 2 , Figure 1 C ). Subgroup analysis indicated significant risk reduction in older patients [60–74 years (aHR = 0.16, 95% CI = 0.05–0.45, P < 0.001); ≥75 years (aHR = 0.29, 95% CI = 0.15–0.55, P < 0.001)], but not in younger patients (age <60 years) (aHR = 0.02, 95% CI = 0.00–6.49, P = 0.175). Both male and female patients in the programme cohort had significantly lower risk reduction compared to the control cohort, with a larger risk reduction observed in males (aHR = 0.14, 95% CI = 0.06–0.31, P < 0.001) than in females (aHR = 0.41, 95% CI = 0.19–0.91, P = 0.028).

Changes in self‐reported health status in the programme cohort

Among the programme cohort, 258 (76.8%) patients completed the KCCQ‐12 at baseline, and 242 (72.0%) of them completed the re‐assessment during the last transition clinic visit. Significant improvements were reported in all domains of the health status, including symptom frequency (P < 0.001), physical (P < 0.001) and social limitation (P = 0.021), quality of life impairment (P < 0.001) and overall summary score (P < 0.001) of KCCQ‐12 (Table 3 ).

Satisfaction in the programme cohort

A total of 167 (49.7%) patients in the programme cohort completed the satisfaction survey when they attended the HF transition clinic. The mean satisfaction score was 3.25 ± 0.40. The item received highest satisfaction score was relating to the understanding of the disease aetiology and its treatment plan (3.29 ± 0.46), while the lowest rating item was about the waiting time (3.14 ± 035). Overall, patients were highly satisfied with the transition clinic, with the mean score of 9.12 (±0.83) out of 10.

Discussion

The findings from this study provide evidence supporting the effectiveness of the nurse‐coordinated multidisciplinary comprehensive programme in reducing adverse outcomes among HF patients compared to the historical control. Specifically, the programme cohort showed significant improvements in several key metrics, including composite endpoints of HF readmission and mortality, HF‐related readmissions and all‐cause mortality within 6 months post‐index admission. Additionally, participants reported enhanced self‐perceived health outcomes and satisfaction, offering valuable insights for shaping future HF management strategies.

These positive outcomes highlight the indispensable role of a multidisciplinary approach that seamlessly integrates care from inpatient settings through discharge preparation and into early post‐discharge care. This comprehensive approach effectively addresses the vulnerable period immediately following discharge, a time marked by increased risks of readmission and mortality. 5 The success of this programme can be attributed to several core components inherent in multidisciplinary care.

Firstly, the programme's foundation lies in the effective collaboration among HF specialists, including cardiologists, nurses, pharmacists and other allied health professionals. This team‐based approach ensures comprehensive patient management by leveraging the diverse expertise of each team member. The cardiologists contribute their advanced and updated clinical knowledge in HF therapies, pharmacists enhance medication management, allied health professionals provide rehabilitation services and nurses coordinate the care among various team members and provide patient‐centred care, collectively creating a robust care network.

Secondly, the rapid initiation and titration of GDMT during hospitalization and post‐discharge are critical to optimizing patient outcomes. Regular multidisciplinary ward rounds help overcome clinical and organizational inertia in GDMT implementation. Our findings are consistent with those of the STRONG‐HF trial, 25 which demonstrated that rapid up‐titration of GDMT based on clinical and laboratory assessments within weeks of acute HF admission significantly reduced the risk of HF readmission or all‐cause mortality at 180 days. 25 This evidence underscores the importance of timely and proactive medication management during this critical window.

Given that the majority of patients in our study had HFrEF, for whom GDMT is well established as an evidence‐based therapy, the focus on early optimization is particularly relevant. However, it is important to acknowledge that, due to the retrospective nature of our study, we lacked detailed data on whether patients were titrated to the guideline‐recommended target doses. This limitation restricts our ability to fully evaluate adherence to GDMT and its direct impact on outcomes. A meta‐analysis indicated that nurse‐ and pharmacist‐led GDMT optimization can improve adherence to guidelines. However, this alone did not significantly reduce all‐cause mortality or HF‐related hospitalization after a median follow‐up of 6 months. 26 This suggests that relying solely on GDMT optimization may not be sufficient to achieve beneficial clinical outcomes.

Given that the early post‐discharge time is a vulnerable period for readmission and death, the 2021 ESC guidelines recommend an early follow‐up visit within 1 to 2 weeks after discharge to assess symptom status and drug tolerance, and initiate or up‐titrate GDMT. 10 The 2023 focused update of the 2021 ESC guidelines further endorses the importance of frequent follow‐up within 6 weeks after a HF‐related admission by classifying this as a Class I recommendation. 27 Several nurse‐led management programmes internationally have demonstrated that structured, multidisciplinary approaches—particularly those involving specialized HF nurses—can significantly improve patient outcomes during this critical period. For example, a recent meta‐analysis pooling data from 30 randomized controlled trials found that nurse‐coordinated multidisciplinary care markedly reduced all‐cause mortality (RR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.72–0.88, P = 0.0001) and HF‐related hospitalizations (RR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.45–0.71, P < 0.001) compared to usual care. 19 These programmes typically incorporate early follow‐up, patient education, medication management and active monitoring—elements that our programme also emphasizes.

A key factor in patient management during this vulnerable period is the coordination of care between the patients and the HF management team. 28 In our programme, HF nurses play a central role within a multidisciplinary framework to coordinate care and provide individualized education. This approach aligns with established evidence indicating that nurse‐led models facilitate greater patient engagement, adherence to treatment and early detection of deterioration. 20 , 21 Such models have been associated with improved clinical outcomes in various settings, 20 , 21 supporting the broader applicability of our findings. Our programme emphasizes patient‐centred care by prioritizing individual needs and preferences, which is known to promote greater engagement and active participation in self‐management. 29 Through tailored education and ongoing support, patients are encouraged to take more responsibility for their health, which can lead to improved adherence to treatment and better self‐care behaviours. 14 , 30 Although our study did not directly measure levels of patient engagement or active participation in self‐management, the observed improvements in patient satisfaction and self‐perceived health outcomes suggest that the programme effectively aligned clinical care with patient experiences. These positive perceptions imply that the programme may have fostered a more patient‐centred approach, contributing to enhanced overall health outcomes.

For the endpoint of HF‐related readmission and the timing of readmissions, the findings favoured the programme cohort. The subgroup analysis revealed that the risk reduction was significant in younger patients (<75 years) and male patients, but not older and female patients. This suggests that while the programme is broadly effective, its impact may vary based on age and sex, potentially due to differences in pathophysiology, risk factors, disease progression, comorbidities or response to treatment. 31 Gender confers different prognosis in the HF population, with studies reporting similar mortality in both genders but female patients had more readmissions. 32 , 33 Our findings indicate the need to provide gender‐specific care to HF patients, with more attention to female patients. Additionally, our subgroup analysis showed significant mortality risk reductions in older patients (ages 60–74 and ≥75) and both sexes, with a more pronounced effect in males. The lack of significant findings in the younger subgroup (age <60) may be due to the relatively low event rate in this group, which limits statistical power.

Patient‐reported outcomes are considered an essential and important metric for HF management. The significant improvement in self‐reported health status across all domains among the programme cohort is highly encouraging, as self‐reported health status is a significant predictor for HF readmission and mortality. 9 , 34 However, the lack of data from the historical cohort prevent us from performing between‐group analysis. The high level of patient satisfaction with the HF transition clinic, particularly regarding understanding of disease aetiology and treatment plans, further emphasize the role of patient‐centred care in improving health outcomes. Studies have shown that patient satisfaction is closely linked to better adherence to treatment and improved clinical outcomes. 35 , 36 The ability to capture and act upon patient feedback allows healthcare providers to tailor interventions more effectively, thus enhancing patient engagement, adherence and overall quality of care. Future HF management strategies should continue to prioritize patient‐reported outcomes to ensure that clinical improvements align with patient experiences and expectations.

The findings of this study must be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, as a retrospective non‐randomized study, the ability to infer causality between the comprehensive HF programme and the observed outcomes is limited. The programme was established as a clinical service, making prospective randomization unfeasible. Instead, a matched historical control group managed within the same healthcare environment was used. Although the two cohorts were relatively well‐balanced in terms of most socio‐demographic and clinical factors, other confounders may not have been adequately controlled. Additionally, the small and heterogeneous sample size might threaten the validity and balance of the propensity score matching procedure, 37 potentially affecting the reliability of the comparative analysis between the cohorts. Second, the two cohorts were admitted during different time periods. Major historical events, such as the COVID‐19 pandemic, could have impacted the outcomes. Third, this was a single‐centre study, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other settings or populations. Fourth, self‐reported health outcomes and satisfaction surveys were administered only to the programme cohort, precluding the possibility of conducting between‐group analyses. Fifth, a significant limitation is the absence of data on medication titration to target doses; we could only determine whether medications were prescribed, but not whether patients achieved or maintained guideline‐recommended target doses. This limits our ability to assess adherence to GDMT fully. Finally, the lack of imaging‐based or echocardiographic assessments is a critical limitation, as these are important tools for evaluating heart failure and could have provided additional insights into the programme's impact.

The study highlights the programme's effectiveness in managing HF patients, leading to fewer readmissions and lower mortality. The consistency of these findings across various subgroups underscores the potential for wide applicability, though the differential effects by age and sex warrant further investigation. Future research should explore the underlying mechanisms driving these differences and aim to optimize the programme to benefit all patient demographics equally. More importantly, an adequately powered randomized clinical trial or prospective observational study is warranted to ascertain the effects of the programme.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the programme cohort showed improved outcomes in HF management compared to the control cohort, suggesting that such programmes may be a beneficial complement to standard care. These findings offer preliminary support for the potential value of broader implementation and further refinement of these programmes to enhance their efficacy and equity across diverse patient populations. Further research is needed to confirm these results and explore the mechanisms behind the observed benefits.
