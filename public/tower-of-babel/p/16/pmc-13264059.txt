Introduction

Elbow dislocations are uncommon in children and pure (isolated) dislocations without associated fracture are exceptionally rare in those younger than 10 years. The immature skeleton typically fails at the physis or apophyseal insertion before the capsuloligamentous structures rupture, so most traumatic events produce avulsion or condylar fractures rather than isolated dislocation [1], [2], [3], [4]. Nevertheless, early diagnosis and prompt reduction are crucial because delay can result in neurovascular compromise, stiffness, or chronic instability. This report describes the successful management of a pure posterior elbow dislocation in a 6-years-old boy and reviews the relevant literature.

Materials and methods

Clinical data were collected with parental consent from the emergency department record.

Evaluation: physical examination including neurovascular status, plain anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs before and after reduction.

Treatment: closed reduction under intravenous sedation using longitudinal traction with counter-traction followed by elbow flexion and supination.

Post-reduction care: stability testing through gentle flexion–extension and pronation–supination, immobilisation in a posterior long-arm splint at 90° flexion for 10 days, then early supervised mobilisation.

Follow-up assessments were performed at 2, 6 and 12 weeks.

Case presentation

A 6-year-old boy fell on his outstretched left arm while running in the school courtyard. He presented 1 h later with marked pain, swelling and obvious deformity of the left elbow. Radial and ulnar pulses were intact and distal neurological examination was normal. Discussion

Pure elbow dislocation in children under 10 years is exceedingly rare because the developing skeleton's growth plates and epiphyseal attachments are biomechanically weaker than the surrounding ligamentous structures. Most pediatric elbow dislocations are therefore fracture-dislocations, with medial epicondyle or lateral condyle avulsions predominating [1], [2]. Hyvönen et al. reviewed 104 pediatric elbow dislocations and found that the majority involved associated fractures, while simple dislocations represented only a small minority [1]. Agar et al. similarly observed better functional outcomes in simple dislocations compared with fracture-dislocations in a cohort of 57 children, but emphasised their relative infrequency [2].

Individual case reports reinforce the rarity of pure dislocation in this age group. Pérez-De León et al. reported two children younger than 10 with isolated posterior dislocation successfully treated with closed reduction and short immobilisation, both achieving full recovery at three months [3]. Aroojis et al. described a 10-year-old with pure medial dislocation who regained full function at four years, with only mild heterotopic ossification [4]. Kushwaha et al. presented a 9-year-old with lateral dislocation and excellent recovery following conservative treatment [5]. Even younger patients have been described: Isaac et al. reported an 18-month-old with isolated posterior dislocation managed successfully by closed reduction and two weeks of splinting [6].

Mechanistically, most cases involve a fall on an outstretched hand with the elbow in extension, creating axial compression and valgus or rotational forces that disrupt the capsuloligamentous complex when the force exceeds the physeal strength. Diagnosis relies on careful clinical assessment and high-quality AP and lateral radiographs; oblique or additional views can help detect subtle medial epicondyle entrapment or small osteochondral fragments. Some authors advocate MRI when there is suspicion of ligament entrapment or when stability is uncertain [7].

Management principles are consistent across reports: urgent closed reduction under adequate analgesia or anaesthesia, post-reduction stability assessment, and a short period of immobilisation—typically 1–2 weeks—to reduce the risk of stiffness. Early, supervised mobilisation is crucial for functional recovery. Prolonged casting is discouraged as it increases the likelihood of flexion contracture [1], [2], [8]. Our patient followed this algorithm and achieved an excellent outcome with full motion at six weeks.

Potential complications include recurrent instability, chronic stiffness, heterotopic ossification, growth plate disturbance, and late deformity. These are rare when early reduction and mobilisation are performed, but careful follow-up is advised to monitor for late-onset stiffness or valgus deformity [4], [5], [8].

This case confirms that pure elbow dislocation can occur even in very young children and supports the current consensus that prompt closed reduction, brief immobilisation, and early rehabilitation are sufficient for excellent functional outcomes. It also highlights the importance of excluding occult fractures and ensuring stability before commencing mobilisation.

Conclusion

Pure elbow dislocation without fracture in a child younger than 10 years is an exceptional but important diagnosis. Rapid recognition, immediate closed reduction, brief immobilisation and early physiotherapy result in excellent functional outcomes and minimal risk of complications.
