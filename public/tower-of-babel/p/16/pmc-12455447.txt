Characterization of insomnia and sleep quality in a cohort with cluster headache
Authors: Felicia Jennysdotter Olofsgård, Caroline Ran, Stefan Spulber, Anna Steinberg, Christina Sjöstrand, Elisabet Waldenlind, Maria Lantz, Anna Sundholm, Marie Söderström, Anna Dahlgren, Andrea Carmine Belin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this cross‐sectional study was to investigate the extent of perceived sleep disturbances in a cluster headache cohort, and to compare how sleep is perceived between participants in an active headache bout to participants in a headache‐free remission period.BackgroundCluster headache is a primary headache disorder characterized by extremely painful headache attacks. These attacks commonly occur with a circadian rhythm, with a majority of patients experiencing nocturnal attacks. Sleep is affected in patients with cluster headache, but there are many uncertainties regarding the pathophysiological connection between cluster headache and sleep disturbances and to what extent sleep disturbances persist into remission.MethodsA digital survey was sent out to 701 individuals with cluster headache between January and May 2024. The survey contained questions regarding general health, clinical cluster headache phenotype, and questions from the Karolinska Sleep Questionnaire, the Insomnia Severity Index, and the Dysfunctional Beliefs and Attitudes about Sleep‐10. Scores extracted from each questionnaire were compared between study participants in an active bout versus study participants in remission, and study participants in short‐term remission (<5 years) versus long‐term remission (≥5 years).ResultsOf the 381 individuals who answered the survey, 325 were included in the final analysis. Participants in an active cluster headache bout reported increased sleep disturbances on all measurements compared to participants in remission (insomnia severity: adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.12 [95% confidence interval (CI), 1.07–1.17] p < 0.001; sleep quality: aOR = 0.67 [95% CI, 0.53–0.83] p < 0.001; daytime sleepiness: aOR = 0.59 [95% CI, 0.44–0.78] p < 0.001; and dysfunctional beliefs: aOR = 1.03 [95% CI, 1.01–1.04] p = 0.001). Of the participants in an active bout, 46.5% scored above the Insomnia Severity Index threshold indicating moderate to severe insomnia (≥15) and 43.7% were considered as having poor sleep quality (≤3 on the Sleep Quality Index extracted from the Karolinska Sleep Questionnaire). This is in comparison to 22.0% of participants in remission being above the moderate insomnia threshold and 22.0% considered having poor sleep quality. Participants in long‐term remission had better sleep scores overall compared to participants in short‐term remission (insomnia severity: aOR = 1.09 [95% CI, 1.03–1.16] p = 0.005; daytime sleepiness: aOR = 0.67 [95% CI, 0.42–1.02] p = 0.072; and dysfunctional beliefs: aOR = 1.03 [95% CI, 1.01–1.05] p = 0.006).ConclusionIndividuals with cluster headache have a large degree of sleep disturbances that are exacerbated during an active bout and not completely alleviated during remission. Future studies are needed to determine if this gradual change in sleep disturbances after an active bout is due to physiological changes, which slowly revert to baseline levels after an active bout or connected to persisting negative behavioral or cognitive associations between sleep and headache.Keywords: dysfunctional beliefs, Dysfunctional Beliefs and Attitudes about Sleep‐10, Insomnia Severity Index, Karolinska Sleep Questionnaire, Sleep Quality Index, sleepiness

INTRODUCTION

Cluster headache (CH) is an excruciatingly painful primary headache disorder with a prevalence of 1 in 1000 in the general population. 1 Patients with episodic CH (ECH) make up roughly 90% of the total CH population. 2 These patients experience bouts with daily headache attacks that last weeks to months, followed by headache free remission periods. 3 The remaining 10% live with chronic CH (CCH) where remission periods are shorter than three months per year. 2

A well‐known characteristic of CH is the circadian timing of headache attacks that a majority of patients experience. In the Swedish CH population, the most common time point for an attack to occur is between 24:00 and 04:00 in the morning 4 and is mirrored by CH cohorts around the world. 5 Sleep has been investigated in association with CH due to the high frequency of nocturnal attacks that disrupt sleep. Furthermore, hypothalamic activation is a trademark of a CH attack and the hypothalamus is home to both the suprachiasmatic nucleus, crucial in regulating the body's molecular clocks and the hypocretin system that helps regulate arousal. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 The hypothalamus is therefore a potential biological link between CH and sleep.

Polysomnography studies have been used to investigate sleep parameters including sleep efficiency, sleep time, and distribution of sleep stages, identifying a longer sleep latency and lower sleep efficiency in patients with CH as compared to controls. 10 Two studies have compared sleep parameters between controls, participants with CH in remission, and participants with CH in an active bout using a combination of actigraphy and sleep diaries. 11 , 12 A Swedish study found that participants with CH spent more time in bed, had longer sleep latency, and experienced worse self‐rated sleep than controls. 12 Additionally, the analysis showed that sleep parameters were significantly worse among participants with CH than controls also when removing participants with nightly attacks, suggesting the sleep difficulties were not entirely due to nightly headache attacks. 12 A Danish study showed similar results, although in this study, participants with CH slept significantly more than controls. 11 Together, these studies indicate that sleep difficulties are common in patients with CH. Sleep and CH have a bidirectional effect on each other with many patients stating poor sleeping habits can trigger both onset of bouts and attacks whereas the attacks cause worse sleep. 4

There are substantial knowledge gaps regarding the link between sleep and CH and how this connection affects the life of individuals living with CH. Poor sleep can greatly affect an individual's quality of life and is strongly linked to mood disorders, cognitive impairment, and lifestyle. 13 , 14 , 15 The most commonly reported sleep disorder in the general population, insomnia, includes symptoms such as trouble falling asleep, trouble maintaining sleep, and excessive daytime sleepiness, and develops into a disorder when some of those parameters are met three times a week for at least three months. 16 , 17 Although a clinical history is needed to diagnose insomnia, subjective surveys can give clues to the extent to which insomnia symptoms are observed in a population. This can be interesting to investigate because the high likelihood of CH attacks occurring at night can lead to patients associating nighttime and sleep with headache pain. Similarly, anxiety and dysfunctional beliefs around sleep can contribute to a negative cycle leading to worse sleep. The aim of this cross‐sectional study was to further investigate the extent of sleep disturbances, including insomnia symptoms, faced by patients with CH and elaborate on the differences between patients in bout compared to remission to understand if altered sleep patterns continue into remission. Our hypothesis was that participants in an active bout would have worse sleep quality, more insomnia symptoms, daytime sleepiness, and dysfunctional attitudes to sleep compared to participants in remission and the entire CH cohort would score worse on all scales compared to the general Swedish population.

METHODS

Participant recruitment, setting, and study design

A preregistration of the study and the intended statistical analyses was uploaded on the OSF platform before results were analyzed (OSF Link). Participants were recruited from January 2024 to May 2024. An email with a link to the survey was sent out via the electronic data capture tool, REDCap, (hosted at Karolinska Institutet) to 701 individuals (the number of email addresses attained during participant recruitment described below). 18 , 19 Two reminder emails were sent out in the following month. Of the responders, 88.6% were part of the Centre for Cluster Headache biobank at Karolinska Institutet and had a CH diagnosis verified by a neurologist described in detail previously. 4 , 20 The remaining participants were recruited via the website of the Swedish patient organization Huvudvärksförbundet (Headache Society) and The Centre for Cluster Headache's web page. All participants gave written informed consent. The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority in Stockholm (diary number 2014/656‐31/4).

Survey (including data measurement)

The survey was comprised of a section with questions relating to demographics and lifestyle including a question regarding other diagnosis/disorders apart from CH including sleep disorders. From this information the occurrence of insomnia comorbidities/contributory factors as described in Table 5 of the European Insomnia Guidelines 2023 could be deduced. 21 The remaining sections contained questions from validated scales measuring insomnia symptoms, attitudes and dysfunctional beliefs about sleep, sleep quality, and free text answers regarding sleep and CH in general. The Karolinska Sleep Questionnaire (KSQ) has been extensively used in sleep research. 22 , 23 It measures quality of nocturnal sleep, sleepiness during daytime, and includes questions on snoring/sleep apnea. Several indexes can be calculated from the KSQ, such as the Sleepiness Index used to analyze daytime sleepiness and the Sleep Quality Index (SQI). 24 , 25 The SQI was extracted by averaging four parameters (“ease falling asleep,” “sleep quality,” “calm sleep,” and “slept throughout”). 25 A score of 3.0 or lower is considered as poor sleep quality. 25 The KSQ Sleepiness Index was calculated by averaging five questions: “sleepiness during work,” “sleepiness during free time,” “unintentional dozing off (naps) during work,” “unintentional dozing off (naps) during free time,” and “have to fight sleep to be able to stay awake.” 24 The scores for both the SQI and Sleepiness Index range from 1 to 6 with higher scores indicating better quality of sleep or less daytime sleepiness. Insomnia symptoms were measured via the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) questionnaire that includes questions relating to subjective perceptions about sleep. The ISI score was calculated by summing up the total points of all seven questions (0–4 points for each question). 26 A threshold of an ISI score ≥15 is commonly used as an indication of moderate to severe insomnia symptoms. 21 The Dysfunctional Beliefs and Attitudes about Sleep‐10 (DBAS‐10) survey, a shortened version of the original DBAS, was used to investigate perception around sleep. 27 , 28 , 29 Each question is answered by selecting a point/number along a 100‐mm visual analogue scale with “strongly disagree” on the extreme left (0) and “strongly agree” on the extreme right (100). The score is then calculated by averaging all questions. 29 Higher DBAS‐10 scores indicate more dysfunctional beliefs about sleep. Scores for each scale were calculated only for participants who had answered all questions pertaining to that score.

In short, using three scales (KSQ, ISI, and DBAS‐10), we were able to evaluate four measures: sleep quality, insomnia, daytime sleepiness, and dysfunctional beliefs about sleep. Furthermore, we were able to evaluate this in participants with active headache bouts and remission periods (including short remission periods, long remission periods, and time since their last CH bout).

Statistical analysis and definition of variables

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) in the RStudio 2024.09.1 environment (Posit PBC, Boston, MA). The R packages MASS, epitools, and psych were used for part of the analysis. Figures were created in PowerPoint (Version 2024) or MATLAB (R2024b). Normality of data was analyzed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical data are presented as percentages and frequency counts. Statistical power calculation was not conducted before the study because the sample size was based on the available data. As a primary analysis, a Pearson's chi‐square (χ2) analysis was performed to compare participants with scores below and above the threshold for insomnia and poor sleep quality.

As a continuation of the primary analysis, comparisons of sleep scores between the different subgroups were analyzed using logistic regression with age, sex, and occurrence of a self‐reported insomnia comorbidity/contributory factor as described in the European Insomnia Guideline 21 as covariates (1 = comorbidity/contributory factors, 0 = no comorbidity/contributory factors). Subgroup analysis included participants in an active bout, remission, short‐term remission (defined as a remission period lasting <5 years), and long‐term remission (5 years or more). For the secondary analysis, participants with ECH and CCH (self‐reported data) were compared. We chose 5 years as the cutoff for long‐term remission, because previous research has shown that remission periods longer than 5 years rarely occur (~5% of cases), and we wanted to investigate if sleep problems linger in people who have not experienced CH symptoms for a longer period of time than the normal remission period length. 30 The active bout and remission group contained both participants with ECH and participants with CCH. In the case of participants with CCH in remission, both current participants with CCH who were in shorter remission periods (<3 months; n = 5) and participants who had previously been diagnosed with CCH and were now in an extended remission (more than 3 months; n = 9) or had not specified a remission length (n = 3) were included. To evaluate whether the duration of remission is associated with sleep scores, we used robust multiple regression models based on maximum likelihood‐like estimator (M‐estimator) with Tukey's biweight and fixed scale for identification of outliers. Individual models to fit the remission duration (years since last bout, winsorized between 0 (<1 year) and 15 years, equivalent to percentile 97) were built for each sleep score, with age, sex, and insomnia comorbidities/contributory factors as covariates. As a post hoc analysis, a Spearman rank correlation test was used to investigate the correlation between DBAS‐10 scores and other sleep scores. Additionally, in post hoc analyses the statistical analyses were rerun only including participants with ECH. Statistical testing was two‐tailed and p‐values <0.05 were considered significant for all tests.

RESULTS

Demographics

Of the 381 individuals who submitted the survey, 325 were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Four individuals did not consent, and 37 individuals did not answer any of the questions or only the first part of the survey and were therefore excluded from the final analysis. Fifteen individuals from the CH biobank were excluded due to unclear diagnosis.

Sleep quality

The average sleep quality score, as measured by the SQI, for the entire CH cohort was 3.8 ± 1.2 (n = 323) (detailed responses KSQ: Table S1). More than one fourth (27.9%) had a score of ≤3.0 (poor sleep quality). 25 Participants in an active bout (average score: 3.3 ± 1.2 [n = 87]) reported poorer sleep quality than participants in remission (average score: 3.9 ± 1.1 [n = 236], adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.67 [95% confidence interval (CI), 0.53–0.83], p < 0.001) (Table 2). Additionally, participants in an active bout were more likely to score below the 3.0 threshold (OR = 2.74 [95% CI, 1.63–4.63], 43.7% [n = 38] vs. 22.0% [n = 52], p < 0.001) (Table 3). There were no significant differences in sleep quality between participants in short‐term remission (average score: 3.9 ± 1.1 [n = 158]) and participants in long‐term remission (average score: 4.1 ± 1.0, n = 62, aOR = 0.78 [95% CI, 0.56–1.05], p = 0.111 (Table 2) or proportion of participants below the 3.0 threshold (OR = 1.53 [95% CI, 0.71–3.32], p = 0.275) (Table 3), but there was an association between number of years since last bout and SQI scores (n = 218, adjusted β‐coefficient [aβ] = 0.49 [95% CI, 0.08–0.90], p = 0.020 (Figure 2 and Table 4).

Daytime sleepiness

Daytime sleepiness as measured by the Sleepiness Index from the KSQ showed that the average Sleepiness Index of participants with CH was 4.4 ± 0.9 (n = 312, where six is maximum and corresponds to no daytime sleepiness). Participants in an active bout (n = 83) had an average score of 4.1 ± 1.0 that was lower (aOR = 0.59 [95% CI, 0.44–0.78], p < 0.001) (Table 2) than participants in remission who had an average of 4.6 ± 0.9 (n = 229). Participants in short‐term remission displayed a trend for lower scores than participants in long‐term remission (4.5 ± 0.9 [n = 155] vs. 4.8 ± 0.7 [n = 59], aOR = 0.67 [95% CI, 0.42–1.02], p = 0.072) (Table 2). The number of years since last active bout was associated with the Sleepiness Index, namely longer remission periods were associated with a lower daytime sleepiness score (n = 212, aβ = 0.58 [95% CI, 0.07–1.09], p = 0.026) (Figure 2 and Table 4).

Insomnia symptoms

Insomnia symptoms as measured by ISI showed that 28.6% of our cohort had symptoms indicating moderate to severe insomnia (detailed responses: Table S2). The average ISI score for the entire CH cohort was 10.3 ± 6.7 (n = 318). Participants in active bout reported more symptoms of insomnia (average score: 14.0 ± 6.6 [n = 86]) than participants in remission (average score: 8.9 ± 6.2 [n = 232], aOR = 1.12 [95% CI, 1.07–1.17], p < 0.001) (Table 2) and a higher percentage scored above the ≥15 total score cutoff (46.5% [n = 40] vs. 22.0% [n = 51], OR = 3.09 [95% CI, 1.82–5.22], p < 0.001) (Table 3). Participants in long‐term remission had less insomnia symptoms (average score: 6.5 ± 5.0 [n = 58]) compared to participants in short‐term remission (average score: 9.8 ± 6.5 [n = 159]) (aOR = 1.09 [95% CI, 1.03–1.16], p = 0.005) (Table 2). Less participants in long‐term remission were above the threshold for moderate to severe insomnia (OR = 3.32 [95% CI, 1.33–8.27], p = 0.007) (Table 3). The number of years since last bout was associated with insomnia symptoms (n = 215, aβ = −0.13 [95% CI, −0.20 to −0.06], p < 0.001) (Figure 2 and Table 4).

Attitudes to sleep

The average DBAS‐10 score, investigating attitudes toward sleep, for the entire cohort was 47.3 ± 19.4 (n = 299) (detailed responses: Table S3). Participants in an active CH bout had higher scores (average score 54.3 ± 18.7 [n = 82]), indicating more dysfunctional beliefs, compared to participants in remission (average score: 44.7 ± 19.0 [n = 217], aOR = 1.03 [95% CI, 1.01–1.04], p = 0.001) (Table 2), and participants in short‐term remission had higher scores (47.1 ± 18.2 [n = 148]) than participants in long‐term remission (38.4 ± 19.1 [n = 56], aOR = 1.03 [95% CI, 1.01–1.05], p = 0.006) (Table 2). The number of years since last bout was significantly associated with the DBAS‐10 score (n = 202, aβ = −0.05 [95% CI, −0.07 to −0.02], p < 0.001) (Figure 2 and Table 4). Although we did not statistically compare individual questions from the DBAS‐10 survey, looking at average scores for specific questions can give us an insight into what differed between the groups. Participants in an active bout were more concerned about the consequences on their physical health due to perceived chronic insomnia than participants in remission (active = 53.4 ± 32.2 [(n = 85], remission = 38.4 ± 31.8 [n = 229], Question 3, DBAS‐10). This group thought it was more important to sleep at least 8 h per day to feel refreshed (active = 75.0 ± 27.9 [n = 86], remission = 58.2 ± 30.3 [n = 229], Question 1) and were additionally more worried that one poor night's sleep would affect their sleep schedule for the remaining part of the week (active = 32.9 ± 29.4 [n = 84], remission = 22.8 ± 24.1 [n = 227], Question 8). In contrast, they reported similar scores for the degree to which an individual gets overwhelmed by thoughts at night (active = 46.9 ± 34.9 [n = 84], remission = 49.2 ± 34.2 [n = 225], Question 10).

To better understand if the DBAS‐10 scores correlated to the other sleep scores, Spearman rank correlation test was used to assess possible correlations. The DBAS‐10 score was significantly correlated with the ISI (ρ = 0.679, p < 0.001 [n = 296]), the SQI (ρ = −0.585, p < 0.001 [n = 297]), and the daytime sleepiness score (ρ = −0.481, p < 0.001 [n = 288]).

Chronic and episodic subgroups

Analysis comparing sleep quality, daytime sleepiness, insomnia severity, and attitudes toward sleep in participants with CCH versus participants with ECH reflected the results from the above analysis on participants in active bout versus remission. Both SQI and sleepiness scores were worse in the CCH group as compared to ECH (Tables S5 and S6). Insomnia severity index and DBAS‐10 scores followed the same patterns with higher scores for the CCH group as compared to ECH (Tables S5 and S6). When only including participants with ECH in the analysis, participants in an active bout (n = 28) had lower ISI and a trend for lower DBAS‐10 scores than participants in remission (n = 200), but there was no difference in the SQI nor Sleepiness Index between the groups (Table S7). Additionally, participants with ECH in an active bout were more likely to score above the 15‐point insomnia threshold but did not reach significance for the poor sleep SQI threshold (Table S8).

Data for potential confounders

Several factors can impact sleep, including the presence of nocturnal attacks in active patients. The average ISI score for participants in an active bout with nocturnal attacks (69) was 14.9 whereas for active participants without nocturnal attacks (17), the average ISI score was 10.1. Medication use can also affect sleep; therefore, current medication usage was reported in Table 5 with a specific focus on medications that can influence sleep as suggested by previous literature. 31 Of the participants in an active phase, 57.5% were taking triptans whereas 35.6% were taking verapamil. Only a small percentage of the total cohort took melatonin (1.2%) whereas 9.2% took other forms of sleep medication. More details on the self‐reported diagnosis/disorders that can be considered a comorbidity or a contributing factor to insomnia as described by the European Insomnia Guidelines were additionally presented in Table 5. Cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, were the most commonly reported comorbidity/contributory factor (13.2% total cohort) followed by other forms of chronic pain (9.8%). Besides investigating potential confounders, participants were also asked if they had any previously known disorders related to sleep. Their self‐reported answers were grouped in Table 6. Notably, 7.7% of the total cohort had self‐reported sleep apnea. Tables S9 and S10 groups the potential confounders based on CH subtype (ECH vs. CCH). The individual subgroups were too small to compare using statistical tests; however, they should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results.

DISCUSSION

Our results demonstrated that participants with CH in active bout had worse sleep quality, more symptoms of insomnia, and a higher degree of dysfunctional beliefs about sleep and daytime sleepiness compared to participants with CH in remission. Overall, 35.7% of the study participants, including 54.0% of participants in an active bout, can be considered having sleep disturbances when looking at either measurements of sleep quality and/or insomnia. Interestingly, participants who reported longer remission periods (5 years or more), slept better and felt more energized compared to participants who had <5 years since their last bout. Similarly, the number of years since last bout was significantly associated with less sleep disturbances, suggesting sleep parameters gradually improve after an active bout.

Comparing average scores of the different sleep scales of the CH cohort to the general population reveals substantial differences. When comparing the CH cohort to a representative sample of the Swedish population, participants with CH reported more problems related to sleep quality (Table S1). 32 This extended to participants in remission and included repeated awakenings (“always”: 6.7% [CH in remission] vs. 3.4% [general population]), premature awakenings (“never”: 8.0% vs. 25.1%), and disturbed sleep (“never”: 8.1% vs. 19.9%) as measured by the KSQ (Table S1). The general population cohort reported less trouble falling asleep with 25.6% reporting they never had difficulties falling asleep compared to 13.0% of participants with CH in remission. 32 It can be important to note that the general Swedish population had a lower percentage of males (45.4%) than our CH cohort (62.2%), although participants in an active bout had a more similar sex distribution (52.9% males).

Furthermore, a German study investigating insomnia symptoms in adolescents, university students, and first responders had average ISI scores of 6.7, 6.6, and 7.0, respectively. 33 Similarly, a study conducted on a general Swedish population of individuals over the age of 65 had an average ISI score of 9.8, all of which are lower than in our CH cohort (ISI = 10.3). 34 Of the 65+ population, 20.0% had scores ≥15 compared to 28.6% in our cohort. For comparison, a subgroup in the 65+ cohort with chronic pain had an ISI score of 10.9. Here, participants with CH in active bout had an average ISI score of 14.0 whereas the remission group scored lower (8.9), possibly indicating an effect of pain. A high percentage of participants in an active bout in our cohort experienced nocturnal attacks; however, with only 17 participants without nocturnal attacks, performing statistical tests was difficult. The average ISI score for participants with nocturnal attacks was 14.9 (n = 69) compared to 10.1 in participants in active bout without nocturnal attacks. This suggests that nocturnal attacks contribute substantially to insomnia symptoms. This could possibly be due to the direct effects of pain or underlying physiological disturbances related to sleep; however, nocturnal timing of attacks may also create fear conditioning around sleep during active bouts. We hypothesize that it is not solely the presence of pain that contributes to poor sleep, and sleep‐related anxiety can linger into remission because participants with CH in remission lasting <5 years reported more insomnia symptoms than those in long‐term remission. Furthermore, a higher number of years since last CH bout was associated with lower ISI scores, suggesting extended remission periods are associated with better sleep.

Excessive daytime sleepiness can be attributed to various causes with poor sleep quality and circadian dysregulation being two prominent ones. 35 Individuals with insomnia symptoms are classified as having insomnia disorder if daytime activities/daily function is impacted by lack of sleep, such as having excessive daytime sleepiness. 21 Here, participants in an active bout and to some extent short‐term remission had more daytime sleepiness than participants in long‐term remission indicating the impact of CH on daytime energy levels, likely through poor sleep and energy draining attacks.

The DBAS‐10 questionnaire is designed to investigate negative beliefs about sleep that are known to exacerbate or maintain insomnia. 36 Researchers studying dysfunctional beliefs in a population of “normal sleepers” (average age: 56.9 years) in comparison to a cohort of individuals with insomnia (average age: 59.0 years) found “normal sleepers” had less dysfunctional beliefs about sleep (DBAS‐10: 26.4) than individuals with insomnia (DBAS‐10: ≥35.5). 29 Our CH cohort scored even higher, with a DBAS‐10 score of 54.3 when in an active bout. The large percentage of participants with nocturnal attacks could also contribute to the high scores, associating sleep and nighttime with the CH attacks. Perceived sleep deprivation seems to particularly affect participants in an active bout. Participants in an active bout reported it more important to have 8 h of sleep to feel refreshed than participants in remission and were more concerned of a poor night's sleep disturbing the sleeping schedule for the rest of the week. Interestingly, the feeling of being overwhelmed by thoughts at night and having a racing mind that was hard to control was similar between participants in an active bout compared to remission. This indicates that anxiety at night could be a contributing factor to the sleep disturbances persisting into remission. This is further backed by the strong correlation between DBAS‐10 scores and the insomnia, SQI, and daytime sleepiness scores we found in our cohort.

It has been shown that cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) can improve insomnia and sleep quality. 37 A study on digital CBT found improvements in DBAS scores; in other words, they found that improving an individual's attitudes toward sleep to be the major factor driving the improvements in fatigue and insomnia scores. 38 The elevated DBAS scores and heightened ISI scores seen in participants with CH, which continue into remission, indicate CBT as a potentially useful tool to treat sleep impairments and restructure dysfunctional ideas regarding sleep in patients with headache, which may improve both the headache and quality of life. It is important to note that the cross‐sectional design of this study limits the extent to which causal inferences can be drawn. Therefore, we can only speculate on the possible causes behind the worse sleep scores during an active bout or shorter remission periods.

As previously mentioned, participants in an active bout scored worse for all sleep measurements compared to participants in remission. The CCH group overlapped to a large extent with the participant group who was in an active bout, (Table 1), which could partially explain the large differences in sleep scores between the participants with ECH and participants with CCH. The opposite could also be true, where the higher number of participants with CCH in the active bout group could contribute to the worse sleep scores present in this group. Because the primary analysis of this study was to compare sleep in different disease phases, participants who were previously CCH patients but had entered into an extended remission period were also included in the remission group. When only including participants with ECH in the post hoc analysis, the difference between participants in an active bout versus in remission remained significant when investigating insomnia symptoms and showed a trend for dysfunctional beliefs, but not for sleep quality nor daytime sleepiness. This could partially be due to the small group size (28 participants with ECH were in an active bout) and partially because insomnia and dysfunctional beliefs scores cover more general aspects of subjective sleep whereas sleep quality and daytime sleepiness scores are based on only a few narrow parameters. Additionally, participants with CCH are more likely to have more extreme scores and more severe phenotypes (such as daytime sleepiness) due to the increased burden of the disease, not just their increased likelihood of being in an active bout. In the free text comments section of the questionnaire, multiple participants reported additional information regarding their nocturnal attacks. Several participants mentioned waking up after 90 min of sleep to a CH attack (File S3). This has been reported in other CH cohorts and was a reason why rapid eye movement (REM) sleep was suggested to be associated with CH attacks. Although more recent studies have not observed an association, 10 90 min after sleep onset seems to be a particularly common time for attacks to occur. People themselves cannot accurately determine what sleep stages they are in before awakening and this/it is therefore only an estimate, but the timing of nocturnal attacks should be investigated in more detail in the future. The transition from the parasympathetic cholinergic system, crucial in REM sleep, to the primary sympathetic system active in non‐REM sleep has been proposed as a possible trigger but more research is needed. 39

Although not as large of a difference as for active bout compared to remission, participants classified as being in a short‐term remission (<5 years) compared to long‐term remission scored significantly worse on some of the screened scales. This is in accordance with previous results from our cohort, showing that participants with CH in remission have affected sleep, primarily manifested as increased sleep latency and sleep‐related stress. 12 Moreover, an increasing number of years since the last active bout was associated with improved sleep. A Danish study using polysomnography data similarly stated that the sleep problems associated with CH continued into remission or slowly dissipated as remission was prolonged. 10 This could indicate that it is not merely the absence of headaches that improves sleep and daytime sleepiness. During a CH bout, several changes may occur in the brain in relation to the circadian behavior that could explain the slow pace of the reversal to a neutral state once the bout has ended. Several participants stated in free text answers that for some time after their bout had ended, they would have disturbed sleep and wake up at the same time they usually got headache attacks during the night (File S3). Similarly, study participants have mentioned that they have disturbed sleep a period before the CH bout starts. In these cases, it can be difficult to separate poor sleep as a symptom of entering a CH bout or if poor sleep can be a trigger of the bout. It is also worth noting that some participants experienced sleep disturbances only during CH bouts, indicating not all participants feel alterations in sleep and circadian behavior outside of their bout (File S3).

It is important to note that participants with CH in remission had in general worse scores regarding sleep quality and dysfunctional beliefs compared to reports from the general Swedish population; however, insomnia symptoms as measured by ISI scores were not worse. This indicates that even though improvement of CH symptoms somewhat restores sleep function, this patient group experiences worse sleep than the general Swedish population and that the patient group would possibly benefit from sleep interventions also when in remission. Poor sleep is associated with many health problems, including mental health disorders such as depression and anxiety, and worse cognitive performance, which may affect these individuals throughout their lives. 40 , 41

Limitations

A possible limitation of our study is that we had an approximately 50% survey response rate, which could favor individuals with more severe sleep problems answering more readily. Likewise, the long‐term remission group is relatively small (n = 62), which can increase the risk for type II errors. When comparing to the whole CH biobank, most demographic factors such as sex and age are similar (data not shown) although CH subtype does substantially differ (study participants 22.5% CCH vs. biobank 12.5% CCH). 4 Many factors influence sleep and we could not take all of them into account; however, by including sex, age, and comorbidities of insomnia as covariates when analyzing differences between subgroups, we can correct for the most apparent factors that influence sleep. 21 Another limitation of our study is the low number of participants in an active bout not reporting nocturnal attacks, which makes us unable to compare participants in an active bout with and without acute pain during nighttime. Furthermore, sleep apnea was not objectively/directly investigated for this study. Of the total cohort, 7.7% had self‐reported sleep apnea, which is not higher than the general population; however, our cohort has an overrepresentation of males and an average age of 52.6 years. 42 Sleep apnea is known to have a higher prevalence in the older population, especially in males; therefore, some caution should be taken. Objective tests such as polysomnography are needed to mitigate the tendency for sleep apnea to be underreported. Medication, including medication related to CH, can impact sleep and daytime sleepiness. 31 We have included a list of medications that can potentially impact sleep, including some medications that are not specifically used for CH but were relatively common in our cohort (Tables 5 and S9). Of our study participants in an active bout, 57.5% report using triptans. There is some evidence that triptans can induce somnolence; however, triptans in the form of nasal spray or injections commonly used by patients with CH seem to have a low impact on sleep. 31 Melatonin has been prescribed to improve sleep but has also been suggested to improve CH. 43 Only four participants in our cohort reported use of melatonin and it was therefore not considered in our analysis. Moreover, lithium can impact circadian rhythm and sleep, commonly seen in bipolar disease. Only one individual reported taking lithium currently and therefore lithium intake should not affect our data. Verapamil, on the other hand, can potentially influence our results because 35.6% of participants in an active bout take it. A study investigating the effect of verapamil on circadian rhythm in mice found that verapamil could shorten the oscillations of the PER2 protein in the fibroblasts of mice and likewise reduce the free‐running period in vivo indicating a potential effect on the molecular clock proteins and circadian rhythm. 44 It was difficult to take medication usage into account in the statistical models due to the large variety of medications used; however, it should not be overlooked when interpreting the results. Most medications can negatively impact sleep although obviously some, such as medications specifically designed to induce sleep, have the opposite effect. Similarly, lifestyle factors such as alcohol and caffeine consumption were not corrected for.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, sleep and daytime sleepiness are significantly altered in participants with CH. This is especially true in an active bout but persists into remission. Sleep disturbances continuing in remission should be considered when planning sleep interventions in patients with CH. These interventions have the potential to benefit patients with CH in different disease phases, especially patients recently diagnosed to improve long‐term sleep and potentially prevent sleep related problems.
