Conveying organic character with a label: the role of color in the packaging of organic yogurt
Authors: Begoña Hernández, Paula Elso, Carlos Sáenz, María Remedios Marín‐Arroyo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThis work evaluates the influence of label color in consumers’ perception of ‘organic’ in plain organic yogurt. Five labels were specifically designed for this purpose, using recurring hues and images found in a preliminary study of the packaging of 86 organic yogurts in six European countries. Through online surveys, 124 Spanish respondents participated in an experiment structured in three parts: selection of terms that apply to each label to find out color subjective associations using check‐all‐that‐apply (CATA) methodology, evaluation of purchase intent and ranking of the designs according to their closeness to the ‘organic’ concept.RESULTSOrganic yogurt is regarded as healthy, homemade and a high‐quality product. Green labels are viewed as the most ‘organic’, ranked in first place in all positive attributes and best rated in purchase intent. Among green labels, that with a green landscape conveys superior expectations of pleasant sensory experiences. Black and multicolored dotted labels are regarded as unconventional for this product and are less liked. A blue label is generally well considered, but respondents tend to place it in intermediate positions. Consumers’ purchase intent coincides with the ranking according to their ‘organic’ character. Furthermore, purchase intent scores are accurately predicted by a strong linear relationship (R 2 = 0.9992) with the average citation frequency of positive CATA terms.CONCLUSIONFor organic yogurt, label designs based on green colors, possibly containing evocative images, better express the idea of organic and its related positive attributes, enhancing consumers’ expectations and purchase intent. © 2025 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Direct observation of the color of a food product allows us to estimate its state of ripeness or healthiness and its suitability for consumption. 1 However, this is not possible in packaged products, where all the responsibility for capturing our attention falls on the packaging design. Identifying the package and label features most appealing to consumers provides the keys to creating a design that meets consumer needs and expectations. 2 Packaging and other extrinsic product characteristics seem to have more influence in abstract and functional conceptualization than sensory properties. 3 Color in particular plays a crucial role in design, since people associate colors with ideas, concepts, sensations and feelings, obtained as a consequence of educational, empirical and cultural environments. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Color generates unconscious expectations even in terms of flavor and texture. 2 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 In the specific case of dairy desserts, the shape and the color of the package affect liking scores, with color even surpassing the nutritional information in the frequency of the words selected by consumers about their purchase intent. 2 , 9 Some studies have found that the presence of images in the label is another key factor to consider. For instance, the image of a blue sky on a yogurt label elicits feelings of freshness, calm and relaxation in consumers 11 and the presence of an image with a cup of coffee with foam and coffee beans in coffee packages increases purchase intent. 13

Organic product development is a rapidly growing food sector throughout the world 14 as a consequence of an increasing demand for these kinds of products by consumers. 15 In the specific case of Spain, in December 2021 the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food published a report about the importance of Spanish organic production in 2020, showing that it is undergoing an intense growth process and becoming an important area of differentiated consumption within the Spanish food system, with milk and dairy derivatives occupying an important place within this sector. 16 The mere fact that a food product displays a label that certifies its organic nature makes the consumer perceive it as healthy and be willing to pay a higher price for it. 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 Other aspects such as environmental friendliness and animal welfare are an added value 14 , 20 although health benefits outweigh environmentally altruistic motives in purchasing decisions. 21 Organic labels do positively affect hedonic evaluations of consumers’ overall liking of a product. 22 Considering the particular case of yogurt, consumers value organic yogurt as superior to conventional types. 15 Within the products certified as organic, some can be assessed by direct observation of the product itself (e.g. meat, vegetables), while in others, such as yogurt, all the clues about its quality lie in its packaging. This makes organic yogurt a very good candidate for this study.

The application of novel techniques such as check‐all‐that‐apply (CATA) has provided useful results in discovering the expectations that a food product conveys to consumers. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 A questionnaire with a list of terms is offered to respondents, asking them to select all those that apply for describing a focal product. The list of terms must be carefully selected and it can contain not only sensory attributes but also ideas, adjectives or even feelings. This methodology is a quick and simple test that provides detailed information about consumers' perception of the sensory characteristics of a product, offering practical and reliable results. 3 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 CATA methodology combined with penalty analysis also makes it possible to identify liking drivers for the development of new products. 32 , 33 , 34

The aim of the work presented here was to find out how the color of the label design influences the perception of organic products. To achieve this objective, we chose organic yogurt as target product, focusing the research on three main aspects: relationships with specific sensory attributes using CATA methodology, purchase intent and the closeness of design to the ‘organic’ concept. The study was carried out through the collaboration of researchers specialized in color science together with researchers specialized in food technology, joining and complementing knowledge about color physics and sensory evaluation. This work was evaluated and approved by the School of Agricultural Engineering and Biosciences following the regulatory rules of the Public University of Navarre.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Information‐gathering: organic yogurt packaging and label design in the European market

A preliminary study was conducted to analyze the present trends in yogurt packaging and label design in a representative selection of European markets. In total, 87 packages of plain organic yogurts from six European countries (Austria, France, Germany, Italy, Spain and the UK) were analyzed. These countries have the highest percentage of organic production in Europe, according to a report published by the Research Institute of Organic Agriculture. 35 The information was obtained from three sources: specialized stores in organic products, conventional supermarkets and websites of the most representative supermarket chains.

The objective was to identify the predominant or primary color, i.e. the color that occupies a larger area on the label and attracts consumer attention at first sight, and the secondary color, the second color in relevance according to the covered area. We also studied the most recurrent color combinations and the presence of images. In general, the labels of the analyzed yogurts are characterized by simple designs devoid of stunning or bright colors. Detailed results can be found in Part 1, Figs A1–A4, of the supporting information.

Label design and construction of yogurt samples

In accordance with the results of the preliminary study, five prototypes of fictitious organic yogurt labels (A, B, C, D and E) were designed and placed on typical glass jars used for these kind of products (Fig. 1). Labels A, B and C have identical design except for the background color (primary color) that is green (A), blue (B) or black (C). In these labels, text color (secondary color) is white. Therefore, these labels contain the most common primary and secondary colors (white, green, blue and black) as well as the most frequent color combinations (green/white, blue/white and black/white) found in the preliminary survey. Label D shows a multicolor design composed of dots of striking colors on a white background, a completely unconventional design for this kind of product. To add a cozy look to designs, a simple drawing of a small daisy was included in the center of each of the first four labels (A, B, C and D) since this flower is also widely used on yogurt labels due to its association with the concept of ‘natural’. 11 Finally, label E was designed to weigh the importance of the presence of an image versus using only solid colors. The image depicts a teaspoon of yogurt over a green country landscape and it was inspired by the most common topics found in the market. Its overall aspect corresponds to a green/white combination.

Yogurt label designs used in this study. The corresponding version with Spanish text is available in the supporting information.

The same text indicating ‘100% organic plain yogurt’ was used in all labels. Text color is white in all labels except label D that uses black text because it has a white background with the purpose of highlighting the color dots. White is often used in typography to convey ideas such as freshness, calm and peace. 11 Color is more important than typeface in nonverbal communication, but the importance of this element in design cannot be underestimated since it also influences feelings such as product healthiness. 8 In consequence, the same typeface was used in all labels. Microsoft Office simple tools were used for the designing process. Pictures and motifs applied on the labels have been created exclusively for this work and, therefore, they do not correspond to any commercial brand.

Labels were printed on high‐resolution adhesive photographic paper and added to transparent glass yogurt jars filled with milk to provide a uniform white background in all of them. The simulated jars of yogurt were photographed using a smartphone camera, to optimize resolution (ISO: 64, 1/100 exposure time, f: 22 aperture, 35 mm focal length, without flash) and fast loading (816 kB, jpg format) in web browsers. Photographs were taken by placing the jars in a controlled lighting booth (Macbeth SpectraLight III) that provided daylight illumination simulating the D65 standard illuminant. This process was carried out in the color research laboratory of the Public University of Navarre.

Surveys

Surveys were structured in three sections: (a) semiotic selection of words and expressions to apply the CATA methodology, (b) valuation of purchase intent and (c) estimation of the closeness of the different label designs to the term ‘organic’. Examples of each of these sections are provided in Part 2 of the supporting information.

Sections (a) and (b) had to be completed for each label design individually. For this purpose, each of the five yogurt images was offered, one by one and in a balanced design order, to respondents. First, the image was accompanied by a list of terms, asking the respondents to select from the list all the terms suggested by the image. The text with the instructions read: ‘Please, look at the picture and from the following list, pick all the words that you relate to the product shown in it’. The list contained 14 descriptors: ‘appealing’, ‘cheap’, ‘creamy’, ‘expensive’, ‘fresh’, ‘healthy’, ‘high quality’, ‘homemade’, ‘low calorie’, ‘low quality’, ‘new’, ‘organic’, ‘tasty’ and ‘without additives’. Some of these terms were based on those used by Ares et al. 11 to identify the influence of label design on the perception of plain yogurt by consumers. The list was completed with terms focused on the organic characteristic of the product. Questionnaires were designed in a balanced way using a Williams' Latin Square, to avoid biased results by consumers' tendency to mark more frequently the descriptors that are at the top positions of the lists. 36 , 37

Once the selection of terms was completed, participants were asked to score their purchase intent about the displayed yogurt. For this section, the yogurt image was shown together with a 9‐point structured scale with meaning indications of the extremes: (1) ‘I would never buy it’ and (9) ‘I would buy it without a doubt’.

After the completion of sections (a) and (b) for each yogurt image, the five label designs were shown simultaneously on the screen, as in Fig. 1, to complete section (c). Respondents were asked to rank them according to their proximity to the idea of ‘organic’.

Surveys were delivered via social media through online questionnaires developed using the Google Docs survey application.

Respondents

Consumers were recruited, based on their interest and availability to participate in the study, from the consumer database of the AENOLTEC research group (Advances in Oenology and Food Technologies) of the Public University of Navarra, among university students and workers, and through snowball recruitment. The study was explained to consumers in the online questionnaire that was completed anonymously. They were informed that all data would be de‐identified and reported only in aggregated form. All participants acknowledged an informed consent statement in order to participate in the study. Cash incentives were not provided.

During the time available for the study, 124 yogurt consumers (65 females and 35 males) completed the entire survey. As a prerequisite to participate in this study, a section on the frequency of consumption of plain yogurts was included, it being necessary to consume this kind of product at least once a week. The majority of respondents (54%) consumed 2 to 4 yogurts per week. With respect to the age distribution, the largest group (68%) was in the age range between 18 and 40 years old, perhaps because this group is more familiar with social networks than are older consumers. The complete demographic summary of respondents by gender, age and frequency of yogurt consumption can be found in Table A1 of the supporting information.

Statistical analysis

Cochran's Q test followed by a Sheskin multiple pairwise comparisons test were carried out to identify significant differences among yogurt label designs for each of the offered descriptors. Subsequently, a correspondence analysis (CA) was executed.

Purchase intent scores were analyzed using a one‐way analysis of variance to determine significant differences among label designs. Once the null hypothesis was rejected, a Tukey post hoc test was conducted considering significant differences when P < 0.05. To contrast purchase intent with CATA results, a penalty analysis was performed to determine the drop in purchase intention associated with the presence or absence of each attribute. The relationship between the average citation frequencies of relevant CATA attributes was analyzed using linear regression analysis.

For the rank experiment, a Friedman test followed by a multiple pairwise comparisons procedure was used to weigh the closeness of the label designs to the respondents' idea of the organic concept.

All the statistical analysis was performed using the XLSTAT software package. 38

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

CATA results

Table 1 summarizes the selection frequencies of CATA terms, for each label design, and the results of Cochran's Q test and Sheskin post hoc analysis. The most cited attributes are, in this order, ‘organic’ (75.0% to 91.1%), ‘without additives’ (64.5% to 73.4%) and ‘healthy’ (50.0% to 74.2%). The correlation matrix between term citations, Table A2 of the supporting information, shows that these attributes are related and they are frequently picked in a coordinated way. Furthermore, the citation frequencies of ‘organic’ and ‘healthy’ are very sensitive to the label design (P < 0.0001). We note that the word ‘organic’ is printed on the five label designs and in despite of this, not all respondents picked it, 91% at most in the cases of labels A and E. Label differences in the citation frequency of ‘without additives’ are very close (P = 0.070) to being significant at the 95% confidence level and this attribute will be also included in the further analysis. Most probably consumers unconsciously link these attributes, which is a logical result since a product with additives is not considered organic and, in addition, the term organic food is promoted as a healthy food. 14 , 19 , 21 Although some studies indicate that the term ‘low calorie’ is associated with organic products when they are compared to conventional food products, 17 , 19 , 39 in our study this term has not been marked frequently (16%), neither has it been an attribute significantly influenced by the color of the label.

Together with ‘organic’ and ‘healthy’, the most significant attributes (P < 0.01) are ‘fresh’, ‘appealing’, ‘creamy’, ‘healthy’, ‘high quality’ and ‘tasty’. All these sensory characteristics are highly appreciated by consumers, especially creamy, healthy and appealing. 2 Regardless of the label design, descriptors ‘cheap’ and ‘low quality’ were rarely selected (<6%) suggesting that these words hardly fit the idea of organic yogurt. Apparently, consumers do not expect that an organic yogurt is going to be either cheap or of poor quality. Quite the contrary, the terms ‘high quality’ and ‘expensive’ were offered as antonym options and marked much more frequently (45%) and selectively depending on the label designs. Finally, term ‘new’, being cited only 24% of the time, clearly differentiates label D from all other labels but without noticeable relationships with other attributes.

The resulting sensory map (Fig. 2) of the CA applied to the contingency table generated from the answers to CATA questionnaires is a representative summary of the underlying structure in the data, confirms the previous analysis of individual CATA terms and provides new insights on the relationships among label designs and attributes. The percentage of inertia explained by the first two dimensions of the CA is 75.44%. In consequence, only the first and second dimensions of the CA will be considered, since the percentage of explained variance drops significantly for the third dimension. Nearest to the center, we find the focus term ‘organic’, closely accompanied by the terms ‘homemade’, ‘healthy’, ‘high quality’ and ‘without additives’, all of them in the lower quadrants. This clearly suggests that consumers naturally associate all these attributes with an organic product. In general, the attributes that we could classify as positive tend to be located towards the left of the diagram. Terms associated with taste and texture characteristics, ‘appealing’, ‘tasty’, ‘creamy’ and ‘fresh’, are placed in the upper left quadrant, at some distance from the term ‘organic’. Consumers apparently consider these positive attributes as a benefit, not necessarily implied by the organic character of the yogurt. Characteristics interpreted as negative or not expected for this product, ‘cheap’, ‘low quality’ and ‘new’, are located in the upper right quadrant, far away from ‘organic’.

Sensory map of the CA showing the five label designs (filled black triangles) and CATA descriptors (open circles). Not significant (P > 0.1) terms after Cochran's Q test are shown in gray.

Our findings are aligned with the general statement that consumer perception is biased by the color and design of a food package. In this diagram label designs A and B lie in the lower left quadrant, C in the lower right, E in the upper left and finally D in the upper right, far away from the rest of the designs. It is also suggestive that dimension 2 apparently discriminates between simple designs using a solid background color (A, B, C) in the lower quadrants, and more complex designs (D, E) in the upper quadrants. This description agrees with a first glance, simple visual classification of the label designs without regard to their particular color. Labels, however, were not designed to test this characteristic and definite conclusions cannot be extracted.

Specifically, CATA results show that the predominantly green designs, both the solid green background (A) and the one with the landscape (E), evoke the organic character more convincingly than the multicolor (D) and black (C) designs. Similarly, label designs A and E are also clearly viewed as healthier than D or C. In addition to organic, healthy and high quality, the green designs (A, E) are also clearly perceived as fresh and homemade. The label showing a green country landscape with a teaspoon of yogurt over it (E) was, without a doubt, the most pleasant for consumers. In addition to its organic character, it creates also sensorial expectations of a positive taste and texture experience. This design accounted for the highest number of selections in all positive attributes offered in CATA, ‘appealing’, ‘tasty’, ‘creamy’, ‘fresh’ and ‘homemade’ among them. It was numerically surpassed only in the term ‘healthy’ by the solid green design (A) but without statistically significant differences between them. The simple fact of showing a teaspoon with yogurt also elicited in consumers the idea of ‘creamy’. This agrees with the literature since, in milk desserts, consumers give a great importance to the label color but they also find appealing those that contain some pictures of milk desserts. 2 , 11 , 40 In addition, including illustrations influences purchase intent as it provides consumers with information about what to expect inside the package. 13

Respondents find the black design (C) just ‘expensive’ and, in short, it is a design that no one seems to like. It is known that color symbolism, particularly in the case of black, is related to cultural identity 4 , 6 and, on occasions, this may lead to apparently conflicting results. For instance, Ares and Deliza 9 found contradictory results in consumer opinion towards black containers, sometimes identified with terms such as interesting and premium but also found it unpleasant and associated with terms like bitter. In their symbolic roles at the marketplace, colors that appear to be suitable for one product may not be appropriate for another. Dark browns and black are commonly used for coffee and tea but not for dairy products, since they recall neither milk nor the countryside. 4

The multicolor dotted label (D) did not work too well either. It is an unusual design conceived to investigate the effect of breaking stereotypes in consumer impressions. In this sense, the design was successful and label D stands out with the highest score in the attribute ‘new’, a characteristic that significantly differentiates it from the rest. In the CA plot, label D is isolated in the first quadrant and surrounded by the terms ‘new’, ‘cheap’ and ‘low quality’. Although this design was also the most selected in these two last attributes, differences between labels are not statistically significant. Label D also scores poorly in many positive attributes. Multicolored designs are commonly used for the children's market 4 but adult consumers do not like this type of novelty in products unconsciously linked with health. They prefer not to take risks with unconventionalities. 41

Finally, interpretation of the CATA results for the solid blue design (B) is somewhat inconclusive. For many attributes, its role cannot be statistically differentiated from any other label design. Consumers view it as fresh, healthy and homemade but, on the other hand, it does not convey terms such as appealing.

Influence of label design on purchase intent

Ranking according to purchase intent

According to the mean scores in purchase intent, label designs can be ranked in ascending order as C, D, B, A and E. The analysis of variance confirms (P < 0.05) that the label design influences respondents' purchase decision. Figure 3 shows the mean scores obtained by the five label designs, together with the homogeneous groups (significance level α = 0.05) as determined by the post hoc Tukey test. The highest value (6.13 ± 0.17) corresponds to design E, the label with the green landscape, showing significant differences from designs C and D but not from designs A nor B. On the other hand, label C, with black background, received the lowest score (5.01 ± 0.19) with significant differences from labels A and E. Respondents placed label B (blue background) in an intermediate position (5.51 ± 0.16) without significant differences from any of the other designs. These results agree with the overall picture obtained from the CATA analysis, where consumers mainly identified the black label C as the more expensive and the least tasty, while label E collected the largest number of positive attributes.

Mean purchase intent scores for each of the five label designs evaluated obtained with a 9‐point structured scale from ‘I would never buy it’ (1) to ‘I would buy it without a doubt’ (9). Same characters over the bar identify labels without significant differences at 95% confidence level.

Relationships between sensory term citation frequency and purchase intent

Inspection of Table 1 points to a correspondence between purchase intent and the average citation frequency of some CATA terms, in particular, those that potential consumers may identify with positive characteristics for this product. The results of a penalty analysis, Fig. 4, identified the attributes ‘appealing’, ‘tasty’, ‘healthy’, ‘high quality’, ‘creamy’, ‘without additives’, ‘fresh’, ‘homemade’ and ‘organic’ as positively related with purchase decisions. To explore this relationship in a quantitative way we used the overall citation frequency associated with all these terms as a predictor variable for purchase intent. The resulting fit, Fig. 5, exhibits a very high linear correlation between these two variables (R 2 = 0.9934) confirming that purchase intent scores can be adequately predicted from the average citation frequency of positive CATA terms. Interestingly, neither ‘expensive’ nor ‘cheap’ appear in the list, clearly indicating that price is not a decisive parameter for purchasing this product.

Partial correlations in the citation frequency of the terms in this list are always moderate (Table A.2 in supporting information) and therefore no attribute can be considered equivalent to another. On the other hand, it is possible to reduce the list of attributes without compromising the robustness in the prediction of purchase intent. For instance, after removing the terms ‘without additives’ and ‘organic’ from the list, the average citation frequency is even better correlated with purchase intent (R 2 = 0.9992). This produces the apparently contradictory result that the attributes ‘without additives’ and ‘organic’ are not required to determine consumers’ preference to buy an organic product, despite the fact that they are highly significant in the CATA survey. This can only be explained assuming that, with respect to purchase intent, these two attributes convey the same information as the remaining ones.

According to these results, there are two different kinds of attributes that positively affect purchase decisions. First, yogurt labels should effectively evoke the expected characteristics of organic products (‘healthy’, ‘homemade’, etc.). In addition, the consumer will favor those that generate expectations of a more positive sensory experience (‘creamy’, ‘fresh’, etc.). It is known that choosing a food is a complex process involving sensory and non‐sensory factors. 2 Consumers simplify their decisions by using certain visual heuristic‐like colors and images on the package, but different categories of products have different preferences about these visual characteristics of design. 40 At the time of acquiring a dairy dessert, color is the most significant attribute. 9 This agrees with the preference for green designs (A, E). The design with the image of a landscape (E) is, however, favored because of its association with favorable taste and texture characteristics. Clearly, once the product has been purchased and tasted, if it disappointed consumers' expectations, they will not buy it again. In any case, it is necessary to attract a potential buyer to persuade her or him to taste the product, and the package design plays a key role in this objective.

In the selection process, consumers often doubt between uniqueness versus conformity 42 and tend to categorize a type of product, preferably choosing those that match their mental stereotypes. 6 This is also in accordance with the preference of green designs, a widely used color for organic yogurt. Unusual colors, on the other hand, can be advantageous to stand out on the shelf, but they can also generate incongruences leading to consumer rejection. 43 This seems to be the case of the unconventional dotted color design (D), that was also viewed as the furthest from the organic‐like attributes.

Yogurt design ranking according to closeness with the ‘organic’ concept

Friedman test results revealed significant differences (P < 0.01) among the five designs in terms of their closeness to consumers’ notion of organic. Post hoc multiple pairwise comparisons arrange the five designs into three differentiated groups at 95% confidence level. The country landscape and the green labels (E, A) are the closest to the idea of organic, the blue design (B) occupies an intermediate position and the multicolor (D) and black (C) designs are more distant from this concept.

This classification is also in accordance with the term citation frequencies in the CATA test, presented in Table 1. The term ‘organic’ is highly significant (P < 0.0001) and its citation frequency differentiates, at 95% confidence level, green designs A and E as ‘more organic’ than designs C and D, while the blue design B occupies an intermediate position, not clearly differentiated from either group.

Because of its association with nature, green has been traditionally related to organic products. 6 In fact, the expression ‘green supermarkets’ refers to stores that trade only in organic products 12 and terms such as ‘tasting green’ are used with the meaning ‘tasting an organic product’. 18 For the potential consumer, these expectations are enhanced by the image in design E that anticipates a more positive sensorial experience. At the other end, designs C and D, that may be appropriate for other products, are less effective in expressing those characteristics that consumers expect in organic yogurts and are unlikely to be chosen by consumers in the first place. Results obtained about the blue design (B) do not allow us to draw clear conclusions about its acceptance. In general, it is overall well rated, somewhat closer to the A and E labels than to C and D labels, but it tends to be ranked in intermediate positions, with lesser consensus among respondents and larger dispersion in their responses. For many attributes, its role cannot be statistically differentiated from any other label design.

It should not go unnoticed that the CATA questionnaire and the evaluation of the purchase intent were completed showing each design separately, while for the ranking experiment the five yogurts were shown together at the same time on the screen. For color evaluation, being able to see objects together or separately is not an inconsequential fact, as the human visual system has poor color memory, but high color discrimination when it can evaluate all products together at once. 44 This reinforces the importance of the relationship between the results of the first two sections of the survey with the last one.

The average rank, assigning a score from 1 to 5 to the position in the classification according to the organic concept, is linearly correlated with the average selection frequency of positive CATA terms (R 2 = 0.9561) and with the average purchase intent (R 2 = 0.9227) strengthening the relationship between the three sections of this study.

CONCLUSIONS

The present study was designed to investigate the relationship between color and the concept ‘organic’ by consumers, using yogurt as target product. Five original designs of yogurt labels were created, including the most frequent colors and color combinations used for organic yogurt in European supermarkets. Green labels were rated the closest to ‘organic’ and the top ranked in positive CATA terms like appealing or tasty. This clear preference was reinforced in the label with a suggestive image of a spoonful of yogurt on a green landscape probably because it elicited prospects of a better sensory experience.

According to CATA results, organic yogurt is viewed as a healthy, homemade and high‐quality product. Interestingly, consumers do not expect that an organic yogurt will be cheap, an attribute rarely selected and without significant differences between labels. On the contrary, almost half of the participants assume that an organic yogurt will be expensive, a characteristic that especially affects the black label, considered significantly more expensive than the rest.

The blue label was globally placed in a middle position; it is difficult to draw clear conclusions about this particular design other than the lack of a consensus between respondents. Finally, the unconventional multicolor design was perceived as the most innovative and the furthest from the idea of healthy.

At the time of purchasing, consumers prefer those designs with a closer resemblance to the organic concept. In turn, this is directly connected with the number of positive attributes that they associate with a particular label. In fact, numerical ratings of the purchase intent scores can be accurately predicted by a linear relationship with the average selection frequencies of those positive attributes. The agreement between the results obtained by different methods supports their credibility and reinforces the usefulness and versatility of the CATA methodology.

Knowing what the label design conveys to consumers provides useful information for companies to improve their communication strategies. For the label of an organic yogurt, being green is a very good starting point, but it is not enough. Instead of a single, solid green color, an image can reinforce this idea, taking advantage of the relationship between organic and the natural environment. If the image brings positive sensory expectations to consumers' minds, our success will be even greater. To what extent these conclusions apply to non‐European countries, with other cultural color associations, remains an open question that requires further research.
