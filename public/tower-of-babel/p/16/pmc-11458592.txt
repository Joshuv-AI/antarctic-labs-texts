Numerical study on the lateral behavior of pile groups in Toyoura sand using the hypoplastic constitutive model
Authors: Jiandong Li, Yuting Zhang, Guangzong Yuan, Tianqi Bian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A pile group under lateral loading is a complex pile‒soil-pile interaction problem. In this study, numerical analysis was used to investigate the lateral behavior of pile groups arranged in a 3 × 3 configuration with varying piles spacings. The hypoplastic constitutive model embedded into ABAQUS software through the UMAT subroutine was used to characterize the stress‒strain relationship of Toyoura sand. The effects of the pile spacing on the load‒displacement relationship of the pile group, the bending moment-depth relationship of each pile in the pile group, the deflection-depth relationship, and the soil resistance distribution were investigated. The results indicate that the lateral bearing capacity of the pile group decreases by more than 50% when the pile spacing is reduced from 7D to 3D. When the pile spacing is greater than 5D, the “edge effect” of the laterally loaded pile group gradually decreases, and when the pile spacing is greater than 7D, the “shadowing effect” can be negligible, and the wedge-shaped failure zone of the pile group occurs within a depth of 5D below the mud surface.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74494-2.

Introduction

Pile foundations, which bear the axial load from the superstructure and the lateral load caused by natural phenomena such as wind, waves, and earthquakes are the most widely used foundation forms in engineering practice1. In onshore structures, the lateral load on pile foundations is approximately 10–15% of the vertical load, while in coastal and offshore structures, the lateral load is approximately 25–30% of the vertical load2. The pile group under lateral loading is a challenging problem for pile‒soil-pile interactions, in contrast to a single pile that is laterally loaded3–7. A “group effect” exists for laterally loaded pile groups, where each pile in the pile group pushes the soil in front of it and creates a shear zone in the shallow soil. With increasing lateral load, the shear zone in front of each pile gradually expands, and the shear areas in front of piles at different positions overlap.

To study the lateral behavior of pile groups and single piles, 1-g model tests, centrifuge tests and full-scale tests were conducted in previous work8–10. Centrifuge tests and field tests are expensive and time-consuming, and it is impossible to carry out corresponding tests for all of the studied parameters, while 1-g model tests can only be used to draw some qualitative conclusions. The theoretical analysis of laterally loaded pile groups mostly adopts the wedge-shaped failure model in shallow soil, and the overlap of wedge-shaped failure zones of pile groups is examined to determine the impact of the “edge effect” and “shadowing effect.”4,11. The response of individual piles at different locations in the pile group is characterized by multiplying the p‒y curve of the lateral load acting on single piles by a reduction factor. When the reduction factor is less than 1, the commonly used methods for determining the reduction factor are the p-multiplier12,13, y-multiplier14, and modulus -multiplier11 methods. In addition, the analysis of the pile‒soil interaction by numerical simulation is efficient and allows for the immediate observation of the deformation mode of the soil surrounding the pile under lateral load15,16. However, the validity of the numerical analysis depends on the accuracy of the numerical model and the soil constitutive model. Therefore, to ensure that the numerical analysis can accurately characterize the response of the lateral load acting on pile groups, an advanced soil constitutive model is required to reflect the mechanical response of the soil in the pile‒soil interaction problem.

In this study, the stress‒strain relationship of sand was characterized using a hypoplastic constitutive model. The constitutive model was embedded into the ABAQUS software through the UMAT subroutine to study the lateral response of pile groups arranged in 3 × 3 piles with different pile spacings, and the finite element model (FEM) was validated via centrifuge tests. This study mainly analyses the influence of the pile spacing of the pile group on the load–displacement relationship curve, the deformation mode of the soil around the pile, the bending moment and the soil resistance of the pile.

Finite element model description

Model parameters for piles and soil

The pile has a diameter of 1.8 m, a length of 26.6 m, and a depth of 21.6 m below the mud surface. In the finite element model, the pile is made of elastic material, the elastic modulus is 26 GPa, and Poisson’s ratio is 0.317. The relative density of the Toyoura sand in the model is 70%. Unit tests were performed and showed that the mechanical behavior of sand exhibits state dependence and nonlinearity and is influenced by factors such as the stress history, stress level, and stress path18,19. To accurately characterize the complex mechanical behavior of sand, a hypoplastic model with intergranular strain was used to characterize its mechanical response. The hypoplastic constitutive model was first proposed by Kolymbas (1991) based on the theoretical mechanics and basic mechanical properties of sand20; this model was further improved by Von Wolffersdorff (1996), and a hypoplastic constitutive model with eight parameters was proposed21. Hypoplastic theory is based on continuum mechanics and abandons some concepts assumed in traditional elastic‒plastic mechanics, such as the yield criterion, hardening laws, plastic potential and flow rule. Finite element model

Figure 1 shows the mesh of the typical FE model used in this study. Due to the symmetry of the model of the pile group under lateral loading, only half of the model is built. The length, width and height of the soil domain in the FE model are 150, 75, and 54 m, respectively. The horizontal distance of the soil domain is greater than 20D, and the vertical distance of the soil domain is greater than 15D. Therefore, the influence of the boundary effects can be ignored. Both the sand and piles are simulated by C3D8R elements (i.e., “linear continuum brick elements” in the ABAQUS software) with enhanced hourglass control. In the soil domain, the displacements of the side surface are set to zero in the x and y directions. The symmetry plane is set as the symmetry boundary. The displacements in the three directions are all set to zero. The Coulomb friction method was used in the analysis of the behavior of the pile‒soil interface, and its friction coefficient was set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu =\tan {{2\varphi } \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{2\varphi } 3}} \right. \kern-0pt} 3}=0.4$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi$$\end{document} is the internal friction angle of the sand24. Separation between the pile and soil was allowed. The mesh in the finite element model is refined close to the piles, and the convergence analysis of the mesh shows that the error of the load‒displacement curve was within 2% for increasing the mesh of the model.

The finite element model consists of three analysis steps:

(i) Initialization of the in situ stress field of sand and the boundary conditions.(ii) The model pile is installed with the “wish in place” method and the pile‒soil interface’s behavior is activated.(iii) Lateral loads are applied to the pile groups via the displacement control method, while the presence of an elevated rigid cap fixed to the piles is considered with a multipoint constraint connecting the pile heads to a master node25.

Validation of the finite element model

A 3 × 3 pile group with 3D pile spacing in loose sand was used to validate the FE model in this study, and a centrifuge test was performed by McVay (1998)26. The pile in the centrifuge test is made of a 6061 aluminum tube, its width is 0.43 m, and its length is 13.7 m in the model, the elastic modulus is 68.9 GPa, and Poisson’s ratio is 0.3. Since the hypoplastic constitutive parameters of the sand used in the centrifuge test have not been calibrated, the mechanical response of the sand was characterized using the Mohr‒Coulomb (MC) constitutive model in the centrifuge test. The unit weight γ is equal to 14.05 kN/m3, the friction angle φ is equal to 0.35, and the relative density of the sand Dr is 36%. The other parameters in the finite element model are consistent with those in the centrifuge test, the details of which can be found in McVay (1998).

The load‒displacement curves of the FE model and the McVay centrifuge test are shown in Fig. 2(a). The FE model utilized in this study can be demonstrated to properly represent the load–displacement relationship of the pile group, and the error is within 2%. Therefore, the FE model adopted in this study is reasonable.

A centrifuge test of the lateral bearing capacity of a single pile under scour conditions performed by Chortis et al. (2020)27 was used to verify the accuracy and effectiveness of the hypoplastic constitutive model in finite element analysis. A case with a scour depth of 2D is selected for model verification. The established finite element model parameters are consistent with those used in the corresponding centrifuge tests. The load‒displacement curve is shown in the figure. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the hypoplastic constitutive parameters and the parameters of the centrifuge test used in the finite element model are taken from the study performed by Chortis et al. (2020).

Parametric study on the response of pile groups with different pile spacings

In this section, four pile groups were calculated based on the validated FE model using the hypoplastic constitutive model. The primary research variable in this study is the pile group spacing. Figure 3 shows the normalized load‒displacement curves of the 3 × 3 pile group with different pile spacings. When the lateral displacement is less than 0.025D, the lateral bearing capacity of the pile groups is largely influenced by the pile spacing, and as the lateral displacement increases, this influence becomes increasingly substantial. When the lateral displacement reaches 0.3D28, the lateral bearing capacity of the pile group with a pile spacing of 3D is less than half of that of the pile group with a pile spacing of 7D. With increasing lateral displacement, the pile spacing increases, and the magnitude of the lateral load increase becomes more significant. The main reason is that under a small lateral load, the wedge-shaped area in front of the pile at different positions is smaller, and the pile group effect is correspondingly smaller. As the lateral load gradually increases, the pile group effect gradually increases. An increase in the pile spacing significantly reduces the pile group effect.

Fig. 3Normalized load‒displacement curves of pile groups with different pile spacings.

Normalized load‒displacement curves of pile groups with different pile spacings.

Under the conditions of four different pile spacings, the curve of the change in the normalized lateral load with the pile spacing is shown in Fig. 4. When the lateral displacement is 0.05D, the pile spacing has little effect on the lateral load of the pile groups. The influence of the pile spacing increases with increasing lateral displacement. Under the same lateral displacement, the normalized lateral load increases linearly with the pile spacing. However, as the lateral displacement increases, the slope of the line also increases gradually. The results show that the group effect of the pile group is not fully exerted under a small lateral displacement load. As the lateral displacement load increases, the wedge-shaped overlap area between the piles at each position caused by the shielding effect and edge effect gradually increases.

Figures 5 and 6 show the pile‒soil displacement contours at the symmetrical surface and the top surface of the model when the lateral displacement of the pile group is 0.3D. Figure 5 shows the “shadowing effect” of the laterally loaded pile group under different pile spacings. The wedge-shaped failure zone of the pile is mainly within 5D depth below the mud surface. The wedge-shaped failure zone of shallow soil that surrounds piles of the same row overlaps when the pile spacing is narrow. With increasing pile spacing, the overlapping region of the wedge-shaped failure zone gradually decreases. When the pile spacing reaches 7D, the wedge-shaped failure zones in front of the piles at different positions are independent of each other. Therefore, the “shadowing effect” of the laterally loaded pile group is negligible when the pile spacing is greater than 7D. Figure 6 shows that a larger pile spacing on the mud surface corresponds to a larger soil mobilization range of the pile group. The overlapping soil area between the piles at different positions is almost zero when the pile spacing exceeds 5D. In other words, if the pile spacing exceeds 5D, the “edge effect” of the laterally loaded pile groups can be ignored.

Figures 7, 8, 9 and 10 show the deflection-depth relationships of the piles at various positions under different lateral displacements when the pile group has varying pile spacings. For convenience, the piles in the pile group are labeled L, M and R, representing the front row, middle row, and rear row along the direction of the lateral load, respectively, and O and C represent the piles in the middle position and the piles in the outer position in the same row, respectively. For all cases, the piles in the front row show the behavior of flexible piles, and the piles in the middle row and rear row exhibit obvious rotation points. When the lateral displacement is small, the difference in the deflection-depth relationship along the pile shaft of piles at different positions is slight. As the lateral load increases, the difference between the deflection curves of the piles at different positions gradually becomes significant. The position where the lateral deflection of the front row piles is zero is 15 m below the mud surface, and the positions where the deflection of the middle row and rear row of piles is zero are 17 m and 19 m below the mud surface, respectively. This shows that for the same depth, the lateral displacement of the front row of the piles is greater than that of the middle row of the piles and the rear row of the piles. The front row of the piles exhibits rigid rotation, while the rear row of the piles exhibits flexible deformation. The displacement of the piles at a depth below 15 m is close to 0.

In the ODB results file of the finite element model, the model pile can be divided into equal intervals along the depth of the pile shaft through the “Free Body Cut” function in the ABAQUS software, and the bending moment distribution along the shaft can be obtained by outputting the bending moment of each slice under different lateral loads29. Figures 11, 12, 13 and 14 show the curves of the bending moment versus depth of each pile at different positions in the pile group with varying pile spacings. Below the mud surface, the pile’s bending moment steadily increases with greater depth until it reaches its maximum value and then gradually decreases with the further increase in the depth until it reaches zero. For piles in the same row, the maximum bending moment is greater at the outside position than at the center position. If the pile spacing in the pile group reaches 3D or 4D, the maximum bending moment of the LO piles can reach twice that of the RO piles. For the front row and the rear row of the piles, with increasing pile spacing, the maximum bending moment decreases gradually, which means that the bearing capacity of the rear row piles gradually increases with increasing pile spacing. For piles at the same position, as the pile spacing increases, the maximum bending moment gradually increases, particularly for the front row piles. In addition, for these piles, the maximum bending moment is located at one-third of the depth of the pile below the mud surface, while for the rear row piles, the maximum bending moment is located almost at the center of the pile below the mud surface, and as the pile spacing increases, the maximum bending moment will eventually approach the mud surface. Therefore, increasing the pile spacing may increase the strain in the bearing capacity of the middle row and back row piles in the pile groups.

The soil resistance distribution in laterally loaded piles can be determined by taking the second derivative of the bending moment of the pile with respect to depth30. In this study, to determine the bending moment along the depth with greater accuracy, a sixth-order polynomial was fitted to the bending moment curve of the pile, as shown in Figs. 11, 12, 13 and 14. This approach yielded a lower error and improved the precision of our analysis31,32. Figure 15 shows the soil resistance-depth distribution of piles at different positions for a lateral displacement of 0.3D. For the front row piles, the soil resistance within a depth of 15 m below the mud surface is positive, and when the depth is greater than 15 m, the soil resistance is negative. The results demonstrate that an increase in the pile spacing leads to a corresponding increase in the maximum soil resistance, and the location of the maximum soil resistance gradually moves closer to the mud surface. Notably, for a pile spacing greater than four times the pile diameter (4D), the soil resistance of the rear row piles is significantly affected. However, for the pile groups with the spacings of 5D and 7D, the difference in the soil resistance among the rear row piles is relatively small.

Conclusions

In this study, the stress‒strain relationship of Toyoura sand was characterized using a hypoplastic constitutive model, and the effect of different pile spacings on the lateral bearing capacity of 3 × 3 pile groups with Le/D = 12 was studied via the finite element method. The main conclusions obtained in this work are as follows:

When the lateral displacement is less than 0.025D, the pile spacing has little effect on the lateral bearing capacity of the pile groups, and as the lateral displacement increases, the influence of the pile spacing on the lateral bearing capacity of the pile group becomes increasingly significant. When the lateral displacement reaches 0.3D, the lateral bearing capacity of the pile group with a pile spacing of 3D is less than half of that of the pile group with a pile spacing of 7D.
