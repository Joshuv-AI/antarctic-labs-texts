Emergence of the 2nd Law in an Exactly Solvable Model of a Quantum Wire
Authors: Marco Antonio Jimenez-Valencia, Charles Allen Stafford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As remarked by Boltzmann, the Second Law of Thermodynamics is notable for the fact that it is readily proved using elementary statistical arguments, but becomes harder and harder to verify the more precise the microscopic description of a system. In this article, we investigate one particular realization of the 2nd Law, namely Joule heating in a wire under electrical bias. We analyze the production of entropy in an exactly solvable model of a quantum wire wherein the conserved flow of entropy under unitary quantum evolution is taken into account using an exact formula for the entropy current of a system of independent quantum particles. In this exact microscopic description of the quantum dynamics, the entropy production due to Joule heating does not arise automatically. Instead, we show that the expected entropy production is realized in the limit of a large number of local measurements by a series of floating thermoelectric probes along the length of the wire, which inject entropy into the system as a result of the information obtained via their continuous measurements of the system. The decoherence resulting from inelastic processes introduced by the local measurements is essential to the phenomenon of entropy production due to Joule heating, and would be expected to arise due to inelastic scattering in real systems of interacting particles.

1. Introduction

Deriving the 2nd Law of Thermodynamics—the law of increase of entropy—from microscopic dynamics is a project dating back to Boltzmann. Whether using classical or quantum dynamics, the issue is that entropy is conserved under exact time evolution, taking all degrees of freedom into account. The 2nd Law arises because, in practice, a macrosopic observer is unable to keep track of all the information encoded in the microscopic degrees of freedom of a system, so that the information [1] needed to specify those degrees of freedom (and hence the system entropy) at later times continuously increases [2]. This scenario has been implemented for open quantum systems to describe the increase of the von Neumann entropy [3] associated with the reduced density matrix of a quantum system coupled to a macroscopic reservoir [4], and has led to a large body of work on entropy production in the quantum thermodynamics of such systems [5,6,7,8]. Contrary viewpoints concerning the role of information and irreversibility in the thermodynamics of quantum systems are discussed in [9,10].

However, the phenomenon of Joule heating, the key manifestation of the 2nd Law of Thermodynamics in electrical transport, which has been observed experimentally even in atomic-scale conductors [11,12,13,14], has not yet been derived from a fully microscopic quantum Hamiltonian (although phenomenologial approaches have been presented for instance in Refs. [15,16]), and is apparently not amenable to the reduced-state approach pioneered in Ref. [4] and reviewed in Ref. [8]. Computational approaches such as Ref. [17] touch upon the electron–phonon relaxation mechanisms leading to Joule heating, reminiscent of the reduced-state approach. A quasi-microscopic description is provided by the two-temperature model [18,19,20,21,22], wherein hot electrons with short relaxation times scatter inelastically on longer timescales with phonons of lower temperature that subsequently thermalize to an environment where this temperature can be measured.

In this article, we analyze the flow of entropy in a quantum electric circuit using a new unitary entropy current formula [23], which correctly accounts for the fact that the entropy of the universe, including all microscopic degrees of freedom, is conserved under the electrical transport process. In this microscopic description of entropy flow, inelastic scattering processes leading to thermalization and entropy production need to be explicitly included in the model in order to account for the 2nd Law. This is achieved by attaching a large number of floating thermoelectric probes [24,25] to a quantum wire that continuously measure the local temperature and chemical potential along the length of the wire, serving as sources of inelastic scattering and decoherence [26]. The information about the local nonequilibrium electron distribution in the wire obtained by these probes is not stored, but is injected as entropy into the quantum wire.

The physical picture that describes these measurements is the following: When a steady flow propagates into a reservoir, the relaxation processes that it undergoes lead to entropy production. Eventually, the distribution describing this flow into a reservoir relaxes until it is a linear perturbation of the equilibrium distribution at infinity. In other words, macroscopic measurements in electric circuits imply that the particles involved in the transport have undergone processes of relaxation into equilibrium with the leads they end up at, generating entropy. Examples of protocols for direct measurement of entropy in these types of systems can be found in [27,28,29,30]. These processes occur naturally in real circuits due to the fact that there are multiple sources of decoherence that in turn permit this relaxation. This implies that only energy and particle fluxes are conserved, a situation that is described by the conventional formula for heat currents, from which a description of entropy currents can be defined for quasi-reversible processes in accordance with thermodynamics by dividing heat currents by temperature T. It is an open question how to define entropy currents in general irreversible processes [31].

The unitary description of entropy flow utilized in the present article expands on the analyses of Refs. [23,32], which investigate persistent and microscopic flows of entropy in open quantum systems, and is to be contrasted with the conventional quantum formulas [33,34,35,36] describing the flow of charge and heat into macroscopic reservoirs and that implicitly assume complete thermalization of the outgoing distributions at infinity.

This paper is organized in the following way: A description of both the standard paradigm and the proposed (unitary) approaches for describing entropy currents and the differences in their consequences in terms of entropy generation is discussed in Section 2. In Section 3, following an explanation of these discrepancies, a proposal to recover the conventional entropy generation from the unitary description is tested with positive results in an infinite-chain system. The limit behavior of the disparity in the different approaches is explained as an end effect in Section 4 and a summary of the results can be found in Section 5. Appendix A provides a derivation of the unitary entropy current formula for independent quantum particles based on scattering theory. Three further Appendix B, Appendix C and Appendix D describe the basics of the Non-Equilibrium Green’s Functions (NEGF) formalism used in this work, the limit achievable by a single probe in the system in terms of entropy generation, and the quasi-classical resistance-network behavior in the chain as well as its dependence on the system parameters, respectively.

2. Discrepancies Between Unitary and Dissipative Entropy Flows

The introduction of unitary entropy currents brings about the challenge of reconciling it with previous descriptions of the flow of entropy and heat. Among these is the discussion of entropy production.

The conventional description of (dissipative) heat flow in a multiterminal system is given by the Büttiker–Sivan–Imry formula [33,34,35](1)Iα(ν)=1h∫dϵϵ−μαν∑βTαβfβ−fα, where α labels the reservoirs, h is Planck’s constant, and ν=0 corresponds to the particle current and ν=1 to the heat current, respectively, into reservoir α. Tαβ(ϵ) is the transmission function from reservoir β to α and fα(ϵ)=eβα(ϵ−μα)+1−1 is the Fermi–Dirac distribution function of reservoir α, where βα=1/kBTα.

Equation (1) implies that the electrical work supplied as electrons are transmitted between the reservoirs is converted entirely into heat, a process known as Joule heating:(2)∑αIα(1)=−∑αμαIα(0), where the LHS is the total rate of heat production and the RHS is the electrochemical power P supplied by the external electric bias. The total entropy production rate due to Joule heating is(3)∑αIα(1)Tα=Tα=T01T0−∑αμαIα(0)≥0, where positivity is ensured since particles flow spontaneously from high to low chemical potential. The conventional expression successfully describes the entropy production in the irreversible process of currents flowing between macroscopic reservoirs.

In contrast with this, the unitary flow of entropy governed by the Schrödinger equation leads to the conserved entropy current(4)IαS=1h∫dϵ∑βTαβsβϵ−sαϵ, where sα(ϵ) is the single-particle entropy contribution from a distribution fα at energy ϵ and IαS is the entropy current into reservoir α. In the case of Fermions sα(ϵ)=−kBfαlnfα+(1−fα)ln(1−fα), while for bosons sb(ϵ)=−kBfblnfb−(1+fb)ln(1+fb), where fb(ϵ)=eβ(ϵ−μ)−1−1 to describe, for instance, phonon or photon entropy fluxes if chemical potential is zero or conserved bosonic particles otherwise. Equation (4) is analogous to the multi-terminal Büttiker formula [33] for charge transport, and is justified within a local treatment of entropy flow [23,32] that keeps track of all microscopic degrees of freedom (see Appendix A for a derivation of Equation (4) using the scattering theory of quantum transport). A similar formula was derived in Ref. [31]. The key point to highlight in this context is that this expression takes into account a higher degree of knowledge of the system with respect to the previous case, by respecting and accounting for the exact unitary quantum evolution of all degrees of freedom in the system and reservoirs.

A central consequence of Equation (4) is that the total entropy is conserved(5)∑αIαS=0. It is important to note that this should not be unexpected if this formula is understood, the same way it is clear that the unitary evolution of a quantum system leads to an unchanging von Neumann entropy. To describe the currents via Equation (4) is to fully take into account degrees of freedom that are disregarded in a dissipative framework, one that assumes decoherence and thermalization of particles that come from a reservoir and end up in another one, without modeling its mechanisms explicitly. This assumption of decoherence is implicit [37] in the conventional current Formula (1) wherein an order of limits [38,39] is taken to prevent coherent backscattering in a noninteracting translationally invariant reservoir (see also Refs. [40,41,42] for modern mathematically rigorous exploration of this issue and its subsequent generalization to Hartree–Fock-type self-consistent interactions [43], which can be used as a basis to generalize our entropy flow formulas).

This discrepancy in the description of the production of entropy between the conventional dissipative entropy current and the exact unitary entropy current formulas is the main subject of this work.

3. Emergence of Entropy Generation: Unitary to Dissipative

If Equation (4) successfully describes the flow of entropy at the microscopic level, then there must be a way to recover the expression for the entropy production due to Joule heating, Equation (3), by leaving out the information contained in the degrees of freedom that are ignored in the conventional dissipative current formula, Equation (1). A key hypothesis of this work is that this discrepancy can be traced back to the mechanisms of decoherence and inelastic scattering, which are implicitly assumed in the derivation of Equation (1) to lead to complete thermalization of the outgoing fluxes into the macroscopic reservoirs.

3.1. Entropy Production in an Infinite Chain

As a tractable model to study the hypothesized emergence of irreversibility and entropy generation from the unitary entropy current, Formula (4), we consider a quantum wire modeled as an infinite tight-binding chain connecting source and drain reservoirs, coupled to N floating thermoelectric probes whose continuous measurements [24,25] of the local temperature and chemical potential serve as sources of decoherence and inelastic scattering in the wire (see Figure 1). The source and drain reservoirs are held at a common temperature T0 but with an electric bias μ1−μ2 between their chemical potentials.

The total Hamiltonian is(6)H=HS+HR+HP+Hcoupling, where(7)HS=t∑i=1N−1di†di+1+h.c. describes the central region of the wire, t being the tight-binding hopping matrix element between neighboring sites of the chain; the reservoirs are described by(8)HR=∑α=L,Rt∑j=1∞cj,α†cj+1,α+h.c., where α labels the reservoirs, L for the source on the left and R for the drain on the right. The labeling is such that the subscript (1,L) corresponds to the site at the interface between the left reservoir and the first site in the system, and (1,R) to the site at the interface between the right reservoir and the Nth site in the system counted from the first probe. The floating probes are also reservoirs described as metallic Fermi gases(9)HP=∑β=1NHPβ=∑β=1N∑k∈Pβεkβck,Pβ†ck,Pβ, and the couplings are described by(10)Hcoupling=tc1,L†d1+c1,R†dN+∑β=1N∑k∈PβVkβck,Pβ†dβ+h.c., where the first term corresponds to the coupling of the semi-infinite chains to the left and right and the second term corresponds to the coupling of the N probes to the corresponding sites in the system.

The semi-infinite source and drain reservoirs coupled to the ends of the N-site quantum wire can be treated by the NEGF technique, as discussed in Ref. [44], leading to self-energy terms at either end of the wire Σ1R(ϵ)=[Λ(ϵ)−iΓ(ϵ)/2]|1〉〈1| and Σ2R(ϵ)=[Λ(ϵ)−iΓ(ϵ)/2]|N〉〈N|, where the “Lamb shift” Λ and tunneling width Γ are given by(11)Λ(ϵ)=12ϵ+(ϵ2−4t2)12,ϵ<−2t,ϵ,|ϵ|<2t,ϵ−(ϵ2−4t2)12,ϵ>2t,(12)Γ(ϵ)=(4t2−ϵ2)1/2,|ϵ|<2t,0,otherwise. The tunneling-width matrices describing the coupling of the N floating probes to the quantum wire are given by(13)ΓPn(ϵ)=2π|n〉〈n|∑k∈Pn|VkPn|2δ(ϵ−εkPn), and may be evaluated in the broad-band limit as ΓPn=γp|n〉〈n| without loss of generality, with zero Lamb shift. The corresponding tunneling-width matrices for the source and drain electrodes are Γ1(ϵ)=Γ(ϵ)|1〉〈1| and Γ2(ϵ)=Γ(ϵ)|N〉〈N|, respectively.

3.2. Floating Probe Measurements

Each floating probe labeled by Pn performs a continuous measurement that takes electrons or holes from the system locally and replaces them with particles of the same average energy, but with uncorrelated phases, acquiring information about the electronic states in the wire and disposing of that information by injecting entropy into the system, not storing it. This mechanism of decoherence and entropy production is akin to continuous environmental monitoring as discussed by Zurek [6], and it puts this problem in the broader context of the thermodynamics of information [45,46]. Similar considerations have been used to derive the increase in entropy from measurements by local “demons” in classical statistical mechanics simulations [47].

These probes serve as neither sources of particles nor energy in the wire, and their chemical potentials and temperatures adjust to enforce this floating condition [24,25,26,48,49](14)IPn(0)=0andIPnE=0,∀n=1,…,N, where IPnE is the current of energy into Pn, which is equivalent to(15)IPn(ν)(μP1,…,μPN,TP1,…,TPN)=0,∀n=1,…,N,andν=0,1, where the currents defined in Equation (1) are calculated using NEGF [50,51]. Such floating probes are widely used in scanning potentiometry [52,53,54,55,56,57] and scanning thermometry [12,58,59,60,61,62].

The floating conditions define a set of 2N nonlinear equations in 2N unknowns, which are the chemical potentials and temperatures of the thermoelectric probes. This set of equations is intractable analytically and expensive to solve numerically, as it involves not only nonlinear equations but integrations as well.

An important simplification can be made by performing a Sommerfeld expansion of Equation (15), applicable when μα≫kBT0∀α as done in [63], leading to(16)IPn(0)=1h∑αTPnα(μ0)μα−μPn=0,n=1,…,N,(17)IPn(1)=1h∑αTPnαμ0μα−μPn22−π2kB26Tα2−TPn2=0,n=1,…,N, where μ0=12μ1+μ2 and terms involving Tαβ′(μ0) and higher derivatives have been dropped, valid to leading order in the electrical bias, as needed to correctly describe Joule heating.

The N equations (16) define a linear system for the N unknowns μPn, which can then be introduced as parameters for the N equations (17), which define another linear system on the N temperatures of the probes squared TPn2. This method then not only puts the 2N system of equations into two N systems, but linearizes them and avoids the necessity of integration.

It is important to highlight that the transmission functions appearing in Equations (16) and (17) are calculated exactly using NEGF, while the currents themselves are calculated in the Sommerfeld expansion, which is exact to leading order in the electrical bias and absolute temperatures.

Figure 2 shows chemical potential and temperature profiles along the quantum wire, illustrating the generic behavior found for all but the weakest probe coupling (in the cases of very low γp/t, 2kF oscillations of both the chemical potential and temperature were observed, as discussed in Refs. [48,64]), where the chemical potential drops nearly linearly between the source and drain contacts, and the temperature exhibits a parabolic maximum in the center, where the local distribution is maximally mixed.

3.3. From Unitary to Dissipative Entropy Current

Given the temperatures and chemical potentials of the floating thermoelectric probes, the unitary entropy current injected by a single probe may be calculated from Equation (4) (using the same assumptions and Sommerfeld expansion),(18)−IPnS=1h∑αTPnαμ0π2kB23TPn−Tα, and the total entropy production is the sum over all these contributions(19)S˙P=−∑nIPnS. The entropy injected by the probes due to their continuous measurements reflects a constant generation of entanglement between the electrons in the probes and those in the quantum wire, which models the generic inelastic scattering processes in a current-carrying wire wherein electronic degrees of freedom in the wire become entangled with environmental degrees of freedom, leading to dissipation.

In contrast, in the conventional framework of dissipative transport, the entropy production due to Joule heating, described by Equation (2), takes the form(20)T0S˙=I1(1)+I2(1)=I1(0)μ2−μ1=P.

Our hypothesis is that the entropy injected into the system by the probes due to their continuous measurements should approach the entropy expected due to Joule heating in the limit that the local thermalization processes introduced by the probes is sufficient to fully relax the outgoing distributions into the reservoirs. That is, as the number of probes and their coupling strength increases, the ratio of Equations (19) and (20) should approach unity(21)limNγp→∞T0S˙PP→1. Conventionally [33,34,35,38], the equilibration processes introduced explicitly in our model by the floating thermoelectric probes are implicitly assumed to take place within the reservoirs themselves. The role of coherence in transport in this context aligns with the discussion given in Ref. [65] (see Appendix D).

The central result of this article is the direct numerical verification of Equation (21) presented in Figure 3, which shows moreover that this ratio exhibits joint scaling, that is, many weakly coupled probes are as effective as few strongly coupled probes for the purpose of generating entropy. This fact may be of use in future analyses, as it is cheaper to simulate a few rather than many probes. However, there is a limit to this freedom, that is, a single probe, however strongly coupled to the system, can lead to the ratio, Equation (21), of at most 1/2 (see Appendix C), as indicated by the leftmost points of each data set in Figure 3, which represent the results for a single probe. It is notable that according to Figure 3, a very great number of inelastic scattering events are needed to completely thermalize the outgoing distributions and recover the full entropy due to Joule heating.

The predicted dependence of entropy production on the number of probes in this quantum transport problem appears to be consistent with results on entropy production due to a finite number of local “demons” coupled to a (classical) Ising spin chain [47].

4. 1/N Scaling: End Effects

The deficit of entropy injected by the microscopic probe measurements relative to that due to macroscopic Joule heating scales as 1/N for large N and its slope depends on γp/t, as can be seen in Figure 4. This fact can be qualitatively understood as an end effect, since the deviation from complete thermalization is greatest at the ends of the wire, as shown in Figure 5.

As shown in Figure 2, the local temperature is greatest in the central region of the quantum wire, where thermalization is maximum as the local distribution involves the greatest mixing of the source, drain, and probe distributions. The local temperatures near the ends of the wire are reduced, reflecting the greater admixture of source and drain distributions, respectively, at temperature T0=115K. This thermalization behavior is confirmed in Figure 5, where the equilibrium Fermi–Dirac distributions fPn(ϵ) of the probes (characterized by the measured values of TPn and μPn) are contrasted with the local non-equilibrium distributions fn(ϵ) in the quantum wire (see Ref. [25] and Appendix B for a definition and discussion of fn(ϵ)). The local distributions in the center of the wire are visibly closer to the corresponding equilibrium probe distributions. This figure also validates the expressions obtained through Sommerfeld’s expansion (16) and (17) in the current case, as the local non-equilibrium distributions satisfy the conditions in [25].

The equilibrium probe and non-equilibrium system distributions differ more the closer they are to the source and drain reservoirs, as equilibration is incomplete near the ends where few scattering events are not enough to fully thermalize the local distribution. In contrast, near the center of the wire, electrons will have experienced multiple inelastic scattering events such that propagation is akin to a random walk. This is consistent with the fact that a single probe is unable to fully thermalize the local distribution, no matter how strongly it is coupled to the system (see Appendix C), and the probes at or near the boundary are mixing a pure Fermi–Dirac distribution from its neighboring source or drain reservoir with the quasi-equilibrium distribution that has propagated down from the other side of the chain. It is also observed that the stronger the coupling to the probes, the more sequential is the transport from one side to the other, the more effective the probes are as scatterers and the less likely particles keep their coherence, which results in a local non-equilibrium distribution closer to the equilibrium distribution of the corresponding probes.

Figure 6 shows that the probes near the ends of the quantum wire inject the greatest amount of entropy due to the less complete thermalization of the distributions at the ends of the wire. (Note that when a floating probe performs such a measurement on a system that is in thermal and diffusive equilibrium, the steady-state rate of entropy injected by the probe goes to zero.)

The greater deviation from equilibrium of the local distributions near the ends of the wire shown in Figure 5 can be quantified [63] by comparing the entropy Sn of the local distribution with the corresponding local entropy SPn if the system was populated by the equilibrium distribution of its locally coupled probe. The latter is greater due to the maximum entropy principle, so the difference ΔSn=SPn−Sn can serve as a metric for the distance from local equilibrium, where(22)SPn=−∫dϵfPnlnfPn+(1−fPn)ln(1−fPn)gn and(23)Sn=−∫dϵfnlnfn+(1−fn)ln(1−fn)gn, where gn(ϵ)=〈n|A(ϵ)|n〉 is the local spectrum of the system, A(ϵ) being the spectral function (see Appendix B). Figure 7 shows that the local distributions are quantitatively much closer to local equilibrium for large probe coupling, as expected, and that the wire ends are significantly farther from local equilibrium than the central regions of the wire, for both weak and strong probe coupling.

5. Summary

We have investigated the emergence of irreversibility in an exactly solvable model of a quantum wire under electrical bias, coupled to N floating thermoelectric probes. Using a new microscopic formula for entropy flow inspired by Refs. [23,32], we show how entropy production and irreversibility arise due to the measurement process within a framework of unitary evolution wherein global entropy is conserved. Here we have succeeded in a project dating back to Boltzmann himself, to derive the 2nd Law of Thermodynamics from a microscopic Hamiltonian.

In our model, the probes serve the role of the “environment” of the quantum wire. The information obtained through the continuous measurements by the probes is injected as entropy into the quantum wire, representing entanglement between the electronic degrees of freedom in the probe reservoirs and those in the quantum wire. This process is akin to continuous environmental monitoring as discussed by Zurek [6], but in the context of a quantum scattering problem as opposed to projective quantum measurements. We find that as the number of probes and the strength of their coupling to the wire increase, the entropy injected approaches the value expected due to Joule heating in a macroscopic analysis of the system. We have thus derived this macroscopic law from a fully microscopic analysis.

The deficit in entropy production for finite N was shown to scale as 1/N due to end effects in the quantum wire. The local electron distribution near the ends of the wire remains further from a local equilibrium than the distribution in the central region, where many probes participate in the equilibration process. The entropy production due to probes near the ends of the wire is thus maximal.
