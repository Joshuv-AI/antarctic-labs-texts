High HIV incidence density among middle-aged and older populations in Luzhou, China: evidence from retrospective cohort study, 2018–2023
Authors: Yuqing Zhang, Hang Chen, Xiwei Sun, Chen Chen, Dan Ma, Yifan Zhao, Gang Wang, Hong Liu, Ticheng Xiao, Dinglun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHIV incidence is crucial for monitoring epidemics. This analysis of retrospective, programmatic HIV screening data aimed to estimate HIV incidence density within a repeatedly tested cohort, reflecting new infection detection among individuals who accessed testing services, and to explore associated demographic factors. We analyzed data from 1,863,802 individuals who accessed routine services across all city health facilities, tracking those initially seronegative. The overall incidence density was higher in males (1.21 per 1,000 person-years) than females (0.46), with peaks observed in males aged 70–79 (1.42) and females aged 50–59 (0.72). Cox regression identified demographic characteristics associated with higher incidence: male gender (HR = 2.69, 95% CI: 2.51–2.90), age 50–59 years (HR = 4.19, 95% CI: 2.83–6.21), and widowed/divorced status (HR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.81–2.22). Higher education was associated with lower incidence (HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.52–0.90). The findings highlight a substantial HIV burden among middle-aged and older males, with females in these groups having approximately half the incidence of males. Tailored interventions should focus on these populations, particularly individuals with lower educational attainment and those who are unmarried, divorced, or widowed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13599-7.Keywords: HIV incidence, Retrospective cohort study, Middle-aged and older adults, Expanded HIV testing

Introduction

The global HIV epidemic has undergone a profound demographic shift, with middle-aged and older adults now representing a growing proportion of new infections in diverse settings [1, 2]. According to the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) 2024 report, approximately 39.9 million people were living with HIV globally. The proportion of new infections among individuals aged 50 years and older is projected to increase from 28% in 2010 to 73% by 2030 [3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the number of older adults (aged ≥ 50 years) living with HIV doubled between 2000 and 2016, and it is estimated that by 2040, one-quarter of people living with HIV in the region will be aged 50 years and older [4]. This trend is also evident in China: the number of newly reported HIV/AIDS cases among individuals aged ≥ 50 years increased from 11,751 in 2010 to 51,856 in 2022. During the same year, among those aged ≥ 60 years, reported cases increased from 17,451 in 2015 to 27,004, accounting for 25.1% of all newly reported cases [5, 6].

The HIV epidemic has been traditionally monitored through case report systems, which played an important role in evaluating the epidemic trend of AIDS [7]. However, following the introduction of the 95-95-95 HIV testing and treatment cascade targets by UNAIDS [8], the adequacy of case report data for judging recent epidemic dynamics has come under increasing scrutiny. Expanded screening efforts, as recommended by UNAIDS, have the potential to identify more HIV cases, including those with long-standing, previously undiagnosed infections [9]. Research suggests that newly reported cases may also include these previously undiagnosed cases [10]. Consequently, changes in HIV prevalence over time may not accurately capture trends of HIV incidence or differences in incidence rates across population subgroups. It remains unclear whether observed declines in prevalence signify true reductions in new infections—indicating successful containment of the HIV epidemic—or merely reflect saturation in the detection of previously undiagnosed cases.

HIV incidence provides a more direct measure of ongoing transmission and is essential for monitoring epidemic trajectories and evaluating prevention efforts [11]. In recent years, China’s vigorous promotion of expanded HIV screening programs has generated multiple test records for a large number of participants [12]. This data reservoir allows for the application of cohort analytical methods to estimate incidence and characterize the epidemic.

Our study was conducted in Luzhou, a prefecture-level city in southwestern China with approximately 4 million residents, where an expanded HIV screening program was implemented from 2018 to 2023. Although newly reported HIV cases in Luzhou have declined steadily since 2019 [13], it remains unclear whether this reflects genuine transmission reduction or diminishing returns from sustained screening. Therefore, we analyzed retrospective data from this program, to estimate HIV incidence and indentify associated determinants of infections. This research provides critical insights into the current state of the epidemic, enabling more accurate assessment and development of targeted interventions to curb HIV/AIDS transmission.

Method

Study design, setting, and data source

This was a large‑scale, retrospective cohort study based on data from an ongoing, government‑led expanded HIV screening program implemented across all healthcare facilities in Luzhou, a prefecture‑level city in southwestern China with approximately 4 million residents, between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2023. Under this program, HIV testing was routinely offered to patients accessing clinical services and to individuals attending compulsory or voluntary health examinations. All test results and accompanying demographic information were prospectively recorded in a centralized electronic database maintained by the Luzhou Municipal Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The present study constitutes a secondary, retrospective analysis of this pre‑existing, de‑identified programmatic data.

Luzhou’s healthcare system comprises 4,520 medical and health institutions, including 144 hospitals (122 public, 22 private), 4,337 primary care facilities (community health centers/stations, township health centers, clinics, and village clinics), 28 specialized public health institutions, and 11 other health‑related organizations. All CDC facilities, all public hospitals, ten private hospitals, and 18 community health centers actively participated in the expanded HIV screening program and contributed data to this study. An information management system captured data in real time, with all participating facilities required to upload HIV test results and patient demographics to a central platform maintained by the Luzhou Municipal CDC using standardized electronic forms.

To enable longitudinal follow‑up while protecting privacy, each resident’s unique national ID number was irreversibly hashed and encrypted, allowing deterministic linkage of test records across visits and facilities. For research purposes, the Luzhou Municipal CDC extracted all HIV testing records and associated demographic data from the centralized electronic database. Prior to release, the CDC performed an irreversible one‑way hashing and encryption of the national ID numbers, generating a unique, non‑identifiable token for each individual while preserving the ability to link multiple test records belonging to the same person. All direct personal identifiers (e.g., name, address) were removed, and the research team received only the de‑identified hashed tokens along with corresponding test dates, results, and demographic information.

Participants and cohort definition

The source population comprised all individuals who underwent HIV testing within the Luzhou healthcare system between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2023. During this period, a total of 8,701,860 HIV tests were performed on 3,178,247 unique individuals.

For the present incidence analysis, we included individuals who met the following criteria: (1) underwent at least two HIV tests during the study period; and (2) had a negative result on their first recorded test. Individuals with only a single test (n = 1,314,445) and those with an initial positive result (n = 3,046) were excluded (Fig. 1). Seroconversion was defined as a documented change from HIV-negative to HIV-positive status during follow-up. For individuals who seroconverted, person-time was calculated from the date of the first negative test to the date of the first positive test. For all other participants, person-time was calculated from the first negative test to the date of the last negative test.

Laboratory procedures

All testing procedures strictly adhered to the National Diagnostic Criteria for HIV/AIDS (WS 293–2019) and followed the recommended algorithm outlined in the National Guideline for Detection of HIV/AIDS (2020) [14]. Venous or finger-prick whole blood samples were collected by trained personnel using aseptic technique. HIV testing followed a standardized two-step algorithm: an initial screening phase followed by confirmatory testing of all reactive specimens.

Initial screening: All specimens were initially tested using a high-sensitivity fourth-generation HIV-1/2 antigen/antibody enzyme immunoassay (Wantai HIV Ag/Ab ELISA Kit; Beijing Wantai Biological Pharmacy Enterprise Co., Ltd., China). All reactive samples were immediately retested in duplicate using the same assay. Specimens that were repeatedly reactive were classified as preliminarily positive and referred for confirmatory testing.

Confirmatory testing: All preliminarily positive specimens were transferred to the central reference laboratory at the Luzhou CDC for definitive confirmation. Confirmation was performed using a licensed Western blot assay (HIV Blot 2.2; MP Biomedicals Asia Pacific Pte Ltd., Singapore) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In cases where Western blot results were indeterminate or required further validation, supplementary nucleic acid testing was performed using either the COBAS® TaqMan HIV-1 Test v2.0 (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc., USA) or the m2000 RealTime HIV-1 Assay (Abbott Laboratories, USA).

A confirmed HIV-positive case was strictly defined by positive results on both initial screening and supplemental confirmatory assay(s). All testing was conducted under established internal and external quality control procedures.

Measures and definitions

Demographic characteristics were extracted from electronic medical records and the centralized testing database. Age was derived from national ID card numbers and categorized into eight groups: <20, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years. Gender was recorded as male or female. Educational attainment was classified into four levels: primary school or illiterate, junior high school, senior high school, and college or above. Marital status was categorized as unmarried, married, or widowed/divorced based on status at the time of testing. Occupation was classified according to standard national categories and included farmers, commercial and service workers, production and transport equipment operators, professionals and technicians, administrative and clerical support workers, senior managers and public officials, and others.

Incidence density was expressed as the number of new HIV infections per 1,000 person-years of follow-up, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated based on the Poisson distribution.

Data quality assessment and statistical analysis

To evaluate potential selection bias arising from our cohort definition, we systematically compared the demographic characteristics of individuals included in the incidence cohort (repeat testers, n = 1,863,802) with those excluded due to having only a single HIV test (single testers, n = 1,314,445). Between-group differences were quantified using risk differences (RD) and odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs (Supplementary Table S1). We further conducted a comprehensive assessment of missing data for all key demographic variables within the repeat tester cohort. Missingness was examined overall, by gender, and by follow-up interval (< 2 years, 2–<4 years, 4–<6 years) to identify temporal patterns and subgroup variations (Supplementary Figure S1). For all descriptive analyses, categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages based on non-missing observations.

For the main analysis, incidence density was calculated overall and stratified by gender, age group, marital status, educational attainment, and occupation, with crude and stratified estimates presented alongside 95% CIs. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify factors independently associated with HIV seroconversion. We fitted univariable and multivariable models, with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CIs reported. The multivariable model included gender, age group, marital status, educational attainment, and occupation as covariates. Analyses were conducted using complete cases, excluding observations with missing values for any covariate included in the model. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and was satisfied.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 was used for all tests.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the West China School of Public Health, Sichuan University (ethical approval number: Gwll2022063). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The HIV testing data were derived from a government-led public health program that promotes voluntary HIV testing among key populations. Within this program, healthcare providers conduct mobilization and pre-test information sessions, during which individuals are informed about the purpose and benefits of HIV testing, as well as their right to decline participation without any penalty. Verbal agreement to proceed with the test after this information session was considered as consent for the service. For the purpose of this secondary data analysis, the ethics committee granted a waiver of individual written informed consent, provided that all personal identifiers were removed from the dataset used for research.

Results

Cohort characteristics and overall incidence

A total of 1,863,802 individuals with at least two HIV tests were included in the retrospective cohort, contributing 5,069,813.7 person-years of follow-up between 2018 and 2023. During this period, 3,975 new HIV infections were diagnosed, yielding an overall incidence density of 0.78 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 0.76–0.81) and a point prevalence of 0.21% within the tested population.

The cohort comprised 823,457 males (44.2%) and 1,040,022 females (55.8%). The most common age group was 60–69 years (25.8% of males, 21.0% of females), while the majority of participants were married (79.8% of males, 81.9% of females), and had primary school education or were illiterate (63.6% of males, 67.6% of females). Farmers constituted the largest occupational group, accounting for over half of both male (53.3%) and female (52.2%) participants (Table 1).

Incidence density by demographic subgroups

The burden of new infections was distributed unevenly across demographic groups. A pronounced gender disparity was observed: the incidence density was 1.21 per 1,000 person-years in males (95% CI: 1.17–1.26), which was over 2.6 times higher than the 0.46 per 1,000 person-years in females (95% CI: 0.44–0.48).

The age-specific patterns of incidence differed between genders. Among males, incidence increased substantially from middle age onward, peaking in the 70–79 years age group at 1.42 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 1.30–1.55). Among females, the peak occurred earlier, in the 50–59 years age group, at 0.72 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 0.66–0.79).

Clear sociodemographic gradients were evident. Widowed or divorced individuals had the highest incidence densities in both genders, whereas married individuals had the lowest. There was a strong association between lower educational attainment and higher incidence; incidence was higher among those with primary school education or who were illiterate. Occupationally, farmers had among the highest incidence densities, whereas senior managers, public officials, and administrative workers had the lowest estimates across all comparisons (Tables 2 and 3).

Characteristics associated with HIV seroconversion

Cox proportional hazards regression identified demographic characteristics independently associated with HIV seroconversion (Table 4). In the multivariable model, male gender was associated with a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.69 (95% CI: 2.51–2.90) relative to female gender. Age was strongly associated with higher incidence, with the highest hazard observed in individuals aged 50–59 years (HR = 4.19, 95% CI: 2.83–6.21) when using the youngest age group (aged < 20 years) as the reference.

Discussion

This large-scale retrospective cohort study, drawing on data from an expanded citywide HIV screening program, provides estimates of HIV incidence density in Luzhou, China, from 2018 to 2023. Our primary finding is a marked concentration of newly detected HIV infections among middle-aged and older adults. Incidence density was substantially higher in males (1.21 per 1,000 person-years) than in females (0.46), peaked in males aged 70–79 years (1.42) and females aged 50–59 years (0.72). Higher incidence density was also observed among unmarried or widowed/divorced individuals and those with lower educational attainment. Importantly, these estimates reflect HIV detection within a testing cohort and may differ from true community incidence due to selection and testing frequency biases. These results delineate a clear epidemiological profile of subpopulations experiencing a high burden of newly identified HIV infection within this regional screening context, offering critical evidence to inform targeted public health strategies.

Our findings align with and quantitatively extend a growing body of evidence from China documenting a shift in the reported HIV epidemic toward older age groups. National surveillance and regional studies have consistently shown an increasing proportion of newly reported HIV cases among individuals aged 50 years and above [15–17]. This study advances this understanding by moving beyond case report and prevalence to calculate incidence density among individuals with a documented negative baseline test, thereby providing a more direct metric of active transmission risk within this demographic. The steep rise in incidence observed from age 40–49 onward in both genders, culminating in the distinct peaks noted above, underscores that middle and older adulthood represents a period of higher incidence in this setting. Increased case detection in older adults may partly reflect the identification of long-standing, previously undiagnosed infections [18, 19]. However, our cohort design—tracking individuals from a confirmed seronegative baseline—confirms that active seroconversion is occurring within this age group.

The particularly high incidence observed among men aged 70–79 years warrants specific attention. Previous studies have shown an increasing proportion of middle-aged and elderly men among newly reported cases [20]. From the perspective of HIV incidence density, our research has reached similar conclusions. Furthermore, to our knowledge, this is the first study to report such a pronounced peak in HIV incidence density in this oldest age stratum using longitudinal data. This pattern is not readily explainable by our data alone, as we lack behavioral information. However, it is consistent with prior research documenting continued sexual activity into later life, low levels of HIV risk perception among older adults, and low rates of condom use in this population [21–25]. The elevated incidence observed in these populations may be associated with social and behavioral factors documented in HIV social science research, including social isolation, stigma, negative self-image [26], or the absence of partner-based protective negotiation [27, 28]. These interpretations remain speculative, however, and require confirmation through studies incorporating individual-level behavioral data. Importantly, we must acknowledge that this age-specific peak may also be partially driven by selection bias inherent to repeat testing, as older adults with more frequent healthcare encounters are more likely to be captured in a longitudinal cohort.

Our study also reveals a substantial incidence burden among middle-aged and older women, with rates approximately half those of their male counterparts. This finding has been underemphasized in the existing literature on aging and HIV in China [29, 30]. The age pattern differs from that observed in high-prevalence settings such as sub-Saharan Africa, where young women bear the greatest burden [31]. While we cannot directly ascertain transmission direction or partnership characteristics from these data, the observed gender- and age-specific patterns provide an empirical basis for prioritizing couples-based HIV testing and prevention services in this demographic.

Sociodemographic factors emerged as strong correlates of HIV incidence. Unmarried and widowed/divorced individuals experienced approximately twice the hazard of infection compared to married individuals. This association was particularly pronounced among men during early follow-up. Although we cannot identify the mechanisms underlying this association, plausible pathways include differences in sexual network structure, reduced social support, and lower likelihood of regular HIV testing in the absence of partner-based health-seeking cues [32–35]. Educational attainment showed a clear inverse gradient with HIV infection. Individuals with college education or above had a 32% lower hazard of infection compared to those with primary education or less. This association may operate through multiple, interrelated pathways including higher health literacy, greater economic resources, and differential access to preventive services [36, 37]. These findings underscore that HIV incidence in this setting is not uniformly distributed but is concentrated among populations experiencing forms of social and economic disadvantage.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the absence of individual-level behavioral data—sexual practices, partner characteristics, injection drug use—precludes direct assessment of transmission modes or mechanisms underlying the observed demographic associations. While heterosexual contact, particularly commercial and non-marital partnerships, now predominates in China’s aging epidemic [6], our data can only identify affected populations, not transmission pathways. Future studies could address this gap by administering targeted behavioral surveys to a subsample of screening participants, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of transmission dynamics. Second, the cohort overrepresents healthcare-seeking individuals, limiting generalizability to those never accessing services. Crucially, the incidence density reported here reflects the rate of new detections within a specific testing cohort, and should not be directly interpreted as the true population-level transmission risk. Comparison with census data suggests our estimates are most applicable to older, rural, and less-educated populations—the very groups with elevated incidence—but community-based sampling would strengthen future estimates. Third, restricting to repeat testers may introduce selection bias. Older adults generally have more frequent healthcare visits requiring routine blood work, making them more likely to be included in our cohort. This selection bias may inadvertently inflate the observed incidence peaks in older age groups compared to younger populations who test less frequently. Differential testing frequency across age groups influences our estimates. More frequent testing among older adults narrows the observed seroconversion window and affects person-time denominators, potentially yielding more precise but systematically different incidence estimates compared to demographic groups with longer intervals between tests. Meanwhile, persons who seroconvert might avoid retesting, potentially underestimating incidence, while frequent testers may be more health-conscious. Our comparison of repeat and single testers (Supplementary Table S1) helps characterize this bias, and future linkage with treatment registries could quantify loss to follow-up effects. Finally, variability in data entry across facilities and voluntary self-report of education and occupation resulted in non-negligible missingness, particularly during early follow-up. Future program iterations could improve completeness through standardized electronic fields.

Despite these limitations, the descriptive epidemiology itself is actionable. Our findings provide an evidence‑based rationale for reorienting HIV prevention and testing services toward older populations—particularly older men and those with limited formal education. This can be achieved by integrating routine HIV testing into chronic disease management, senior health check‑ups, and community‑based aging services; designing age‑appropriate, low‑literacy health communication materials; and offering targeted HIV testing for unmarried, widowed, or divorced individuals. Future research should employ mixed-methods designs incorporating targeted behavioral surveys and qualitative inquiry to elucidate specific drivers of HIV acquisition in this growing demographic and could leverage linkage with treatment registries to quantify loss to follow-up effects or adopt community-based sampling to strengthen generalizability. In the interim, the clear epidemiological signal from this study offers a compelling rationale for immediate public health action.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the burden of newly diagnosed HIV infection is disproportionately concentrated among middle-aged and older adults. Incidence peaked distinctly among men aged 70–79 years and women aged 50–59 years, and consistently higher incidence was observed among individuals who were unmarried, widowed or divorced, and those with lower educational attainment. These findings delineate, with precision, the demographic contours of the HIV epidemic as currently detected through routine expanded screening. By quantifying incidence density within this aging population, our results provide an evidence-based foundation for targeting prevention and testing services to the groups with the highest incidence—particularly older men and those with limited formal education.
