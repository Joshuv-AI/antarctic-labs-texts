Trapeziometacarpal Arthrodesis With Tendon Interposition Resection Arthroplasty at the Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid Joint for Eaton Stage IV Trapeziometacarpal Osteoarthritis
Authors: Masanori Hayashi, Hiroyuki Kato, Shigeharu Uchiyama, Hiroko Iwakawa, Yo Kitamura, Jun Takahashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTrapeziectomy and proximal trapezoid excision with ligament reconstruction and tendon interposition or suspensionplasty is the standard method for the treatment of Eaton stage IV trapeziometacarpal (TMC) osteoarthritis (OA). However, these procedures have potential risks, such as residual metacarpophalangeal (MCP) hyperextension and thumb shortening. The aim of this study was to present the surgical technique and clinical results of TMC arthrodesis with tendon interposition resection arthroplasty (TIRA) at the scaphotrapeziotrapezoid joint.MethodsThirteen hands of 10 patients with stage IV TMC OA underwent TMC arthrodesis with TIRA between 2014 and 2024, with a minimum follow-up of 12 months. Pre and postoperative visual analog scale score, quick disabilities of arm, shoulder, and hand score, patient-rated wrist evaluation score, grip strength, and side pinch strength were evaluated. Pre and postoperative radiographs were obtained to evaluate bone union, the angles of the MCP joint, and the capitolunate angle.ResultsThe mean visual analog scale score decreased from 77 (SD: 13) before surgery to 10 (SD: 13) at follow-up. The mean disabilities of arm, shoulder, and hand score was 46 (SD: 17) before surgery and 22 (SD: 16) at the final follow-up. The mean patient-rated wrist evaluation score was 66 (SD: 21) before surgery and 15 (SD: 17) at the final follow-up. The mean side pinch was 2.7 kg (SD: 1.1) before surgery to 4.3 kg (SD: 1.2) at the final follow-up. Bone union at the TMC joint was achieved in all cases. The mean MCP joint angle was −20° (SD: 22°) before surgery and −4° (SD: 19°) at the final follow-up. The mean capitolunate angle was 0° (SD: 13°) before surgery and −2° (SD: 8°) at the final follow-up.ConclusionsCompared with preoperative conditions, TMC arthrodesis with TIRA at the scaphotrapeziotrapezoid joint achieved satisfactory results with substantial improvement. This novel procedure could be used for stage IV TMC OA.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic IV.Key words: Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid osteoarthritis, Tendon interposition resection arthroplasty, Thumb metacarpophalangeal hyperextension, Trapeziometacarpal arthrodesis, Trapeziometacarpal osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis (OA) of the trapeziometacarpal (TMC) joint is one of the most common causes of pain around the hand and one of the most common targets for hand surgery.1 According to the Eaton-Littler classification, TMC OA with concomitant scaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT) OA is classified into Eaton stage IV.2 Katzel et al3 reported the presence of STT OA in 64% of patients with TMC OA. Until recently, trapeziectomy and proximal trapezoid excision with ligament reconstruction and tendon interposition (LRTI) or suspensionplasty have been recommended in the treatment of Eaton stage IV OA.4, 5, 6, 7 However, these procedures have potential risks, such as residual metacarpophalangeal (MCP) hyperextension and thumb shortening.8 Trapeziometacarpal arthrodesis provides excellent pain relief and greater pinch strength in patients with stage II or III TMC OA but is generally contraindicated for patients with stage IV TMC OA.9 Despite this contraindication, some authors have reported the outcomes of arthrodesis in patients with stage IV TMC OA.10, 11, 12, 13, 14 However, in these reports, the presence of STT OA symptoms was not clearly observed.

We recently performed an original technique for the surgical treatment of stage IV TMC OA with symptoms in both the TMC and STT joints based on TMC arthrodesis with resection of the distal pole of the scaphoid with tendon interposition to maintain a stable thumb. This study reports the surgical technique and clinical results of TMC arthrodesis with tendon interposition resection arthroplasty (TIRA) with the use of the palmaris longus (PL) tendon at the STT joint in patients with symptomatic stage IV TMC OA.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the institutional review board of Shinshu University Hospital. A total of 13 hands in 10 patients (one male and nine females) with stage IV TMC OA underwent TMC arthrodesis with TIRA at Shinshu University Medical Hospital between 2014 and 2024. In our study, the term sex is used to indicate the biological classification of participants as male or female, based on medical records. Information on gender identity was not collected. All patients reported severe pain at the thumb base and radial side of the wrist, and all patients also exhibited difficulty performing daily activities before surgery. The mean age of the patients was 66 years (range: 55–86 years). The average follow-up was 50 months (range: 12–87 months). Prior to surgery, all patients received appropriate conservative treatment for at least 6 months. This included hand therapy involving exercises or education aimed at achieving a more stable thumb opposition position, the use of a custom-made or prefabricated orthosis, administration of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or steroid injections to the TMC or STT joint. Surgeries were usually performed at least 3 months after steroid injections. Indications for TMC arthrodesis with TIRA include provocation of severe pain at the thumb base during pinch motion and the radial side of the wrist during wrist flexion–extension or radial–ulnar deviation motion, tenderness at the TMC and STT joints, osteoarthritic changes in both joints on radiography, and transient pain relief at the thumb base and radial side of the wrist by separate injection of 1% lidocaine into the TMC and STT joints under fluoroscopic control.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by one hand surgeon with level IV experience according to Tang.15 A longitudinal incision was made dorsally over the TMC and STT joints. The area between the abductor pollicis longus tendon and the extensor pollicis brevis tendon was exposed (Fig. 1A). The TMC and STT capsules were incised longitudinally, and the joint surfaces were exposed. The remaining cartilage and eburnated subchondral bone were removed from the TMC joint surface to expose the cancellous bone, and the distal articular surface of the scaphoid was removed to less than 3 mm. The TMC joint was fixed with a 2.0 mm T-shaped plate (Modular Hand System or VA Locking Hand System, Synthes) with 20° to 30° radial abduction and 30° palmar abduction. Cancellous bone chips from the iliac crest were impacted into the TMC joint space. The PL tendon harvested from the ipsilateral or contralateral side was rolled up like an anchovy filet and was subsequently interposed in the gap between the scaphoid and trapezium (Fig. 1B). When the bilateral PL tendons are deficient, the plantaris or half-slip of the flexor carpi radialis tendons can be used. After surgery, the thumb and the wrist were immobilized in a thumb spica cast in the neutral position for 4 weeks, followed by rehabilitation, including active and passive thumb and wrist motion.

Outcome evaluation

Before and after surgery, the pain intensity was assessed with a 100-point visual analog scale score.16 For the functional evaluation, the disabilities of the arm, shoulder, and hand and patient-rated wrist evaluation scores were used.17,18 Grip strength was quantified with a Jamar dynamometer (Sammons Preston Rolyan). The side pinch strength was quantified with a pinch meter (Sakai). The active range of motion of the wrist joint and thumb MCP and interphalangeal joints in flexion and extension was measured with a goniometer. After surgery, the Kapandji opposition score was used to evaluate thumb opposition.19 Posteroanterior and lateral radiographs of the thumb in the resting position and wrist were obtained for pre and postoperative evaluations. The stages of TMC OA and bone union were assessed on thumb radiographs. The angles of the MCP joint were measured according to a previous report.20 Negative angles denote hyperextension. On the wrist radiographs, STT OA was classified via the modified Eaton–Glickel classification.21 Dorsal intercalated segment instability (DISI) deformities were evaluated by measuring the capitolunate (CL) angle on true lateral wrist radiographs. Positive angles denoted dorsal–volar lunate angulation, and negative angles denoted dorsal lunate angulation. A DISI deformity was defined as a CL angle < −15°.22 Postoperative complications were determined by a combination of chart review, radiographic review, and examination. Patients were asked about their satisfaction with the operation at the final follow-up, according to our previous report.23 Patients who underwent bilateral surgery were asked to provide an answer for each hand.

Results

Clinical findings

The mean visual analog scale score decreased from 77 (SD: 13) before surgery to 10 (SD: 13) at the follow-up. The mean disabilities of the arm, shoulder, and hand score changed from 46 (SD: 17) before surgery to 22 (SD: 16) at the final follow-up. The mean patient-rated wrist evaluation score changed from 66 (SD: 21) before surgery to 15 (SD: 17) at the final follow-up. There were no changes in grip strength in most patients. The mean side pinch strength changed from 2.7 kg (SD: 1.1) before surgery to 4.3 kg (SD: 1.2) at the final follow-up. The mean wrist joint angles, MCP joint angles, and interphalangeal joint angles did not show remarkable changes after surgery. The postoperative Kapandji opposition scores were as follows: 10 points, eight hands; 9 points, three hands; 7 points, one hand; and 4 points, one hand (Table 1). Following surgery on 13 hands, the patients were satisfied with the results.

Radiological findings

Among the 13 cases, two had stage I, two had stage II, and nine had stage III STT OA before surgery. Bone union at the TMC joint was achieved in all cases within at least 3 months. The space between the distal end of the scaphoid and the proximal end of the trapezium was preserved at the follow-up. The mean before surgery and final postoperative MCP joint angles were −20° (SD: 22) and −4° (SD: 19°), respectively. The mean before surgery and final postoperative CL angles were 0° (SD: 13°) and −2° (SD: 8°), respectively (Fig. 2A–C). Although DISI deformity was found in only one patient before surgery (CL angle: −22°), the angle did not change after surgery (CL angle: −21°) (Patient No. 8, right side). The other patients did not develop DISI deformities after surgery.

Complications

Two hands in two patients developed impingements of the STT joints caused by spare resection of the distal pole of the scaphoid after surgery (Patient No. 3, left side; Patient No. 7, right side). Those hands were treated with the re-resection of impingement sites through the same dorsal approach as the first operation within 1 year.

Discussion

This study revealed that TMC arthrodesis with TIRA achieved satisfactory results with substantial improvement compared with preoperative conditions. The results were comparable with the outcomes of surgery for stage II, III, or IV TMC OA presented in previous reports.4,23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29 The MCP joint angle changed from extension to flexion in most cases at the final follow-up. The DISI deformities did not develop in any cases after surgery.

We placed our focus on the decision for the indication of surgical treatment of stage IV TMC OA. It is particularly important to evaluate pain at the STT joints caused by wrist motion.30 Cobb et al24 described the use of diagnostic injections under fluoroscopic control for confirmation of stage IV TMC OA. We routinely perform separate injections into the TMC and STT joints to verify decreased pain intensity during thumb and wrist motion. In this study, all patients experiencing both symptomatic TMC and STT OA and intra-articular lidocaine injection experienced temporary pain relief; however, our cohort study of the general elderly generation revealed that the prevalence of TMC OA and STT OA was 17.3% and 5.3%, respectively, and most of them were asymptomatic.31,32 Therefore, this procedure is contraindicated when either the TMC or the STT joint does not exhibit symptoms or does not effectively respond to injections.

Although there is no consensus on the optimal surgical treatment of stage IV TMC OA, trapeziectomy and proximal trapezoid excision with LRTI or suspensionplasty have been generally accepted as the standard method.4, 5, 6, 7 However, these procedures have potential risks of complications, such as residual MCP hyperextension and thumb shortening.8,33, 34, 35 Barron and Eaton25 reported that double interposition arthroplasty with minimal trapezial resection is a comparable procedure to complete trapeziectomy with or without tendon interposition and protects against thumb shortening. Two other authors reported that limited resection arthroplasty of the TMC and the STT joints for stage IV TMC OA is a viable alternative to existing surgical treatments.24,26 Although the authors reported good results following these procedures, they did not mention the possibility of residual MCP hyperextension. We previously reported that MCP joint motion improved from extension to flexion during the performance of activities of daily living tasks after TMC arthrodesis.20 In this series, the mean angle of the MCP joint in the resting position improved from hyperextension to flexion, and no change in the active range of motion of the MCP joint was observed after surgery, which is consistent with previous reports.20,23

Trapeziometacarpal arthrodesis has been proven to provide effective pain relief, stability, and strength for patients with stage II and III TMC OA.13,23,27, 28, 29 Moreover, STT OA might progress and become symptomatic following the treatment of TMC OA with TM arthrodesis.36 Therefore, if TMC arthrodesis is to be performed for TMC OA with symptomatic STT OA, some procedures must be performed on the STT joint to prevent worsening of STT symptoms. Various procedures have been used for isolated STT OA, such as scaphotrapeziotrapezoid arthrodesis, distal scaphoidectomy with or without a tendon or pyrocarbon implant, trapeziectomy with or without LRTI, and trapeziectomy and proximal trapezoid excision with LRTI.37 Brouwers et al38 reported that neither the resection height nor the DISI deformity that developed after distal scaphoidectomy with tendon interposition was considerably correlated with functional scores. Iida et al39 analyzed the correlation between resection height and radiographic outcomes and concluded that distal scaphoidectomy greater than 3 mm may result in postoperative carpal malalignment. In this series, we resected the distal pole of the scaphoids as little as possible. However, two patients developed impingements in the STT joints after surgery. Meticulous resection of the scaphoid while avoiding excessive resection is necessary in the first surgery. We also inserted the PL tendon in the STT joint as an additional procedure. TIRA using the flexor carpi radialis or the PL tendon for isolated STT OA has been reported to be less technically demanding and to yield satisfactory results.40, 41, 42 It has also been reported that compressive load induces fibrocartilage development in the tendon.43 The inserted tendons may change into fibrocartilaginous tissue, contributing to the stability of the STT and potentially preventing the progression of DISI deformity.

Nonunion is one of the most frequent complications following TMC arthrodesis.14,44,45 Smeraglia et al46 reported that bone union is not necessary for a good outcome and that the creation of a new fibrous TMC joint seems to protect against secondary STT arthritis. However, nonunion sometimes causes implant failure, requiring revision surgery and placing a greater burden on patients.47 We previously reported that bone graft and robust fixation via a T-shaped plate achieved a 100% union rate.23 Matsuo et al48 reported that bone union was achieved with bone grafts, regardless of the type of implant or the source of the bone graft. Stage IV TMC OA is sometimes accompanied by a reduced thickness of the trapezium and less bone stock because of TMC and STT OA. Therefore, we routinely perform bone grafting for stage IV TMC OA. In this series, bone union was achieved in all cases.

One of the limitations of our study is that it was a retrospective study, and the number of patients was small. The sample included three patients who underwent surgery on both hands, all of whom were subjected to the same procedure on the contralateral side. A second limitation is that the lack of a control group prevents comparisons of this procedure with other techniques. Finally, this study included two patients whose follow-up periods were as short as 1 year.

This study revealed that TMC arthrodesis with TIRA is a satisfactory procedure for patients with stage IV TMC OA. The results from our preliminary case series suggest that TMC arthrodesis with TIRA can be used as an alternative technique for treating symptomatic stage IV TMC OA, especially with MCP hyperextension.
