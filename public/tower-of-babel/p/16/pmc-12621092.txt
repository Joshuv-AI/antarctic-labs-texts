The transcription factor BnaWRKY75 enhances resistance to the necrotrophic pathogen Sclerotinia sclerotiorum by promoting salicylic acid biosynthesis in oilseed rape
Authors: Dongxiao Liu, Jialin Fan, Yi Ye, Yu Liu, Sichao Ren, Wenjing Lei, Xingrui Zhang, Ancheng He, Junqiang Xing, Qinfu Sun, Li Lin, Youping Wang, Jian Wu, Monica Höfte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, a devastating necrotrophic fungus causing stem rot in oilseed rape, results in significant global yield losses. Deciphering plant–S. sclerotiorum interactions is crucial for disease control. Here, we report that the transcription factor BnaWRKY75 positively regulates resistance to this pathogen. BnaWRKY75 was the most highly induced WRKY gene upon S. sclerotiorum infection. Overexpressing BnaA10.WRKY75 in both Arabidopsis and oilseed rape enhanced resistance to S. sclerotiorum, whereas BnaWRKY75 knockout plants exhibited reduced resistance. RNA sequencing and DNA affinity purification sequencing showed that the isochorismate synthase gene BnaICS1, a key gene required for salicylic acid (SA) biosynthesis, is a potential target of BnaA10.WRKY75. Yeast one-hybrid, dual-luciferase, and electrophoresis mobility shift assays indicated that BnaA10.WRKY75 directly binds to the BnaCnn.ICS1 promoter to activate its transcription, thereby promoting SA biosynthesis and activating SA-dependent defenses. Genetic analysis in Arabidopsis confirmed ICS1 functions downstream of BnaA10.WRKY75. SA treatment significantly enhanced resistance to S. sclerotiorum in oilseed rape, further highlighting the importance of SA in defense against this pathogen. Finally, we observed differences in transcriptional regulation among BnaICS1 homologous genes in allotetraploid oilseed rape. Our ﬁndings suggest that the BnaWRKY75–BnaICS1 module regulates SA-dependent resistance to S. sclerotiorum, expanding our understanding of plant immune responses to necrotrophic pathogens.Keywords: Brassica napus, ICS1, necrotrophic pathogen, salicylic acid, sclerotinia stem rot, WRKY75

Introduction

Oilseed rape (Brassica napus) is one of the most important oil crops worldwide. Sclerotinia stem rot (SSR), caused by the necrotrophic fungus Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, is a devastating disease affecting oilseed rape. In China, SSR contributes to annual yield losses of 10–20% in oilseed rape. In addition to reducing yields, SSR affects the oil and fatty acid contents of oilseed rape, and thereby affects its quality. Hence, developing new oilseed rape varieties with enhanced disease resistance is an important objective. However, breeding SSR-resistant oilseed rape is challenging because of the limited availability of immune or highly resistant germplasms. Furthermore, SSR resistance is a complex quantitative trait governed by multiple quantitative trait loci, and the mechanism underlying this quantitative resistance remains unclear (Wu et al., 2019; Ding et al., 2021). Therefore, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive analysis of the resistance mechanisms associated with SSR resistance, to provide a robust theoretical foundation for breeding SSR-resistant oilseed rape.

Plants exhibit a set of innate immune responses during their interactions with various pathogens, including complex mechanisms for the perception, transduction, and exchange of signals (Verhage et al., 2010). Disease resistance mechanisms consist of two-tiered defense responses. The first layer is known as pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP)-triggered immunity (PTI), in which PAMPs and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are perceived by plant pattern recognition receptors, followed by the activation of defense responses. The second layer, effector triggered immunity (ETI), involves plant resistance proteins that interact with pathogen avirulence effector proteins, thereby activating defenses (Jones and Dangl, 2006).

Downstream of PTI or ETI activation, various plant hormones play pivotal roles in triggering the plant immune signaling network (Pieterse et al., 2012). Salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), and ethylene (ET) are the most important phytohormones regulating plant defense responses. Which hormone predominates in an immune response depends on the type of pathogen. In general, SA contributes to resistance to biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens, whereas JA and ET predominantly promote resistance to necrotrophic pathogens (Glazebrook, 2005; Bari and Jones, 2009).

Several lines of investigation point to a key role for SA in the defense against S. sclerotiorum, even though it is a necrotrophic pathogen. SA levels and expression of the SA signaling pathway marker gene pathogenesis-related gene 1 (PR1) both increase in oilseed rape following S. sclerotiorum infection (Nováková et al., 2014). Arabidopsis mutants lacking the SA perception gene NONEXPRESSOR OF PR1 (NPR1) displayed increased susceptibility to S. sclerotiorum compared with wild-type plants (Guo and Stotz, 2007). The lesion mimic mutation lmm1 confers basal resistance to S. sclerotiorum in oilseed rape via a SA-dependent pathway (Yu et al., 2023). Furthermore, S. sclerotiorum secretes the cerato-platanin protein 1 (SsCP1) virulence factor, which interacts with PR1 in planta and triggers plant defense responses through the SA signaling pathway. Overexpressing PR1 enhanced resistance to S. sclerotiorum in Arabidopsis (Yang et al., 2018). During the early stage of infection, S. sclerotiorum secretes integrin-like protein (SsITL), which inhibits SA accumulation by interacting with a calcium-sensing receptor, thereby facilitating infection (Tang et al., 2020). However, although SA appears to be involved in defense against S. sclerotiorum, the molecular mechanism underlying the increased SA content following S. sclerotiorum infection has not yet been elucidated.

Members of the WRKY family, an important superfamily of transcription factors (TFs) found in higher plants, play pivotal roles in plant stress responses. WRKY TFs typically consist of two domains: the highly conserved amino acid sequence WRKYGQK and the zinc-finger-like motif CCHH or CCHC (Eulgem et al., 2000; Bakshi and Oelmüller, 2014). These TFs bind to W-boxes [TTGAC(C/T)] in the promoter regions of their target genes, thereby modulating their expression (Eulgem et al., 2000; Rushton et al., 2010).

WRKY TFs play crucial roles in the transcriptional reprogramming associated with plant immune responses in various plant species (Eulgem and Somssich, 2007). For instance, several WRKY TFs, such as Arabidopsis WRKY8, WRKY18, WRKY33, WRKY40, WRKY60, WRKY70, and WRKY75, play positive or negative roles in immune responses (Li et al., 2004; Xu et al., 2006; Zheng et al., 2006; L. Chen et al., 2013; X. Chen et al., 2013). WRKY33 is a key positive regulator of resistance to the necrotrophic fungus Botrytis cinerea in Arabidopsis. Following infection, WRKY33 is released and activated, subsequently binding to the promoters of ACS2 and ACS6 to activate ET biosynthesis and to the promoter of PAD3 to activate camalexin biosynthesis (Mao et al., 2011; Li et al., 2012). In oilseed rape, WRKY33 is a S. sclerotiorum-responsive gene whose encoded protein positively regulates resistance to this pathogen by increasing the expression of phytoalexin biosynthesis-related genes (Wang et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2018; K. Zhang et al., 2022). However, whether other WRKY TFs play roles in the intricate interaction between oilseed rape and S. sclerotiorum, and if so, how they govern defense responses, remains to be elucidated.

In this study, by analysing both overexpression and CRISPR/Cas9-based knockout mutants, we identified BnaWRKY75 as a pivotal TF that positively regulates resistance to S. sclerotiorum in oilseed rape. We demonstrate that BnaWRKY75 increases SA biosynthesis by inducing the transcription of the isochorismate synthase gene ICS1/SID2. These findings offer valuable insight into the important role of SA in conferring resistance to the necrotrophic pathogen S. sclerotiorum in oilseed rape and the regulatory mechanisms responsible for the increased SA content following infection with S. sclerotiorum.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The oilseed rape pure inbred line J9712, provided by Prof. Yongming Zhou from Huazhong Agricultural University, was used for transformation. All transgenic plants were cultivated in an experimental ﬁeld at Yangzhou University, Jiangsu, China or in a climate chamber under a 20 h light (∼300 µmol m−2 s−1)–4 h dark photoperiod at 22 °C with a relative humidity of 65%.

All Arabidopsis mutants and transgenic plants used in this study were in the Col-0 background. The wrky75–25 mutant and WRKY75RNAi line, kindly provided by Prof. Hongwei Guo from Southern University of Science and Technology, were described previously (Guo et al., 2017). The ics1 (SALK_093400C) mutant was obtained from The Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center. Arabidopsis seeds were incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 2 d, sown on nutrient soil, and cultivated in a growth chamber under a 16 h light (∼300 µmol m−2 s−1)–8 h dark photoperiod at 22 °C during the day and 20 °C at night, with a relative humidity of 60%.

Plasmid construction and transgenic plant generation

To generate the overexpression construct, the full-length coding sequence (CDS) of BnaA10.WRKY75 (BnaA10g20210D) was amplified from J9712, digested with PacI and AscI, and cloned into the plant binary vector pMDC83 under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter.

To knockout BnaWRKY75 via CRISPR/Cas9, multiplex gene editing was performed based on the tRNA-processing system described by Xie et al. (2015). The vectors pRGEB32 and pGTR were provided by Prof. Kabin Xie from Huazhong Agricultural University. To adapt this CRISPR system for gene editing in oilseed rape, the pRGEB32 vector was modified by replacing the RNA polymerase III promoter controlling polycistronic tRNA–gRNA expression from the rice U3 snoRNA promoter with the oilseed rape BnU6-29 snoRNA promoter, that is, pRGEB32-Bn. Four speciﬁc sgRNAs targeting BnaWRKY75 were designed using CRISPR-P 2.0 (http://cbi.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/). For mutant identification, the target regions of BnaWRKY75 were amplified with gene-specific primers and sequenced.

To construct the promoter::GUS expression vector, a ∼1800 bp sequence upstream of the BnaA10.WRKY75 translation start codon was cloned from J9712 using specific primers. The promoter was then inserted into the pBI101 vector at the BamHI restriction site via homologous recombination (ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

The recombinant plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. To generate transgenic oilseed rape, the A. tumefaciens-mediated hypocotyl method was employed as previously described (Liu et al., 2021). To generate transgenic Arabidopsis, the A. tumefaciens-mediated floral dip method was performed as described by Zhang et al. (2006). BnaA10.WRKY75 was overexpressed in both wild-type Col-0 and ics1 plants, generating overexpression (OE)-BnaA10.WRKY75 and OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 ics1 lines, respectively.

Subcellular localization

The full-length CDS of BnaA10.WRKY75 was inserted into the N-terminus of enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) of pBI221 to construct the recombinant plasmid 35S:WRKY75-eGFP. mCherry fused to the nuclear localization signal SV40 was used as a nuclear marker. The fusion constructs were co-transfected into Arabidopsis protoplasts as previously described (Yoo et al., 2007). After a 12–16 h transfection period, fluorescence signals were captured under a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany) with specific excitation/detection wavelengths for GFP (488 nm/510–550 nm) and mCherry (587 nm/607–650 nm).

Histochemical β-glucuronidase staining

Histochemical β-glucuronidase (GUS) staining was performed as previously described (Lin et al., 2022). Samples were incubated overnight at 37 °C in GUS staining buffer (10 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM potassium ferricyanide, 0.5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 10 mM sodium phosphate, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 0.5 mg ml−1 X-gluc, pH 7.2). After incubation, the samples were bleached in 75% ethanol and photographed using a stereomicroscope equipped with a digital camera (EZ4W, Leica, Bensheim, Germany).

Assessment of sclerotinia stem rot resistance in oilseed rape and Arabidopsis

The S. sclerotiorum isolate SS-1 was cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA) at 23 °C in the dark. The leaves and stems of oilseed rape were inoculated as described previously (Wu et al., 2013). For stem inoculation, plants were inoculated with S. sclerotiorum to assess resistance at the end of flowering under field conditions. Approximately 8–10 plants per replicate were inoculated at a level of 50 cm aboveground using mycelial agar plugs (7 mm in diameter). Each plug was secured with plastic wrap to ensure close contact with the stem surface and to maintain high humidity. Lesion length along the stems was measured at 168 hours post-infection (hpi). For leaf inoculation, the newest fully expanded leaves from eight to ten 6-week-old field-grown oilseed rape plants were detached and placed on soaked gauze. Mycelial agar plugs (7 mm in diameter) were then applied to the leaf surface. The inoculated leaves were covered with plastic film to maintain moisture and incubated at 23 °C in the dark. Lesion area on the leaves was measured at 48 hpi. These assessments were conducted with three replications.

For cotyledon inoculation, seeds were sown in soil and grown in a climate chamber until the cotyledons were fully expanded. Six mycelial agar plugs (7 mm in diameter) were excised from the actively growing edge of a 2-day-old colony, transferred to a 250 ml flask containing 200 ml of potato dextrose broth (PDB), and cultured on a platform shaker at 150 rpm and 23 °C for 48 h. The mycelia were washed, homogenized, and the concentration adjusted to 105 fragments ml−1 as described previously (Wu et al., 2022). The cotyledons were detached and placed on soaked gauze, and 10 μl of mycelial suspension was applied to each cotyledon lobe. The inoculated cotyledons were kept under the same conditions as described for leaf inoculation. Lesion area on the cotyledons was measured at 72 hpi. This experiment was conducted with three replications.

To examine the role of SA in S. sclerotiorum resistance, 1 mM SA or water (mock) was sprayed onto 4-week-old J9712 plants, which were grown in a climate chamber under a 20 h light (∼300 µmol m−2 s−1)–4 h dark photoperiod at 22 °C. Four hours after treatment, mycelial agar plugs (7 mm in diameter) were attached to the leaf surface. The inoculated plants were covered with plastic film to maintain moisture. Lesion area on the leaves was measured at 48 hpi.

For S. sclerotiorum inoculation of Arabidopsis, approximately 30 fully expanded leaves were detached from 4-week-old plants grown in a growth chamber. For SA treatment experiments, plants were sprayed with 1 mM SA or water (mock) 4 h prior to leaf detachment. All leaves were inoculated with 2 mm mycelial agar plugs as described by Lin et al. (2022). The lesion size on the inoculated leaves was measured at 24 hpi.

RNA-Seq

Twenty-four samples were harvested from OE-#1 and J9712 leaves at 3, 6, and 12 h after S. sclerotiorum or mock inoculation. Tissues extending 10 mm beyond the inoculation site on the leaves were harvested. Approximately 10 inoculated tissues were collected per replicate, with two biological replicates performed. Total RNA was extracted from the samples using an RNAiso Reagent kit (Vazyme) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced by Majorbio Company (Shanghai, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 2× 150 bp paired-end reads. RNA sequencing was performed as described previously (Lin et al., 2024). Genes with an absolute value of log2 fold change ≥1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). A heatmap displaying the expression levels of the DEGs was generated using TBtools (Chen et al., 2020). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) enrichment analysis was conducted on the online platform Majorbio (https://cloud.majorbio.com) with the parameter FDR<0.05.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the samples using an RNAiso Reagent kit and transcribed into cDNA with HiScript® Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme). Gene expression levels in oilseed rape were analysed via qRT-PCR using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) in an ABI Step One Plus real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). BnaActin7 and BnaUBC9 (BnaC08g12720D) were used as the internal controls. BnaA10.WRKY75 expression in Arabidopsis was measured in the OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 and OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 ics1 lines. AtACTIN2 (AT3G18780) and AtEF-1α (AT5G60390) were used as the internal control. All qRT-PCR experiments were performed with three biological replicates. In addition, AtICS1 expression in Atics1 plants was analysed by RT-PCR using Taq DNA polymerase. Primers used in the qRT-PCR and RT-PCR assays are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

DNA affinity purification-seq and data analysis

DNA affinity purification (DAP)-seq was conducted by Bluescape Hebei Biotech Co., Ltd (China) as previously described (Bartlett et al., 2017). The full-length CDS of BnaA10.WRKY75 was cloned into the pFN19 K HaloTag T7 SP6 Flexi expression vector. The fusion protein was expressed using the TNT SP6 Coupled Wheat Germ Extract System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and purified using Halo Tag Beads (Promega).

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was separately extracted from J9712 and OE-#1 leaves and mixed at a 1:1 ratio. Five micrograms of gDNA was fragmented to an average length of 200 bp using a Covaris M220 focused-ultrasonicator (Woburn, MA, USA) and purified using MICH DNA Clean Beads (Bluescape). DNA libraries were then constructed using a MICH TLX DNA-Seq Kit (Bluescape).

The BnaA10.WRKY75-HaloTag bead mixture was incubated with 50 ng of adapter-ligated gDNA fragments for 1 h at room temperature in 50 μl of wash/bind buffer. The beads were washed three times with the same buffer to remove any unbound DNA fragments. The DNA was eluted from the beads in 30 μl of elution buffer, used for DAP-seq library construction, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The DAP-seq experiments were performed in two replicates. Negative control mock DAP-seq libraries were prepared without the addition of protein to the beads.

For DAP-seq data analysis, clean reads were mapped to the Darmor-bzh reference genome (v4.0) of oilseed rape using BWA-MEM (Vasimuddin et al., 2019). Peaks from the two replicates were merged using MACS2 callpeak (Zhang et al., 2008) and Homer software (Heinz et al., 2010) with Q<0.05. Motif discovery was conducted using MEME-ChIP (Machanick and Bailey, 2011), and bound peaks were annotated using ChIPseeker (Yu et al., 2015).

Yeast one-hybrid assay

A yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay was conducted as previously described (Lin et al., 2022). The full-length CDS of BnaA10.WRKY75 was cloned into pGADT7 as prey. The BnaICS1 promoter was cloned into pHIS2 as bait. Two plasmids were co-transformed into yeast strain Y187 according to the Yeast Protocols Handbook (Clontech, USA). Transformed clones were cultured on SD/−Trp−Leu medium and screened on SD/−Trp−Leu−His medium containing 100 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT) at 30 °C for 3 d. Primers used in the YIH assay are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Dual-luciferase assay

A dual-luciferase (dual-LUC) assay using N. benthamiana plants was conducted as previously described (Lin et al., 2022). For the effectors, the full-length CDS of BnaA10.WRKY75 was cloned into pGreenII 62-SK. For the reporter, the BnaCnn.ICS1 promoter was inserted ahead of the Firefly LUC gene in the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector; 35S-driven Renilla LUC was used as an internal control for normalization. The effector and reporter constructs were co-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves via A. tumefaciens-mediated transfection, and the leaves were harvested 48 h post-infiltration. LUC and REN activities were measured with a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Kit (Vazyme) in a microplate reader (Tecan Spark, Tecan Trading AG, Zurich, Switzerland). Primers used in the dual-LUC assay are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The full-length CDS of BnaA10.WRKY75 was cloned into the pGEX6P-1 vector. The glutathione S-transferase (GST)–BnaA10.WRKY75 fusion protein and GST protein were expressed and purified from Escherichia coli strain BL21. Oligonucleotide probes of BnaCnn.ICS1 (W1, W2, W3, and W4) were synthesized and labeled with biotin using an EMSA Probe Biotin Labeling kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). For the mutated probes, the mutation sites were located at the core sequence of the W-box (change from TGAC to TATT). Biotin-unlabeled fragments of the same sequences were used as competitors, and GST protein alone was used as the negative control. The electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was conducted using a Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Primers used in EMSA are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Salicylic acid measurements

SA extraction and measurement were conducted as previously described (Lin et al., 2024). Samples were harvested from OE-#1 and J9712 leaves at 3, 6, and 12 h after S. sclerotiorum or mock inoculation, as described for RNA-seq. Three biological replicates were performed. The samples were ground into a powder in liquid nitrogen, and approximately 0.1 g of the ground tissue was subjected to SA extraction. SA content was measured using a HPLC–MS/MS system (QTRAP® 6500+ LC-MS/MS System, SCIEX) consisting of an HPLC system coupled to a QTRAP 6500 mass spectrometer.

Results

BnaWRKY75 genes are induced by S. sclerotiorum infection

Transcriptional reprogramming involved in both PTI and ETI is mediated by various classes of TFs, among which the WRKY family plays a significant role (Tsuda and Somssich, 2015). To pinpoint the WRKY TFs that are activated during S. sclerotiorum infection in oilseed rape, we analysed transcriptomic data that we acquired previously (Wu et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2024). Thirty-seven WRKY genes showed more than 2-fold differential expression in both leaves and stems following S. sclerotiorum inoculation (Fig. 1A). Notably, all four BnaWRKY75 genes ranked among the top five most strongly induced WRKYs (Fig. 1A), suggesting that BnaWRKY75 might play crucial roles in mediating the immune response of oilseed rape against S. sclerotiorum.

The RNA-seq data were acquired in a previous study (Wu et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2024). (B) Phylogenetic analysis of WRKY75 genes in oilseed rape and Arabidopsis using MEGA 7.0 software. Bootstrap values (1000 replications) are shown at each branch as percentages. A branch length scale bar is shown below the tree. At, Arabidopsis thaliana; Bna, Brassica napus. (C) Histochemical GUS staining of transgenic Arabidopsis plants harboring pBnaA10.WRKY75:GUS after S. sclerotiorum inoculation. Scale bars, 0.5 cm. (D) Subcellular localization of BnaA10.WRKY75–green fluorescent protein (GFP) fusion protein in Arabidopsis protoplasts. mCherry fused to the nuclear localization signal (NLS) SV40 was used as a nuclear marker. The four BnaWRKY75 genes, BnaA10.WRKY75 (BnaA10g20210D), BnaC09.WRKY75 (BnaC09g44020D), BnaA03.WRKY75 (BnaA03g04160D), and BnaC03.WRKY75 (BnaC03g05760D), share highly conserved deduced amino acid sequences and exhibit the highest homology to AtWRKY75 (Fig. 1B). Amino acid sequence analysis of the BnaWRKY75 proteins revealed that all four proteins contain a WRKYGQ motif and a zinc finger structure (Supplementary Fig. S1). Tissue-specific expression profiling showed that BnaWRKY75 genes are highly expressed in aging tissues, such as older leaves and mature siliques (Supplementary Fig. S2A–C), suggesting they might function in senescence in oilseed rape. Additionally, BnaWRKY75 genes were induced by SA, methyl jasmonate, and ethephon (ETH) treatment (Supplementary Fig. S2D), suggesting they might be involved in phytohormone signal transduction. In this study, we focused on the roles of BnaWRKY75 genes in SSR resistance. To elucidate the molecular mechanism of the functions of these genes in the plant response to S. sclerotiorum, we selected the most highly induced homolog, BnaA10.WRKY75, for further analysis.

To further verify the induced expression of BnaA10.WRKY75 in response to S. sclerotiorum infection, we generated transgenic Arabidopsis plants harboring the β-glucuronidase (GUS) gene driven by the BnaA10.WRKY75 promoter (pBnaA10.WRKY75:GUS) and performed histochemical GUS staining. The leaves exhibited visible GUS staining at 24 hpi, with much stronger staining observed at 36 hpi (Fig. 1C), suggesting that BnaA10.WRKY75 expression is significantly induced by S. sclerotiorum infection.

To investigate the subcellular localization of BnaA10.WRKY75, we generated a construct expressing this protein fused with GFP and transiently expressed it in Arabidopsis protoplasts. Fluorescence microscopy showed that BnaA10.WRKY75 localized exclusively to the nucleus (Fig. 1D), which is consistent with its function as a TF.

BnaA10.WRKY75 positively regulates S. sclerotiorum resistance in oilseed rape

To further investigate the role of BnaA10.WRKY75 in resistance to S. sclerotiorum, we generated transgenic oilseed rape lines overexpressing (OE) BnaA10.WRKY75 driven by the CaMV 35S promoter (Supplementary Fig. S3A). We obtained 12 independent transgenic plants in the T0 generation. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed that the transcript level of BnaA10.WRKY75 was significantly elevated in in the T1 generation transgenic plants, with transcript levels hundreds of times higher than that in the non-transgenic line J9712 (Supplementary Fig. S3B, C).

To evaluate the SSR resistance of the OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 lines, we inoculated five independent lines (#1–#5) with S. sclerotiorum, including cotyledon, leaf, and stem inoculation. Compared with the J9712 control, OE plants in the T2 generation had 24.3–39.3% smaller lesions on their cotyledons at 72 hpi (Fig. 2A, B), 16.1–25.6% smaller lesions on their leaves at 48 hpi (Fig. 2D, E), and 12.2–21.9% shorter lesions on their stems at 168 hpi (Fig. 2G, H). This enhanced resistance was also observed in the T3 generation (Fig. 2C, F, I).

We also employed CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing to generate BnaWRKY75 knockout mutants. We designed four gRNAs to specifically edit BnaWRKY75: gRNA1 and gRNA2 were specifically targeted to both BnaA10.WRKY75 and BnaC09.WRKY75, while gRNA3 and gRNA4 were simultaneously targeted to BnaA03.WRKY75 and BnaC03.WRKY75 (Fig. 3A, B). Despite the initial absence of editing events in the 25 T0 plants generated, we were fortunately able to identify two lines with editing events in the subsequent T1 generation. CRI-#1 exhibited homozygous mutations at Target4 of both BnaA03.WRKY75 and BnaC03.WRKY75, with deletions of 22 bp and 1 bp, respectively (Fig. 3C). CRI-#2 displayed a homozygous 1 bp deletion at Target1 of BnaC09.WRKY75 (Fig. 3C). All these mutations resulted in translational frame shifts.

We evaluated the SSR resistance of the BnaWRKY75 mutants obtained by cotyledon and leaf inoculation. Only the double mutant CRI-#1 exhibited significantly larger lesions on leaves and cotyledons compared with J9712 (Fig. 3D–G). The single mutant CRI-#2 displayed slightly larger lesions on leaves and cotyledons than the control, but this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 3D–G). These results indicate that homologous copies of BnaWRKY75 have a dosage effect on SSR resistance.

As we were unable to obtain BnaWRKY75 quadruple mutants, we used the Arabidopsis wrky75–25 mutant and WRKY75RNAi plants to further validate the role of WRKY75 in SSR resistance. Following inoculation with S. sclerotiorum, wrky75–25 and WRKY75RNAi plants exhibited more severe symptoms than wild-type Col-0 plants at 24 hpi (Supplementary Fig. S4). Collectively, these results suggest that WRKY75 positively regulates SSR resistance in both oilseed rape and Arabidopsis.

BnaA10.WRKY75 is involved in the activation of salicylic acid biosynthesis

To gain insight into how BnaA10.WRKY75 promotes resistance to S. sclerotiorum, we conducted RNA-seq analysis of the OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 line (OE-#1) and J9712 at different time points following inoculation with S. sclerotiorum or the mock controls. On average, approximately 91% of the reads obtained from all samples were successfully mapped to the oilseed rape genome, with ∼84.71% of these alignments being unique matches (Supplementary Table S2). We identified 14 818 and 27 592 DEGs between S. sclerotiorum-inoculated and mock-inoculated samples in J9712 and OE-#1, respectively. Specifically, in J9712, we identified 2313, 852, and 13 479 DEGs at 3, 6, and 12 hpi, respectively. OE-#1 exhibited a more dramatic transcriptional response, with 4146, 6632, and 23 630 DEGs at 3, 6, and 12 hpi, respectively (Fig. 4A). These findings suggest that overexpressing BnaA10.WRKY75 in oilseed rape triggered a more extensive transcriptional regulatory response during S. sclerotiorum infection compared with J9712.

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs in both OE-#1 and J9712 were significantly enriched in KEGG pathways such as plant–pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway (Fig. 4B). The plant hormone signal transduction pathway showed the highest enrichment, particularly in OE-#1 at 6 hpi and 12 hpi (Fig. 4B), suggesting that transcriptional activities related to plant hormone-mediated resistance were enhanced in the BnaA10.WRKY75 overexpression line. In general, genes related to SA biosynthesis and signaling were up-regulated, including the SA biosynthetic gene BnaICS and the SA signaling pathway-related genes BnaNPR, BnaTGA, and BnaPR1 (Fig. 4C). Most of these genes showed more dramatic transcriptional changes in OE-#1 versus J9712 at different time points (Fig. 4C). To validate the RNA-seq data, we subjected the SA marker gene BnaC03.PR1 to qRT-PCR analysis. The expression of BnaC03.PR1 increased more rapidly after S. sclerotiorum infection in OE-#1 compared with J9712 (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. S5), which is consistent with the RNA-seq results. These results suggest that overexpressing BnaA10.WRKY75 enhanced SSR resistance by modulating the SA biosynthesis and/or signaling pathway in oilseed rape.

Given the obvious up-regulation of the key genes for SA biosynthesis and signaling, we measured endogenous SA levels in OE-#1 and J9712 plants. In the absence of S. sclerotiorum infection, both lines exhibited similar, relatively low endogenous SA levels. Upon S. sclerotiorum infection, the OE-#1 plants showed a significant increase in endogenous SA contents at both 3 hpi and 12 hpi, whereas J9712 plants showed a significant increase in endogenous SA contents only at 12 hpi (Fig. 4E). These results indicate that SA accumulated earlier in OE-#1 after S. sclerotiorum infection. SA typically contributes to resistance to biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens in plants (Glazebrook, 2005; Bari and Jones, 2009). To examine the role of SA in resistance to the necrotrophic pathogen S. sclerotiorum, we sprayed oilseed rape plants with 1 mM SA, incubated them for 4 h, and inoculated them with S. sclerotiorum. Compared with mock-treated plants, the SA-treated plants showed less severe symptoms and a 24.6% decrease in lesion size (Fig. 4F, G), suggesting that SA contributes to S. sclerotiorum resistance in oilseed rape. Taken together, these observations indicate that one of the key roles of BnaA10.WRKY75 in mediating SSR resistance is promoting SA biosynthesis.

BnaA10.WRKY75 directly targets the salicylic acid biosynthetic gene BnaICS1

We then carried out DAP-seq to investigate BnaA10.WRKY75 binding sites genome-wide using mixed genomic DNA from J9712 and OE-#1. A total of 17 050 binding peaks were identified from the two biological replicates, with 6613 overlapping peaks between them (Fig. 5A). These overlapping peaks were considered to be high-confidence binding peaks. Genomic distribution analysis revealed that 38% of all peaks were located in promoter regions, while 41% were located in intergenic regions (Fig. 5B). We identified three major enriched motifs using MEME-ChIP (Fig. 5C). Motif 1, with a core sequence of ‘DTTGACY’, was the most highly enriched motif (E-value, 1.2e-312) (Fig. 5C). Distribution analysis showed that Motif 1 is predominantly positioned at the summit of the BnaA10.WRKY75 binding peaks (Fig. 5D). These results are consistent with the previously reported WRKY recognition motif W-box (TTGACY) (Eulgem et al., 2000; Rushton et al., 2010), confirming the reliability of our DAP-seq data.

Among the 6613 high-confidence binding peaks, 2470 were localized in promoter regions, corresponding to 2458 genes. To narrow down the list of genes that are directly regulated by BnaA10.WRKY75, we compared the DAP-seq and RNA-seq data. This analysis revealed a core group of 41 genes that overlapped between DEGs in the OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 line at 3, 6, and 12 hpi, as identified by RNA-seq, and the genes bound by BnaA10.WRKY75, as identified by DAP-seq (Fig. 5E). Interestingly, the key SA biosynthetic gene BnaCnn.ICS1 (BnaCnng08300D) was among these 41 genes. Signiﬁcantly enriched BnaA10.WRKY75 binding peaks were identiﬁed in the promoter region of BnaCnn.ICS1 in both DAP-seq replicates (Fig. 5F). Enriched binding peaks were also identified in the promoter region of another BnaICS1 homolog, BnaC06.ICS1.a (BnaC06g35540D), in both DAP-seq replicates (Supplementary Fig. S6). This gene was also up-regulated in the OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 line at 12 hpi (Fig. 4C). These results suggest that BnaA10.WRKY75 promotes SA biosynthesis by activating the expression of BnaICS1 genes.

We conducted Y1H, dual-LUC, and EMSA to determine whether BnaA10.WRKY75 directly binds to the BnaCnn.ICS1 promoter to regulate its transcription. We cloned the ∼1800 bp promoter sequence of BnaCnn.ICS1 (pBnaCnn.ICS1), which contains three putative W-box sequences, and used it for Y1H and dual-LUC assays (Supplementary Fig. S7). In the Y1H assay, yeast cells co-transformed BnaA10.WRKY75-pGADT7 and pBnaCnn.ICS1-pHIS2 plasmids were able to grow on selective dropout (SD) medium (SD/−Trp−Leu−His) with 100 mM 3-AT. By contrast, cells co-transformed with pGADT7 and pBnaCnn.ICS1-pHIS2 plasmids failed to grow on this medium (Fig. 5G). These results suggest that BnaA10.WRKY75 interacts with pBnaCnn.ICS1.

To explore whether BnaA10.WRKY75 activates the expression of BnaCnn.ICS1, we perform a dual-LUC assay by co-infiltrating Nicotiana benthamiana leaves with pBnaCnn.ICS1-driven firefly luciferase (LUC) and 35S promoter-driven Renilla luciferase (REN, the internal control) as the reporter (Fig. 5H) and plasmids with or without BnaA10.WRKY75 as the effector. Co-expression of pBnaCnn.ICS1::LUC/p35S::REN with BnaA10.WRKY75 significantly increased the LUC:REN ratio compared with the control (pBnaCnn.ICS1::LUC/p35S::REN+empty effector) (Fig. 5H), indicating that BnaA10.WRKY75 positively regulates the expression of BnaCnn.ICS1.

Additionally, in an EMSA, the GST–BnaA10.WRKY75 recombinant protein (Supplementary Fig. S8), but not GST alone, bound to probes containing W-box 2 or W-box 3 of pBnaCnn.ICS1, but not W-box 1 (Fig. 5I). The intensity of the binding signals gradually decreased with increasing amounts of competitor probe. When the W-box element in the probe was mutated, the binding was abolished (Fig. 5I). These findings indicate that BnaA10.WRKY75 binds directly to pBnaCnn.ICS1. Collectively, these results demonstrate that BnaA10.WRKY75 directly binds to the promoter of BnaCnn.ICS1 and activates its expression.

ICS1 acts genetically downstream of BnaA10.WRKY75 to regulate Sclerotinia stem rot resistance

We then investigated the genetic relationship between BnaA10.WRKY75 and BnaCnn.ICS1 in regulating SSR resistance. Since there are four copies of BnaICS1 in oilseed rape, making it challenging to generate a complete Bnaics1 knockout mutant, we conducted genetic analysis using the Arabidopsis ics1 mutant (SA-deficient 2, sid2; Supplementary Fig. S9), which fails to accumulate SA upon pathogen infection (Wildermuth et al., 2001). We overexpressed BnaA10.WRKY75 in both wild-type Col-0 and ics1 plants, generating OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 and OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 ics1 lines, respectively. qRT-PCR analysis showed that BnaA10.WRKY75 was highly expressed in both OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 and OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 ics1 transgenic plants (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. S10). We assessed the SSR resistance of OE-BnaA10.WRKY75, OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 ics1, ics1, and Col-0 plants. Overexpressing BnaA10.WRKY75 in Arabidopsis significantly increased SSR resistance compared with wild-type Col-0, with the lesion area on OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 plants reduced by 45.5% at 24 hpi (Fig. 6B, C). This result is consistent with the findings in oilseed rape. The ics1 plants exhibited more severe symptoms (Fig. 6B, C) compared with Col-0. OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 ics1 plants developed disease lesions comparable to those of the ics1 mutant (Fig. 6B, C), suggesting that the mutation of ICS1 impaired the enhanced SSR resistance conferred by the overexpression of BnaA10.WRKY75. In addition, when ics1 and OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 ics1 plants were treated with 1 mM SA, the SSR resistance was restored to levels comparable to Col-0 (Supplementary Fig. S11), further supporting that the enhanced resistance conferred by BnaA10.WRKY75 depends on SA biosynthesis via ICS1. These results demonstrate that ICS1 functions downstream of BnaA10.WRKY75 to positively regulate SSR resistance.

Transcriptional regulation varies among BnaICS1 homologs

RNA-seq analysis showed that all four BnaICS1 homologs were up-regulated in the OE-BnaA10.WRKY75 line following S. sclerotiorum inoculation (Fig. 4C). However, DAP-seq indicated that BnaA10.WRKY75 specifically bound to the promoters of BnaCnn.ICS1 (BnaCnng08300D) and BnaC06.ICS1.a (BnaC06g35540D), whereas there was no detectable binding to BnaA07.ICS1 (BnaA07g22090D) or BnaC06.ICS1.b (BnaC06g22820D) (Fig. 5F; Supplementary Fig. S6), suggesting that BnaA10.WRKY75 does not activate all BnaICS1 homologs in response to S. sclerotiorum.

To confirm these findings, we conducted Y1H and dual-LUC assays with two representative BnaWRKY75 homologs (BnaA10.WRKY75 and BnaC03.WRKY75) and the promoters of four BnaICS1 homologs. Both transcription factors showed binding activity with three of the four BnaICS1 promoters (BnaCnn.ICS1, BnaC06.ICS1.a, and BnaA07.ICS1) in Y1H assays (Fig. 7A) and significantly activated their transcription in dual-LUC assays (Fig. 7B). This finding is inconsistent with the DAP-seq data, which showed no binding to the BnaA07.ICS1 promoter. Further examination of the cloned BnaA07.ICS1 promoter sequences from both J9712 and Darmor-bzh revealed that the lack of binding peaks on the BnaA07.ICS1 promoter might be attributed to a possible assembly error in the BnaA07.ICS1 promoter sequence in the Darmor-bzh (V4.1) reference genome (Supplementary Fig. S12). Reanalysis of the DAP-seq data using the ZS11 reference genome confirmed binding peaks on the BnaA07.ICS1 promoter (Supplementary Fig. S13). These findings suggest that different BnaICS1 homologs are regulated by distinct TFs in response to S. sclerotiorum, with three homologs activated by BnaWRKY75 and BnaC06.ICS1.b potentially regulated by other TFs.

In Arabidopsis, WRKY75 positively regulates JA-mediated defense against the necrotrophic fungal pathogens Botrytis cinerea and Alternaria brassicicola by binding directly to OCTADECANOID-RESPONSIVE ARABIDOPSIS 59 (ORA59), a key regulatory gene in the JA and ET signaling pathway (Chen et al., 2021). Upon examining the DAP-seq data, we found no signiﬁcantly enriched BnaA10.WRKY75 binding peaks in the promotors of the four BnaORA59 homologs (BnaA09g50010D, BnaA10g04090D, BnaC05g04210D, and BnaC08g44670D) in oilseed rape (Supplementary Fig. S14). Further analysis revealed extensive genetic variation in the promoter sequences between the orthologous genes AtORA59 and BnaORA59 (Supplementary Fig. S15), which might explain these differences.

Discussion

The resistance mechanisms of plants against necrotrophic pathogens like S. sclerotiorum and B. cinerea are not yet fully understood, primarily due to the complex network of defense responses triggered by these pathogens. Upon pathogen recognition, rapid and massive transcriptional reprogramming serves as a crucial step in plant–pathogen interactions (Birkenbihl et al., 2017). WRKY TFs play key roles as immune regulators in this process. In this study, we revealed that BnaWRKY75 genes are the most highly inducible WRKY TF genes in response to S. sclerotiorum infection and that these TFs positively regulate resistance to this pathogen in oilseed rape. BnaWRKY75 positively regulates the expression of BnaICS1, a key gene required for SA biosynthesis, by directly targeting its promoter. This regulation enhances SA biosynthesis, thereby activating SA-dependent defenses (Fig. 7C).

SA is a key plant hormone required for establishing resistance to many pathogens (An and Mou, 2011; Ding and Ding, 2020). SA promotes resistance to various biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens. For example, the enhanced activation of SA biosynthesis in Arabidopsis hybrids produced by crossing different accessions contributes to their increased resistance to the biotrophic bacterial pathogen Pseudomonas syringae (Yang et al., 2015). However, our findings indicate that exogenous SA treatment or enhanced SA biosynthesis through BnaWRKY75 overexpression in oilseed rape significantly enhanced its resistance to S. sclerotiorum, a necrotrophic pathogen (Figs 2, 4). The isochorismate synthase (ICS) pathway mediated by ICS1 serves as the primary route of SA production for immune responses in Brassicaceae species (Peng et al., 2021). Consistent with this finding, Arabidopsis ics1 plants exhibited reduced resistance to S. sclerotiorum (Fig. 6), further highlighting the importance of SA in defense against S. sclerotiorum. Other studies have suggested that SA contributes to plant defense against necrotrophic pathogens (Tsuda et al., 2009; Sanchez et al., 2012; Kouzai et al., 2020). Thus, the model proposing that SA mainly contributes to resistance to biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens but JA and ET predominantly promote resistance to necrotrophic pathogens may oversimplify the complex signaling events initiated upon pathogen recognition.

WRKY75 functions in several biological processes in Arabidopsis, including root hair formation (Rishmawi et al., 2014), leaf senescence (Guo et al., 2017; H. Zhang et al., 2022), ﬂowering (Zhang et al., 2018), phosphate acquisition (Devaiah et al., 2007), and defense against fungal pathogens (X. Chen et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2021). Although BnaWRKY75 positively regulates resistance to S. sclerotiorum in oilseed rape (Yu et al., 2024), the molecular mechanisms underlying its role in this resistance remain largely unknown. In this study, we determined that BnaWRKY75 enhances SA biosynthesis and activates SA-dependent defense responses by directly targeting the BnaICS1 promoter. By contrast, in Arabidopsis, WRKY75 positively regulates JA-mediated defense against necrotrophic fungal pathogens by directly binding to ORA59 (Chen et al., 2021). The mechanistic differences between oilseed rape and Arabidopsis may be due to genetic variation in promoter sequences across species. Additionally, we observed distinct patterns of transcriptional regulation among the BnaICS1 homologs in response to S. sclerotiorum: three of the four homologs were activated by BnaWRKY75, and the remaining homolog appears to be regulated by other TFs (Fig. 7A, B; Supplementary Figs S6, S13). These findings indicate that while the functions of homologous genes in polyploid species and orthologous genes in closely related species are largely conserved, as indicated by the minimal differences in their coding sequences, their transcriptional regulation might diverge due to extensive genetic variation in their promoter regions.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that the TF BnaWRKY75 is S. sclerotiorum-responsive and positively regulates resistance to S. sclerotiorum in oilseed rape. BnaWRKY75 contributes to SSR resistance by activating the expression of BnaICS1, thereby enhancing SA biosynthesis and triggering SA-dependent defense responses. Our findings highlight the pivotal role of SA in conferring resistance to the necrotrophic pathogen S. sclerotiorum in oilseed rape, while also uncovering the regulatory mechanisms that drive increased SA accumulation following infection. We also observed that transcriptional regulation can diverge due to extensive genetic variation in the promoter regions of homologous genes. These findings provide valuable insight into plant immune responses to necrotrophic pathogens.
