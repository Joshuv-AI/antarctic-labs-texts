Acute cholecystitis revealing gastric adenocarcinoma with osseous metastasis: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Ahmed Vadel Jidou, Mahmoud Aberkane, Abdelali Guellil, Rachid Jabi, Amal Bennani, Mohammed Bouziane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Gastric cancer is a major cause of cancer-related death worldwide and is often difficult to diagnose, especially in advanced stages. Its clinical presentation varies widely, ranging from common gastrointestinal symptoms (epigastric pain, dyspepsia, and weight loss) to atypical manifestations caused by its metastatic spread. Gastric adenocarcinoma most frequently metastasizes to lymph nodes, the liver, and the peritoneum, whereas bone metastases are rare and associated with poor prognosis. In some cases, bone metastases are detected before the primary gastric tumor is diagnosed, as their nonspecific symptoms can delay identification. Gallbladder metastases are uncommon and are more often linked to breast or lung cancers, or melanoma; metastasis from gastric adenocarcinoma is exceptionally rare. This article presents a rare case of acute cholecystitis revealing gastric adenocarcinoma with bone metastases, along with a literature review to better understand its clinical, diagnostic, and therapeutic implications.Case presentation:A 57-year-old woman was admitted with 1 week of abdominal pain. She was clinically stable, non-icteric, with diffuse abdominal tenderness. Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis (15 530/mm3) and elevated CRP (209 mg/L). Imaging showed acute calculous cholecystitis without biliary duct dilatation, associated with retroperitoneal infiltration encasing the ureters and causing bilateral hydronephrosis (right predominant), as well as multiple osteosclerotic bone lesions. She underwent cholecystectomy. Histopathology demonstrated secondary gallbladder involvement by adenocarcinoma of suspected upper gastrointestinal origin. Bone marrow biopsy revealed metastatic adenocarcinoma with signet-ring cell features, immunohistochemically suggestive of gastric origin. Bilateral double-J ureteral stents were placed, and the patient was referred for endoscopic evaluation and palliative management.Clinical discussion:This case report describes an exceptionally rare clinical presentation in which acute cholecystitis revealed an underlying gastric adenocarcinoma with bone metastases. Although gastric cancer is the fifth most common malignancy worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, bone metastases occur in a minority of cases and are seldom the initial manifestation. Acute cholecystitis secondary to metastatic gastric involvement of the gallbladder is even more uncommon. Several pathophysiological mechanisms may account for this presentation, including lymphatic or direct tumor spread to the gallbladder, hematogenous dissemination explaining concomitant bone metastases, and peritoneal carcinomatosis with secondary gallbladder infiltration. The nonspecific nature of symptoms often complicates preoperative diagnosis and may delay the identification of the primary malignancy. Diagnosis relies on comprehensive imaging, histopathological confirmation following cholecystectomy, and systematic staging investigations to assess metastatic dissemination. Therapeutic management should be individualized, combining surgical intervention for symptom control and diagnostic confirmation with systemic therapy guided by tumor molecular characteristics, and palliative measures when indicated. Given the association with advanced metastatic disease, prognosis is generally poor. This case underscores the importance of considering an underlying malignancy in atypical presentations of acute cholecystitis and highlights the need for thorough diagnostic evaluation and multidisciplinary management.Conclusion:Acute cholecystitis revealing a gastric adenocarcinoma with bone metastases remains extremely rare, but it should be considered in the differential diagnosis of atypical presentations. The combination of a surgical approach for symptomatic control and systemic chemotherapy remains the standard in the management of metastatic patients.

Gastric adenocarcinoma most commonly metastasizes to regional lymph nodes, the liver, and the peritoneum. Bone involvement is much less frequent[4,5]. Bone metastases are considered an unusual presentation and are typically associated with a poor prognosis. They present with nonspecific symptoms that can delay identification of the primary tumor[3,5]. Several reports have described cases in which bone metastases were detected before the primary gastric cancer was diagnosed[5,6].

Acute cholecystitis is classically caused by lithiasic or inflammatory factors, but it can also be the initial clinical manifestation of an underlying malignancy[7,8]. Metastasis to the gallbladder is very rare; in the literature, documented cases mainly originate from breast cancer, lung cancer, or melanomas, whereas secondary involvement from gastric adenocarcinoma is exceptionally reported[9]. The few published observations suggest that gallbladder metastases can present as acute cholecystitis, sometimes without visible gallstones, making preoperative diagnosis challenging[10,11].

A study by Bilici et al reported cases where gallbladder metastasis secondary to gastric cancer was identified in patients presenting with acute cholecystitis, highlighting the need to consider a malignant etiology in atypical presentations[12,13]. Similarly, Sugita et al described a rare case of acalculous acute cholecystitis caused by a gastric cancer metastasis due to peritoneal dissemination[13,14]. These case reports demonstrate that clinical suspicion must remain high, especially in older patients or those with atypical signs.

Furthermore, although the literature includes studies on the prevalence of bone metastases in gastric cancer and their prognostic factors, such cases remain rare and are largely based on small series or case reports[4–6]. This lack of extensive documentation underscores the importance of detailed clinical reports to better understand the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and to optimize diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

In this context, we report a rare case of acute cholecystitis revealing a gastric adenocarcinoma with bone metastases, accompanied by a review of the literature to better characterize this unusual clinical presentation, its diagnostic implications, and its impact on patient management.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old female patient was admitted for abdominal pain evolving over 1 week.

The patient was conscious, hemodynamically and respiratory stable, non-icteric, and with diffuse abdominal tenderness. Laboratory findings: white blood cell count: 15 530/mm3, C-reactive protein: 209 mg/L. Abdominal ultrasound revealed acute calculous cholecystitis with gallbladder wall thickening measuring 8 mm. No dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts or the common bile duct was observed. Contrast-enhanced CT scan (Fig. 1) revealed uncomplicated acute calculous cholecystitis, infiltration of the midline retroperitoneum containing lymphadenopathy and encasing the ureters, resulting in bilateral hydronephrosis, more pronounced on the right side, and multiple osteosclerotic lesions involving the axial and appendicular skeleton. Discussion

This case illustrates a rare clinical scenario in which acute cholecystitis served as the presenting manifestation of a gastric adenocarcinoma with bone metastases. Such a presentation is exceptionally reported in the literature and raises several diagnostic, pathophysiological, and therapeutic questions.

Gastric cancer is the fifth most common malignancy worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality[1,16]. Bone metastases occur in 0.9–13.4% of cases, often in advanced stages, and are rarely the initial manifestation[4,5]. Acute cholecystitis as a presenting feature is even more uncommon, as most cases of cholecystitis are related to gallstones or bacterial infections. The few reported cases suggest that gallbladder involvement by gastric tumor cells can induce local inflammation, leading to a clinical presentation mimicking cholecystitis[6,7].

Direct or lymphatic metastases: gastric cancer can spread via regional lymph nodes and infiltrate the gallbladder, causing acute inflammation[9,17].Hematogenous dissemination: the bone metastases observed in our patient suggest spread through the bloodstream, which may also account for gallbladder involvement distant from the primary site.Peritoneal metastases with gallbladder infiltration: reported by Sugita et al, where acalculous cholecystitis was secondary to peritoneal dissemination[13].

The rarity of these events makes preoperative diagnosis challenging, especially when the clinical presentation is nonspecific. In some cases, abdominal pain or acute fever may be attributed to benign causes, delaying the detection of advanced gastric cancer.

In the literature, only a few cases of acute cholecystitis revealing gastric cancer have been reported. Bilici et al described patients presenting with acute cholecystitis secondary to gallbladder metastases from gastric adenocarcinoma, often with a delayed diagnosis of the primary tumor[12]. Similarly, most bone metastases are identified during staging work-up or following bone-related symptoms, such as localized pain or pathological fractures[5,13].

The combination of acute cholecystitis, bone metastases, and primary gastric cancer is exceptionally rare, highlighting the importance of reporting such cases to expand clinical knowledge and enhance diagnostic vigilance.

The prognosis of this type of presentation is generally poor due to bone dissemination and visceral involvement. Patients presenting with both bone metastases and secondary gallbladder involvement from gastric adenocarcinoma have a short median survival, underscoring the importance of early detection and a multidisciplinary approach[5,12,13].

Conclusion

Although extremely rare, acute cholecystitis may occasionally reveal an underlying advanced malignancy, such as gastric adenocarcinoma with distant bone metastases. This case highlights the importance of considering a malignant etiology in patients presenting with atypical clinical and radiological features of cholecystitis, particularly when associated with additional systemic findings. Early recognition through a combination of imaging, histopathological evaluation, and staging investigations is essential for an accurate diagnosis. Management is primarily multidisciplinary, focusing on the surgical control of acute symptoms, confirmation of diagnosis, and prompt initiation of systemic therapy and palliative care in view of the advanced stage and poor prognosis.
