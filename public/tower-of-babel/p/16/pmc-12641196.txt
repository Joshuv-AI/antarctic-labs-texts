Comparison of Subxiphoid and Lateral Intercostal Thoracoscopic Surgery for Anterior Mediastinal Tumors: A Propensity Score‐Matched Analysis
Authors: Quanbin Zhao, Yangyuxuan Liu, Haoyang Li, Shenhu Gao, Rong Yang, Yihe Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the short‐term clinical outcomes of subxiphoid approach thoracoscopic surgery (SATS) versus lateral intercostal approach thoracoscopic surgery (LIATS) for anterior mediastinal tumors.MethodsClinical data from patients who underwent video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery for anterior mediastinal tumors between April 1, 2020 and December 31, 2023 were analyzed. Patients were stratified into two cohorts according to the surgical approach used: the SATS group (n = 679) and the LIATS group (n = 461). Intraoperative and postoperative outcomes were compared between the two groups.ResultsA total of 1140 patients were included in the statistical analysis after screening and assessment. After propensity score matching, a total of 417 SATS patients were matched with 417 LIATS patients. In the analysis of the outcomes, the LIATS group had a shorter operation time than the SATS group (p < 0.001). There were no statistical differences in Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores on Postoperative Day 1 (p = 0.113), Day 2 (p = 0.189), or Day 3 (p = 0.462). Postoperative atelectasis was more common in the SATS group than in the LIATS group (p = 0.025). There were no perioperative deaths.ConclusionsThe SATS did not demonstrate significant improvements in postoperative pain compared with the LIATS. However, the LIATS was associated with shorter operative time in the overall cohort.

Introduction

Video‐assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) is the standard surgical approach for anterior mediastinal tumors; it primarily includes two techniques: subxiphoid approach thoracoscopic surgery (SATS) and lateral intercostal approach thoracoscopic surgery (LIATS) [1, 2]. The SATS emerged in the past decade and has rapidly gained popularity in thoracic surgery [3]. Many studies have reported that SATS offers significant advantages over LIATS for anterior mediastinal tumors, particularly in surgical field exposure and postoperative pain management, leading to its wide adoption among surgeons [4, 5, 6].

Based on our center's experience, our department has been performing SATS since 2017, and this approach has been gradually popularized within the department. Over the past 8 years, surgeons at our center have selected the appropriate surgical approach based on the location and size of the anterior mediastinal tumor, as well as their personal preferences. Currently, both SATS and LIATS are routine surgical approaches for anterior mediastinal tumors at our center. However, most surgeons at our center have informally reported that SATS is less ergonomically optimal, citing prolonged operative time and frequent thumb numbness or pain during procedures (formal data on such surgeon feedback were not collected in this retrospective study). Our center's clinical observations indicate that SATS does not offer a significant advantage in the completeness of thymectomy or postoperative pain relief for patients. Therefore, this retrospective study analyzed our center's clinical data to evaluate the perioperative outcomes of the two surgical approaches (SATS and LIATS) for anterior mediastinal tumors and clarify their real‐world clinical utility.

Study Design and Participants

This single‐center retrospective study analyzed data from patients who underwent attempted VATS for anterior mediastinal tumors at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University, from April 1, 2020 to December 31, 2023. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) preexisting chronic pain disorders, (2) concurrent lung resection, and (3) reoperation during the same hospitalization. Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on the initially intended surgical approach: the SATS (n = 679) and the LIATS (n = 461). Case screening and follow‐up flow chart for this study.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All patients underwent a standardized preoperative assessment that included cardiopulmonary function testing, neurological evaluation, and radiological examination with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Radiological findings demonstrated high concordance between preoperative imaging characteristics and intraoperative observations. Specifically, when major vascular resection was not required, the assessment of tumor resectability was highly accurate [7]. Both groups received identical perioperative anesthetic management protocols, which included standardized anesthetic techniques, pharmacological agents, and dosage regimens.

Postoperative Management

Chest tubes were removed when all of the following criteria were met: (1) drainage volume < 200 mL/24 h; (2) serosanguinous drainage fluid; (3) absence of air leak on drainage. Chest tube removal was confirmed via a post‐procedural chest radiograph. Intraoperative analgesia (from anesthetic induction to extubation) was administered using short‐acting opioids, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), or multimodal combinations, in accordance with a standardized protocol. All patients completed a surgical outpatient follow‐up visit 1 week after discharge.

Surgical Technique

All thoracoscopic thymectomies for preoperatively suspected thymic malignancies adhered strictly to the International Thymic Malignancy Interest Group (ITMIG) consensus guidelines for minimally invasive resection criteria [8]. Total thymectomy is the gold standard for treating myasthenia gravis and thymoma. It involves the most extensive resection, requiring complete removal of the entire thymus (both lobes and the isthmus) along with all anterior mediastinal fatty tissue. The anatomical boundaries extend superiorly to the lower pole of the thyroid gland, inferiorly to the diaphragm, and laterally to the phrenic nerves, which must be carefully preserved. Hemithymectomy is suitable for benign lesions confined to one lobe. The resection encompasses the entire affected thymic lobe and part of the isthmus, while preserving the contralateral healthy thymic lobe and its blood supply. In contrast, partial thymectomy has the most limited scope, removing only the lesion and a small amount of surrounding thymic tissue to maximize preservation of normal thymus. It is reserved only for benign anterior mediastinal cysts or small benign nodules [9, 10].

The total thymectomy proceeded as follows: (1) Mobilization: The thymus was initially mobilized from the retrosternal space, extending superiorly to the thoracic inlet and inferiorly to the diaphragmatic reflections. The cardiophrenic angle adipose tissues were resected bilaterally. (2) Right‐side dissection: The right pleural cavity was entered. The thymic horn was carefully separated from the innominate vein only after the venous junction was clearly identified. Thymic veins draining into the innominate vein were localized and meticulously ligated with an energy device. (3) Left‐side dissection: The left mediastinal pleura was incised along the phrenic nerve, and all pretracheal adipose tissue anterior to the nerve was excised. (4) Extraction: Finally, the completely freed thymus and mediastinal fat were placed in a retrieval bag and extracted through the observation port [7, 11]. SATS: After induction of general anesthesia, patients were placed in a supine position. A 2‐cm subxiphoid incision served as the primary thoracoscopic port, supplemented by two auxiliary instrument ports (which are located at the intersection of the costal arch margin and bilateral midclavicular lines). Pneumomediastinum was established via CO2 insufflation (8–10 mmHg). Tumor resection involved en bloc dissection with capsular preservation, and specimens were retrieved through the subxiphoid port. Upon completion, an 18‐Fr chest tube was placed for postoperative drainage.

LIATS: Under general anesthesia, patients were placed in a 30° semi‐supine position. A three‐port technique was employed, consisting of two 5‐mm operative ports and one 10‐mm observation port. The standard port configuration included an observation port in the fourth intercostal space (anterior axillary line), with the two main operating ports in the third intercostal space (anterior axillary line) and the fifth intercostal space (midclavicular line). Right‐sided thoracic access was standard for anterior mediastinal tumors, while left‐sided thoracic access was reserved for lesions with left‐sided dominance. In patients with myasthenia gravis, bilateral anterior mediastinal fat located anterior to the phrenic nerves was resected. All other technical details, including tumor dissection and chest tube drainage protocols, were consistent with those used in the SATS. Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the postoperative 24‐h Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores and operative time (defined as the duration from skin incision to skin closure). The secondary outcomes included intraoperative blood loss, length of postoperative hospital stay, anesthesia duration, postoperative NRS pain scores on Postoperative Day 2 (POD 2) and Day 3 (POD 3), volume of postoperative chest tube drainage, duration of postoperative chest tube drainage, hospitalization costs, and postoperative complications (including atelectasis, chylothorax, conversion to open thoracotomy, phrenic nerve injury, atrial fibrillation, and pneumonia).

Definitions

Postoperative pain was assessed using the NRS, where 0 = no pain and 10 = worst imaginable pain. Postoperative pneumonia was defined according to the ATS/IDSA guidelines for hospital‐acquired pneumonia [12], as patients with a clinical pulmonary infection score (CPIS) > 6. Postoperative atelectasis was diagnosed based on a combination of clinical manifestations and radiological findings [13], including: (1) presence of clinical symptoms; (2) new areas of increased density or volume loss on chest CT; (3) exclusion of other potential causes. Phrenic nerve injury was defined as unilateral diaphragmatic dysfunction confirmed by clinical symptoms, physical examination, hemidiaphragmatic elevation on chest CT, and reduced diaphragmatic movement on dynamic ultrasound [14].

Statistical Analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student's t‐test, whereas non‐normally distributed continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (and percentages, where appropriate) and analyzed using Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test. And perform a multivariate regression analysis on the continuous variables and ordinal variables. Exclude independent variables with a variance inflation factor greater than or equal to 5. A propensity score‐matched analysis was conducted to ensure comparability between the groups by accounting for differences in all measured baseline characteristics. We performed propensity score matching using a 1:1 nearest neighbor approach. The scores were generated via logistic regression, which included all baseline independent variables as covariates to select comparable patients from each group, with a caliper of 0.02. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0.1). Statistical significance was set at a two‐tailed p ≤ 0.05.

Primary Outcomes of Anterior Mediastinal Tumors

There was a statistically significant difference in operative time between the two groups. The mean operative time was 74.79 ± 34.42 min in the LIATS group and 111.90 ± 43.07 min in the SATS group. The LIATS group had a significantly shorter operative time than the SATS group. Regarding early postoperative pain, no significant difference was observed (p = 0.113). The median 24‐h NRS pain score was 2 (2‐2) in the LIATS group versus 2 (2‐2) in the SATS group.

Secondary Outcomes of Anterior Mediastinal Tumors

The mean difference in anesthesia time was 31.00 min (95% CI: 25.69–36.31; p < 0.001). In addition, the mean difference in the volume of postoperative drainage was 137.58 mL (95% CI: 67.15–208.02; p = 0.001). There were differences in the median duration of chest tube drainage (3 (2–3) vs. 2 (2–3); p ＜ 0.001), the median length of postoperative hospital stays (4 (3–5) vs. 4 (3–5); p < 0.001), and the median intraoperative blood loss (20 (10–20) vs. 20 (10–20); p = 0.040). On the basis of the predetermined significance level of p < 0.05, the SATS group had a longer operation time, anesthesia time, length of postoperative hospital stays, and postoperative chest tube drainage time than the LIATS group, and the SATS group had a greater volume of postoperative drainage and intraoperative blood loss than the LIATS group.

Besides, there were no significant differences in NRS pain scores on POD 2 (2 (2–2) vs. 2 (2–2); p = 0.189) or POD 3 (2 (1–2) vs. 2 (1–2); p = 0.462). On the basis of a predetermined significance level of p < 0.05, there was no significant evidence of a difference between the SATS and LIATS groups with regard to the NRS pain scores on the first, second, and third days after surgery. Furthermore, the mean difference in the hospitalization costs was 31.97 RMByuan (95% CI: −1359.62 to 1423.56; p = 0.964). There was no significant difference in the hospitalization costs between the two groups.

Compared with the SATS group, the incidence of postoperative atelectasis was significantly reduced in the LIATS group (p = 0.025). There was no significant difference in the incidence of chylothorax between the two groups. No statistically significant difference in conversion to open thoracotomy and the incidence of phrenic nerve injury was found between the two treatment arms. In addition, there was no significant difference in the incidence of postoperative pneumonia and postoperative atrial fibrillation between the two groups (Table 2).

Among all patients who required conversion to thoracotomy, in the SATS group, four cases were due to large tumor size, two due to inadequate exposure of the anterior superior mediastinum, and eight due to difficult dissection caused by tumor adhesion to surrounding tissues. Multivariate Regression Analysis of Outcomes

Compared to the SATS, the LIATS was associated with a statistically significant reduction in both operation time (β = −31.84 min, 95% CI: −37.08 to −26.60; p < 0.001) and anesthesia time (β = −26.12 min, 95% CI: −31.37 to −20.86; p < 0.001). Furthermore, the LIATS approach demonstrated superior outcomes in postoperative recovery, manifesting as a significantly lower volume of postoperative drainage (β = −94.48 mL, 95% CI: −168.44 to −20.52; p = 0.012) and a shorter duration of chest tube drainage (β = −0.28 days, 95% CI: −0.51 to −0.05; p = 0.017). However, no significant intergroup differences were observed in intraoperative blood loss (β = −2.12, 95% CI: −9.50 to 5.27; p = 0.575), the length of postoperative hospital stay (β = −0.10 days, 95% CI: −0.42 to 0.22; p = 0.531), or total hospitalization costs (β = 1057.84, 95% CI: −394.67 to 2510.35; p = 0.154). Regarding postoperative pain, no statistically significant differences in pain scores were observed between the LIATS and SATS groups on POD 1 (OR = −0.297, 95% CI: −0.614 to 0.020; p = 0.067), POD 2 (OR = −0.267, 95% CI: −0.620 to 0.086; p = 0.138), or POD 3 (OR = 0.026, 95% CI: −0.257 to 0.309; p = 0.856). Discussion

Prior studies have reported that SATS yields superior postoperative pain outcomes compared to LIATS [2, 5, 15, 16]—an advantage attributed to its avoidance of intercostal neurovascular bundle dissection and sternal attachments, which eliminates direct intraoperative trauma to intercostal nerves and reduces neuropathic pain [16]. In contrast to these findings, our study showed no statistically significant difference in postoperative pain between SATS and LIATS. This apparent discrepancy may be attributable to heterogeneity in postoperative analgesic regimens. The main mechanism of postoperative pain in transthoracic intercostal approaches involves direct mechanical irritation of intercostal nerves [17], and early removal of the chest drainage tube can also help alleviate early postoperative pain. As shown in our results, the chest tube removal time was shorter in the LIATS group than in the SATS group. In addition, our study used the NRS pain score, whereas other studies used the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain score. Research has shown that NRS and VAS are equally effective for assessing pain [18].

SATS has limitations related to surgical maneuverability, which makes it less suitable for complex surgical procedures or larger thymic tumors [19]. While SATS offers a wider operative field, it requires more extensive tissue dissection, which leads to greater surgical trauma, higher postoperative drainage volume, and longer drainage duration. Research has indicated that the surgical indications for SATS should be limited to cases with relatively small tumors, such as those where the maximum tumor diameter does not exceed 4 cm [20]. Due to the constraints of the anterior mediastinal space, SATS may also be unsuitable for certain patients, including those with chest deformities, large tumor burdens, preoperative imaging findings indicating tissue invasion (which poses a higher bleeding risk), or severe pleural adhesions [21]. This suggests that the range of indications for SATS is relatively narrow, which in turn, limits its application.

Presently, LIATS remains widely used for thoracoscopic thymectomy [22, 23, 24, 25]. Owing to the advancement and refinement of thoracoscopic techniques and surgical skills, most anterior mediastinal masses can now be resected via thoracoscopy. LIATS offers several advantages, including a smaller wound size, simpler preoperative preparation, and shorter operation time. These factors contribute to improved intraoperative oxygenation and better postoperative recovery quality [15].

In total, the thymectomy performed via the LIATS approach in this study, the ipsilateral phrenic nerve was consistently well visualized and protected. However, identification of the contralateral phrenic nerve posed a challenge due to limitations in the surgical viewing angle and instrument maneuverability, generally precluding direct visualization comparable to that achieved on the ipsilateral side. Based on our experience, the course of the contralateral phrenic nerve could be identified in most cases through blunt dissection and the magnification afforded by the thoracoscope, thereby facilitating its preservation. Nevertheless, the clarity of visualization was contingent upon the patient's specific anatomical configuration and the tumor characteristics.

We also observed a higher incidence of postoperative atelectasis in the SATS group after surgery for anterior mediastinal tumors. Intraoperative administration of systemic opioids has been shown to have a dose‐dependent relationship with the development of postoperative atelectasis [26]. Furthermore, the supine position used in SATS induces upward displacement of the diaphragm due to compression by intra‐abdominal organs [26]. These factors collectively increase the likelihood of postoperative atelectasis in SATS. In addition, SATS may exert pressure on the heart, which means caution is needed when using this approach for patients with impaired cardiac function to prevent cardiovascular complications [2]. However, a study by Dunning et al. [27] reported one case of subxiphoid incisional hernia among 72 patients, and this case required surgical revision. This serves as an important reminder for surgeons that, despite their rarity, postoperative hernias warrant significant attention.

Some thoracic surgeons tend to prefer SATS for managing patients with preoperative suspicion of thymoma or thymic carcinoma. This approach provides a clear surgical view of the superior thymus pole and bilateral phrenic nerve [16]. This adequate exposure facilitates the removal of adipose tissue from the anterior mediastinum and reduces the risk of other surgical injuries [28]. When dealing with larger anterior mediastinal tumors, surgeons often prefer LIATS due to limited space and difficulty in tumor removal via SATS. Another disadvantage of SATS is that it is more complex to manage major bleeding compared to LIATS. The main challenge lies in the difficulty of reaching deep areas [29]. In contrast, LIATS allows for easier suturing, clamping, and other hemostasis procedures when managing massive hemorrhage. Furthermore, if necessary, LIATS allows for incision extension or conversion to open thoracotomy.

In recent years, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols have been increasingly integrated into thoracic surgery, leading to shorter hospitalization durations and improved patient outcomes [30]. Nevertheless, the placement of postoperative chest drainage tubes exacerbates wound pain, impairs the effectiveness of coughing, and restricts mobility—all of which hinder early postoperative recovery [31, 32]. Notably, LIATS indirectly addresses these challenges by reducing operative time and postoperative drainage duration. Consistent with our NRS pain score results, LIATS also does not compromise postoperative pain management. While surgeons at our center have reported anecdotally greater comfort with LIATS (e.g., less thumb fatigue during prolonged procedures), formal assessments of surgical ergonomics or workload were not conducted in this study, so this observation requires further validation.

Study Limitations

As a retrospective study, this research has several inherent limitations that should be acknowledged. First, there is heterogeneity in analgesic protocols: clinical needs for individualized postoperative pain management precluded standardization of analgesic regimens, and this variability in medication selection and dosing may introduce confounding factors into the analysis of pain outcomes. Second, there is potential residual confounding: after propensity score matching, the baseline characteristics were not fully balanced; therefore, the possibility of residual confounding due to measured variables cannot be completely ruled out. Third, there are single‐center limitations: all data were collected from a single tertiary institution, potentially limiting generalizability. In addition, population‐specific practices and referral patterns may substantially constrain external validity, so these findings require validation in multicenter prospective studies. Finally, the selection of surgical approach is operator‐dependent: variations in surgeons' technical preferences for similar cases introduce potential selection bias and confounding related to operator experience.

Conclusions

This study showed no statistically significant difference in postoperative pain outcomes between SATS and LIATS. Moreover, analysis revealed that LIATS was associated with significantly shorter operative times in the overall cohort of patients with anterior mediastinal tumors.
