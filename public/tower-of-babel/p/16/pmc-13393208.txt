An Experimental Study of the Effect of Dynamic N2 Injection on the Emission and Diffusion Dynamics of Coalbed Methane
Authors: Haiteng Xue, Junjian Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
N2 displacement technology is widely regarded as a promising approach for enhancing coalbed methane recovery. However, the broader application of conventional N2-ECBM has been constrained by low N2 utilization efficiency and high operating costs. Dynamic N2-ECBM can significantly improve both N2 utilization efficiency and displacement performance, yet it still lacks sufficient theoretical support. Therefore, to gain a deeper understanding of the mechanism by which dynamic N2-ECBM enhances coalbed methane recovery, a self-developed experimental platform for N2 displacement of CH4 was employed, and dynamic injection modes were, for the first time, introduced to analyze the diffusion behavior of CH4 under the influence of N2. At an average injection rate of 100 mL/min, two dynamic injection modes, namely sinusoidal and stepped modes, were designed with three flow-rate variations and compared with the constant-flow injection mode. The results showed that, compared with the Constant flow-100 mode, the final CH4 production under the Sin-150–50, Sin-175–25, Laddered-150–50, and Laddered-175–25 modes increased by approximately onefold. The average effective diffusion coefficients of CH4 under the Sin-150–50 and Sin-175–25 modes increased by factors of 1.15 and 1.10, respectively. Before N2 breakthrough, dynamic N2 injection was more effective in enhancing the average effective diffusion coefficient of CH4. Although the Sin-200–0 mode resulted in relatively low CH4 production, the times required to reach T 50%, T 30%, and T 10% were shortened by 183, 263, and 558 s, respectively, while the cumulative N2 injection volumes were reduced by 233 mL, 393 mL, and 668 mL, respectively, thereby substantially lowering both the process duration and the N2 injection cost of the N2-ECBM process. The Sin-150–50 mode effectively shortened the time required to reach the concentration threshold limit value (TLV) and reduced the residual gas content at the TLV, which is beneficial for methane hazard control in coal mines. Under dynamic N2 injection, smaller amplitudes led to higher CH4 production and larger average effective diffusion coefficients. When the CH4 concentration dropped below 10%, the stepped injection mode yielded higher CH4 production. Assuming that the effects of different injection modes on gas residence time and adsorption-retention behavior are broadly universal, dynamic injection may also be more favorable for CO2 adsorption and retention in coal. This experimental study demonstrates that different injection modes exhibit distinct advantages and provides theoretical guidance for enhancing coalbed methane recovery, improving N2 utilization efficiency, controlling methane hazards in coal mines, and promoting CO2 sequestration. Further field-scale validation is still needed in future work.

Introduction

The essence of gas injection for coalbed methane (CBM) recovery lies in injecting gas into methane-bearing coal seams. This process effectively promotes the desorption of adsorbed CBM, thereby enhancing the recovery efficiency of CBM wells. It offers multiple benefits, including reducing dependence on traditional fossil fuels, alleviating shortages of natural gas resources, and facilitating the achievement of carbon peaking and carbon neutrality goals. − However, the gas injection-driven displacement process for enhanced CBM recovery involves complex mechanisms, including the nonlinear dynamics of CBM desorption, diffusion, and seepage, as well as nitrogen diffusion and seepage. Within this complex process, gas molecular diffusion plays a critical role and is one of the dominant factors controlling CBM migration, ultimately determining late-stage CBM production. Therefore, to optimize gas injection for enhanced CBM recovery, it is essential to clarify the effects of displacement gas injection on CBM diffusion behavior. Such an understanding is crucial for maximizing CBM production from individual wells and achieving the efficient development of CBM resources.

Currently, research on the use of CO2 and N2 as injection gases has yielded substantial findings. − CO2 exhibits a stronger adsorption capacity than both N2 and CH4. Injected CO2 can effectively compete with CH4 for adsorption sites, thereby promoting CH4 desorption, and it also has a larger diffusion coefficient. Consequently, CO2-ECBM generally exhibits better displacement performance than N2-ECBM. However, CO2 injection into coal seams induces adsorption-induced swelling of the coal matrix, which is approximately twice that caused by methane (CH4). This swelling increases the risk of coal and gas outbursts, thereby posing a potential threat to coal mine safety. Therefore, the widespread application of CO2 injection in underground coal mines is not suitable for coal mine production. At present, most studies on CO2 injection for enhanced coalbed methane recovery are limited to laboratory experiments and surface well tests. In contrast, trials of N2 injection for enhanced coalbed methane recovery in underground coal mines have achieved favorable engineering results. , Consequently, the superior N2-ECBM makes this technology advantageous and worthy of further investigation. However, previous studies have mainly focused on displacement experiments using lump coal samples. Numerous studies have systematically investigated the displacement behavior and permeability evolution mechanisms. For example, Connell et al. investigated the effect of nitrogen injection on the displacement of coal seam gas under different applied stress conditions and established a corresponding permeability model for the displacement process. Kumar et al. examined the permeability variation following CO2 injection into coal seams. They found that coal permeability decreased when the injection pressure was below 4 MPa but began to increase when the pressure exceeded 4 MPa. Wang et al. investigated the changes in pore structure and permeability of coal after N2 injection. Their results showed that coal permeability increased by 29.9%. Li et al. introduced liquid CO2-ECBM technology and verified its feasibility through both field tests and laboratory experiments. Fang et al. analyzed the permeability rebound phenomenon in CO2-ECBM using numerical simulations. Their study showed that, when CO2 had not diffused into the production well, the permeability near the injection well was mainly controlled by competitive adsorption and effective stress.

Unlike displacement experiments using lump coal, gas diffusion characteristics govern gas migration between the coal matrix and fractures and constitute another key factor determining late-stage gas production. Regarding the diffusion behavior of CH4 in the coal matrix and fractures, previous studies have often focused on the unidirectional diffusion mechanism of single-component gases. For example, Pillalamarry et al. analyzed the applicability of the unidirectional diffusion model for fitting diffusion experimental results and found that it performed better in the low-pressure range. Xu et al. proposed a new experimental method for determining the CH4 diffusion coefficient in the coal matrix, in which a thin coal slice rather than coal particles was used as the test sample. The results showed that the CH4 diffusion coefficient determined by this method was close to the actual reservoir value. Li et al. proposed a time-dependent diffusion coefficient model and verified its accuracy using experimental data. Numerous researchers have investigated the kinetic characteristics of CH4 desorption and diffusion under different physical conditions, such as moisture content, particle size, and temperature. − However, unlike the single-component gas diffusion mechanism, N2-ECBM involves a bidirectional diffusion process in which the injected N2 and the desorbed gas diffuse through the same pore network in opposite directions. Therefore, it is important to investigate the bidirectional diffusion process of binary gases to reveal the mechanism of N2-ECBM and improve coalbed methane recovery. Bai et al. explored the kinetic characteristics of gas diffusion in coal under N2 injection conditions. Bai et al. investigated the effects of injected CO2 on CH4 desorption and diffusion and concluded that increasing temperature and pressure could increase the effective diffusion coefficient. However, it should be noted that these studies were conducted under fixed-pressure injection boundary conditions, whereas dynamic gas injection is considered more effective for achieving efficient CH4 recovery. Meanwhile, with the development of displacement technology, dynamic N2-ECBM has gradually become a major research focus. Lin and Ji , pointed out that, compared with fixed-pressure gas injection, dynamic N2-ECBM resulted in lower recovery, but higher N2 utilization efficiency and a shorter time required to reduce the TLV. However, previous studies have indicated that the semicontinuous injection method is superior to the continuous injection method during gas hydrate exploitation. In addition, studies on stepwise CO2 injection have shown that the SBS-CO2 injection method can effectively improve CBM recovery and CO2 injection efficiency. Therefore, we believe that this may be related to the dynamic gas injection mode. Xue et al. pointed out that dynamic CO2 injection is more effective in promoting gas diffusion and enhancing gas recovery, and they proposed multiple dynamic displacement modes for different displacement objectives. However, studies specifically describing the diffusion process of coalbed methane during dynamic N2 injection remain limited. Existing research in this field has focused more on gas production characteristics and N2 utilization efficiency during dynamic N2 displacement, or on the evolution of coal permeability during the dynamic N2 injection process. In contrast, the coal matrix, which serves as the primary storage medium for coalbed methane, has received relatively little attention in studies of methane diffusion during dynamic N2 displacement. It is therefore clear that further experimental investigation and characterization of the diffusion process during dynamic N2 injection for coalbed methane displacement are needed to provide stronger theoretical support for the development of this technology.

The primary purpose of theoretical and laboratory studies is to develop effective solutions to specific engineering problems and promote their practical application. However, previous research on gas injection and displacement technology still leaves several issues unresolved. First, existing studies have mainly focused on gas seepage during injection while neglecting the critical role of gas diffusion. Second, experimental designs commonly adopt fixed-pressure boundary conditions for diffusion processes, without considering dynamic gas injection scenarios. Third, previous studies have often relied solely on pressure as the boundary condition, while ignoring variations in flow rate. However, gas diffusion, particularly under N2 injection, has a significant influence on late-stage coalbed methane production and constitutes a key mechanism in CBM recovery. − In addition, variations in gas injection flow rate inevitably alter the diffusion behavior of CH4. Therefore, to clarify the mechanism of N2-ECBM and improve CBM recovery, it is imperative to investigate CH4 diffusion under dynamic N2 injection conditions.

To this end, we used a self-developed experimental apparatus for the dynamic injection of N2 to displace CH4 in coal. Dynamic gas injection was applied to investigate N2-ECBM with the aim of enhancing coalbed methane recovery. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to employ this method for this purpose. Constant-flow injection with an average rate of 100 mL/min was used as the baseline for comparison. Two dynamic injection modes, namely sinusoidal and stepped modes, were designed, each with three flow-rate variations. During the experiments, the CH4 concentration, mixed-gas flow rate, N2 injection volume, and CH4 production during the displacement process were monitored dynamically. In addition, the time-dependent variation in the CH4 diffusion coefficient was calculated using a unidirectional diffusion model. The variation patterns of CH4 production and diffusion coefficient under different injection conditions were discussed with increasing N2 injection cycles. The N2 utilization efficiency and the time required to reduce the TLV under different injection conditions were also compared and analyzed. Finally, implications for CO2 sequestration and underground dynamic N2-ECBM were discussed. This study is expected to improve the operational efficiency of N2-ECBM technology and has important theoretical significance for both efficient coalbed methane recovery and CO2 sequestration.

Experimental Programs and Procedures

Coal Sample Preparation

The coal samples were collected from the No. 11–2 coal seam of Liuzhuang Coal Mine in Fuyang, Anhui Province. To weaken the dominant effect of fracture seepage on gas transport in lump coal and reduce structural heterogeneity, the raw coal was crushed using a pulverizer, and coal particles with sizes of 1–3 mm were selected by sieving, to better emphasize diffusion and desorption processes at the coal matrix scale. To mitigate the effects of moisture, the sieved coal particles underwent drying in an oven. The coal sample screening process is illustrated in Figure .

Experimental Device and Program

Experimental Systems

The entire experimental system consisted of a gas injection source (N2), a mass flow controller for regulating the injection gas flow rate and monitoring the discharged gas flow rate, pressure sensors for monitoring the inlet and outlet pressures, an adsorption vessel serving as the location for coal particle placement and gas displacement, a back-pressure valve for controlling the outlet pressure, a CH4 gas concentration sensor, a data acquisition and storage unit, and a vacuum pump. The backpressure valve, acting as a pressure regulating device, discharges gas exceeding 1 MPa when the set pressure is 1 MPa, with a mechanical device error of ±0.02 MPa.

Schematic and Hypothesized Process of Replacement

N2-ECBM aims to displace the gas originally adsorbed in and equilibrated with the coal seam. Before displacement, the coal body mainly contains two forms of gas: free gas and adsorbed gas (Figure (a)). Once N2 injection begins, N2 gradually diffuses into the coal body, leading to an increase in pore pressure. When the pore pressure exceeds the original adsorption equilibrium pressure, gas begins to flow out of the coal body, consisting primarily of free gas, a portion of the desorbed gas, and N2 (Figure (b)). Previous studies have typically set the outlet pressure to atmospheric pressure. − However, we argue that gas can only be discharged from the coal body when the internal gas pressure exceeds the original adsorption equilibrium pressure. This treatment is more consistent with practical field conditions. Therefore, the outlet pressure was set to 1 MPa.

Analysis of Experimental Results

Change in Volume Fraction of Exported CH4, N2

Figure shows the temporal evolution of the outlet gas volume fractions under different gas injection conditions. The variation in outlet gas volume fractions can be divided into three distinct stages. In the first stage, during the initial period of N2 injection, nitrogen diffusion mainly promotes the release of a large amount of free CH4 from the coal body, resulting in CH4 dominating the outlet gas. Consequently, the CH4 volume fraction accounts for a large proportion and remains relatively stable. In the second stage, continuous N2 injection promotes nitrogen diffusion into the coal matrix and micropore spaces, thereby facilitating CH4 transport and release, while the partial-pressure effect of the injected nitrogen also induces continuous CH4 desorption. However, because the CH4 desorption rate is lower than the N2 injection rate, the N2 volume fraction at the outlet gradually increases, whereas the CH4 volume fraction correspondingly decreases. In the final stage, N2-driven CH4 desorption–diffusion gradually ceases, and N2 becomes the dominant gas phase in the coal body, with almost no further CH4 desorption occurring. As a result, the volume fractions of N2 and CH4 gradually stabilize and no longer change significantly with time.

Changing law of volume fraction of outlet gas components.

N2 promotes CH4 desorption mainly by reducing the partial pressure of CH4 in coal. − Because the adsorption affinity of N2 is weaker than that of CH4, it exhibits little competitive adsorption with CH4. Therefore, N2 can penetrate the coal body relatively rapidly. The penetration times of N2 under different injection conditions are presented in Table . As shown in Table , under different N2 injection modes, the penetration time of N2-defined as the time at which the CH4 volume fraction begins to decrease-was 211 s for the Sin-200–0 mode, 229 s for the Sin-175–25 mode, 231 s for the Sin-150–50 mode, 192 s for the Laddered-200–0 mode, 203 s for the Laddered-175–25 mode, 212 s for the Laddered-150–50 mode, and 261 s for the Constant flow-100 mode. Overall, under different injection conditions, a higher initial N2 flow rate during the first half-cycle produced a larger concentration gradient in the coal matrix and micropore system, thereby increasing the CH4 migration rate and shortening the N2 penetration time. Compared with the constant-flow and sinusoidal injection modes, the stepped injection mode had a higher initial N2 flow rate and a longer duration in the first half-cycle. Therefore, the stepped injection mode resulted in a shorter N2 penetration time.

N2 Breakthrough Time for Different Gas Injection Methods

With increasing N2 injection time, the Constant flow-100 mode showed a continuous decrease in the CH4 volume fraction accompanied by a continuous increase in the N2 volume fraction. Under both the sinusoidal and stepped injection modes, when the N2 injection time exceeded 240 s, the CH4 volume fraction decreased during periods of higher N2 injection flow rate but increased slightly during periods of lower N2 injection flow rate. As a result, the CH4 volume fraction exhibited periodic fluctuations with the injection flow rate. This phenomenon mainly arose because desorption–diffusion of adsorbed CH4 in the coal matrix and micropore system continued during the stage when the CH4 volume fraction increased. However, owing to the low N2 injection flow rate, the driving force for gas diffusion in the coal body was insufficient, resulting in substantial CH4 retention within the coal body. Subsequently, when the N2 injection rate increased, the retained CH4 in the coal body was transported to the outlet, leading to an increase in the CH4 volume fraction. As the N2 injection volume further increased, the dilution effect of N2 became stronger, and the amount of CH4 desorbed became smaller than the amount of injected N2, thereby reducing the CH4 volume fraction.

In addition, the decrease–increase fluctuation in CH4 volume fraction was more pronounced under the stepped injection mode. The main reason is that, under the sinusoidal injection mode, the gas injection flow rate varies continuously with time and does not remain at either its maximum or minimum value for long. In other words, when the injection flow rate in the sinusoidal mode reaches its minimum, it begins to increase again in the next moment, thereby providing the driving force required for rapid gas diffusion within the coal body over a relatively short period. By contrast, under the stepped injection mode, the injection flow rate remains at its minimum value for a longer time. Because of the absence of external gas injection during this period, the methane concentration in the coal body tends to become more uniform. Therefore, compared with the sinusoidal injection mode, the stepped injection mode prolongs the residence time of desorbed gas within the coal body during periods of low N2 injection rate, resulting in a more pronounced increase in the CH4 volume fraction when the injection rate rises again. However, this phenomenon became increasingly evident as the CH4 concentration decreased, indicating that the CH4 desorption rate was low.

Change in Outlet Mixing Flow Rate

Figure shows the variation in the mixed-gas flow rate with time. As shown in Figure , under the Constant flow-100 injection condition, with increasing N2 injection time, the mixed-gas flow rate at the outlet exhibited a pattern of increase–decrease-increase-stabilization. This behavior was mainly attributed to the initial stage of N2 injection, during which nitrogen gradually diffused into the coal matrix and micropores, causing the originally high-concentration CH4 in the coal matrix and micropores to diffuse outward and resulting in a gradual increase in the outlet gas flow rate. However, at this stage, the N2 concentration within the coal body was low, resulting in small concentration gradients both within the coal matrix and between the coal matrix and the micropores. This, in turn, slowed the diffusion of both CH4 and N2 within the coal body and consequently reduced the mixed-gas flow rate at the outlet. As the N2 injection time increased, the cumulative N2 injection volume into the coal body increased, leading to a gradual rise in the N2 concentration. After N2 penetration, CH4 in the matrix fractures was largely discharged, and N2 opened the pre-existing microfracture system, thereby gradually increasing the concentration gradient of CH4 between the coal matrix and the matrix fractures, accelerating CH4 diffusion out of the coal body while also increasing the diffusion rate of N2, which in turn caused the mixed-gas flow rate at the outlet to increase gradually. However, because the total gas discharge at the outlet exceeded the injection flow rate, the injected gas could not be replenished sufficiently, resulting in a temporary decrease in the gas concentration gradient within the coal body. This slowed the diffusion rate and caused a temporary decrease in the outlet mixed-gas flow rate. With continued N2 injection, the N2 within the coal body was replenished, and after a brief decline, the mixed-gas flow rate at the outlet gradually increased again. When the mixed-gas flow rate at the outlet reached a certain level, continuous N2 injection caused the ratio of N2 injection volume to outlet mixed-gas discharge to stabilize, the gas concentration gradient within the coal body to approach a steady state, and the outlet mixed-gas flow rate to stabilize at 1.99 mL/s. In addition, Figure shows that under the sinusoidal and stepped injection modes, the outlet mixed-gas flow rate exhibited periodic variations with changes in the N2 injection flow rate. Higher N2 injection rates led to larger outlet mixed-gas flow rates, whereas lower N2 injection rates resulted in smaller outlet mixed-gas flow rates.

Changing law of outlet gas mixture flow rate ((a) Sinusoidal and constant current injection of N2; (b) Ladder and constant-current injection of N2).

A comparison of the mixed-gas flow rates under different injection modes is shown in Figure . As shown in Figure , when the injection flow rate varied between 200 and 0 mL/min, the maximum mixed-gas flow rate at the outlet under the stepped injection mode was higher than that under the sinusoidal injection mode, whereas the minimum mixed-gas flow rate at the outlet was lower than that under the sinusoidal injection mode as the N2 injection time increased. The main reason is that, under the stepped injection mode, gas was injected into the coal body directly at either 200 or 0 mL/min, resulting in the instantaneous formation of relatively large or small concentration gradients within the coal body. N2 therefore diffused into the coal body relatively rapidly or slowly, and these high- or low-concentration regions persisted for a certain period. The abrupt change in injection flow rate caused sharp fluctuations in gas concentration within the coal body, thereby significantly affecting the diffusion rate of gas molecules. In contrast, under the sinusoidal injection mode, the gas flow rate changed gradually in a sinusoidal manner from 100 to 200 mL/min, from 200 to 0 mL/min, and from 0 to 100 mL/min. This periodic variation in flow rate caused periodic changes in the concentrations of N2 and CH4 within the coal body, but with smaller amplitudes and shorter durations. As a result, compared with the stepped injection mode, the migration rates of N2 and CH4 into and out of the coal body were relatively lower in the high-flow region and relatively higher in the low-flow region under the sinusoidal injection mode. This indicates that, during a given period, the gas concentration gradient within the coal body could be either higher or lower under the stepped injection mode than under the sinusoidal injection mode. Specifically, in the high-flow region, the maximum outlet mixed-gas flow rate was 3.6224 mL/min for the stepped injection mode, whereas it was 3.30011 mL/min for the sinusoidal injection mode. In contrast, in the low-flow region, during the transition from high to low and then back to high flow rates, the minimum outlet mixed-gas flow rate was 0.00021 mL/min for the stepped injection mode, whereas it was 0.00631 mL/min for the sinusoidal injection mode.

Comparison of mixed flow between sinusoidal and step injection methods.

When the injection flow rate varied between 175 and 25 mL/min or between 150 and 50 mL/min, the differences in outlet mixed-gas flow rate between the sinusoidal and stepped injection modes gradually decreased with increasing N2 injection time. This can mainly be attributed to two factors. First, as the injection flow range changed from 175 to 25 to 150–50 mL/min, the difference between the constant flow rate of 175 mL/min in the stepped injection mode and the initial increase in the sinusoidal injection mode from 100 to 175 mL/min became smaller during the transition from low to high injection flow rate. Consequently, the difference in gas concentration gradients generated by the two injection modes decreased, and the gas diffusion rates became more similar. Second, when the injection flow rate decreased from high to low, although the stepped injection mode had a lower injection flow rate, the gas concentration gradient within the coal body did not decay rapidly. This implies that the driving-force difference for gas transport within the coal body between the two injection modes became smaller. As a result of these two factors, the outlet mixed-gas flow rates under the stepped and sinusoidal injection modes became closer when the injection flow rate varied between 175 and 25 mL/min or between 150 and 50 mL/min. Specifically, when the injection flow rate varied between 175 and 25 mL/min, the maximum outlet mixed-gas flow rate was 2.8379 mL/min for the stepped injection mode and 2.84501 mL/min for the sinusoidal injection mode. When the injection flow rate varied between 150 and 50 mL/min, the maximum outlet mixed-gas flow rate was 2.50467 mL/min for the stepped injection mode and 2.43323 mL/min for the sinusoidal injection mode. These results further indicate that the lower the peak injection flow rate and the higher the trough injection flow rate, the closer the outlet mixed-gas flow rates under the stepped and sinusoidal injection modes. In addition, we speculate that this phenomenon may also be related to the period set in the present study.

Changes in Cumulative CH4 Production

Different flow-rate adjustment modes may produce temporal fluctuations in the injection rate. However, if the average flow rate remains constant, the cumulative injected volume should remain essentially unchanged. This is because the average flow rate represents the total volume or mass of fluid passing through a given point per unit time. Regardless of temporal fluctuations in flow rate, maintaining the same average flow rate ensures that nearly the same volume or mass of fluid passes through that point over the same period. Therefore, under different injection modes, the cumulative injected volume of N2 within the same time interval should remain nearly constant, as shown in Figure . However, slight deviations in the cumulative N2 volume were observed under different gas injection conditions due to experimental apparatus errors, although we consider these deviations to have no significant influence on the overall experimental results. As shown in Figure , the cumulative injected volume of N2 under different gas injection conditions was approximately 24 L.

Cumulative N2 injection.

The variation in CH4 production under different gas injection modes is shown in Figure . As shown in the figure, although the cumulative CH4 production differed among different injection modes or among different flow-rate conditions within the same injection mode, the overall temporal trend was similar. Stage 1 (cycles 1–8) was characterized by a rapid increase in cumulative CH4 production, Stage 2 (cycles 9–17) showed a gradual leveling off, and Stage 3 (cycles 17–24) exhibited an approximately stable cumulative CH4 production. During Stage 1, both the free CH4 in the pore spaces and the CH4 desorbed from the adsorbed state were expelled from the coal samples. In Stages 2 and 3, the produced CH4 mainly originated from CH4 desorbed from the adsorbed state into the free state within the coal samples. As reported in ref, the desorption rate of CH4 decreases with time. Therefore, in Stages 2 and 3, CH4 production gradually stabilized after leveling off.

Changing law of CH4 production.

It was found that the final cumulative CH4 production varied under the same N2 injection time and average flow-rate conditions. Specifically, under the Sin-200–0, Sin-175–25, and Sin-150–50 conditions, the final cumulative CH4 production was 3865.130, 4025.816, and 4027.109 mL, respectively. Under the Laddered-200–0, Laddered-175–25, and Laddered-150–50 conditions, the final cumulative CH4 production was 3969.377, 3999.436, and 4011.541 mL, respectively. In addition, the final cumulative CH4 production under the Constant flow-100 condition was 3996.506 mL. The experimental results indicated that, for the same injection duration, the CH4 production capacities under different injection conditions followed the descending order: Sin-150–50 > Sin-175–25 > Laddered-150–50 > Laddered-175–25 > Constant flow-100 > Laddered-200–0 > Sin-200–0. These results suggest that when the average N2 injection flow rate was 100 mL/min and the injected N2 volume within the same period was constant, variable-flow N2 injection modes (Sin-150–50, Sin-175–25, Laddered-150–50, and Laddered-175–25) could desorb more CH4 and increase CH4 production, thereby improving coalbed methane recovery. The maximum cumulative CH4 production under the dynamic injection condition was 1.0076 times that under the Constant flow-100 condition. The experimental results further showed that, when the injection duration and average injection flow rate were kept constant, the stepped injection mode was more effective when the injection flow-rate range was 200–0, whereas the sinusoidal injection mode was more effective when the injection flow-rate ranges were 175–25 and 150–50.

Notably, under the same injection mode, CH4 production at the 150–50 flow-rate condition was higher than that under the other flow-rate conditions. Therefore, maintaining a certain N2 injection rate in the low-flow region was more effective in promoting CH4 production, and this effect was more pronounced under the sinusoidal injection mode. The main reasons are as follows. First, in the low-flow region, the driving force provided by N2 was insufficient to promptly transport the desorbed CH4 out of the coal matrix. Second, the decrease in pore pressure caused contraction of the coal matrix, thereby increasing the diffusion resistance of CH4. These combined effects may have caused part of the desorbed CH4 to be readsorbed into the coal matrix, requiring subsequent N2 injection to flush it out. Under sinusoidal injection, the gas concentration within the coal matrix changed gradually, without abrupt transitions. This implies that when the N2 injection rate reached its minimum, and the gas concentration gradient within the coal matrix decreased to a certain level, the subsequent increase in injection flow rate gradually raised the gas concentration within the coal matrix. This gradual process facilitated continued N2 diffusion into the coal matrix while driving CH4 outward. In contrast, under stepped injection, each injection stage maintained a constant flow rate. Under the low-flow condition, N2 diffused more slowly into the coal matrix, which reduced the driving force for CH4 migration and increased the likelihood of readsorption of desorbed CH4, thereby requiring the higher-flow stage in the subsequent cycle to flush the CH4 out. However, the flushing efficiency of N2 decreased with increasing injection cycles. Therefore, under sinusoidal injection conditions, maintaining a certain N2 injection rate in the low-flow region more effectively promoted coalbed methane recovery. Meanwhile, the final CH4 production results indicate that when the injection mode, injection duration, and average flow rate were kept constant, varying the injection flow rate from 200 to 0 not only failed to desorb more CH4, but also required the same N2 injection volume, leading to resource wastage and making this condition unsuitable for N2 displacement of coal seam gas. For example, when the injection flow rate varied from 200 to 0, the final CH4 production was 3865.130 mL under sinusoidal injection and 3969.377 mL under stepped injection. Under the same injection mode, the final CH4 production under Sin-150–50 and Sin-175–25 was 1.0419 and 1.0415 times that under Sin-200–0, respectively, whereas that under Laddered-150–50 and Laddered-175–25 was 1.0106 and 1.0074 times that under Laddered-200–0, respectively. Therefore, under sinusoidal injection, maintaining a certain N2 injection rate in the low-flow region had a more pronounced effect on promoting CH4 production. If the goal is to reduce coal seam hazards, such as coal and gas outbursts, gas explosions, and gas poisoning, a combination of sinusoidal, constant-flow, and stepped injection modes may be more effective. In recent years, some progress has also been made in the utilization of low-concentration gas (1%–6%), including heat-storage oxidation, porous-medium premixed combustion, and pulsating combustion technologies. Therefore, the stepped injection mode may also offer certain advantages for promoting the utilization and production enhancement of low-concentration gas.

Figure (b) presents the variation in the average effective diffusion coefficient under different amplitudes for both sinusoidal and stepped injection modes. Overall, under the same average injection rate, the average effective diffusion coefficient exhibits a clear decreasing trend under different dynamic injection boundaries, and the sensitivity of this decline varies with waveform type. This indicates that the gas release capacity of the coal matrix during the N2-ECBM process is not determined solely by the external injection intensity in a monotonic manner, but rather by the degree of matching between the external driving rhythm and the internal mass-transfer response of the matrix.

For sinusoidal injection, the average effective diffusion coefficient decreases from 1.65 × 10–5 for Sin-150–50 to 1.58 × 10–5 for Sin-175–25, and then further drops markedly to 1.21 × 10–5 for Sin-200–0. For stepped injection, the average effective diffusion coefficient declines from 1.48 × 10–5 for Laddered-150–50 to 1.43 × 10–5 for Laddered-175–25 and 1.42 × 10–5 for Laddered-200–0. These results indicate that, during N2 displacement of CH4 in coal, a moderate dynamic perturbation is conducive to maintaining a relatively high equivalent mass-transfer capacity, whereas excessively large flow fluctuations instead weaken the average effective release of gas from the matrix. In other words, dynamic injection does not follow a “the stronger the fluctuation, the better” principle. Instead, there exists an optimal perturbation range, beyond which the diffusion process becomes significantly inhibited.

Furthermore, comparison between the two waveforms shows that under low- and moderate-amplitude conditions, the average effective diffusion coefficient under sinusoidal injection is higher than that under stepped injection; specifically, Sin-150–50 is higher than Laddered-150–50, and Sin-175–25 is higher than Laddered-175–25. However, under the maximum amplitude condition, Sin-200–0 is lower than Laddered-200–0. This phenomenon indicates that the external boundary differs not only in magnitude but also in its mode of variation, both of which can significantly affect the mass-transfer response of the coal matrix. Under low- and moderate-amplitude conditions, the smooth and continuous variation of the sinusoidal boundary allows the gas-phase conditions on the fracture side to adjust gradually, thereby facilitating the continuous tracking of external forcing by CH4 desorption, diffusion, and matrix-fracture interfacial exchange within the coal matrix, which results in a higher average effective diffusion coefficient. In contrast, the stepped boundary represents an abrupt perturbation, where the instantaneous switching between high and low flow rates interrupts the mass-transfer process before it has been fully established within the matrix. This makes short-term mismatch between boundary variation and internal response more likely, leading to an overall lower equivalent diffusion capacity.

However, when the amplitude further increases to 100, the average effective diffusion coefficient under sinusoidal injection drops to the lowest value, indicating that a smooth boundary is not always advantageous under all conditions. When the amplitude is sufficiently large, sinusoidal injection no longer represents merely a smooth variation,” but instead turns into a continuously forced perturbation over the entire cycle: the flow rate, composition, and CH4 partial pressure environment on the fracture side remain in a state of rapid change throughout. For CH4 within the coal matrix, the release process involves multiple stages, including desorption from the adsorbed state, diffusion through micro- and nanopores, and matrix–fracture exchange, all of which are characterized by pronounced intrinsic lag and a limited supply rate. Under such conditions, although the external boundary remains continuous, its rate of variation exceeds the adjustment rate of internal mass transfer within the matrix. As a result, before the internal concentration field and partial-pressure field can fully respond to the current boundary condition, a new boundary perturbation has already been imposed, causing the internal release process to be repeatedly reset and interrupted. It is precisely under this continuous forcing that the time scale mismatch between fracture-side driving, and matrix gas supply becomes most severe, which is why Sin-200–0 exhibits the lowest average effective diffusion coefficient.

Implications for Gas Disaster Prevention

N2-ECBM, a coal mine gas extraction technology with significant engineering potential, not only enhances coal bed methane production but also plays a crucial role in mitigating coal mine gas disasters. In China, the gas concentration in underground coal mining working faces should not surpass 1%. Additionally, the residual gas content of the coal seam in underground coal mining should be lower than the Threshold Limit Value (TLV). , Hence, we employ a gas concentration of 1% and a minimal residual gas amount as the TLV for comparative analysis. Here, the residual gas amount is defined as the difference between the cumulative gas production at a given time and the preadsorbed gas amount, as expressed in eq (). 6 Re(CH4)=Pt(CH4)Ad(CH4) Where Re (CH4) denotes the residual CH4 content, mL, and Ad (CH4) denotes the preadsorbed CH4 amount, mL.

Figure illustrates the variation of TLV for different injection methods. Implications for CO2 Storage

CO2 sequestration in abandoned mines has been a prominent research topic in recent years. This approach can not only replace CH4 in coal seams to be used as natural gas but also sequester CO2 into the coal body to reduce the production of greenhouse gases, with multiple significant benefits. The CO2-ECBM project aims to achieve three primary goals: First, to replace a large amount of CH4 by consuming as little CO2 as possible; Second, to sequester more CO2 into the coal body; and last, to control the injection pressure of CO2 to avoid CO2 protrusion accidents. For the following reasons, our study focuses on investigating the effect of different injection N2 flow methods on CH4 transport and diffusion under conditions that minimize injection pressure, thereby avoiding excessive injection pressure. Consequently, we do not focus on the impact of pressure size on CH4 desorption and diffusion, despite its potential to increase CBM output. We propose two gas injection methods and six variations in injection flow rates to explore how these injection conditions can increase CBM production while improving the N2 utilization rate. Discussion on the change rule of CH4 cumulative production with the same injection time is presented in Sections and 3.4, and the change rule of N2 utilization and residual CH4 in Sections and 4.2. Subsequently, we discuss N2 retention in the coal body to provide guidance for CO2 sequestration.

Compared various injection methods of N2 within the coal body at identical injection durations, Figure presents the N2 retention levels. It is evident from Figure that the Constant flow-100 injection method yields the least N2 retention within the coal body, whereas under dynamic injection conditions, N2 retention within the coal body consistently exceeds that of Constant flow-100. The primary rationale behind this observation lies in the weaker adsorption affinity of N2 compared to CH4. Consequently, after the desorption of CH4 from the coal body, the remaining adsorption sites further adsorb N2 with minimal competition for adsorption. However, N2 adsorption by the coal body occurs only after a certain duration. The Constant flow-100 injection method maintains a stable and continuous flow rate, thereby providing a steady driving force. This sustained injection of N2 flushes out the N2 displaced by CH4 in the preceding moment, which hampers N2 adsorption. Conversely, under rheological injection, during low-flow conditions, the injection rate is lower, affording the coal body sufficient time to adsorb N2, thus favoring N2 adsorption and resulting in greater N2 retention within the coal body. Similarly, CO2 exhibits a stronger adsorption affinity than CH4. Therefore, we hypothesize that coal body adsorption of CO2 occurs more rapidly than that of N2. Consequently, this dynamic gas injection method is more conducive to CO2 sequestration. If the mechanisms by which different injection modes affect gas residence time and adsorption-retention behavior are to some extent universal, dynamic injection may, on this basis, be more favorable for the adsorption and retention of CO2 in coal, given that the adsorption capacity of CO2 is stronger than that of CH4 and significantly greater than that of N2.

Implications of Dynamic N2-ECBM for Engineering Applications and Research Perspectives

Researchers in the coal mining field have been committed to exploring methods for improving coal seam gas recovery. − However, Due to limitations in the capacity of underground facilities such as nitrogen injection and pressurization, the availability of nitrogen for underground N2-ECBM is restricted. The efficiency of utilizing this limited nitrogen resource determines the future engineering applicability of nitrogen injection displacement technology. This study compares dynamic injection and constant flow injection methods in terms of methane production, nitrogen utilization rate, and prevention of coal mine gas disasters. It analyzes the superiority of different injection methods in terms of injection flow rate magnitude. We believe that, when the nitrogen injection time is equal, the sinusoidal injection method demonstrates advantages in final methane production, nitrogen utilization rate, and prevention of coal mine gas disasters. However, as described in Section , with an increase in the number of nitrogen circulation cycles, the stepped injection method shows higher methane production. Therefore, in future on-site studies, a combined rheological nitrogen injection method should be given more attention for N2-ECBM applications. However, our experimental conditions utilized granular coal as the medium, focusing mainly on the adsorption–desorption-diffusion process of methane, without considering the seepage process of methane within the matrix and fractures. For block coal, the influence of nitrogen injection volume on coal permeability is also significant, being one of the primary factors determining coalbed gas production, which will be a focal point in our future research endeavors.

Conclusion

In this study, two gas injection methods, each with six variations in injection flow, were designed, using the average injection flow as the condition for injection. The study explores the mechanism through which variations in injection flow affect CH4 diffusion by different injection methods. The results of this study are anticipated to augment the efficacy of N2-ECBM technology, thereby significantly contributing to its widespread adoption and utilization in downhole applications. The primary conclusions drawn from the study are as follows:1Under the same average N2 injection rate and injection duration, a higher injection rate in the early stage led to earlier N2 breakthrough. Compared with the Constant flow-100 mode, dynamic injection with a certain level of N2 maintained during the low-flow period significantly increased CH4 production. Under the same dynamic injection mode, a smaller difference between the peak and trough injection rates resulted in higher CH4 production. Sinusoidal injection and constant-flow injection were more favorable to produce high-concentration CH4, whereas stepped injection was more suitable for the utilization of low-concentration methane. A higher initial injection rate corresponded to a lower average effective diffusion coefficient of CH4. The average effective diffusion coefficient of CH4 under the Sin-150–50 mode was 1.15 times that under the Constant flow-100 mode.2For the same N2 injection duration, the Sin-200–0 mode yielded the lowest CH4 production but the highest N2 utilization efficiency. Compared with the Constant flow mode, the times required for Sin-200–0 to reach T 50%, T 30%, and T 10% were shortened by 183, 263, and 558 s, respectively, while the cumulative N2 injection volumes were reduced by 233, 393, and 668 mL, respectively, substantially lowering the cost of N2-ECBM. Considering both the CH4 concentration threshold limit value (TLV) and the residual CH4 content at the TLV, the Sin-150–50 mode required less time to reduce the CH4 concentration to the TLV and resulted in a lower residual CH4 content at the TLV, making it more suitable for methane hazard control in coal mines. Under dynamic injection conditions, the amount of N2 retained in the coal was higher than that under Constant flow-100, suggesting a potentially better application prospect for CO2 sequestration.3The gas source of downhole N2-ECBM is limited, and how to improve the utilization efficiency of the limited gas source determines the future engineering applicability of nitrogen injection and replacement technology. The combined rheological N2 injection method can be more effective in improving N2 utilization, CH4 production, and gas disaster management. In the future, it should be more worthy to be paid attention to be applied in the implementation of N2-ECBM in underground coal mines. However, our experimental conditions used granular coal as the medium, which was mainly the adsorption–desorption-diffusion process of CH4 and did not involve the seepage process of CH4 matrix and fissures. For lump coal, the effect of N2 injection on the permeability of the coal body is also important and is one of the main factors determining the production of CBM, which will be emphasized by us in future research.
