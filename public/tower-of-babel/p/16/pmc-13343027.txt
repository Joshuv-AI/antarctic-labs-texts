Temporal association of constipation and diarrhoea with a subsequent diagnosis of colorectal cancer: a propensity score–matched case–control study using real-world primary care data
Authors: Ernst W Kolbe, Sarah Krieg, Christoph Roderburg, Sven H Loosen, Andreas Krieg, Karel Kostev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigated whether chronic constipation and diarrhoea are temporally linked to colorectal cancer (CRC) diagnosis, aiming to clarify if these symptoms function as modifiable risk factors or early indicators of malignancy.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective case–control study using real-world data from the German Disease Analyser database. After 1:5 propensity score matching for age, sex, index year and comorbidities, 10 941 patients with CRC and 54 705 matched controls without cancer were included in the study. Documentation of constipation and diarrhoea was evaluated in six time intervals up to 5 years before CRC diagnosis. Multivariable conditional logistic regression was used to calculate ORs and 95% CIs for CRC risk.ResultsConstipation was significantly more frequent in patients with CRC within 6 months prior to CRC diagnosis (OR 3.23; 95% CI 2.85 to 3.66). A similar pattern was observed for diarrhoea within 6 months prior to diagnosis. However, the association was stronger the more frequently diarrhoea was documented (at least one diagnosis: OR 3.37; 95% CI 3.03 to 3.75; at least two diagnoses: OR 4.18; 95% CI 3.31 to 5.28; at least three diagnoses: OR 6.82; 95% CI 4.43 to 10.49). No associations were identified beyond 1 year before diagnosis.ConclusionConstipation and diarrhoea were associated with the subsequent diagnosis of CRC only in the immediate months before diagnosis, suggesting reverse causality rather than a causal relationship. These symptoms may serve as red flags rather than risk factors, emphasising the need for timely diagnostic evaluation, especially in the presence of persistent changes in bowel habits.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignancies worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality.1 Recent global estimates indicate that CRC accounts for nearly 2 million new diagnoses and close to 1 million deaths per year, with particularly high incidence levels in regions such as Europe, Australia/New Zealand and North America.1

It is broadly recognised that shifts in lifestyle and dietary habits constitute the principal drivers of rising CRC incidence in populations undergoing socioeconomic transition. Numerous modifiable and non-modifiable factors influence CRC risk. Non-modifiable determinants include age, sex and hereditary factors, whereas lifestyle-related exposures—most notably smoking, limited fibre intake, frequent consumption of processed or red meat, alcohol use, excess body weight and insufficient physical activity—have repeatedly been implicated in elevating CRC risk.2 3

In addition, irregularities in bowel habits, including chronic constipation or diarrhoea, have been proposed as potential risk factors.4 However, whether such alterations represent modifiable risk determinants or early manifestations of malignancy remains controversial.58

Sonnenberg et al demonstrated in their meta-analysis of nine case–control studies a statistically significant increase in the risk of CRC among individuals with constipation or infrequent bowel movements.9 A subsequent and substantially larger synthesis of epidemiologic evidence, encompassing both cohort and case–control research and totalling more than 1.4 million individuals, found no compelling association between constipation and CRC.10 Still, subgroup analyses suggested a positive association in case–control studies, which are particularly prone to recall bias.11 Moreover, a meta-analysis focusing solely on prospective cohort studies also failed to establish a significant connection when comparing individuals with daily versus less frequent bowel movements.12

Other research has explored whether diarrhoea or frequent stools might contribute to CRC risk, potentially due to chronic irritation of the intestinal lining. One meta-analysis12 involving seven prospective studies1316 found no consistent link between bowel frequency and colon cancer, although a significant association was observed between frequent defecation and rectal cancer.

Nonetheless, constipation and diarrhoea can also represent symptoms of CRC, raising the possibility that observed associations may reflect reverse causality rather than causation.17

Establishing a definitive causal link between bowel habit alterations—specifically constipation or diarrhoea—and CRC remains inherently difficult, primarily due to the methodological limitations of prior observational research. Although several meta-analyses and large observational studies have explored this topic, their findings are inconsistent and sometimes contradictory.5 6 8 10 12 18 In many cases, the interpretation of results has been limited by differences in study design, reliance on self-reported symptoms and the absence of a precise temporal framework that distinguishes long-term risk factors from early clinical signs of malignancy. In addition, previous studies often lacked comprehensive adjustment for confounding factors or did not make use of real-world data from routine primary care, where most patients first present with gastrointestinal complaints.

In this context, the present case–control study was conducted to address these gaps. Using a large, representative database from general practices in Germany, we examined the temporal association between documented constipation or diarrhoea and a subsequent diagnosis of CRC. By analysing symptom documentation across several predefined time intervals before diagnosis and applying a propensity score–matched design, we aimed to clarify whether alterations in bowel habits act as genuine risk factors or rather as early indicators of CRC. Although changes in bowel habits are widely recognised as alarm symptoms in clinical practice, previous research has not systematically examined when constipation or diarrhoea begin to differ between individuals who later receive a CRC diagnosis and those who do not. Most prior studies relied on self-reported symptoms or single baseline assessments, limiting the ability to evaluate temporal patterns. By analysing six discrete time intervals over a 5-year period using routinely documented primary care diagnoses, our study provides temporal resolution that has been lacking in the literature. This approach enables us to distinguish long-standing symptom patterns from those that emerge only shortly before diagnosis, thereby helping to clarify whether observed associations may reflect early manifestations of CRC rather than long-term risk indicators.

Methods

Database

This retrospective study was based on anonymised routinely collected outpatient data obtained from the Disease Analyser database (IQVIA), which includes information from approximately 3000 general and specialist practices across Germany. Real-world data (RWD) refer to health-related information routinely collected in everyday clinical practice, such as electronic health records, outpatient documentation and physician-recorded diagnoses. The database provides longitudinal data on patient demographics, physician-recorded diagnoses and prescribed medications. Information on the structure and sampling of participating practices has been described previously,19 where the database is reported to reflect the distribution of outpatient care providers in Germany. The Disease Analyser database has been widely used in epidemiological research, including multiple studies focusing on CRC.20 21

Study population

The study population comprised individuals aged 18 years or older with an initial diagnosis of CRC (ICD-10, International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th revision; codes C18–C20) recorded between January 2015 and December 2023. The date of the first CRC diagnosis was defined as the index date. To ensure adequate temporal assessment of exposures, inclusion required at least 5 years of continuous observation prior to the index date and no documented malignant disease during this period. This requirement ensured that all individuals contributed complete information to each of the predefined 5-year time intervals. Patients with shorter observation periods would not have been eligible for the temporal analyses and were therefore excluded. This criterion was applied symmetrically to cases and controls to maintain comparability and avoid differential misclassification.

A cancer-free control group was established using nearest-neighbour propensity score matching (PSM) at a ratio of 1:5 without replacement. The propensity score included age, sex, index year and relevant comorbidities documented during the preceding 5 years, including obesity (ICD-10: E66), diabetes mellitus (ICD-10: E10–E14), inflammatory bowel diseases (ICD-10: K50, K51), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD; ICD-10: J44) and benign gastrointestinal neoplasms (ICD-10: D12). To ensure adequate comparability between cases and controls, a calliper width of 0.1 SD of the logit-transformed propensity score was applied.

Obesity was defined based on physician-recorded diagnoses, as body mass index values were incompletely documented and therefore not used. Information on smoking status was available for only a small proportion of patients (<5%); consequently, COPD was included as an approximate proxy for long-term tobacco exposure, acknowledging its limited ability to capture individual smoking intensity or duration.

For control participants, the index date was assigned to a randomly selected consultation occurring between January 2015 and December 2023. A schematic overview of the study population is provided in figure 1.

Study outcome

The primary outcome was a diagnosis of CRC, defined by ICD-10 codes C18–C20. The main exposure variables were physician-documented diagnoses of constipation (ICD-10: K58.2, K59.0) and diarrhoea (ICD-10: K58.1, K59.1, K52.9, A09.9) recorded during the 5-year period preceding the index date. These ICD-10 codes represent specific diagnostic categories and do not function as umbrella terms. Conditions such as IBS-C (irritable bowel syndrome–constipation predominant), opioid-induced constipation, overflow diarrhoea or general ‘change in bowel habit’ were only captured if the treating physician explicitly coded one of the included ICD-10 diagnoses. Broader symptom descriptions recorded in free-text fields were not accessible in the database and therefore could not be incorporated. To examine the temporal relationship between bowel habit alterations and CRC diagnosis, the timing of constipation and diarrhoea diagnoses was evaluated across predefined intervals relative to the index date: ≤6 months, 6 months to 1 year, >1 to 2 years, >2 to 3 years, >3 to 4 years and >4 to 5 years. Because information on symptom duration or severity was unavailable, the frequency of recorded diagnoses (at least one, two or three occurrences) was used as a pragmatic proxy to differentiate between potentially acute and more chronic symptom presentations. For each predefined time interval, all constipation or diarrhoea diagnoses documented within that interval were counted. Patients were classified as having ‘at least one’, ‘at least two’ or ‘at least three’ diagnoses if the total number of diagnoses recorded within that specific interval met the respective threshold. Diagnoses occurring outside the interval were not considered for that interval. Thus, for the ≤6-month interval, for example, only diagnoses documented during the 6 months immediately preceding the index date contributed to the classification.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of cases and controls after PSM were assessed using standardised mean differences (SMDs), with values below 0.1 indicating adequate covariate balance.22

Associations between prior diagnoses of constipation or diarrhoea and a subsequent CRC diagnosis were examined using conditional logistic regression models with multivariable adjustment. All variables included in the PSM—age, sex, index year, obesity, diabetes mellitus, inflammatory bowel disease, COPD and benign gastrointestinal neoplasms—were incorporated into the regression models to ensure consistent adjustment for potential confounders. Results are reported as ORs with corresponding 95% CIs. To reduce the risk of type I error associated with multiple comparisons, a more stringent significance threshold of p<0.01 was applied. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

As analyses were based on routinely coded diagnoses from electronic medical records, missing data for the main exposure variables were not present. Variables with incomplete documentation, such as smoking status and body mass index, were not included directly in the models.

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline, and the completed checklist is provided in online supplemental table S1.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Following 1:5 PSM, 10 941 patients with CRC and 54 705 matched controls (individuals without CRC) were included. The mean age at diagnosis was 70.4 years, and 46.3% were female. Coexisting diagnoses were well balanced across groups (all SMDs <0.1). Among CRC cases, 7558 (69.1%) were diagnosed with colon cancer, 3172 (29.0%) with rectal cancer and 211 (1.9%) with cancer of the rectosigmoid junction (table 1).

Association between constipation and subsequent CRC diagnosis

Within the 6 months preceding CRC diagnosis, 3.8% of cases and 1.2% of controls had at least one constipation diagnosis; 0.6% vs 0.3% had at least two; and 0.2% vs 0.1% had three or more. In previous intervals, these proportions were similar between groups (figure 2). A single constipation diagnosis within 6 months was significantly associated with CRC (OR 3.23; 95% CI 2.85 to 3.66), although the strength of the association appeared to weaken as the number of recorded constipation diagnoses increased, becoming statistically insignificant after three or more instances (table 2).

Association between diarrhoea and subsequent CRC diagnosis

In the same 6-month window, diarrhoea was documented in 5.4% of patients with CRC and 1.6% of controls at least once; 1.2% vs 0.3% at least twice; and 0.5% vs 0.1% three or more times. Between 6 months and 1 year prior to diagnosis, 1.9% of cases and 1.3% of controls had one or more diarrhoea diagnoses. For previous time periods, frequencies were similar between groups (figure 2). Absolute numbers corresponding to these proportions are provided in online supplemental table S2.

Diarrhoea within 6 months before diagnosis was significantly associated with CRC, and the strength of this relationship increased with repeated documentation of diarrhoea (at least one diagnosis: OR 3.37; 95% CI 3.03 to 3.75; at least two diagnoses: OR 4.18; 95% CI 3.31 to 5.28; at least three diagnoses: OR 6.82; 95% CI 4.43 to 10.49) (table 2). Between 6 months and 1 year before CRC diagnosis, the association remained significant for a single documentation of diarrhoea (OR 1.46; 95% CI 1.24 to 1.70) but was not present beyond 1 year (table 2).

Discussion

This nationwide case–control study used routinely collected primary care data from practices across Germany to investigate the potential relationship between altered bowel movements and CRC diagnosis. In order to limit confounding, PSM was applied to enhance the comparability of exposure groups in observational settings.23 Through this method, we found that both constipation and diarrhoea were more commonly recorded in patients with CRC than controls within 6 months prior to diagnosis. However, these associations diminished or disappeared when symptoms occurred further in the past. To clarify the concept of reverse temporality, we explicitly examined whether constipation or diarrhoea were documented long before CRC diagnosis. If these symptoms were associated with long-term CRC risk, one would expect elevated frequencies several years prior to diagnosis. Instead, the associations appeared abruptly within the 6 months before diagnosis and were absent beyond 1 year. This temporal pattern suggests that constipation and diarrhoea are more likely to reflect early clinical manifestations of CRC rather than long-standing exposures associated with future disease.

While previous case–control studies2426 and one cohort study27 have also reported a significant association between constipation and CRC, a recently published meta-analysis found no significant association between constipation and CRC when all included studies were considered.10 However, this meta-analysis revealed a pronounced heterogeneity, which can be explained by the variability of the observed effects in the different studies. The same observation pertains to cohort studies examining the correlation between high frequency of bowel movements and CRC.12 In this context, a meta-analysis published in 2020, which also exhibited substantial heterogeneity, could not substantiate a significant association between high frequency of bowel movements (defined as >1/day) and CRC.12 However, a subsequent subgroup analysis revealed an association between rectal cancers and a previous high stool frequency.12

A general shortcoming of the majority of studies published to date is the recording of bowel movement frequency, which has usually been based on self-report questionnaires. This reliance on retrospective self-report increases the likelihood of misclassification, a limitation particularly relevant to case–control designs where differential recall is common. In addition, several studies reported that some participants had taken laxatives before baseline data were collected, which may have affected the results. Another challenge is the lack of standardisation in how constipation and frequent bowel movements are defined, which contributes to substantial methodological variability across studies.

Notwithstanding, the temporal relationship has hitherto received consideration only in one study. Sundbøll et al reported a notably elevated standardised incidence ratio (SIR) for CRC within the first year following a constipation diagnosis, while lower SIRs were observed in subsequent years—especially for rectal cancer.18 The findings of our study support this observation, whereby we defined narrower follow-up periods of ≤6 months, >6 months–1 year, >1–2, >2–3, >3–4, and >4-5 years, respectively. This more refined follow-up period enabled us to demonstrate that the association between a diagnosis of CRC and constipation was only noted in the early phase of the follow-up period (≤6 months). A similar observation was made in the context of the association between diarrhoea and CRC. These results suggest that constipation in proximity to diagnosis is more plausibly interpreted as an early manifestation of occult CRC rather than a causal contributor, consistent with a reverse-causation framework.

Interestingly, the ORs observed for constipation and diarrhoea exhibited opposite trends with increasing frequency of documentation. While the association between constipation and CRC appeared to diminish with three or more recorded diagnoses, the association for diarrhoea became progressively stronger. One possible explanation for this divergence may relate to differences in coding and consultation behaviour. Patients with chronic constipation often receive repeated documentation of the same diagnosis during routine visits without a change in clinical severity, leading to potential dilution of the observed association. In contrast, recurrent documentation of diarrhoea may more frequently reflect persistent or worsening symptoms that prompt further diagnostic evaluation, including colonoscopy, thereby strengthening the temporal association with a subsequent CRC diagnosis. Alternatively, physicians may be more inclined to repeatedly code diarrhoea when it remains unresolved or clinically concerning, whereas constipation might be under-coded once deemed chronic or benign. These differences likely reflect variations in both patient and physician behaviour rather than true pathophysiological disparities between the two symptoms.

Despite the fact that constipation is discussed as a potential risk factor in the scientific literature28 29 and the hypothesis was already proposed in the early 1970s that a low stool frequency with a subsequent slowing of intestinal passage prolongs mucosal contact with suspected carcinogens,30 this theory remains to be confirmed. Also, it has been proposed that diarrhoea and soft stools irritate the intestinal mucosa and may induce pathologic proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells as well as abnormal intestinal epithelial structure. At the molecular level, the role of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is a subject of repeated discussion. PGE2 plays a dual role by promoting colonic inflammation and increasing secretory activity in the intestinal lining, both of which are implicated in CRC development.123133 Steven and colleagues were among the first to provide evidence linking PGE2 to excessive fluid and electrolyte secretion from rectal villous adenomas.34

However, it is important to emphasise that the transformation of colonic adenomas into malignant tumours is a prolonged process. Available longitudinal evidence suggests that the adenoma-carcinoma sequence unfolds over well more than a decade.35 Large-scale registry analyses show that in individuals aged 55 and older with advanced adenomas, the annual progression rates rise from approximately 2.5–3.8% in middle-aged adults to about 5.0–5.6% in older populations.36 Based on this, the findings from Brenner and colleagues suggest that between 25% and 43% of patients with advanced adenomas will develop CRC within a 10-year time frame.36 Taken together, these data support the understanding that the adenoma-carcinoma sequence typically spans more than a decade and that the likelihood of progression increases with advancing age. In light of this extended latency period—and in combination with our own findings and those of Sundbøll et al18—it appears improbable that recent onset of constipation or diarrhoea represents a contributing cause to CRC. Instead, these symptoms are more plausibly interpreted as early clinical signs of an already existing malignancy.

Another important consideration is the potential for detection bias. Patients who present to their physicians with gastrointestinal symptoms such as constipation or diarrhoea are more likely to be referred for diagnostic evaluation, including colonoscopy. This increased likelihood of diagnostic testing may partly explain the strong association between these symptoms and CRC observed shortly before diagnosis. Consequently, the temporal clustering of constipation and diarrhoea in the months preceding CRC detection may reflect heightened medical attention and diagnostic activity rather than a true pathophysiological link. While detection bias may contribute to the clustering of symptom documentation shortly before diagnosis, the absence of any association in earlier years argues against a long-term relationship. If increased diagnostic activity alone explained the findings, a more gradual increase in symptom coding over time would be expected. Instead, the sharp temporal concentration within 6 months supports the interpretation that these symptoms tend to arise in close temporal proximity to CRC diagnosis, consistent with early clinical presentation rather than long-term risk. Importantly, constipation or diarrhoea documented more than 12 months before diagnosis was not associated with CRC. This finding suggests that long-standing bowel habit changes alone are unlikely to represent independent red flag symptoms. In contrast, newly documented symptoms within the immediate pre-diagnostic period may warrant further diagnostic evaluation.

Nonetheless, isolated symptoms such as constipation or diarrhoea alone are unlikely to have sufficient diagnostic weight to justify a colonoscopy, as existing studies have demonstrated that constipation should not be used as a sole indication for lower endoscopy.37 38 Similarly, no uniform guideline recommends endoscopic evaluation solely based on the presence of diarrhoea or increased bowel frequency. However, one study did find CRC in approximately 7% of adults who underwent colonoscopy due to chronic diarrhoea.39 Going forward, there is a pressing need to identify symptom constellations that can more reliably indicate CRC and serve as red flag markers. In this context, a comprehensive case–control analysis involving 5075 individuals diagnosed with CRC before the age of 50 identified four specific warning signs—abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, diarrhoea and iron deficiency anaemia—that appeared at least 3 months before diagnosis and were associated with a heightened cancer risk.40 While additional research is needed to confirm these findings, the study highlights the potential role of symptom-based indicators in CRC screening strategies.

It is important to emphasise several key strengths inherent to the present study. Foremost among them is the inclusion of a large and heterogeneous patient cohort drawn from a broad range of primary care practices, which minimises selection bias commonly associated with studies limited to secondary or tertiary care, where diagnostic access often dictates patient inclusion. Furthermore, the application of PSM was a central methodological element, enabling the adjustment for baseline differences between study groups and thereby enhancing internal validity. This strategy was specifically implemented to reduce the influence of confounding factors and to strengthen the interpretability of RWD. Additionally, the study design accounted for potential reverse causation—namely, the possibility that constipation represents an early manifestation of CRC rather than a contributing factor—by stratifying the 5-year pre-diagnostic interval into six discrete temporal categories.

Nonetheless, it must be acknowledged that observational research is inherently subject to the risk of residual confounding, which may limit the causal interpretability of findings. Moreover, the retrospective design of this study imposes certain methodological constraints. Notably, data on actual stool frequency were unavailable, precluding a more refined categorisation of bowel habits. Additionally, the analysis was restricted to records from general practitioners, with no inclusion of hospital-based data, thereby limiting insights into disease severity at presentation. Due to the absence of information on tumour staging or the anatomical extent of disease at the time of CRC diagnosis, it was not feasible to determine whether altered bowel patterns—such as constipation or diarrhoea—were correlated with more advanced malignancies.

Furthermore, the categorisation of constipation and diarrhoea according to the number of recorded diagnoses represents a simplified proxy for chronicity. Although this approach is commonly used in RWD studies, it may not fully capture the clinical persistence or intensity of symptoms. Another important limitation relates to the specificity of ICD-10 coding. The constipation and diarrhoea codes used in this study do not encompass broader symptom categories such as IBS-C, opioid-induced constipation, overflow diarrhoea or general ‘change in bowel habit,’ unless these were explicitly coded under the included ICD-10 diagnoses. As free-text clinical notes were not available, we were unable to identify these additional conditions. Consequently, our findings reflect associations with the specific ICD-coded diagnoses rather than the full clinical spectrum of altered bowel habits. Additionally, data on smoking status were available in only a small proportion of patients (<5%), preventing its inclusion in the models. To at least partially adjust for smoking-related effects, COPD was used as a proxy variable. However, COPD cannot fully capture individual smoking behaviour, and residual confounding related to tobacco exposure cannot be ruled out. Similarly, body mass index values were not consistently available, and physician-recorded diagnoses of obesity were used instead. This approach may have led to misclassification and residual confounding.

The relatively low prevalence of coded constipation (3.8%) and diarrhoea (5.4%) within 6 months before CRC diagnosis should be interpreted in the context of real-world primary care documentation. In German outpatient practice, bowel habit changes are frequently recorded in free-text notes or managed without formal ICD coding. Therefore, our estimates likely represent conservative lower bounds of the true symptom burden. Despite this under-documentation, the temporal pattern of associations remains consistent and robust. Furthermore, key lifestyle and clinical variables known to influence bowel function and CRC risk—including alcohol intake, physical activity, family history and dietary composition—were not captured in the dataset. It should also be considered that, in certain cases, diarrhoea may have been induced by the use of laxatives, which have been discussed in the literature as a potential factor in CRC pathogenesis.41

No separate sensitivity analyses were performed beyond the primary PSM design. Given the large sample size, balanced covariate distribution and consistent findings across multiple time windows, we consider the results to be robust; nevertheless, future investigations may complement our approach by applying alternative matching ratios or model specifications as sensitivity analyses.

In addition, it is important to emphasise that the present study did not account for tumour location, which may influence symptom patterns in CRC. Future research should therefore aim to investigate whether the temporal patterns and symptom profiles observed in this study differ according to tumour location within the colon. Given that right-sided and left-sided CRC often present with distinct clinical characteristics—such as anaemia, changes in stool consistency or bowel obstruction—examining constipation and diarrhoea in the context of tumour site could provide additional insights into early symptom manifestation and diagnostic pathways. A more granular analysis integrating tumour location data may therefore enhance the clinical utility of these findings and inform tailored approaches to early CRC detection.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the temporal pattern of constipation and diarrhoea across six predefined intervals over 5 years using real-world primary care data. Previous studies have not provided comparable temporal granularity, nor have they demonstrated the absence of associations beyond 1 year before diagnosis. This temporal mapping offers new empirical evidence on how bowel symptoms are distributed in the years preceding CRC diagnosis. From a clinical perspective, it is crucial to interpret constipation and diarrhoea not as isolated findings but as part of a broader constellation of symptoms that may indicate underlying CRC. These bowel habit changes should be assessed together with other established red flag signs, such as rectal bleeding, unexplained anaemia or unintended weight loss, and always in the context of the patient’s overall risk profile. Such an integrated assessment may enhance diagnostic accuracy, support timely referral for colonoscopy, and ultimately improve early detection of CRC in primary care settings.

In summary, our results indicate that both constipation and diarrhoea were documented with greater frequency during the 6 months preceding a CRC diagnosis. However, the observed temporal proximity and the short-lived nature of these associations lend support to the hypothesis of reverse causality, suggesting that such symptoms more likely represent early clinical indicators of an existing malignancy rather than independent aetiological factors. As such, constipation and diarrhoea may function as warning signs in the pre-diagnostic phase of CRC. Given these findings, a causal contribution of these bowel symptoms to colorectal carcinogenesis appears unlikely. Our findings indicate that constipation and diarrhoea should be interpreted primarily as short-term warning signs when newly documented, rather than longstanding risk indicators. This temporal distinction may help refine symptom-based CRC diagnostic pathways in primary care. By demonstrating that constipation and diarrhoea differ from controls only in the immediate pre-diagnostic period, our study provides temporal evidence that supports their interpretation as short-term warning signs rather than long-standing risk indicators within CRC diagnostic pathways.
