Interferon-induced protein IFIT3 as a molecular nexus of neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease and HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders
Authors: Ranjit Kumar Das, Nirakar Sahoo, Deepa Roy, Lauren Nguyen, Hansapani Rodrigo, Asim K Duttaroy, Jerel Adam Fields, Upal Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) and HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND) are significant global health concerns characterized by cognitive impairment and shared pathological features, including chronic neuroinflammation, amyloid deposition, and immune dysregulation. However, the precise molecular connections between these disorders remain unclear. Here, we identify IFIT3 as a critical shared mediator of neuroinflammatory responses in both AD and HAND. Using complementary approaches, including neuronal and microglial cell cultures, the APP/PS1 mouse model, and human postmortem brain tissues, we demonstrate consistent IFIT3 upregulation in response to amyloid-beta (Aβ) and HIV-1 exposure, with notably enhanced expression under combined conditions. Treatment with combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) partially mitigated IFIT3 induction. Additionally, siRNA-mediated silencing of IFIT3 significantly reduced key inflammatory mediators, including mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS), nuclear factor-κB, and proinflammatory cytokines. Clinically, elevated IFIT3 expression was associated with early HAND and progressively increased across advancing AD Braak stages. Together, these findings identify IFIT3 as a potential molecular bridge between HAND and AD, highlighting its promise as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target for inflammation-driven neurodegeneration.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03713-6.Keywords: IFIT3, HAND, Alzheimer’s disease, Biomarker, Aging, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative condition that primarily impairs memory and cognition in the elderly population [1]. It represents the most common cause of dementia, currently affecting an estimated 7.2 million Americans aged 65 and above, with this number projected to reach approximately 13.8 million by 2060 due to the aging demographic [2, 3]. Neuropathologically, AD is defined by extracellular deposition of Aβ plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles formed by hyperphosphorylated tau protein [4]. The progression of tau pathology is categorized by the Braak staging system (I–VI) [5, 6], which reflects a stereotypical pattern of spread beginning in the transentorhinal cortex, advancing through the hippocampus, and eventually affecting widespread neocortical areas [7]. Intermediate stages (III–IV) involve the limbic system, particularly the hippocampus, and correlate with mild cognitive impairment [8, 9]. This pathological development is associated with neuronal degeneration, synaptic dysfunction, and brain atrophy [10]. Despite extensive research, the precise etiology of AD remains elusive and is thought to result from complex interactions among genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors [11]. Increasing evidence also points to chronic neuroinflammation and impaired innate immune signaling as essential contributors to disease onset and progression [12, 13]. In parallel, the success of cART has significantly prolonged the lifespan of individuals living with HIV [14, 15], resulting in a growing population at risk for neurodegenerative disorders, including AD [16]. Despite effective viral suppression, a substantial proportion of people with HIV, up to 50% experience HAND [17, 18], which spans a clinical spectrum from asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI) to HIV-associated dementia (HAD) [19]. Notably, hallmark features of AD pathology, such as Aβ accumulation and tau hyperphosphorylation, have also been observed in HAND patients, suggesting pathological overlap and potential mechanistic convergence [20, 21]. These overlapping features raise essential questions about whether the molecular mechanisms that drive HAND and AD are interconnected and whether common therapeutic targets exist.

A key point of intersection between AD and HAND lies in sustained activation of immune signaling pathways within the central nervous system (CNS). Both conditions exhibit chronic neuroinflammation marked by interferon pathway activation, elevated oxidative stress, and dysregulated cytokine production [22, 23]. Neuroinflammation in the CNS contributes to neuronal injury and synaptic loss, and it is now considered not just a byproduct but a driving force in neurodegenerative diseases [24]. Among the interferon-stimulated genes implicated in this process, IFIT3 and signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) have emerged as candidate regulators of CNS immune responses. STAT1 plays a well-characterized role in controlling antiviral and inflammatory gene expression, while IFIT3 is increasingly recognized for its involvement in immune regulation, apoptosis, and cellular stress responses [25–27]. Similarly, IFIT3 plays a pivotal role in antiviral immunity and is involved in regulating apoptosis, immune signaling, and cell proliferation [28–31]. Recent transcriptomic and proteomic analyses have identified differential expression of both IFIT3 and STAT1 in the brains of individuals with AD and HAND, suggesting their possible roles as molecular bridges between these two diseases [32]. However, the functional relevance of their upregulation in these disorders remains largely unexplored [28, 33, 34]. Nevertheless, the precise functional contribution of IFIT3 in these disease contexts remains to be elucidated. Given the need to clarify the shared pathogenic mechanisms underlying AD and HAND, our study employed a comprehensive approach to investigate the expression dynamics of IFIT3. Our research aimed to elucidate the expression patterns of IFIT3 in the AD and HAND pathologies through a multifaceted approach using both in vitro and in vivo model. Furthermore, we validated our findings using postmortem brain tissues from individuals with HAND and AD. Through this integrative analysis, the present study has uncovered the neuroimmune pathways and assessed the translational potential of IFIT3 as a biomarker and mechanistic link between these neurodegenerative conditions.

Materials and methods

HIV-1 Tat protein (catalog #7002, ImmunoDx, Woburn, MA, USA); RNase AWAY™ Surface Decontaminant (catalog #7002, Thermo Fisher Scientific); DEPC-treated water (catalog #AM9939, Thermo Fisher Scientific); RNeasy Mini Kit (catalog #74104, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany); TaqMan™ RNA-to-CT™ 1-Step Kit (catalog #4392938, Thermo Fisher Scientific); TaqMan™ gene expression assays for STAT1 (catalog #Hs01013996_m1), IFIT3 (catalog #Hs01922752_s1), and GAPDH (catalog #Hs02786624_g1); and TaqMan™ PCR Master Mix (catalog #4304437, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for RNA analysis. CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (catalog #G3582, Promega, WI, USA) and a BioTek Synergy HT multimode microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, VT, USA) were used for viability assays. Poly-D-lysine-coated coverslips (catalog #NC0672873, Fisher Scientific), 4% paraformaldehyde (catalog #J61899.AK, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Fluoromount-G™ with DAPI (catalog #010001, SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, AL, USA). RIPA Lysis and Extraction Buffer (catalog #89901, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (catalog #78440, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (catalog #23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific), pre-stained protein ladder (catalog #26616, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX gels (catalog #4561095, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and Trans-Blot® Turbo™ RTA Mini 0.2 μm PVDF Transfer Kit (catalog #1704272, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) were used for Western blot analysis. SH-SY5Y cell culture

Human SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells were cultured in 75 cm² flasks using DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. For neuronal differentiation, SH-SY5Y cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/cm² in T75 flasks containing DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin: streptomycin. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh differentiation medium containing 10 µM of RA. The RA-containing medium was replaced every 3 days until 80% confluence was achieved. Cells with fewer than seven passages and reaching ~ 80% confluence were used for all the experiments.

HMC-3 cells

Human microglial clone 3 (HMC3; ATCC® CRL-3304™) cells were cultured in 75 cm² flasks using EMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. All experiments were performed using cells with fewer than 10 passages to ensure phenotypic stability and reproducibility of results. Although HMC3 cells express minimal CD4 and are not fully permissive to productive HIV-1 infection, they are widely utilized as a model for studying microglial immune activation and interferon-mediated inflammatory responses to HIV-related stimuli [35–37]. Accordingly, in this study, HMC3 cells were used to assess IFIT3-associated signaling rather than viral replication.

Cytotoxicity assay

Differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were treated with 100 µL of Aβ (25–35) at concentrations of 10 µM, 20 µM, or 60 µM for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega, Cat# G3580), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. On the day of the assay, the reagent was equilibrated to room temperature. Cells were gently washed with PBS and replenished with fresh medium prior to reagent addition. Subsequently, 20 µL of the assay reagent was added to each well, and plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a BioTek Synergy HTX plate reader after both 1-hour and 4-hour incubation periods. Cell viability was calculated using the following formula: % Viability = (OD of treated cells / OD of control cells) × 100.

Preparation of the Aβ fragment

The Aβ (25–35) peptide (catalog #A4559, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was prepared following previously established protocols [38, 39]. Briefly, lyophilized peptide was reconstituted in autoclaved distilled water to a final concentration of 1 mM. To ensure full solubilization and peptide aggregation, the solution was gently agitated at 37 °C for 96 h without vigorous mixing. Care was taken to equilibrate the vial to room temperature prior to reconstitution to prevent condensation. After incubation, the peptide stock was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles. Working concentrations of 10 µM, 20 µM, and 60 µM were freshly diluted from the stock prior to cell treatment [40–42].

Exposure of HIV-1 to the cells

HIV-1 (strain NIH-ARP, Lot No. 140318) was obtained from the NIH AIDS Reagent Program. Prior to use, the virus stock was thawed on ice and gently vortexed. SH-SY5Y and HMC3 cells were exposed to HIV-1 at a final concentration of 100 pg/mL. Viral inoculation was performed 24 h after cell plating, and cells were co-incubated with HIV-1 and Aβ for an additional 24 h. Control wells received an equivalent volume of virus-free medium.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from SH-SY5Y cells, HMC3 cells, and APP/PS1 mouse brain tissues using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a DeNovix DS-11 fluorometer. For gene expression analysis, cDNA synthesis and amplification were performed using the TaqMan™ RNA-to-CT™ 1-Step Kit (Applied Biosystems). The following TaqMan gene-specific probes were used: human IFIT3 (Hs01922752_s1), STAT1 (Hs01013996_m1), IL-6 (Hs00174131_m1), TNF-α (Hs00174128_m1), IL-10 (Hs00961622_m1), IL-1β (Hs01555410_m1), NF-κB1 (Hs00765730_m1), MAVS (Hs00920075_m1), and GAPDH (Hs99999905_m1); and mouse IFIT3 (Mm01704846_s1) and GAPDH (Mm99999915_g1). RT-qPCR reactions were performed using an Applied Biosystems real-time PCR system under the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Negative controls were included to verify specificity. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the endogenous control.

APP/PSI mouse model for AD pathology

To examine IFIT3 expression in vivo, we employed brain tissue from the APP/PS1 double-transgenic mouse model of AD. This model carries human amyloid precursor protein (APP) with the Swedish mutation (K595N/M596L) and presenilin-1 (PSEN1) with the L166P mutation, both driven by the mouse prion protein (PrP) promoter [43–45]. These genetic modifications lead to age-dependent amyloid plaque deposition and cognitive deficits, recapitulating key features of human AD pathology [46]. A total of 15 mice were used in the study, divided into three groups (n = 5 per group): wild-type controls, APP mutants, and PS1 mutants. All animal procedures adhered to institutional guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Kentucky. Frozen brain tissues were processed for RNA and protein isolation to assess IFIT3 expression via RT-qPCR and Western blot. Additionally, paraffin-embedded cerebellar sections were used for immunohistochemical analysis of IFIT3.

HAND clinical cohort

Postmortem brain tissues were obtained at autopsy from individuals diagnosed with HAND and age-matched cognitively normal controls. The HAND cases were categorized into three diagnostic groups based on established clinical criteria: asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI), mild neurocognitive disorder (MND), and HIV-associated dementia (HAD). A total of 22 specimens were analyzed, comprising 5 cognitively normal controls (all male) and 17 HAND donors. The HAND cohort included 6 ANI cases (3 females, 3 males), 5 MND cases (1 female, 4 males), and 6 HAD cases (1 female, 5 males). The mean age of the control group was 44.4 ± 7.6 years, while the average ages for the HAND subgroups were 37.0 ± 6.7 years (ANI), 35.6 ± 8.9 years (MND), and 45.2 ± 8.2 years (HAD). Brain tissues were processed for immunoblotting to evaluate the expression of molecular markers implicated in HIV neuropathogenesis and cognitive decline.

AD clinical cohort

Human postmortem brain tissues were obtained from individuals representing a spectrum of AD pathology, stratified according to Braak staging (0–VI), which reflects the progression of neurofibrillary tangle accumulation. A total of 40 samples were analyzed, including both male and female subjects across a wide age range. Braak stage 0 (n = 2; 1 male, 1 female; mean age: 62 ± 7.1 years) served as cognitively normal controls. AD cases were distributed as follows: Braak I (n = 8; 3 males, 5 females; mean age: 68.9 ± 29.9 years), Braak II (n = 6; 5 males, 1 female; mean age: 82.8 ± 10.8 years), Braak III (n = 4; 4 males; mean age: 87.5 ± 0.0 years), Braak IV (n = 6; 2 males, 4 females; mean age: 84.5 ± 7.4 years), Braak V (n = 4; 2 males, 2 females; mean age: 82.8 ± 6.9 years), and Braak VI (n = 10; 3 males, 7 females; mean age: 79.9 ± 5.1 years). All specimens were collected and processed under standardized conditions to preserve protein integrity. These tissues were subjected to Western Blot analysis to evaluate the expression of molecular markers across different stages of AD pathology.

Immunoblotting

HMC3 and SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 6-well culture plates at a density of 7.5 × 10⁵ cells/well using EMEM and DMEM media, respectively. After treatment, cells were harvested, and total protein lysates were prepared using ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with Halt™ protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were incubated on ice for 15 min and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each sample, 15 µg of protein was mixed with 4× Laemmli sample buffer and β-mercaptoethanol, followed by denaturation at 90 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated on 4–12% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX precast SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes using the Trans-Blot® Turbo™ Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in TBST containing 5% (BSA) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IFIT3 (1:500), phospho-STAT1, and β-actin. After three 5-minute washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse or anti-rabbit, 1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate, and images were captured using an Alliance Q9 Advanced imaging system (UVITEC).

Immunocytochemistry

SH-SY5Y cells were seeded onto poly-L-lysine-coated glass coverslips in 24-well plates and differentiated with RA as previously described. Following differentiation, cells were treated with Aβ, HIV, or a combination of Aβ and HIV for 24 h. After treatment, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature (RT), followed by three PBS washes. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min and incubated in blocking buffer for 30 min at RT to reduce nonspecific binding. After blocking, cells were washed three times with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:500 dilution in blocking buffer). The next day, cells were washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500 dilution) for 1 h at RT in the dark. Coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using Fluoromount-G™ mounting medium containing DAPI for nuclear counterstaining. Fluorescent images were acquired using an Olympus Fluoview FV10i confocal microscope equipped with a 60× objective. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD), and values were normalized to assess relative protein expression across treatment groups.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical analysis of APP/PS1 mouse brain sections was performed as previously described [47, 48]. Briefly, brains were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, followed by paraffin embedding through three sequential immersions in molten paraffin (1 h each). Tissues were embedded in standard histological cassettes and sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices using a rotary microtome. Sections were mounted on Superfrost™ Plus slides, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 75%, and 50%), then washed three times in PBS for 5 min each. To block nonspecific binding, slides were incubated in 1% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with mouse anti-IFIT3 primary antibody (1:300 dilution). The following day, after PBS washes, slides were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with Alexa Fluor-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody and anti-NeuN primary antibody (both at 1:500 dilution). Nuclei were counterstained using DAPI, and coverslips were mounted with Fluoromount-G™. Images were acquired using an Olympus Fluoview FV10i confocal microscope equipped with a 60× objective. Quantification of IFIT3 fluorescence intensity and its colocalization with NeuN-positive neurons was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Fluorescence signals were normalized to allow comparative assessment across experimental groups.

Measurement of reactive oxidative species

SH-SY5Y cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Prior to ROS measurement, the culture medium was removed, and wells were washed once with PBS. Subsequently, 100 µL of PBS was added to each well. For negative controls, PBS containing 1 mM catalase was added, and cells were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Following this preincubation, the PBS was aspirated, and cells were incubated with 100 µL of 100 µM 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA) in PBS for 1 h at 37 °C. DCF-DA is a cell-permeable fluorogenic dye that is converted to highly fluorescent DCF upon oxidation by intracellular ROS. After dye loading, DCF-DA was removed, and cells were treated with 10 µM, 20 µM, or 60 µM Aβ, hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂; positive control), or catalase (negative control) in fresh PBS. Fluorescence intensity was measured immediately using a BioTek Synergy HTX multimode microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc.) with excitation/emission wavelengths set at 480/20 nm and 528/20 nm, respectively. ROS levels were quantified and expressed as relative fluorescence units (RFU) [49].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with multiple independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Prior to statistical testing, the assumption of normality was evaluated to confirm the appropriateness of parametric analysis. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences among treatment groups. When significant main effects were detected, Dunnett’s post hoc test was applied to compare each treatment group against the corresponding control. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA), and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Aβ dose-dependent modulation of IFIT3 and STAT1 in SH-SY5Y cells

To establish an optimal in vitro model for AD, we first assessed the cytotoxic effects of Aβ in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells. Cells were treated with Aβ at concentrations of 10 µM, 20 µM, or 60 µM for 24 h, and cell viability was assessed using the MTS assay. Aβ at 60 µM significantly reduced cell viability (p < 0.0001), whereas 10 µM and 20 µM Aβ did not show statistically significant (Fig. 1A). These data indicate that 60 µM Aβ is sufficient to induce cytotoxicity, validating its use for modeling Aβ-mediated neurodegeneration. To investigate the transcriptional response to Aβ exposure, we quantified IFIT3 and STAT1 mRNA levels by RT-PCR, and both of the gene transcripts were upregulated in a dose-dependent manner, with a significant increase observed at 20 µM and 60 µM Aβ (p < 0.0001). While IFIT3 mRNA was modestly increased at 10 µM, and STAT1 was not significantly altered (p = 0.115) (Fig. 1B–C). We next examined protein expression levels by Western blot. Both IFIT3 and STAT1 proteins were significantly upregulated at 10 µM Aβ (IFIT3, p = 0.003) and (STAT1, p = 0065). At 60 µM Aβ, STAT1 protein was reduced despite elevated transcript levels (Fig. 1D–G). Additionally, intracellular ROS production was significantly increased only in cells treated with 60 µM, indicating increased oxidative stress under high-dose exposure (Fig. 1H). Overall, these findings demonstrate that 60 µM Aβ induces cytotoxicity, transcriptional activation, and oxidative stress. Contrarily, 10 µM modulates IFIT3 and STAT1 expression without reducing cell viability, suggesting its suitability for downstream mechanistic investigations.

Co-treatment of Aβ and HIV elevates IFIT3 and STAT1 expression

To investigate the combined effects of HIV and Aβ on the expression of IFIT3 and STAT1, SH-SY5Y cells were treated with Aβ, HIV, HIV + Aβ, HIV + Aβ + cART, or cART alone for 24 h. RT-PCR analysis demonstrated a noticeable upregulation of IFIT3 and STAT1 transcripts in the HIV + Aβ group relative to all other treatment conditions (p < 0.0001), indicative of a synergistic transcriptional response (Fig. 2A, B). Consistent with the mRNA data, Western blot analysis showed significantly elevated IFIT3 protein levels in both the HIV + Aβ and HIV + Aβ + cART groups (p < 0.0001). In contrast, cART alone produced a modest yet statistically significant reduction in IFIT3 expression (p = 0.003) (Fig. 2C, E). Similarly, STAT1 protein expression was markedly increased in the HIV + Aβ and HIV + Aβ + cART groups (p < 0.0001), while cART treatment alone led to a significant downregulation (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2D, F). These findings suggest that co-exposure to HIV and Aβ amplifies neuroinflammatory signaling, which is partially mitigated by antiretroviral therapy but does not normalize to levels observed in untreated control cells. To visualize these molecular alterations at the cellular level, immunocytochemical staining for IFIT3 was performed in SH-SY5Y cells. Quantitative fluorescence analysis demonstrated a significant increase in IFIT3 signal intensity in cells treated with HIV + Aβ (p < 0.0001), along with a significant increase in those exposed to HIV alone (p = 0.0021), when compared to controls (Fig. 2G, H). Overall, the study compared all groups to the control to address the primary question of induction over baseline, which translates to a healthy individual in a clinical setting. Therefore, cART was not included as a background for comparison. These observations together indicate that HIV and Aβ synergistically enhance IFIT3 and STAT1 expression.

Co-treatment of Aβ and HIV induces IFIT3 expression in HMC3 cells

To further validate IFIT3 expression in an in vitro model, we used HMC3, which are permissive to HIV infection. HIV replication was confirmed by quantification of the p24 antigen, where a significant reduction in viral antigen levels was observed upon cART treatment, indicating effective viral suppression (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3A). Subsequent RT-qPCR analysis of IFIT3 mRNA revealed significant upregulation following HIV and HIV + Aβ exposure (p < 0.0001). In contrast, Aβ alone resulted in a moderate but statistically significant upregulation of IFIT3 transcription compared to control (p = 0.0025) (Fig. 3B). These gene expression patterns were corroborated at the protein level by Western blot, showing elevated IFIT3 expression in the HIV and HIV + Aβ groups (p < 0.0001), and reduced expression in the HIV + cART group (Fig. 3D, F). To elucidate the functional role of IFIT3, we employed siRNA-mediated gene silencing in HMC3 cells under the same treatment conditions. RT-qPCR confirmed IFIT3 was silenced across all groups, irrespective of treatment (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3C), and corresponding reductions were observed in protein expression via Western blot (Fig. 3E, G). Cumulatively, these findings support HMC3 cells as an appropriate model for investigating IFIT3 regulation under HIV and Aβ co-exposure and establish siRNA-based silencing as a viable tool for future mechanistic studies aimed at dissecting the role of IFIT3 in neuroinflammatory signaling.

IFIT3 Silencing reveals MAVS/NF-κB-driven inflammatory signaling

To delineate the downstream effects of IFIT3 gene silencing, we investigated the expression of key inflammatory mediators, including MAVS and NF-κB, in HMC3 cells transfected with IFIT3 siRNA and treated with Aβ, HIV, HIV + Aβ, HIV + Aβ + cART, or cART alone. RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated marked upregulation of MAVS and NF-κB transcripts in the HIV and HIV + Aβ groups (p < 0.0001). At the same time, no significant changes were observed under Aβ alone, HIV + Aβ + cART (Fig. 4A and B). To further characterize the inflammatory milieu, we quantified the expression of key cytokines, including both proinflammatory (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and anti-inflammatory (IL-10) factors. Notably, all cytokines were significantly increased in the HIV and HIV + Aβ groups (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4C–F), mirroring the transcriptional activation of MAVS and NF-κB and indicating an intensified inflammatory state. These results indicate that IFIT3 regulates the MAVS/NF-κB signaling pathway and contributes to the coordination of cytokine responses during neuroinflammation.

Cross-model validation confirms IFIT3 as a neurodegeneration-linked marker

To validate our in vitro findings, we evaluated IFIT3 expression in an in vivo model utilizing APP/PS1 transgenic mice. The RT-qPCR analysis showed a significant increase in IFIT3 mRNA expression in cerebellar tissue tissue from APP/PS1 mice compared to controls (Fig. 5A). This transcriptional upregulation was further validated at the protein level by Western blotting, which showed significant upregulation of IFIT3 protein expression in the transgenic group (Fig. 5B, D). Immunohistochemical staining of brain sections confirmed increased IFIT3 expression and concurrent APP accumulation, consistent with hallmark AD pathology (Fig. 5C, E). To contextualize these observations within a clinical framework, we analyzed postmortem brain tissue from individuals diagnosed with AD and HAND. In HAND samples, IFIT3 levels were directionally higher in ANI and MCMD compared with controls but did not reach statistical significance, whereas STAT1 expression remained unchanged. (Fig. 5F–H). In AD samples, across Braak stages I–VI, IFIT3 and STAT1 did not exhibit significant stagewise differences (Fig. 5I–K). Based on our analysis, it was also found that there was no significant difference between the mean age of the groups in the HIV + HAND or the AD vs. the control samples. Collectively, these data demonstrate a consistent pattern of IFIT3 upregulation across cellular, animal, and human models of neurodegeneration.

Discussion

AD and HAND exhibit overlapping neuropathological features, including chronic neuroinflammation, amyloid pathology, and immune dysregulation. However, the molecular mechanisms connecting these two conditions remain poorly understood [32, 50]. This knowledge gap has been a barrier in developing effective interventions for these disorders.

In the present study, we investigated whether IFIT3 functions as a key contributing factor in neuroinflammatory responses in both AD and HAND and evaluated its potential as a diagnostic biomarker or therapeutic target. In our in vitro model, we found that exposure to Aβ led to a concentration-dependent upregulation of IFIT3 at both the mRNA and protein levels. Interestingly, at higher Aβ concentrations, STAT1 exhibited elevated transcript levels but reduced protein expression. This pattern is consistent with integrated stress response (ISR)-linked translational repression and increased protein turnover at the 24-hour time point. It is also possible that interferon signaling, and RNA-binding proteins contribute to mRNA stabilization, allowing transcript levels to remain elevated while translation is transiently inhibited or protein degradation is accelerated. Our findings indicate that IFIT3 and STAT1 modulate through distinct regulatory mechanisms in response to amyloid-induced stress, supporting the concept that interferon-associated mediators show different effects on AD pathology [27, 51]. Our observation aligns with reports that STAT1-driven inflammatory signaling contributes to AD neuroinflammation [52, 53]. While our study focuses on IFIT3 as a cytosolic adaptor that amplifies MAVS-dependent immune signaling, prior work has shown that genetic variation in other ISGs, such as IFITM3 (a membrane-associated viral entry inhibitor), can also modulate AD pathological outcomes, highlighting the broader relevance of interferon-regulated mediators in neurodegeneration [54]. While IFIT3 is a canonical interferon-stimulated gene primarily regulated by type 1 interferon (IFN-1)/JAK–STAT and pattern-recognition receptors (PRR) (RIG-I/MDA5–MAVS and TLR ) pathways, our study was designed to define its downstream functional role through MAVS/NF-κB signaling rather than its upstream activation. The concurrent upregulation of STAT1 and IFIT3 following Aβ and HIV exposure supports interferon-driven regulation consistent with prior findings in neuroinflammatory and viral models [55, 56]. However, dissecting the upstream initiating signals was beyond the scope of this work, and future studies will focus on delineating the specific receptor- and interferon-dependent mechanisms driving IFIT3 induction under amyloid and viral stress conditions.

In our study, the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 were elevated alongside the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, indicating a coordinated activation of the innate immune response. This pattern suggests a tightly regulated inflammatory network in which NF-κB–driven induction of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 initiates the immune cascade [57–59], while the concurrent upregulation of IL-10, acting through the STAT1/SOCS feedback axis [60], serves to suppress excessive cytokine production and limit tissue damage [61, 62]. In mixed cellular environments, paracrine communication among neurons, glial cells, and immune-responsive cells likely reinforces this balance, producing a parallel rise in both pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators.

Furthermore, co-treatment with Aβ and HIV-1 in vitro resulted in a synergistic increase in IFIT3 and STAT1 expression, supporting the concept that HIV infection amplifies amyloid-induced neuroinflammation. Recent evidence suggests that HIV infection may contribute to amyloid pathology through mechanisms that overlap with those observed in AD. These reports indicated an increased Aβ deposition in HIV-positive individuals, particularly those with cognitive impairment, and linked this to altered APP processing, impaired Aβ clearance, and chronic neuroinflammation [63, 64]. Subsequent studies have shown that persistent immune activation in HIV, even under cART [65], can promote amyloidogenic APP cleavage via cytokine-driven upregulation of β- and γ-secretase enzymes and inhibit Aβ degradation through the actions of HIV proteins such as Tat and gp120 [21, 66, 67]. These processes may contribute to both intracellular Aβ accumulation and diffuse plaque deposition, as observed in autopsy studies of HIV-positive individuals with HAND [21, 68, 69]. In our study, the synergistic upregulation of IFIT3 and STAT1 following co-exposure to HIV and Aβ further supports this convergence. This suggests that Aβ may act not only as a downstream consequence of HIV-induced neuroinflammation but also as an upstream amplifier of innate immune responses, potentially sustaining a pathogenic loop that links HAND and AD through shared neuroimmune pathways such as the IFIT3–MAVS–NF-κB signaling cascade.

To better understand the functional contribution of IFIT3, we silenced its expression in HMC3 cells using siRNA. IFIT3 silencing significantly decreased the expression of MAVS and NF-κB, as these are two core components of the innate immune response, as well as downstream pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines [70]. These results indicate that IFIT3 acts upstream in the antiviral signaling cascade, likely modulating the activation of MAVS and NF-κB pathways [71]. The IFIT3 is a canonical interferon-stimulated gene induced via JAK–STAT1 signaling upon type I interferon exposure [72], which induces IFIT3 to translocate to mitochondria where it interacts with MAVS, promoting MAVS oligomerization and facilitating downstream TBK1/IRF3 and NF-κB activation with subsequent cytokine production [29]. Our data expand on this mechanism by demonstrating IFIT3’s role in amplifying neuroimmune responses in AD and HAND, potentially sustaining chronic microglial activation and neurodegeneration. In addition to HAND and AD, emerging transcriptomic and proteomic studies have identified IFIT3 upregulation in several neurological conditions, including viral encephalitis, traumatic brain injury, and autoimmune neuroinflammatory disorders [31, 73, 74], suggesting that IFIT3 participates in a broader CNS interferon-driven stress response rather than a disease-specific pathway.

The pathophysiological relevance of IFIT3 was further supported by our in vivo and clinical findings. In the APP/PS1 transgenic mouse model, we observed substantial upregulation of IFIT3 alongside increased APP levels and elevated expression of inflammatory markers (IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β), indicating a sustained neuroinflammatory environment characteristic of amyloid pathology [75]. Similarly, postmortem brain tissues from individuals with early-stage HAND, including those diagnosed with ANI and MCMD, demonstrated increased IFIT3 expression in conjunction with microglial activation and elevated proinflammatory cytokines [33]. Across AD clinical samples, IFIT3 and STAT1 showed directionally increasing patterns with advancing Braak stage, but these differences did not reach statistical significance in this cohort. Accordingly, we consider IFIT3 a promising neuroimmune mediator rather than a validated biomarker, and we note that larger, region-matched, covariate-adjusted cohorts will be required to assess diagnostic performance. Despite inter-sample variability, the consistent upregulation of IFIT3 observed across our in vitro, in vivo, and clinical subjects highlights its potential role as a central neuroimmune mediator in both AD and HAND.

Although we used a multifaceted approach in this study, including in vitro, in vivo, and clinical subjects, there are still some limitations to acknowledge. The use of immortalized cell lines may not fully recapitulate the complex human neuroinflammatory environment. Although HMC3 cells lack CD4 and are not permissive to productive HIV-1 infection, they remain a widely used model for studying HIV-induced microglial activation [76, 77]. Multiple studies have shown that HMC3 cells respond robustly to HIV-1 proteins such as gp120 and Tat by activating innate immune pathways including NF-κB and NLRP3, and by producing proinflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6 [37, 78–80]. These effects occur independently of viral entry, mediated instead through chemokine co-receptors (e.g., CCR5) and pattern recognition receptors (PRR) [81]. Thus, HMC3 cells serve as a tractable and relevant system to study HIV-driven neuroinflammatory responses in vitro, even in the absence of productive infection. To attenuate this limitation, we validated our findings in APP/PS1 mouse brains and human tissues, supporting the translational relevance of IFIT3-mediated neuroinflammatory responses. Further research may incorporate primary microglia or iPSC-derived neural models for validation of IFIT3 role. Additionally, our clinical sample sizes were limited, a larger and better-characterized cohorts will be needed to establish IFIT3 as a biomarker and therapeutic target firmly and to control for confounding factors. An additional consideration is the potential influence of HIV subtype–specific or strain-dependent differences in neuroinflammatory signaling. Prior studies have demonstrated that clade B and clade C viruses can differ in their ability to drive neuroimmune activation and HAND severity [82–84]. However, whether these subtype variations modulate IFIT3 induction remains unknown. Thus, strain-specific diversity in HIV neuropathogenesis should be considered a possible influencing factor, and future work incorporating multiple viral subtypes will be important to clarify IFIT3 regulation in this context. Although cerebellar tissue was analyzed in APP/PS1 mice, this region provides a relatively stable reference with less confounding plaque pathology, allowing evaluation of systemic IFIT3-associated inflammatory changes. Recent studies have reported that even non-plaque-bearing regions such as the cerebellum exhibit measurable neuroimmune alterations in AD mouse models, supporting its inclusion for transcript-level analysis [85–89]. Nevertheless, future investigations targeting more pathologically vulnerable regions, such as the hippocampus and cortex, are warranted to validate these findings further.

Although IFIT3 represents a conceptually appealing therapeutic candidate given its central role in coordinating interferon-driven neuroimmune signaling, its translational feasibility remains uncertain. As a core antiviral effector, IFIT3 participates in essential innate immune defense pathways, raising the possibility that therapeutic suppression could inadvertently disrupt protective antiviral mechanisms or destabilize tightly regulated interferon signaling networks. Furthermore, the downstream consequences of modulating IFIT3 within chronically inflamed neural environments are not yet well defined. Thus, while IFIT3 may represent a promising and potentially modifiable node within the neuroinflammatory cascade, its consideration as a therapeutic target must be approached cautiously and will require rigorous mechanistic validation and safety evaluation before translational applications can be realistically pursued. The study also acknowledges that it was designed to identify shared molecular/pathway signatures between the two diseases, not to determine their causal hierarchy, which would require additional longitudinal or perturbation-based experiments.

In conclusion, our data identify IFIT3 as a key regulator in both AD and HAND. Its robust upregulation in response to Aβ and HIV-1, coupled with its key role in regulating MAVS–NF-κB signaling and cytokine expression, suggests that IFIT3 lies at the center of a pathogenic inflammatory network linking these two conditions. These findings position IFIT3 as a promising candidate for both biomarker development and therapeutic intervention in neurodegenerative processes driven by chronic inflammation.
