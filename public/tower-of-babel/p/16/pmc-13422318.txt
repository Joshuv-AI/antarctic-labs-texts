Role of methionine metabolism in cancer: recent advances in molecular mechanisms and therapeutic implications
Authors: Pei-wen Yang, Xin-yi Xu, Ju-ying Jiao, Feng-jiao Wang, Zhen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methionine is essential for protein synthesis and serves as a precursor to S-adenosylmethionine, supporting epigenetic modifications and cell growth. Unlike normal cells, cancer cells are dependent on methionine. Therapeutic strategies targeting metabolic vulnerability show promise but face challenges related to tumor heterogeneity and adaptive resistance, limiting clinical translation. This review comprehensively explores methionine metabolism in cancer, detailing its uptake, transport, and metabolic pathways that underpin tumor development. We examine tumor dependency on methionine across various cancer types and subtypes, highlighting oncogene-driven variability, including the pivotal roles of methionine adenosyltransferases (MATs), the methionine salvage enzyme 5’-methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP), and polyamine synthesis enzymes such as ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) and adenosylmethionine decarboxylase 1 (AMD1). Epigenetic contributions involving DNA, RNA, and histone methylation are discussed, emphasizing their tissue-specific implications, such as the MATI/III:MATII ratio in liver cancer and methionine metabolic features in glioblastoma, including PET imaging applications. This review further addresses methionine metabolic plasticity within the tumor microenvironment, focusing on epigenetic and immune reprogramming, metabolic checkpoint regulation, and the role of methionine in tumor-initiating cells and cancer stem cells. Therapeutic strategies targeting methionine dependence, including dietary restriction, methioninase enzyme therapies, transporter inhibition, and combination regimens aimed at enhancing efficacy, are critically evaluated. Finally, we discuss current challenges and future directions, emphasizing the need for tumor-selective interventions, improved delivery systems, and personalized metabolic diagnostics to translate metabolic vulnerabilities into effective clinical cancer therapies. This integrated analysis underscores the potential of targeting methionine metabolism for advancing cancer treatment strategies.

Introduction

Methionine is an indispensable amino acid that plays a central role in cellular metabolism. In addition to functioning as a substrate for protein production, it is converted into S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the primary methyl group donor required for epigenetic regulation, nucleotide formation, and polyamine synthesis [1]. These processes are indispensable for maintaining cellular homeostasis, yet their dysregulation is a hallmark of cancer. Tumor cells exhibit a striking reliance on exogenous methionine, a phenomenon termed “methionine dependence”, driven by metabolic rewiring that prioritizes SAM production to sustain proliferation, survival, and epigenetic plasticity [2]. Compared with differentiated normal tissues, many cancer cells exhibit increased dependence on exogenous methionine; however, this phenotype is heterogeneous across tumor types and can be observed in specific nonmalignant or stem-like cellular states [3].

Therapeutic strategies targeting methionine metabolism, such as dietary restriction, enzyme inhibition, and transporter blockade, have gained traction because of their ability to selectively impair tumor growth while sparing normal tissues [4]. Preclinical studies highlight the potential of these approaches to disrupt oncogenic signaling, reverse immunosuppressive microenvironments, and synergize with chemotherapy or immunotherapy. However, the clinical translation of these findings remains challenging and is hindered by tumor heterogeneity, compensatory metabolic adaptations, and a limited understanding of context-specific dependencies across cancer types.

This review synthesizes recent advances in understanding the role of methionine metabolism in cancer, emphasizing enzymatic and pathway-level variability, epigenetic reprogramming, and immune evasion. We explore how metabolic plasticity varies across tumor types and subtypes, dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying therapeutic resistance, and evaluate emerging strategies to exploit methionine dependency. By bridging mechanistic insights with translational applications, this work aims to inform the development of precision therapies that target metabolic vulnerabilities in cancer.

Methionine metabolism in cancer: an overview

Methionine metabolism is a central metabolic network that links nutrient availability to methyl-donor production, sulfur amino acid utilization, and redox control. As an essential amino acid, methionine must be obtained exogenously and is subsequently used for protein synthesis, SAM production, methionine salvage, and trans-sulfuration-dependent cysteine generation, as shown in Fig. 1. Through these interconnected pathways, methionine metabolism influences epigenetic regulation, polyamine biosynthesis, antioxidant defense, and cellular proliferation [5, 6]. Importantly, methionine metabolism should not be described as a simple linear axis in which altered methionine availability uniformly causes altered SAM output and downstream tumor phenotypes. Although methionine supply is a major upstream determinant of SAM synthesis, intracellular SAM homeostasis is also shaped by methionine transport, remethylation capacity, methionine salvage, methionine adenosyltransferase (MAT) activity, S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) accumulation, and competitive SAM utilization through methylation reactions and polyamine biosynthesis [7]. Consequently, the oncogenic consequences of methionine metabolic reprogramming should be interpreted in the broader context of SAM homeostasis rather than as direct readouts of methionine abundance alone.

Methionine uptake, absorption, and transport

As methionine cannot be synthesized de novo in humans, it must be obtained from dietary sources such as meat, fish, sesame seeds, Brazil nuts, and oats. The average daily requirement for methionine is approximately 10.4 mg per kilogram of body weight, ensuring serum levels between 13 and 45 µM [8–10]. Methionine absorption occurs mainly in the small intestine after protein digestion and luminal release. In intestinal epithelial transport, multiple carriers contribute to methionine handling in a context-dependent manner. Free methionine is transported across intestinal epithelial cells by specialized amino acid transport systems, including L-type amino acid transporters (LATs) [11]. LAT4, encoded by SLC43A2, is best described as a basolateral transporter for large neutral amino acids, including methionine, and is localized to the apical plasma membrane of podocytes and to the distal tubules and collecting ducts [12–15]. In contrast, LAT1, which is encoded by SLC7A5, is more common emphasized in highly proliferative tissues and tumors than in normal intestinal tissue [11, 16–19]. Thus, the distinction between LAT1 and LAT4 is better understood in terms of their predominant tissue and membrane localization rather than assuming that they play identical roles in intestinal methionine transport. In normal intestinal epithelium, methionine absorption and transepithelial handling likely involve multiple transporters, with LAT4 being more closely linked to basolateral epithelial transport, whereas LAT1 is more often associated with methionine uptake in highly proliferative and tumor contexts.

Once transported into cells, methionine links the extracellular nutrient supply to downstream methylation and redox pathways. In particular, limitation of sulfur amino acids can reduce cysteine availability and glutathione synthesis, thereby weakening glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)-dependent antioxidant defense and increasing susceptibility to lipid peroxidation. In some tumor settings, dietary cysteine/methionine restriction (MR) has been shown to sensitize cancer cells to ferroptosis [20, 21].

Pathway description

One-carbon metabolism involves a conserved metabolic network that generates, mobilizes, and transfers one-carbon units to support key biosynthetic reactions, including purine and thymidylate synthesis, as well as the remethylation of homocysteine [5, 6, 22]. Methionine metabolism is functionally interconnected with one-carbon metabolism through methyl-group transfer reactions and plays important roles in amino acid metabolism, methyl-donor homeostasis, and epigenetic regulation. Broadly, methionine metabolism can be divided into three major branches: the methionine cycle, the transsulfuration pathway, and the methionine salvage pathway.

Within the methionine cycle, methionine is converted to SAM from methionine and adenosine triphosphate by MATs. SAM serves as the universal methyl donor for a wide range of methylation reactions and is subsequently converted to SAH after methyl-group transfer. Because SAH is a potent inhibitor of many methyltransferases, the balance between SAM and SAH, which is typically expressed as the SAM:SAH ratio, reflects the cellular methylation potential [9, 23, 24]. SAH is then hydrolyzed by S-adenosylhomocysteine hydrolase to generate homocysteine and adenosine. Homocysteine can be remethylated back to methionine by methionine synthase (MS) in a folate- and vitamin B12-dependent manner, thus completing the methionine cycle. In the liver and kidney, BHMT can also remethylate homocysteine using betaine as the methyl donor.

Alternatively, homocysteine can enter the transsulfuration pathway, in which cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) and cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE) catalyze the conversion of homocysteine and serine into cysteine. The resulting cysteine is then utilized in the separate glutathione biosynthetic pathway to support cellular antioxidant defense. Methionine can also be regenerated from methylthioadenosine (MTA), a byproduct of polyamine biosynthesis, through the methionine salvage pathway, thus helping maintain intracellular methionine homeostasis and functionally linking methionine metabolism to polyamine synthesis, methylation reactions, and cellular proliferation. Disruption of the balance among one-carbon metabolism, methionine metabolism, and SAM-regulated downstream processes, for example, through altered dietary folate availability or genetic polymorphisms, contributes to various pathological conditions, including cancer [5, 6].

Importantly, SAM dysregulation in cancer should not be interpreted as a simple surrogate for methionine dysregulation. Methionine-dependent determinants of SAM homeostasis include methionine availability, transporter-mediated uptake, homocysteine remethylation, and methionine salvage. However, intracellular SAM homeostasis is also shaped by additional mechanisms, including MAT activity, SAH accumulation, one-carbon/folate-cycle flux, and competitive SAM utilization by methylation reactions and polyamine biosynthesis. Therefore, the following sections discuss SAM dysregulation in cancer from both methionine-dependent and broader regulatory perspectives.

In addition to its role in methyl-donor metabolism, methionine may influence ferroptosis in a context-dependent manner. Following its conversion through the methionine cycle, methionine can contribute to intracellular cysteine availability via the transsulfuration pathway, thus supporting glutathione biosynthesis and limiting ferroptotic damage [21, 25–27]. In line with this notion, disruption of homocysteine production or downstream sulfur flux has been associated with increased ferroptosis sensitivity [28–31]. Nevertheless, recent studies have shown that, in some cancer cells, combined methionine and cysteine deprivation is better tolerated than cysteine deprivation alone is, indicating that methionine may also facilitate ferroptosis under particular nutrient conditions [25, 32]. Importantly, these studies suggest that SAM, rather than SAH, is a major determinant of cell fate [33]. Mechanistically, the proferroptotic effects of SAM have been linked to methylation-dependent cell cycle progression [25], polyamine metabolism and reactive oxygen species generation [32, 34–36], as well as methylation-dependent coenzyme Q synthesis, which supports electron transport chain activity [37]. Thus, the net effect of methionine metabolism on ferroptosis appears to depend on the metabolic fate of SAM and the broader nutrient environment.

In summary, methionine metabolism constitutes a central metabolic network for maintaining cellular homeostasis. It governs methyl-donor metabolism through SAM production, supports antioxidant defense through the transsulfuration pathway and downstream glutathione biosynthesis, and contributes to polyamine synthesis. In cancer, dysregulation of this network is associated with altered methylation potential, redox adaptation, and metabolic reprogramming; however, the functional consequences are context dependent and should not be attributed solely to methionine abundance or to a single downstream pathway. Therefore, a comprehensive dissection of the regulatory mechanisms governing methionine metabolism under both physiological and pathological conditions is important for understanding tumor metabolism and for developing metabolism-based therapeutic strategies.

Dependency of tumors on methionine: differences in methionine metabolism across tumor types and subtypes

Tumor heterogeneity reflects molecular, cellular, and metabolic diversity within a tumor. This complexity arises from genetic mutations, epigenetic alterations, tumor microenvironment (TME) interactions, and metabolic adaptations. Methionine metabolism has emerged as a critical determinant of tumor heterogeneity, influencing both the behavior of cancer cells and their interaction with immune cells. Methionine metabolic reprogramming varies across tumor types and subtypes, contributing to differential responses to therapy and resistance mechanisms.

MATs regulate SAM production and methylation capacity

MATs are critical enzymes in methionine metabolism and catalyze the synthesis of the key methyl donor SAM. In cancer cells, MAT expression and activity are often dysregulated, contributing to metabolic reprogramming that promotes rapid proliferation and survival [7, 38]. Reduced SAM availability may contribute to global DNA hypomethylation and genomic instability, whereas tumor suppressor gene silencing in cancer is more typically associated with locus-specific promoter hypermethylation [7, 39, 40]. For a comprehensive discussion of the dysregulation of MATs at the transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational levels, as well as the role of MATs in cancer, we recommend our previous article [41].

Recent research highlights the significant roles of MATs in cancer metabolism and therapy. Methionine adenosyltransferase 2A (MAT2A), which is essential for SAM production, has become a therapeutic target, particularly in cancers with MTAP deletions [42–44]. The inhibition of MAT2A has shown promise in targeting MTAP-deleted cancer cells through the disruption of protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5)-dependent mRNA splicing, the induction of DNA damage, and the inhibition of tumor growth [42]. In liver cancer, MAT2A inhibition induces cell cycle arrest, senescence, and DNA damage, suggesting its therapeutic potential when combined with glycogen synthase kinase-3 inhibitors [45]. MAT2A is also vulnerable to mutation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27)-to-methionine in gliomas, where its loss disrupts methionine metabolism and epigenetic regulation, decreasing tumor oncogenic potential [46].

In androgen receptor-negative castration-resistant prostate cancer, MAT2A is upregulated and cooperates with ERG to promote cell plasticity, stemness, and tumorigenesis [47]. MAT2A regulates transcriptional and epigenetic remodeling, increasing protumorigenic gene expression, and its inhibition reverses these changes, improving the response to androgen receptor and enhancer of Zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) inhibitors [47]. Furthermore, although it lacks catalytic activity, methionine adenosyltransferase 2B (MAT2B) regulates MAT2A enzymatic activity and stabilizes it through nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP+) binding. Disruption of the NADP+ pathway leads to altered MAT2A protein levels, further affecting cancer progression [48].

In addition, dietary factors, such as folate, have been shown to stabilize MAT2A, potentially accelerating cancer progression, particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [49]. Environmental factors, such as formaldehyde exposure, inhibit methionine adenosyltransferase 1A (MAT1A), reducing SAM production and altering cellular methylation patterns, further influencing cancer progression [50]. These findings underscore the importance of MATs in cancer biology and their potential as therapeutic targets. Together, these findings highlight the multifaceted roles of MATs in cancer and their potential as targets for therapeutic intervention.

MTAP sustains methionine salvage and metabolic buffering

The methionine salvage pathway is a conserved metabolic route that recycles MTA, a byproduct of polyamine synthesis, back into methionine while also restoring adenine for nucleotide homeostasis [51]. By sustaining intracellular methionine availability, this pathway supports SAM-dependent methylation reactions that are essential for biomass accumulation, epigenetic regulation, and proliferative growth [52]. In cancer, dysregulation of the salvage pathway frequently arises from genetic lesions that compromise pathway activity. Among these, loss of MTAP is a recurrent event that disrupts MTA clearance and methionine recycling, thus reshaping tumor metabolism [52, 53].

MTAP is broadly expressed in normal tissues, underscoring its fundamental metabolic role, whereas MTAP deletion is common across multiple tumor types, including pancreatic, lung, and biliary tract cancers and glioblastoma [52, 53]. Importantly, due to shared chromosomal loss, MTAP loss often co-occurs with deletion of the adjacent tumor suppressor cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A), creating a compound oncogenic context that can accelerate tumor progression [52, 54]. Rather than uniformly causing absolute methionine depletion, MTAP loss is more consistently associated with MTA accumulation and a shift toward greater reliance on exogenous methionine and one-carbon metabolism to sustain methyl-donor balance and proliferative programs [52, 55]. Notably, this interpretation should be made cautiously because MTA accumulation may be compartment- and context-dependent in primary tumors [56].

Metabolically, MTAP-deficient tumors exhibit coordinated rewiring of methionine, one-carbon, and polyamine networks. Impaired MTA turnover alters flux through polyamine synthesis and has been linked to increased ODC flux, contributing to an atypical metabolic state in MTAP-negative cancers [57, 58]. Concurrently, tumor cells can engage in compensatory mechanisms to preserve SAM production and methylation capacity under salvage pathway impairment, thus highlighting the metabolic plasticity that enables MTAP-deficient cancers to sustain growth despite the loss of methionine recycling [55]. In parallel, methionine-dependent phenotypes have been reported in breast cancer susceptibility gene 1/2 mutation carriers, suggesting a possible intersection between methionine metabolism and DNA repair-associated vulnerabilities in breast cancer susceptibility; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the evidence remains limited [59].

In addition to tumor-intrinsic effects, the loss of 9p21, a chromosomal event that commonly involves MTAP and CDKN2A, is associated with an immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment characterized by reduced immune effector infiltration and poor responses to immune checkpoint therapy [60]. Accordingly, MTAP-deficient tumors are being increasingly studied in the context of immune exclusion and immune evasion, although it remains important to distinguish effects attributable specifically to MTAP loss from those associated more broadly with 9p21 deletion [53, 60]. Mechanistically, elevated MTA is increasingly viewed as a bioactive metabolite capable of modulating methylation-associated signaling and transcriptional programs, but the causal relationships and context dependence warrant further investigation [52, 55].

Therapeutically, MTAP deficiency exposes actionable liabilities and motivates genotype-guided strategies. Early work showed that MTAP-deficient cells could display enhanced sensitivity to methionine depletion and to inhibition of de novo purine synthesis [61, 62].

More recent studies have established a mechanistic basis for synthetic vulnerability along the MAT2A/PRMT5 axis: MTAP loss causes MTA accumulation, which partially inhibits PRMT5-related methylation biology and renders tumor cells more dependent on MAT2A and PRMT5 function [42, 43, 55]. These findings support the rationale for the use of methionine-targeted strategies and PRMT5/MAT2A-directed interventions in MTAP-negative tumors while also emphasizing the need to define therapeutic windows, biomarkers of response, and potential toxicity in normal tissues [52, 55]. Given the immune-exclusion features reported in 9p21/MTAP-deficient settings, rational combination regimens integrating metabolic targeting with immunomodulatory approaches may further enhance antitumor efficacy, although this concept still needs clinical validation [52, 60].

In conclusion, the methionine salvage pathway centered on MTAP serves as a key node linking polyamine byproducts to methionine and adenine recovery. MTAP loss reprograms methionine/polyamine metabolism, promotes dependence on compensatory SAM-generating pathways, and may contribute to immune exclusion in selected genomic contexts [52, 55, 60]. Continued integration of metabolic, epigenetic, and immunologic insights will be essential for the translation of MTAP-guided vulnerabilities into effective clinical strategies.

ODC and AMD1 redirect SAM metabolism toward polyamine biosynthesis

SAM is decarboxylated by SAM decarboxylase to form decarboxylated SAM (dcSAM), which donates aminopropyl groups for the synthesis of polyamines such as spermidine and spermine [63, 64]. Polyamine-small, polycationic molecules, including putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, are essential for various cellular functions, including DNA stabilization, gene expression regulation, and cell proliferation [1]. The biosynthesis of polyamines is orchestrated by key enzymes such as ODC and AMD1, linking methionine metabolism to polyamine synthesis. ODC catalyzes the conversion of ornithine to putrescine, initiating polyamine biosynthesis, whereas AMD1 produces dcSAM, a critical intermediate in this pathway [65, 66]. The levels of SAM and dcSAM, which are influenced by methionine metabolism, are critical for maintaining the balance between these interconnected pathways. Regulatory mechanisms such as feedback inhibition and transcriptional control play pivotal roles in maintaining this balance for normal cell function [1].

In cancer, polyamine metabolism is frequently dysregulated and characterized by the overexpression of key biosynthetic enzymes. The gene encoding ODC, the rate-limiting enzyme in polyamine synthesis, is often amplified or shows hypomethylation of its promoter in various cancers [1]. Similarly, AMD1 is frequently upregulated in tumors because of gene amplification or transcriptional activation [1]. These alterations lead to elevated levels of polyamines, including putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, which are beneficial for supporting rapid cell division and stabilizing DNA structures [1]. To satisfy their high polyamine demand, cancer cells not only increase the expression of ODC and AMD1 but also increase the uptake of exogenous polyamines from the tumor microenvironment [67].

Notably, the v-myc myelocytomatosis viral oncogene homolog (MYC) oncogene is a critical regulator of polyamine metabolism in cancer. MYC directly activates the transcription of ODC, resulting in increased polyamine synthesis [68]. Additionally, MYC influences AMD1 expression, further promoting dcSAM production and promoting polyamine biosynthesis [67]. The hyperactivation of MYC in various cancers sustains elevated polyamine levels, facilitating tumor growth and proliferation [1].

Another significant regulator of polyamine metabolism is the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway. mTORC1 activation enhances AMD1 expression, thus increasing dcSAM production and promoting subsequent polyamine synthesis [69]. Dysregulation of mTORC1 is frequently observed in cancers, often resulting from mutations in upstream regulators such as phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) and tuberous sclerosis complex 1/2, leading to uncontrolled cell growth and proliferation [69].

Recognizing the critical dependency of cancer cells on polyamines, several therapeutic strategies have been developed to target ODC and AMD1. Difluoromethylornithine (DFMO), an irreversible, enzyme-activated inhibitor of ODC, is the most extensively studied pharmacological agent in this context. DFMO effectively depletes intracellular polyamine pools, exerting potent cytostatic effects and increasing the activity of genes such as those involved in ferroptosis across various malignancies [70, 71]. Building on decades of clinical evaluation, ODC targeting achieved a major milestone in late 2023, when the FDA approved DFMO as a maintenance therapy to reduce the risk of relapse in high-risk neuroblastoma patients, firmly validating polyamine synthesis as an actionable clinical target [72]. Similarly, AMD1 represents a compelling therapeutic vulnerability. Small-molecule inhibitors of AMD1, such as SAM486A (sardomozide), selectively block the production of dcSAM, starving cancer cells from aminopropyl donors and inducing profound metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming [73]. Furthermore, because metabolic compensation often blunts the efficacy of monotherapies, contemporary research increasingly focuses on combinatorial strategies. Co-administering ODC or AMD1 inhibitors with polyamine transport inhibitors or immune checkpoint blockades has shown profound synergistic antitumor effects by simultaneously cutting off internal synthesis, blocking external salvage pathways, and reversing the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [70].

Despite promising therapeutic approaches targeting polyamine metabolism, several challenges remain. The intrinsic link between polyamine metabolism and other cellular processes raises concerns regarding off-target effects and potential toxicity in normal cells. Moreover, cancer cells often develop compensatory mechanisms, such as enhanced polyamine uptake, which can undermine therapeutic interventions. In summary, the intricate interplay between SAM, polyamine metabolism, and cancer underscores the potential for therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways. Future research must address existing challenges while optimizing treatment modalities to take advantage of the metabolic vulnerabilities presented by dysregulated polyamine synthesis in cancer.

Epigenetic contributions

Although methionine metabolism provides the metabolic foundation for SAM synthesis, epigenetic outcomes in cancer are not determined by methionine availability alone. Rather, they are influenced by the SAM:SAH ratio, methyltransferase expression and activity, chromatin context, and competition from other SAM-consuming pathways. Accordingly, changes in DNA, RNA, and histone methylation should be interpreted as context-dependent consequences of broader SAM homeostasis. Epigenetic modifications are pivotal for regulating gene expression and influencing cancer progression, revealing a complex interplay between metabolism and epigenetics. Among these modifications, DNA methylation, RNA methylation, and histone modifications stand out because of their significant role in shaping tumor biology. Methionine metabolism, particularly through its primary metabolite SAM, plays a crucial role in these epigenetic processes. By serving as a methyl donor, SAM directly affects DNA methylation patterns, alters RNA processing and stability, and influences histone modifications, ultimately affecting the transcriptional landscape of cancer cells. DNA methylation

DNA methylation is a key epigenetic modification mediated by DNA methyltransferase (DNMT), where SAM serves as the methyl donor for the addition of methyl groups to cytosine residues, leading to the formation of 5-methylcytosine. In cancer, DNA methylation patterns exhibit a striking dichotomy: widespread hypomethylation across the genome contributes to genomic instability, whereas targeted hypermethylation, particularly at tumor suppressor gene promoters, leads to transcriptional silencing by promoting a more compact chromatin structure [74]. These epigenetic alterations play crucial roles in cancer development, progression, and treatment resistance by influencing cellular behavior and interactions between tumors and their microenvironment.

SAM, a critical methyl donor, directly influences DNA methylation in tumor cells. Preclinical studies have shown that SAM can inhibit the growth of various tumors, including osteosarcoma [75], hepatocellular carcinoma [76], and adenocarcinoma of the stomach [77], prostate [78], breast [79] and colorectum [80]. Despite its use as a nutritional supplement with minimal documented toxicity [81], the precise molecular mechanisms of SAM in tumors with dynamic genetic characteristics remain unclear, with some studies suggesting a tumor-suppressing effect and others suggesting a potential tumor-promoting role.

One proposed mechanism of SAM activity involves its interaction with the methionine cycle, influencing DNA methylation patterns [82] by enhancing the activity of enzymes such as DNMT3A [83, 84] and DNMT1. These interactions can promote tumor suppression by stabilizing estrogen-related receptor α (ERRα) or enhancing histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4) methylation [82]. However, contradictory findings have shown that SAM may also promote the growth and metastasis of colorectal cancer (CRC) by increasing the expression of methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), highlighting the complexity of the role of SAM in cancer [85]. Additionally, SAM may induce the demethylation of specific gene promoters, such as B-cell lymphoma 2/adenovirus E1B 19-kDa interacting protein 3, contributing to mitophagic cell death [86]. Because many cellular methylation reactions rely on methionine-dependent pathways, methionine metabolism can influence DNA methylation in ways that vary across time and tissues. This context dependence complicates efforts to define how methionine flux shapes cancer-related methylation landscapes at the global level.

The intracellular SAM pool, which is critical for DNA methylation, is shaped not only by methionine availability but also by one-carbon metabolism, which depends on nutrients such as folate, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, choline, and betaine [87, 88]. Inadequate folate or vitamin B12 intake has been associated with aberrant methionine metabolism and altered DNA methylation patterns [89–93]. Vitamin B6, a cofactor for serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT), is likewise linked to methylation-related cancer risk, including an inverse association with colorectal neoplasia [94, 95]. Altered vitamin B12 status has also been associated with DNA methylation changes in CRC [96]. Consistent with this broader metabolic framework, SHMT2 supports the generation of one-carbon units that contribute to SAM synthesis and downstream DNA methylation. In papillary thyroid cancer, SHMT2-driven SAM production has been reported to suppress the PTEN promoter and promote metastasis through the activation of AKT signaling [97].

Defects in folate cycle enzymes can further perturb this methyl-donor network. For example, the 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) 677T polymorphism reduces the conversion of 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate to 5-MTHF, thus limiting methionine regeneration, restricting SAM synthesis, and altering global DNA methylation patterns [98]. Beyond these effects on DNA methylation itself, emerging evidence suggests that methionine availability can also modulate pluripotency-associated gene expression and methylation-related regulators in stem cell contexts, including cancer stem cells. Specifically, methionine supplementation has been shown to alter the expression of octamer-binding transcription factor 3/4, the homeobox transcription factor Nanog, and DNMT, the SAM/SAH balance, and histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) in mesenchymal stem cells and cancer stem cells, highlighting the broader link between methyl-donor metabolism and cell state regulation [99]. Collectively, these observations indicate that the effects of methionine metabolism on DNA methylation are highly context dependent and extend beyond tumor cell-intrinsic methylation changes alone. Current evidence supports a role for methyl-donor metabolism in shaping tumor behavior and stem cell-associated programs, although its effects on immune and stromal compartments within the tumor microenvironment remain unclear.

RNA methylation

N6-methyladenosine (m6A), a prevalent RNA modification involving methylation at position 6 of adenosine, has become among the most extensively studied RNA modifications [100]. It influences nearly every aspect of RNA metabolism, including alternative splicing, nuclear export, stability, and translation [101]. Dysregulated m6A modification plays a critical role in cancer progression through the modulation of processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, and metastasis. These effects are often mediated through alterations in cellular processes such as autophagy, glycolysis, mitochondrial metabolism, stemness remodeling, and changes in the tumor microenvironment. In line with this concept, recent reviews have further emphasized that RNA modifications, including m6A, m1A, m5C, m7G, pseudouridylation, and A-to-I editing, can regulate interactions among cancer cells, immune cells, stromal cells, and other TME components, thus influencing the cancer immunity cycle and immunotherapy response [102, 103].

SAM, as a methyl donor for methyltransferases, regulates RNA stability, processing, and translation efficiency, thus influencing gene expression at the post-transcriptional level [104]. Key RNA methyltransferases such as METTL3, methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14), and Wilms tumor 1-associated protein use SAM to install m6A marks on RNA [105]. METTL3, in particular, has been shown to increase the translation of specific transcripts in acute myeloid leukemia [106]. Consistent with these findings, dysregulated RNA methylation has been implicated in tumorigenesis, metabolic reprogramming, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance across multiple cancer types, and m6A-related signatures have been shown to have diagnostic and prognostic value in gastrointestinal malignancies [107–109]. However, the functional consequences of RNA methylation are highly context dependent. In glioblastoma stem cells, for example, knockdown of METTL3 or METTL14 promoted growth, self-renewal, and tumorigenesis, whereas METTL3 overexpression or fat mass and obesity-associated protein inhibition suppressed these phenotypes, indicating that components of the m6A machinery can exert tumor-suppressive effects in specific cellular contexts [110]. Similar context-dependent findings have been reported in other settings: zinc finger CCCH-type containing 13 has been associated with better prognosis and tumor-suppressive activity in hepatocellular carcinoma, whereas in oral carcinogenesis, hypermethylation with increased expression of carbon chain 4B, dynein axonemal heavy chain 9, and neurocalcin delta has been linked to favorable prognosis [111, 112]. Thus, rather than uniformly promoting tumor progression, m6A-dependent regulation may either support or restrain malignancy depending on the tumor type, the affected transcripts, and the surrounding immune context.

RNA methylation is also emerging as a key player in tumor immunity, prompting further investigation into the broader implications of RNA methylation and methionine metabolism in cancer immunity. SAM-mediated m6A methylation of immune checkpoint molecules such as programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) and V-domain immunoglobulin suppressor of T-cell activation (VISTA) in tumor cells has been shown to suppress T-cell immunity [113]. Additionally, methylation of cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) by SAM dampens type I interferon signaling in cancer cells. Reduced methylation of cGAS is associated with increased immune cell infiltration and heightened sensitivity to immunotherapy in vivo, suggesting that enhanced methionine metabolism in tumor cells may hinder the response to antitumor immunotherapy [114]. These findings highlight the potential of SAM derivatives as biocoupling tools for targeting and labeling specific tumor markers [115].

Although the precise mechanisms linking these processes to cancer incidence and progression are still not fully understood, these findings underscore the significant role of methionine metabolism in regulating cancer-related epigenomic programs. This complex interplay among methionine metabolism, RNA methylation, and tumor biology offers promising avenues for therapeutic strategies targeting the molecular pathways involved in cancer progression and immunity.

Histone methylation

Histone methylation is a critical epigenetic mechanism that regulates chromatin state and gene expression. This process, mediated by histone methyltransferases (HMTs), involves varying degrees of methylation on lysine (mono-, di-, or trimethylation) and arginine residues (mono- or dimethylation) on histone proteins. More than 30 enzymes dynamically regulate these modifications, which can either activate or repress gene expression depending on the specific residues involved. Notably, histone methylation plays a significant role in tumorigenesis and metabolic regulation, often independent of DNA methylation [74]. SAM serves as the methyl donor for HMTs, modifying lysine and arginine residues and thus influencing chromatin structure, transcriptional activity, and epigenetic memory. Alterations in SAM availability, as observed in cancer cells, impact key histone methylation marks such as H3K4me3 and H3K27 trimethylation (H3K27me3). For example, SAM treatment in prostate cancer cells alters the profiles of these markers, revealing the dynamic interplay between methionine metabolism and histone methylation [116, 117].

Further genome-wide studies have indicated that while MR does not significantly change the position of H3K4me3 peaks, it does affect their width, ultimately influencing gene expression related to cell identity and cancer-associated pathways [118]. Additionally, the knockdown of SERPINE1 mRNA binding protein 1 leads to reduced methionine levels, which decreases histone methylation (e.g., H3K27me3) and upregulates genes associated with neurogenesis and neuronal differentiation [119].

The balance between SAM and SAH is critical for maintaining histone methylation dynamics. Cancer-associated fibroblasts express the stromal methyltransferase nicotinamide N-methyltransferase (NNMT), which depletes SAM, reducing histone methylation levels and resulting in extensive gene expression changes within the tumor stroma [120]. Notably, inhibiting NNMT activity has shown the potential to reverse these effects and normalize the metastatic stroma, suggesting exciting avenues for therapeutic strategies.

Furthermore, histone methylation within cancer cells is closely linked to one-carbon metabolism through the integration of the methionine and folate cycles. By supplying one-carbon units for SAM synthesis, folate-dependent pathways help determine methyl-donor availability and thus influence both DNA and histone methylation. For example, SHMT2 supports one-carbon flux, which can promote epigenetic silencing of tumor suppressor genes such as SAM and SH3 domain containing 1 and protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type M, thus contributing to lymphomagenesis [121]. Similarly, disruption of folate metabolism, such as MTHFR deficiency, reduces H3K27 and H3K9 methylation and has been linked to resistance to MYC-targeting therapies in acute myeloid leukemia; notably, supplementation with 5-MTHF can reverse these effects [122].

At the microenvironmental level, methionine metabolism also affects histone methylation through stromal regulation and nutrient competition. Cancer-associated fibroblasts express NNMT, which depletes SAM and decreases histone methylation in the tumor stroma, thus reshaping stromal gene expression programs [120]. In parallel, tumor cells can outcompete T cells for methionine, reducing intracellular SAM and H3K79 dimethylation (H3K79me2) at genes such as signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) and impairing T-cell effector function [13, 123, 124]. In addition, the accumulation of acidic metabolic byproducts in the tumor microenvironment can further impair methionine uptake by T cells, decreasing SAM and H3K27me3 levels. Under these conditions, T cells may adopt a stem-like memory state characterized by reduced immediate effector activity but enhanced long-term persistence and antitumor potential after adoptive transfer [17]. Together, these findings indicate that methionine metabolism regulates histone methylation at both the cell-intrinsic level and the microenvironmental level, linking intracellular methyl-donor metabolism to broader TME remodeling.

In summary, the role of SAM as a methyl donor in histone methylation underscores the intricate connections among methionine metabolism, epigenetic regulation, and cancer biology. SAM-mediated histone methylation dynamically influences chromatin structure and gene expression, with significant implications for tumor progression, immune modulation, and therapeutic resistance. Understanding these complex interactions paves the way for potential therapeutic strategies that target both metabolic and epigenetic pathways in cancer. Targeting SAM metabolism presents a promising avenue for the development of new epigenetic cancer therapies.

Tissue-specific dependencies

The metabolism of methionine plays a crucial role in cancer progression. Understanding these dependencies is essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies, as different tumors exhibit distinct metabolic adaptations on the basis of their tissue origin. In this section, we explore how methionine metabolism varies across specific cancer types, with a focus on liver cancer and glioblastoma as prime examples. These variations are fundamentally related to the expression of key enzymes and transporters that influence the utilization of methionine and its downstream metabolic pathways. For instance, in HCC, the balance between MATs shifts because of changes in enzyme expression, affecting SAM levels and consequent DNA methylation patterns. Similarly, glioblastoma relies on extracellular methionine because of the frequent loss of MTAP, leading to unique metabolic adaptations that can be exploited for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, such as with methionine PET imaging. Liver cancer and the MATI/III: MATII ratio

Methionine metabolism is crucial for HCC progression, particularly through its regulation of key enzymes such as MAT I, MAT II, and MAT III. MAT1A, which is expressed in differentiated hepatocytes, serves as a marker of normal liver function. MAT1A encodes the MATα1 isoform, which is predominantly expressed in the liver and catalyzes the synthesis of SAM. MATIII is a liver-enriched isoform derived from the MAT1A gene and is typically associated with differentiated hepatocyte metabolism and maintenance of hepatic SAM homeostasis. In contrast to the MAT1A/MAT I/III-dominant state in normal liver, many liver cancers exhibit a shift toward MAT2A expression, which is associated with dedifferentiation and metabolic reprogramming. MAT1A expression is downregulated in most cirrhotic patients and patients with HCC. Prohibitin 1 and MAT1A positively regulate each other, and their deletion results in primary liver cancer [125]. However, during rapid liver growth or dedifferentiation, MAT2A and MAT2B expression increases, whereas MAT1A expression decreases [126]. During fetal liver development, MAT2A expression is gradually replaced by MAT1A expression, reflecting a shift toward more differentiated states [127, 128]. Notably, MAT2A overexpression is frequently observed in various epithelial tumors, especially in liver cancer, and the latest clinical research provides preliminary evidence of the clinical activity and proof of mechanism of MAT2A inhibition [42, 129].

MAT1A is expressed mainly in hepatocytes and maintains the differentiated state of these cells, whereas MAT2A and MAT2B are expressed in extrahepatic tissues as well as nonparenchymal cells of the liver (e.g., hepatic stellate and Kupffer cells). In several liver disorders and during hepatic growth or dedifferentiation, expression tends to shift from MAT1A toward MAT2A/MAT2B, a transition that is associated with decreased SAM availability in the liver [130].

In the context of liver cancer, hypoxia-induced activation of MAT2A by hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha increases MAT II enzyme activity, which in turn reduces SAM production, leading to DNA demethylation [131, 132]. Furthermore, MAT2B, a regulatory subunit of MAT II, has been shown to promote tumor cell migration and invasion, with its interaction with RNA-binding protein human antigen R and sirtuin 1 proteins modulating the response to therapeutic agents such as resveratrol [133]. These findings underscore the importance of MATs in liver tumorigenesis.

The deregulation of SAM is also a hallmark of HCC. Glycine N-methyltransferase, which typically regulates SAM levels in the liver, is downregulated in HCC, contributing to a metabolic shift that increases SAM levels [39, 134–137]. However, in chronic liver disease and HCC, the MAT1A to MAT2A/MAT2B switch contributes to reduced SAM levels [130]. This dysregulation alters critical pathways such as phospholipid synthesis and DNA methylation, both of which are essential for cancer cell survival and proliferation. Notably, choline depletion exacerbates these changes, whereas choline supplementation has shown potential in reversing some of these effects, including enhancing DNA methylation and regulating fatty acid oxidation [138, 139].

Researchers have studied the tissue specificity of methionine–cysteine conversion and detected the expression of methionine-based cysteine synthesis-related metabolic enzymes in 12 major organ tissues of mice [37]. The investigators additionally reported that enzymes mediating the conversion between methionine and cysteine were expressed almost entirely in hepatic tissue. The results showed that liver tissue has a complete methionine-initiated cysteine synthesis pathway and that methionine-initiated cysteine synthesis through the transsulfuration pathway may play a tissue-specific role.

As HCC progresses, the MAT1A to MAT2A switch, which is accompanied by reduced methionine resynthesis and increased SAM utilization for polyamine synthesis, plays a pivotal role in driving tumor aggressiveness. This shift is correlated with decreased DNA methylation, increased cell proliferation, and reduced apoptosis-related hallmarks of cancer progression. The MAT1A:MAT2A expression ratio is inversely associated with cellular proliferation and genomic instability but is positively correlated with apoptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and overall DNA methylation. Clinically, a lower ratio has been linked to poorer prognosis, greater tumor aggressiveness, and reduced survival in patients with HCC [130]. The MATI/III: MATII activity ratio, reflecting the balance between MAT isoforms, serves as a reliable biomarker for HCC prognosis. A lower MAT1A: MAT2A ratio is consistently associated with poorer survival outcomes and increased tumor proliferation [38].

Overall, the progressive deregulation of methionine metabolism in HCC, including the shift from MAT1A to MAT2A expression, altered SAM levels, and increased polyamine synthesis, significantly contributes to liver cancer progression. The MATI/III:MATII ratio, along with other molecular markers, such as DNA methylation and genomic instability, holds promise as a prognostic biomarker for HCC. Targeting these metabolic alterations could offer potential therapeutic strategies to halt tumor progression and improve patient outcomes.

Methionine metabolic characteristics in glioblastoma and the application of methionine PET

The methionine-related metabolic phenotype of glioblastoma is associated with recurrent genetic and metabolic alterations, including frequent MTAP loss [140]. MTAP deficiency disrupts methionine salvage and promotes the extracellular accumulation of MTA [56], which may increase the reliance on extracellular methionine in at least a subset of glioblastomas. In contrast, methionine uptake in the normal brain is generally relatively low, contributing to the tumor-to-background contrast observed on methionine-based imaging. Accordingly, altered methionine metabolism may serve as a useful metabolic biomarker and a potential therapeutic vulnerability in selected glioblastoma contexts.

These features provide a rationale for the clinical use of 11C-methionine PET (MET-PET) in glioblastoma and other brain tumors [141–143]. MET uptake primarily reflects sodium-independent amino acid transport and is influenced by intracellular amino acid metabolism [144–148]. Because background uptake in the normal brain is generally low [149, 150], compared with FDG-PET, MET-PET is often advantageous for lesion delineation [142, 151]. However, its diagnostic specificity is not absolute, as increased uptake may also occur in inflammatory lesions and other non-neoplastic conditions [142, 152, 153]. In addition, treatment-related changes such as radiation necrosis may complicate image interpretation in post-therapy settings [142, 154, 155].

In glioblastoma, methionine tracer accumulation is thought to be influenced primarily by transporter-mediated uptake, while blood–brain barrier disruption and tumor vascularization may also contribute, particularly in more aggressive lesions [142, 143]. In addition, several studies have reported associations between MET uptake and biological features such as proliferative activity and microvessel density, suggesting that MET-PET may provide complementary information on tumor behavior [156, 157]. In radiation treatment planning, MET-PET has shown utility in refining gross tumor volume and clinical target volume delineation, although its use remains constrained by the short half-life of 11C, which generally requires onsite cyclotron support. Future integration of MET-PET with molecular profiling of methionine pathway components, including MTAP and MAT2A, may further improve patient stratification and therapeutic monitoring in glioblastoma.

Methionine transporter alterations and transporter-targeted probes

The dependency of cancer cells on methionine, an essential amino acid critical for methylation reactions, redox homeostasis, and epigenetic regulation, is pronounced. This metabolic vulnerability has been associated, in part, with increased expression of methionine transporter genes such as SLC43A2 and SLC7A5, whose corresponding transporters contribute to methionine acquisition in selected tumor contexts and in the tumor microenvironment.

SLC43A2 has been implicated in methionine acquisition in selected tumor and immune contexts, although it should not be interpreted as a universally dominant methionine transporter across all cancers. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, methionine/cysteine restriction suppresses tumor growth by disrupting the SLC43A2-nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)-GPX4 feedback loop, thus promoting ferroptosis through reduced glutathione synthesis and enhanced lipid peroxidation [158]. In HCC, SLC43A2 upregulation mediated by tumor-associated macrophage-derived circPETH-147aa impairs CD8+ T-cell function by depleting methionine, thus weakening antitumor immunity [159].

Beyond its role in methionine transport, SLC43A2 has been linked to oncogenic signaling in specific cancer settings. Yes-associated protein (YAP) activation enhances SLC43A2 transcription, promoting methionine uptake and drug resistance, whereas treatment with the SLC43A2 inhibitor BCH sensitizes tumors to chemotherapy through disruption of this YAP-driven axis [160]. These findings suggest that SLC43A2 may contribute to metabolic adaptation, immune regulation, and drug responses in specific tumor settings.

In addition to SLC43A2, increased expression of SLC7A5 has been reported in aggressive tumors such as upper aerodigestive squamous cell carcinoma. At the protein level, LAT1-mediated methionine uptake has been linked to EZH2-dependent epigenetic reprogramming and sustained tumor proliferation [161]. This transporter’s high specificity for methionine has been leveraged for imaging applications: probes such as near-infrared methionine-N-hydroxy succinimide Au nanoclusters and dual-modality agents such as 2,2′,2″-(10-(2-(1-carboxy-3-(methylthio)propylamino)-2-oxoethyl)-1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7-triyl)triacetic acid selectively accumulate in LAT1-expressing tumors, allowing precise diagnostic visualization [162, 163]. The PET tracer 3-(18)F-l-α-methyl-tyrosine further confirms the diagnostic utility of LAT1, as uptake is closely correlated with LAT1 expression [164].

Methionine metabolism in cancer stem cells

Emerging evidence suggests that in several tumor contexts, cancer stem cells (CSCs) and tumor-initiating cells (TICs) exhibit increased reliance on methionine metabolism to maintain stemness-associated programs [165, 166]. Enhanced methionine cycle activity can support SAM production and methylation-dependent epigenetic states linked to self-renewal and an undifferentiated phenotype [167–169]. Accordingly, disrupting methionine availability or methionine-derived methyl-donor metabolism has been shown to impair CSC-associated phenotypes in preclinical models [167–169]. In addition to its role as a methyl donor, methionine metabolism is linked to oxidative phosphorylation, autophagy, and redox homeostasis, which may help CSCs adapt to nutrient limitation and therapeutic stress [166]. In addition, methionine-derived metabolites such as homocysteine can enter the trans-sulfuration pathway to support glutathione synthesis, thus strengthening antioxidant defenses and potentially contributing to resistance against radiation- or chemotherapy-induced oxidative damage [7, 170]. Glioblastoma TICs: folate-cycle coupling and SAM-dependent stemness

Glioblastoma TICs appear to display methionine dependency in part because of impaired coupling between folate metabolism and methionine regeneration [169]. Under methionine-deprived conditions, these cells are unable to efficiently generate the one-carbon units required to convert folate derivatives into methionine and SAM, resulting in impaired tumorsphere formation. Supplementation with folate derivatives such as 5-methyl-tetrahydrofolate can partially restore this phenotype, highlighting the functional interplay between folate metabolism and methionine availability in glioblastoma TICs [169]. In parallel, methionine deprivation has been linked to reduced SAM-dependent stemness-associated epigenetic states in glioblastoma models [166]. Methionine metabolism has also been linked to mTOR-associated control of energy metabolism and protein synthesis, further supporting the maintenance of TIC stemness [172, 173].

Lung TICs: enhanced one-carbon flux and methionine cycle activation

In lung tumor-initiating cells, elevated methionine cycle flux is associated with tumor-initiating capacity [167]. Increased expression and activity of glycine decarboxylase (GLDC) can preferentially shunt one-carbon units toward methionine and SAM production, thus promoting histone methylation and supporting stem-like properties [174]. Consistent with this model, GLDC silencing has been reported to reduce methionine and SAH levels, decrease histone methylation, and impair tumor initiation [167, 174]. Additional evidence suggests that this metabolic state may be reinforced by MAT2A, whose upregulation can help sustain SAM synthesis and maintain DNA and histone methylation programs linked to stemness-associated transcriptional programs [167]. A bidirectional relationship with autophagy has also been proposed, whereby SAM-dependent methylation may restrain autophagy, whereas autophagy-mediated recycling of intracellular components may help provide substrates that support methionine cycle function under stress conditions [1, 165].

Breast CSCs: SAM biosynthetic vulnerability and therapeutic resistance

Similarly, breast CSCs exhibit methionine-related metabolic vulnerability. Methionine deprivation has been shown to reduce mammosphere formation, lower intracellular SAM levels, decrease H3K4me3, and suppress pluripotency-associated features such as SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) expression and the CD44⁺/CD24⁻ phenotype [168]. Mechanistically, methionine depletion decreases SAM availability and enhances the loss of H3K4me3 together with the suppression of SOX9, supporting a link between SAM biosynthesis, H3K4 methylation, and CSC maintenance [168]. More recent work further supports this concept by showing that BRCA2-interacting transcriptional repressor-driven methionine metabolism promotes breast CSC self-renewal and tumorigenesis in an H3K4 methylation-dependent manner and that methionine deprivation decreases H3K4 methylation in these models [175]. These observations support the concept that SAM biosynthesis represents a targetable metabolic dependency in at least a subset of breast CSC models.

O-GlcNAcylation as a potential CSC-associated modifier of methionine-linked epigenetic states

An additional emerging theme is the potential interaction between methionine-linked epigenetic regulation and O-GlcNAcylation in CSC maintenance. In breast CSCs, elevated O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) levels and O-GlcNAcylation have been shown to maintain stem-like potential and tumor-initiating capacity [176, 177]. However, a direct mechanistic link between O-GlcNAcylation and methionine metabolism has not yet been established in CSCs. Current support for such a connection is largely indirect, including evidence from pluripotent stem cell models showing that O-GlcNAcylation can regulate the methionine cycle to maintain pluripotency [178]. Thus, in the context of CSCs, this relationship is best considered a putative crosstalk pathway rather than a core established mechanism of methionine dependency. One plausible point of convergence is epigenetic regulation, as OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation can stabilize EZH2 and promote H3K27 methylation, suggesting that O-GlcNAc-associated signaling states may influence SAM-dependent chromatin regulation [179].

In addition, methionine/SAM signaling and polyamine metabolism may intersect with autophagy- and stress-adaptation pathways that support CSC survival under nutrient limitation or therapeutic pressure, although the direct relevance of this axis to CSC maintenance remains to be fully defined [180, 181]. Taken together, the current evidence supports a model in which methionine metabolism contributes to CSC-associated phenotypes primarily through SAM-dependent epigenetic regulation, one-carbon metabolic support, and adaptation to oxidative or nutrient stress, whereas additional links to O-GlcNAcylation and autophagy remain more provisional. Although the degree of methionine dependency likely varies across tumor types and CSC states, these studies suggest that methionine-related metabolic programs can sustain self-renewal and therapy resistance in selected settings. Accordingly, targeting methionine availability or key enzymes involved in SAM biosynthesis may represent a rational strategy to weaken CSC-associated phenotypes and improve the therapeutic response.

An integrative framework linking methionine metabolism to cancer biology

Taken together, the diverse effects of methionine metabolism in cancer can be organized into four interconnected mechanistic layers. First, tumor-intrinsic methionine/SAM dependency reflects the increased requirement of many cancer cells for exogenous methionine, methionine transport, MAT-mediated SAM synthesis, MTAP-dependent salvage, and polyamine-associated SAM utilization. These processes support anabolic growth, redox adaptation, and proliferative fitness, but their relative importance varies across tumor types and genetic contexts. Second, altered methionine and SAM homeostasis reshapes epigenetic and transcriptional programs through DNA, RNA, and histone methylation, thus influencing cell identity, stemness, immune checkpoint expression, and therapeutic resistance. Third, methionine metabolism extends beyond malignant cells to remodel the immune and stromal compartments of the TME, where nutrient competition, immune cell methylation states, stromal SAM consumption, and intercellular metabolic exchange can collectively regulate immune evasion and microenvironmental adaptation. Finally, these tumor-intrinsic, epigenetic, and microenvironmental mechanisms create therapeutic vulnerabilities, including sensitivity to dietary methionine restriction, methioninase-based depletion, transporter inhibition, MAT2A/PRMT5-directed strategies, and rational combination therapies. However, compensatory remethylation, metabolic plasticity, tumor heterogeneity, microbiota-derived metabolites, and toxicity to normal methionine-dependent cells may contribute to resistance or limit therapeutic windows. This framework provides a mechanistic bridge between the metabolic, epigenetic, microenvironmental, and therapeutic sections of this review.

Methionine metabolic plasticity in microenvironment remodeling

Methionine metabolic plasticity has emerged as an important determinant of tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling, extending the significance of methionine metabolism beyond tumor cell-intrinsic proliferation and survival. In addition to promoting one-carbon metabolism, redox balance, and methylation reactions within malignant cells, methionine availability can influence immune-cell fitness, stromal-cell epigenetic states, and intercellular nutrient competition across the TME in a context-dependent manner. Together, these observations support the view that methionine metabolism serves as an important metabolic node that links cancer-cell metabolic rewiring to broader microenvironmental adaptation. This concept is consistent with recent views of the TME as a metabolically competitive ecosystem in which nutrient availability, metabolic byproducts, and immune-cell metabolic reprogramming collectively shape immune suppression, stromal remodeling, and therapeutic responses [182]. In this context, methionine-dependent signaling may contribute to immune evasion through checkpoint regulation and cytokine-associated programs while also affecting stromal remodeling, extracellular communication, and the spatial distribution of metabolic resources. Accordingly, methionine metabolic plasticity should be viewed not only as a feature of tumor metabolic adaptability but also as a potential contributor to multicellular ecosystem remodeling that integrates tumor, immune, and stromal compartments. Understanding how these methionine-centered interactions operate across distinct cellular populations will be important for clarifying the context-specific consequences of methionine targeting and for designing more effective, metabolically informed therapeutic strategies.

Immune reprogramming in the TME

Methionine metabolism is an important regulator of tumor–microenvironment interactions. In addition to supporting anabolic growth and epigenetic plasticity in tumor cells, methionine availability also affects nutrient competition, stromal remodeling, and antitumor immune responses within the TME. Collectively, these interactions indicate that methionine metabolism contributes to TME remodeling through both cell-intrinsic epigenetic regulation and intercellular metabolic crosstalk.

Tumor cell-intrinsic immunoregulatory signaling

In tumor cells, increased methionine uptake can increase intracellular SAM availability and support methylation-dependent programs that regulate immune-modulatory transcripts. One example is the methionine-YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 1 (YTHDF1)-m6A axis, which has been linked to enhanced translation of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 and VISTA, thus reinforcing immune evasion [113]. Mechanistically, YTHDF1 binds m6A-modified PD-L1 and VISTA transcripts and promotes their translation without necessarily altering transcript abundance, whereas YTHDF1 depletion or MR reduces checkpoint protein expression and enhances CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor killing [113].

In addition to checkpoint regulation, methionine metabolism in tumor cells can influence the secretion of other immune-modulatory factors. For example, MR inhibits the secretion of macrophage migration inhibitory factor in gastric carcinoma cells, thus suppressing tumor-promoting M2 macrophage polarization and favoring the tumor-suppressive M1 phenotype [183]. Together, these findings indicate that tumor-cell methionine metabolism can shape immune evasion not only through SAM-dependent post-transcriptional checkpoint regulation but also through altered secretion of immune-modulatory factors.

Immune-cell and systemic methionine restriction effects

In immune cells, methionine availability has distinct consequences for effector differentiation and antitumor function. Activated T cells require methionine to sustain protein synthesis, SAM production, and methylation-dependent transcriptional programs, and MR can impair these processes, including by reducing H3K79me2-associated STAT5 transcriptional activity, thus compromising CD8+ T-cell activation [1, 15]. In CD4+ T helper cells, MR can also alter histone methylation and lineage-associated transcriptional programs, reducing Th17 proliferation and interleukin-17 production [124]. Within the TME, tumor cells may outcompete T cells for methionine, creating a metabolic disadvantage for the immune compartment. Reduced methionine availability in T cells decreases intracellular SAM levels and contributes to impaired effector fitness, whereas acidic metabolic byproducts in the TME can further restrict methionine uptake and alter histone methylation states linked to T-cell function. Under some conditions, such stress may promote a stem-like memory phenotype characterized by lower immediate effector activity but greater long-term persistence after adoptive transfer [17].

At the systemic level, dietary MR can reshape antitumor immunity in a context-dependent manner. Under methionine-restricted conditions, reduced methylation of cGAS has been reported to activate cGAS-STING signaling, thus enhancing type I interferon production and CD8+ T-cell recruitment [114, 184]. In some preclinical settings, MR also synergizes with PD-1 blockade by promoting CD8+ T-cell infiltration and lowering checkpoint protein expression on tumor cells [113, 185]. However, these immunomodulatory effects are not uniformly beneficial. In some contexts, gut microbiota-derived methionine may buffer dietary restriction, but the magnitude and therapeutic relevance of this effect in mammalian tumors remain to be established. Together, these findings indicate that methionine-dependent immune reprogramming extends beyond a single checkpoint axis and includes T-cell epigenetic fitness, innate immune signaling, and host-level metabolic context.

Stromal and intercellular metabolic remodeling

Methionine metabolism also affects stromal and intercellular interactions within the TME, including fibroblast-associated methyl-donor remodeling, nutrient competition between tumor and immune cells, and broader changes in intercellular communication [13, 120, 123, 124]. In the stromal compartment, cancer-associated fibroblasts express NNMT, which consumes SAM and decreases histone methylation in the tumor stroma, thus reshaping stromal gene expression programs and metastatic niche properties [120]. These findings suggest that methionine-related methyl-donor metabolism is not only restricted to malignant cells but also contributes to epigenetic remodeling of non-malignant TME populations.

Intercellular methionine exchange may further influence tumor progression and immune exclusion. In some settings, stromal cells can affect local methionine availability, whereas tumor cells can redistribute methionine resources in ways that disadvantage immune cells and reinforce immunosuppressive conditions. In parallel, MR has been associated with altered extracellular vesicle production, suggesting that methionine metabolism may influence intercellular communication beyond intracellular methylation states [186].

Emerging technologies may help clarify these spatially organized metabolic interactions. Single-cell multiomics and spatial metabolomics provide complementary resolution for identifying cell type-specific and spatially organized methionine-related states within tumors, but these approaches do not directly measure metabolic flux. In contrast, fluxomics approaches, including stable isotope tracing and flux analysis, are needed to directly quantify methionine utilization, methyl-donor turnover, and pathway partitioning across the methionine, folate, trans-sulfuration, and polyamine networks. Integrating these descriptive and flux-based methods should improve the understanding of how methionine metabolism is distributed across tumor, immune, and stromal compartments in vivo.

Collectively, the current evidence supports a multifaceted role for methionine metabolism in shaping the tumor microenvironment through tumor cell-intrinsic immunoregulatory signaling, nutrient competition with immune cells, and stromal epigenetic remodeling. Moreover, important gaps remain regarding the cell-type specificity, temporal dynamics, and therapeutic window of methionine-targeted interventions within the TME. A clearer integration of tumor, immune, and stromal methionine biology will be essential for translating these insights into rational combination therapies.

Therapeutic strategies targeting methionine dependence

Methionine dependence has emerged as a therapeutically actionable metabolic vulnerability in cancer, providing multiple opportunities to intervene at the levels of nutrient availability, transport, and intracellular utilization. Building on the central role of methionine in one-carbon metabolism, redox homeostasis, and methylation-dependent gene regulation, current therapeutic strategies aim not only to deprive tumor cells of this essential nutrient but also to exploit the metabolic inflexibility that distinguishes methionine-dependent cancers from normal tissues. As summarized in Fig. 6, these approaches include dietary MR, methioninase-based enzymatic depletion, targeting of methionine transporters, small-molecule inhibition of methionine metabolism enzymes, and rational combination regimens with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy. Dietary methionine restriction

MR has emerged as a promising nonpharmacological strategy to selectively target methionine-dependent tumors. Cancer cells typically exhibit a heightened reliance on exogenous methionine because of their increased anabolic and epigenetic demands, distinguishing them from normal cells that can utilize alternative pathways for methionine synthesis. This metabolic vulnerability provides the foundation for dietary MR as a selective anticancer intervention [187].

Methionine plays a central role in one-carbon metabolism, redox regulation, and methylation through the production of SAM. The metabolic inflexibility of tumor cells, particularly their inability to efficiently recycle homocysteine to methionine, renders them uniquely sensitive to methionine deprivation. Consequently, MR has been extensively explored in preclinical models for its antitumor efficacy and ability to enhance conventional therapies. A growing body of evidence demonstrates that MR can suppress tumor growth across a wide spectrum of cancer types. In pancreatic cancer models, MR significantly reduced tumor burden and was linked to decreased signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling, indicating that suppression of the Janus kinase 2/STAT3 axis is a key mechanism [188]. In gastric cancer, MR impaired tumor cell migration and invasion by downregulating Tripartite motif 47, stabilizing IkappaBalpha, and attenuating NF-κB pathway activity, ultimately reducing metastatic capacity in vivo [189].

In glioma, MR acts in synergy with cysteine deprivation to promote ferroptotic cell death. This occurs via enhanced lipid peroxidation and reduced glutathione synthesis. Notably, a diet restricted in both methionine and cysteine potentiated the efficacy of GPX4 inhibition and extended survival in murine glioma models [21]. Beyond metabolic stress, MR also reprograms the glioma epigenetic landscape. It promotes Cbl proto-oncogene-mediated degradation of lysine-specific histone demethylase 1, thus altering histone methylation patterns and reshaping lipid metabolism through C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 upregulation [190].

In addition to its therapeutic potential, MR has shown preventive efficacy in genetically predisposed cancer models. In a transgenic adenocarcinoma mouse prostate model, MR significantly reduced the incidence of high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia, which correlated with decreased cell proliferation and lower plasma insulin-like growth factor 1 levels, without impairing immune competence [191]. These findings are further supported by studies indicating that MR extends both lifespan and healthspan, suggesting systemic benefits beyond tumor suppression [192].

Despite these encouraging results, the response to MR is not uniform across all tumor types. Variability in MR sensitivity has been linked to genetic and metabolic factors. For instance, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α plays a critical role in regulating sulfur-related amino acid metabolism in liver cancer. Its loss disrupts the trans-sulfuration pathway, conferring resistance to MR and promoting the epithelial‒mesenchymal transition and metastasis. Restoring this pathway reverses the resistance phenotype, underscoring the importance of molecular profiling in predicting MR responsiveness [193].

Mechanistically, MR exerts its antitumor effects through a complex interplay of metabolic and epigenetic pathways. By limiting SAM availability, MR reduces histone methylation and activates nutrient-sensing transcription factors such as transcription factor EB and forkhead box A, promoting autophagic flux, which is a key component of MR-induced tumor suppression and longevity [192, 194]. Additionally, MR perturbs redox homeostasis by reducing sulfur metabolite availability and glutathione-dependent antioxidant capacity, which can increase oxidative stress and increase redox vulnerability in cancer cells in several preclinical models [21, 25, 37, 195].

Taken together, these studies underscore the therapeutic potential of dietary MR as a selective metabolic strategy to inhibit tumor growth while sparing normal tissues [196, 197]. Its ability to synergize with ferroptosis inducers, epigenetic modifiers, and conventional therapies highlights its promise as an adjunct to current cancer treatments [185]. Moving forward, efforts should focus on identifying reliable biomarkers of MR sensitivity, refining dietary protocols, and designing clinical trials to evaluate MR in combination with established therapeutic modalities.

Methioninase enzyme therapies

Methioninase (METase), particularly recombinant methioninase (rMETase), has demonstrated significant promise as a cancer therapeutic when used in combination with conventional chemotherapy, offering a stepwise mechanistic rationale for its synergy. Structural insights gained from crystallographic analyses of METase across various organisms, such as Citrobacter freundii and P. putida [233–238], as well as from engineered mutants such as Q349S and C116H [239–241], have elucidated the mechanisms underlying substrate selectivity and catalytic efficiency, thus informing therapeutic enhancements. To mitigate issues such as short plasma half-life and immunogenic responses, approaches involving PEGylation have been developed. For instance, conjugation with the N-hydroxysuccinimidyl ester of methoxypolyethylene glycol propionic acid resulted in a two-fold extension of the half-life in murine models [242], and comb-shaped polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymers, such as AKM1510 and APM2090, extended the half-life to 56 h [242], although at the expense of the activity of some enzymes [242–244]. Furthermore, erythrocyte encapsulation (Erymet) significantly enhanced the pharmacokinetics, prolonging L-methionine depletion for several days and demonstrating synergistic effects with PD-1 inhibitors in breast cancer models [245]. However, maintaining prolonged systemic depletion has raised concerns over potential toxicity [245]. O-rMETase was shown to significantly increase, effectively localizing L-methionine restriction within the gastrointestinal tract to minimize systemic exposure while simultaneously improving efficacy in patient-derived orthotopic xenograft models related to melanoma, pancreatic cancer, and chemotherapy-resistant tumors [219, 226, 231, 246–254]. Site-directed mutagenesis techniques further refined substrate specificity, with the C115H and V358Y mutants exhibiting enhanced anticancer effects [240, 255, 256]. Additionally, a humanized version of METase (hCGL-4.0) was able to maintain prolonged L-methionine depletion without toxicity in prostate cancer models [257]. Innovative strategies incorporating octameric METase-S3 fusion proteins have aimed to target epidermal growth factor receptor-expressing tumors, resulting in improved stability [258]. Despite these advancements, challenges remain: PEG-rMETase requires repeated administration and stabilization of the pyridoxal phosphate cofactor [242], while erythrocyte-loaded METase presents risks of chronic toxicity [245], and the clinical evaluation of oral and humanized METase formulations is still in progress [246–251, 257]. Future research will concentrate on engineering METase variants to enhance stability and lower immunogenicity and develop combinatorial treatment regimens that exploit the synergistic potential of METase with chemotherapy and immunotherapy [231, 232].

Targeting methionine transporters

Targeting methionine transport at the level of SLC43A2/SLC7A5 expression or LAT4/LAT1 function may reduce methionine flux and impair tumor cell fitness in selected preclinical settings. In colorectal cancer, SLC43A2 inhibition restored methionine availability in CD8+ T cells, rescued H3K79me2-dependent STAT5 expression, and revitalized antitumor immunity [15]. Similarly, LAT1 knockdown in HCC models via CRISPR/Cas9 nanoplatforms reduces tumor methionine consumption while activating the cGAS/STING pathway, enhancing immunotherapy efficacy [14]. Pharmacological inhibitors such as BCH (targeting SLC43A2) and norathyriol (blocking circPETH-147aa in HCC) have demonstrated preclinical success in sensitizing tumors to chemotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade [159, 160].

Dietary MR synergizes with transporter inhibition to amplify therapeutic effects. In p53-deficient tumors, MR exacerbates genomic instability by depleting SAM, a methyl donor regulated by SLC43A2 [12]. Conversely, LAT1-driven methionine addiction in UASCC is vulnerable to dietary methionine limitation, which suppresses EZH2 activity and tumor growth [161]. Nanotechnology offers targeted delivery: AS-F-NP, a nanoregulator that delivers anti-SLC43A2 siRNA, restores immune cell function by blocking tumor methionine hoarding, while methionine-modified polymers enable LAT1-targeted drug delivery with minimal off-target effects [18, 274].

Despite progress, key challenges remain. LAT1 and SLC43A2 inhibitors face specificity issues, as methionine transporters also support normal cell functions. For instance, LAT4 has been implicated in methionine competition in the tumor microenvironment, although selective inhibitors remain limited [274]. Additionally, feedback mechanisms, such as the LAT1-EZH2 loop in lung cancer, in which EZH2 repression upregulates Retinoid X receptor alpha to suppress LAT1, highlight the complexity of targeting these pathways [275]. Future strategies may combine transporter inhibition with epigenetic modulators or immunotherapy to address resistance mechanisms.

Small-molecule inhibitors targeting methionine metabolism enzymes

Given the therapeutic focus on metabolic vulnerabilities, small-molecule inhibitors targeting methionine metabolism enzymes have emerged as promising precision-oncology strategies. The best-developed example is the inhibition of MAT2A in cancers with homozygous MTAP deletion, a genomic event present in approximately 15% of human cancers [53, 129]. MTAP loss leads to the accumulation of MTA, which partially suppresses PRMT5 activity and leads to selective vulnerability to further reduce SAM production through MAT2A inhibition [55, 276]. In this context, pharmacologic MAT2A blockade decreases intracellular SAM levels, further impairing PRMT5-dependent methylation and RNA splicing programs, and can induce DNA damage in MTAP-deleted tumor cells [42, 43].

AG-270/S095033 was the first MAT2A inhibitor to enter clinical development. In the first-in-human phase I study, AG-270/S095033 produced clear pharmacodynamic effects, including marked reductions in plasma SAM levels and decreases in tumor symmetric dimethylarginine levels, with a manageable safety profile. However, clinical activity as monotherapy was limited, with 2 partial responses and 5 additional cases of confirmed stable disease lasting at least 16 weeks, indicating proof of mechanism but only modest single-agent efficacy [129]. The corresponding ClinicalTrials.gov records currently list this study as terminated for strategic reasons [NCT03435250].

IDE397, an allosteric MAT2A inhibitor, remains in active phase I clinical development for MTAP-deleted solid tumors, both as monotherapy and in combination with docetaxel, paclitaxel, or sacituzumab govitecan[NCT04794699]. Publicly disclosed interim data have suggested early antitumor activity in selected MTAP-deleted cohorts, particularly those with urothelial cancer and squamous non-small cell lung cancer, but these findings should still be viewed as preliminary pending full peer-reviewed clinical reporting.

Overall, the current landscape suggests that MAT2A inhibition has a strong mechanistic effect on MTAP-deleted cancers but also indicates that combination strategies are likely to be required to maximize clinical benefit. Accordingly, ongoing development is increasingly focused on rational combinations with taxanes, PRMT5-directed approaches, and other agents selected according to the tumor lineage and codependency context [129] [NCT04794699].

Combination therapies involving the targeting of methionine

Tumor cells frequently exhibit methionine auxotrophy, a metabolic liability arising from impaired remethylation of homocysteine into methionine. This dependency renders them particularly susceptible to MR. At the core of MR’s anticancer mechanism is its disruption of one-carbon metabolism, which is essential for nucleotide synthesis and methylation. In CRC, MR enhances the efficacy of chemotherapeutics such as 5-FU by depleting 5,10-methylene-tetrahydrofolate, which in turn inhibits thymidylate synthase activity and halts DNA replication [277]. Similarly, in gliomas, rMETase synergizes with temozolomide, especially in O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT)-deficient tumors, without triggering resistance-associated MGMT upregulation [207].

In addition to DNA synthesis, MR amplifies chemotherapy across different cancers by weakening tumor resilience. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), MR-induced glutathione depletion increases ROS levels, sensitizing cells to the thioredoxin reductase inhibitor auranofin through oxidative stress-mediated pathways [195]. Furthermore, MR disrupts SAM-dependent methylation, sensitizing cancer stem cell populations to MAT2A inhibitors such as cycloleucine, thus targeting both bulk tumors and metastatic precursors [168].

Crucially, MR also acts as a radiosensitizer. In melanoma models, both methionine-deficient and methionine-restricted diets improved responses to stereotactic body radiotherapy, were mediated by MAT2A downregulation and impaired one-carbon flux [199]. These effects were achieved with minimal damage to surrounding healthy tissue, suggesting a broad therapeutic window.

Beyond direct cytotoxic enhancement, MR profoundly modulates the tumor immune microenvironment. In microsatellite-stable CRC-a subtypes that are historically resistant to immunotherapy, MR suppresses proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) via SAM-DNMT1-mediated methylation and sirtuin 6 inhibition. This promotes CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and reverses responsiveness to PD-1 blockade [185]. When combined with PCSK9 inhibitors and 5-FU, this strategy elicits even stronger antitumor effects, suggesting a convergence between metabolic and immune vulnerabilities.

Nevertheless, MR’s impact on immunity is nuanced. While it may enhance cytotoxic T-cell responses through STING pathway activation [184], unintended suppression of Th1 polarization and H₂S production can counteract these gains. Adjunctive agents such as 2-bromopalmitate or H₂S donors may help preserve immune balance and optimize therapeutic synergy [206, 208].

MR also sensitizes tumors to apoptosis-inducing agents. In TNBC, MR upregulates TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand receptor 2 (TRAIL-R2) expression by repressing melanoma-associated antigen family D member 2, increasing susceptibility to TRAIL-R2 agonists such as lexatumumab. This leads to robust caspase activation and enhanced apoptosis [203].

To personalize these combination therapies, biomarker-guided approaches are gaining traction. In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, MR downregulates pluripotency-linked methionine cycle genes, decreasing tumor stemness. A risk stratification model revealed high-risk patients who benefit substantially from MR in conjunction with adjuvant transcatheter arterial chemoembolization, demonstrating the feasibility of precision metabolic therapy [278].

In conclusion, MR represents a versatile and mechanistically rich platform for sensitizing tumors to a spectrum of oncologic therapies. By targeting core metabolic, redox, immunologic, and epigenetic processes, MR amplifies therapeutic efficacy while preserving normal tissue integrity. Future research integrating MR with dynamic biomarker profiling and multiomics stratification will be critical to elucidate its full potential in precision oncology.

Conclusions

The compelling evidence that cancer cells exhibit heightened dependence on methionine metabolism has inspired the development of therapeutic strategies targeting this metabolic vulnerability. Among these, dietary MR has attracted considerable attention because of its low toxicity profile and the broad health-promoting effects observed in preclinical models. MR has demonstrated efficacy in inhibiting tumor growth, both as monotherapy and in combination with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapies. Nevertheless, several key challenges must be addressed to advance these strategies toward clinical implementation.

One of the central issues is the incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying tumor methionine addiction. While MR and the restriction of other one-carbon donors, such as serine and glycine, have antitumor effects, the specific pathways that mediate these responses remain incompletely defined. Methionine plays a central role in the folate and methionine cycles, influencing methylation dynamics, polyamine biosynthesis, and redox homeostasis. Dissecting the context-specific contributions of these pathways across diverse tumor types and genetic backgrounds is essential for refining methionine-targeted therapies.

In addition, tumor adaptability presents a significant obstacle. Cancer cells often activate compensatory metabolic circuits under methionine-limiting conditions, including upregulation of salvage pathways, alterations in the trans-sulfuration and folate cycles, and epigenetic modifications that sustain growth. These adaptive mechanisms suggest that combination therapies will be necessary to simultaneously disrupt multiple metabolic nodes.

Tumor heterogeneity further complicates therapeutic efficacy, as the extent of methionine dependence varies not only across cancer types but also between subclones within individual tumors. This variability complicates patient selection and limits the development of standardized treatment protocols. Furthermore, the absence of reliable biomarkers for predicting methionine dependence or monitoring treatment response remains a major limitation. Advances in multiomics approaches, such as metabolomics, transcriptomics, and plasma metabolite profiling, may facilitate the identification of robust predictive markers and improve patient stratification.

Another critical barrier is the context-dependent nature of MR’s impact on antitumor immunity. Emerging evidence reveals a dichotomous role: in immunocompetent settings, methionine competition between tumor cells and T cells can impair T-cell proliferation and function, potentially undermining antitumor immunity. Conversely, MR has been shown to sensitize tumors to immune checkpoint inhibitors by reversing immunosuppressive methylation patterns (e.g., m6A modifications of PD-L1 and VISTA) and activating pathways such as cGAS-STING [1, 114]. These findings underscore the importance of considering tumor stage and immune competence when MR-based interventions are designed.

Despite promising preclinical data, clinical translation remains limited. Methionine-depleting agents, including methioninase-based therapies, often suffer from poor pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, and systemic toxicity. Moreover, dietary MR poses nutritional challenges, particularly in vulnerable populations such as children, elderly individuals, and malnourished individuals. While short-term MR appears tolerable, long-term safety remains unclear. Precision-designed MR diets that maintain nutritional adequacy and patient adherence are urgently needed and require collaborative efforts among oncologists, dietitians, immunologists, and metabolic researchers.

The integration of methionine-targeted approaches with existing therapies holds significant potential. However, there is a lack of clinical trials evaluating MR in combination with immunotherapy, despite preclinical indications of synergy. Stratified study designs that account for tumor type, immune status, and disease stage are essential. For example, hepatocellular carcinoma progression is associated with decreased MATI/III: MATII activity, increased SAM utilization for polyamine synthesis, and DNA hypomethylation–metabolic shifts that may inform biomarker-guided treatment.

Future research should focus on developing tumor-selective methionine-targeting agents, improving delivery methods (e.g., nanocarriers and engineered probiotics), and enhancing treatment personalization through metabolic imaging and diagnostic methods such as methionine PET. A multidisciplinary approach will be critical to overcoming the current limitations and advancing methionine-targeted strategies in the clinical oncology landscape.

In conclusion, while methionine dependence represents a promising metabolic vulnerability in cancer, its clinical translation necessitates deeper mechanistic insights, careful patient selection, and the development of integrated therapeutic strategies. Ongoing research and well-designed clinical trials will be pivotal to unlocking the full therapeutic potential of targeting methionine metabolism.
