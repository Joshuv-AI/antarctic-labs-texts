Cytogenetic bands and sharp peaks of Alu underlie large-scale segmental regulation of nuclear genome architecture
Authors: Lisa L Hall, Kevin M Creamer, Meg Byron, Jeanne B Lawrence
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCytogenetic bands reflect genomic organization in large blocks of DNA with similar properties. Because banding patterns are invariant, this organization may often be assumed unimportant for genome regulation. Results here challenge that view. Findings here suggest cytogenetic bands reflect a visible framework upon which regulated genome architecture is built. Given Alu and L1 densities differ in cytogenetic bands, we examined their distribution after X-chromosome inactivation or formation of senescent-associated heterochromatin foci (SAHFs). Alu-rich regions remain outside both SAHFs and the Barr Body (BB), affirming that the BB is not the whole chromosome but a condensed, L1-rich core. Hi-C analysis of senescent cells demonstrates large (~10 Mb) G-bands remodel as a contiguous unit, gaining distal intrachromosomal interactions as syntenic G-bands coalesce into SAHFs. Striking peaks of Alu within R-bands strongly resist condensation. Thus, large-scale segmental genome architectur relates to dark versus light cytogenetic bands and Alu-peaks, implicating both in chromatin regulation.KEYWORDS: Repeats, LINE1, Alu, X-inactivation, senescence, SAHFs, Barr body, nuclear structure, chromosome bands, heterochromatin

Introduction

All cells in the body contain the same DNA yet produce a staggering number of different cell types. This is accomplished by cell-type specific gene expression, controlled through changes in chromatin biochemistry and nuclear structure, resulting in the coordinated activation or silencing of specific gene sets. Much has been learned about the molecular changes involved in the regulation of individual genes (e.g. transcription factor binding, nucleosome modifications, etc.), but much less is understood about how the remarkable cell-type specific coordination of ~20,000 genes is achieved. As will be a theme throughout this study, epigenetic regulation of gene expression can occur at different levels, the molecular scale regulation of specific genes, and a much larger scale, where the nuclear genome organizes in two cytologically distinct chromatin regions; more decondensed euchromatin, within which active genes can express, and condensed heterochromatin, which is primarily silent. The general patterns in the spatial organization of the heterochromatin versus euchromatin compartments is cell-type specific, despite some small variations between cells of the same type. As will be discussed below, the large euchromatin compartment is also typically further sub-compartmentalized, including around ~20 nuclear bodies that support active gene expression called nuclear speckles.

The linear human genome displays poorly understood large-scale differences in sequence organization, evident cytologically as cytogenetic bands on mitotic chromosomes. These large blocks of DNA (typically 5–10 Mb or more) stain differently with certain dyes, most commonly Giemsa-trypsin staining to visualize the ‘G-dark’ and ‘G-light’ band DNA [1–4]. G-light bands are also often referred to as ‘R-bands’ (Reverse bands). The number of bands visible will depend on the contraction state of the chromosomes, but high-resolution cytogenetic banding can delineate ~800 alternating light and dark G-bands. Each chromosome has a characteristic pattern of these alternating dark and lighter stained bands that is invariant between people and cell-types; this consistency has enabled cytogeneticists to use banding patterns to identify specific chromosomes and abnormalities by microscopy.

It has long been known that genes have a clearly nonrandom distribution relative to cytogenetic bands, which also differ in GC/AT content, replication timing and repeat sequence composition. Over half the human genome is made up of highly repetitive interspersed DNA sequences, many derived as degenerate forms of previously mobile parasitic elements called SINEs (Short Interspersed Nuclear Elements), or LINEs (Long Interspersed Nuclear Elements). Alu is the most abundant SINE (~10% of genome), while LINE1 (L1) is the most abundant LINE element (~17%) [5]. G-dark bands are generally gene-poor but contain more cell type specific genes, replicate later, are more AT rich and are known to be enriched for LINE elements. Conversely, lightly staining G-light bands (R-bands), are early replicating, GC, CpG and gene rich, and have higher density of SINE elements (reviewed in: [6,7]). Individual bands may also contain smaller segments corresponding to distinct GC or AT isochores [8]. Interestingly, although the primary sequence of most interspersed repeats is not conserved across species, their abundance and distribution in chromosome bands and large ‘synteny blocks’ is [9], suggesting a possible functional significance.

While chromosome bands have been visualized for clinical purposes for about 60 years, the invariant nature of the cytogenetic banding pattern may have been interpreted by many to indicate that this high-level structural feature of the human genome is not important for understanding how changes in genome architecture relates to gene regulation. Instead, modern conformation capture analysis uses DNA cross-linking in cells and then extraction of cross-linked DNA to determine spatially proximal DNA, evident on Hi-C interaction maps [10]. This reveals smaller-scale structural differences in genome organization, evidenced as TADs (topologically associated domains), which are organized within larger clusters in 3D nuclear space, termed A (active) or B (inactive) compartments. Since A & B compartments are generally smaller than metaphase chromosome bands and may show differences between cell-types, there has been little attention to addressing how these levels of organization may relate to the larger chromosome bands.

Although the functional significance of chromosome banding and its associated repeats is currently unknown, we have long suspected that these blocks of DNA with similar genomic properties underlies large-scale nuclear compartmentalization. This organization exists even before chromatin packaging within nuclear structure forms, and we suggest that this very large-scale preexisting sequence organization could be key to this process and to coordinated gene regulation. We hypothesize that the clustering of coding genes into R-bands, and the tandem organization of other gene types, facilitates formation of nuclear ‘hubs’ with nuclear bodies containing RNA metabolic factors. Hence, we termed this the ‘karyotype to hub hypothesis’ [11]. Supporting this, we earlier examined the organization of six different chromosome bands and showed that light R-band DNA decondensed and encircled/contacted SC-35 rich nuclear speckles, whereas G-dark band DNA localized in the peripheral heterochromatic ‘closet’ [12]. This pattern of organization whereby many active genes associate with nuclear speckles has also been affirmed on a genome-wide scale [13]. The mechanisms responsible for this cytological-scale spatial organization of active and inactive chromatin remain largely unknown, although the link between the nuclear lamina and lamina-associated domains (LADs) in heterochromatin have been well studied. However, the role of repeats and the different sequence properties of chromosome bands remains under-studied and, in our view, often overlooked.

Motivated by an interest in whether there is a link between cytogenetic bands and changes in genome regulation, we investigate two model systems that provide unique synoptic views of how L1-rich or Alu-rich DNA, and cytogenetic bands, may rearrange during large-scale changes in chromatin packaging across either a whole chromosome (X-inactivation), or the whole nucleus. (cell senescence). X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) in females is initiated in early embryogenesis by XIST RNA [14,15]. The lncRNA ‘paints’ its parent chromosome [16], and initiates chromosome-wide epigenetic silencing and large-scale structural changes, manifest by the cytological appearance of the condensed Barr body (BB), which typically localizes to the peripheral or nucleolar heterochromatin compartment (reviewed in [17]). Senescent cells also exhibit dramatic rearrangements of euchromatin and heterochromatin across the entire interphase nucleus. When primary somatic cells enter replicative senescence, they irreversibly exit the cell cycle. While the cell nucleus enlarges, most senescent cell types form numerous senescence-associated heterochromatic foci (SAHFs), manifest as condensed DAPI bright foci similar in size to the Barr body of the Xi [18–20].

Results

Relative distributions of LINE1 and Alu repeats on human chromosomes and relationship to cytogenetic bands

We begin by showing the overall distribution of L1 and Alu DNA sequences as detected by two-color FISH on human metaphase chromosomes. L1 and Alu repeats are known to be widely distributed throughout the genome, but with regions of differential enrichment that have been correlated to dark and light cytogenetic G-bands [21]. Figure 1a shows hybridization with probes to Alu (green) and L1 (red), with the overlay between the two colors highlighting differences in their relative densities across each chromosome. At this level of resolution, differences in their enrichment are apparent in very large blocks of DNA (at least several Mb). Although denaturation and hybridization are known to weaken chromosome banding patterns, the rough correlation to the higher-resolution Giemsa banding pattern (Figure 1ai) is still apparent in many regions. For example, the top (short arm) of Chr1 contains a large region of light band DNA, above a region of primarily dark-band DNA. DNA hybridization shows markedly more Alu than L1 in the light band region and more L1 than Alu in the region below that. Some chromosomes with little L1/Alu banding also have less pronounced G-banding pattern (e.g. Chr4), while on others the differential enrichment in bands is striking (e.g. Chr11).

Two-color overlays can sometimes create the appearance that one repeat type is low where the other is high, or is high where the other is low. For example, regions high in Alu may appear lower in L1, but this is not necessarily the case since density of different repeats in a region is not mutually exclusive. Hence it is also important to view the single-color images (at right, enhanced for contrast) to assess the distribution of a given repeat in a chromosomal region (or nuclear region). While at this high-level view, relative enrichments of L1 and Alu show some correspondence to dark and light Giemsa bands, this relationship is quite approximate, as further discussed below. Nonetheless, understanding this difference in global organization on the linear chromosome sequence provides perspective for results below examining L1 and Alu-rich regions in nuclei, or on the inactive X- (Xi) or other chromosomes within nuclei.

Repeat distribution in fibroblast nuclei suggests peripheral heterochromatin is marked more by Alu depletion than L1 enrichment

The overall nuclear distribution of these repeats was examined in primary human fibroblast cultures, to eliminate cancer related changes. L1-rich DNA is reported to be enriched at the nuclear periphery, related to its density of lamina-associated domains (LADs), with some published images indicating much or most L1 DNA is at the nuclear periphery with little L1 in the rest of the nucleus (e.g [7,22]. However, the extent to which this is seen will vary with cell-type, species (we find it more apparent in mouse) and with digital imaging and processing procedures. Figure 1b appears to show enrichment of L1 DNA at the periphery of a fibroblast nucleus, and less internally. However, not all fibroblast nuclei have a clear delineated L1 compartment (e.g. Figure 2e-f), and even in cells with a well-defined compartment (Figure 1b-c), when viewing the distribution of L1 alone (Figure 1d), its peripheral enrichment is not striking relative to the broader nuclear distribution. Importantly, it appears that the pattern of peripheral L1-enrichment is determined more by the exclusion of Alu DNA than by increased L1. While L1 looks highly enriched at the periphery in three-color images (Figure 1b-c), the signal for Alu DNA is largely excluded from that peripheral region (Figure 1e), with minimal photographic manipulation (see methods). This was observed in these normal primary cells but was also apparent to a lesser extent in other cell lines examined. The depletion of Alu at the nuclear periphery would not be inconsistent with the association of L1 sequences with LADs, but does introduce a conceptually distinct possibility: that lack of Alu, rather than enrichment of L1 sequences, may be a hallmark of the peripheral heterochromatin compartment in normal human fibroblasts and potentially other cells.

These are the first results in this study that suggest that Alu-dense regions exhibit a more specific relationship with nuclear compartments than perhaps L1 DNA does. Related to that, we find that within the interior euchromatic regions, brightly staining rings of Alu DNA often surround areas depleted of DNA (Figure 1e arrows & Figure 1f-g). These ‘holes’ in the DAPI DNA stain contain nuclear speckles (SC35 domains) (Figure 1h), which are non-chromatin, membraneless structures highly enriched for poly-A RNA and pre-mRNA splicing factors [23,24], which distribute in an interior central plane within fibroblast nuclei [25]. We and others have shown that many active genes, including very highly expressed genes, specifically localize to and are transcribed at the outer edge of an individual speckle, and various post-transcriptional processes can occur within each of these ‘factories’ (e.g [13]; [26] and reviewed in [27]. We also previously suggested that the preferential localization of protein-coding genes in light R-band DNA may facilitate this clustering of genes with nuclear hubs, which expedites their expression [11], and showed that R-band DNA associated with nuclear speckles, whereas dark G-bands did not [12]. Supporting this hypothesis more broadly, here we show that Alu-rich DNA across the genome, which may be considered a loose proxy for gene-rich R-band DNA, is preferentially associated with nuclear speckles in the interior of the nucleus (Figure 1h) and may explain the exclusion of Alu-rich DNA from the nuclear periphery.

It is important for concepts studied here to have perspective of scale. For example, this organization of L1 or Alu-rich DNA in heterochromatic versus euchromatic compartments exists at a much larger (cytological) scale than much smaller TADs or individual A or B compartments as described by Hi-C studies. A difference in scale is further illustrated by brief examination of human L1 and Alu distribution within the single human chromosome in mouse/human hybrid cells, carrying an active X-chromosome (Figure 1i-k). Since human L1 and Alu probes do not detect mouse LINEs and SINEs, analysis of hybrid cells allows visualization of repeats on the single human chromosome. This shows that in most cells (~80%) there are discrete clusters of L1 versus Alu-rich DNA intermingled within the territory (Figure 1j), however in some cells in which the chromosome abuts the nuclear envelope (~20%), the L1 and Alu-rich clusters congregate in two distinct regions, with the Alu more internal and excluded from the L1-rich compartment associated with the nuclear envelope (n = 55-100 in three experiments)(Figure 1k). The individual clusters that intermingle within the territory suggest one level of organization, possibly related to homotypic clustering suggested from Hi-C analysis in other cells [22]. In contrast, ‘clustering of clusters’ would suggest larger-scale organization of chromosomal DNA that results in differential enrichment of repeat types within the territory, as will be better explored below.

Thus, in normal differentiated human fibroblasts we confirm findings that there is nonrandom distribution of repeat types in heterochromatic and euchromatic compartments. However, our results indicate that peripheral heterochromatin is delineated more by depletion of Alu-rich DNA than increased density of L1. Moreover, the preferential distribution of Alu-rich DNA in the interior is associated with and may be driven by the interaction of Alu-rich, gene-rich chromosomal segments (bands) with nuclear speckles.

To further investigate the large-scale organization of Alu-rich versus L1-rich DNA, we examined this in two natural systems in which whole chromosomes undergo dramatic and large-scale changes in architecture and regulation.

L1-rich DNA coalesces into the condensed Barr Body but Alu-rich, genic DNA remains largely outside this dense chromosome core

When the lncRNA XIST ‘paints’ and silences the X-chromosome in female cells, it forms a large heterochromatic structure called the Barr body (BB), that is visible in most human cells by DNA stains like DAPI [28] (e.g. Figure 2a arrow). This DAPI-dense BB is often presumed to comprise the entire X-chromosome, which has been thought to be formed by condensation of silenced genes across the chromosome. If the entire chromosome is compacted into this dense structure, one would assume the density of both L1 and Alu would be equally enriched in the large BB. We had previously reported that hybridization with a probe to CoT-1 DNA (the highly repetitive fraction of the genome) brightly marks the BB [29], which is devoid of CoT-1 RNA (Figure 2b). This enrichment of the CoT-1 DNA in the BB of female cells suggests high-copy repeats are abundant in this structure, and, as expected, we find the human BB labels brightly with L1 probes (L1 enrichment in 94% of BBs: stdev 5)(Figure 2c-d). The labeling intensity of L1 DNA over the BB is very striking, making the BB stand out as generally the biggest and brightest L1-rich domain in the nucleoplasm. This suggests, the BB contains a significantly higher L1 DNA density (or organization) than any other DNA compartment in the nucleus, including the L1 ‘enrichment’ in the peripheral heterochromatic compartment.

Although L1 specific probes have often been used as a proxy for CoT-1 DNA to label the BB in the past (e.g [30], to our knowledge whether Alu-rich DNA is comparably condensed into the BB has not been examined. Hence, it’s possible that not all high-copy repeats, and chromosomal regions, participate equally in BB formation. Using DNA FISH, we examine whether Alu-labeled DNA is enriched within the repeat-rich BB, along with L1 DNA. Remarkably, we find that Alu DNA was not enriched in the BB in 100% of nuclei scored (e.g. Supp Figure S1a), and very often there was a region from which the Alu signal was depleted, appearing largely excluded (79%: Stdev 16) as a ‘hole’ in the Alu DNA signal over the BB (Figure 2e-f: arrows). The difference in L1 and Alu signals was not due to difficulty in denaturing the condensed BB, as the L1 DNA clearly hybridized, nor was it due to differential probe penetration as both probes were nick-translated into similar fragment size. Instead, results suggest that the chromosome condensation triggered by XIST RNA, nucleates an L1-rich region substantially depleted of Alu sequences. This is similar to the Alu depletion we observed in the nuclear periphery (Figure 1b-e) but occurs irrespective of whether the BB is associated with the nuclear envelope or not (Figure 2f), suggesting peripheral lamina association (through LAD binding) is not required for this large-scale repeat organization.

In addition, because Alu sequences are generally enriched in gene-rich R-bands, this also supports our previous findings that genes are located outside the BB structure [29]. Clemson et al. reported the surprising finding that 14 individual genes, regardless of activity (silenced or escaped), did not distribute within the DAPI-dense BB structure. In fact, all coding genes examined were primarily localized at the outer periphery of the BB (after effects of differential hybridization efficiency or 3-D organization were ruled out). Here we briefly corroborate key findings of this study by independent examination of several genes. Figure 3a-g shows representative localizations for five different genes (see Figure legend for activity status of genes). Furthermore, we used a pool of these genes (4 active & 1 inactive) to show that the collection of genes encircles the very outer edge of the XIST RNA cloud associated with the dense BB (Figure 3e-g). Related to this, an earlier publication by Clemson et al., also showed that the XIST RNA territory (and the Xi DNA paint) appeared slightly larger than the condensed BB seen by DAPI DNA-stain [16] (reviewed in [31]. Thus, during the silencing process, XIST RNA reorganizes the inactive chromosome territory such that the center core of the territory is composed of a highly condensed ball of L1-rich DNA (the Barr body) surrounded by mostly silent and some active genes that are within the more Alu-rich region (Figure 3i).

Interestingly, we find this large-scale reorganization of L1 and Alu-rich regions is also seen in the BBs of an autosome (Chr4) (Figure 3h) previously shown to be silenced by transgenic XIST RNA ([32], and this autosome also exhibited the same pattern of genes located outside the BB [29]. In addition, this separation of two distinct Alu and L1 domains is maintained in mouse/human hybrid cells carrying an inactive human X-chromosome (Xi) (Supp Figure 1b & c), in contrast to the intermingled smaller clusters of L1 and Alu seen for the active X chromosome (Supp Figure 1b & d-e).

Collectively, results suggest this pattern of L1/Alu reorganization likely reflects a universal structure generated by XIST RNA. But how might these findings relate to the organization of L1 versus Alu-rich regions across linear DNA of the mitotic chromosomes? Given the size and limited number of the L1 versus Alu-rich clusters seen on the active X chromosome territory in hybrid cells (e.g. Suppl Figure S1D-E), we suggest that each of these prominent ‘homotypic’ clusters likely reflect different genomic enrichments in large chromosome regions (~5-15 Mb), which loosely correspond to the cytogenetic G-banding pattern. Figure 1i provides a magnified view of two-color Alu/L1 distribution across the human X-chromosome, also shown in Figure 1a. Most of the X-chromosome is L1 rich, but there are still clearly distinct differences in relative Alu/L1 across large regions. Figure 3j illustrates our model for how band-sized DNA blocks with high L1 and low Alu congregate to form the condensed Barr body, with Alu and gene-rich DNA outside it. This concept will be further developed and supported as we examine the formation of large Barr body like structures in senescent cells, below.

Because the Xi appears to form two distinct domains with differential enrichment of repeats and genes, we briefly comment on two reported observations that suggest XIST RNA may have differential affinities for these domains. First, previous reports suggested that the Xi is segregated into two domains differentially marked by H3K27me3 and H3K9me3, and that XIST RNA binds only the H3K27me3 labeled Xi-chromatin and avoids H3K9me3 labeled chromatin [33,34], (reviewed in [35]. As further detailed in the Supplement, we confirm what is seen in most published images, that XIST RNA paints both H3K9me3 labeled chromatin (Supp Figure S2a-b & f-g), as well as H3K27me3 (Supp Figure S2d & f-g), and that it clearly paints the L1-rich BB (Supp Figure S2c). However, we found that choice of XIST probe can compromise antibody labeling, producing images that appear to show XIST RNA failing to bind the H3K9me3 labeled (L1-rich BB) domain of the Xi (Supp Figure S2e). However, this is due to a technical artifact and does not represent its native localization (See Supplement for details).

We also previously showed that as Xist RNA starts to fall off mitotic chromosomes in mouse cells, it can be seen painting the entire chromosome in very early prophase, ([36] (e.g. Supp Figure S2h), which further supports that it paints the entire Xi (as above). However, we also found the RNA is retained slightly longer on R-bands, as it detaches later in mitosis (e.g. Supp Figure S2i), which are Alu-rich. Additionally, we previously published that when XIST transcription is inhibited, XIST RNA is retained longest on the DNA around the edge of the BB [16] (e.g. Supp Figure S2j-m), where we now show Alu-rich DNA resides. Thus, although we see XIST RNA normally binds all silenced chromatin on the Xi, under certain conditions the RNA may show differences in binding affinity or release from gene-poor/L1-rich/H9k9me3 labeled DNA versus gene-rich/Alu-rich/H3K27me3 labeled DNA.

In sum, key points shown in this section are that L1-rich DNA is highly enriched in the BB, whereas more Alu-rich DNA is significantly excluded. These results demonstrate that the large, condensed BB structure is not the whole chromosome, and that gene & Alu-rich DNA are excluded from it. This large-scale two-domain structure built by XIST RNA supports previous findings that most genes, regardless of activation state, are located just outside the dense BB.

Similar to the BB heterochromatin foci formed during senescence represent a condensed L1-rich core whereas Alu-rich regions resist condensation

We next examined the respective distributions of Alu and L1 DNA in relation to the dramatic changes in nuclear heterochromatin organization that occurs genome-wide in cell senescence. Although the nuclei of senescent cells enlarge about two-fold and their centromeric heterochromatin markedly decondenses [20], in most cell types senescence is characterized by formation of numerous large DAPI dense structures called Senescence Associated Heterochromatin Foci (SAHFs)(Figure 4a-c). As previously reported, SAHFs lack repeat-rich (CoT-1) heteronuclear RNA (pre-mRNAs and lncRNAs) which brightly labels the decondensed, euchromatic nucleoplasm [37]. Given the similarities in size and transcriptional inactivity of the BB and SAHFs (Figure 4d-e), we investigated whether each SAHF may represent the inner ‘core’ of a single chromosome territory, analogous to the Barr Body (BB). We analyzed this using chromosome ‘paints’ for individual chromosomes. This was examined in primary human fibroblasts which had undergone replicative senescence (P44), with ~ 50 or more cells examined for each. Representative images are shown to illustrate the consistent observation that in most cells a single SAHF occupied the center of a single chromosome territory, even for very large SAHFs (e.g. Figure 4f & Supp Figure S3), consistent with a suggestion from two earlier studies [38,39]. Importantly, we show that the SAHF does not encompass the entire chromosome territory; since SAHFs are surrounded by especially decondensed DNA in the enlarged nucleus, some decondensed territory-DNA can be seen outside the condensed structure (Figure 4f: green outline, and Supp Figure S3), similar to the BB.

In senescent cells there is loss of Lamin B1, concomitant with rearrangement of peripheral heterochromatin to form SAHFs, which is linked to release of LAD-rich DNA from the periphery [19] and reviewed in [40]. However, we also note that in non-senescent cells Lamins are not only concentrated at the periphery but are also present across the nucleoplasm. Interestingly, in cycling cells with normal Lamin levels we noted diminished nucleoplasmic Lamin B1 staining associated with the BB (Figure 4g and Supp Figure S4), suggesting loss of Lamin B1 from LAD-rich DNA is another parallel between the formation of these two chromosomal ‘core’ structures.

These similarities between BBs and SAHFs suggest that SAHFs might also exhibit similar L1/Alu compartmentalization to the BB. Using L1 and Alu DNA FISH on SAHF-containing senescent fibroblasts, we find the same L1-enrichment and Alu-depletion over DAPI-bright SAHFs; the consistency of this pattern is evident for SAHFs throughout a nucleus (Figure 4h). These results again suggest that there is congregation of smaller segments that have high L1 and low Alu content to form a more central core, with Alu-rich regions resisting condensation and thus segregating outside the core. (Hence, this is not exclusive to XIST-mediated chromosome silencing.) While the L1-rich peripheral heterochromatic compartment has been rearranged in senescent cells, the pattern continues, with regions high in Alu remaining depleted from L1-rich heterochromatin. Instead, Alu-rich DNA remains decondensed in these large senescent nuclei and is often closely associated with non-chromatin speckles, enriched for RNA metabolic factors (Figure 4i, arrows & j).

The human X-chromosome (except the region that escapes silencing) is highly L1-rich, (reviewed in [41], and SAHFs and BBs are formed from DNA rich in L1 but not Alu, this raised the question of whether chromosomes with higher relative L1 density might have enhanced SAHF (or BB) formation. Therefore, we considered the overall content of L1 versus Alu for all human chromosomes, as graphed in Figure 5a. Chromosome 4 (Chr-4) and Chromosome 19 (Chr-19) are standout autosomes with the highest L1 and Alu concentrations respectively, as also evident by DNA FISH (Inserts: Figure 5a), hence comparison of those two chromosomes for SAHF formation was of interest. Related to this, we note that our human cell line carrying an XIST transgene on L1-rich Chr-4 (G3/HT1080) [32] produces a particularly large, bright and round BB (Figure 5b). SAHF formation for Chr-4 and Chr-19 in senescent fibroblasts was determined by size and intensity of DAPI stain and approximate percent of chromosome territory occupied by the SAHF, using chromosome libraries to delineate each territory. (We note that library probes may not detect the more decondensed edges of the territory, so full territory size may be underestimated to some degree.)

This analysis showed interesting differences in SAHF formation between different chromosomes. Results show that 100% of Chr-4 territories make a large SAHF in senescent nuclei, usually one of the most prominent SAHFs (Figure 5c: arrows, 5f and Supp Fig S3a-b). In fact, in 70% of cells, the biggest or brightest SAHFs were formed by the two Chr-4 homologs, second only to the Xi. In addition, the Chr-4 SAHF encompassed the majority of the Chr-4 paint territory (Figure 5c: outline & 5g). Chr-19, on the other hand, localizes in DAPI-dim decondensed nuclear regions in growing cells (Figure 5d: outline), and in senescent cells only about 50% of the Chr-19 territories contained even a tiny DAPI dense region that could represent a small SAHF (Figure 5e: outline). The tiny DAPI-regions of Chr-19 were very dim and occupied only about 20% of the chromosome territory (Figure 5g). Thus, there are marked differences in the frequency and extent of SAHF formation, indicating that the L1 and Alu density of different chromosomes is associated with and may play a role in SAHF/BB formation.

We conclude that DAPI-dense SAHFs represent the condensed core of individual chromosomes, similar to the BB. While this heterochromatic core is dense with L1 repeats, the Alu-rich regions remain largely in the highly decondensed region of the outer chromosome territory. Our demonstration that the most L1-rich autosome (Chr-4) consistently forms a large SAHF whereas the most Alu-rich (Chr-19) does not, provides further evidence of an association between L1 and Alu-rich regions and large-scale chromosome architecture, as further discussed below.

Hi-C reveals gains in distal Intrachromosomal interactions as dark G-bands coalesce into SAHFs while Alu-rich segments resist

As conceptualized in Figure 6a, the above cytological results suggest that in senescent cells the architecture of individual chromosomes changes dramatically as distal L1-rich regions coalesce to form a SAHF in the center of the chromosome territory. FISH analysis shows dense L1 in the SAHF but indicate regions rich in Alu do not coalesce into the SAHF but remain in the decondensed regions of the territory in the enlarged senescent nucleus. To examine the changes on individual chromosomes at higher sequence resolution, we examined changes in Hi-C intrachromosomal interactions between growing and senescent human cells using published data [42]. Because we show SAHFs form from a single chromosome, we hypothesize that L1-rich regions within the chromosome territory (intra-chromosomal) will take on distal but syntenic Hi-C interactions, while Alu-rich DNA will not. This is distinct from the findings of Chandra et al., where they examined inter-chromosomal long-range interactions broadly across the genome, and concluded that SAHFs were actually decondensed structures.

Our analysis and findings bear on three questions/hypotheses which inter-relate around a central concept: that the regulated architecture of nuclear chromosomes is built on the invariant mega-scale sequence organization evidenced in the cytogenetic banding pattern. As described above, cytogenetic bands reflect very large (~5-15 Mb) segments of DNA which tend to differ in gene density and the density of different interspersed repeats. First, we hypothesize that in senescent cells, DNA regions widely separated but on the same chromosome, will coalesce to form the central SAHF, thereby picking up new long-range intrachromosomal interactions, increasing the proportion of long-range (>10Mb) versus short-range (>10 Mb) interactions. Second, we examine the extent to which large contiguous blocks of DNA (several megabases or more) would change in concert, particularly in regions of light versus dark cytogenetic bands. This is key to our hypothesis that this segmental organization of the linear genome provides a framework for large-scale segmental regulation of nuclear chromatin packaging. Third, we examine whether L1 and/or Alu density correlates with (and thus may influence) architectural remodeling, and in particular examine the top 10% L1 rich versus top 10% Alu rich DNA.

Figure 6b shows separate DNA interaction maps for Chr-4 in growing cells (top) and then in senescent cells (bottom), where red indicates high interactions and blue is lower interactions. As apparent in comparing the two interaction maps, the prominent SAHF formed by Chr-4 is associated with increased long-range syntenic interactions, evidenced by the prevalence of red patches (further from the center line) in senescent cells, which are not seen or are much reduced in the non-senescent culture. (We note that proliferative primary fibroblast cultures often have 15–20% senescent cells (e.g [20]). Hence, these changes in intrachromosomal interactions support that large parts of the chromosome condense, which would not be apparent from changes in inter-chromosomal interactions.

Of particular note, these changes in long-range interactions are seen primarily in very large blocks of DNA. Considering that Chr-4 contains 191 Mb of DNA, the blocks of red (indicating high interaction frequency) represent very large chromosome segments. In fact, as illustrated in Figure 6b by approximate alignment with the Chr-4 ideogram, the regions of high-interaction in senescent cells are similar in both size and distribution to the G-dark bands represented in the ideogram. Cytogenetic chromosome bands (at 850-band resolution) are defined by the intensity of their staining with Giemsa and are divided into five classes (Gpos100, 75, 50, and 25, and Gneg), which are represented on these chromosome ideograms, with ‘Gpos100’ the darkest staining ‘G-bands’ (black), and ‘Gneg’ R-bands (white). A similar general pattern is also evident across all of Chr-4 in Figure 7. The heatmap shows alternating large regions of the chromosome that lose short-range interactions (blue pyramids on the horizontal) and gain long-range interactions (red regions on the diagonal above the horizontal). These large regions correspond to the dark staining G-bands on the Chr-4 ideogram, indicating that distant syntenic dark G-bands are compacting together into SAHFs. If so, one would expect the increase in long-distance interactions seen in G-band DNA to be with other syntenic G-bands. This is apparent in the Chr-4 heatmap, where the long-range interactions (red boxes higher in the heatmap) connect all the G-bands along the chromosome (tracing down the left/right diagonals from the boxes: e.g. black arrows). This rearrangement is strongest for the darkest G-bands, which gain the most intra-chromosomal interactions with each other and then to a lesser degree with the lighter G-bands, suggesting the darkest G-bands may make up the central core of the SAHF.

The L1 sequence density for Chr-4 is relatively high across the entire chromosome, but this includes both light and dark bands, which do not show sharp differences in L1 content. Interestingly, although the large dark G-bands contain similar levels of L1 as the rest of the chromosome, Figure 8a illustrates how they still behave as one contiguous region. This suggests to us that some additional property of dark G-band DNA, other than just L1-density, helps drive or allow for heterochromatin compartment formation (see Discussion).

While some level of L1 density may be a pre-requisite, results suggest Alu density, and perhaps relative L1/Alu density, is important, as we see the overall pattern of dark G-bands (black & gray dots) losing short-range and gaining long-range syntenic interactions in senescent cells, while parts of Gneg R-bands (white dots) do not. Most strikingly, the highest density of Alu DNA on Chr-4 is in contiguous stretches that form sharp peaks that reflect marked increases over the baseline levels of Alu that don’t vary as much through most dark and light bands. These peaks in Alu density reside in R-bands that are resisting the interaction changes seen on the rest of the chromosome, remaining near zero for both short-range and long-range interactions. (Note: we find R-bands near telomeres behaved differently than most R-bands). These results fit with our DNA FISH data (above) which indicates Alu-rich DNA is depleted from the condensed core of SAHFs and remains more decondensed in the enlarged nucleoplasm of senescent cells. The resistance of Alu-rich peaks in light R-bands to SAHF coalescence is even more apparent in Figure 8a (green outlined white dots), which also suggests that Alu peaks may impact the structural interactions of neighboring sequences. For example, R-bands that lack a ‘resistant’ Alu-peak (Figure 8a, black arrow) can gain long-range interactions similar to darker bands, and when only a portion of white R-bands contain an Alu peak (Figure 8a, blue arrow), the Alu-depleted part of the band may show increased long-range interactions similar to adjacent dark bands.

In contrast, Chr-19 is remarkably Alu-rich and L1-poor, does not have dark G-bands (Figures 5a & 8b) and it does not form a SAHF (Figure 5d-f). And although there is some variation along the chromosome for long and short-range interactions (Figure 8b), the weakly staining G-bands don’t behave in the same way as the G-dark bands in Chr-4; there is no marked segmental movement of megabases of DNA, that change their packaging and interactions as a unit. Given that Chr-19 is almost entirely composed of the highest (top-10%) Alu content, it doesn’t contain Alu ‘peaks’ like Chr-4, and since it doesn’t make a SAHF, there is nothing for the Alu-DNA to ‘resist’. The pattern of L1-coalescence and Alu-resistance, only becomes apparent in the context of a more L1 enriched chromosome that engages in SAHF formation.

Finally, we show that the resistance of the Alu-rich regions to SAHF formation remains apparent when examining the combined intrachromosomal short-range and long-range interactions for all human chromosomes (at 100kb resolution)(Figure 9). We find essentially all dark band DNA gains long-range syntenic interactions genome-wide, and L1 DNA behaves similarly, consistent with most chromosomes forming SAHFs. However, we do see some light R-bands (Gneg) gaining long-range interactions, likely reflecting findings above, that R-band DNA lacking sharp Alu-rich peaks could gain long-range interactions because it lacks ‘Alu-resistance peaks’ (Figure 8a; black arrow). Collectively, Alu DNA across all human chromosomes shows little change in long-range intrachromosomal interactions (Figure 9: green), suggesting the highest Alu-rich regions of the genome resist these changes en mass.

Whole genome intra-chromosomal interactions show L1-rich and G-band DNA compact, while Alu-rich DNA resists. Intrachromosomal Hi-C short-range (<10Mb) (left graphs) and long-range (>10Mb) (right graphs) interactions for all human chromosomes at 100kb resolution for darkest G-bands (Gpos100) and light R-bands (Gneg) (on top), or highest (90th percentile) Alu (green) or L1 (blue) DNA (below).

In sum, results show L1-rich dark G-band DNA (and L1-rich light R-band DNA) gains long-range interactions, and that the highest Alu-rich peaks within light bands really resist this change. This suggests that Alu-rich DNA possesses a property that keeps it from coalescing into the dense, silent heterochromatin compartments that can form in the core of the chromosome territory (BB or SAHFs). The fact that Alu-rich peaks in the uniformly L1-dense Chr-4 can resist the dramatic changes that form SAHFs, may be facilitated by association of active genes with Nuclear Speckles [11], as we observe Alu-rich DNA is still often closely associated with SC35 domains in the enlarged nucleus of senescent cells.

Discussion

This study investigates the broad concept that the invariant organization of linear DNA into very large segments with similar sequence properties, which is reflected in cytogenetic bands, may be important for understanding regulation of chromatin packaging in the compartmentalized nucleus. We will first discuss our findings that directly address this fundamental concept and then discuss the implications in a few related areas.

Invariant organization of the linear genome as a framework for large-scale segmental regulation of the nuclear genome

This study provides evidence that very large segments of DNA, corresponding to dark versus light chromosome bands, can undergo packaging changes as a collective unit. Evidence for this comes from both molecular cytology and Hi-C data in the context of SAHF formation in senescent cells. Results support that during formation of SAHFs (and likely the BB on Xi) large blocks of DNA, corresponding to dark G-bands, collapse into the territory center and coalesce with other syntenic dark-bands, forming a heterochromatic ‘core’ of the chromosome territory. Importantly, both FISH and Hi-C analyses show that these condensed chromosome cores are depleted of Alu-rich DNA. While BBs and SAHFs are densely labeled for L1, Alu-rich DNA is largely excluded. In fact, high Alu density shows a particularly striking relationship to chromatin packaging. As shown for Chr-4, sharp Alu peaks punctuate the more uniformly L1-rich chromosome, and these ~ 1-2 Mb Alu-rich segments clearly resist the structural change as a unit. These smaller Alu-peaks are generally within light R-bands, but do not encompass the whole band. Much larger segments, which encompass entire dark bands (~5-10 Mb), also undergo changes in long-range interactions as a contiguous unit. This suggests large contiguous preexisting chromosome segments, with poorly understood similarities in sequence properties, facilitate either coalescence or resistance to cytological condensation. We note it had been previously demonstrated that Hi-C analysis does not detect chromosome bands, and shows an essentially uniform packing in loops across metaphase chromosome arms [43]. However, this does not negate the possibility that sequence organization visible as cytogenetic bands plays a role in genome architecture within nuclei.

Based on our collective findings, we propose that chromosomal bands, which are enriched for different repeats and gene densities, are a preexisting structural framework for the regional (multi mega-base scale) regulation of the genome, and the nuclear architecture it builds. This most foundational aspect of nuclear genome architecture may be ‘hiding in plain sight’, in the form of very large blocks of differently staining DNA on mitotic chromosomes, which we have spent the last 60 years observing but not understanding (or even studying).

Potential significance of repeat family distribution

Similar to the X chromosome, Chr-4 is especially L1 rich, and this may well contribute to this chromosome’s ability to form these prominent blocks of heterochromatin. However, while L1 density could be a pre-requisite, it is not determinative because high Alu peak segments resist condensation despite a high density of L1. Nonetheless, our collective results suggest an association between regional density of these repeats and chromatin packaging. A question remains whether the L1 or Alu repeats themselves play active or passive roles. Since their genomic distribution apparently does not reflect a preference for insertion site Sultana et al. [44], this suggests some evolutionary selection for genomic distributions in chromosome bands. L1 sequences are thought to contain features that form unusual DNA structures [45], which may be modulated to promote self-association. And several studies suggest that L1-RNA may play a role in heterochromatin formation, possibly by interacting with L1 DNA [22,46]. In addition, SINES have been reported to act as boundary-elements/buffers/insulators [47], which fits with our finding that Alu-rich peaks anchoring the gene-rich chromosome bands resist compaction into SAHFs. Thus, Alu peaks may buffer/insulate the ‘spread’ of heterochromatin by physically separating active DNA from silent L1-rich DNA within nuclear/chromosome structure.

Although we find DNA interactions in large G-bands change in concert as a unit, distinct from neighboring light bands, the L1 density did not really differ between these bands. Hence other sequence properties, such as differences in simple sequence repeats or microsatellites likely contribute. Since there are several different ‘flavors’ of cytogenetic bands, corresponding to GC isochores [9], the pattern of repeat sequence organization and the relationship to genome packaging is likely more complex, and may relate to the distribution of housekeeping and specialized genes [48]. To our knowledge, the well-defined peaks of Alu and their clear resistance to condensation has not been previously reported but strongly implicates Alu playing a role. However, the resistance of Alu-rich DNA to condensation could still be due more to gene activity (and the presence of euchromatic-associated RNAs) than anything inherent to the Alu-sequence itself. This resistance of the most Alu-rich regions to whole chromosome condensation is likely linked to its relationship with highly expressed genes that associate with euchromatic nuclear structures, most notably nuclear speckles (aka: SC-35 domains). It therefore remains to be established whether the relative density of L1 and Alu repeats themselves strongly promote this regional unity or what other aspects of the band’s sequence content play a role in segmental structure and function.

Formation of BB/SAHFs is not simply due to collective silencing and condensation of canonical genes

The Barr body (BB) of the Xi (and to some extent the SAHFs in senescent cells) were long thought to be the visible manifestation of silenced genes, compacted by local epigenetic (histone/nucleosome) modifications which collectively produced the condensed structure seen by DNA stain [18,49,50]. However, canonical gene silencing would only impact a minor fraction of chromosomal DNA, and we have long argued that the DNA-dense BB is huge relative to what would be expected from silencing the subset of X-linked genes active in any given cell type [31]. In fact, reports suggest there is little difference in the amount of ‘open chromatin’ between the active and inactive X-chromosome (Xa & Xi) [2] Naughton et al. [51]; reviewed in [52], which suggests these more local effects could not be responsible for the large-scale condensation of the BB. And we recently reported that XIST RNA starts to build the condensed BB structure 2–3 days before it silences canonical genes [53]. Since findings here indicate that the gene-poor L1-rich DNA in the BB largely excludes gene-rich Alu-rich DNA, XIST RNA must first act to compact the L1-rich/Alu-poor and gene-poor DNA first, to form the dense silent (BB), before most canonical genes in Alu- rich regions become silenced. This further fits with our previous results showing canonical genes are localized at the periphery of the dense BB regardless of silenced state [29].

Senescent cells also form large condensed L1-rich/Alu-poor, gene-poor cores at the center of most chromosomes and they similarly lack CoT-1 RNA and Alu-rich DNA. Interestingly however, senescent cells are mostly associated with new expression of many genes (e.g. SASP, cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors) rather than genome wide gene silencing [54,55]. Consistent with this, in senescent cells there is not only SAHF condensation, but an expansion of highly decondensed chromatin outside the SAHFs in a much-enlarged nucleus, including a marked distension of normally compact centromeric satellite DNA [20]. While it is less clear why senescent cells form SAHFs, this may serve to render irreversible the silencing of cell cycle genes.

L1-rich chromatin condensation may be facilitated by lack of euchromatin-associated RNAs and/or scaffold attachment

Both SAHFs and BBs lack euchromatin associated RNAs (e.g. CoT-1 RNA), which are thought to promote open chromatin structure through RNA-dependent links to the nuclear scaffold [37,56]. It’s hypothesized that removal of these often lowly expressed, repeat rich RNAs alters chromatin association with the nuclear scaffold and results in DNA condensation on a histological scale (rather than at the histone/nucleosome level). It is possible that the SAHF/BB structure could be formed from already silent L1-rich DNA, making the Cot-1 RNA/RNAPII ‘hole’ over these structures a consequence of L1 DNA re-organization. However, another possibility (not mutually exclusive) is that XIST RNA silences low-level expression of lncRNAs in L1-rich/gene-poor DNA which causes it to collapse into a large condensed nuclear structure.

Disruption of low-level repeat-rich transcripts has been shown to cause cytological-scale DNA compaction accompanied by changes to scaffold proteins [37]. Other reports indicate that XIST RNA adds transcriptionally repressive histone marks (H3K27me3 & H2AK119ub) on gene-poor ‘intergenic’ DNA first, before moving onto more active ‘genic’ regions [57]. Loss of low-level transcripts, that impact the RNP scaffold, would also fit with our observation here that the condensed BB often exhibits reduced staining for Lamin B1, a nuclear scaffold protein. Loss of Lamin B1 throughout the nucleus is also a characteristic of senescent cells [19,40]. Thus, XIST RNA may mediate changes in Lamin B1/L1-rich DNA interactions for a single chromosome that is paralleled, genome-wide, in senescent cells.

Why build a gene-poor heterochromatic compartment? The histone de-acetylation hypothesis

In numerous earlier papers we and others showed that many highly active genes were not only in the euchromatic nuclear compartment, but specifically localized at the periphery of an SC-35 rich nuclear speckle (reviewed in [11,27]. It has since become widely accepted that localization of active genes with domains of concentrated pre-mRNA metabolic factors is widespread and will facilitate gene expression. Hence, this can explain the clustering of canonical genes in contiguous (Alu-rich) segments on the linear genome. However, why form a ‘compartment’ for silenced genes since epigenetic regulation of promoters is known to provide stable silencing? Insight into this came from a recent study [53] in which we showed that a required step to initiate gene silencing, H3K27 de-acetylation, is a highly unstable step that can be rapidly reversed by histone acetyltransferases (HATs), which are prevalent in active chromatin regions. Results showed that high cytological density of XIST RNA (or the XIST A-repeat domain) was necessary to shift the ongoing balance of HATs and HDACs (Histone deacetylases) toward deacetylation, allowing subsequent epigenetic steps to occur that stabilize the silent state. We propose that formation of the large BB, enriched for silent, gene-poor, L1-rich DNA will lack substantial HAT activity and thus provide a ‘silencing compartment’ which favors effective/sustained histone deacetylation. Effective H3K27-deacetylation will then allow methylation of the same residue (PRC2 mediated H3K27me3), and subsequent steps, which stabilize the silent state. Thus, although chromosomes in senescent cells have much more expression than the Xi, we propose that proximity of canonical gene promoters to the edge of the HAT-depleted BB/SAHF structure facilitates gene silencing by preventing re-acetylation of genes that had been in active chromatin regions and subsequently stabilize the silenced state (illustrated in Figure 10). In contrast, regions of more euchromatic, gene-dense, Alu-rich DNA, likely are higher in HAT activity, shifting the balance toward histone acetylation and activity. This might suggest that genes in Alu-rich segments that resist tight association with the condensed silencing domain, may more easily escape from silencing. This concept may explain evidence from X:autosome translocation studies that genes that escape silencing by XIST RNA contain significantly more Alu-sequences [58].

Methods

Cell lines, growth conditions & fixation

All cell lines (List in Supplement), were grown in conditions recommended by suppliers (See supplemental Methods). Drug treatments (STSP & DRB) are detailed in supplement. Our standard fixation protocols have been detailed previously [59–61] (details in Supplement).

FISH and IF

Our standard hybridization conditions for RNA, DNA, simultaneous DNA/RNA, and simultaneous DNA or RNA and IF detection was performed as previously described (e.g [59]) (details in Supplement). Specific probes, chromosome paints and antibodies are listed in Supplement. Nick-translated genomic probes were used for most images unless otherwise indicated.

Microscopy and digital imaging

An Axiovert 200 or an AxioObserver 7 Zeiss microscope equipped with a 100X PlanApo objective (NA 1.4) and Chroma 83,000 multi-bandpass dichroic and emission filter sets (Brattleboro, VT. Images and Z-stacks were captured with the Zeiss AxioVision or Zen software, and an Orca-ER camera or with a Flash 4.0 LT CMOS camera (Hamamatsu). Images were minimally corrected for contrast and brightness (min/max), to best represent signals observed by eye using Axiovision or Zen (Zeiss) software, unless otherwise noted in figure legend or text. Where required, care was taken to eliminate any bleed-thru of red fluorescence into the fluorescein channel. Images are 2D, show a plane from the z-stack or a MIP (as indicated). Most experiments were carried out a minimum of 2 times, with typically 100–300 cells scored in each experiment. Key results were confirmed by at least two independent investigators. All findings were easily visible by eye through the microscope (unless otherwise noted), and images were minimally enhanced for brightness and contrast in Photoshop to resemble what was seen by eye through the microscope (unless otherwise noted).

L1 Alu content in chromosomes

Contribution of L1 and Alu sequences to human chromosomes were calculated for the hg19 February 2009 human genome reference assembly (hg19) using the RepeatMasker repeat library (v4.0.5).

Hi-C analysis

Published Hi-C datasets were downloaded from accession PRJEB80 (PMID 25,640,177). Reads were mapped using Bowtie to the human genome (hg19) and contact matrices were generated using Homer at the indicated resolution (PMID 20,513,432). Options -removePEbg -restrictionSite AAGCTT -removeSelfLigation -removeSpikes 10,000 5 parameters were employed when using the makeTagDirectory tool. Percent LINE-1 and Alu sequences in each genomic interval of the count matrices was calculated using Repbase annotations (downloaded from the UCSC browser) and BEDtools. Chromosome band information was downloaded using the UCSC Genome Browser.
