Risky business: penile prosthesis in high-risk patients
Authors: Sukhjeevan S. Nijhar, Charlotte C. Goldman, Joanna Orzel, Nathan M. Shaw, Krishnan Venkatesan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with diabetes and other immunocompromising conditions may be at higher risk for penile prosthesis complications. However, data on this patient population is limited and at times contradictory. We review our operative and post-operative experience with inflatable penile prosthesis placement in these high-risk patients at a major urban tertiary-care center. We hypothesize that peri-operative hyperglycemia may increase the likelihood of postoperative complications and that a peri-operative glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) greater than 8.5 may be associated with higher rates of device infection, erosion, and malfunction.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients undergoing penile prosthesis insertion at a single institution from 2012–2023. We recorded peri-operative HbA1c in addition to mean and maximum peri-operative blood glucose levels. The primary outcome was device infection leading to reoperation. Secondary outcomes included device erosion or device component malfunction.ResultsTwo hundred and thirty-five cases among 200 patients met inclusion criteria. 179 were first-time implants with 56 revision surgeries. Of the 235 surgeries, patients with diabetes underwent 107 surgeries, those with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) underwent 6, solid organ transplant history underwent 10, and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) underwent 12. The mean patient age was 61.8 years. Perioperative HbA1c was higher among diabetics (7.7 vs. 5.9 g/dL, P<0.001) as was mean blood glucose (167.5 vs. 113.2 mg/dL, P<0.001 and max blood glucose 197.2 vs. 123.3 mg/dL, P<0.001). Infections requiring reoperation occurred in 2.8% of diabetics and 3.4% of non-diabetics (P=0.79). No difference was seen in device reoperation rates at either a HbA1c cut-off of 8.5 or 10 g/dL.ConclusionsDevice-related complications were not more common among diabetic patients compared to non-diabetic counterparts. No differences in rates of device infection, device malfunction, or device erosion were noted in our findings. Furthermore, a preoperative HbA1c cutoff of 8.5 and 10 g/dL was not associated with an increased rate of prosthetic complications.

• Patients with diabetes are at higher risk of erectile dysfunction (ED).

• Implantable hardware is an option for surgical intervention for ED.

• A preoperative glycosylated hemoglobin cutoff of 8.5 or 10 g/dL does not predict higher complication rates.

Introduction

For men with severe erectile dysfunction (ED), penile prosthesis surgery can be the light at the end of a tunnel of failed medical therapies. However, the procedure is not without risks, with device infection and erosion being among the most significant. Implantable hardware may create a setting for bacteria to form biofilms that protect colonies from clearance by the immune system (1,2). As a result of the protective environment that bacterial biofilms provide, prosthetic infections can be extremely difficult to treat and generally require device removal in combination with broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Patients with impaired immunity such as diabetics, patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and those with solid organ transplant (SOT) are generally considered at higher risk for infectious complications. Unfortunately, these patients often have more numerous and severe comorbidities that negatively impact erectile function and response to conservative treatment (3-5). The result is a cohort of men whose comorbidities increase the likelihood they will need a penile prosthesis, while also being at higher risk of complication from penile prosthesis implantation. Patients with diabetes are four times more likely to experience ED than the general population and comprise >20% of men undergoing penile prostheses (3,6). Furthermore, patients who have undergone SOT are also at a higher risk for ED (4).

Data published on outcomes of penile prostheses in the diabetic and immunosuppressed populations has been limited and in places, contradictory. Several studies have demonstrated higher infection among diabetic patients with patients stratified by glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) of 8.5 g/dL by Bishop et al. in 1992 and 11.5 g/dL by Haobus et al. in 2018 (7-13). In 2021, Chen and Eisenberg analyzed Medicare Advantage claims, reporting that with each point increase in HbA1c demonstrated a 29% increased risk in revision surgery secondary to infection (14). In contrast, there are also multiple studies refuting this higher infection risk among diabetic patients, as well as refuting the 8.5 g/dL cut-off value (13,15-18). A 2020 multicenter study, for example, of 932 diabetic patients showed no association of HbA1c or perioperative glucose with infection or explant, however, a higher revision rate was seen among those with a history of complications from diabetes (19).

We therefore sought to examine our own experience, and investigate if immunocompromised patients will be at higher risk for complications. Among diabetic patients specifically, we hypothesized that higher blood glucose during the perioperative period may be critical in facilitating bacterial growth, and that this may result in a higher rate of infection or other complication. In this early time before a capsule is formed around the device, any bacteria introduced during implantation may have the prime opportunity to multiply. In addition to characterizing the impact of perioperative glycemic control, we also sought to validate HbA1c thresholds of 8.5 and 10 g/dL as risk thresholds for device complications (7). We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2025-168/rc).

Methods

Data source

A prospectively maintained registry of patients undergoing implant and reconstructive surgeries at MedStar Washington Hospital Center was retrospectively queried for patients undergoing penile prostheses from December 2012 to July 2023. MedStar IRB approval and informed consent were waived by IRB as this retrospective review met criteria for exemption. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Study population

Patients 18 years of age and older who underwent penile prosthesis surgery from 2012–2023 were considered eligible. Patients with less than 6 months of follow-up were excluded. Patients who presented for explant only and had original implants with another practice were also excluded. Patients were matched based on operative location and surgeon performing intervention.

Clinical pathway and operative technique

All operations were performed by a single surgeon via penoscrotal approach. Both Boston Scientific and Coloplast devices were used based on clinical judgment and patient preference. We currently use a HbA1c cutoff of 10% as an absolute cutoff for diabetic patients desiring device implantation. Intraoperatively, multiple steps were taken to prevent device infection in the mode of minimizing contact with the device (20). Revision surgery with immediate replacement of a device utilized a mini-salvage technique if no evidence of infection, or full Mulcahy salvage if any suspicion or evidence of infection (21,22).

Variables

Patient characteristics, including age at the time of operation, BMI, and co-existing diagnoses were collected using the electronic medical record and are summarized. Additionally, perioperative (checked within 6 months of surgery) HbA1c values, as well as patients’ maximum and average blood glucose values during their immediate post-surgical stay were recorded. Adverse outcomes were collected via retrospective chart review and included device infections resulting in device erosion or explantation. Furthermore, re-operation (for any reason, including device malfunction) was included as a separate variable.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study cohort. Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation). The primary comparison was between immunocompromised and non-immunocompromised patients using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. For categorical variables, the Chi-squared test was used. The Mann-Whitney test was used to compare maximum and perioperative blood sugars between diabetics and non-diabetics. Averages were compared using the Student’s t-test. A statistical value of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical calculations were performed using Excel for Windows (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and Stata 16 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

A total of 200 patients undergoing 235 prosthesis insertion surgeries were included. Of the 235 implanted prosthetics, 56 were revision surgeries for patients who had previously had implants in the past, including those requiring explantation and replacement in a subsequent stage (Figure 1). Of the 235 surgeries, 107 were done on diabetics and 28 with other immunocompromised states including patients with SOTs [10], HIV [6], and end-stage renal disease [12] (Table 1). The proportion of diabetic patients was higher in the revision group (n=34, 53%) compared to primary implant surgeries (n=22, 18.8%) (P<0.05) (Table 1).

The proportion of device infections that resulted in operative intervention was similar between primary (n=5, 2.7%) and revision (n=2; 3.5%) (P=0.76) (Figure 2). Device erosion was seen in three primary procedures (1.7%) and two revision surgeries (3.5%) (P=0.39) (Figure 2). In the revision cohort, 3 patients had devices explanted because of infection compared to 4 in the primary cohort (2.8% vs. 3.5%, P=0.76). Amongst the revision cohort, 14 (27%) resulted in device explant or replacement due to malfunction, compared to 18 (10%) occurrences in patients who had undergone primary surgeries (P=0.29). In examining all complications, revision surgeries were not associated with a statistically significant higher rate of any complication than first-time implants (Table 2).

No differences were seen in the rate of device infection between diabetic and non-diabetic patients: 8.4% (9 patients) vs. 9.4% (11 patients) (P=0.07) (Table 1). Three of the five total device erosions where there was no gross infection occurred in the non-diabetic population (P=0.72) (Table 1). Overall, a total of 7 (3%) procedures resulted in an infection that required operative explant or replacement (Table 1). No difference was also appreciated in the rates of device malfunction or device erosion between diabetic and non-diabetic patients. Diabetics did demonstrate a higher re-operative rate overall compared to non-diabetics (31.1% vs. 18.8% P<0.05). They did not demonstrate a higher proportion of devices explanted due to infection (2.5 vs. 3.7% P=0.62).

Maximum and mean perioperative blood glucose were higher among diabetics than non-diabetics (P<0.05, Table 1). The average perioperative blood glucose of diabetic vs. nondiabetic patients was 167 and 113 mg/dL (P<0.05). Statistically significant differences were demonstrated between maximum and mean perioperative blood glucose as well as perioperative HbA1c between diabetic and non-diabetic patients who experienced device malfunction only (Tables 3-5). No adverse outcomes were statistically more common among patients with HbA1c values >8.5 or >10 g/dL (Tables 6,7).

Discussion

Examination of patients presenting to a single center for penile prosthesis demonstrated no higher rate of device infections and reoperation amongst patients with or without diabetes. In our sample size, statistically significant difference in perioperative average glucose, maximum glucose, and hemoglobin A1c between non-diabetic and diabetic patients was seen. However, on scrutiny of device explantation or replacement secondary to infection, no difference in perioperative mean or max blood glucose was seen between diabetics and non-diabetics. In other words, perioperative hyperglycemia was not a risk for device complication.

In the time frame of the study, patients were routinely observed overnight after penile prosthesis surgery. Unsurprisingly, the maximum levels of blood glucose during admission as well as the average perioperative blood glucose levels were higher in the diabetic group compared to those without diabetes. Interestingly, perioperative hyperglycemia was not associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. Despite the hyperglycemia, diabetic patients did not show an increased rate of reoperation due to infection, device malfunction, or device erosion. These results are consistent with a study by Habous et al. demonstrating the insignificance of perioperative blood sugar on post-operative outcomes (23). It is important to note that their study included malleable penile prostheses, in comparison to our focus on inflatable three-piece penile prostheses. It is possible that hyperglycemia did not play a causative role in complication because once glucose levels were checked, they may have presumably been treated.

Additionally, based on evidence from Habous et al., we theorized that patients with hemoglobin A1c >8.5 g/dL would be at higher risk for infections, however, we found no difference in any of our adverse outcomes among patients above or below an HbA1c threshold of 8.5 g/dL (23). Our data is consistent with trends reported by Talib et al. in 2021 (13).

Although our population of patients with a history of SOT was quite small in this analysis, these patients were not found to be at higher risk for the development of any of our adverse outcomes. This is important as SOT recipients experience higher rates of ED than their peers and are generally considered high risk for inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) complications because of their need for immunosuppression (24,25). The data presented here are consistent with the existing small studies that have shown that SOT is not a risk factor for device infection (4,26,27). Cuellar et al. found a higher complication rate in 46 patients with IPP placement after SOT (22% vs. 7.9% P<0.01) (28). Sun et al. followed this study with a matched cohort study of 26 SOT and 26 non-SOT patients, which did not demonstrate any difference in re-operative rate (11.5% vs. 11.5%; P=1.0) at 30 months of follow-up (26). Despite the immunosuppressed state of SOT patients, our limited data and those of prior groups suggest that these men are likely not at the same risk of infection as those peers with poorly controlled diabetes.

A quarter of the surgeries in our study were revision procedures. However, while the conventional wisdom to date was that infection/complication rates in revision surgery were higher, our data found no difference in device infection, malfunction, or erosion (Table 2) (1,2,11,29). Surprisingly, none of our adverse outcomes were more common among the patients undergoing revision surgery even though a number of patients in our study underwent revision or salvage IPP placement despite their diabetic status. While insulin-dependent diabetes was previously a contraindication to immediate salvage in IPP, our study confirms the results of Peters et al. in 2018, which demonstrated these patients may be a candidate for immediate salvage (30). Even among our small cohort of patients who underwent multiple revision surgeries, there was no increased complication rate or risk observed.

The outcome of “infection” is inconsistently defined in the literature. Some series include superficial site infections, while others tabulate only device infections, erosions, or in some cases only those devices that were explanted. As described by Krzastek et al., superficial infections are simply surgical site infections that can resolve with oral antibiotics, to be differentiated from acute device infections (31). Mulcahy et al. defined their infectious outcome only as reoperation due to infection and reported a 1.8% incidence in diabetic patients (9). Similarly, our focus was on the rate of device infection requiring reoperation, excluding superficial infection. Our data found a 2.8% rate of reoperation due to infection for implants in diabetic patients compared to 3.4% in non-diabetics (P=0.79). Furthermore, we found that no differences existed in erosion outcomes between diabetic and non-diabetic patients despite differences in mean and maximum peri-operative glucose levels (Tables 3,4).

Limitations

A significant limitation of this study is its scale as a single institution study, as well as its retrospective nature. In comparison of hemoglobin A1c, only 36 non-diabetic patients had A1c values available. Certain trends elucidated in this study have the potential to show true statistical significance with more data and perhaps even longer follow-up. However, given the conflicting reports on implantation among diabetics and the paucity of information on SOT and other high-risk patients receiving penile prostheses, further large-scale multi-institution studies are warranted.

It appears the jury is still out on whether traditionally “high-risk” patients such as diabetics and immunocompromised patients experience worse outcomes, and if so, which ones, and how they develop. In our experience, it does not appear these patients should be precluded from penile prosthesis insertion.

Conclusions

In our study, diabetic patients do not have a higher risk of infectious complications compared to those without diabetes. Furthermore, a pre-operative HbA1c threshold of 8.5 or 10 g/dL and peri-operative glucose around the time of the operation did not appear to be predictive of postoperative complications. Immunocompromised patients with HIV or SOT did not experience a higher infection risk with penile implant surgery.
