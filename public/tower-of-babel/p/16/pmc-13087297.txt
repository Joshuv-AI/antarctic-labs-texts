Application research of powder forming technology in the preparation of heated tobacco core materials
Authors: Wenjun Zhang, Jing Liu, Zhen Xiong, Quanrong Jing, Huifan Li, Jianbei Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeated tobacco core material, a key component of heated tobacco products (HTPs), functions as the primary carrier for aerosol, nicotine, and flavor compounds. However, conventional papermaking methods impose inherent limitations that compromise the overall quality of these core materials. This study proposes a novel method for producing reconstituted tobacco (RT) using ultrafine powder (UFP) derived from tobacco raw materials. Comparative analysis revealed distinct physical and chemical properties of UFP-based RT compared to traditional paper-based RT. Specifically, the UFP-based material exhibited reduced thickness (from 0.144 mm to 0.113 mm) and lower air permeability (from 84 CU to 15 CU), while showing significant improvements in tensile strength (from 726 N/m to 965 N/m), elongation (from 2.331% to 2.732%), density (from 0.659 g/cm³ to 0.779 g/cm³), thermal conductivity (from 0.0525 W/(m·K) to 0.0739 W/(m·K)), and smoothness (from 3 S to 13 S). The UFP-based substrate also demonstrated enhanced wettability, which improved coating performance during RT manufacturing. Although conventional chemical indicators were similar between the two materials, the UFP-based core contained 21.2% more volatile flavor compounds. Thermal analysis indicated a higher total weight loss (37.89% for UFP-based vs. 30.40% for conventional material) within the temperature range of 224–378 °C, along with a shift in the maximum weight loss rate from 296 °C to 287 °C for the UFP-based material. These findings highlight the superior functionality and aerosol generation potential of the UFP-processed heated tobacco core material.Keywords: Heated tobacco products, Core material, Ultrafine powderSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

On May 21, 2003, the World Health Assembly appeal to all nations to participate in extensive international collaboration to curb the widespread proliferation of tobacco. With the introduction and enforcement of “public smoking bans” in member states globally, coupled with a continuous enhancement of smokers’ health consciousness, the market sales of conventional combustible cigarettes are gradually diminishing1,2. Heated Tobacco products (HTP) have increasingly emerged as the preferred choice for leading multinational tobacco companies in adapting to evolving circumstances, owing to their low tar content, reduced harm potential, environmentally sustainable attributes, and widespread acceptance3,4. When utilizing HTP, the cigarette cartridge must be inserted into the heating device for the purpose of heating and suction. Smoke is produced through the homogenization of reconstituted tobacco via heating. The smoke passes through a hollow section to enter the cooling segment in order to lower its temperature, and subsequently traverses the filtering section prior to consumption by end-users5.

The aerosol composition in combustion type cigarettes and heated tobacco cigarettes typically consists of thermal decomposition products, oxidation-reduction reaction products during thermal cracking, and dry distillation of aroma substances. However, combustion type cigarettes reach temperatures ranging from 600 ℃ to 900 ℃ during the smoking process, leading to elevated levels of harmful substances such as benzo pyrene, TSNAs, phenolic compounds, and styrene in the aerosols. The heating temperature for heated tobacco cigarettes typically ranges from 250 ℃ to 350 ℃, with reliance on heating agents to generate gas-soluble vapors for aroma extraction and a relatively lower formation of thermal cracking products compared to combustion-type cigarettes. The aerosols primarily consist of dry distillation products and thermal cracking products. Relevant research findings indicate that the emission of harmful substances from heated cigarettes is reduced by over 90% in comparison to traditional combustion-type cigarettes6–9. The aerosols generated by heated cigarettes play a crucial role as carriers of nicotine and other aromatic components. Their smoking sensation closely resembles that of traditional combustion type cigarettes, thus rendering them more readily accepted by consumers and facilitating the transition of traditional combustion type cigarette consumer groups10,11.

The core material, primarily composed of reconstituted tobacco (RT) processed from tobacco waste, serves as the main component in heated cigarettes. Within the heated tobacco cartridge, these materials function as carriers for nicotine, smoking agents, essence and flavor, and tobacco concentrate. Heated tobacco relies on regenerated tobacco leaves to generate aerosols at high temperatures, containing a concentrated dose of tobacco, nicotine, and other components. As a result, the amount of reconstituted tobacco used in heated tobacco can be approximately 9 times that of traditional cigarettes, placing exceptionally high demands on its performance. Conventional RT is typically manufactured using processes such as the paper-making process, dry method, or rolling method, with the paper-making process being one of the most widely adopted in the industry12–14. For instance, the core material utilized in the circular heating type heated cigarette, developed by BAT company i-Glo/Glo, is manufactured using this conventional paper-making technique for tobacco reconstitution15–17.

However, the traditional paper-making method for RT production has inherent limitations that severely impact its suitability for high-performance HTPs. This process often results in a base web with defects such as low thickness, a tight and dense surface structure, and consequently, poor liquid absorption and permeability18,19. This inherently poor absorbency hinders the effective penetration of coating liquid (containing flavors and nicotine) into the interior of the base web. A critical consequence is that when the coating rate exceeds 40%, a crystalline layer forms from the excess coating liquid on the surface, leading to product clumping and caking after compression, which disrupts subsequent production. Furthermore, the diversification and heterogeneity of raw materials used in RT often result in insufficient extraction and uneven pulping. This manifests in a final product with a rough surface, unstable physical indicators, poor uniformity, and inadequate absorption performance, making it difficult to achieve high and uniform loading of the coating liquid20,21. Ultimately, these shortcomings confer fundamental deficiencies to the final RT material, including a coarse surface, uneven coating, low thermal conductivity, substantial fluctuations in processing resistance, and notable variations in overall quality22–24. These deficiencies directly constrain the sensory quality, consistency, and user experience of HTPs.

Given these profound limitations of conventional RT, there is a compelling necessity to develop innovative processes that can fundamentally alter the material’s microstructure. The Ultrafine Powder (UFP) process represents such an innovation. Conceptually, the UFP process aims to create a material matrix composed of ultrafine tobacco particles, facilitating the formation of a highly porous, three-dimensional network structure with a large specific surface area. This structural paradigm is fundamentally different from the dense, fiber-oriented sheet produced by the paper-making process.

Based on the traditional paper-making method employed in reconstituted tobacco production lines, this article presents an innovative process for producing UFP to be used as heated tobacco core material. The resulting UFP-based core material represents a significant departure from conventional RT materials. This study demonstrates that the developed preparation process significantly enhances the hydrophilicity of the substrate, thereby improving the penetration degree of coating liquid and increasing the retention rate of flavor compounds. Furthermore, the UFP structure contributes to enhanced thermal conductivity of the material, which effectively lowers the maximum weight-loss temperature while increasing the maximum weight-loss rate. These combined improvements in coating performance and thermal properties establish this innovative material as a versatile and potent platform for the design and development of advanced heated tobacco products (HTPs), providing a solid foundation for superior end-product characteristics.

Materials and methods

The formulation of the CTS-A heated tobacco core material comprises ultrafine powder of CTS-A type heated tobacco core material (China Tobacco Schweitzer (Yunnan) Reconstituted Tobacco Co., Ltd.), soft wood Kraft (Inno-chem technology co., ltd, Beijing), glycerol (Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd, Purity ≥ 99%), and propylene glycol (Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd, Purity ≥ 99%).

The composition of the tobacco raw materials is as follows: 55% tobacco leaf fragments + 37% tobacco stem + 8% soft wood Kraft. The coating liquid recipe was as follows: 40% of smoke agent (including 12% of propylene glycol and 28% of glycerol), 8% of essence and fragrance, and 52% of tobacco extract concentrated solution.

Preparation of ultrafine tobacco powder

The tobacco raw materials of the CTS-A heated tobacco formula were meticulously divided into two distinct groups. One group underwent a process of grinding to produce 400 mesh tobacco powder by using a KFC-600 airflow crusher. Following extraction and sheet-forming procedures, a reconstituted tobacco base sheet with Ultra-Fine Pulverization (UPF) was obtained. In contrast, the other group did not receive the ultra-fine pulverization treatment. After undergoing the same extraction and sheet-making steps, a reconstituted tobacco base sheet without UPF was procured.

Characterization of ultrafine tobacco powder

The microstructure of ultrafine tobacco powder was examined using the SU8600 micro fluorescence field emission scanning electron microscope, while the specific surface area, average pore size, and pore volume were determined using the JW-K nitrogen adsorption specific surface area and pore size distribution tester. All samples were degassed in vacuum at 100 ℃ for 3 h before testing, and tested at liquid nitrogen temperature. The specific surface area was calculated by BET formula, and the average pore diameter and pore volume were calculated by BJH method. Additionally, the particle size distribution of ultrafine tobacco powder was analyzed with the BT9300ST laser particle size analyzer.

Microstructure inspection

The microstructure of both the substrate and finished product were investigated by scanning electron microscope (VEGA SB, TES-CAN Company, Czech). The specific steps are as follows: a certain number of samples were fixed on the sample table with conductive adhesive, and then put into the vacuum system of the instrument after gold spraying treatment. The acceleration voltage was 5.0 kV, the working distance was about 8.556 mm, the magnification was adjusted to 400 times to observe the morphology of each sample, and the scanning speed was set to slow.

Conventional physical index testing

According to GB/T 12,914 − 2008, the determination of tensile strength of paper and paperboard25, GB/T 458–2002, the determination of air permeability of paper and paperboard (Schober Method)26, GB/T 451.3–2002, the determination of thickness of paper and paperboard27, and GB/T 451.2–2002, the determination of quantitative weight of paper and paperboard28 are utilized for assessing the tensile strength, breathability, thickness, and quantification of the fundamental web and core material. Density is defined as the ratio between quantity and thickness.

Base web absorption performance testing

The base web is conditioned in a constant temperature and humidity chamber at (22 ± 1) ℃ and a relative humidity of (60 ± 2)% for 48 h. Subsequently, the coating liquid is loaded into the sample storage tube, and the operation software of the contact angle measuring instrument is initiated. The sample volume is set to 2 µL, and the sampling pump is activated to fill the instrument pipeline with the coating solution under investigation. The test substrate is positioned beneath the contact angle measuring instrument, and based on images captured through a high-speed camera lens, the baseline level of water is selected as the testing position. The equipment then commences testing, with two parallel experiments conducted for each sample. Evaluation of absorbency/ hydrophilicity performance of the sample relies on data depicting changes in contact angle over time obtained through continuous imaging of droplets.

Thermal conductivity testing

Thermal conductivity testing of the base web and core material were carried out on a thermal conductivity analyzer (TPS2500S, Hot Disk AB, German). Disk-shaped samples (diameter: 25 mm, thickness: 3 mm) with parallel surfaces were prepared. Prior to measurement, the instrument was calibrated with a fused silica standard. For testing, the sensor was sandwiched between two identical sample pieces. A measurement power of 50 mW and a time period of 20 s were applied to ensure a minimal temperature rise (< 2 °C). The thermal conductivity value was automatically calculated by the proprietary software from the transient temperature response.

Determination of conventional chemical indicators of core materials

Use FUTURA-II continuous flow analyzer to detect the conventional chemical indicators of the core material, including total sugar, reducing sugar29, total alkaloids30, total nitrogen31, potassium ions32, chloride ions33, and nitrate34 content. The specific procedures for testing total sugar, reducing sugar, total plant alkaloids, nitrate, potassium ions, and chloride ions were as follows: A sample of 0.25 g was accurately weighed to a precision of 0.001 g, and the exact mass was recorded. The sample was then transferred into a 50 mL conical flask, followed by the addition of 25 mL of a 5% acetic acid extraction solution. The flask was placed on a shaker and shaken for 30 min (shaking speed: 175 r/min). After extraction, the sample was filtered through qualitative filter paper into another 50 mL conical flask, and then loaded into the sample tube for instrumental analysis. The specific procedure for determining total nitrogen was as follows: A sample of 0.1 g was accurately weighed to a precision of 0.001 g, and the exact mass was recorded. The sample was transferred into a 100 mL digestion tube. Then, 2.00 g of potassium sulfate and 0.16 g of copper (II) sulfate pentahydrate were added to the same digestion tube. After adding 5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid, the digester was started under the following program: 150 °C for 60 min. Once the digestate had cooled, water was slowly added along the tube wall to dissolve the sample, and the volume was adjusted to 100 mL. The tube was stoppered and mixed thoroughly until the solution became homogeneous without stratification. The mixed sample was then filtered through qualitative filter paper into a 100 mL conical flask and loaded into the sample tube for analysis.

Analysis of volatile aromatic components in core materials

Using the simultaneous distillation extraction (SDE) method for determining the aroma components in the sample, 20.00 g of the sample was carefully weighed and placed in a 500 mL two-necked round bottom flask. Subsequently, 20 g of sodium chloride and 250 ml of distilled water were added to create an optimal extraction environment. Additionally, 50 mL of dichloromethane was transferred into a 250 mL round bottom flask, followed by the addition of 0.10 mL from a solution containing benzyl acetate internal standard at a concentration of 1 mg/ml. The simultaneous distillation and extraction process continued for a duration of three hours, after which anhydrous sodium sulfate was introduced to dry the dichloromethane solution for at least two hours. Finally, concentration at 40 ℃ under atmospheric pressure yielded approximately 1mL volume suitable for analysis purposes.

The particle size distribution of 400 mesh ultrafine powder is shown in Fig. 2. (a1), which conforms to a normal distribution. The median particle size D50 is 37.50 μm, and the particle size range is 0.396–428.7 μm. The microstructure of ultrafine tobacco powder with different magnifications is shown in Fig. 2. (b1) and Fig. 2. (b2). The complex, hierarchical architecture of the ultrafine powder—comprising irregular clusters with multi-level structures including blocks, sheets, tubes, and their composites—dramatically increases its specific surface area by introducing extensive external nanoscale roughness and, more importantly, a highly porous internal network. This intricate system of micropores, mesotubes, and tortuous bends creates a vast interfacial area (2.337 m²/g), providing an abundance of active sites for physical and chemical adsorption. The high surface energy of these nanostructured surfaces enhances interactions with adsorbates, significantly improving adsorption capacity by facilitating greater molecular contact and stronger binding through van der Waals forces, capillary condensation, and surface complexation35.

The microstructure of the base sheet and core material products prepared by the UFP process is shown in Fig. 2. (c3) and (c4), which differs significantly from the conventional process (corresponding base sheet and products are shown in Fig. 2. (c1) and Fig. 2. (c2), respectively). In the UFP process, there are a large number of ultrafine powder particles (mainly smaller particles) adsorbed onto the fiber backbone; At the same time, there are also large-sized ultrafine powder particles wrapped by small fibers near and on the surface of the fiber backbone. It is speculated that there are two main reasons for this phenomenon. Firstly, the fine fibers that have been spun into fibers are more prone to mechanical entanglement with ultrafine powder clusters due to their size; Secondly, the exposed hydroxyl groups and binding sites of the fine fibers in the process of filamentation are more abundant, making it easier for them to bind with the functional groups on the surface of ultrafine powders.

The results of scanning electron microscopy, particle size distribution detection, and specific surface area testing reveal that ultrafine tobacco powder exhibits irregular particle structures of varying sizes with a limited amount of pore structure. Its specific surface area measures 2.337 m2/g, indicating a larger available surface area for the adsorption of other substances.

The microstructure of the finished core material closely resembles that of the substrate, however, there are notable disparities in microstructure between the base web and the finished core material prepared using conventional processes versus UFP processes. In the UFP process, ultrafine tobacco powder particles adhere to, intertwine with, and integrate into the fibers.

Physical Indicators of Base web and Core Materials.

The physical properties of the base web exert a significant influence on the physical characteristics of the final core material, and there exists a strong correlation among various physical parameters. With regard to the intrinsic attributes of the core material itself, high density and thermal conductivity are advantageous for promoting heat transfer and smoke release during the heating process. According to experimental findings, the density and thermal conductivity of the final core material fabricated via UFP process exhibit an increase of 18.2% and 40.8%, respectively, compared to those produced by conventional methods. The tensile strength and elongation rate significantly impact the processing resistance of subsequent core materials, such as winding and cutting. According to indicator testing results, the tensile strength and elongation rate of core material products prepared by UFP process have increased by 32.9% and 17.2%, respectively, compared to conventional processes. High air permeability is not conducive to the heat conduction of the core material during heating. Based on indicator test results, the air permeability of core material products prepared by UFP process is much lower than that of conventional processes.

The drastically reduced particle size enables much tighter particle packing within the fibrous matrix, significantly increasing the sheet density by minimizing void spaces. This compaction simultaneously drastically reduces air permeability by creating a more tortuous path for gas flow. This low permeability is critically beneficial for heating performance, as it minimizes convective heat loss and channels thermal energy more efficiently into heating the substrate itself rather than dissipating into the air, thereby improving overall thermal transfer efficiency. The enormous increase in specific surface area of the UFP provides a greater number of potential binding sites. This leads to enhanced interfacial adhesion—through both mechanical interlocking and physicochemical bonds (e.g., hydrogen bonding)—between the particles and the fibers. This strengthened internal cohesion is the primary mechanism for the significantly improved tensile strength and elongation at break, as stress is distributed more effectively across a greater number of robust bonds, delaying failure. Furthermore, the combination of high density and the extensive network of inter-particle contacts created continuous pathways for heat transfer. This reduced thermal contact resistance within the structure, coupled with the decreased heat capacity of the air being replaced by solid material, results in a substantial enhancement in thermal conductivity, facilitating more uniform and efficient heat transfer during the heating process36.

The order of the contact angle at the sixth second is in the following order: No-UFP 1# > No-UFP 2# > UFP 1# > UFP 2#. And based on the area accumulation chart Fig. 4. (d5) and trend chart Fig. 4. (d6), it can be seen that the rate of decrease in contact angle is in the following order: UFP 2# > UFP 1# > No-UFP 2# > No-UFP 1#. Generally, a smaller contact angle indicates better wettability/ hydrophilicity of the base web, and a faster decrease in contact angle indicates a faster water absorption rate of the base web over time37. The magnitude of the curve slope/rate can simultaneously simulate the dynamic permeation curve of the base web with time for coating liquid which is shown in Fig. 4. (d6). The base web made from UFP pre-treated raw materials has the following advantages compared to No-UFP base web: stronger hydrophilic properties; faster absorption rate of concentrate in unit time; can be closer to the saturated carrying state of the base web for concentrate in a shorter time; as time goes on, it can carry a larger amount of liquid from non-saturated state to saturated state. The enhanced hydrophilicity of the base sheet produced via the ultrafine powder (UFP) process, compared to that from conventional methods, is primarily attributed to the substantially increased specific surface area and the greater exposure of hydrophilic functional groups. The drastically reduced particle size in UFP creates a vastly larger interfacial area. This not only amplifies the overall surface energy but also significantly increases the availability of polar sites, such as hydroxyl (-OH) and carboxyl (-COOH) groups, which are inherent to the tobacco-derived material, for interaction with water molecules. Furthermore, the intricate micro- and nano-scale porous network formed by the UFP facilitates capillary action, which actively wicks and retains moisture, thereby markedly improving the wettability and overall hydrophilic character of the base sheet. Therefore, the base web made from UFP pre-processed raw materials is more favorable for the main process of reconstituted tobacco at the coating stage.

Analysis of volatile aroma components in finished core materials

Although the conventional chemical indicators of the core material prepared by UFP process do not exhibit significant differences from those of the conventional process, there is a substantial variation in the content of volatile aroma components which is showed in Fig. 5. (e1-e2). The total amount of volatile aroma components in the core material prepared by UFP process is 21.2% higher than that of the conventional process, and the total content and categories of volatile aroma components also surpasses that of the conventional process which are showed in Fig. 5. (e2). Upon analysis, it is attributed to the utilization of ultrafine tobacco powder as raw material in the UFP process, with most plant cell diameters falling within 10 μm and 30 μm. After undergoing ultrafine grinding treatment, the tobacco cells undergo wall breakage, leading to the release of effective substances contained within the cells. This ultimately results in an increase in both the total amount and quantity of volatile aroma components present in the core material product. The augmentation of volatile aroma components within the core material is advantageous for enhancing sensory quality.

Thermogravimetric analysis of finished core materials

From the Fig. 6, it can be seen that within the range of 224–378 ℃, this is the release stage of the main aroma of heated tobacco products. The total weight loss of the UFP process sample shown in Fig. 6. (f2) is 37.89%, while the total weight loss of the conventional process sample shown in Fig. 6. (f1) is 30.40%. The difference in thermal weight loss of the sample is significant, and the mass change of the conventional process is smaller than that of the UFP process, with a difference of about 8% in mass change. It can be inferred that in this temperature range, the UFP process sample can release more aroma substances; Within the temperature range above 378 ℃, there is no significant difference in the quality changes of the two samples.

(f1) TG and DTG curves of sample without UFP process core material, (f2) TG and DTG curves of sample with UFP process core material.

From the DTG curve, it can be seen that there is a slight difference in the maximum weight loss rate of different process samples within 100 ℃, which is speculated to be related to the different moisture content of the samples; Peaks were observed in samples from different processes within the temperature range of 180–190 ℃, but the numerical differences were not significant; Within the temperature range of 400 ℃, the maximum weight loss rate of conventional processes shown in Fig. 6. (f1) occurs at about 296 ℃, while the maximum weight loss rate of UFP processes shown in Fig. 6. (f2) occurs at about 287 ℃. At this temperature, the main pyrolysis processes are cellulose, lignin, volatile aroma compounds, and some polysaccharides. Based on the peak area of the DTG curve and the maximum weight loss rate, it can be inferred that the pyrolysis rate of substances in UFP processes is higher than that in conventional processes at this stage, and the temperature shifts forward. The higher maximum mass loss rate and lower peak temperature observed in the UFP-processed base material are direct consequences of its optimized physical structure. The significantly larger specific surface area provides more active sites for thermal decomposition, enabling a faster overall reaction rate. Simultaneously, the superior thermal conductivity ensures rapid and uniform heat transfer throughout the material, allowing the entire sample to reach its decomposition temperature more synchronously. This efficient heat distribution reduces the need for localized overheating, explaining the earlier onset of major mass loss. Furthermore, the lower air permeability of the UFP structure temporarily restricts the escape of volatile decomposition products. This confinement promotes secondary cracking reactions and increases local pressure, which further accelerates the primary decomposition process, contributing to the sharper and more intense mass loss peak.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a significant advancement in heated tobacco core material production through the development of an ultrafine powder (UFP)-based reconstituted tobacco (RT) substrate. Compared to conventional paper-based RT, the UFP-based material exhibits superior functional properties, including enhanced tensile strength (965 vs. 726 N/m), elongation (2.73 vs. 2.33%), density (0.78 vs. 0.66 g/cm³), thermal conductivity (0.074 vs. 0.053 W/(m·K)), and smoothness (13 vs. 3 S), alongside reduced thickness (0.113 vs. 0.144 mm) and air permeability (15 vs. 84 CU). These improvements contribute to better mechanical processability, enhanced heat transfer, and increased retention of volatile flavor compounds (21.2% higher). Thermal analysis revealed more efficient aerosol generation, evidenced by a higher total mass loss (37.89% vs. 30.40%) and a lower peak mass loss temperature (287 vs. 296 °C). The UFP-based process effectively addresses key limitations of traditional papermaking methods and provides a promising foundation for the design of next-generation heated tobacco products.
