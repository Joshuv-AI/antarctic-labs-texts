Comprehensive analysis of small GTPases in Microsporidia reveals new extremes in eukaryotic cell biology
Authors: Romana Vargová, Vladislava Majnušová, Marek Eliáš
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicrosporidia are a fungi-related rapidly evolving lineage of obligate intracellular parasites with poorly known cell biology. Cellular processes in eukaryotes heavily rely on a group of proteins known as the Ras GTPase superfamily, so a comprehensive analysis of these proteins in Microsporidia is expected to provide fundamental insights into the microsporidian cell biology. Adopting a complex bioinformatic approach to cope with the high divergence of microsporidian genes, we reconstructed the evolutionary history of the Ras superfamily of GTPases in Microsporidia and their closest relatives.ResultsOur results demonstrate that gene loss along the whole microsporidian phylogeny has been a vastly dominating factor shaping the Ras superfamily gene complements in extant Microsporidia. This trend has most massively affected Hepatospora eriocheir with a mere 12 Ras superfamily genes, the smallest number recorded so far for an autonomously reproducing eukaryote. Also notable is the loss of the virtually ubiquitous Rab GTPase Rab5 and its dedicated regulators in two different microsporidian lineages, or the Rho family GTPases of most Microsporidia having lost C-terminal prenylation. Most unexpectedly, two different microsporidian species lack detectable orthologs of the beta subunit of the signal recognition particle (SRP) receptor, a loss unknown from any other eukaryote. Strikingly, the loss of SRβ is apparently compensated for by the SRP receptor alpha subunit of these species uniquely possessing a predicted transmembrane domain, potentially anchoring the protein in the ER membrane.ConclusionsOur findings thus define new extremes in the impact of reductive evolution on core processes of the eukaryotic cell.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02639-y.Keywords: Cell regulation, Cryptomycota, Extreme divergence, GTPases, Membrane trafficking, Microsporidia, Protein evolution, Ras superfamily, Rozella, SRP receptor

Background

Microsporidia constitute a group of > 1400 known species of parasitic unicellular eukaryotes with the ability to infect a wide range of eukaryotes, predominantly animals [1, 2]. Their biology has been shaped by their intracellular parasitic lifestyle in two different directions: reduction of various eukaryote-specific features on the one hand and acquisition of cellular and molecular traits facilitating their parasitic lifestyle on the other [3]. As a result, Microsporidia possess a noncanonical Golgi apparatus, reduced genome-less mitochondrion-derived mitosomes unable to produce ATP, non-flagellated infectious spores, and a specific infection apparatus, the polar tube (or polar filament), through which the content of the spore penetrates the host cell. A similar pattern is noticeable also at the genomic level [4–6]. Genome sequencing projects for numerous species from across the group have established that the ancestors of Microsporidia underwent massive gene and genome reduction resulting in a small number of genes retained. The genes are generally shorter compared to their eukaryotic orthologs and their sequences are highly divergent due to an accelerated evolutionary rate [4, 6, 7]. On the other hand, the adaptation to intracellular parasitic lifestyle is intertwined with the acquisition of new genes, with contribution of horizontal gene transfer and presumably, by de novo birth from non-coding sequences [4, 8, 9]. It is also likely that some apparently novel genes are in reality ancestral genes transmogrified beyond recognition.

For some time thought to represent a deeply branching eukaryote lineage primitively lacking mitochondria, peroxisomes or the Golgi apparatus [10, 11], Microsporidia were eventually reconsidered as extremely divergent fungi-related eukaryotes with the unusual cell organization resulting from secondary simplification [12, 13]. Subsequent phylogenetic studies showed that the endoparasitic genus Rozella, originally thought to belong to the fungal group Chytridiomycota, is related to Microsporidia, and these taxa together form a lineage sister to all (other) Fungi [14]. The Rozellida + Microsporidia (RM) clade is now robustly established by phylogenomic analyses [15–18] and its composition has expanded by identifying additional members phylogenetically and phenotypically placed in between Rozella and Microsporidia in their traditional sense, including intracellular parasites of crustaceans (Mitosporidium daphniae [19]) and amoebae (such as Paramicrosporidium saccamoebae [20–22]). Molecular data also confirmed the long-suspected specific relationship of canonical Microsporidia and Metchnikovellida, hyperparasites of gregarines [23, 24]. Furthermore, environmental DNA studies have indicated a tremendous undescribed and uncultured phylogenetic diversity of the RM clade [25–28]. It remains unsettled whether the RM clade (recently proposed to be renamed Opisthophagea; [29]) should be viewed as a protist lineage related to fungi or instead classified as a bona fide fungal lineage [30, 31]. Once thought to be related to the RM clade [32], the group of algal parasitoids called Aphelidea has now been robustly resolved as a lineage more closely related to Fungi (sensu stricto) than to the RM clade [17, 29, 33], and hence is not systematically analysed in this study.

Somewhat regrettably, the exploration of the phylogenetic neighbourhood of Microsporidia has blurred the concept of this group and introduced a highly confusing and inconsistent taxonomic nomenclature. Firstly, the terms Rozellida (sensu Lara et al. [25]), Cryptomycota [26], Rozellomycota [20, 34], and Rozellosporidia [35] all seem to refer to essentially the same paraphyletic subsection of the RM clade excluding Microsporidia (in their traditional sense). Secondly, Bass et al. [27] proposed to expand the term Microsporidia to embrace those “traditional” members of Cryptomycota (or Rozellomycota) that share some phenotypic features with Microsporidia in the traditional sense and are more closely related to them than to Rozella. The term “SB-Microsporidia” was introduced by these authors as a designation of the basal paraphyletic assemblage of short-branching representatives of the expanded Microsporidia, including the genome-sequences representatives M. daphniae and P. saccamoebae. For the lack of a more satisfying taxonomic arrangement, we have adopted the broader concept of Microsporidia advocated by Bass et al., modified by replacing their term “canonical Microsporidia” with the recently proposed formal taxonomic name Eumicrosporea [34]. The identification of a number of lineages with a progressively more distant relationship to Eumicrosporea offers an opportunity for tracing the evolutionary trajectory towards this highly peculiar group of eukaryotes [3]. In addition to cytological investigations, genome sequencing has been employed to provide data for comparative analyses from relatives of Eumicrosporea, so far having targeted two metchnikovellids [23, 24], two species of SB-Microsporidia [16, 19], and three species of the genus Rozella [15, 38]. Exploration of these resources has helped to define key differences between individual subgroups of the RM clade. For example, rozellids as well as SB-Microsporidia have retained more or less conventional genome-bearing aerobic mitochondria, whereas metchnikovellids seem to possess genome-lacking mitosomes like Eumicrosporea. On the other hand, spores of SB-Microsporidia lack a flagellum and exhibit cellular structures reminiscent of polar filaments found in Eumicrosporea (and in a rudimentary form also in metchnikovellids), whereas Rozella species are disseminated by uniflagellated wall-less zoospores lacking any apparent polar filament equivalent [27]. Comparative genomic analyses have yielded a global picture of the gene content evolution in the RM clade, highlighting continued massive gene loss along the whole evolutionary lineage from the ancestor of the RM clade to the ancestor of Eumicrosporea [24]. However, attention has been uneven when it comes to specific aspects of the biology of the RM clade members. For instance, the evolution of metabolic capabilities in the RM clade is understood in some detail [17, 39], but much less has been learned about the evolutionary diversification of the basic cellular infrastructure in this group.

Here we set to contribute to the evolutionary cell biology of Microsporidia by analysing in detail a particular group of proteins known as the Ras superfamily of GTPases (often called “small GTPases”). Our focus not only reflects our long-term interest in the evolution of this vastly diversified group of genes in eukaryotes in general: characterizing the Ras superfamily complement of particular taxa provides a proxy to the complexity of cellular processes of the organisms studied. Functioning as molecular switches with the ability to integrate multiple regulatory inputs and to interact with diverse effector proteins and complexes, Ras superfamily GTPases play key parts in a plethora of major cellular processes including membrane trafficking, cytoskeleton dynamics, cellular signalling or flagellum biogenesis [40–42]. Eukaryotes vary substantially in the size and composition of their Ras superfamily gene complements, with tens to hundreds of paralogs encoded by individual genomes. This variation stems from both differential retention of paralogs that have been present already in early eukaryotes, including the last eukaryotic common ancestor (LECA), and lineage-specific family expansions [43–46]. Therefore, studying Ras superfamily GTPases can provide fundamental information not only about the cellular processes of the organism in question, but also about the evolution of particular cellular systems of larger taxonomic groups.

Despite the central role of the Ras superfamily in the eukaryotic cell biology, essentially nothing has been published about this gene group in Microsporidia. Taking advantage of published genome assemblies spanning the RM clade, from rozellids to the Eumicrosporea, we fill in this noticeable gap and provide a comprehensive account of this important protein group in one of the most fascinating corners of the eukaryote phylogeny. We point to methodological challenges posed by the highly divergent nature of microsporidian genes and present a set of strategies that can provide a general solution to analyses of other groups of genes and other rapidly evolving organisms. Crucially, the well-curated catalogue of microsporidian Ras superfamily genes we have built will not only serve as a useful resource for future functional studies but also provides interesting biological insights on its own, pointing to previously unrealized extremes of eukaryotic cell biology that Microsporidia can reach.

Results and discussion

A combination of approaches to annotate Ras superfamily genes in Microsporidia

Considering taxa with genome sequence available at the time when this study was initiated, we selected for analysis 31 members of the RM clade (Table S1), which can be conveniently divided into four subgroups (see also Fig. 1): R. allomycis (a representative of Rozellida sensu Bass et al. [27]), SB-Microsporidia (represented by Paramicrosporidium saccamoebae and Mitosporidium daphniae), metchnikovellids represented by Amphiamblys sp. WSBS2006 and Metchnikovella incurvata, and Eumicrosporea comprising the remaining 26 species in our dataset. Only at a later stage we also annotated and added to some analyses genes from two more recently sequenced rozellids (Rozella multimorpha and Rozella sp. PSC023; [38]). Using the program BLAST and its variants complemented with profile HMM-based searching methods, we identified 641 Ras superfamily genes in the target organisms (for a simplified flowchart of our procedure see Fig. S1A). Each gene model was manually checked and curated whenever necessary using appropriate genome assembly and/or transcriptomic data (if available). Close inspection of the data revealed multiple genome sequencing or assembly errors, which were corrected using raw sequencing data (Table S2). While Ras superfamily genes of rozellids and SB-Microsporidia typically contain one or more introns, no introns were found in any of the genes from Eumicrosporea and metchnikovellids, in accord with the generally very low intron content in the genomes of these two taxa [23, 24, 47].

Each sequence was preliminarily assigned to a particular ancestral pan-eukaryotic paralog based on the results of BLASTp similarity searches against a custom protein sequence database (see “Methods”). Virtually all sequences from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids could be readily annotated based on the BLASTp search results, and the assignment of the majority of them was afterwards verified by phylogenetic analyses (for details see “Methods”). However, annotation of the genes from the Eumicrosporea was less straightforward. The Ras superfamily in this taxon is represented by 16 different groups of orthologs (Fig. 2, Table S2), but only ten of them could be assigned to respective ancestral pan-eukaryotic paralogs by the BLASTp searches with reasonable confidence; these were Rab1, Rab5, Rab6, Rab11, Ran, Rho1, Arl2, Arl8, Sar1, and SRβ. The remaining groups of Ras superfamily genes in Eumicrosporea were initially assigned only to broader families within the superfamily, presumably due to their divergence. These enigmatic groups included one Rab family gene, two Rho family genes, and three Arf family genes.

We then used various bioinformatics methods and data from close relatives in order to infer the most likely origin of these genes (details are described in “Methods” and Fig. S1B). Briefly, phylogenetic analyses with a set of pan-eukaryotic homologs and/or with sequences from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids provided conclusive results only for a minority of the enigmatic genes. Hence, we proceeded with an approach based on profile HMM-HMM comparisons using HHsearch, whereby we created a profile HMM for each group of eumicrosporean orthologs and compared it against a collection of profile HMMs representing all ancestral groups of orthologs known to be present in the LECA (in the case of Rab and Arf families) or in the ancestor of the RM clade (Rho family). In addition, we constructed a sequence logo of each elusive eumicrosporean Ras superfamily group and compared it to sequence logos of pan-eukaryotic Ras superfamily orthologous groups, separately constructed for sets of corresponding sequences from non-eumicrosporean RM clade members and from reference eukaryotes outside the RM clade. Combining all these results, we were able to define the most likely origins of virtually all divergent genes and to delineate the evolution of the Ras superfamily from the common ancestor of rozellids and Microsporidia up to extant species.

An important aim of our study was to reconstruct the history of losses of Ras superfamily genes in the RM clade, which obviously depends on our ability to accurately determine the presence/absence pattern of the genes in the taxa studied. Hence, we exerted a maximal effort to minimize the risk that we missed particular genes due to whatever technical or methodological reasons (see Methods for details). To this end we relied not only on existing genome annotations (predicted protein sequence sets), which do not always capture all the proteins encoded by the given genome, but also analysed the nucleotide genome sequences by tBLASTn searches and conceptual translations of all open reading frames above 100 bp present in the target genomes. In several cases we also reassembled available raw sequencing reads or searched them directly to verify the absence of particular genes. Another challenge is posed by the extreme sequence divergence of microsporidian genes. However, the evolution of the GTPase domain shared by the Ras superfamily proteins is generally constrained by its functional requirements, such as guanine nucleotide binding, particularly across the main families including Rab, Rho, Ras, Arf, and Gpa. We did experience difficulties when searching for the eumicrosporean SRβ protein (see below), but this Ras superfamily member is rather exceptional, being poorly conserved at the sequence level even between less rapidly evolving eukaryotes. We, therefore, assume that our careful searches employing the sensitive HMM-based methodology are likely to have identified most Ras superfamily members in Microsporidia, while those that we have possibly missed are so much derived that they are unlikely to have the same function as their evolutionary precursors or are part of the genomic regions not covered by the available sequence data.

Extreme reduction of the Rab family gene complement in Microsporidia

The central function of Rab family members is the regulation of the endomembrane trafficking, although some have also been implicated in cell signalling or cilium-associated functions. The LECA was endowed with more than 20 Rab family genes (Rabs) that were then repeatedly lost or duplicated throughout the eukaryote evolution [45, 48]. The most recent investigation of the Rab family on a broad eukaryote scale inferred the stem lineage of Fungi to have harboured at least 13 Rabs [45], but the analysis did not include any member of the RM clade. Interestingly, the P. saccamoebae genome encodes a representative of an ancestral Rab paralog undetected in Fungi, Rab20 (Table S2, Fig. S2). The majority of Eumicrosporea possess five Rab family genes (Fig. 2), four of which were tentatively annotated by the BLAST-based approach as Rab1, Rab5, Rab6, and Rab11. BLAST searches with individual sequences representing the fifth Rab gene yielded inconsistent results, with the most similar non-eumicrosporean hits representing a mixture of different paralogs (frequently corresponding to Rab8 or its Metazoa-specific in-paralogs in addition to Rab1 and other Rab paralogs). Notably, the Rab complement of each SB-microsporidian and metchnikovellid includes one Rab gene lacking an obvious ortholog in Eumicrosporea, yet clearly orthologous to Rab8 in other eukaryotes (Fig. S2). We reasoned that the enigmatic eumicrosporean Rab is most parsimoniously interpreted as orthologous to Rab8 (which is also consistent with Rab8 being the closest relative of Rab1 [45]). An alternative hypothesis on the origin of this Rab requires invoking loss of Rab8 in the eumicrosporean stem lineage and duplication of some of the remaining Rab genes followed by extreme divergence of one of the copies. Still another possibility is that the enigmatic Rab found in Eumicrosporea is a highly divergent ortholog of a pan-eukaryotic Rab family member absent from SB-Microsporidia and metchnikovellids, which would however require three independent loss events (separately in P. saccamoebae, M. daphniae, and metchnikovellid lineages, given their paraphyletic branching pattern with respect to Eumicrosporea).

To possibly distinguish between these alternatives, we performed a phylogenetic analysis of the whole RM clade Rab family dataset, including eumicrosporean sequences together with Rab and Ran family sequences from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids. The result (Fig. 3, Fig. S3) confirmed the BLAST-based annotations of Rab5, Rab6, and Rab11. However, the putative eumicrosporean Rab1 sequences formed a lineage sister to a broader cluster combining Rab1, Rab8, and Rab18 from other RM clade members. The fifth eumicrosporean Rab orthogroup (the putative Rab8) branched off in an unresolved position. Manual inspection of the sequence logos of putative Rab1 and Rab8 from Eumicrosporea and sequence logos from pan-eukaryotic Rab family proteins revealed several positions supporting the assignment of the enigmatic eumicrosporean genes as Rab1 and Rab8, although never conclusively (Fig. S4). On the other hand, comparing with HHsearch the profile HMMs of the eumicrosporean candidate Rab1 and Rab8 groups to profile HMMs representing all individual ancestral eukaryotic Rab paralogs retrieved Rab1 and Rab8 as the respective best hits (Table S3). Hence, identification of the two eumicrosporean Rab groups as Rab1 and Rab8 orthologs is the most likely interpretation of the data at hand.

The Rab family complement frequently amounts to tens of genes in a eukaryote species [45], with the smallest number reported so far for an autonomously propagated eukaryote being five in the multicellular red alga Neopyropia (previously Pyropia) yezoensis [49]. When viewed in the context of the RM clade phylogeny, the distribution of the different Rab genes in extant taxa indicates a massive reduction of the Rab family complement in the earliest nodes of the microsporidian phylogeny, with no further losses marking the emergence of Eumicrosporea and with only a single additional loss within a particular eumicrosporean lineage (Fig. 4). The set of five Rab paralogs ancestral for Eumicrosporea and characteristic for most of their extant members overlaps only partly with that in N. yezoensis, as this alga has retained Rab7 and Rab18 yet lacks Rab5 and Rab8. An even lesser number of Rab genes — three (two Rab1 in-paralogs and Rab2) — occurs in the kinetoplastid Perkinsela sp. CCAP 1560/4, an obligate vertically inherited endosymbiont of Paramoeba pemaquidensis not known to exist in any form outside the host [50, 51]. Hence, the microsporidian Hepatospora eriocheir, which in contrast to other Eumicrosporea lacks Rab5 and has thus only four Rabs preserved, pushes the reduction of the Rab gene complement to a new extreme for an autonomously reproducing eukaryote.

Interestingly, Rab5 is lacking not only in H. eriocheir, but also in metchnikovellids (Fig. 2). Rab5 is a key regulator of the endocytic pathways [56] and is nearly universally conserved in eukaryotes, with its absence so far reported only from Giardia intestinalis and red algae [45, 49]. To cast more light onto the absence of Rab5 in different microsporidian lineages, we searched the RM clade genomes for proteins with the VPS9 domain known to function as guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) specific for Rab5 and the closely related paralogs (not found in the RM clade) Rab17, Rab21, and Rab22 [57]. A previous broad survey of genes for VPS9 domain-containing proteins in eukaryotes (omitting any representative of the RM clade) identified at least one such gene in all species investigated, with the notable exceptions of G. intestinalis and rhodophytes, i.e., eukaryotes lacking Rab5 or the other Rab paralogs regulated by the VPS9 domain [57]. Our search for VPS9 domain-encoding genes in the RM clade representatives revealed at least one gene in all taxa, with three notable exceptions: H. eriocheir and metchnikovellids, which is unsurprising given the loss of Rab5 in these organisms, but also all species of the genus Nematocida (in Eumicrosporea; Table S4). While we cannot completely rule out the possibility that VPS9 domain-containing proteins are present in Nematocida spp. yet diverged beyond recognition by our HMM-based identification strategy, it is noteworthy that the Rab5 sequences from this microsporidian genus are much more divergent than any other RM clade Rab5 sequence (note the extremely long stem branch of the Nematocida Rab5 group in our phylogenetic tree of the Rab family; Fig. S3). This indicates acceleration of Rab5 sequence evolution in the Nematocida lineage, coinciding and presumably causally linked to the apparent loss of the VPS9 domain. How guanine nucleotide exchange on Nematocida Rab5 is catalysed in the absence of the only known GEF for this Rab class is an interesting question for future research.

Apart from Rab5, the metchnikovellid Amphiamblys sp. WSBS2006 possibly lacks also Rab6, as ascertained even by searching raw sequencing reads (Fig. 2). The presumptive Rab6 loss in this organism is notable, as the related M. incurvata does have this Rab gene and Rab6 absence has so far been noticed only in G. intestinalis [45] and Perkinsela sp. [51]. Furthermore, all Microsporidia, including both SB-Microsporidia members with genome sequences available, consistently lack Rab7, which again is a nearly ubiquitous ancient Rab family member so far known to be missing only from G. intestinalis [45]. Analogously to investigating the correspondence of the distribution of Rab5 and its dedicated GEF, we tried to find out whether the loss of Rab6 or Rab7 within the RM clade similarly correlates with the loss of the corresponding GEFs, namely the Rgp1-Ric1 complex (Rab6-specific GEF) and the Mon1-Ccz1 complex (Rab7-specific GEF [58]). Unfortunately, although not that unexpectedly, we found out that the respective proteins tend to be poorly conserved in members of the RM clade and their presence or absence in most of the species cannot be established with any confidence even when using sensitive HMM searches (data not shown). Altogether, the limited complement of Rab GTPases in Microsporidia, especially in Eumicrosporea and metchnikovellids, indicates a high degree of simplification of the membrane trafficking in these organisms, in particular affecting the endocytic pathway (Fig. 5).

Loss of prenylation of Rho family GTPases in most Microsporidia

The typical members of the Rho family are Rho (proper), Rac1, and Cdc42 studied especially in Metazoa and Fungi [60, 61]. These proteins have been implicated in the regulation of the cytoskeleton organization, cell cycle, cell polarization, migration, and gene expression [62, 63]. Rho family genes in many lineages exhibit a high propensity for duplication resulting in a large number of diversified paralogs, which poses a challenge for phylogenetic analyses of the family [60, 64]. The last common ancestor of opisthokonts possessed at least three Rho family members, Rac1, Cdc42, and Rho (proper), with the latter having undergone duplication most likely before the radiation of Fungi (sensu stricto), resulting in fungal paralogs called Rho1 and Rho4 [60]. Our analyses indicate that the ancestor of the RM clade most likely possessed three Rho family genes, Rac1, Cdc42, and a gene we label Rho1 (Figs. 2, 4, Table S2; see Methods for details concerning the annotation). Whether the latter is strictly orthologous to the fungal Rho1 while Rho4 is secondarily missing in the RM clade, or whether the Rho1/Rho4 paralogous pair emerged only after the divergence of the RM clade, could not be conclusively resolved. The Rac1, Cdc42 and Rho1 gene set has been retained by rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids (with Rho1 duplicated in some rozellids; Fig. 4, Fig. S5).

Although three orthologous groups of Rho family genes were likewise identified in Eumicrosporea, matching them to the genes present in other RM clade members proved difficult. While one group could be preliminarily annotated as Rho1, the other two groups defied a specific annotation when the BLAST-based analysis was employed. Phylogenetic analysis of eumicrosporean Rho family genes and their closest relatives divided the sequences analysed into two branches: Rho1-like and Rac1/Cdc42-like (Fig. 6). In our phylogenetic analysis, the Eumicrosporea Rho-like genes constituted three separate clades intermingled with Rho1 sequences from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids. One of the clusters, denoted Rho1a, comprising sequences preliminarily annotated as Rho1, included representatives from all eumicrosporeans analysed, whereas the other two clusters included a different paralog from each eumicrosporean (except for Edhazardia aedis lacking this gene), denoted Rho1b. The Rho1b sequences segregated into a clade restricted to sequences from the basally branching genus Nematocida and a clade comprised of sequences from the other eumicrosporeans. However, low support values for the branching order within the Rho-like branch allow for assuming the most parsimonious interpretation, i.e. that the genes in these two clades are orthologous (i.e. derived from the same ancestral gene present in the common ancestor of all Eumicrosporea) despite not forming a clade in the inferred tree. Nearly all Cdc42 and Rac1 proteins from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids formed sister groups to the exclusion of the Rac1 from M. daphniae and third Rho family group of eumicrosporean orthologs, which were, therefore, preliminarily called Rac1/Cdc42.

Construction and close inspection of sequence logos of Rac1/Cdc42 from Eumicrosporea and Rho family proteins from closely related taxa and other Holomycota species revealed that the eumicrosporean Rac1/Cdc42 sequences exhibit a sequence pattern at two conserved positions that support their evolutionary derivation from Rac1 rather than Cdc42 (Fig. S6). In particular, they share a tryptophan residue conserved in the G3 GTPase motif of Rac1 sequences but substituted by another amino acid in Cdc42. Similarly, the majority of eumicrosporean Rac1/Cdc42 sequences harbour a lysin residue in the G4 GTPase motif that is also shared with Rac1 sequences. We note that the sequence pattern distinguishing Rac1 from Cdc42 is plesiomorphic and shared by the majority of Rho1 (as well as Rho2, Rho3, and Rho4) sequences, so it is critical to consider that phylogenetic analysis excludes the possibility of the putative eumicrosporean Rac1 to be more closely related to Rho1 than to Rac1 (Fig. 6). HHsearch supported the annotation of this gene as Rac1, since the profile HMM constructed from eumicrosporean Rac1/Cdc42 sequences retrieved as the best hit the Rac1 profile HMM (Table S3). Synthesizing these results, we infer that Cdc42 was presumably lost in the ancestor of Eumicrosporea, whereas the Rho1 gene underwent duplication resulting in two paralogs, Rho1a and Rho1b (Fig. 4). The Rho1 duplication has preserved the overall ancestral complexity of Rho-based signalling (at least in terms of the number of different GTPase variants) in most extant eumicrosporeans, except for a few lineages with additional Rho family streamlining (Fig. 4).

Apart from their divergence in the GTPase domain itself, Rho family proteins in Microsporidia turned out to have undergone a notable transformation also in their C-terminal tail, which mediates interaction of the proteins with membranes. Rho proteins characteristically possess a cysteine residue, to which a farnesyl or geranylgeranyl moiety is covalently attached post-translationally and serves as a membrane anchor. The respective enzymes, i.e., farnesyltransferase (FTase) or geranylgeranyltransferase I (GGTase I), typically prenylate substrates that possess the characteristic CaaX box (with “a” standing for an aliphatic residue) at the very C-terminus of the protein, although recent work suggests that the requirement for this sequence motif is not strict and cysteine prenylation can occur also in the context of a Caa or CaaaX motif [65, 66]. Rho1, Rac1, and Cdc42 from metazoans and fungi exhibit the typical C-terminal prenylation motif consistent with all being prenylated [61], as is the ancestral state of the Rho family in general. Our analysis shows the same holds for Rho family members of rozellids and SB-Microsporidia, but some tinkering with the mechanism of membrane attachment has evidently occurred in Rho family evolution in metchnikovellids and most Eumicrosporea, as instead of a CaaX box they usually have at the C-terminus two or more cysteine residues predicted as palmitoylated (Fig. S7, Table S5, Table S6). A notable exception is the Rho1b paralog from three species of the eumicrosporean genus Nematocida, which possesses a CaaX box predicted as a farnesylation target.

These observations prompted us to investigate the conservation of the prenylation machinery in Microsporidia and relatives. FTase and GGTase I are heterodimers that share the same alpha subunit but are differentiated by enzyme-specific beta subunits [67]. Using HMM-based searches we found the alpha subunit in rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and the metchnikovellid Amphiamblys sp. WSBS2006, but among Eumicrosporea the protein seems to be present only in the genera Nematocida and Hamiltosporidium (Table S7). This occurrence is paralleled by the distribution of the FTase-specific beta subunit, which was additionally found in the metchnikovellid M. incurvata, suggesting that the apparent absence of the FTase/GGTase I alpha subunit in this species is most likely due to incompleteness of the available genome assembly. These findings indicate that protein farnesylation has been retained by only some lineages of Microsporidia and lost at least three times independently by different Eumicrosporea lineages (Fig. S8). Furthermore, the GGTase I-specific beta subunit is restricted to rozellids among all RM clade organisms analysed. This suggests that Microsporidia (sensu lato) are devoid of protein geranylgeranylation, except for geranylgeranylation of Rab proteins, which is mediated by a specific geranylgeranyltransferase II (GGTase II; [67]). We found genes for both GGTase II subunits (alpha and beta) in the majority of RM clade members, with notable exceptions of metchnikovellids and H. eriocheir (Table S7). Our inability to identify both GGTase II subunits in both metchnikovellid species examined and two different strains of H. eriocheir is puzzling, as their Rab GTPases do possess C-terminal cysteine residues and most likely are geranylgeranylated, as this modification is crucial for the normal Rab functioning.

The complete loss of protein prenylation (except for the Rab-specific geranylgeranylation) here uncovered to have affected most Eumicrosporea is, to our knowledge, unprecedented among eukaryotes and further underscores the extreme reduction of the basic cellular infrastructure in this group. The retention of FTase in Nematocida spp. correlates with one of their Rho family proteins being predicted as farnesylated (see above), whereas there is no candidate farnesylation target among Ras superfamily proteins in Hamiltosporidium; which other proteins are farnesylated in the latter eumicrosporidian is to be determined in the future. As detailed below, metchnikovellids (in contrast to Eumicrosporea) have retained an ortholog of Rap1, a member of the Ras family, which, like the Rho family, also commonly contains a prenylated CaaX box [68]. However, the Rap1 protein from both metchnikovellids lacks a CaaX box, in contrast to the conventional Rap1 from rozellids and SB-Microsporidia, and instead possesses cysteine residues predicted to be palmitoylated (Fig. S7). Hence, like in Hamiltosporidium, the FTase in metchnikovellids serves in modifying currently unknown protein targets from outside the Ras superfamily, but it is also tempting to speculate that this enzyme prenylates Rab GTPases in place of the apparently missing GGTase-II. Based on these findings, we may paint the following evolutionary picture. Rho family proteins in metchnikovellids, and independently in the subclade of Eumicrosporea after the early divergence of the lineage leading to Nematocida spp., lost C-terminal prenylation and instead rely on S-palmitoylation to anchor in membranes. The same arrangement has affected the sole metchnikovellid Ras family protein, but whether the change evolved only in this lineage or earlier, i.e., prior to the Metchnikovellida-Eumicrosporea divergence, is unknown due to loss of Rap1 in eumicrosporeans.

A conserved putative pseudoGTPase in the reduced set of Arf family proteins in Eumicrosporea

The Arf family embraces proteins involved in vesicle trafficking, microtubule dynamics, mitochondrial fusion, and biogenesis and function of the cilium [69–72]. An extensive study of the Arf family in major eukaryotic lineages inferred up to 15 ancestral pan-eukaryotic paralogs [46], with 13 retained by the last common ancestor of Holomycota [73]. This number was further reduced in the RM clade, as we inferred only eleven genes in the ancestor of rozellids and Microsporidia (Fig. 4). While the annotation of the Arf family members in rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids was straightforward in most cases (see “Methods”), the situation was different in the Eumicrosporea. Of the six groups of Arf family orthologs present, five could be preliminarily assigned to specific ancestral pan-eukaryotic paralogs, namely Arf1, Arl1, Arl2, Arl8, and Sar1, by the BLAST-based approach, whereas the sixth exhibited an equal degree of similarity to various members of the Arf family. As discussed below, this group exhibits certain unusual features and its origin could not be deciphered, so we dubbed it ArlEM (i.e., Arl specific for Eumicrosporea).

To confirm the preliminary annotation of Arf family genes in Eumicrosporea, we performed a phylogenetic analysis of these sequences together with readily annotated Arf family sequences from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids. We expected that eumicrosporean sequences would form a monophyletic group with their putative orthologs from less-derived relatives bearing out our preliminary annotation. However, this proved true only for Arl2, Arl8, and Sar1 (Fig. 7). The eumicrosporean Arf1 and Arl1 groups each formed a separate monophyletic lineage placed far from Arf1 and Arl1 sequences from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids. The latter genes formed a higher-level clade additionally including the Arf6 group (apparently lost from the microsporidian lineage after the divergence of P. saccamoebae; Fig. 4) and the Arl5 group (restricted to rozellids), thus recapitulating the branching pattern of Arf1, Arf6, Arl1, and Arl5 as established in the previous pan-eukaryotic analysis [46]. The union of the Arf1 and Arl1 sequences from less divergent RM clade members with the closest ancestral paralogs to the exclusion of their respective eumicrosporean orthologs reflects a dominance of plesiomorphic sequence features erased in the highly divergent eumicrosporean sequences, analogous to the situation with Rab1 and Rab8 in Eumicrosporea discussed above.

HHsearch supported our annotation of eumicrosporean Arf1 and Arl1 sequences (Table S3). Meanwhile, comparison of sequence logos of putative Arf1 and Arl1 groups from Eumicrosporea with Arf family proteins from other RM clade members and other eukaryotes revealed one position in which all Arl1 sequences, including the eumicrosporean ones, exclusively share a cysteine residue (Fig. S9). The presence of Arf1 orthologs in Eumicrosporea is a priori expected given the fact this GTPase was identified in all 114 previously studied phylogenetically diverse eukaryotic taxa (other than RM clade members), indicating that Arf1 mediates molecular processes essential for a eukaryotic cell, whereas Arl1 loss outside Eumicrosporea has known precedents [46]. Furthermore, the propensity for duplication markedly differs between the two paralogs: Arf1 is usually present in more than one copy, but Arl1 is usually present as a single-copy gene. The eumicrosporean Arf1 and Arl1 follow this pattern: the putative Arf1 gene is omnipresent and sometimes duplicated, whereas the putative Arl1 has been presumably lost at least five times in Eumicrosporea and no duplication has been observed (Figs. 2 and 4).

Truly enigmatic among the Arf family members in Eumicrosporea is the ArlEM group, as neither BLAST searches nor phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 7) shed light on its origin. We were able to find ArlEM orthologs in all members of Eumicrosporea, but in none of their relatives (Fig. 2). All ArlEM proteins have one to three cysteine residues in the N-terminal extension and in silico prediction suggested that they are S-palmitoylated (Fig. 8A, Fig. S10, Table S6). Moreover, ArlEM sequences from Nematocida, Anncaliia, Vairimorpha, and Nosema species are predicted to be N-myristoylated at the glycine residue immediately following the initial methionine (Fig. 8, Fig. S10). S-palmitoylation has been reported in a majority of Arl13 and Arl16 proteins studied by Vargová et al. [46]. Interestingly, Arl13 proteins from rozellids also possess one or two cysteine residues near their N-termini predicted to be palmitoylated (Table S6). Thus, ArlEM could theoretically represent an ortholog of Arl13 that persisted in Eumicrosporea in an extremely divergent form but was independently lost in lineages leading to P. saccamoebae, M. daphniae and metchnikovellids. However, HHsearches against profile HMMs of all ancestral Arf family members using as queries two different ArlEM profile HMM versions built from alternatively created multiple sequence alignments (see Methods) retrieved two non-overlapping lists of three best hits, none of them corresponding to Arl13 (Table S3).

The origin of ArlEM thus remains undecided, but if it is an Arl13 ortholog after all, its extreme divergence may be related to a major functional shift this protein would be expected to have undergone. This is because Arl13 has a flagellum-specific role and has so far been encountered only in eukaryotes capable of building a flagellum [75, 76], whereas Eumicrosporea certainly do not make flagella. The predicted S-palmitoylation and N-myristoylation suggest that ArlEM associates with the membrane [77]. Despite its undeniable membership in the Arf GTPase family, the ArlEM GTPase domain is generally extremely divergent. The canonical GTPase motifs are typically mutated in ArlEM proteins (Fig. 8A and Fig. S10), and it is likely that most of them no longer function as GTPases or may even be incapable of binding a nucleotide; they would thus constitute a new group in the class i of the so-called pseudoGTPases [78]. This interpretation is further supported by structural modelling of ArlEM proteins using AlphaFold 3, as exemplified in Fig. 8B. Some ArlEM proteins exhibit longer deletions within the GTPase domain (Fig. 8A and Fig. S10) that result in the loss of canonical secondary structure elements from the protein (Fig. 8C). Despite its extreme divergence, ArlEM nevertheless must have a specific important cellular role, as indicated not only by its ubiquity in Eumicrosporea, but also by the fact that the S. lophii ArlEM (= SLOPH 2328) is the second most abundant transcript among all protein coding genes of the organism [79]. In two other species with available transcriptomic data, Nosema bombycis (two different stages — mature spore and sporoplasm) and Trachipleistophora hominis, the ArlEM gene is not expressed as highly as in S. lophii, but its expression level still remains above the median of all expressed genes (this holds for both stages of N. bombycis; Fig. S11). Considering its distribution and extraordinary features, ArlEM can be considered a eumicrosporean evolutionary innovation, but additional work, ideally including experimental characterization of the protein, is needed to resolve whether ArlEM indeed arose by extreme sequence divergence of the Arl13 gene, or whether it originated from another ancestral Arf family gene (directly or after duplication).

The first eukaryotes found to lack the beta subunit of the signal recognition particle receptor

The protein usually referred to in an abbreviated form as SRβ is a component of the cellular machinery responsible for the transport of nascent polypeptide chains bearing signal peptide to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). The machinery consists of a ribonucleoprotein complex called the signal recognition particle (SRP) and its receptor (SR) composed of two subunits, alpha (SRα) and beta (SRβ), the latter being anchored to the ER membrane via an N-terminal transmembrane region. Interaction between SRβ and SRα is crucial for the targeting of translating ribosomes to the ER. Several components of the SRP and SR function as GTPases, but only SRβ is a member of the Ras superfamily, distantly related to Arf family GTPases [46, 80, 81]. The very detection of SRβ in Eumicrosporea turned out to be very challenging, since only SRβ sequences from Nematocida species still bear features necessary for detecting the SRβ domain by an InterProScan. Using these and subsequently found SRβ from other Eumicrosporea sequences as queries in BLAST or HMMER searches (for details see “Methods”), we were able to detect divergent SRβ homologs with an indispensable N-terminal transmembrane region in all other taxa selected for systematic analysis in this study, with the exception of Spraguea lophii (Fig. 2, Table S2). Crucially, a SRβ ortholog could not be identified in any of the 11 genome assemblies from different S. lophii strains [9, 79] making data incompleteness an unlikely explanation. This species is part of a broader eumicrosporean clade, Glugeida [82], which also contains a subclade represented in our reference eumicrosporean dataset by three species (Pseudoloma neurophilia, Trachipleistophora hominis, and Vavraia culicis) that all do possess an identifiable SRβ (Fig. 2, Table S2). To evaluate whether the putative absence of SRβ is restricted to S. lophii, we extended our analyses to Glugeida members that were not included in our reference set. As a result, we found that an SRβ gene is not identifiable in Cucumispora dikerogammari, but it does occur in Dictyocoela muelleri (KAG0440753.1).

Since SRβ function is tightly connected to the SRα protein, we were interested in its occurrence and features in the RM clade. As expected, we detected SRα in all Eumicrosporea, including S. lophii and C. dikerogammari (Table S8). The part of the SRα protein responsible for the interaction with SRβ is its N-terminal SRX domain, a variant to the longin domain [81, 83]. SRX adopts the ββαβββαα topology with a highly conserved glycine residue in the β1-β2 hairpin [83, 84]. Although the overall sequence similarity of the N-terminal part of eumicrosporean SRα sequences is rather low, the inspection of a multiple sequence alignment revealed several insertions and deletions in the N-terminal part of the S. lophii and C. dikerogammari SRα (Fig. S12), which prompted us to investigate its possible deviations from the typical conformation. Prediction and comparison of the secondary structure of the SRα from S. lophii, C. dikerogammari, and the rest of the Eumicrosporea revealed that the N-terminal part of S. lophii and C. dikerogammari SRα putatively adopts a different organization of secondary structure elements conformation than the ββαβββαα arrangement found in the rest of Eumicrosporea (Fig. S13). This structural change goes hand in hand with the loss of the aforementioned conserved glycine residue in the β1-β2 hairpin present in all other Eumicrosporea (Fig. S12). Strikingly, SRα proteins of both S. lophii and C. dikerogammari exhibit a predicted transmembrane (TM) helix in the equivalent N-terminal region of the protein, whereas no TM domain was predicted in SRα sequences from other Eumicrosporea (Fig. S12, Table S8). To illustrate these differences, we used AlphaFold 3 to model the 3D structure of SRα from Encephalitozoon cuniculi, as the representative of the standard SRα, and from S. lophii and C. dikerogammari (Fig. 9A–C). While the GTPase domain adopts a similar fold in all three proteins, their N-terminal parts differ, reflecting the changes discussed above.

This unique presence of SRα with a TM helix could explain our failure to identify an SRβ ortholog in S. lophii and C. dikerogammari. We hypothesize that in contrast to the situation in other eukaryotes including most Microsporidia (Fig. 9D), the SRα protein of these species is stably anchored to the ER membrane via its TM helix, rendering SRβ redundant (Fig. 9E). As a result, the SRX domain, typically interacting with SRβ, is no longer necessary, leading to the mutations in the SRX domain affecting the structure of this region. Although this hypothesis needs to be supported by experimental data and the idea of combining the functions of SRα and SRβ into one protein would be a novelty for eukaryotes, a similar situation occurs in prokaryotes where a single FtsY protein (an SRα ortholog) interacts with both the SRP and directly with a membrane, although employing a different mechanism [80, 85]. Notably, the putative switch from the standard SRβ-dependent mechanism of SRα attachment to anchoring the latter protein in the ER membrane by a TM helix seems to have evolved twice independently in Glugeida, since a multigene phylogenetic analysis contradicts the possibility of S. lophii and C. dikerogammari forming a clade to the exclusion of SRβ-containing species; specifically, C. dikerogammari is with high statistical support placed closer to the Pseudoloma/Trachipleistophora/Vavraia subclade than to S. lophii [86]. Furthermore, the extremely divergent nature of SRβ in most Microsporidia, including the lack of the G1 to G5 boxes characteristic for the Ras superfamily (see Table S2), raises the question of whether SRβ in these organisms binds GTP and operates in the same way as in other eukaryotes. It is thus possible that the putative SRβ losses in S. lophii and C. dikerogammari are just a culmination of a general stepwise simplification of the SRP receptor in Microsporidia.

Stepwise reduction of the Ras superfamily in the evolution of the RM clade

One final Ras superfamily GTPase that we could identify in Eumicrosporea is, unsurprisingly, Ran (the sole member of the Ran family), an essential component of trafficking between the nucleus and the cytoplasm [87]. Ran is present in all eukaryotes investigated so far, usually as a single-copy gene [45]. Rozellids and Microsporidia also contain only one Ran gene per species, together forming a strongly supported group in phylogenetic analysis of Rab and Ran family (Fig. 3). Adding Ran to the GTPases discussed in the previous sections, Eumicrosporea seem to have inherited from their ancestors a set of maximally 15 Ras superfamily genes (only 14 if ArlEM is not a direct descendant of an ancestral gene). However, investigations into relatives of Eumicrosporea unveil a much richer representation of the Ras superfamily in the RM clade (Figs. 2, 4, Table S2). This is noticeable already when considering the total number of Ras superfamily genes in some of these taxa (Table S2), especially rozellids with roughly three or four times more genes than is the usual complement of Eumicrosporea (and metchnikovellids). The reduction of the Ras superfamily complement observed in Microsporidia and especially Eumicrosporea is partly accounted for by selective loss of individual members of some of the major families. Thus, the loss of multiple Rab family members in early microsporidian evolution resulting in the simplification of the endomembrane system, commented in detail above, was paralleled by the loss of several members of the Arf family with membrane-trafficking roles analogous to Rab GTPases, namely Arl5, Arf6, and Arfrp1 [46, 73], in different tree nodes before the radiation of Eumicrosporea (Fig. 4). In addition, Eumicrosporea have completely lost several major families or stand-alone genes belonging to the Ras superfamily that have been retained in at least some other RM clade members (Figs. 2, 4, Table S2).

One of them is the Ras family, in rozellids represented by Rap1, Ral, and two to four Ras genes. This complement is different from the inferred set of Ras family genes resident in the LECA, which includes Rap1, Rheb, a single Ras paralog [44] and potentially CPRas ([88] and unpublished observations). Although Eumicrosporea completely lack Ras family genes, we identified a single Ras gene in P. saccamoebae and a Rap1 gene in both SB-Microsporidia as well as metchnikovellids (Table S2). We performed phylogenetic analysis of Ras family sequences from rozellids and Microsporidia in combination with a reference Ras family dataset from ten Holomycota species (Table S9B). According to the resulting tree (Fig. S14), the ancestor of Holomycota possessed Ral, Rap1, Rheb, and four Ras genes here labelled Ras1, Ras2, Ras3, and Ras4 (note that these designations do not match the yeast Ras gene nomenclature; see Methods). Rheb was apparently lost in the stem lineage of the RM clade (see also [89]). The complement of Ras genes may differ between the three Rozella species, with all four (Ras1 to Ras4) present in Rozella sp. PSC023 but some of them not found in R. allomycis and R. multimorpha, although this may simply reflect incompleteness of their genome assemblies. The most extensive reduction of the Ras family, i.e. loss of Ral, Ras2, Ras3 and Ras4, occurred in the stem lineage of Microsporidia, and further continued by the loss of Ras1 gene after the divergence of P. saccamoebae and the loss of Rap1 gene in the stem lineage of Eumicrosporea (Fig. 4).

The rozellid genomes encode multiple homologs of the alpha subunit of heterotrimeric G proteins (Gα or Gpa), which also belongs to the Ras superfamily [90]. Heterotrimeric G proteins are known for their function in intracellular signalling in conjunction with G-protein-coupled receptors [91] and exhibit a complex evolutionary history including frequent losses, lineage-specific family expansions, and even architectural rearrangements of the proteins [43]. Eumicrosporea lack Gα, but we found one Gα gene in metchnikovellids and two Gα genes in SB-Microsporidia (Table S2). To understand the origin of these paralogs, we assembled a reference Gα sequence dataset from eleven Holomycota species (Table S9C) and performed a phylogenetic analysis together with Gα from rozellids and Microsporidia (Fig. S15). Despite the overall poor tree resolution, we could distinguish four groups with an inferred presence in the ancestor of Holomycota, Gpa1, Gpa2, Gpa3, and Gpa4, as they occur in rozellids and several of the remaining Holomycota lineages (see “Methods” for details concerning the Gα proteins nomenclature). Gpa3 and Gpa4 have been retained only in rozellids among RM clade members, whereas Gpa2 was lost after the divergence of M. daphniae and Gpa1 later in the ancestor of Eumicrosporea (Fig. 4). Multiple paralogs of Gpa1, Gpa2, and Gpa4 were found in some or all Rozella species (Fig. S15), at least partly stemming from recurrent gene duplications in rozellids, which points to a remarkable evolutionary plasticity of heterotrimeric G protein-based signalling in this lineage.

A significant factor that has shaped the history of the Ras superfamily in the RM clade is the flagellum. Although there is evidence that some hitherto uncharacterized members of Microsporidia (sensu lato) may have flagellated life stages [27], none of the members of this group investigated in this study builds flagella, as indicated both by morphological observations and genome analyses [16, 22, 92]. In contrast, rozellids form uniflagellated zoospores and the genome of R. allomycis, which was specifically investigated in this regard, unsurprisingly contains genes for typical proteins constituting the eukaryotic flagellum [15, 92]. This category of “flagellar” (or “ciliary”) genes in eukaryotes also includes a number of Ras superfamily members, such as Rab23 [93], Rab28 [94], IFT22 [95], IFT27 [96], RJL [97, 98], and D2LIC [99], which are all present in rozellids but were apparently lost in the stem lineage of Microsporidia (Fig. 4, Table S2). Rozellids also encode a GTPase that we identified as a putative ortholog of another cilium-associated Ras superfamily protein, Arl13 [75, 76], based on HHsearch (Table S3). However, as discussed above, in this case we cannot exclude the possibility that a divergent descendant of this gene has survived in Eumicrosporea (as ArlEM). Two of the rozellid members investigated have also retained the cilium-associated Arf family members Arl3 and Arl6, while their absence in R. allomycis indicates that reductive evolution of the flagellum is in progress even in rozellids (Fig. 4). Two additional broadly conserved cilium-associated Ras superfamily members, Arl16 [76] and RABL2 [100], are completely missing from the RM clade, the former having been lost already in the stem lineage of the whole Holomycota clade [73] and the latter in the stem branch of the RM clade, owing to its presence in zoosporic fungi [100].

The mitochondrion is another cellular structure the fate of which has entailed reductive evolution of the Ras superfamily in the RM clade. Miro (“mitochondrial Rho”) is a large broadly conserved eukaryotic protein with two Ras superfamily GTPase domains anchored by its C-terminal transmembrane region to the outer membrane of the mitochondrion and involved in the regulation of its dynamics and morphology [101, 102]. We found Miro in rozellids, P. saccamoebae, and M. daphniae that all possess aerobic genome-containing mitochondria [15, 16, 19] but not in metchnikovellids and Eumicrosporea characterised by the much simpler mitosome (Table S2; [23, 24]). Still another cellular feature that has undergone reduction in microsporidian evolution is the so-called Tor (or mTOR) signalling pathway that senses the nutrient status of the cell and with variations is conserved across most eukaryotes [103]. The canonical form of the Tor pathway includes a pair of closely related Ras superfamily GTPases called Gtr1 and Gtr2 (or RagA/RagB and RagC/RagD, respectively, in vertebrates). An earlier study analysed genomes of three Eumicrosporea representatives and concluded that the Tor pathway is completely absent from these organisms [104]. Here we extend the absence of the Gtr1 and Gtr2 genes to all Eumicrosporea, metchnikovellids, and M. daphniae, while both proteins have been retained by rozellids and P. saccamoebae (Fig. 2). Whether the loss of other Tor pathway components coincided with the loss of Gtr1/Gtr2 is to be determined in a future study, but it is notable that Microsporidia (as well as rozellids) also lack Rheb (see above), another Ras superfamily GTPase that is a key component of a Tor pathway branch parallel to that including Gtr1/Gtr2 [103]. Rozellids are the only RM clade lineage in which we could detect the intermediate light chain of cytoplasmic dynein 1 (D1LIC), a divergent member of the Ras superfamily [105]. The loss of this protein in Microsporidia might indicate a major modification of the cytoplasmic dynein 1 complex, which is consistent with the extremely divergent nature of the respective heavy chain (the motor subunit of the complex) in Microsporidia reported previously [106]. A few additional Ras superfamily members were presumably lost in early stages of microsporidian phylogeny (Fig. 4), including RABL3, Spg1, RABL6, and two novel uncharacterized proteins that seem to be conserved across diverse eukaryotes (details to be published separately), but limited or completely absent understanding of their cellular roles even in model taxa precludes drawing specific conclusions about the functional significance of their absence in Microsporidia.

To sum up, as inferred from the current sampling of the RM clade, the most severe reduction of the Ras superfamily occurred in the stem lineage of Microsporidia, that is prior to the divergence of P. saccamoebae, but additional losses at different nodes of the microsporidian phylogeny further sculpted the set of Ras superfamily genes up to the ancestor of Eumicrosporea. As a result, the set of > 40 Ras superfamily genes present in the last common ancestor of the RM clade was stripped down to a mere 16 genes in the last common ancestor of Eumicrosporea (Figs. 2, 4). Further gene losses and/or duplications within this taxon have resulted in the Ras superfamily gene complement varying in extant Eumicrosporea from 12 in H. eriocheir to 17 in A. algerae and E. aedis (Fig. 2, Table S2), suggesting independent adaptation and slightly different strategies for parasitic lifestyle in various microsporidians.

Conclusions

Our detailed analysis of the Ras superfamily in Microsporidia and their closest relatives fills an important gap in the knowledge of cell biology of a biologically interesting lineage of microbial eukaryotes. A single previous report we are aware of addresses the same gene group in RM clade representatives [107]. As part of their review paper on “small GTPases” in Fungi (sensu lato), the authors catalogued these proteins in three RM clade members (R. allomycis, M. daphniae, and E. cuniculi). However, they provided only summary numbers of genes for the different gene families without details on the identity of the family representatives. Furthermore, their numbers are not completely accurate. For instance, the number of Rab family genes indicated by them (10) implies they must have missed two of the Rabs identified by us, and they indicate the absence of the Ran family in R. allomycis, while we could readily find a Ran GTPase in this organism (Table S2). Furthermore, they completely ignored certain Ras superfamily members (such as RJL, IFT27, SRβ, or Gpa proteins). On the other hand, they included in their inventory proteins denoted BRG (Big Ras GTPases) and indicated the presence of a single representative in R. allomycis, but we found out that the BRG family (defined earlier by the same authors; [108]) in fact represents a group of GTPases previously classified as paraseptins [90] and thus is not directly related to the Ras superfamily. Hence, we believe that our study provides a refined, and much more comprehensive, picture of the repertoire of Ras superfamily genes in the RM clade and provides a solid basis for their future functional investigations.

Crucially, our analyses provide interesting biological insights that reach beyond mere gene cataloguing and underscore Microsporidia as a unique evolutionary “laboratory” testing the limits of eukaryotic cell biology. The overarching theme in microsporidian evolution is reduction, i.e., loss of components that are common, if not ubiquitous, in other eukaryotes. The species with the smallest detected Ras superfamily gene complement among microsporidians, H. eriocheir harbouring only 12 superfamily members, reaches the extreme so far recorded among autonomously reproducing eukaryotes as a whole. Furthermore, we provide evidence that in Microsporidia loss has more generally affected broader functional modules in which particular Ras superfamily members take part, although a systematic analysis of these modules is yet to be done to unveil the actual extent of their reduction. Notably, our analyses of Ras superfamily proteins led us to notice that most microsporidians have undergone an unprecedented loss of common eukaryotic protein prenylation machineries that service proteins well beyond the Ras superfamily [109], indicating a systemic simplification of the spectrum of protein post-translational modifications in these organisms. The similarly unique radical modification of the very basic cellular mechanism of co-translational protein targeting to the ER, discovered by us in S. lophii and C. dikerogammari, further attests to the ability of Microsporidia to extend the limits of what is still compatible with successful function of the eukaryotic cell. Hence, in light of our work, Microsporidia look even more peculiar and worth closer attention than before.

Methods

Identification and curation of Ras superfamily genes in RM clade

We used genome and protein sequences of 33 representatives of the RM clade available from several sequence databases (listed in Table S1). For the species Hamiltosporidium tvaerminnensis the analyses were primarily done using the originally published genome assembly from the strain OER-3–3 (then referred to with the species name Octospora bayeri; [110]), but as some of the genes of interest proved to be incomplete or missing in it, we later extracted the respective sequences from the more complete genome assembly of the strain FI-OER-3–3 published later [111] (the exact provenance of all sequences is indicated in Tables S2, S4, and S7). For Hepatospora eriocheir we primarily analysed the genome assembly of the strain canceri, but given the surprisingly small set of GTPases genes and the absence of genes for GGTPase II subunits we in parallel checked the assembly available for the strain GB1, with no additional genes identified. The process of detection and annotation is described in Figure S1. Candidate Ras superfamily genes were detected using BLAST searches [112]. BLASTp was used for searching against appropriate protein datasets and tBLASTn was used to search in genome assemblies in order to detect genes that were missed in genome annotation and hence lack the encoded product in sets of annotated proteins. As queries we used Ras superfamily GTPases from various species to cover the entire diversity of this superfamily. The sequences used as input are available on figshare [113]. All sequences retrieved by BLAST were further validated using CD-Search, followed by manual inspection. While sequences containing only non-GTPase domain(s) were discarded, those with no identifiable domain or GTPase domain were subjected to an additional online BLASTp search against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) database. We excluded sequences where the best hits were non-Ras superfamily members, unannotated proteins, or those yielding no hits. Only sequences with best hits within the Ras superfamily were retained. Since NCBI annotations for specific Ras superfamily members are often unreliable, we preliminarily annotated all remaining candidates by BLASTp searches against a curated reference database. This database comprised reference eukaryotic proteomes from UniProt (Release 2026_01 [114]) complemented with published annotated datasets of the Rab family, the Ran family, and IFT27 and RABL2 sequences [45], the Arf family and SRβ sequences [46], and the Rho family [60]. These previously published datasets [45, 46, 60] were consolidated and are provided on figshare, along with the BLASTp outputs for the candidate Ras superfamily sequences from the RM clade [113]. While preliminary annotation was unequivocal in most cases, exceptions are discussed in the following sections. If a gene that was present in related species could not be found in the given target organism (e.g., Rab5 in both metchnikovellids), we used sequences from the related RM clade members as queries for another search to increase the sensitivity of homology detection. The BLAST searches were complemented with a HMMER search (version 3.0; [115]) if no homologous sequences were found using program BLASTp and/or tBLASTn in rapidly evolving genes, e.g., SRβ and D1LIC (details are provided below). Profile HMMs were constructed using the hmmbuild command (in HMMER) with default parameters from multiple sequence alignments of selected reference sequences. These corresponded to relevant (purpose-dependent) sets of sequences from Eumicrosporea, the RM clade, Fungi, and eukaryotes (all these taxa together or their different combinations). Details are provided below for particular proteins and all used custom profile HMMs are available on figshare [113]. Homologs were searched in the existing sets of predicted protein sequences as well as in protein sequence files generated for each genome assembly using getorf (part of the EMBOSS package version 6.6.0.0; [116]) with default settings, except for the “Minimum nucleotide size of ORF to report” parameter set to 100.

All existing predictions of Ras superfamily protein sequences were manually checked and corrected whenever necessary considering the underlying nucleotide sequence of the respective genomic locus. In some cases, sequencing or assembly errors were identified in the genome sequence and corrected using the corresponding raw sequencing data or the respective transcriptome sequences. De novo protein sequence predictions were carried out manually in the case of missing gene models. All relevant sequences together with information concerning protein model modifications or de novo prediction are listed in Table S2. The genome assembly of Nosema antheraeae contains several more or less complete tandem duplications of some genes. Since the genomic reads are not available and the possibility of genome assembly artefacts cannot be ruled out, we considered for further analyses only one gene copy per such duplication. We also encountered similar problems in several other taxa (for example, Nosema bombycis) and tried to resolve the issues using genomic sequence reads, if possible. All identified and verified Ras superfamily sequences were annotated using different methods depending on their divergency and source organism. The annotation process is described in detail below for individual gene families and groups of organisms (see also Fig. S1).

Multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic analyses

All multiple protein sequence alignments were constructed using the online program MAFFT (version 7) or its stand-alone implementation (v7.505) with default settings [117]. For phylogenetic analyses, the alignments were manually checked in BioEdit, version 7.2.6 [118], adjusted when necessary and trimmed using trimAl version v1.4.rev15 with gappyout option [119]. Phylogenetic trees were calculated using the stand-alone IQ-TREE versions 2.1.2 or 2.2.5 [120]. Substitution models were selected with the model selection program implemented in the IQ-TREE program. Branch support was assessed using the SH-aLRT test [121] and the ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) approximation [122]. To follow the IQ-TREE manual, only branches with branch support equal to or higher than 80% (SH-aLRT) and 95% (UFBoot) were considered as sufficiently supported. Phylogenetic trees were visualized in the program iTOL [123] and modified in Inkscape (www.inkscape.org). The described approaches for the construction of the multiple sequence alignments, trimming, and phylogenetic analyses were used for all analyses in this study, unless otherwise indicated in the subsequent parts of Methods. The versions of the programs used for particular phylogenetic analyses are listed in Table S10. Multiple alignments of eumicrosporean ArlEM, SRα and Rho and Ras family protein sequences were visualized for representation purposes using the program CHROMA, version 1 [124]. As the sequence divergence of ArlEM proteins made obtaining a credible multiple sequence alignment difficult with the standard approach, we alternatively employed FoldMason [125] to construct an alignment based on structural models for individual proteins generated by AlphaFold 3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/; [126]) with default settings.

HHsearch analyses

To provide further support for the annotation of genes with uncertain identity, we utilized the HHsearch 3.3.0 program implemented in the software package HH-suite3 ([127]; https://github.com/soedinglab/hh-suite). As input data for constructing query profile HMMs, we used eumicrosporean Arf, Rab, and Rho protein sequences, as well as divergent Arf family genes from rozellids. These were aligned using the stand-alone MAFFT, followed by the manual trimming of N- and C-terminal extensions. These FASTA alignments were then converted into query profile HMMs using the hhmake command with the -M 50 option. To create custom databases, we gathered reference Rab, Rho and Arf family protein sequences from taxa outside of the Eumicrosporea. These datasets comprised sequences from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids complemented with appropriate orthologs from other Holomycota protist lineages. Specifically, we included the aphelideans Paraphelidium tribonemae [17], Amoeboaphelidium protococcarum, and A. occidentale, as well as the Cristidiscoidea members Fonticula alba and Parvularia atlantis [128]. The datasets were further enriched with sequences from established family-specific studies [45–47]. Accession numbers and data sources for included sequences are listed in Table S11. Alignments were built and trimmed in the same way as the query alignments. From these FASTA alignments, we created a single FFindex database using the ffindex_build command, generated A3M alignments using hhconsensus with the -M 50 option, and for each A3M produced a profile HMM (hhm format) and a CS219 prefilter file. Finally, both HMM and A3M databases were sorted using the CS219 index. Each query HMM was then searched against the database with hhsearch. For the divergent ArlEM we did an extra search against the database using a profile HMM built from the FoldMason-generated multiple sequence alignment. The results of these searches are provided in Table S3. The multiple sequence alignments and profile HMMs are available on figshare [113].

Annotation of the Ras superfamily genes in non-eumicrosporean RM clade members

The preliminary assignment of each sequence was carried out using a BLASTp search against the custom reference database described above. D1LIC proteins were identified using hmmsearch with a profile HMM built from the seed alignment of the respective family (PF05783) in the Pfam database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/; [129]). While most BLASTp-based annotations were unequivocal, exceptions included one Arf family protein from each Rozella species, Ras family proteins from M. daphniae and metchnikovellids, and one incomplete Rab family sequence from R. multimorpha. In further analyses, the Arf and Ras family candidates were annotated as Arl13 and Rap1, respectively, whereas the Rab family sequence eluded more specific annotation. Confirmation of the preliminary annotation by phylogenetic analyses was conducted for the Rab, Ran, Rho, Ras, and Arf families and also for the alpha subunit of heterotrimeric G proteins (Gα) and the Rab-like protein IFT27. Additional members of the Ras superfamily, including SRβ, were annotated solely based on the sequence similarity assessed by BLAST searches. Annotation of the majority of SRβ sequences was also supported by the presence of the SRPRB domain detected by CD-Search [130, 131] against the Conserved Domain Database [132].

Rab, Ran, and IFT27 sequences from our dataset were added to the reference alignment from Elias et al. [45] using the –add function of MAFFT, the resulting alignment was trimmed according to the mask used before [45], and phylogenetic analysis was performed as described above. The Rab-like sequence ROZMUL_005349-T1 from R. multimorpha was excluded from the presented phylogenetic analysis due to its incompleteness (the region presumably containing the conserved GTPase boxes G3-G5 is missing) and high divergence evident from preliminary phylogenetic analyses. The majority of the added sequences cluster inside the anticipated cognate pan-eukaryotic paralogous groups with sufficient bootstrap support, except for Rab14 and Rab20 sequences forming unsupported monophyletic groups (Fig. S2). Annotation of Rab20 from P. saccamoebae was confirmed in separate phylogenetic analyses with the dataset from Elias et al. [45] (the tree available on figshare [113]).

Arf family sequences were added to the multiple alignment composed of reliably annotated fungal Arf family protein sequences from Vargová et al. [46] using the –add function of MAFFT and the alignment was trimmed and subjected to phylogenetic analysis as described above (Fig. S16). Most of the sequences from our dataset branched within cognate fungal paralogous groups with sufficient support, except for Arfrp1 sequences (their annotation was confirmed by focused phylogenetic analysis; the tree available on figshare [113]) and a group of three orthologous sequences from rozellids. To check if these genes may correspond to an ancestral Arf family gene, we performed several phylogenetic analyses combining the Rozella sequences with various subsets of sequences from Vargová et al. [46], but without convincing results (the tree available on figshare [113]). However, a HHsearch result (method details are provided above) suggests that these sequences are very divergent Arl13 orthologs (Table S3).

As a reference for annotating the Rho family, we used the dataset from Eliáš and Klimeš [60] and performed phylogenetic analysis of sequences from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids combined with the reference dataset (Fig. S5). In order to analyse the evolution of Gα (Gpa) proteins, we assembled a reference holomycotan dataset (Table S9C), combined it with Gα sequences from our dataset, and performed a phylogenetic analysis (as described above; Fig. S15). Since the nomenclature of Gα proteins is not well established, we attempted to follow the yeast nomenclature. Saccharomyces cerevisiae harbours two Gα genes named Gpa1 and Gpa2 which probably represent two genes ancestral to the RM clade. The nomenclature of Mycosarcoma (= Ustilago) maydis was also considered, although it does not correspond entirely with the nomenclature used for S. cerevisiae (e.g., Gpa3 from M. maydis and Gpa2 from S. cerevisiae are both members of well-supported group we decided to call Gpa2 according to S. cerevisiae). Gpa2 from M. maydis is part of a highly supported group within the Gpa1 denoted in Fig. S15 as Gna-2 based on annotation established in Neurospora crassa. Although the Gna-2 sequence from N. crassa was not included in our phylogenetic analysis, it has been established before that Gpa2 from M. maydis and Gna-2 from N. crassa are orthologous [133]. An additional potentially ancestral Gpa gene was named Gpa3 and a large group of most of the remaining Gpa genes was named Gpa4 according to Gpa4 from M. maydis (Fig. S15).

For the annotation of the Ras family, we created a reference dataset of holomycotan Ras family sequences (Table S9B), constructed a multiple sequence alignment together with sequences from our dataset and performed phylogenetic analysis as described above (Fig. S14). The analysis revealed the existence of four distinct groups of Ras genes, which we denoted Ras1, Ras2, Ras3, and Ras4. The Ras1 group was named after the Ras1 gene from S. cerevisiae, although the orthologous sequence in Aspergillus fumigatus is named RasA. The Ras2 group includes A. fumigatus RasB and no yeast S. cerevisiae gene (the “Ras2” gene in S. cerevisiae represents a yeast-specific Ras1 duplication). Ras3 and Ras4 groups are not present in S. cerevisiae and A. fumigatus and the names are simply increments to Ras1 and Ras2 groups.

Table S2 provides detailed information on the basis of our annotation of individual Ras superfamily proteins (phylogenetic analysis, unsupported phylogenetic analysis, HHsearch or BLAST similarity). Sequences marked as annotated based on “phylogenetic analysis” are those which cluster with cognate ortholog sequences from the reference dataset with sufficient support (as described above). Annotation based on phylogenies lacking statistical support is applied only for Gpa1 and Gpa4 proteins. Sequences the annotation of which was not resolved by phylogenetic analyses were annotated either based on HHsearch or only by sequence similarity based on BLAST search result. For some proteins (e.g., SRβ) the annotation is based on the presence of a diagnostic domain.

Annotation of the Ras superfamily in Eumicrosporea

The basis for the specific annotation of individual Ras superfamily proteins in Eumicrosporea is also detailed in Table S2, following the same criteria as described above for proteins from non-eumicrosporean RM clade members. Preliminary annotation was based on the results of BLAST search against the custom reference database described above. However, many sequences could only be assigned to a particular family rather than a particular pan-eukaryotic paralog. In the case of one eumicrosporean Arf family paralog, the best BLAST hits were typically both Arl2 and Arl3 proteins, which represent closely related paralogs. Given that Arl3 is known for its cilium-associated functions and the Arl2 hits generally yielded lower e-values, these proteins were initially annotated as Arl2. This assignment was subsequently confirmed by phylogenetic analysis (see below). Reference datasets used for the annotation of Ras superfamily proteins from rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids were inapplicable to the annotation of the eumicrosporean sequences due to the extreme divergence of the latter, resulting in a very poor tree resolution (the trees are available on figshare [113]). Therefore, we performed phylogenetic analyses of sequences from Eumicrosporea, rozellids, SB-Microsporidia, and metchnikovellids (Figs. 3, 6, 7), which supported our initial annotation for several groups of orthologs: Rab5, Rab6, Rab11, Ran, Arl2, Arl8, Sar1, and two Eumicrosporea-specific Rho1 paralogs. However, two Rab family genes (including sequences preliminarily annotated as Rab1), three Arf family genes, and one Rho family gene were not annotated by this approach. HHsearch analyses (see above) carried out as the next step ultimately enabled the annotation of all eumicrosporean proteins.

The detection of eumicrosporean SRβ was more complicated and required an iterative approach. No SRβ candidates were found by the initial BLAST searches in any species. We then searched a set of eumicrosporean proteins generated by getorf (as described above) with HMMER (hmmsearch) and a profile HMM built from the sequence alignment of the respective family (SRPRB; PF09439) in the Pfam database. Most significant hits represented other Ras superfamily members, but two sequences from Nematocida spp. contained SRPRB domain when assessed with InterProScan [134, 135] and were therefore annotated as SRβ. Using these two sequences as queries in BLASTp searches, we identified SRβ orthologs in two additional Nematocida species, but not in the rest of Eumicrosporea. A new profile HMM was built from these four Nematocida SRβ sequences and used for another HMMER search, retrieving (previously undetected) sequences in a majority of eumicrosporean species that were confirmed as divergent SRβ orthologs by BLASTp searches against the NCBI nr database and our custom Ras superfamily sequence database including previously annotated microsporidian Ras superfamily proteins. A profile HMM was then built from all identified eumicrosporean SRβ sequences to probe for SRβ orthologs in the species where they remained undetected. This way we identified the missing SRβ genes in E. aedis and V. culicis, as well as in H. tvaerminnensis when the genome assembly of the isolate FI-OER-3–3 was employed. For the final searches, the profile HMM was further updated by adding the newly identified eumicrosporean SRβ sequences, but no SRβ candidate could be identified in S. lophii (multiple strain; [9, 79]) and C. dikerogammari.

Identification of specific sequence features and motifs

Presence of the five conserved motifs (G1, G2, G3, G4, G5) characteristic of GTPases of the Ras superfamily was evaluated using CD-Search [130, 131] against the Conserved Domain Database [132]. Results are provided in the columns labelled G1, G2, G3, G4, and G5 in Table S2. The InterProScan was used for the search of SRβ and VPS9 domains in candidate sequences [134]. Transmembrane regions were detected using the TMHMM Server v. 2.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM/). The results are provided in Table S2. For SRα sequences, we used TMHMM complemented by the Phobius predictor (http://phobius.sbc.su.se/index.html; [136]) and only TM regions predicted by both programs were considered as trusted. Results for SRα sequences are provided in Table S8. Prediction of N-myristoylation of proteins with a glycine residue at the second position (right after the N-terminal methionine) was carried out with three programs: ExPASy Myristoylator tool (http://web.expasy.org/myristoylator/; [137]), NMT—The MYR Predictor (http://mendel.imp.ac.at/myristate/SUPLpredictor.htm; [138]), and GPS-Lipid (http://lipid.biocuckoo.org/webserver.php; [139]). All programs were used with default settings. Only proteins predicted as N-myristoylated by at least two programs were considered as bona fide modified this way. The prediction of S-palmitoylation of cysteine residues was carried out with three tools: CKSAAP-Palm (http://doc.aporc.org/wiki/CKSAAP-Palm; [140]), CSS-Palm 4.0 (https://css-palm.software.informer.com/, [141]) and GPS-Lipid. All programs were used with default settings. Five sequences (RalGpa4a, RmuArl3, RmuArfrp1, VcoArf1, VcoSar1) contain no cysteine residues and were therefore omitted. The cysteine residue was considered to be predicted as S-palmitoylated if at least two tools gave a positive result. Complete results of the predictions of N-myristoylation and S-palmitoylation are provided in Table S6, a summary of the results is provided in columns R and S of Table S2. Prediction of C-terminal prenylation was done with the online prenylation prediction suite PrePS (https://mendel.imp.ac.at/PrePS/, [142]). In the case of Rho1b from H. tvaerminnensis, we used the complete sequence from the FI-OER-3–3 isolate.

Other in silico analyses

The secondary structure of SRα proteins was predicted using the program Jpred 4 [143]. As inputs, multiple sequence alignment of all but S. lophii and C. dikerogammari SRα sequences from Eumicrosporea; the single SRα sequence from S. lophii; and the single sequence from C. dikerogammari were used. The exclusion of S. lophii and C. dikerogammari SRα sequences was based on a preliminary analysis suggesting that these sequences differ from the rest of SRα and largely influence the Jpred4 outcome for all SRα (data not shown). The tertiary structures of SRα protein sequences from S. lophii, C. dikerogammari, and E. cuniculi were modelled using the AlphaFold 3 server with default settings and addition of an Mg2+ ion and a GTP molecule as a ligand. For each species, the model with the highest-ranking score was visualized and annotated in ChimeraX (v1.10.1; [144]). GTPase domains and TM regions, if present, were colour-coded, N- and C-termini were labelled in Inkscape. Sequence logos for the Rab, Rho, and Arf families were constructed using sequences from the RM clade members specifically analysed in this study, other protist holomycotans (P. atlantis, F. alba, P. tribonemae, A. protococcarum and A. occidentale), and subsets from previously published dataset [45, 46, 60]. For each GTPase family, protein domains were detected using CD-search, sequences were trimmed to the GTPase domain using a custom python script and aligned using stand-alone MAFFT. Alignments were processed with trimAl using the gappyout option and then partitioned by taxonomic criteria. Final logos were generated using WebLogo (ver. 3.7.12; [145]). The list of sequences used for logo construction is provided in Table S12.

VPS9 proteins were searched for using hmmsearch as described above and a profile HMM built from the seed alignment of the Pfam family VPS9 (PF02204). For the search, sets of existing protein sequence predictions and/or sets of sequences obtained from the genome sequence of the given microsporidian species by using getorf were used. Candidate proteins identified by hmmsearch were subsequently analysed using InterProScan, and only those confirmed to contain the VPS9 domain were classified as VPS9 proteins. SRα sequences were searched for using BLAST and hmmsearch against the file containing the set of protein sequences generated by getorf, from all analysed genome assemblies. For sequences with existing protein models, the corresponding protein model and its ID were then used instead of the sequence generated by getorf. Alternatively, the scaffold/contig number was used and the model was created manually. The SRα-specific profile HMMs the Pfam database (SRP-alpha_N: PF04086.13; SRX PF09201.10) were used in hmmsearch to identify candidate SRα sequences, which were then analysed using InterProScan and only those containing SRα domains were annotated as such. The search for subunits of prenyltransferases was carried out analogously to the identification of SRα using BLAST and hmmsearch. Profile HMMs for the prenyltransferase subunits were downloaded from the KofamKoala website (https://www.genome.jp/tools/kofamkoala). For species where the sought-after prenyltransferase subunit homolog was not identified this way, we carried out additional HMMER searches. For this, we created HMMs for the individual prenyltransferase subunits from our alignments of initially identified sequences from the RM clade members (as described above). Candidate prenyltransferase subunits were annotated based on phylogenetic analysis (phylogenetic trees are available on figshare; [113]), or BLASTp search results against the NCBI nr database as indicated in Table S7.

To rule out the possibility that genes missing from the genome assembly of M. incurvata, and Amphiamblys sp. WSBS2006 are present in raw genome sequence reads yet absent from the public genome assemblies, we created our own de novo assemblies. Illumina reads from M. incurvata (SRR7440175, SRR7440176) and Amphiamblys sp. WSBS2006 (SRR4045567, SRR4045568) were downloaded from Sequence Read Archive. Reads from both runs of each organism were trimmed with Trimmomatic version 0.39 [146] to remove adapters and low-quality bases (ILLUMINACLIP:TruSeq3-PE-2.fa:2:20:10 LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:75). The resulting paired and unpaired reads were jointly assembled for each organism using SPAdes version 3.15.5 [147] in single-cell mode. Once the combined assemblies were created, we proceeded with getorf and hmmsearch as described above to identify the missing VPS9 and prenyltransferases. For Rab genes, we searched for the missing Rab5 (and Rab6 in the case of Amphiamblys sp. WSBS2006) using tBLASTn with Rab5 and Rab6 sequences from the RM clade representatives as queries.

For the analysis of ArlEM gene expression, we used RNA-seq data from N. bombycis (separately from mature spore and sporoplasm) published by He et al. [148] and from T. hominis published by Watson et al. [149]. In both cases, the authors mapped the RNA-seq reads to the respective reference genomes, and reported expression levels as FPKM values. To generate histograms of gene expression levels, we used the average FPKM values (mean across all replicates) and plotted them with a custom Python script. Only genes with non-zero FPKM values were included, and their distribution was plotted using log-spaced bins on a log10-scaled x-axis. Since three variants of the ArlEM gene are present in the genomic assembly of N. bombycis (Table S2), there are three values plotted in Fig. S11A and S11B.
