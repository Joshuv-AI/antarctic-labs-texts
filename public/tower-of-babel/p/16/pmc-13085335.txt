The effects of cognitive-motor and motor-motor dual tasks on gait performance and dynamic stability in older adults with and without sarcopenia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuting Zhao, Jingkun Zhang, Kexin Yang, Mengyu Liu, Liduan Wang, Chen Yang, Zhipeng Zhou, Liangliang Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the effects of cognitive and motor dual tasks on gait characteristics and dynamic stability in older adults with sarcopenia compared with healthy older adults, and to elucidate the mechanisms underlying their increased fall risk under dual-task conditions.MethodsThis cross-sectional study, with a limited sample size, recruited 40 older adults (mean age: 73.0 ± 4.2 years), including 20 with sarcopenia and 20 healthy controls. Gait parameters and the margin of stability (MoS)—a biomechanical measure quantifying dynamic stability as the shortest distance from the extrapolated center of mass to the boundary of the base of support—were assessed during single-task (ST), cognitive-motor dual-task (CMDT), and motor-motor dual-task (MMDT) walking. A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the effects of group and task on gait and stability.ResultsFor the primary outcome, the anterior–posterior margin of stability (MoSₐₚ) was significantly reduced in older adults with sarcopenia compared to healthy controls during dual-task walking (P < 0.05). Overall, the sarcopenia group demonstrated impaired dynamic stability, also marked by reduced center of mass velocity (P < 0.05). Compared to the healthy group under the same walking conditions, the sarcopenia group exhibited significant gait alterations, including shorter step length, slower gait speed, prolonged gait cycle, and wider step width (P < 0.01). Relative to ST, dual-task conditions further decreased anterior–posterior MoS and center of mass velocity, while increasing mediolateral MoS (MoSₘₗ) as a compensatory mechanism. Additionally, step length shortened, gait speed declined, and both gait cycle and double support phase prolonged (P < 0.05). CMDT particularly impaired postural control, leading to greater reductions in gait speed, MoSₘₗ, and center of mass velocity in the sarcopenia group (P < 0.05). The correlation analysis revealed a strong positive association between gait speed and MoSₐₚ (r = 0.530, P < 0.001), as well as moderate negative correlations between MoSₘₗ and both stride length (r = -0.375, P = 0.003) and step length (r = -0.371, P < 0.004) among older adults with sarcopenia. No significant correlation was observed between MoS and step width.ConclusionOlder adults with sarcopenia exhibit pronounced gait and dynamic stability impairments during dual-task conditions, particularly under cognitive-motor demands. The primary gait alterations observed include reduced stride length, decreased gait speed, diminished mediolateral control of the center of mass, prolonged gait cycle, and increased double support time. These findings may inform clinical assessment by highlighting the utility of dual-task paradigms for evaluating functional deficits and tailoring interventions aimed at enhancing postural control and mitigating fall risk in this population.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Dual-task paradigm, Margin of stability, Walking, Postural control, Falls

Introduction

Sarcopenia is an age-related syndrome characterized by a progressive loss of muscle mass, strength, and physical function [1, 2]. It poses a significant public health challenge in aging populations, substantially increasing the risk of falls, fractures, functional disability, and mortality. In this study, the diagnosis of sarcopenia was based on the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 criteria. Globally, sarcopenia affects 10%–27% of older adults [3], with prevalence exceeding 45% among those aged 80 years and older [4]. Falls represent the second most common cause of death from unintentional injuries worldwide among older adults, with annual fall rates of 33% in adults over 65 years and 50% in those over 85 years [5]. Due to sarcopenia-related impairments in balance and gait, affected older adults have a 2.5–3.3-fold higher risk of falls compared with their healthy counterparts [6]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of balance control mechanisms in this population is essential for developing effective fall prevention strategies.

Falls in older adults frequently arise from the interplay between physical frailty and cognitive decline [7]. In sarcopenia, the primary physical deficit—reduced muscle mass and strength—directly compromises lower-limb support and neuromuscular coordination, impairing postural control and gait stability [8, 9]. Compounding this physical vulnerability, emerging evidence indicates a bidirectional relationship between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment, particularly in domains critical for safe mobility such as executive function and attentional control [10, 11]. These specific cognitive domains are fundamental for the allocation of attentional resources and the coordination of movement under complex, real-world conditions. Therefore, assessing gait under conditions that challenge these integrated cognitive-motor processes—specifically through cognitive-motor dual-task (CMDT) paradigms—is crucial for understanding the mechanisms of fall risk in sarcopenia. However, the mechanisms underlying the increased incidence of falls in sarcopenia are not yet fully elucidated. The margin of stability (MoS) is a robust and well-validated measure for quantifying dynamic stability during gait [12, 13]. This biomechanical metric combines the position and velocity of the body’s center of mass with the base of support geometry. It has been extensively validated as a sensitive measure of dynamic stability in diverse clinical populations, such as post-stroke and amputee individuals [14], and specifically during dual-task overground walking in older adults to quantify stability adaptations under divided attention [15]. Despite its established utility, few studies have employed MoS in combination with spatiotemporal gait parameters to quantitatively evaluate balance control during walking in older adults with sarcopenia.

Conventional balance assessments frequently employ single-task paradigms, such as quiet standing or straight-line walking [16, 17]. However, daily functioning often requires concurrent motor and cognitive tasks, such as walking while engaging in conversation, which compete for attentional resources and can compromise gait stability [16]. Dual-task paradigms, which combine a primary motor task with either a motor or cognitive secondary task, more accurately reflect real-world functional demands [18, 19]. Evidence indicates that dual-task walking increases gait variability in older adults and is more strongly associated with fall risk than single-task assessments. Dual-task paradigms are broadly categorized by their secondary task demand: CMDT involves a cognitive secondary task (e.g., mental calculation), competing for attentional resources; motor-motor dual-tasks (MMDT) involve a motor secondary task (e.g., object carriage), increasing biomechanical and coordinative demands. Thus, CMDT may disrupt postural control by diverting cognitive resources, whereas MMDT may impose greater biomechanical constraints. Nevertheless, the effects of both CMDT and MMDT on walking stability in older adults with sarcopenia have been inconsistently reported across studies, with findings ranging from impaired stability to adaptive compensation [20, 21]. Consequently, the distinct effects of these dual-task paradigms in this population warrant further investigation.

To address this gap, the present study aims to compare older adults with sarcopenia to healthy controls under single-task (ST), CMDT, and MMDT conditions, and to assess alterations in dynamic stability and gait parameters. Correlation analyses will be conducted to investigate associations between gait metrics and dynamic stability in order to elucidate the biomechanical mechanisms underlying stability impairments in sarcopenia. By clarifying these mechanisms, this study aims to inform the future development of more targeted assessment and intervention strategies for fall prevention in this vulnerable population. We hypothesize that older adults with sarcopenia will exhibit reduced dynamic stability (lower MoS) and altered gait compared to healthy controls across all walking conditions. Furthermore, we hypothesize that within the sarcopenia group, the CMDT will affect stability and gait more severely than the MMDT.

Methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for both groups were: (1) age ≥ 65 years; (2) ability to stand and walk independently without assistive devices; (3) no history of trauma or surgery in the past six months; (4) no falls within the past three years; (5) Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score meeting education-adjusted cutoffs (≥ 17 for illiteracy, ≥ 20 for primary education, ≥ 24 for secondary education or higher) [22]; and (6) provision of signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of fracture or surgery affecting the pelvis, trunk, or lower limbs; (2) significant structural abnormalities such as leg length discrepancy, genu valgum, or genu varum; (3) neuromuscular or musculoskeletal conditions affecting gait; (4) severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease; or (5) significant hearing impairment.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Shandong Sport University (Approval No. 2024044) on September 9, 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion.

Participants

A priori power analysis was carried out in G*Power 3.1 (v3.1.9.2; Universität Kiel, Germany), adopting an alpha of 0.05 and statistical power of 0.80. Based on a previously reported effect size for gait speed across task conditions (ST: 0.81 ± 0.11 m/s; CMDT: 0.75 ± 0.11 m/s; MMDT: 0.72 ± 0.10 m/s; P = 0.009, η2 = 0.102) [21], the estimated required sample size per group was 16. In the current study, a total of 40 older adults from Jinan, China, were enrolled through community-based recruitment efforts, including the distribution of informational flyers and public presentations. The cohort comprised 20 patients diagnosed with sarcopenia and 20 matched healthy controls.

Participants were diagnosed with sarcopenia based on the AWGS 2019 criteria [2], which require the presence of low muscle mass and either low muscle strength or poor physical performance. Low muscle mass was defined as an appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) < 7.0 kg/m2 for men and < 5.4 kg/m2 for women, measured via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA; GE Lunar, Madison, WI, USA). Low muscle strength was defined as handgrip strength < 28 kg for men and < 18 kg for women (assessed with a handheld dynamometer, recording the maximum of three trials), and poor physical performance was defined as a completion time ≥ 12 s on the 5-Times Chair Stand Test (5CST).

Procedures

Prior to testing, participants donned form-fitting clothing and athletic footwear. Fifty-three reflective markers were affixed to anatomical landmarks based on the Helen Hayes marker configuration [23]. All the participants were instructed to walk under three conditions: 1) ST, 2) CMDT, and 3) MMDT. The order of the three walking conditions (ST, CMDT, MMDT) was randomized for each participant. A complete randomization procedure was used, assigning each possible sequence with equal probability to minimize order effects. Before formal data acquisition, all participants completed a minimum of three practice trials for each condition. Each trial began with a “start” cue, followed by walking a 6-m distance at a self-selected comfortable speed. A trial was considered successful when the participant’s foot completely landed on a single force platform. The gait tasks were defined as follows: 1) ST: Walking without any secondary task; 2) CMDT: Walking while subtracting serial threes from a randomly provided two-digit number [24]; 3) MMDT: Walking while carrying a glass filled with 0.67 kg of water (to 1 cm below the rim) in their right hand [25]. To ensure task difficulty was comparable across participants, the starting number for the CMDT was randomly selected from a pre-determined set (e.g., 50–99). For the MMDT, the water volume and container were identical for all participants. Performance was monitored: during CMDT, the number of correct subtractions and errors was recorded by an experimenter; for MMDT, a trial was considered successful only if no water was spilled. Hand dominance was assessed before testing by self-report. All participants were right-hand dominant; therefore, the glass was carried in the dominant (right) hand to standardize task execution. Three successful trials were conducted for each condition, with a one-minute rest period between trials. It should be noted that the use of only two dual-task paradigms, while allowing for a focused comparison, may limit the generalizability of the findings to other untested task combinations. An assistant stood behind the participant during testing to ensure safety and prevent falls.

Kinematic and kinetic data were collected using a 12-camera infrared motion capture system (Vicon, Oxford Metrics, UK) sampling at 200 Hz and two force platforms (AMTI, Inc., Watertown, MA, USA) sampling at 1000 Hz.

Data analysis

Data were processed using Vicon Nexus (v2.12.1; Oxford Metrics, UK) and exported in C3D format for subsequent biomechanical analysis in Visual3D (v2023.06.3; C-Motion, USA). Marker trajectories interrupted by occlusion were gap-filled using cubic spline interpolation. Raw marker data were filtered with a 4th-order zero-lag Butterworth low-pass filter at 10 Hz [26]. Ground reaction force (GRF) signals were low-pass filtered at 50 Hz [27]. Center of mass (CoM) position and velocity were derived in Visual 3D from the trajectories of a full-body marker set.

Dynamic stability was evaluated using the margin of stability (MoS), which represents the shortest distance from the extrapolated center of mass (XCoM) to the boundary of the base of support (BoS) [13]. The BoS boundaries were defined according to the Plug-in-Gait full-body model using the following anatomical landmarks: the toe marker(TOE), the first metatarsophalangeal (1MTP) marker, the fifth metatarsophalangeal (5MTP) marker, the heel marker(HEEL), the lateral malleolus marker(LM), and the medial malleolus marker(MM). In the anterior–posterior (AP) direction, the BoS boundary was determined by the most anterior (TOE) and most posterior (HEEL) marker positions. In the medio-lateral (ML) direction, the BoS boundary was defined by the most lateral (5MTP and LM) and most medial (1MTP and MM) marker positions [28]. In the laboratory coordinate system, the AP direction corresponds to the X-axis (positive = forward) and the ML direction to the Y-axis (positive = left). MoSap > 0: XCoM is posterior to the BoS (behind heel), indicating forward stability; MoSap < 0: XCoM is anterior to the BoS (in front of toe), indicating forward instability; MoSml > 0: XCoM is left of the BoS (relative to right foot), indicating leftward lateral stability; MoSml < 0: XCoM is right of the BoS, indicating rightward lateral instability. Larger absolute MoS values reflect greater XCoM deviation from the BoS, corresponding to reduced dynamic stability [12]. This means that when the body’s predicted momentum carries it beyond the limits of the feet, an immediate corrective action (like taking a step) is required to prevent a fall. This metric provides an objective biomechanical measure of balance control during gait by integrating CoM kinematics with the spatial constraints of stability. Previous studies have indicated that foot strike during the swing phase represents a critical point of increased instability due to elevated forward momentum and a reduced BoS during single-limb support [13]. As the dominant limb typically bears greater responsibility for balance maintenance and sustains higher mechanical loads, the instant of foot strike on the dominant limb (determined as the preferred leg for kicking a ball) was the focus of the present analysis.

The analyzed gait parameters included temporal and spatial measures, defined as follows: step length (m) was the distance from heel strike of one foot to heel strike of the opposite foot; stride length (m) was the distance traveled between successive heel strikes of the same foot; step width (m) was the horizontal distance between the centers of both heels; gait speed (m/s) was the distance walked per unit time; gait cycle (s) was the interval between two successive heel strikes of the same foot; and double support phase (s) was the time from heel strike of one foot to toe-off of the opposite foot. Data from the dominant limb were selected for analysis.

Statistical analysis

The normality of distribution for all outcome measures was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Baseline characteristics between groups were compared using independent-samples t-tests. To evaluate the main effects of group and task condition, as well as their interaction effect on dependent variables, a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Where a significant interaction was identified, pairwise post hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction were performed to elucidate specific between-group and within-group differences. Effect sizes for ANOVA are presented as partial eta-squared (ηp2), with values ≥ 0.14 interpreted as large, between 0.06 and 0.14 as medium, and between 0.01 and 0.06 as small. The relationships between dynamic stability and gait metrics were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed data and Spearman’s rank correlation for non-normally distributed data. Pearson's correlation was specifically applied to the variables of interest in this analysis as they met the assumption of normality, confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test (P > 0.05). Correlations were categorized as strong (|r|≥ 0.50), moderate (0.30 ≤|r|< 0.50), or weak (0.10 ≤|r|< 0.30) [29]. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was adopted for all tests. Analyses were performed in SPSS, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Consistent with the diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia, the sarcopenia group exhibited significantly lower values in body weight, body mass index (BMI), appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI), and handgrip strength, along with a significantly prolonged completion time on the Five-Times Sit-to-Stand Test (5CST) (P < 0.05). Gait parameters of older adults during walking under different dual-task conditions

CMDT showed the most pronounced decline in gait stability. Significant task-by-group interactions were observed for key gait parameters, including step length, stride length, gait speed, gait cycle duration, and double support phase (P < 0.05, η2 = 0.089–0.504). As shown in Table 2, the sarcopenia group exhibited shorter step lengths than the healthy group during both ST and CMDT (P < 0.01). Both groups showed reduced step length during dual tasks compared to ST (P < 0.05). Regarding stride length, the sarcopenia group exhibited shorter stride lengths during ST than the healthy group (P = 0.001). The healthy group demonstrated reduced stride length in both dual tasks (P < 0.001), whereas the sarcopenia group only showed a reduction during MMDT (P = 0.014). Gait speed decreased under dual-task conditions in both groups (P < 0.001), and the healthy group consistently walked faster than the sarcopenia group across all tasks. The sarcopenia group exhibited longer gait cycle durations during MMDT than the healthy group (P < 0.001), and both groups demonstrated prolonged gait cycles during dual tasks compared to ST (P < 0.001). The double support phase was increased during CMDT and MMDT in both groups relative to ST (P < 0.001). Although no significant interaction was observed for step width (P = 0.683), the sarcopenia group exhibited wider step widths than the healthy group during dual tasks (P < 0.05). The healthy group demonstrated an increased step width only during CMDT (P = 0.039).

Correlation between dynamic stability and gait parameters

The correlation analysis in patients with sarcopenia indicated a strong positive correlation between gait speed and MoSap (r = 0.530, P < 0.001; Fig. 2C). In contrast, no significant associations were observed between MoSap and stride length (r = –0.017, P = 0.899; Fig. 2A), step width (r = 0.219, P = 0.093; Fig. 2B), or step length (r = 0.000, P = 0.998; Fig. 2D). Furthermore, moderate negative correlations were observed between MoSml and both stride length (r = 0.375, P = 0.003; Fig. 3A) and step length (r = 0.371, P = 0.004; Fig. 3D). However, MoSml showed no significant correlation with step width (r = 0.115, P = 0.380; Fig. 3B) or gait speed (r = −0.187, P = 0.153; Fig. 3C).

Discussion

This study examined gait parameters and the MoS in older adults with sarcopenia during dual-task walking. Compared with healthy controls, the sarcopenia group exhibited greater declines in MoS and gait performance under both dual-task conditions. Specifically, reductions in MoSap and vCoM were observed that were partially compensated by an increase in MoSml. Nonetheless, overall dynamic stability remained impaired. The CMDT exerted a stronger effect than the MMDT, as evidenced by larger decreases in step length, gait speed, and vCoMml during CMDT. These findings suggest that sarcopenia is associated with an increased risk of falls during cognitive dual tasks, indicating that CMDT may serve as a candidate tool for fall risk assessment. Furthermore, these results could provide a foundation for future research exploring cognitive-motor training interventions to enhance gait adaptability. This study extends previous findings by employing the biomechanical measure MoS to quantitatively demonstrate that cognitive-motor interference, rather than simply motor load, is the primary mechanism underlying dynamic stability impairments in sarcopenia under dual-task conditions.

The gait cycle reflects integrated neuromuscular control, while postural stability depends on the capacity of the sensorimotor system to respond to external perturbations [30]. During MMDT, patients with sarcopenia exhibited reduced MoSap and vCoMap, which were partially compensated by increased MoSml. In addition, increases were observed in step width, gait cycle duration, and double support time, alongside decreases in step length, stride length, and gait speed. These effects were more pronounced under CMDT, indicating a generalized impairment of gait. Consistent with cognitive load theory, excessive task demands may exceed available attentional resources, thereby impairing postural control [31, 32]. Sarcopenia-related reductions in muscle mass and strength compromise lower limb support and neuromuscular coordination, which may account for the observed gait deficits [21]. Additionally, impaired muscle-brain signaling may diminish sensorimotor integration. During dual-tasking—particularly CMDT—competition for attentional resources may further disrupt the central control of gait and posture.

MMDT may compromise postural stability by directly increasing demands on the motor system. The object-carrying task elevates upper limb load and alters the distribution of the center of mass, thereby raising postural control requirements—particularly in older adults with pre-existing muscle weakness [21]. Notably, the sarcopenia group appeared to adopt a “stability-first” strategy under MMDT, prioritizing balance over mobility, as evidenced by increased step width (and MoSₘₗ) at the cost of reduced gait speed—a finding consistent with Montero-Odasso et al. [33]. In contrast, CMDT exerted more specific and pronounced effects in the sarcopenia group, including further reductions in step length and gait speed, as well as impaired control of mediolateral center of mass velocity. These observations are consistent with research by Mori et al. [34], whose study revealed extended support phases in individuals with sarcopenia, supporting the view that longer stance times may represent a compensatory strategy to improve stability. Taken together, these results indicate that performing dual-task conditions may worsen gait adaptations and hinder stability control, potentially elevating fall risk among older adults with sarcopenia.

Correlation analyses further elucidated the relationship between gait parameters and dynamic stability. Gait speed was positively correlated with MoSₐₚ, and this association was consistent across tasks, suggesting that slower speed may impair anteroposterior stability. In contrast, MoSₐₚ did not correlate significantly with stride length, step width, or step length, indicating that anteroposterior stability appears to depend more on overall gait speed than on discrete spatial parameters. A negative correlation was observed between MoSₘₗ and both step length and stride length, suggesting that shorter steps may be associated with enhanced mediolateral stability. This observed trade-off between shorter strides and maintained lateral stability may reflect a compensatory gait strategy, wherein spatial parameters could be adjusted to preserve stability at the cost of locomotor efficiency. However, the absence of correlation between MoSₘₗ and step width or gait speed suggests that mediolateral stability may be more strongly influenced by spatial rather than velocity-related factors.

The effect of dual-tasking on older adults with sarcopenia remains debated. Some studies report a greater impact of motor dual tasks due to increased motor load [35], whereas others indicate that cognitive dual tasks are more detrimental, although mechanistic evidence remains limited [21, 36]. The present study demonstrated that control of mediolateral center of mass velocity was significantly impaired in the sarcopenia group during CMDT. This supports the notion that cognitive dual tasks exert a greater adverse effect and highlights impaired lateral control as a key underlying mechanism. According to cognitive-motor interference theory, cognitive tasks compete for limited attentional resources, thereby disrupting neural control of gait [35]. In sarcopenia, age-related cognitive decline and potential muscle-brain axis dysfunction may further diminish available attentional resources [10]. Electroencephalography (EEG) studies have demonstrated that dual-tasking enhances long-range phase synchrony (functional connectivity) between parietal and frontal cortical areas, which may suggest heightened cortical involvement [37]. When performing concurrent tasks, the scarcity of attentional resources challenges sensorimotor integration and neuromuscular coordination. Adaptive gait strategies—such as reduced cadence, prolonged gait cycle, and longer support time—may reflect a compensatory cautious strategy [21]. The pronounced deficit in lateral control during CMDT is likely attributable to the fact that mediolateral stability requires precise cognitive monitoring and rapid adjustments, functions that may be impaired in sarcopenia. These findings underscore reduced cognitive-motor integration as a potential key factor contributing to increased fall risk in this population, further supporting the consideration of CMDT in fall risk evaluation.

This study employed the biomechanical measure MoS to assess dual-task gait in older adults with sarcopenia, which overcomes the limited sensitivity of conventional gait parameters in evaluating stability. By integrating the center of mass trajectory with base of support data, MoS provides a more comprehensive and objective assessment of postural control [14]. Correlation analyses further quantified the relationships between gait parameters and dynamic stability, providing empirical insight into how specific gait adaptations affect stability. The study compared different dual-task paradigms and identified CMDT as especially detrimental in sarcopenia, revealing mechanistic pathways and thereby supporting targeted approaches to risk assessment. While ST evaluation can detect basic gait abnormalities and stability deficits, dual-task conditions—particularly CMDT—uncover compensatory limitations and reveal deficits, such as impaired lateral control, that may remain undetected during ST. These dual-task responses more accurately reflect real-world fall risk. Thus, incorporating CMDT into fall risk assessment for sarcopenic older adults appears clinically warranted. The study employed rigorously standardized protocols to ensure reliability, though the findings are limited to the specific experimental paradigms used.

In summary, based on the observed associations, CMDT paradigms may be considered as a potential component of fall risk assessments for older adults with sarcopenia, though further longitudinal studies are needed to establish their predictive validity and clinical utility. Standardized protocols, for example, walking while performing serial subtraction, can enhance the detection of subtle postural control deficits. Clinically, these results could support the exploration of cognitive-motor dual-task training in rehabilitation. The identified compensatory strategies—such as increased step width—and the observed relationships between gait parameters and stability may inform personalized interventions, thereby enabling therapists to design tailored balance training programs that account for individual adaptive patterns.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the relatively small sample size and the cross-sectional design limit the ability to infer causal relationships between sarcopenia and the observed dual-task deficits. Second, by excluding individuals with a history of recent falls, our sample primarily applies higher-functioning, community-dwelling older adults with sarcopenia. While this design strengthened internal validity, it limits the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should include more diverse populations to enhance the generalizability of findings. Third, the ecological validity of our findings is limited by the use of only two dual-task paradigms. The CMDT mimics divided attention during walking, and the MMDT represents a common object-transport task. These tasks represent specific types of interference but may not capture the complexity of real-world multitasking. Future studies should therefore examine a broader range of dual-task conditions. Finally, our study has several methodological and clinical limitations. Sarcopenia was diagnosed using the height-adjusted ASMI, a common method that may, however, underestimate the condition in individuals with high adiposity. Additionally, we did not assess key potential confounders such as vestibular function, medication use (especially drugs affecting balance or cognition), vitamin B12/D levels, or depression. The absence of these assessments represents a limitation that may have influenced the outcomes and should be considered in future research.

Conclusion

Older adults with sarcopenia exhibit pronounced gait alterations and reduced dynamic stability under dual-task conditions, characterized by decreased stride length, slower gait speed, and diminished mediolateral control of the center of mass, particularly during cognitive-motor dual tasks. These changes are accompanied by prolonged gait cycles and double support durations. The strong association between gait speed and MoSₐₚ, as well as the moderate inverse correlations between MoSₘₗ and both stride and step length, underscore the interdependence between gait and stability in contributing to fall risk. The integration of dual-task evaluation into clinical practice may be considered as a useful assessment option for identifying functional deficits that could inform the development of tailored intervention strategies aimed at improving balance control and mitigating fall risk in this population.
