Prenatal omega-3 fatty acids supplementation mitigates some schizophrenia-like deficits in offspring: A PET and MRI study in a rat model
Authors: Diego Romero-Miguel, Marta Casquero-Veiga, Nicolás Lamanna-Rama, Raquel Chávez-Valencia, Vanessa Gómez-Rangel, Ravit Hadar, Christine Winter, Manuel Desco, María Luisa Soto-Montenegro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOmega-3 fatty acids (OM) have been employed as adjunctive therapy in patients with schizophrenia, but their potential as a preventive strategy remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate whether dietary OM supplementation during pregnancy may prevent schizophrenia-like deficits in a maternal immune stimulation (MIS) model. Pregnant Wistar rats were injected with PolyI:C (i.e. MIS) or Saline on gestational day 15. They were fed with OM-enriched chow, either from MIS until delivery (OM7) or throughout gestation (OM21). Two control groups (Saline-naïve & MIS-naïve) were maintained on standard chow. In adulthood, offspring were assessed through tests for locomotion, anxiety and short- and long-term memory (STM, LTM). Brain glucose metabolism and structural in vivo neuroimaging studies were performed using PET and MRI techniques. OM7/OM21 treatments prevented the hyperlocomotion in MIS-offspring while STM deficits were only improved by OM7. Both OM7/OM21 treatments prevented certain metabolic and volumetric changes in the cortex, as well as the enlargement of the ventricles. Additionally, OM21 protected WM integrity in MIS-offspring. Altogether, our study highlights the potential of prenatal OM dietary supplementation to ameliorate some of the SCZ-like deficits observed in the MIS model.Subject terms: Schizophrenia, Long-term memory

Introduction

Schizophrenia, a chronic neurodevelopmental disorder, affects approximately 1% of the global population. Increasing knowledge of its aetiology, particularly its association with prenatal and early postnatal environmental factors, has generated interest in prevention [1–4]. Consequently, research has focused on the prodromal stage to evaluate early interventions that may reduce the risk of psychosis or prevent its onset [5–8].

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant (anti-IOS) drugs are widely used as therapeutic strategies [9–12]. Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (OM) have garnered attention not only due to their anti-IOS properties [13, 14] and safety [15], but also due to the abnormalities in OM metabolism observed in patients with schizophrenia [16, 17]. Accordingly, 25 clinical trials are investigating the use of OM or eicosapentaenoic acid alongside antipsychotics in schizophrenia [18, 19]. Although several studies have suggested that OM supplementation may enhance antipsychotic efficacy and ameliorate certain symptoms of schizophrenia [20–23], such as reductions in serum triglycerides [21] and associations with less severe psychopathology [24], findings across clinical trials are disparate. Early OM interventions during the first-episode of psychosis (FEP) showed promising results as adjunct therapy [25, 26], but initial findings have not been consistently replicated in subsequent trials [27, 28]. These inconsistencies highlight the importance of exploring even earlier intervention windows However, to date, the potential of OM strategies during pregnancy, a developmental period highly sensitive to environmental influences, remains unexplored in clinical settings, despite its relevance for long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Animal models are essential for evaluating early therapeutic interventions. Among them, the maternal immune stimulation (MIS) rat model, which links maternal systemic inflammation during pregnancy to a higher schizophrenia risk, is a well-established neurodevelopmental model of schizophrenia [29]. Previously, we showed that early peri-adolescent treatment with risperidone [30] or anti-IOS compounds, such as n-acetylcysteine [31], OM [32], and minocycline [33] prevented sensorimotor gating deficits, hippocampal volume reduction, and partially counteracted IOS deficits via the iNOS and Nrf2-ARE pathways. Given these promising results and OM’s safety profile, we extended the preventive strategy to the prenatal period. Recent findings showed OM supplementation during pregnancy partially mitigated dopamine deficits and protected against behavioural deficits and lateral ventricle enlargement in the MIS offspring [34]. Building on this, our study aimed to examine OM supplementation’s impact during two treatment periods: from immune stimulation until delivery (OM7) and throughout gestation (OM21). This is the first study to explore these effects using functional neuroimaging (positron emission tomography [PET] with 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-D-glucose [FDG]) and morphometric neuroimaging (using magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] voxel-based morphometry [VBM]) in rodents, along with behavioural assessments.

Material and methods

Animals

Thirty-two pregnant Wistar rats and 68 males from their offspring were maintained at constant temperature (24 ± 0.5 °C) under 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 am), with free access to standard or OM-supplemented rodent chow, as appropriate, and water. The sample size was estimated using the Lenth software (http://www.stat.uiowa.edu/~rlenth/Power). All animals received the same procedures, behaviour and imaging protocols. All procedures were conducted in conformity with the European Communities Council Directive 2010/63/EU and the ARRIVE guidelines [35], and approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation of our hospital and Community of Madrid (PROEX-41/18).

Pharmacological treatment

Figure 1A illustrates the study design. Pregnant Wistar rats were intravenously injected with PolyI:C (4 mg/kg, N = 17) or Saline (N = 15) on gestational day (GD) 15 [32, 36]. Four groups of pregnant dams had free access to OM-enriched chow ( ~ 18% EPA and 12% DHA, Ssniff Spezialdiäten GmbH, Germany), either from the immune stimulation until delivery (GD15–GD21, Saline-OM7 & MIS-OM7), or during the whole gestation (GD1–GD21, Sal-OM21 & MIS-OM21). The OM-enriched chow was stored at −20 °C and food was replaced every two days to prevent oxidation. Besides, two groups of animals received normal chow (Saline-naïve & MIS-naïve). After weaning, 2–3 males from each litter were randomly selected for inclusion in the study to control for litter effects, and were assigned to their corresponding experimental group.

Behavioural evaluation

Behavioural assessments were conducted during the animals’ active phase (i.e., dark cycle) at postnatal day (PND) 70–80 (9–12 animals/group). The sequence of tests included the open field (OF), novel place recognition (NPR), and novel object recognition (NOR) tests.

OF test

The OF test was used to examine hyperactivity and anxiety. Animals were placed in the centre of a square Plexiglas arena (50 x 50 x 50 cm) under dim illumination (30 lx) [37] for 10 min and videotaped. The floor was divided into 25 squares (10 × 10 cm). The nine central squares (40 × 40 cm) represented the “central zone” while the surrounding border zone represented the “surrounding area”. The time spent in the central (indicative of anxiety-related behaviour) and surrounding areas, the number of square limit crossings with both forepaws (indicative of hyperactivity), rearing, and grooming were recorded.

NPR and NOR tests

The NPR test was used to evaluate spatial learning memory whereas the NOR test was used to evaluate non-spatial learning memory of object identity [38]. Both short and long-term memory were evaluated. On day 1, rats were habituated to the Plexiglas arena in the absence of objects for 10 min, during which the OF test was conducted. On day 2, rats underwent a 10-min NPR acquisition with identical objects located in opposite corners. One hour later, short-term memory (STM-NPR) was tested by relocating one object to a new placement, and rats were allowed to explore for 10 min. On day 3, long-term memory (LTM-NPR) was tested. The old location was changed by a new one and rats were given 10 min to explore it. One hour later, rats were tested for STM-NOR. During this phase, one familiar (F) object was replaced by a novel one (N) and rats were allowed to explore the arena for 10 min [39]. Finally, on day 4, rats were tested for long-term memory (LTM-NOR). The object N was replaced by a new one (N’) and rats were given 10 min to explore. All sessions were videotaped and manually scored by a blind-experimenter. The time of exploration of the new object or place were evaluated. The difference in the exploration time of novel and familiar object/place was calculated using the discrimination index (DI) as (time new - time old) / (time new + time old).

Imaging studies

Saline and MIS-offspring were scanned at PND120 (adulthood) (10–15 animals/group).

Positron emission tomography (PET) and computed tomography (CT)

Animals were scanned using a small-animal ARGUS PET/CT scanner (SEDECAL, Spain) under sevoflurane anaesthesia (6% induction, 3.5% maintenance in 100% O2). FDG (~37 MBq) was injected through the tail vein and, after 45 min of uptake, animals were imaged for 45 min. PET acquisition and 2D-OSEM reconstruction protocols followed a previously published study [32]. CT images were acquired (40 kV, 340 µA, 8 shots, 360 projections and binning 4) and reconstructed using a Feldkamp algorithm [40]. PET data post-processing and intensity normalization were performed using a data-driven approach, to performed a voxel-by-voxel analysis [32, 41]. Additionally, six regions of interest (ROIs) (caudate-putamen, prefrontal cortex (PFC), cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, whole brain) were manually segmented by a blinded and experienced operator according to the Paxinos and Watson Rat Brain Atlas [42]:, and normalized in intensity to the same non-significant area used in the SPM analysis.

Anatomical magnetic resonance imaging (T2-MRI)

Animals were scanned using a 7-Tesla Biospec 70/20 scanner (Bruker, Germany) under sevoflurane anaesthesia (6% induction, 3.5% maintenance in 100% O2). Coronal T2-weighted images of the brain were acquired with a Spin-Echo sequence with TE = 33 ms, TR = 3732 ms, and a slice thickness of 0.8 mm (34 slices). The matrix size was 256 × 256 pixels covering a squared field of view of 3.5 × 3.5 cm. VBM analysis was performed following a protocol described elsewhere [30]. Grey matter (GM) and white matter (WM) images were modulated and smoothed using a 10-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Additionally, four ROIs (hippocampus, ventricles, corpus callosum, whole brain) were manually segmented according to the rat brain atlas [42].

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS)

In vivo 1H-MRS images were acquired with a PRESS isotropic mono-voxel sequence in the dorsal hippocampus (including CA1 subregion) and the prefrontal cortex (FC) (Supplementary Figure 1). The acquisition parameters were as follows: TR = 2500 ms, TE = 16.5 ms, acquisition points = 2048, 128 averages and band width = 3,301.06 Hz. Voxel size was adjusted to the anatomical structure (1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm3). An unsuppressed water signal was used as an internal reference for quantification. In vivo 1H-MRS spectra were analysed using LCModel (Version 6.2-0U, S.W.Provencher) [43]. Only metabolites with a Cramer-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) < 25% [44] were included in the analysis, utilizing a “basis set” obtained from in vitro spectra [43]. Metabolites were normalized to the creatine value, since it is the most stable metabolite. Moreover, metabolites with overlapping resonances were shown as the sum of both species. Macromolecules (MM) and lipids (Lip) were also included.

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)

Structural changes in the WM were evaluated using DTI. SE-EPI sequences were acquired, with TR = 2000 ms, TE = 26.55 ms, 3 repetitions, diffusion gradients applied in 27 directions (b-value = 1000 s/mm2), 35 slices, 0.4 mm thickness, matrix size of 128 × 128 voxels and FOV of 35 × 35 mm2. Fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) were determined in the corpus callosum, PFC, and hippocampus [42].

Statistical analysis

Behavioural tests, 1H-MRS and PET, T2 and DTI ROIs

The main factors of the study were phenotype (Saline, MIS) and treatment (naïve, OM7, OM21). Outliers were identified using the ROUT test (Q set to 1%) and removed from analyses. The normality and homoscedasticity of each variable were tested using Shapiro–Wilk’s and Levene’s tests, respectively. Non-parametric analyses were performed when normality or homoscedasticity were not respected. Data were analysed using 2-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post-hoc or Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Statistical analyses and graphical representation were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

PET and T2-MRI voxel wise analyses

The analyses of FDG-PET, GM and WM data were conducted using the SPM12 software (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). Groups were compared using 2-way ANOVA, setting a significance threshold of p < 0.01 (PET data), and p < 0.001 (GM and WM data), uncorrected for multiple comparisons at the voxel level but cluster-based corrected by false discovery rate (FDR) in order to control type II errors [45]. Moreover, only significant clusters with a minimum of 100 connected voxels for PET data and 1000 voxels for MRI data were included.

Results

Behavioural studies

OF test (Fig. 1B, Suppl. Table 1-A)

MIS-naïve offspring exhibited higher activity levels, as shown by an increased number of crossings compared to saline-naïve offspring (p < 0.01). This hyperactivity was prevented by both OM7 (p < 0.05) and OM21 (p < 0.01). Additionally, MIS-offspring spent more time in the central zone and less time in the surrounding zone, behaviours that were also prevented by both OM7 (p < 0.001) and OM21 (surrounding zone: p < 0.001 and central zone: p < 0.05). Additionally, OM supplementation increased grooming frequency in MIS-offspring in a dose-dependent manner.

NPR and NOR test results (Fig. 1C, Suppl. Table 1-B)

MIS-offspring showed deficits in the STM for spatial information, as evidenced by reduced DI in the NPR test compared to Saline-naïve animals (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C-1), that was prevented by OM7 (p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found in the NPR-LTM or the NOR at the STM and LTM (Fig. 1C-2).

VBM analysis

Grey matter: Pharmacological effect (Fig. 3B, Table 1B): OM7 induced a bilateral enlargement in the cortex in Saline-offspring, while in MIS-offspring an enlargement was observed in the septum, nucleus accumbens and ventral pallidum. OM21 induced a shrinkage in the left amygdalohippocampal area and cingulate cortex in Saline-offspring, and in the corpus callosum and cingulate cortex in both hemispheres in MIS-offspring. Additionally, OM21 induced an enlargement in the brainstem (raphe nucleus), and cerebellum in Saline-offspring, and in the inferior and superior colliculi, cerebellum and left globus pallidus in MIS-offspring. Treatment effect (Fig. 3C, Table 1B): OM7 enlarged the GM in the cortex, cerebellum and olfactory bulb in the MIS-offspring compared to naïve Saline-offspring. On the other hand, OM21 induced a shrinkage in the frontal and cingulate cortices, while it produced an enlargement in the cerebellum and ectorhinal/entorhinal cortex in the MIS-offspring compared to naïve Saline-offspring.

White matter: Pharmacological effect (Fig. 4A, Table 1C): OM7 induced an enlargement in the left forceps major of the corpus callosum and hippocampal fissure in Saline-offspring, while no changes were found in MIS-offspring. OM21 induced a shrinkage in the medial longitudinal fasciculus and dorsal tegmental bundle and an enlargement in the cerebellar white matter in Saline-offspring, while it induced a shrinkage in the left inferior cerebellar peduncle and the right intermediate white layer of the superior colliculus in MIS animals. Treatment effect (Fig. 4B, Table 1C): OM7 and OM21 induced a shrinkage in the dorsal tegmental tract and dorsal longitudinal fasciculus in MIS-offspring compared to naïve Saline-offspring.

Post-hoc test revealed an almost significant reduction in MD in MIS-offspring compared to Saline-offspring (p < 0.056), which was prevented by OM21. Additionally, in Saline-offspring, OM7 reduced MD compared to controls (naïve animals), while OM21 increased MD relative to OM7-treated animals. Lastly, an increase in FA was found in the OM21 group of MIS-offspring. Hippocampus: ANOVA revealed a significant effect of OM on MD (p < 0.001) as well as a significant interaction between MIS and OM in MD (p < 0.05). Post-hoc test showed increased MD in the MIS-OM21 group compared to MIS-naïve animals. Corpus callosum: ANOVA showed a significant effect of OM on both FA (p < 0.05) and MD (p < 0.01). An interaction was found in MD (p < 0.01). Post-hoc test revealed an increase in MD in the MIS-OM21 group compared to MIS-naïve animals.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of omega-3 fatty acid (OM) supplementation during gestation as a preventive strategy to alleviate the progression toward a schizophrenia-like condition after inflammation-mediated neurodevelopmental disturbances. Notably, this is the first study to compare OM supplementation at two distinct temporal windows, starting from the MIS event on gestational day 15 or during the whole gestation. Interestingly, the latter approach aligns with similar strategies currently used and widely standardized to prevent congenital complications, such as folic acid supplementation during pregnancy [46, 47]. Our findings demonstrate that OM treatment during pregnancy prevents some schizophrenia-related abnormalities in adult MIS animals: 1) OM7 and OM21 prevent MIS-induced high activity levels, and OM7 also avoids short-term memory deficits, 2) OM7 increases glucose metabolism in cortical brain regions, while decreasing it in subcortical areas, 3) OM7 and OM21 prevent volumetric abnormalities in the ventricles, although not in the hippocampus, and 4) OM21 increases MD in the frontal cortex.

Prenatal OM supplementation improves some behavioural alterations in MIS offspring

Early intervention is increasingly recognized as crucial for preventing late symptoms. Nutraceuticals, with lack of significant side effects, are gaining traction [48, 49]. Currently, 25 clinical trials are evaluating OM supplementation in ultra-high risk individuals, patients with FEP, and those with stable schizophrenia [18]. Most report improvements in psychotic, negative, depressive, and cognitive symptoms [18]. Preclinical studies also show promising results with anti-IOS treatments during adolescence, including OM, preventing schizophrenia-like alterations in the MIS model [30, 32, 33, 50, 51]. Our own research demonstrated that OM supplementation during adolescence prevented sensorimotor gating deficits by regulating reduced DA concentration and DOPAC levels in the mPFC [52], while also preventing hippocampal reduction and partially counteracting IOS imbalances via the iNOS and Nrf2-ARE pathways in adulthood [32]. However, no clinical studies in schizophrenia have yet reported the effects of OM supplementation in a prenatal temporal window, as proposed here, and only one preclinical study performed by our group has addressed this issue [34]. Previously, we reported that OM7 supplementation during pregnancy led to a partial relief of later symptoms related to sensorimotor gating and spatial learning, as assessed through various hippocampal-dependent operant paradigms (PPI and discrimination reversal paradigm) [34]. In our current study, MIS-offspring exhibited higher activity in response to a novel environment, as demonstrated by the increased number of crossings between the central and surrounding zone in the open field test, along with impaired short-term memory in the novel place recognition task. These findings align with observations in schizophrenia-like animal models [53–55]. Surprisingly, in our study, MIS animals spent more time in the centre of the arena compared with the surrounding area, a behaviour consistently observed in this model in our laboratory (data not shown). Typically, increased grooming and reduced time spent in the centre are indicative of anxiety-related behaviour, often observed in preclinical models of mental disorders, such as depression, and stress disorders [56]. In schizophrenia models, anxiety is usually assessed through other behavioural tests, such as the social interaction test or the elevated plus maze (EPM), with the latter being the gold standard for measuring rodent anxiety [54, 57]. Indeed, some authors have suggested that the fear of heights may play a role in creating the phobic anxiety state measured in this behaviour assay rather than open spaces [51]. Thus, we hypothesize that the increased time spent in the centre of the arena, traditionally considered an “unsafe area”, may be less related to an anxiety effect and more reflective of altered sensory filtering or disinhibition caused by MIS, leading to a dysregulation of reactivity in a novel environment [58].

Remarkably, OM supplementation during both temporal windows effectively attenuated the increased activity observed in MIS animals, while short-term memory deficits were only improved by OM7 supplementation. Consistent with our study, a randomized controlled trial (COPSYCH study), is currently exploring the impact of prenatal OM supplementation on neurodevelopment, considering early life exposures such as infections and inflammatory processes [59]. The follow-up of these children has already demonstrated a link between MIS and attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) development at age 10 [60], aligning with the higher activity observed in our MIS-offspring. However, results regarding prenatal OM supplementation are yet to be published. The prevention of symptoms observed in our MIS model by prenatal OM supplementation is clearly promising for future clinical applications, supporting the hypothesis that OM metabolism plays a role in the aetiology of cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia.

Prenatal OM supplementation prevents cortical metabolic abnormalities in MIS offspring

PET studies in patients with schizophrenia have demonstrated the existence of cortical and subcortical dysfunctions [61], with global [62] and regional [63–66] alterations in brain glucose metabolism. However, limited clinical and preclinical PET neuroimaging studies have explored the relationship between OM supplementation and brain glucose metabolism, showing beneficial effects in Alzheimer’s disease, depression, and aging [67–74]. Few preclinical studies have examined the effect of OM-supplementation on brain glucose utilization, showing increased brain metabolism [32, 75], and highlighting the timing and duration of OM-supplementation as key to mitigating the negative effects of aging or disease [75].

So far, our group has conducted the only PET study evaluating changes on brain glucose metabolism induced by OM supplementation during adolescence in adult MIS-offspring [32]. We found that OM supplementation during this critical period reduce glucose metabolism in the deep mesencephalic nucleus (DMN) in these animals, suggesting a possible modulation of the dopaminergic activity by the treatment, which may counteract abnormal metabolic patterns in striatal and limbic areas [52].

In our study, OM7 supplementation during pregnancy significantly modulated brain glucose metabolism in MIS-offspring compared to Saline-offspring. These findings suggest that OM-induced effects depend on the brain’s pathological state, requiring a threshold to trigger its broad modulatory impact. The metabolic pattern was similar to that observed when comparing MIS-OM7 with MIS-naïve, though more pronounced due to the absence of phenotypic influence. Interestingly, in 2015, we reported that untreated adult MIS-offspring exhibited the exact opposite metabolic pattern to that expressed by MIS-OM7, with reduced cortical FDG uptake and increased limbic subcortical activity [52]. Thus, OM7 appears to effectively reverse these metabolic abnormalities, which were accompanied by significant prevention of symptoms.

Regarding OM21, our study showed no effect on brain glucose metabolism in MIS-offspring compared to naïve Saline-offspring, which could be interpreted as a positive outcome. This suggests that OM supplementation throughout gestation had a significant impact on regulating the immune response triggered by PolyI:C, preventing the metabolic schizophrenia-like deficits in MIS-offspring, as previously published [52]. It is possible that OM supplementation had counterbalanced the inflammatory response against the placenta and foetus, thereby preventing abnormalities in foetal brain development which may be associated with the observed behavioural improvements. Further studies are needed to elucidate how OM supplementation during pregnancy benefits brain glucose metabolism.

Finally, although not statistically significant, our MRS results revealed a marked reduction in NAA in the frontal cortex of OM21 animals, potentially indicating functional alterations within neuronal networks. Importantly, no corresponding behavioral impairments were detected, suggesting that this reduction may reflect an adaptative neurochemical response rather than overt neuronal damage. Notably, previous studies have shown that OM supplementation can influence resting metabolic rates, fatty acid oxidation, and neurotransmitter synthesis, factors that may indirectly modulate NAA levels [76]. Therefore, it is plausible that the observed NAA decrease reflects a neurochemical adaption rather than neuronal loss. Supporting this interpretation, our data showed no changes in glucose uptake in the frontal cortex of either saline or MIS animals, indicating that general brain metabolism in this area remains intact. This points instead to possible changes in neurotransmitter synthesis or cycling rather than in energy metabolism per se. Further studies should explore whether OM supplementation modulates specific neurotransmitter pathways to better understand the mechanisms underlying this finding.

Prenatal OM supplementation prevents volumetric abnormalities in the ventricles

Clinical neuroimaging studies investigating the association between OM supplementation and brain structure in schizophrenia are scarce. Schizophrenia has been associated with reduced global brain volume, particularly in the frontotemporal cortical [77] and subcortical GM regions [78], as well as increased ventricular volume [79]. OM supplementation has shown promise in attenuating cortical thickness or GM loss when used prophylactically in healthy individuals [80, 81] or at early stages of neurodegenerative processes [82, 83]. Additionally, it has been observed to reduce lateral ventricular volume in a drug-naive patient with schizophrenia, along with symptom remission and cerebral atrophy reversion [84], highlighting its potential as an adjunctive therapy [83].

In the MIS model, two consistent volumetric hallmarks induced by PolyI:C administration are reduced hippocampal volume and increased ventricular volume [30, 32, 52]. Our study replicated these ventricular abnormalities, although the MIS-related reduction in the hippocampus did not reach statistical significance. Furthermore, OM treatment in both temporal windows prevented this ventricular enlargement in the MIS model, underscoring OM supplementation’s potential as a preventive strategy against these deficits. Other preclinical studies have investigated the effects of OM supplementation using MRI techniques, primarily in rodent models of aging [44, 85, 86], but only one has examined its use during pregnancy in the MIS model [34]. In aging models, OM supplementation has been shown to increase volumes in the hippocampus, retrosplenial and prefrontal cortices [87], as well as enhanced hippocampal neurogenesis and dendritic arborization, leading to increased neuronal volume in mice [88]. However, other studies have shown no variation in brain volume in aged rats fed an OM-deficient diet [89]. In the MIS model, OM supplementation starting from the time of insult until delivery, same to one of the temporal windows explored in this study (OM7), was associated with prevention of lateral ventricle enlargement [34], consistent with our findings.

Additionally, VBM analysis revealed that OM7 supplementation in Saline-offspring induced GM enlargements in cortical areas. However, this effect was not observed neither in MIS-offspring nor with supplementation throughout gestation (OM21). Indeed, GM changes in OM21-offspring were subtle compared to those in OM7-offspring. Neuroimaging studies have consistently demonstrated GM volume reductions in cortical areas, including the prefrontal cortex, linked to positive symptoms in schizophrenia [90]. Additionally, OM supplementation combined with conventional antipsychotics has shown efficacy in reducing symptoms in schizophrenia [20–23] and has a favourable impact on cell membrane OM levels [24]. The lack of clinical studies on prenatal OM supplementation’s effect on brain structure limits direct comparisons with our results. However, given that early OM interventions in FEP patients reduce the risk of progression to psychosis [25], we hypothesize that initiating OM supplementation during pregnancy could offer comparable or even greater benefits.

Notably, OM21 supplementation resulted in few changes in brain volume and metabolism in MIS-offspring compared to Saline-offspring, although it did prevent hyperactivity deficits. Particularly, a reduction in GM was observed in the frontal cortex, potentially explaining the poor performance in the novel place recognition test, as the prefrontal cortex is crucial for memory and attention tasks [91]. The minimal GM changes in these animals may be related to the inflammatory state of the pregnant dam at the time of the insult. Thus, OM21 supplementation could be modulating the response to the PolyI:C effect by counteracting the exacerbated immune response, thereby reducing cerebral structural changes and preventing specific behavioural deficits.

WM integrity is crucial for effective communication between brain regions. In this study, we detected an almost significant decrease in MD in the PFC of MIS-offspring. This contrast with clinical findings showing increased MD in patients with schizophrenia and adolescents with early-onset psychosis [92], but agree with previous reports in the MIS model [36]. In human imaging studies, lower MD is typically interpreted as increased cellularity and structural integrity, while higher MD is associated with reduced cellular density and structural damage or structural disorganization. On the contrary, several DTI studies in the MIS model have consistently reported the opposite pattern [36, 93]: reduced MD would be associated with WM microstructural abnormalities, while increased MD might reflect improved WM integrity. Currently, we do not have an explanation for this reduction in MD in MIS-offspring, but it is a phenomenon consistently observed in this animal model in DTI studies [36, 93]. Importantly, OM treatment, particularly when administered throughout gestation, prevented this effect, suggesting a potential neuroprotective or modulatory role. Since MD is sensitive to microstructural changes in lipid-rich tissues like myelinated axons, it is plausible that OM supplementation influences the composition or organization of the fatty lipid bilayers within WM tracts. This aligns with the known role of OM in modulating membrane fluidity and promoting myelination. Further histological and microstructural studies of the WM fibres are needed to clarify this question. Additionally, we observed an unexpectedly significant MD reduction in the frontal cortex of healthy OM7-treated animals. One possible explanation involves NAA, a metabolite linked to myelin synthesis and maintenance [94]. However, this MD decrease was not accompanied by reduced NAA levels, or behavioural impairments. This could reflect minor or compensatory neurobiological adaptations rather than significant dysfunction, and insufficient to cause functional consequences. Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted with caution and may be considered in future investigations. Lastly, we found an interesting shrinkage in WM in the brainstem and thalamus, including the dorsal longitudinal fasciculus, tegmental tract and medial forebrain bundle in MIS-offspring from OM21-treated dams compared to Saline-offspring. These WM fibres connect the limbic forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain, and are involved in motivated behaviour, mood, and memory regulation [95]. Indeed, evidence of increased structural connectivity in schizophrenia has been previously reported, including the involvement of subcortical pathways, such as thalamocortical connections [96] and the medial forebrain bundle [92, 97]. In line with these observations, we found reduced WM in these brain areas due to OM treatment, highlighting potential thalamic and brainstem dysfunctions in MIS-offspring that may be prevented by OM treatment. This prevention likely contributed to better performance in behavioural tasks, underscoring the role of the thalamus and brainstem as critical hubs for the integration of multimodal processes [98].

Finally, this study has some limitations. First, it focused only on male-offspring. Female MIS-offspring do not exhibit the same deficits as males at the same age [99], likely due to the different onset of disease, which occurs earlier in males than in females [100]. Therefore, future studies are needed for a complete understanding of the disease across sexes. The second limitation is the lack of adjustments for multiple comparisons in the voxel-wise imaging analyses at the voxel level. Such adjustments would be too restrictive for an exploratory approach like ours. Nevertheless, we applied cluster-based corrections to prevent type I errors. Similarly, no attempt was made to correct for the multiple variables included in the study. Further replications of our results are warranted to solidly confirm the findings. Finally, methodological issues could be behind the lack of statistically significant differences in the MRS study. Further studies may consider increasing the number of averages and voxel size to achieve better spectra.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first PET/MRI combined study to show metabolic and volumetric changes in the brain induced by prenatal OM supplementation in the MIS model. We demonstrate that OM7 supplementation prevents cortical deficits in glucose metabolism, structural GM abnormalities in the cortex and the ventricular system, and modulated WM integrity in the MIS model. Notably, these changes were accompanied by better cognitive performance, normalized activity levels, and improved short-term memory. Altogether, our study highlights the potential of prenatal OM dietary supplementation to ameliorate some of the SCZ-like deficits observed in the MIS model.
