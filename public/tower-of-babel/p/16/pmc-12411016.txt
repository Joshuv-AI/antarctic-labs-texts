How reliable are surgeons at assessing soft-tissue laxities in robotic arm-assisted total knee arthroplasty?
Authors: James A Corbett, Darren B Chen, Price Gallie, Anthony Leong, Jonathan S Mulford, David Penn, Jil A Wood, Samuel J MacDessi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsRegardless of one’s alignment philosophy and belief in ligament releases, the precise estimation of soft-tissue laxities is crucial in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). This study prospectively assessed the reliability of surgeons’ and assistants’ measurements of ligament tension during robotic arm-assisted TKA.MethodsA prospective, surgeon-blinded study was performed in 123 patients undergoing TKA by six surgeon/assistant pairs. Ligament tensions were captured using manual stress and instrumented distraction techniques for four gaps: medial extension, lateral extension, medial flexion, and lateral flexion. Assessors were blinded during gap measurements. The primary outcome was intrarater and inter-rater reliability using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Secondary outcomes were the reliability of manual compared with instrumented distraction measures and comparison of planned pre-resection virtual gaps against actual post-implantation laxities.ResultsThe intrarater mean ICC for surgeons’ measurements of gap laxities was 0.95 (0.90 to 0.97) and 0.94 (0.91 to 0.96) for assistants’ measurements. These results indicate excellent reliability for all four gaps for both surgeons and assistants. For inter-rater reliability, both extension gaps returned an ICC > 0.90, indicating excellent reliability between surgeons and assistants. Lateral flexion ICC was 0.75 and medial flexion 0.86, indicating good reliability. Comparing manual with instrumented distraction techniques, the mean ICC was 0.72, indicating moderate reliability (0.55 to 0.88). The mean difference between planned and final gap measures was 0.5 mm (-1.5 to 3.0, SD 0.71).ConclusionSurgeons’ ability to assess knee laxities using manual stressing during robotic arm-assisted TKA has excellent intrarater and good-to-excellent inter-rater reliability. Distraction values for the medial side were more reliable than the lateral side, where constitutional laxity and the technical challenge of assessment may increase variability. High reliability of manual stressed gap assessment in robotic arm-assisted TKA should give surgeons confidence in using this information for measuring and achieving soft-tissue balance.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2025;6(9):1044–1052.Keywords: Ligament balance, Robotic surgery, Reliability, Functional alignment, Total knee arthroplasty, Knee balancing, soft-tissue laxities, Robotic Arm, total knee arthroplasty (TKA), laxities, flexion, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), Ligament, soft tissue balance, knee laxities, knees

Introduction

Irrespective of alignment technique, precise estimation of soft-tissue tension is crucial in total knee arthroplasty (TKA).1 This can be performed using manual stressing or distraction methods that use blunt instruments or laminar spreaders. Quantifying the tension beyond ‘feel’ in the hands of the surgeon involves optical navigation techniques that compute virtual gap laxities based on assessed soft-tissue tension and simulated bony resections.2 These laxity measurements are used to guide implant position and, if required, ligament releases, during robotic arm-assisted TKA (RA-TKA) with functional alignment (FA).3 Balancing these laxities by adjusting planned component position is the goal of FA, with the aim of reducing soft-tissue imbalance and improving outcomes after TKA.4

It has previously been demonstrated that gap laxities correlate with sensor-based intracompartmental loads.5 In a study comparing bicruciate stabilized and bicruciate-retaining knees, laxities measured with distraction technique using a tensioner device differed from laxities measured using manual varus-valgus stress.6 Another unblinded retrospective study showed that manual stressed laxities may differ depending on surgeon experience.7 A study comparing pre-resection manual stressing of flexion gaps with post-resection flexion gap assessment with a ligament tensor device found excellent correlation between the two measures.8 The alignment precision achieved with optical navigation technology has been conclusively established.9 Despite optimization of soft-tissue balance holding comparable relevance as alignment in TKA,10 surgeon differences in laxity assessment to guide balancing has yet to be established.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the reliability of manual stressed gap laxity measurements during primary RA-TKA. In addition, we aimed to determine if measures varied between manual stress and instrumented distraction techniques, and furthermore, whether manual stressed laxities differed between pre-resection and post-component implantation. The primary hypothesis of this trial was that there would be good-to-excellent inter-rater and intrarater reliability of gap laxity measures using a manual stressed technique. The secondary hypotheses were that there would be no difference between laxities measured by manual stress compared with distraction techniques, and that there would be no difference between laxities assessed prior to and after component implantation. If measures show satisfactory reliability, this will give surgeons confidence in their assessment of soft-tissue laxities, which not only underpin the particular philosophy of functional alignment, but of all TKA techniques that utilize navigation-derived laxities to quantify knee balance. Importantly, if measures between raters are reliable, this will demonstrate that the technique is generalizable.

Methods

Study group

A consecutive series of 123 patients (140 knees) undergoing primary unilateral or bilateral TKA by six senior surgeons acrossAustralia were included in the study (Figure 1). Sample size was calculated using an expected reliability of 0.84, with a precision of 0.05 and α value of 5%, and two raters with two repetitions per subject to give a sample size of 140.

The baseline characteristics of the study population are shown in Table I. Of these patients, 49 self-reported as male and 74 as female. There were 67 left-sided and 73 right-sided procedures. There were 45 Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) Type I knees and 58 CPAK Type II knees.11 Patients were excluded if they had a history of grade 3 collateral ligament injury, prior collateral ligament reconstruction, or presented with severe instability requiring constrained implants. Approval for the study was granted by theRamsay Health Care Research Ethics and Governance Committee (2022/ETH/0116).

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed through a medial parapatellar approach using the Stryker Triathlon CR system (Stryker, USA) with alignment and gap balancing using the Mako robotic arm-assisted system (Mako Robotic Arm Interactive Orthopaedic System version 2.0; Stryker) according to the technique published by Clark et al.2 Initial implant position was planned according to a restricted kinematic alignment principle. The joint surface was mapped, osteophytes removed, and anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) resected, as these alter extension laxities.12 Furthermore, this replicates the prosthetic environment to allow appropriate comparison of pre- and post-implantation laxity measurements. Limb alignment was registered, then pre-resection maximal stressed virtual gaps were assessed using the navigation software.

Raters

All six primary surgeons in the study were experienced robotic TKA users, each having previously performed more than 100 cases. Each surgeon-assistant pair was required to perform a minimum of 20 cases. The assistants (secondary raters) were predominately fellowship-trained surgeons, but in 20 cases the assistant was a general practitioner, and a nurse assistant in 11 cases. Each secondary rater practiced at least five cases prior to the study, which is representative of the accepted learning curve for surgeons adopting robotic surgery, thereby maximizing generalizability of the results. All assistants were trained by their senior surgeon on performing the test measurements.

Manual stressed gap laxity technique

The technique for manual stressing of gaps was the primary method tested because it is reproducible before and after implantation of components. Four gap measurements were performed separately: 1) maximum medial extension gap; 2) maximum lateral extension gap; 3) maximum medial flexion gap; and 4) maximum lateral flexion gap. Extension gaps were measured in 10° of flexion to de-tension the posterior capsule and minimize the influence of any posterior osteophytes. The patella was maintained in a reduced position where possible, as everting it can result in variable laxities and loads.13

To stress the medial extension gap, the leg was held in 10° flexion, with one hand grasping the heel and the other hand acting as a post at the level of the joint line, applying a valgus force. To assess the lateral gap, a varus force was applied using the same technique. Flexion gaps were assessed with the knee flexed at 90°. At this stage, the hip and knee were first positioned in neutral rotation as changes in tibiofemoral rotation can affect medial and lateral soft-tissue tension. For the medial flexion gap, the rater’s arm was placed under the knee, with the hand on the medial side. The other hand grasped the ankle, internally rotating the limb. For the lateral flexion gap, external rotation was applied by hooking the arm around the knee and either the biceps or the body acting as a medial post, aiming to minimize hip rotation. These gap measurements were assessed twice by each rater (for a total of four repetitions) to determine intrarater and inter-rater reliability of each test condition.

Joint distraction technique

To distract the joint to its maximal stressed position (maximum opening of the compartment), an instrument such as a Cobb elevator or distraction spoon was placed into the medial compartment in extension with maximal leverage applied to distract the medial side, measuring the medial extension virtual gap. The instrument was then swapped to the lateral compartment to measure the lateral extension virtual gap. In 90° of flexion, it was inserted into the medial and lateral compartments and leverage was applied to the point of maximal tension, thereby measuring the medial and lateral virtual gaps, respectively. To reduce potential for iatrogenic ligamentous injury after repeated testing, these distraction measurements were performed only once by the operating surgeon.

Final laxity assessments

The workflow then continued with the surgeon’s standard TKA technique. Following implantation of the prosthesis, final gaps were measured once with manual stress by the surgeon to determine the secondary endpoint.

Blinding

To remove measurement bias, all assessors were blinded by having the computer screen turned away from the operating team during laxity measurements. Measurements were recorded by the independent observer.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measures were the intrarater and inter-rater agreements for manual stressed assessment of the four virtual gaps. Secondary outcomes were the mean difference of manual compared with instrumented distraction gap laxity measures, and comparison of planned pre-resection virtual gap laxities with actual post-implantation gap laxities (expressed as the mean difference between the two readings).

Statistical analysis

Baseline data including patient characteristics, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA)14 grade and preoperative CPAK type were recorded.11 For the primary outcome measure, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) estimates were calculated, where previously established estimates > 0.90 indicate excellent reliability, values between 0.75 and 0.90 indicate good reliability, values between 0.50 and 0.75 indicate moderate reliability, and ICC values < 0.50 are indicative of poor reliability.15 ICCs were calculated for both intrarater and inter-rater reliability. Experience level (defined by grade of surgeon) was sub-categorized and the ICCs compared. For the secondary outcomes, ICC estimates were again used to compare manual with instrumented distraction methods, applying the criteria as described above. Mean differences between planned and post-implantation virtual laxities were assessed using a paired t-test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Intrarater reliability

The ICC for repeated measurements for surgeons’ measures showed excellent reliability, with a range of 0.91 to 0.97 across gaps. Similarly, ICCs for repeated measures for assistants showed excellent reliability, with a range of 0.92 to 0.96 across gaps. The mean difference across all four gaps was 0.4 mm (SD 0.5) for surgeons and 0.4 mm (SD 0.4) for assistants. Detailed results for each gap are presented in Figure 2 and Table II. Across all four gaps for both surgeons and assistants, 84% of repeated measures had an absolute difference of < 1 mm and 98% had an absolute difference of < 2 mm (Figure 3).

Inter-rater reliability

The inter-rater ICC comparing surgeon to assistant was 0.91 for the medial extension gap and 0.90 for the lateral extension gap. Both measures indicate excellent reliability. The inter-rater ICC was 0.86 for the medial flexion gap and 0.77 for the lateral flexion gap ICC, indicating good reliability for both (Table II, Figure 4). Across all four gaps, the absolute difference was < 1 mm in 60% and in 90% the absolute difference was < 2 mm (Figure 5).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective, blinded study to assess reliability of surgeons’ gap measurement during RA-TKA, both between different surgeons and within repeated measurements by the same surgeon. The results demonstrate that when a surgeon repeats an assessment of laxities during RA-TKA in a blinded fashion, the reliability of measures is excellent. Importantly, there was little difference between surgeons who were more experienced with the technology and those with less experience, suggesting that the use of this technology is generalizable. Surgeons can be confident in the repeatability of gap laxity assessment and can use these measurements to plan component positioning based on reliable data.

A surgeon’s ability to ‘feel’ knee balance has been shown to be unreliable in multiple studies.16-18 Furthermore, there exists no true reference point or gold standard of knee balance.19 This study, however, demonstrates that RA-TKA enables reliable assessment of ligament tensions — not just for a surgeon’s repeated measures, but also between surgeons — and since those ligament tensions correlate with intracompartmental loads,20 it may be considered a reliable instrument to assess knee balance.

The current study found that measurement by both the surgeon and assistant groups of the lateral flexion gaps only achieved good reliability, while measurement of extension gaps and medial flexion gaps was excellent. This finding may be explained in part by the inherent increased compliance of the lateral ligamentous structures compared to the stiffer medial collateral ligament, a characteristic that increases with greater flexion.21 As the ligament becomes less stiff, a greater force is required to tension it.22 Moreover, there are technical difficulties in assessing this gap since application of varus force to the knee in flexion tends to externally rotate the hip. It is for this reason that some surgeons use the instrumented distraction technique in the lateral flexion gap. This finding is in keeping with that of a previous study by Thompson et al23 that assessed reliability of sensor readings in each compartment at varying flexion angles. They found lateral flexion gap measurements to be less reliable than those of the other gaps, and concluded that reliability of sensor pressure measurements depends on the angular position of the knee.

Knee balance assessment using distraction instrumentation has previously been shown to be unreliable between surgeons.24 Additionally, a retrospective non-blinded study using a robotics platform that relies on instrumented distraction compared gap measurements between two surgeons, and found that less experienced surgeons tended to under-stress the knee, particularly in flexion.7 This is in keeping with the findings in our study, where the reliability of instrumented distraction techniques was rated as good for the medial side and moderate for the lateral side. The differential stiffness of the medial and lateral ligaments may explain these asymmetric results.22,25

The mean difference between planned and post-implant measured gaps was less than 1 mm for all four gaps. This can be caused by posterior osteophyte removal after implant planning. The knowledge of the exact laxity and safe range in TKA is lacking, and should be an area of future research. The implant system used in this study allows for 9, 10, 11, and 12 mm polyethylene implants, and as such when planning for a 10 mm polyethylene bearing, a difference of 1 or 2 mm could be corrected with use of a different bearing thickness.

This study does have limitations. While consistent techniques for manual stressing and distraction were agreed upon as part of the study protocol, there are inevitably non-negligible differences in technique across surgeons and assistants. However, we feel this is in part negated by the inclusion of multiple surgeons, along with assistants with varying levels of experience. Additionally, we did not assess differences related to patient body habitus (such as differences in limb girth or length), but we accepted this limitation as it reflects normal variations in practice. Although we repeated the manual assessment measures four times, we performed the distraction method only once to reduce the risk of iatrogenic ligamentous damage with repeated testing. Finally, the Mako 2.0 system measures laxities between components’ surfaces rather than the bone cuts. As a result, orientation of the components will affect the final gaps measured.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that gap laxity assessment in RA-TKA has excellent intrarater reliability and good-to-excellent inter-rater reliability. Distraction values for the medial side are more reliable than the lateral side, where increased constitutional laxity and the technical challenges of lateral gap assessment likely create increased variability between measurements. The high intrarater and inter-rater reliability of manual stressed gap assessment in RA-TKA should give surgeons confidence in using this information for executing and achieving balance in TKA.

Take home message

- Manual stressed gap assessment in robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty has good-to-excellent inter- and intrarater reliability, which supports its use for measuring and achieving soft tissue balance.

- Regardless of surgeon experience, the use of robotic-assisted technology to measure laxities is generalizable.

- Medial gap assessment between surgeons and assistants is more reliable than lateral gap assessment, particularly as flexion increases.
