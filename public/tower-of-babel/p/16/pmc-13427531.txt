Primary testicular diffuse large B-cell lymphoma with cutaneous metastasis to the chest: a case report
Authors: Rahim Ben Haddaoui Sedogo, Eric Hervé Mourfou, Sidd-Bé Hamidou Yoni, Victor N'so Gnegba, Aïda Sandrine Ouédraogo, Aimé Sosthène Ouédraogo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present the case of primary testicular diffuse large B-cell lymphoma diagnosed at the stage of pectoral skin metastasis in a 40-year-old male patient. He presented with a painless mass in the left scrotum that was complicated by ulcerated gynecomastia. An orchiectomy combined with a pectoral tumorectomy led to this diagnosis. The tumor was of the non-centro-germinative B subtype and classified as pT3NxM1b, Ann-Arbor stage IV E. The patient died while undergoing chemotherapy. This case underscores the poor prognosis associated with this histological type and the value of immunohistochemistry in diagnosis.

Introduction

Testicular cancer is rare, accounting for 1.5% of urological cancers and 0.2% of all cancers in Burkina Faso.1 It is dominated by germ cell tumors in 80% to 98% of cases, is common in young people aged 20 to 40, and often has a good prognosis.2 Primary testicular lymphoma (PTL) is rare, accounting for 1 to 2% of extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL) and approximately 5% of malignant testicular tumors. PTL is aggressive, commonly seen in men over the age of 60, and is predominantly diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL).3 Skin metastases are rare, reported in 6 to 13% of cases 4, 5, 6. We report the case of a non-germinal center, primary testicular diffuse large B-cell lymphoma diagnosed at the stage of pectoral skin metastasis in a young patient.

Case presentation

The patient was a 40-year-old man with no significant past medical history, admitted for a painless mass in the left testicle that had been gradually increasing in size over the past 8 months. The condition was complicated by an ulcerated chest mass and bilateral inguinal lymphadenopathy, along with a deterioration in the patient's general condition. Physical examination revealed a hard, painless mass in the left testicle. The signs of Chevassu, Prehn, and Gouverneur, as well as transillumination, were all negative. The patient presented with fever, a general condition classified as WHO stage II, and bilateral, painful, and mobile inguinal lymphadenopathy. The neurological examination was normal.

The levels of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and human chorionic gonadotropin (β-HCG) were normal. The complete blood count showed anemia. Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) serology was negative. Testicular and chest ultrasound, as well as thoraco-abdominal-pelvic scan, revealed a heterogeneous testicular mass and a subcutaneous mass in the chest, with no other secondary sites. Positron emission tomography (PET scan), brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), lumbar puncture, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level measurements had not been performed.

Upon macroscopic examination, we had a 242-g specimen from a left orchidectomy, measuring 10.5 x 9.5 × 5 cm, and a 280-g specimen from a pectoral skin tumorectomy with ulceration, measuring 11 x 8 × 5 cm. On standard histological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, diffuse tumor proliferation was observed, invading the tunica albuginea, the epididymis, the spermatic cord, and the pectoral dermis. The tumor consists of large, round cells with a lymphoid or sarcomatous appearance. The diagnosis of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, non-germ cell, testicular primary (DLBCL-PT non-GC), classified as pT3NxM1b, Ann-Arbor stage IV E, was confirmed.

The patient received three courses of chemotherapy according to the CHOP protocol (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) every three weeks. The patient died two weeks after his third course of treatment, due to multiple organ failure.

Discussion

Primary testicular lymphomas are rare and mainly affect men over the age of 60.3 However, there is a high incidence of primary testicular diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (PT-DLBCL) among younger individuals (median age of 36 years) who are HIV-positive.7 The risk factors identified are similar to those for germ cell tumors. These include testicular dysgenesis syndrome (cryptorchidism, hypospadias, hypo fertility, or infertility), testicular atrophy, and a personal or family (first-degree) history of testicular cancer.8 In this case, the patient is a young, HIV-negative individual with no identified risk factors.

The tumor most often presents as a painless testicular mass, usually unilateral (with no preference for one side). It may sometimes be accompanied by scrotal discomfort or pain and a hydrocele. Some cases are diagnosed late, at the metastatic stage, due to the tumor's misleading appearance, especially if germ cell tumor markers are normal.9 B symptoms are present in 83.33% of cases. They include weight loss of more than 10% over the previous 6 months, unexplained fever, and profuse night sweats.10 In this case, the symptoms developed insidiously, initially presenting as an isolated swelling on the left side of the scrotum, with a delayed diagnosis at the metastatic stage.

The complete blood count is often normal, but may reveal anemia and lymphocytosis. Serum LDH levels are often elevated, while alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-HCG) levels are normal. A brain MRI, lumbar puncture, and bone marrow biopsy are indicated as part of the staging workup.11 An 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET scan is essential for staging, monitoring, and assessing treatment response. FDG uptake reflects tumor cell activity.3 The Ann Arbor staging system is the system used to stage lymphomas following this initial evaluation. In most cases (70%–80%), the disease is diagnosed at a localized stage (stages I–II) and rarely at an advanced stage (stages III–IV).8 They often metastasize to the lung, the contralateral testicle, and the brain. In this case, a metastasis was found on the chest wall.

On macroscopic examination, the testis appears enlarged and contains a firm or fleshy tumor mass (average size 6 cm) that is diffuse or lobulated and has a uniform yellow-brown color. A clear demarcation from the testicular parenchyma is often observed. The tumor may extend to the tunica albuginea, the epididymis, and the spermatic cord.12 Histologically, it is a new entity classified under the category of diffuse large B-cell lymphomas (DLBCL) of immunoprivileged sites in the WHO Classification of Neoplasms of the Blood, 5th edition (2022). This category includes primary DLBCL of the central nervous system, the vitreoretinal region, and the testis.13 It is characterized by a diffuse, sheet-like lymphomatous proliferation composed of large, atypical, mitotic round cells. The tumor cells exhibit a centroblastic morphology (90%) and an immunoblastic morphology (10%). The tumor initially infiltrates the seminiferous tubules and the interstitial before completely replacing the parenchyma. Necrosis is common.14

On immunohistochemical analysis, the tumor cells express B-cell markers (CD20, CD79a, CD19), BCL2 (75–90%), MUM1 (40–70%), CD10 (20–40%), BCL2 (approximately 75%), and MYC (13%). Ki67 ranges from 40% to 90% (average of 70%). They do not express T-cell markers (CD3, CD2, CD3, CD4, CD8), CD30, Cyclin D1, SALL4, and EBER.12 According to Hans' algorithm, the majority of cases of DLBCL exhibit a non-germinal center (GC) or activated (CD10−, BCL6+/−, MUM1+) profile.15 Cytogenetics and molecular biology reveal rearrangements of IGH (98%), BCL6 (40%), MYC (15%), and BCL2 (10%).16 Macroscopically, our tumor occupied the entire testicle; histopathological examination revealed complete destruction of the parenchyma by atypical immunoblasts.

The differential diagnosis may include primary cutaneous DLBCL of the leg type, which frequently affects older women in the leg region and has a positive immunohistochemical profile (CD20, CD79a, MUM1, and BCL2) and a negative profile (CD10 and EBER).17 Even though our tumor's profile is consistent with this diagnosis, a primary tumor of the chest skin with testicular metastasis would be extremely rare.6 The immunohistochemical analysis ruled out round-cell sarcoma due to CD20 positivity.

The prognosis depends on the initial stage, age, and response to treatment. However, it remains an aggressive tumor with a poor prognosis and a 5-year survival rate of 17% to 48%, which is negatively correlated with advanced stage.8 LTP is characterized by a high risk of relapse, sometimes occurring late, in extranodal sites, particularly immune sanctuaries (contralateral testis, CNS, serous membranes).10 The non-centro-germinative B subtype is characterized by a more aggressive clinical course and a relatively high relapse rate.9

Treatment is multidisciplinary. It consists of an extended inguinal orchiectomy, followed by adjuvant systemic combination chemotherapy (CHOP: Cyclophosphamide, Doxorubicin, Vincristine, and Prednisone) combined with anti-CD20 immunotherapy (Rituximab) and bilateral lumbosacral and para-iliac radiation therapy. Prophylactic treatment with an intrathecal injection of methotrexate and radiation therapy to the contralateral testicle is also indicated to prevent recurrence or relapse.3An autologous stem cell transplant may also be recommended. From a medical and legal standpoint, it is advisable to preserve fertility by cryopreserving sperm before treatment begins.2 The course of this case was unfavorable, as the patient died after receiving the initial rounds of chemotherapy. Immunotherapy was not administered due to its unavailability and high cost.

A few cases have been reported in the literature. Frioui et al. in Tunisia reported a case of right testicular DLBCL detected through a skin nodule on the arm, in a 65-year-old man.6 Hermis et al. in Tunisia also reported a case of right testicular DLBCL, with multiple right inguinal skin metastases, in a 60-year-old man. The patient died a few days later, before chemotherapy could begin 5. Mathur et al. in Nepal also reported a case of treated right testicular DLBCL, with evidence of recurrence 8 years later in the form of multiple metastases on the chest, in a 72-year-old.4

Conclusion

Primary testicular diffuse large B-cell lymphoma is rare and aggressive. Skin metastases are also rare. Although it is common in individuals over 60 years of age, it can also occur at a young age and be diagnosed at a metastatic stage, as in our case. This case highlights the poor prognosis associated with this histological type and the value of immunohistochemistry in diagnosis. Therefore, any testicular mass should be considered cancerous and require investigation for early diagnosis. The availability and cost-effectiveness of immunotherapy should be considered and could be beneficial for improved management of lymphomas.
