Developmental changes in phonological awareness in Chinese-English bilingual children: An fNIRS longitudinal study
Authors: Yueh-Lin Li, Li-Ying Fan, Hsin-Chin Chen, Shiou-Yuan Chen, Ioulia Kovelman, Tai-Li Chou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLearning to read triggers a cascade of changes in children’s minds and brains, changes that lead to the formation of the “reading brain”. Importantly, the developmental trajectory of these changes differs across languages. The development of phonological literacy skills comes first for learners of alphabetic languages such as English. In Chinese, however, phonological literacy skills take longer to finetune due to the character’s phonological opacity. Bilingualism theories, however, suggest that such developmental trajectories are plastic and subject to change due to cross-linguistic interactions between bilinguals’ two languages. Our study puts these theoretical assumptions to the test by examining neurodevelopmental changes in phonological literacy skills in young Chinese-English bilinguals and Chinese monolinguals (27 monolinguals and 40 bilinguals). At two time points, in grades one and three, the children completed an auditory phonological awareness initial sound-matching task during functional Near Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) neuroimaging (27 monolinguals and 40 bilinguals). In line with bilingual transfer theories, bilingual children showed a more robust fine-tune of the phonological or dorsal neural literacy pathway relative to monolinguals. Moreover, we used prediction over time to demonstrate the additional recruitment of the phonological pathways as English proficiency increased in bilinguals. The lower English phonological skills at time 1 were predictive of age-related increases in STG activation along the dorsal pathway in the Chinese task. The findings inform theories of brain development for learning to read by revealing the plasticity in foundational literacy pathways as a function of early and systematic dual-language literacy instruction.Keywords: Bilingualism, Development, fNIRS, Longitudinal, Phonological awareness

Introduction

In light of the burgeoning global interconnectedness facilitated by the widespread adoption of a common international language, English, nations worldwide are progressively assuming the characteristics of a global community. Many countries where English is not the primary language are instituting bilingual education programs aimed at fostering English proficiency among children. However, Chinese and English exhibit notable differences in orthographic and phonological mapping at the word level, posing challenges for native Chinese-speaking children in acquiring English proficiency. Nevertheless, both languages share a common trait in phonological awareness, which transcends linguistic boundaries. Research indicates that phonological awareness can be transferred between Chinese and English contexts (Chen et al., 2010; Yeung et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). Given the pivotal role of phonological awareness in the acquisition of both Chinese and English literacy, investigating phonological transfer within bilingual contexts is imperative (Adams, 1990; Chen et al., 2010; He et al., 2005; Liberman et al., 1974). However, prior research on bilingualism has predominantly relied on cross-sectional methodologies, which may not fully elucidate the precise causal mechanisms underlying transfer effects. Hence, adopting a longitudinal approach holds promise for unraveling the intricacies of literacy development through transfer effects among Chinese-English bilingual children.

The difference between English and Chinese lies primarily in their distinct writing systems (Wu and Chou, 2000; Reich et al., 2003). English employs an alphabetic script, while Chinese utilizes a logographic script. Cross-linguistic differences in sound-to-print are especially notable between the two languages. In English, word pronunciations map onto letters. Due to the correspondence between English orthography and phonology, it is possible to predict which English word is being heard, even if encountered for the first time. Although English has an opaque orthography relative to other alphabetic languages such as French and Spanish (Ziegler et al., 2010), phonological decoding strategies can still be applied during early word learning. This predictive accuracy, based on established grapheme-to-phoneme correspondences rules, can reach up to 70 % (Share, 2008), thereby allowing young learners to establish robust phonology-orthography associations (McBride-Chang et al., 2005; Ruan et al., 2018). In contrast to alphabetic languages, Chinese characters map on to morphemes. Chinese operates as a morpho-syllabic language, with individual syllables matching morphemes. A single Chinese character is typically considered a morpheme and corresponds to a unique pronunciation without an equivalent phonetic correspondence rule. For example, the character “譴” has a specific pronunciation / tɕʰjεnˇ/. Additionally, the presence of numerous homophones, such as the characters “則” and “責,” both pronounced as /tsɤˊ/, results in a lower sound-to-print correspondence, estimated at 23 %–26 % (Shu et al., 2003), instead demonstrating a stronger semantic-orthography association (Sun et al., 2023). The correlation between morphological awareness and early literacy development proves to be more pronounced in Chinese compared to English (Tong et al., 2017).

The differentiation between Chinese and English extends beyond their respective writing systems to encompass variances in neural mechanisms. Within the adult brain, word recognition is mediated by two distinct pathways: the dorsal and ventral pathways (Hickok and Poeppel, 2007). The ventral pathway primarily processes semantic information, engaging regions such as the middle temporal gyrus (MTG) and the ventral inferior frontal gyrus (vIFG). Notably, this ventral pathway predominates in Chinese language processing (Ge et al., 2015; Ge and Gao, 2023). Previous studies have shown that Chinese reading engages the ventral pathway, which facilitates holistic visual word recognition and semantic processing (Sun et al., 2011), and also plays a critical role in semantic judgment tasks (Fan et al., 2021). Moreover, heightened connectivity strength within the ventral pathway correlates positively with improved accuracy in semantic judgment tasks whether in the Chinese native speakers or the proficient learners of Mandarin Chinese (Lai et al., 2021). In contrast, the dorsal pathway specializes in phonological processing and encompasses regions such as the superior temporal gyrus (STG), inferior parietal lobule (IPL), and the dorsal inferior frontal gyrus (dIFG). This dorsal pathway is more prominently engaged during language processing in English, as learning to read English prompts greater neural activation within the phonological-related network compared to learning to read Chinese (Brennan et al., 2013).

Despite the differences between Chinese and English, they both contribute to the development of a crucial metalinguistic skill known as phonological awareness (PA) (Chen et al., 2010; Geva and Wang, 2001). PA is the capacity to manipulate the smallest units of sound, namely phonemes, by analyzing, segmenting the spoken words, and combining phonemes (Rvachew et al., 2003; Stanovich, 1986; Wagner et al., 1994). PA is regarded as a metalinguistic competence that transcends linguistic boundaries and plays a pivotal role in literacy development, spanning both non-alphabetic languages such as Chinese (He et al., 2005) and alphabetic languages like English (Adams, 1990). Moreover, research indicates that PA significantly influences the acquisition of English proficiency among native Chinese speakers (Chen et al., 2010; Yeong and Liow, 2012).

The interaction between two languages manifests in the bilingual mind (Grosjean, 1989), even in the early stage of L2 acquisition (Chen et al., 2010; Kou et al., 2024). The Interactive Transfer Framework (Chung et al., 2019) posits the transfer of PA between languages in children, exemplified by the influence of the first language (L1) on the second language (L2), known as the L1-L2 transfer effect (Haigh et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2023; Yeung et al., 2023). Despite cross-linguistic divergence, phonological transfer occurs (Chen et al., 2010), persisting even in the early stages of L2 acquisition (Chen et al., 2010; Kou et al., 2024). For languages in two different systems, such as logographic Chinese (L1) and alphabetic English (L2), longitudinal studies have also found behavioral evidence of L1-L2 transfer effects. Chinese phonological skills at Time 1 can indeed predict English phonological skills at Time 2 (Zhang et al., 2025). There is also evidence supporting the opposite direction, namely the L2-L1 transfer effect, where L2 influences L1. The cross-sectional research has demonstrated that the L2-L1 transfer effect enables early Chinese-English bilingual children to process their L1(Chinese) utilizing bilateral frontal brain regions (Kou et al., 2024). A longitudinal study using behavioral measures has also shown a significant correlation between English phonological skills at Time 1 and Chinese phonological skills at Time 2, indicating that phonological skills in English may transfer to Chinese phonological skills (Zhang et al., 2025). However, longitudinal studies using imaging measures to address such a transfer effect on logographic languages are still scarce (Chung et al., 2019), particularly those examining neural correlates based on brain plasticity. Longitudinal studies distinguish changes from cohort effects and additionally allow prediction over time (Chou et al., 2019). There are limited studies examining developmental trajectories and causal relationships under controlled conditions to investigate the transfer effect in the early stage. In addition, the previous finding predominantly focused on alphabetic languages rather than languages utilizing different writing systems such as Chinese and English (e.g. Abu-Rabia et al., 2013; Cirino et al., 2009; Pasquarella et al., 2014; Wise et al., 2016).

There are four primary rationales for examining transfer effects in early development among Chinese-English bilingual children. Firstly, the predominant utilization of cross-sectional methodologies in most studies limits the discourse to correlations, thus precluding the establishment of causal relationships regarding transfer effects in Chinese-English bilingualism (Chung et al., 2019). Secondly, recognizing the pivotal role of transfer effects in shaping early bilingual proficiency (McBride-Chang et al., 2005; Ruan et al., 2018), there exists a pressing need for research to focus on the early developmental stages. Thirdly, given the substantial linguistic differences between Chinese and English, individuals from different language backgrounds are likely to develop distinct cognitive pathways for processing each language. Prior research suggests that around the age of seven to eight, neural specialization in the brain becomes discernible (Wang et al., 2023), implying that bilingual children may develop different cognitive pathways for processing each language. Moreover, as most prior discussions of the dorsal and ventral pathways have focused on adults, the present study also attempts to extend this framework to early school-age children. Lastly, although existing literature acknowledges the occurrence of L1-L2 transfer during the initial stages of literacy acquisition (Haigh et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025), scant attention has been paid to L2-L1 transfer, particularly during early literacy acquisition stages. Consequently, a longitudinal study targeting Chinese-English bilingual children at the age of six is warranted to investigate L2-L1 transfers comprehensively.

The present study

The present study aims to explore the development of phonological awareness processes over time and across languages, with an eye towards cross-linguistic interaction in Chinese-English bilingual children educated in Taiwan. Guided by cross-linguistic transfer frameworks, Chinese-English bilinguals will strengthen their phonological, the dorsal neural pathway, in Chinese compared to Chinese monolinguals. We base the hypotheses on the idea that the literacy feature that is salient or of high predictability in one language would transfer into the language where it is of lower salience - in this case, phonological processing being more salient in English than Chinese and thus transferring into Chinese. To test this prediction, we recruited Chinese-English bilinguals and Chinese bilinguals educated in Taiwan. The children were age six at time one (T1) and eight at time two (T2). The children completed the auditory phonological awareness initial sound matching task in Chinese (all children) and English (bilinguals) along with traditional literacy measures. The findings inform theories of brain development for learning to read by revealing the plasticity in foundational literacy pathways as a function of early and systematic dual-language literacy instruction.

Methods

Participants

Sixty-seven first graders (aged 6–7) from elementary schools in Taipei were recruited in the present study, including 27 Chinese monolingual and 40 Chinese-English bilingual children. To evaluate their linguistic performance between these two groups, all parents of children completed a modified version of the Bilingual Language Background and Use Questionnaire (Kovelman et al., 2008). This questionnaire was to collect children’s cognitive and language development, history of physical health, and home and school language background. Bilingual children had learned English before the age of five, with a minimum of one year of English exposure prior to testing. In addition, all bilingual children received formal bilingual education at school, with an average of over 17 h out of 35 h of English exposure per week. Moreover, subjects such as mathematics and science were also delivered in English by these teachers. In contrast, monolingual children had less than one year of English learning experience. They received regular education at school, with less than three hours of English instruction per week, all taught by native Chinese-speaking teachers. All participants were neurotypical and native speakers of Mandarin Chinese, with normal hearing and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Informed consent was obtained from all child participants after providing them with comprehensive study information.

As a longitudinal study, all the children participated in the experiments at two time points. During each time point, child participants received both behavior tasks and fNIRS scanning. The first time point was at their first grade, and the second time point was at their third grade in an interval of twelve to twenty-four months. At the second time point, there were 67 graders (aged 8–9), including 27 Chinese monolingual and 40 Chinese-English bilingual children. The present study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University.

Behavioral assessments

Participants completed a battery of language and literacy tasks. The bilingual children did the assessments in both Chinese and English, but the monolingual children only did them in Chinese. The performance of behavioral assessments was shown in Table S1.

Chinese phonological awareness (PA)

The Chinese phonological awareness task utilized in this study was adapted from the Elision subtest of the Comprehensive Test of Phonological Processing (CTOPP), originally described by Newman et al. (2011). Participants completed 36 items, consisting of 6 items at the syllable level and 30 at the phoneme level. In each trial, participants were asked to repeat a word and then say it again after removing a specified sound segment. For instance, participants heard prompts such as, “Please repeat /fuˊ/. Now, say /fuˊ/ again, but without the /f/ sound” (e.g., /fuˊ/ → /uˊ/).

Chinese morphological awareness (MA)

Chinese morphological awareness was evaluated using a method adapted from Song et al. (2015). Participants were instructed about the morphological rule behind certain compound words and then asked to generate new compound words by applying this rule to another morpheme. For example, the examiner presented an item such as, “We call a chain worn on the hand a ’hand chain’; what would we call a chain worn on the foot?” (Correct response: “foot chain”; in Chinese: 手鏈 → 腳 鏈).

Chinese receptive vocabulary

Chinese receptive vocabulary was measured with the Chinese version of the revised Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (PPVT-R), developed by Lu and Liu (1998).

Chinese character recognition and reading

To measure Chinese character recognition and reading abilities, participants completed two separate tasks. In the recognition task (30 items), they heard a character pronounced and then selected the corresponding character from a set of four options. In the reading task (60 items), they were instructed to read aloud a different set of Chinese characters presented visually.

Chinese sentence reading fluency

The Chinese sentence reading fluency test was adapted from the Reading Fluency subtest of the Woodcock-Johnson IV (WJ-IV; Schrank et al., 2014). Participants were required to silently read 100 Chinese sentences within a 3-minute time limit and determine whether each sentence was semantically accurate or incorrect.

Working memory

The forward and backward digit span task was used to evaluate working memory capacity (Richardson, 2007). Participants listened to digit sequences starting from two digits in length, increasing by one digit every two trials, and were asked to repeat the sequences either in the presented order or in reverse order.

English phonological awareness

The Elision subtest from the CTOPP (Wagner et al., 1999), which closely resembles the phonological awareness assessment used for Chinese in this study.

English morphological awareness

The Early Lexical Morphology Measure (ELMM; Marks et al., 2021) employed a paradigm resembling the task used in assessing Chinese morphological awareness in the present study.

English receptive vocabulary

Receptive vocabulary was assessed using the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test, fifth edition (PPVT-5; Dunn, 2019). In addition to the PPVT-5, this study utilized another measure to evaluate receptive vocabulary. Single-word reading was measured by the Letter-Word Identification subtest from the Woodcock-Johnson IV (WJ-IV; Schrank et al., 2014), which shares similarities with the Chinese vocabulary assessment administered in the current study.

English reading comprehension

The Passage Comprehension measure from the Woodcock-Johnson IV (WJ-IV; Schrank et al., 2014) was utilized in this study.

English reading decoding skill

The Word Attack assessment from the Woodcock-Johnson IV (WJ-IV; Schrank et al., 2014) was employed in this research.

English sentence reading fluency

The Reading Fluency subtest from the Woodcock-Johnson IV (WJ-IV; Schrank et al., 2014) follows a format comparable to the Chinese reading fluency assessment used in this study.

fNIRS stimuli and tasks

The present study included a Chinese phonological task and an English phonological task. Both tasks included an experimental condition and a control condition. In each trial during the task, participants heard three spoken words in a sequential order, the target – word 1 – word 2, and were instructed to choose which word shared the phonological onset with the target (i.e. the answer). The other word (i.e. the distractor) had a similar sound but did not share the phonological onset with the target. In the experimental condition, the distractor was semantically related to the target, requiring the participants to ignore semantic interference and focus on the phonological onset to identify the answer. In contrast, in the control condition, the target and the answer were identical in a trial, along with a different onset phoneme of the distractor. The spoken stimuli were recorded by a female speaker for the Chinese task and by another female English speaker for the English task.

For the Chinese phonological task, all three spoken Chinese words were compound words. The second characters of all word pairs were the same sound. In the experimental condition, for example, there were three spoken Chinese words, 家屬 (/tɕja ʂuˇ/, family, the target) – 金屬 (/tɕɪn ʂuˇ/, metal, the answer) – 親屬 (/tɕʰɪn ʂuˇ/, relative, the distractor). In the control condition, the answer was identical to the target, 新年 (/ɕɪnˉ njεnˊ/, new year, the target) – 新年 (/ɕɪnˉ njεnˊ/, new year, the answer) – 兔子 (/tʰuˋ tsɯ˙/, rabbit, the distractor).

For the English phonological task, the rules of the stimuli in the conditions were the same as that in the Chinese phonological task, except that all the stimuli were English words. For example, the participants heard “Sunday (target) – subway (answer) – Monday (distractor)” in the experimental condition, and “hamster (target) – hamster (answer) – garlic (distractor)” in the control condition.

For each task, the experimental and control conditions were matched for word frequency based on the word frequency report (National Languages Committee, 2000) and the most recent version of the Corpus of Contemporary American English (COCA, https://www.english-corpora.org/coca/, Davies, 2020).

Each task, for the Chinese or English task, was approximately seven minutes. In each trial, there was a 10-ms blank presented first, then a white fixation square displayed on the top of the screen for 1500 ms with the first auditory spoken word played. Then, a blue fixation square was displayed on the bottom-left of the screen for 1500 ms with the second auditory spoken word played. Finally, a yellow fixation square displayed on the bottom-right of the screen for 3500 ms with the third auditory spoken word played. The participants were instructed to choose the answer between the left (i.e. the second) and the right (i.e. the third) words, using the keyboard on the laptop, as quickly and correctly as possible after they had heard the third word. The inter-trial-interval was 1000 ms. To optimize the detection of task-related hemodynamic responses and facilitate condition-wise contrasts, the experiment adopted a block design: every four trials of the same condition constituted a block, followed by a 3000 ms rest period (Fig. S1). Both tasks lasted approximately 7 min using E-prime 2 (Psychology Software Tools, Inc.) on a Lenovo G450 laptop. Auditory stimuli were presented through the internal speaker of the laptop at an approximated intensity of 75 db Reaction times were calculated as the duration between the onset of the third word and the button press for a response. Behavioral performance in the tasks was evaluated based on accuracy and reaction times.

Experimental procedure

After introducing the informed consent to the participants, experimenters measured the head circumference of the children and positioned the fNIRS cap and optodes. Then the room lights were dimmed, and participants were provided with instructions for the task. Prior to all main experimental sessions, a practice session was conducted, which included both the experimental and control conditions, with practice stimuli designed to be distinct from those used in the testing sessions. Feedback was given after each practice trial to inform participants and ensure their understanding of the task rules. Then, the bilingual participants completed both the Chinese and English phonological tasks in a counterbalanced order, and the monolingual participants completed only the Chinese phonological task.

Behavioral data analysis

To compare the differences in behavioral performance, there were two analyses conducted in the present study. For each analysis, the dependent variables were accuracy and reaction time. The first analysis was to compare the behavioral performance in language interaction between the Chinese and English tasks within bilingual children. A 2 task (Chinese and English) by 2 time (first and second) by 2 condition (experimental and control) analysis of variance (ANOVA) was done. The three factors were all within-subject factors. The second analysis was to compare the behavioral performance in language transfer between bilinguals and monolinguals in the Chinese task. The analysis was a 2 group (bilingual and monolingual) by 2 time (first and second) by 2 condition (experimental and control) ANOVA. Only the group was a between-participants factor, and the rest two were within-participant factors.

fNIRS data recording

The 16×16 NIRSscout (NIRx Medical Tech.) was used to detect the hemodynamics response (HDR), with illumination wavelengths set at 760 nm and 850 nm. Sixteen near-infrared light emitters (sources) and 16 detectors were utilized, resulting in 39 data channels that were sampled at a rate of 3.91 Hz (Channels 1~19 were to detect HDRs of the right hemisphere, Channels 20~39 were to detect them of the left hemisphere; Fig. 1). These optodes were positioned on the fNIRS cap using the international 10–10 system. Based on the previous fMRI studies, fNIRS neuroanatomical localization was set in the lateral frontal, parietal, and temporal regions for bilateral hemispheres, as regions of interest (Booth et al., 2002; Hickok and Poeppel, 2007; Lau et al., 2008). The cortical activity within these areas would increase during lexical tasks in Chinese or English (Brennan et al., 2013; Booth et al., 2004; Chou et al., 2006a, 2006b; Chou et al., 2009a, 2009b; Chou et al., 2019; Fan et al., 2010; Hickok and Poeppel, 2007; Hu et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2015, 2016; Mei et al., 2013, 2015; Müller, 2003; Zhu et al., 2014).

fNIRS probe setup. Red dots: light sources; blue dots: light detectors; lines: data channels (Channels 1~19: right hemisphere; Channels 20~39: left hemisphere).

fNIRS data analysis

The fNIRS data were analyzed using the NIRS Brain AnalyszIR, an analysis toolbox based on MATLAB (Santosa et al., 2018).

Subject-level analysis

Raw data were processed in a series of procedures for subject-level analysis. First, the data were converted to hemoglobin concentration data, applying the modified Beer-Lambert law. Second, 5 s before the onset of the first stimuli and 5 s after the last stimuli were trimmed from each channel. Then, the trimmed hemoglobin concentration data were analyzed using the general linear model (GLM, Friston et al., 2007; Friston, 2009). The effects of confounding signals such as physiological or motion-induced signals were corrected using the AR-IRLS method (Barker et al., 2013). The signal drift over time was reduced by including temporal and dispersion derivatives, and also a DCT matrix with a cutoff frequency of 0.008 Hz in the model. The peak time of the hemodynamic response function (HRF) was set as 6 s (Brauer et al., 2008). These procedures yielded subject-level regression coefficients (beta values in each condition) for oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) signals from each channel.

Group-level analysis

Linear-mixed-effect (LME) models for each data channel were used to perform further group-level analysis. The first LME was fitted to examine the neural basis of phonological interaction during bilingual children performing both the Chinese task and the English task at two different time points. The task, time point, and condition were modeled as fixed effects, with the subject as a random effect. The beta values of each subject for HbO and HbR as dependent variables. The corresponding formula was “beta ~ −1 + Task:Time:Cond + (1|Subject)”, written in the Wilkinson Notation. The estimated group-level effects of each channel were extracted to calculate the contrasts of experimental minus control conditions as phonological processing.

The second LME was fitted to examine the neural basis of phonological transfer between bilingual children and monolingual children performing the Chinese task at two different time points. The group, time point, and condition were modeled as fixed effects, with the subject as a random effect. The beta values of each subject for HbO and HbR as dependent variables. The corresponding formula was “beta ~ −1 + Group:Time:Cond + (1|Subject)”, written in the Wilkinson Notation. The estimated group-level effects of each channel were extracted to calculate the contrasts of experimental minus control conditions as phonological processing.

All the contrasts used only the HbO signal since HbO would be the major contributor to the fNIRS signal (HbO~76 %; HbR~19 %; Strangman et al. 2002). The threshold level of significance in all the contrasts was set at p < .05, with family-wise error (FWE) correction for 39 channels.

Brain-behavior regression

To further examine the relationship between the activities in brain regions and the children’s language skills, brain-behavior regression analysis was conducted. The channels around the brain regions that showed significant activation in previous contrasts were set as regions of interest (ROIs). The beta values of the experimental minus control condition from ROI channels in each participant were extracted from the Subject-level GLM and imported into JASP (JASP Team, 2022).

We computed the regression analysis using initial behavioral assessments at the first time point to predict the developmental change in brain activity over time. The Chinese CTOPP and English CTOPP were used to evaluate the behavioral assessments of phonological awareness in each language. The English Word Attack assessment was used to evaluate the phonological decoding skill in English. The threshold level of significance was set at p < .05, with family-wise error (FWE) correction applied to account for the number of significant regions in each model.

Results

There were sixty-seven children, including 27 in the monolingual group and 40 in the bilingual group, for the behavioral and fNIRS results. An independent t-test was conducted to check the age difference. There were no differences in their age at the first time point, the second time point, and the duration between the two time points. The ages of the child participants are presented in the top of Table 1. Furthermore, the two groups did not differ significantly in their performance on Chinese phonological awareness, morphological awareness, receptive vocabulary, character recognition and reading, sentence reading fluency, or working memory, either at the first time point or in age-related changes between the two time points (all ps > 0.05). These results indicated that the monolingual and bilingual groups were comparable in their baseline language and cognitive measures.

Behavioral results

The task performance of two phonological tasks is presented in the bottom of Table 1. There were two sets of data analysis to present phonological effects, including within-subject analysis for language interaction in bilingual children, and between-subjects analysis for language transfer in the Chinese task.

Differences between Chinese and English tasks within bilingual children

Considering accuracy with a 2 time (first and second) by 2 task (Chinese and English) by 2 condition (control and experimental) ANOVA, there were two main effects of time, F (1, 39) = 45.46, p < .01, ω2 = 0.22 and condition, F (1, 39) = 186.45, p < .01, ω2 = 0.60, and one second order interactions of time and condition, F (1, 39) = 15.06, p < .01, ω2 = 0.06. The main effect of task, the second order interaction of time and task, and task and condition, and the third order interaction were not significant (ps > 0.05). To unpack the interaction of time and condition (Fig. S2), the accuracy difference between the control and the experimental condition was 31.23 % at the 1st time point, F (1, 39) = 181.29, p < .01, ω2 = 0.99, but significantly declined to 21.55 % at the 2nd time point, F (1, 39) = 89.38, p < .01, ω2 = 0.98.

Considering reaction time, there were a main effect of condition, F (1, 39) = 67.90, p < .01, ω2 = 0.28, and a second order interaction of task and condition, F (1, 39)= 28.62, p < .01, ω2 = 0.07. To unpack the interaction of task and condition (Fig. S3), the simple main effects of task were significant in the control condition, F (1, 39) = 14.33, p < .01, ω2 = 0.87, and in the experimental condition, F (1, 39) =12.02, p < .01, ω2 = 0.85. In the control condition, the reaction time of the English task was slower than the Chinese task; in the experimental condition, the reaction time of the English task was faster than the Chinese task.

Differences between bilinguals and monolinguals in the Chinese task

Considering accuracy with a 2 group (monolingual and bilingual) by 2 time (first and second) by 2 condition (control and experimental) ANOVA, there were two main effects of time, F (1, 65) = 54.05, p < .01, ω2 = 0.17, and condition, F (1, 65) = 242.78, p < .01, ω2 = 0.57, and a second order interaction of time and condition, F (1, 65) = 23.15, p < .01, ω2 = 0.06. To unpack the interaction of time and condition (Fig. S4), the accuracy difference between the control and the experimental condition was 36.07 % at the 1st time point, F (1, 65) = 247.10, p < .01, ω2 = 0.99, but significantly declined to 23.43 % at the 2nd time point, F (1, 65) = 102.37, p < .01, ω2 = 0.98.

Considering reaction time, there was only a significant main effect of the conditions, F (1, 65) = 171.42, p < .01, ω2 = 0.42, with no other significant main effects or interaction effects (ps > 0.05). It only indicated that the reaction time of the control condition was faster than the experimental condition.

In the meantime, we found the group by time interaction approaching significance (p = .124) for phonological awareness by the Chinese PA assessment (CTOPP), after excluding two monolingual participants who did not understand the rules. Specifically, the bilingual group showed greater improvements in Chinese PA performance relative to the monolingual group, suggesting that bilinguals’ English background may have contributed to their performance in Chinese PA assessments.

fNIRS results

To illustrate the developmental difference, we presented the contrast of the second minus the first time point. Brain-behavior regression

The regression analysis used the initial behavioral assessments at the first time point to predict the developmental change in brain activity. Based on the activated brain regions in the contrasts of the experimental condition minus the control condition, the channels that showed significant differences in activation during the between-group comparison or the within-group comparison were selected as the ROIs for regression analysis, including channels 32 (the left STG).

The developmental changes in L2-L1 interaction were observed in bilingual children. Negative regression lines were identified using the English phonological scores at the first time point to predict developmental changes in activation in the left superior temporal gyrus (STG) during the Chinese task. The lower English CTOPP and Word Attack scores at the first time point were predictive of age-related increases in left STG activation during the Chinese task in bilingual children (β0 = −3.3, p = .02, Fig. 7b; β0 = −6.6, p = .01, Fig. 7c). In sum, children with lower English phonological performance at the first time point exhibited greater brain activation changes in the left STG during the Chinese task in bilinguals. In contrast, the English CTOPP and Word Attack scores at the first time point were not predictive of the left STG activation during the Chinese task at the first time point in bilingual children (ps > 0.05).

Discussion

The current study employed a longitudinal design to elucidate the transfer effects during the initial stages of literacy acquisition. Our findings reveal evidence of transfer effects among bilingual participants, particularly regarding the influence of L2 (English) on L1 (Chinese) during early developmental stages. Over time from age 6 to age 8, the development along the phonological pathway, the dorsal pathway, was observed in bilingual children. This supports our hypothesis that highly salient and predictable literacy features, the phonological awareness, in English transfer to the less salient Chinese language. This L2-L1 influence on transfer from phonological transparent to phonological opaque skills required the dynamic recruitment of the dorsal pathway from two types of analyses. Firstly, using within-participant analysis to examine language interaction with bilingual children showed that the bilingual group exhibited additional development and utilization of the dorsal pathway for Chinese tasks compared to English tasks. Moreover, using between-participant analysis to examine language transfer showed that the bilingual group demonstrated greater development of the dorsal pathway for Chinese tasks relative to the monolingual group. Secondly, regression analysis to observe brain-behavioral relationship indicated that bilingual children with lower English phonological scores in the first grade showed more development in the STG, the key phonological processing region (Brennan et al., 2013; Cao et al., 2011), when performing Chinese tasks after two years of learning English.

The current study reveals that the highly salient and predictable literacy features in English influence the development of the less salient Chinese language, which can be observed through the greater development of the dorsal pathway from age six to eight. Language interaction within bilingual children revealed enhanced involvement of the dorsal pathway when comparing the Chinese task to the English task over time. Bilingual children exhibited heightened brain activity along the dorsal pathway over time, encompassing regions such as the superior temporal gyrus (STG), inferior parietal lobule (IPL), and dorsal inferior frontal gyrus (dIFG), during the Chinese task relative to the English task. The aforementioned interaction effects can also be observed in the regression analysis. The regression results revealed that English phonological skills at the first time point in bilingual children negatively predicted changes in STG activation over age during the Chinese task. The left STG, a part of the dorsal pathway, plays a crucial role in English phonological processing (Brennan et al., 2013). For bilingual children, those with lower English phonological performance at the first time point showed greater developmental changes in the STG along the dorsal pathway to complete the Chinese phonological task. This prediction over time indicates that English proficiency may influence the dorsal pathway associated with phonological processing to complete the Chinese task, evidencing an L2-L1 interaction in brain development.

The longitudinal predictions demonstrate the additional recruitment of phonological pathways as English proficiency increased in bilingual children. Namely, lower English phonological skills were predictive of age-related increases in STG activation. This finding suggests that the development of phonological awareness in bilinguals requires additional recruitment in the left STG. Previous research has indicated that bilingual individuals may allocate more cognitive resources to manage two languages simultaneously (Folke et al., 2016; Jones et al., 2012). Additionally, exposure to English in bilinguals may necessitate greater support or compensation from neural mechanisms of the dorsal pathway (Cao et al., 2011). Our current findings provide developmental evidence of the dorsal pathway’s involvement, demonstrating that bilinguals show additional recruitment of the dorsal pathway during tasks involving the native language (Chinese). In contrast, such a predictive relationship between initial English phonological proficiency and left STG activation was absent during the Chinese task at Time 1. This contrast highlights the dynamic nature of neural development in response to bilingual language exposure and proficiency over time. In sum, the results suggest that early language proficiency influences later neural development in bilingual children, with lower initial proficiency in the second language leading to greater neural adaptation in the native language.

In addition, a language transfer contrasting bilinguals versus monolinguals engaged in the Chinese task demonstrated pronounced enhancements of the dorsal pathway. The between-group analysis may underscore the occurrence of L2-L1 transfer effects. Bilingual children demonstrated increased STG activation during the Chinese task as compared to monolingual peers, indicative of a heightened reliance on the dorsal pathway to complete the Chinese phonological task at age eight relative to age six. Consistent with previous research, enhanced activation of the dorsal pathway has been linked to heightened phonological awareness proficiency (Cao et al., 2011; Chyl et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2016; Norton et al., 2014; Preston et al., 2016), which in turn correlates with improved English proficiency (McBride et al., 2021). Consequently, following two years of English instruction, the Chinese-English bilingual children with the higher dorsal pathway activation exhibited improved phonological awareness as compared to monolingual peers over time. Phonological awareness, recognized as a metalinguistic skill across languages (Luo et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023), demonstrated transfer effects from English acquisition to Chinese, as evidenced by performance in Chinese phonological tasks. Additionally, our findings provide evidence of neural specialization occurring between ages six and eight (Johnson, 2011; Wang et al., 2023), with children displaying heightened brain activation in the dorsal pathway to accommodate phonological awareness demands in English, indicative of developmental trajectories within this domain.

In this study, although between-group comparisons may be influenced by potential confounding variables such as socioeconomic status, school environment, or family practices, this study also observed evidence of transfer effects from a within-subject longitudinal perspective. Specifically, bilingual children showed greater developmental changes in dorsal pathway activation during the Chinese task than in the English task. These results were derived from comparisons between the same participants at the 1st and the 2nd time point, and thus would not be confounded by inter-individual differences in background factors. This within-subject approach highlights one of the key strengths of longitudinal designs: by tracking the same individuals over time, it is possible to minimize the influence of group-level confounds and more precisely attribute developmental changes to learning experiences (Farrington, 1991). Therefore, in addition to between-group findings, the current study provides within-subject neural evidence for cross-linguistic transfer effects, strengthening the claim that early English learning may shape the neural mechanisms involved in Chinese phonological processing.

The present study offers insights into various facets of language transfer effects, incorporating several key features: the utilization of a longitudinal approach to capture transfer effects, implementation of Chinese and English language tasks within a Chinese-English bilingual cohort, comparisons of bilingual participants with monolingual counterparts across the same Chinese tasks, exploration of neural mechanism development influenced by transfer effects, and examination of phonological processes relative to control conditions. Especially, the present study used a longitudinal approach to examine the neural development between the ages of six and eight, during which bilingual children may develop a different pathway for processing each language (Kou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). While prior discussions of the dorsal and ventral pathways have largely focused on adults, the present study extends this framework to early school-age children. Because of learning English (L2), Chinese-English bilingual children could develop the dorsal pathway to process their Chinese. Previous research has also established that the development of the dorsal pathway is positively correlated with language literacy (Wagley and Booth, 2022). Consequently, the present study highlights the potential benefits of second language acquisition.

Despite the longitudinal approach adopted in this study to investigate causal relationships stemming from transfer effects on neural mechanisms, certain limitations warrant consideration. Primarily, the sample size in our study is relatively small, with a disproportionately low number of monolingual participants compared to bilingual individuals, potentially constraining the depth of our discussion. In addition, although the present study observed large effect sizes (e.g., Cohen’s d ≥ 0.84) in the between-group comparisons, a post-hoc power analysis using G*Power indicated that a sample size of approximately 50 participants per group would be required to achieve 80 % power at a family-wise error (FWE) corrected alpha level of α = 0.05 / 39 ≈ 0.00128. Our current sample size falls short of this threshold, which may limit the statistical power to detect smaller but meaningful effects. Another methodological consideration concerns the use of fNIRS. While fNIRS offers clear advantages in its portability, tolerance to head motion, and compatibility with auditory tasks in young children, a limitation is its lower spatial resolution compared to fMRI, which may reduce the precision in localizing specific brain regions involved in phonological processing. Additionally, the absence of English tasks conducted by monolinguals limits the discussion of the L1-L2 transfer effect. Although we did not find significant differences in developmental brain regions between English and Chinese tasks within the bilingual cohort, we cannot adequately discuss this outcome due to the lack of comparison with monolinguals in English tasks. Consequently, our ability to provide a comprehensive explanation of the underlying brain mechanisms during English tasks among bilingual individuals is constrained. Furthermore, the current findings—based on Chinese-English bilingual children in Taiwan—may not be directly generalizable to other bilingual populations, as cross-linguistic transfer and neural plasticity may vary depending on language-specific features and instructional contexts. Additionally, because family language use and dialectal background were not formally assessed, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all Chinese-English bilingual contexts. Nevertheless, given that most previous studies have focused on bilinguals of alphabetic–alphabetic language combinations, our study provides novel insight into logographic–alphabetic bilingual development, an area that remains relatively underexplored.

In conclusion, the present study provides empirical evidence of developmental transfer effects within the context of bilingual education during the early school years, shedding light on the impact of such education on the development of neural mechanisms. Specifically, our findings indicate that exposure to bilingual education, particularly with early English immersion comprising half of the school curriculum, fosters the development of neural mechanisms associated with English language processing. This underscores the significance of early bilingual exposure in shaping neural architecture. Ultimately, our utilization of a longitudinal approach adds depth and rigor to the investigation of transfer effects, advancing the discourse within the field of bilingual studies.
