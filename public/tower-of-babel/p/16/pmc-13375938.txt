Short‐term psychological impacts of Alzheimer's disease risk disclosure based on blood‐biomarker results
Authors: Bhargavi Ramamurthy, Adena Cho, Maryam Abdolmaleki, Nathan Fayez, Dominic Roby, Karen B, Maynard, Angela L. Jefferson, Corey Bolton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBlood‐based biomarkers (BBMs) of Alzheimer's disease (AD) provide important information about risk for dementia, but little is known about the psychological impact of sharing BBM results to participants with mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Most AD risk disclosure studies have been conducted in cognitively unimpaired individuals and have not used BBMs. This pilot randomized trial tests the hypothesis that disclosing AD risk based on BBMs is not associated with increased psychological harm compared to standard disclosure methods.METHODSParticipants (n = 27, 73 ± 11 years) with MCI were enrolled and randomized to the intervention (n = 16) or control (n = 11) arm. Participants received a risk estimate based on their age, sex, and equivalent Mini‐Mental State Examination score for both groups, with plasma phosphorylated tau 181 results included for the intervention group. Participants completed questionnaires assessing depression, anxiety, and hopelessness prior to disclosure and 2 weeks later. A repeated measures multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to determine changes in psychological responses over the follow‐up period with a time x group interaction term assessing group differences.RESULTSParticipants in the intervention and control group were similar across baseline demographic and clinical factors (P values ≥ 0.23). Repeated measures MANOVA showed a significant reduction in psychological symptoms from baseline to 2‐week follow‐up (P = 0.006) in the combined sample. Follow‐up univariate models showed the reduction was driven by a significant reduction in anxiety (mean ± standard deviation; baseline = 13.3 ± 7.7; follow‐up = 9.8 ± 9.9; P = 0.02). Time x group interaction was non‐significant (P = 0.31) indicating similar psychological responses across groups.DISCUSSIONIn this pilot study of participants with MCI, psychological response to learning risk of progression to dementia did not differ between those who received a risk estimate based on a BBM versus standard clinical information. Two weeks after disclosure, there was an overall decline in anxiety. These preliminary findings suggest that AD risk disclosure using BBMs is not associated with psychological distress.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, and its prevalence is ever increasing as the global population ages. With the economic burden of AD also increasing and expected to hit ≈ 2 billion dollars by 2030, early detection and intervention is necessary to reduce the burden of disease as much as possible. 1 Plasma phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) biomarkers are accurate and accessible tools for detecting AD pathology 2 , 3 , 4 that can help with identifying the cause of cognitive impairment 5 and risk of future decline through predicting cognitive stage transitions. 6 , 7 , 8 These tools have emerged in recent years and hold great potential for clinical use, For example, incorporation of plasma p‐tau181 markedly improves prediction of 4‐year conversion to AD dementia, increasing discrimination from moderate to high (area under the curve 0.70 to 0.88) and substantially improving model fit (Δ Akaike information criterion = −34). 9 Such risk prediction algorithms, strengthened by inclusion of accessible blood‐based biomarkers (BBMs), could have great clinical utility; however, there are still important factors to consider before widespread clinical implementation. One such factor is the psychological impact of sharing these results to individuals at imminent risk of developing AD dementia.

Past work has demonstrated that disclosure for AD dementia risk using genetic factors like apolipoprotein E (APOE) does not cause significant emotional distress. 10 More recently, the apparent safety of risk disclosure in AD has been established for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), 11 , 12 as well as positron emission tomography (PET) biomarkers. 13 , 14 , 15 Generally, people indicate an interest in knowing their dementia risk. 16 Many older adults feel that screening for dementia would allow them to plan for their future financially, implement advanced care, and even encourage them to lead a healthier lifestyle. 17 When asked about biomarker testing, interviews conducted on patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and their care partners indicated an interest in biomarker testing to help them inform treatment decisions and plan for the future. 18 Studies have previously largely measured reactions to AD risk disclosure in cognitively unimpaired individuals, 10 , 19 , 20 , 21 but some have examined risk disclosure in cognitively impaired patients as well, arguing that APOE 22 and amyloid PET 23 disclosure may not negatively impact psychological well‐being. However, there has been some indication of increased psychological distress related to amyloid PET disclosure in individuals with MCI compared to cognitively unimpaired individuals. 24 Similarly, elevated and sustained levels of distress related to amyloid PET disclosure was found in patients with MCI who received a positive result versus patients with MCI who received a negative result. 25 Of note, in each of these studies, symptoms of depression and anxiety were unchanged and below cutoffs for clinical significance, indicating that while disclosure may be distressing, it does not necessarily contribute to clinically significant mood changes. The further development of proper disclosure protocols along with novel anti‐amyloid therapies making AD risk a clinically actionable finding have increased confidence in disclosing AD biomarker results in research studies, and the practice of disclosing amyloid PET results among Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs) is increasing. 26 BBMs are understudied in terms of disclosure, with no studies, to our knowledge, describing the psychological impact, acceptability, and comprehension of AD BBM results disclosure. As the clinical implementation of BBMs continues to rapidly increase, there is a pressing need to better understand how best to communicate results and subsequent AD risk estimates in a comprehensible and effective way, and to understand how patients with MCI respond to learning their dementia risk based on BBMs.

AD is a complex disease that can be challenging for older adults and their families to navigate. Older adults have lower levels of health literacy overall, 27 likely due to a variety of factors, such as less education, lower socioeconomic status, and associated reduced access to quality health care, as well as declining cognitive health. 28 As adults age, their health literacy declines significantly, both in cognitively impaired and unimpaired adults. 29 To address these challenges, we have developed educational materials specifically for disclosing AD risk based on BBM results for adults with MCI. 30 These educational materials were developed based on iterative feedback from research participants with MCI and their care partners. Focus group participants favored visual aids to communicate complex information, AD risk reported as a percentage, and strategies to reduce the risk of AD. The materials were well received, with participants rating them comprehensible and useful; yet, their effectiveness in disclosing actual biomarker results to people with MCI is as yet undetermined.

This exploratory pilot study aimed to use these previously developed materials and examine the emotional impact of disclosing AD dementia risk based on BBMs to individuals with MCI. To do so, we conducted a small, randomized trial comparing the psychological impact of receiving a dementia risk estimate based on demographic information and a cognitive screening test, to a dementia risk estimate based on this same information plus plasma p‐tau181. We hypothesize that providing a dementia risk estimate to patients with MCI based on BBM results is not more psychologically harmful than providing a risk disclosure based on variables commonly considered in standard clinical practice, and that communication of a dementia risk estimate in general would not cause psychological harm.

METHODS

Participants and study design

A convenience sample of English‐speaking participants with MCI were recruited from the Tennessee Alzheimer's Project (TAP), the clinical core cohort of the Vanderbilt ADRC. Participation in this study was offered to all eligible participants in the parent study. MCI diagnosis was pre‐determined during the participant's most recent TAP study visit through a diagnostic consensus conference with a panel of expert clinicians based on results of multiple neuropsychological assessment, questionnaires, clinical interview, and review of medical history. Participants were excluded if they had a history of major psychiatric illness (e.g., bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, severe depression and anxiety) or were deemed by the study clinician to be unsafe to receive risk estimates. Participants were randomized using a demographically stratified randomization approach to one of two groups: a control group in which participants received a risk estimate based on standard clinical information (i.e., age, sex, cognitive test score), and an intervention group in which participants received a risk estimate based on standard clinical information and plasma p‐tau181 results. Participants completed two study visits: first, a risk disclosure session in which they completed baseline questionnaires, received educational information, and learned their personalized dementia risk estimate, and second, a phone call 2 weeks after the disclosure visit to complete follow‐up questionnaires. The initial disclosure session was conducted by the lead study clinician, and the follow‐up phone call was completed by study staff.

Institutional review board approval

The Vanderbilt University Medical Center Institutional Review Board granted ethical approval. Participants were given a written informed consent document which was explained by study staff at the beginning of their study visit. Participants were assessed for decisional capacity and if found to not possess decisional capacity, informed consent was provided by their loved one who also attended the study visit.

Bloodwork processing

Participants underwent morning fasting venous blood draw with standardized sample processing protocols as part of their participation in the parent study. Plasma samples were separated from whole blood by centrifugation at 2000 g and 4°C for 10 minutes and aliquoted into 0.5 mL polypropylene tubes in which they were stored at –80°C. Samples were analyzed at the Vanderbilt University Medical Clinic by laboratory staff who were blinded to clinical information using the Quanterix Simoa HD‐X Analyzer. Quanterix Simoa p‐Tau 181 Advantage PLUS assays were used to quantify plasma levels of p‐tau181. Plasma p‐tau positivity was based on a cutoff score that was developed in the parent study using the Youden index from receiver operating characteristic curves to estimate sensitivity and specificity of p‐tau181 for predicting AD biomarker positivity as determined by other established methods such as PET and CSF.

Risk estimates and disclosure

Risk estimates were based on a previously published algorithm that was developed across two independent cohorts, providing a percentage chance of converting from MCI to dementia within the next 4 years. 9 In the control group, participants received a risk estimate based on their age, sex, and equivalent Mini‐Mental State Examination total score, which was converted from a Montreal Cognitive Assessment test using previously published guidelines. 31 Risk estimates were presented as a percentage chance of developing dementia within the next 4 years, with an accompanying visualization of this percentage. Risk estimates were provided in a written format (see Romano et al. 30 for more information and example documents) and reviewed verbally with participants. An explanation of the risk estimate was provided, but whether the percentage represented a “high risk” or “low risk” status was left open to participant interpretation. In the intervention group, risk estimates were based on these same factors with the additional component of plasma p‐tau181 results (positive or negative). In addition to the risk estimate, participants in the intervention arm received a binary positive or negative p‐tau181 result. Risk disclosure sessions were conducted by one trained study clinician to ensure consistency and were also audio recorded for transcription. The disclosure session was structured as follows: (1) baseline levels of anxiety and depression were assessed; (2) educational materials were reviewed, providing background information on MCI, dementia, and AD pathology, as well as information about the plasma p‐tau blood test for individuals in the intervention group; (3) a personalized risk estimate was reviewed; (4) educational materials regarding risk reduction strategies were reviewed; (5) hopelessness was measured. Throughout the disclosure session, participants were encouraged to ask questions and comprehension of the information was assessed.

Questionnaires

The Geriatric Anxiety Scale (GAS), 32 composed of 30 questions, was used to assess symptoms of anxiety. The questionnaire measures the somatic, cognitive, and affective symptoms of the patient, measured on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from “not at all” (0) to “all the time” (3). The last five questions are not computed as part of the total score but are used to provide additional clinical context for symptoms. Individual scores are then summed for a raw score from 0 to 75. Higher total scores represent greater anxious symptomatology. A cutoff score > 16 has shown the best psychometric validity for determining clinically significant anxiety. 33

The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), 34 composed of 30 questions, was used to assess depressive symptoms. The questionnaire measures depression in older adults based on self‐report. Responses are a binary yes/no with total scores ranging from 0 to 30. Higher scores represent greater depressive symptomatology. A cutoff score ≥ 9 has been shown to best distinguish clinically significant depressive symptomology. 35

The four‐item version of the Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS) 36 was used to assess participants’ self‐reported feelings of hopelessness. Questions assess various aspects of an individual's thoughts about the future, including their ability to succeed in what they hope to accomplish in the future. Each question was answered as true or false, with total scores ranging from 0 to 4. Higher scores indicate greater levels of hopelessness.

Approximately 2 weeks after the disclosure session, a telephone‐based follow‐up conducted by the study staff was completed in which participants again completed the GAS, GDS, and BHS.

Statistical analysis

A repeated measures multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) assessed changes in psychological symptoms from baseline to follow‐up, with secondary univariate models examining changes in each questionnaire individually. We then repeated models with a time x group interaction term to determine whether there were significant differences in changes in psychological symptoms between intervention and control groups. The threshold for statistical significance was set a priori at 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v. 29.0.2.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

The intervention (n = 16) and control (n = 11) groups were comparable in terms of sex, race/ethnicity, age, education, cognition, reading level, and dementia risk estimates (P values ≥ 0.23). The risk estimates ranged from 31% to 74% for the control group (mean ± standard deviation [SD] = 56.6 ± 15.2) and 6% to 94% for the intervention group (mean ± SD = 56.5 ± 37.4). Likewise, the intervention and control groups had similar levels of depression, anxiety, and hopelessness symptomatology at both baseline and the 2‐week follow‐up visit (P values ≥ 0.23). Three participants did not complete follow‐up assessment (two from the intervention group, one from the control group), resulting in follow‐up sample sizes of n = 14 and n = 10, respectively. The three participants who were lost to follow‐up did not differ from those who completed follow‐up across any demographic or clinical variable measured (P values ≥ 0.39; data not shown). See Table 1 for details on participant characteristics and group comparisons on demographic and clinical variables. There were no missing data observed other than the participants lost to follow‐up, who were excluded from analyses. P‐tau–positive participants (n = 9) started off with higher average anxiety symptoms (mean = 17.11 ± 7.3) than p‐tau–negative participants (n = 5; 9.6 ± 3.4), and both p‐tau–positive (10.6 ± 8.8) and –negative (6.8 ± 5.6) groups had reduced mean anxiety levels at follow‐up. The p‐tau–positive group had similar levels of depressive symptoms at baseline (6.7 ± 4.7) and follow‐up (6.8 ± 6.9) while the p‐tau–negative group had small declines in depressive symptoms (baseline = 6.0 ± 4.3; follow‐up = 4.8 ± 4.3). The p‐tau–positive group had a slight increase in hopelessness from baseline (0.1 ± 0.3) to follow‐up (0.7 ± 1.1), while the p‐tau–negative group did not report any symptoms of hopelessness at either time point.

Changes in psychological symptoms over 2‐week follow‐up

First, we conducted a MANOVA in the entire sample to evaluate overall changes in psychological symptoms from baseline to the 2‐week follow‐up. Homogeneity of variance was evaluated using the Levene test (P value > 0.05). A Mauchly test of sphericity was used to test for sphericity (P value > 0.05), and normality was assessed by a visual read of q‐q plots. The analysis revealed a significant multivariate effect of time, Wilks Λ = 0.56, F(3,21) = 5.46, P = 0.006, partial η 2 = 0.44, indicating that the combined dependent variable representing psychological symptoms differed significantly over the follow‐up period.

Follow‐up repeated measures univariate analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted for each outcome to identify the source of the significant multivariate effect. The analysis revealed a significant effect of time for anxiety, F = 6.55, P = 0.02, partial η 2 = 0.22, indicating that anxiety levels differed significantly from baseline (mean ± SD = 13.3 ± 7.7) to the 2‐week follow‐up (9.8 ± 9.9). In contrast, no significant time effects were observed for depression or hopelessness (P values ≥ 0.06), suggesting that the overall multivariate result was primarily driven by reductions in anxiety rather than by changes across other symptom domains. Group differences in psychological symptoms over time (time × group interactions)

A repeated measures MANOVA was conducted to examine whether changes in psychological symptoms from baseline to the 2‐week follow‐up differed between intervention and control groups. The multivariate test of the time × group interaction was not significant (P = 0.36), indicating that the overall pattern of change in anxiety, depression, and hopelessness was similar across group (Figure 1). Follow‐up repeated measures ANOVAs supported this conclusion, with non‐significant time × group interactions for anxiety (P = 0.13), depression (P = 0.56), and hopelessness (P = 0.87). DISCUSSION

After the disclosure of AD dementia risk estimates, participants in this pilot study who received a risk estimate based on plasma p‐tau181 did not have a significantly different emotional response than participants in the control group, with similarly low levels of psychological symptoms of depression, anxiety, and hopelessness being observed in both groups. After 2 weeks, participants experienced an overall decline in psychological symptoms, especially anxiety, across the entire sample. Based on these preliminary findings in a small pilot sample, disclosing risk for AD dementia, whether based on BBMs or standard clinical information, does not seem to be associated with a negative psychological response, and may in fact serve to reduce anxiety in patients with MCI.

The added value of BBMs in predicting dementia risk was evident in our study. Inclusion of BBM results in the risk estimate shifted individuals into very high and very low risk strata, with risk estimates averaging 83% for p‐tau–positive participants, as opposed to 12% for p‐tau–negative participants. Individuals in the control group, who received risk estimates that did not consider biomarker status, had risk estimates ranging from 31% to 74%, leaving much room for uncertainty about prognosis. Our findings that BBM‐based risk disclosure was well tolerated psychologically offers increased confidence in the clinical implementation of these tools and invites future confirmatory studies in larger samples.

All participants in this pilot study had been diagnosed with MCI, making these findings particularly relevant to clinical practice, as BBMs are currently only suggested for individuals with cognitive impairment. 37 Risk disclosure in cognitively unimpaired patients inherently examines a hypothetical future risk for dementia rather than an immediate concern. Individuals with MCI have already noticed significant changes in their cognitive abilities, and biomarker results are thus relevant for understanding their current health and their risk of progressive decline within the immediate future. This study extends previous work, 10 , 13 , 14 , 15 which had focused primarily on cognitively unimpaired individuals and suggests that patients with MCI can emotionally withstand risk disclosure. Despite concerns that individuals with MCI might be more vulnerable to emotional distress given the potentially imminent risk of dementia, no increases in depression, anxiety, or hopelessness were observed after disclosure. This stands in opposition to some recent studies showing increased psychological distress related to learning of a positive amyloid PET result in participants with MCI. 24 , 25 There were a number of key differences between our study and these past studies that may have contributed to our finding of reduced, rather than increased, post‐disclosure psychological symptoms. Specifically, rather than providing a binary positive/negative result alone, we contextualized the finding in a risk estimate that concretely quantifies the participant's chance of developing dementia. This may have reduced anxiety levels by removing uncertainty, even for those with a positive biomarker and high risk estimate. Additionally, our risk disclosure was immediately followed by information on evidence‐based risk reduction strategies. It is likely that providing participants with actionable next steps that they can complete to reduce their risk served to increase sense of agency and thereby reduce anxiety. Finally, these differences could be related to symptom measurement. Prior studies assessed test‐related distress after disclosure while this study focused on clinical symptoms without directly measuring the construct of test‐related distress.

At the 2‐week follow‐up, improvements across the entire sample were observed in psychological symptomatology, driven by a significant reduction in anxiety. There are a multitude of potential reasons for this reduction in anxiety over time. First, individuals who already are aware of cognitive issues could feel reassured to have an explanation for their symptoms. Similarly, anxiety is often higher when a diagnosis is unknown versus known, indicating that knowledge impacts anxiety levels. 38 Stratified models did show that anxiety was significantly reduced in individuals who received their biomarker result and not in those participants whose risk estimate did not include a biomarker status, despite non‐significant interaction effects which may be related to small sample size. This provides support for the idea that more information about health status, even if undesirable, can serve to reduce anxiety. After diagnosis, more education about symptoms, such as what they learned from the educational materials provided during disclosure, may have also led to a reduction in anxiety. Previous studies have shown that emotional support from social connections and relationships can help patients cope with a diagnosis. 39 Because the participants in this study needed a study partner to be eligible, there is a possibility this social support system may have helped them cope with their results and contribute to the decreased anxiety as well.

Biomarker and genetic testing in AD has largely been relegated to use in research studies and clinical trials rather than clinical practice, 40 as results were thought to be not actionable in terms of treatment. Without a disease‐modifying treatment available, the psychological risks of disclosure were thought to outweigh the potential benefits. This equation has shifted in recent years with the advent of novel anti‐amyloid therapies, along with mounting evidence of the psychological safety of disclosure. These results provide further support for risk disclosure in AD by demonstrating that disclosing even an imminent risk of dementia to individuals with cognitive impairment is not associated with negative psychological responses. Indeed, our results suggest that there may be a psychological benefit of disclosure, strongly suggesting that the numerous potential benefits of biomarker testing and results disclosure in AD outweigh any perceived risks relating to psychological impact. Importantly, our study excluded individuals with serious mental illness; more work is needed to determine the psychological safety of disclosure in individuals who are at increased psychological risk.

There are numerous strengths we would like to highlight in this pilot randomized trial. First, we used a stratified randomization approach that balanced the control and intervention group evenly across several relevant demographic variables. We measured psychological response both at the time of the disclosure visit, as well as at 2‐week follow‐up, thus allowing us to measure participants’ immediate reaction to learning of their dementia risk and their psychological response after having some time to process the information and discuss with their loved ones. Additionally, we measured psychological symptoms across several domains, giving us a varied picture of the emotional response of participants. Another key strength of this study is the use of our previously developed educational materials 30 to improve the understanding and knowledge of the participants regarding AD. Despite these strengths, there are some important limitations to consider. As a pilot trial, our sample size was intentionally small; thus, results should be viewed as preliminary with further investigation in a larger sample required to confirm findings. While the groups were not exactly evenly distributed, the slight imbalance is lower than published guidelines, suggesting that this would not influence the measurement of group differences. 41 Given that we recruited participants from an ongoing longitudinal cohort study, selection bias should also be considered. Individuals who are engaged in research and willing to undergo AD screening may be unique in their desire to learn of their risk status compared to general clinical populations. Finally, despite our efforts to recruit a diverse sample in terms of race/ethnicity, education, and reading level, the small sample sizes in these subgroups further limit generalizability to diverse populations.

Overall, disclosure of AD dementia risk, whether based on standard clinical information or supplemented with BBM results, was not associated with emotional harm. On the contrary, participants experienced a decrease in anxiety after learning about their results. These findings, while preliminary, suggest that BBMs can be incorporated into dementia risk discussions without increasing psychological distress and may represent a valuable addition to standard clinical practice.
