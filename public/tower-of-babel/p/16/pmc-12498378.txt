Validation of a new textile sensor band for on-farm heart rate variability assessment in calves
Authors: Luca Turini, Gaia Pesenti Rossi, Giulia Sala, Giulia Foggi, Giulia Gislon, Sara Mondini, Sara Barbieri, Sara Calonaci, Elisabetta Canali, Francesca Bonelli, Marcello Mele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart rate variability (HRV) has been studied as an indicator of stress and welfare in various species, including dairy calves, where significant changes in HRV were linked to cow-calf separation, isolation and disbudding. Wearable bands with smart textiles biotechnology offer a promising, low-stress alternative due to their comfort and ease of use without shaving or taping. The aim of this study was to examine if smart textiles biotechnology could be compared to a Standard base-apex ECG for measuring HRV in dairy calves. Twenty healthy Italian Holstein calves aged (20–60 days old) were recruited. Standard base-apex ECG and Smartex ECG were simultaneously acquired for 16 min in the standing, unsedated, unclipped calves. After extracting HRV parameters, comparisons were made using time, frequency, and nonlinear analyses. Agreement between devices was assessed using the Bland-Altman test and Spearman’s 𝜚 correlations. Sperman’s 𝜚 showed a very strong correlation for Mean RR, SDNN and LF (p value < .001), and a strong correlation for RMSSD, LF/HF and SampEn, indicating the most stable relationship between the two systems. Smart textiles biotechnology can be used for HRV measurement in calves, offering a valuable tool for animal welfare assessment.

Introduction

The measurement of physiological indicators through wearable sensors is a non-invasive tool for on-farm real-time assessment of parameters linked to animal welfare. Heart activity measurements have been widely used for the monitoring of autonomic nervous system activity (von Borell et al. 2007): in particular, heart rate variability (HRV), namely, the variability between beats (Lewis et al. 2007), has been outlined as more precise than Heart Rate (HR) (Kovács et al. 2014). Measurement of HRV allows to separate the assessment of sympathetic and vagal activity at the same time (Malik et al. 1996), but also the balance between the two (von Borell et al. 2007).

In dairy cows, HR and HRV indices have been used to detect stress caused by environmental conditions, routine management practices, and milking (Gygax et al. 2008; Kézér et al. 2017; Erdmann et al. 2018; Linstädt et al. 2024). HRV was also studied in calves for assessing stress responses related to specific management procedures such as disbudding, social isolation, separation from the dam and environmental changes (Stewart et al. 2008; Stewart et al. 2010; Clapp et al. 2015; Byrd et al. 2019). In contrast, studies about HRV changes in response to positive handling (e.g. human-animal relationship, animal behavior) showed inconclusive results in calves (Stewart et al. 2013; Lürzel et al. 2015; Silva et al. 2023).

Given the potential of HRV for stress and animal welfare assessments, practical measurement tools are needed for reliable on-farm HRV monitoring. Heart rate monitors (HRMs) are typically fitted with a band behind the calf’s foreleg, and it is suggested to shave the application site and use ultrasound gel to improve electrode contact with the thorax (Byrd et al. 2019; Silva et al. 2023). However, HRMs may be susceptible to displacement and damage. Moreover, handling calves for HRMs application may potentially influence the calf’s normal behavior, affecting results (Stewart et al. 2017). As already demonstrated in dairy sheep, an alternative could be represented by wearable band with smart textiles biotechnology, given their comfort and the possibility to be worn without shaving or tape, thus reducing animal handling and associated stress (Turini et al. 2022). The development of a new wearable device was necessary because literature and market lack of a technology specifically designed for calves. With the development of this new device, we aimed to design a well fit technology, avoiding lesions, stress, or injuries that may occur during the application of an adapted technology (i.e. those already used in humans) (Turini et al. 2022). Thus, this study aimed to examine if smart textiles biotechnology could be compared to a Standard base-apex ECG for measuring HRV in dairy calves, as an alternative and practical tool for welfare assessment.

Methods

Animals’ management and inclusion criteria

The present prospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pisa (49/2019). An owner’s written consent was obtained for ECG recording for calves included in the study.

Twenty healthy female Italian Holstein calves, ranging in age from 20 to 60 days, were randomly selected from one commercial dairy farm in Northern Italy. Eligible calves were randomly chosen with an Android application (Randomizer, Darshan Institute of Engineering and Technology, Rajkot, India), using the list of identification numbers (written on the ear tag of each calf) provided by the farmer. Animals were housed in multiple straw bedded boxes in accordance with the European Directive 2008/119; they were fed with reconstituted milk via an automatic feeder providing ad libitum amount of milk. Grass hay, starter feed and clean water were freely available to calves.

To be included, calves must be healthy at the physical examination performed by a veterinarian the day of recording, with a special focus on cardiovascular evaluation. Clinical history of calves was also recorded to exclude animals with past problems (House et al. 2020).

Smartex system information

The method is based on the application of textile fabric electrodes, which can be used for extended periods of time since the fabric makes more reliable contact with the animal’s skin than ordinary electrodes that are connected by gel, such as the Ag/AgCl ones. Gel’s hydration level gradually drops, but a textile electrode that is specifically made with multiple layers to absorb water can have a higher humidity level for a longer amount of time. Additionally, the fabric’s structure allows it to adhere to the skin and is flexible. The Smartex system was composed by two movable electrodes (a) integrated into the inner side of an elastic band (b) that is made to fit the chest size of calves (30–300 kg). The two rectangular electrodes (6.5 × 2.5 cm) were connected by a flexible tissue-compatible cable (c) to a connector that could be inserted into a portable electronic device (RUSA, 52X50X15 mm, 50 gm) (Figure 1).

Smartex system. Note: Smartex system composed by an elastic band with two movable electrodes, a connector, the portable electronic device and the neoprene pocket.

The portable electrical device was placed in a pocket located just behind the calves’ shoulders. This device had an Inertial Motion Unit (IMU) to track the calves’ physical activity and could record a single lead ECG at 250 samples/sec with a frequency of 25 Hz. To verify the sensors’ condition and remotely manage the process of saving the physiological data on a digital card, the sensors were also wirelessly connected via Bluetooth to a mobile device.

Data acquisition

Calves were allowed to move freely within their box until the time of measurements. Recordings were performed at the same hour, between 9:00 am and 1:00 pm within two working days.

Standard base-apex ECG (hereinafter called Televet) and Smartex ECG (hereinafter called Smartex) were simultaneously acquired for 16 min in standing, non-sedated, unclipped calves. To maximize contact and get a high-quality ECG signal for both techniques, a small amount of 70% isopropyl alcohol was applied locally to the skin where Televet electrodes were attached before each session. Animals were manually contained while the ECG tracings were being taken.

A conventional base-apex ECG (TeleVet 100, Kruuse, Denmark) was obtained as described in literature (Bonelli et al. 2019; Turini et al. 2022). Electrodes were placed as follows: the negative electrode was positioned over the right jugular groove; the positive electrode was placed caudal to the left olecranon, near the cardiac apex; the ground electrode was placed on the left shoulder, distant from the recording electrodes; and a ground electrode was placed on the sternum (Van Loon and Patteson 2010). Simultaneously, the Smartex band was positioned vertically no more than 2 cm below the standard ECG electrodes (Figure 2).

Application of the two systems (Televet and Smartex) to the calf during recording of ECG traces.

A single lead ECG trace was obtained by each ECG recording device (Televet and Smartex).

The ECG traces recorded by the Smartex device were stored on a memory card integrated within the portable electrical unit, while the ECG traces from the Televet system were saved directly onto a computer connected via Bluetooth.

HRV parameters extraction and preprocessing

Smartex and Televet registrations were imported into Kubios® HRV Standard (Kubios HRV software [version 4.1.2], Biomedical Signal Analysis Group, Department of Applied Physics, University of Kuopio, Finland) for analysis of time, frequency, and nonlinear domain variables to determine HRV measures. The first minute of recording was excluded from analysis and used for synchronizing the two monitoring ECG systems. The other 15 min of recording of ECGs were segmented in 5-min time laps. As a matter of fact, three-time laps were used for analyzing data for each animal. In each time lap, we extracted for the time domain, the mean value of the RR time series (meanRR), the Standard Deviation Normal to Normal (SDNN), the root mean square of successive differences between normal heartbeats (RMSSD). For the frequency domain, we computed the ratio between the low frequency band (LF), from 0.04 to 0.3 Hz, and the high frequency band (HF), from 0.3 to 0.8 Hz (Mohr et al. 2002). LF/HF was considered an index of sympathovagal balance since the powers at LF and HF bands are assumed to be the result of sympathetic and parasympathetic modulation to the heart, respectively. Finally, for the nonlinear domain, we extracted the sample entropy (SampEn), which is used for assessing the complexity of physiological time-series signals.

A manual artifact correction was used to decrease inaccuracy throughout the sampled ensemble as a first step in creating consistency in signal analysis. These data were not subjected to detrending. All HRV analyses from Kubios were reviewed for the percent of artifacts corrected. Time-lapse HRV analyses (from both Smartex and Televet) with artifact correction exceeding 5% were excluded from the study, along with their corresponding paired HRV recordings. Kubios’ results from extracted HRV can be found in Results section.

Data analysis

After parameters extraction, tests in time, frequency, and nonlinear methods were used to compare Smartex to Televet. Data were not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk test), and continue variables were reported as median, 25° and 75° percentiles. To compare the performance of Smartex, Sperman correlation test was used. The Spearman’s rho (𝜚) coefficient was interpreted as follow: <0.10 as negligible correlation, 0.10–0.39 as weak correlation, 0.40–0.69 as moderate correlation, 0.70–0.89 as strong correlation, 0.90–1.00 as very strong correlation (Schober et al. 2018). Agreement between the two methods was assessed using both the Bland-Altman plot and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), providing complementary information on the level of concordance and the consistency of measurements. Specifically, the Bland-Altman test was used to determine the compliance between the data collected from the two different instruments (Martin Bland and Altman 1986). It is usual to compute 95% limits of agreement for each comparison (average difference ± 1.96 standard deviation), to evaluate how far apart two techniques’ measurements were more likely to be for most of the examination period. In the present study, the analysis evaluated the bias and the 95% limits of agreement between Televet vs Smartex ECGs for almost all the parameters. Moreover, the agreement between measurements was assessed also using the ICC. ICC values were interpreted according to the guidelines proposed by Koo and Li (2016): values ≤ 0.50 indicated poor agreement, values between 0.51 and 0.70 moderate agreement, values between 0.71 and 0.90 good agreement, and values > 0.90 excellent agreement.

Statistical analysis was made using the commercial software IBM SPSS Statistics v. 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and JMP (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC) to compute any significant difference among the parameters. A p value < .05 was considered significant.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine whether smart textiles biotechnology could be used for measuring HRV in dairy calves, instead of a standard base-apex ECG, as an alternative tool for HRV assessment.

HRV was characterized using time and frequency domain analyses to evaluate the correlation between two different systems for assessing ECG in calves. A strong correlation and a general agreement between the two systems was confirmed by the statistical approaches considered for the present study. The Bland-Altman concordance test and ICC showed a general agreement between the two systems across all parameters. The moderate correlation of HF between the two systems could be due from differences in extraction and preprocessing of the ECG and the approaches used to manage noise and artifacts. Even when the same algorithm is applied, discrepancies in HF and LF components can still occur due to differences in estimation window lengths or model orders across devices (Shaffer and Ginsberg, 2017). Additionally, no missed heartbeats were recorded during the trials, highlighting the accuracy of the sensor in detecting cardiac signals.

Beyond its accuracy, the proposed system offers several practical advantages that enhance its feasibility in animal monitoring. Unlike conventional electrodes, which require gel application, the fabric-based sensor ensures more stable and reliable contact with the animal’s skin. Its multilayer structure enhances moisture absorption, maintaining optimal conductivity over extended periods. The sensor’s flexibility allows it to conform to the animal’s body, ensuring both comfort and minimal disturbance during data collection. These features make the system a feasible and animal-friendly alternative for HRV monitoring.

To date, this is the only validated study using this type of sensor for calves. Previous studies have validated the same sensor technology in sheep (Turini et al. 2022) and horses (Guidi et al. 2017; Felici et al. 2021; McCrae et al. 2022; McCrae et al. 2023). In the previous work from Turini et al. (2022), Smartex was compared to Televet in standing, non-sedated sheep, and a good agreement of HRV was found between the two devices.

Studies on horses evaluated the use of smart textile technology using different approaches: in the work of Guidi et al. (2017) on free-moving horses a comparison between textile and standard electrode performance was made through the quantification of Motion Artifacts per hour (expressed as percentage, MA%) and the assessment of their distribution, to evaluate the sensitivity to MAs along the temporal dimension. Smart textile electrode solution outperformed the standard solution: in fact, both MA% and the evaluation of the distribution of MAs over time highlighted the better performance of the textile electrodes (Guidi et al. 2017). Subsequently, Felici et al. (2021) compared textile and Ag/AgCl electrodes in horses during standardized exercise on treadmill with different gait: also in this context, textile electrodes were found to be easy to use and comfortable, with an improved and constant signal quality, as confirmed by the Kurtosis value was calculated for each ECG trace as an index of ECG signal quality.

McCrae et al. (2022) evaluated the differences between textile and Ag/AgCl electrodes, demonstrating that smart textile girth band could be used to obtain very high-quality three-lead ECGs in horses at rest, and subsequently found an excellent agreement between the smart textile system and the gold-standard telemetric device in horses at rest and during submaximal exercise, highlighting Strong to perfect correlations between the two devices were observed for HR and metrics of HRV (McCrae et al. 2023).

On the contrary, considering other sensors used for HRV assessment in ruminants, Polar HR monitor has been validated for its use in cows (Hopster and Blokhuis 1994) and widely used in calves’ studies (Mohr et al. 2002; Stewart et al. 2008; Stewart et al. 2010; Clapp et al. 2015; Byrd et al. 2019), but no validation study has been conducted on its accuracy in younger animals. This highlights the need for further research to establish comparative validation across different sensor technologies.

Further development of this tool will allow us to extend battery life and enable the creation of algorithms for the early identification of stress and disease. With the implementation of decision support algorithms, data interpretation will be easy even for the farmers, as alerts about potential health or welfare issues will be sent directly to a smartphone application, enabling timely intervention. Thus, as for any new device developed, a validation was mandatory.

Although the current cost of these band is relatively high (around €500), their large-scale adoption is expected to significantly reduce the price.

A limitation of the study is the small sample size and the fact that all animals were housed in a single dairy farm. Nevertheless, we expect that this technology can be successfully applied across different calf-rearing environment.

Conclusions

Overall, our findings confirm that the tested sensor provides reliable HRV data comparable to the Gold Standard method, while also offering significant advantages in practical application. Its validation in calves, along with its feasibility and comfort, supports its use in future research and on field applications.
