Immune checkpoint inhibitors in bladder cancer: from mechanistic insights to emerging combination frontlines
Authors: Mohammad Mahdi Ajalli, Yalda Sansebli, Davood Ekrami, Fatemeh Alaeenejad, Ainaz Abbasi, Pooya Eini, Farnaz Hassanzadeh, Nastaran Bahrami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bladder cancer (BC), primarily urothelial carcinoma, remains one of the most common and lethal genitourinary malignancies worldwide, with limited long-term outcomes from standard treatments such as chemotherapy and radical cystectomy. These conventional modalities often fail to achieve durable responses and can severely affect patients’ quality of life, underscoring the need for more effective and targeted therapeutic strategies. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTsLA-4) have revolutionized BC therapy by restoring antitumor immune function. Nevertheless, treatment resistance, immune-related adverse events, and patient heterogeneity continue to limit their universal efficacy. This review provides an in-depth overview of ICI mechanisms and highlights evolving therapeutic approaches, including combination strategies with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG), and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs). It also discusses emerging biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and DNA damage repair (DDR) deficiency as predictive tools for treatment selection. Overall, this study underscores the pivotal role of immunotherapy in reshaping bladder cancer management and outlines future directions toward safe, personalized, and biomarker-driven therapeutic paradigms.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) is the ninth most common cancer worldwide, with over 600,000 new cases diagnosed annually [1], and projections suggest the incidence may approach one million cases by 2 + 040 [2]. Moreover, the disease is associated with significant morbidity, causing more than 200,000 deaths each year [3]. BC primarily affects older adults, with an average age at diagnosis of over 75, and occurs 3 to 4 times more frequently in men than in women [4]. In addition, approximately 90% of cases are urothelial carcinoma (UC), while the remaining 10% include squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, and small cell carcinoma [5]. Based on the depth of invasion, BC is classified into non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), which affects 70–75% of patients, and muscle invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), which penetrates deeper layers of the bladder wall [6].

Furthermore, major risk factors include smoking, linked to 50–65% of cases in men and 20–30% in women, as well as occupational exposures and genetic predispositions. Mutations in DNA repair genes such as MSH2 and MLH1, and metabolic genes such as IDH1 and ME1, are implicated in BC development [5, 7, 8]. More importantly, diagnostic methods include cystoscopy and urine cytology as gold standards, with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) increasingly used for staging. Additionally, molecular markers such as FGFR3, MAN1B1, COL6A1, NXPH4, and METTL3 contribute to diagnosis and prognosis [9, 10].

Treatment options include chemotherapy, RC for localized disease, and systemic therapy for metastatic cases [11]. However, these approaches often compromise quality of life. For example, RC may lead to incontinence, sexual dysfunction, and psychological distress, especially in women. Moreover, NMIBC treated with transurethral resection shows high recurrence rates, and the overall cost of treatment remains substantial [12, 13].

In recent years, immunotherapy has emerged as a promising alternative. It includes allogeneic stem cell transplantation, antineoplastic vaccines (e.g., Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG)), pro-inflammatory cytokines, adoptive T-cell transfer, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) therapy, and monoclonal antibodies. Among these, Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown particular promise by blocking inhibitory molecules such as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), thereby restoring T-cell–mediated antitumor responses [14]. Despite their potential, ICIs are not universally effective and may cause immune-related adverse events affecting the skin, gastrointestinal tract, liver, lungs, and endocrine system [15]. This review aims to explore the therapeutic role of ICIs in bladder cancer, their combination with other modalities, associated side effects, predictive biomarkers, and future challenges.

Mechanisms of action of immune checkpoint inhibitors

BC provides a valuable model for studying immune evasion and strategies to enhance antitumor immunity [16]. Moreover, a deeper understanding of the tumor microenvironment (TME) is essential for improving the efficacy of immunotherapies such as ICIs [17]. The TME is composed of various immune cells, including regulatory T cells (Tregs), B cells, natural killer (NK) cells, tolerogenic dendritic cells (tDCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), as well as immunosuppressive molecules like transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), and Artemin [18].

These components interact to suppress T-cell function and promote tumor progression. For example, collagen secreted by TAMs activates the Phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathway via integrin α2β1, enhancing tumor proliferation [19]. Similarly, the Cyclooxygenase-2/microsomal prostaglandin E synthase-1/prostaglandin E2 (COX2/mPGES1/PGE2) axis regulates PD-L1 expression in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, contributing to immune escape [20, 21]. Additional factors such as low pH, hypoxia, nutrient competition, and nitric oxide production further impair T-cell activity within the TME [22].

Immune checkpoints play a critical role in maintaining immune tolerance and regulating inflammation. ICIs work by blocking inhibitory pathways, primarily PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, to restore T-cell function. PD-1 is expressed on activated T and B cells and macrophages, while PD-L1 is found on antigen-presenting cells and tumor cells. Binding of PD-L1 to PD-1 induces T-cell anergy or apoptosis, allowing tumors to evade immune surveillance [14]. ICIs such as Atezolizumab, Avelumab, and Durvalumab target PD-L1, while Nivolumab and Pembrolizumab inhibit PD-1 [23].

CTLA-4 is another checkpoint molecule expressed on activated T cells. It competes with CD28 for binding to B7 ligands (B7.1 and B7.2) on antigen-presenting cells, thereby downregulating T-cell activation [24]. Although its exact mechanism remains unclear, hypotheses suggest either competitive inhibition or direct signaling suppression via its cytoplasmic tail. Anti-CTLA-4 antibodies such as Ipilimumab and Tremelimumab have been developed to block this pathway and enhance immune responses [25–27]. ICIs in bladder cancer

CTLA-4

CTLA-4 is a surface molecule expressed on activated cytotoxic T cells. It binds to CD80 and CD86 on antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby inhibiting T-cell activation and dampening antitumor immune responses. Therapeutic antibodies targeting CTLA-4 are designed to block this inhibitory signal, enabling T cells to more effectively recognize and eliminate cancer cells. These agents enhance immune surveillance by disrupting CTLA-4–mediated suppression [28].

Furthermore, both preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that CTLA-4 blockade can amplify immune responses against tumors, offering therapeutic benefits in BC settings. Evidence suggests that this approach may induce immune-mediated tumor regression and improve outcomes in high-risk BC patients [29]. Specifically, patients with aggressive disease subtypes have shown greater responsiveness to anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapy, highlighting its potential to promote antitumor immunity and enhance treatment efficacy [30].

However, ICI therapy is not without risks. For instance, a BC patient developed a grade 3 asthma exacerbation following ICI administration. Subsequent analysis revealed elevated CTLA-4 expression in CD4⁺FOXP3⁺ regulatory T cells during the early phase preceding the asthma attack [31]. Additionally, endocrine-related adverse events such as hypophysitis appear to be uniquely associated with CTLA-4 inhibitors. These events often present with nonspecific symptoms, necessitating vigilant screening and, in many cases, lifelong hormone replacement therapy [32].

In BC patients, increased CTLA-4 signaling and accumulation of regulatory T cells near the TME have been linked to poor immune responses. Repeated administration of anti-CTLA-4 antibodies has been shown to shift the immune balance, increasing CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells relative to Treg cells, thereby enhancing tumor control. This underscores the importance of CTLA-4 inhibition in overcoming resistance and improving ICI efficacy [33].

Among anti-CTLA-4 agents, Ipilimumab has demonstrated a generally favorable safety profile in BC patients, though in some cases, severe toxicity has led to delays in surgical intervention. Tremelimumab, currently under investigation, has shown mild adverse effects in advanced BC cases, but rare life-threatening complications have also been reported. These findings emphasize the critical need for close monitoring of immune-related toxicities during CTLA-4–targeted therapy [14].

Furthermore, while agents such as ipilimumab and tremelimumab facilitate infiltration of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells into bladder tumors, they do not significantly reduce the number of FOXP3⁺ regulatory T cells. This limitation may compromise the overall immune response, suggesting that strategies aimed at depleting Tregs could enhance therapeutic efficacy [34].

A case report described a BC patient who developed acral vasculitis following treatment with tremelimumab. The condition improved markedly with oral corticosteroid therapy, reinforcing the importance of proactive management of immune-related adverse events during CTLA-4 blockade [35].

PD-1/PD-L1

ICIs, such as PD-1 and Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1), play critical roles in T cell regulation by modulating immune responses to tumorigenesis. PD-1 is mainly expressed on T lymphocytes, but PD-L1 is predominantly expressed on tumor cells. Interaction of these two proteins leads to the repression of T cell activities and hence to the tumoral immune suppression. Monoclonal antibodies against PD-1 and PD-L1 have shown the ability to restore T- cell functionality and have shown remarkable success in the therapy of BC, which has a high mutational burden and elevated antigenicity [36, 37].

In preclinical studies, intravesical administration of anti-PD-1 therapy in syngeneic models of BC effectively inhibited tumor growth, enhanced infiltration of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, and induced sustained immune activation upon secondary tumor exposure. Survival outcomes were comparable to those observed with systemic anti-PD-1 therapy, while minimizing the adverse effects typically associated with intravesical chemotherapy. These findings underscore the potent antitumor efficacy and durable immune stimulation achieved through localized anti-PD-1 treatment [38].

Clinical trials involving BC patients treated with anti-PD-1 agents have reported endocrine-related immune-related adverse events (irAEs) in approximately 19% of cases, with thyroid dysfunction being the most prevalent. In most instances, hormonal therapy proved effective in managing these complications, emphasizing the importance of close monitoring of endocrine function during ICI treatment [39].

Moreover, In BC, there is a high correlation between the expression levels of certain genes and the effectiveness of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatment. Patients carrying a high-risk genotype are likely to exhibit a reduced response to immunotherapeutic treatment. Immune cell composition and certain molecular pathways are determinant in regulating resistance or sensitivity to Anti-PD-1 treatment. The data can be useful for anticipating the results of anti-PD-1 treatment [40].

Single-cell proteomic analysis of muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) identified the presence of PD-L1 positive cancer stem cell-like clusters, which were associated with poor prognosis. Notably, the cluster of CD274 + ALDH + was significantly associated with immune checkpoints like PD-1, suggesting T cell exhaustion. The present findings highlight an important role of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in immune suppression within MIBC. Identification of this cluster opens up potential targets for promoting immunotherapy with ICIs [41].

Combination strategies involving ICIs

Monotherapy with ICIs has shown promising but variable efficacy in BC, with many patients experiencing only partial or transient responses [42]. To overcome primary or acquired resistance, recent research has shifted toward combination strategies that integrate ICIs with other therapeutic modalities [43]. These approaches aim to enhance antitumor immunity through complementary mechanisms, such as increasing tumor antigen release, improving immune cell infiltration, and modulating the tumor microenvironment [44]. Various preclinical and clinical studies have explored combinations of ICIs with chemotherapy, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), radiotherapy, and intravesical immunotherapies like BCG. ICIs + Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy remains a foundational component of cancer treatment, targeting rapidly proliferating cells through mechanisms such as DNA damage induction, cell cycle disruption, and apoptosis. In MIBC, platinum-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy is considered the standard of care. Recent studies suggest that combining ICIs with chemotherapy may enhance therapeutic efficacy, either as a complementary or alternative strategy to conventional regimens [58, 59].

Although chemotherapy is widely used in BC, its tolerability and effectiveness vary significantly across agents. A meta-analysis revealed that agents such as vinflunine, gemcitabine, and taxanes offer limited clinical benefit and are associated with substantial toxicity. Therefore, optimizing therapeutic outcomes requires careful patient selection and regimen customization, though these factors were not the primary focus of the referenced study [45].

Additional research confirms that neoadjuvant chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of MIBC management; however, the integration of ICIs, such as durvalumab, may confer added benefits. A practice-changing example of ICI–chemotherapy integration in operable MIBC is the phase III NIAGARA trial. In this study, cisplatin-eligible patients received neoadjuvant durvalumab plus gemcitabine/cisplatin followed by radical cystectomy and adjuvant durvalumab, compared with neoadjuvant gemcitabine/cisplatin followed by cystectomy alone. Perioperative durvalumab significantly improved event-free survival (24-month EFS 67.8% vs. 59.8%; HR 0.68) and overall survival (24-month OS 82.2% vs. 75.2%; HR 0.75), supporting the incorporation of perioperative chemoimmunotherapy as a new standard option for resectable MIBC and directly reshaping the curative-intent landscape [48]. In addition to adjuvant nivolumab, perioperative durvalumab with gemcitabine/cisplatin followed by adjuvant durvalumab after cystectomy represents a practice-changing perioperative strategy for cisplatin-eligible resectable MIBC based on phase III evidence. Notably, oxaliplatin (OXP) exhibits dual functionality in BC treatment, acting both as a chemotherapeutic and an immunomodulatory agent via the induction of immunogenic cell death. Co-administration of OXP with a PD-1 inhibitor significantly enhances immune cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment, thereby amplifying antitumor immune responses. Furthermore, OXP modulates the expression of key regulatory molecules, including CD134, T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), and PD-L1, reinforcing its immunostimulatory effects [46]. Despite the widespread use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, complete pathological responses are achieved in only 30–40% of MIBC patients. Studies evaluating the combination of ICIs with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) have yielded promising results, suggesting that this approach may overcome the limitations of chemotherapy alone and improve clinical outcomes [47]. (See Table 1)

In a study by Pezeshki et al. (2023), immunotumoroid models derived from BC patient tumor tissues, containing both immune and non-immune cell populations, were screened against chemotherapy agents and ICIs. The models demonstrated predictive accuracy, with most responding to chemotherapy and a subset showing sensitivity to ICIs, thereby reflecting real-world patient responses [61].

Moreover, a clinical study involving 74 patients (mean age 67) revealed that first-line ICI combined with chemotherapy resulted in significantly longer median survival (27.5 vs. 8.9 months) compared to chemotherapy alone. Additionally, treatment costs were lower in the combination group relative to sequential ICI administration, indicating improved cost-effectiveness [49].

Neoadjuvant ICIs, when used in conjunction with chemotherapy, have shown encouraging efficacy in MIBC and are associated with a more favorable toxicity profile compared to cisplatin-based regimens. However, most ICIs currently lack cost-effectiveness, with the exception of atezolizumab in select comparative evaluations. Further randomized controlled trials and real-world studies are essential to validate clinical benefits and guide cost-effectiveness assessments for neoadjuvant applications [62].

ICIs + ADCs

ADCs represent a novel class of cancer therapeutics in which cytotoxic agents are chemically linked to monoclonal antibodies [63]. This strategy enables selective targeting of malignant cells while minimizing damage to healthy tissues. In the treatment of BC, ADCs are designed to target specific tumor-associated antigens such as nectin-4, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (TROP-2), and epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) [59, 64].

Promising therapeutic combinations involving ICIs and ADCs have emerged for advanced BC [65]. Notable agents include enfortumab vedotin (targeting NECTIN-4), RC48-ADC (targeting HER2), and sacituzumab govitecan (targeting TROP-2), all of which have demonstrated encouraging clinical outcomes [66, 67]. Continued real-world research is essential to refine these approaches and enhance clinical applicability [52].

Enfortumab vedotin (EV) is an ADC directed against NECTIN-4, approved for the treatment of advanced urothelial carcinoma (UC) [68]. The EV-301 trial showed that EV significantly improved overall survival (OS) and disease control following prior chemotherapy and ICI therapy [69]. However, its precise efficacy in patients previously treated with avelumab or pembrolizumab remains to be fully elucidated [51] (See Table 1). Although first-line combination therapy with EV and pembrolizumab has shown effectiveness in advanced BC, variability in patient responses underscores the need for ongoing evaluation and optimization of combination strategies [53].

Sacituzumab govitecan (SG) is a TROP-2–directed antibody–drug conjugate with clinically meaningful activity in pretreated urothelial carcinoma and a strong rationale for combination with ICIs. In TROPHY-U-01 cohort 3, SG plus pembrolizumab demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity after progression on platinum-based chemotherapy, including a notable objective response rate and durable responses in a subset of patients. However, in the randomized phase III TROPiCS-04 trial (SG versus chemotherapy of physician’s choice after prior platinum and checkpoint inhibitor therapy), the primary endpoint of overall survival was not met, underscoring the need for careful patient selection and proactive toxicity mitigation (e.g., neutropenia) [70, 71].

Disitamab vedotin (RC48) is a HER2-directed antibody–drug conjugate with rapidly emerging evidence in HER2-expressing urothelial carcinoma. In a phase III trial, disitamab vedotin plus toripalimab significantly improved progression-free survival (13.1 vs. 6.5 months; HR 0.36) and overall survival (31.5 vs. 16.9 months; HR 0.54) compared with platinum-based chemotherapy, with a higher objective response rate, supporting a more evidence-based positioning of HER2-targeted ADC–ICI strategies beyond anecdotal reports [72].

The phase 3 EV-302 trial further validated the efficacy of combining EV with pembrolizumab, demonstrating superior outcomes compared to platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with advanced BC. Median OS reached 31.5 months versus 16.1 months, and median progression-free survival was 12.5 months versus 6.3 months. These findings support the adoption of ADC–ICI combinations as a new first-line standard of care in advanced BC (See Table 1) [49].

Importantly, ADC–ICI combinations have also advanced into the perioperative setting. In the phase III KEYNOTE-905/EV-303 study in predominantly cisplatin-ineligible MIBC, perioperative enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab (neoadjuvant therapy followed by cystectomy and adjuvant therapy) demonstrated significant improvements in event-free survival and overall survival versus surgery alone, together with a substantially higher pathologic complete response rate. These data extend the clinical impact of EV-based regimens beyond metastatic disease and support a new curative-intent option for patients unable to receive cisplatin [73, 74].

Recent systematic and narrative reviews provide an integrated overview of ADC classes, biomarkers, resistance mechanisms, and evolving ADC–ICI combinations in urothelial carcinoma, further strengthening the contextual framework of this Sect [75].

ICIs + Radiation therapy

ICIs have emerged as a transformative therapeutic option in the management of advanced malignancies. However, the proportion of patients achieving durable responses remains relatively limited [76]. Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), which delivers high-dose radiation in a limited number of precisely targeted fractions, has shown efficacy in treating isolated metastases and may act synergistically with ICIs by enhancing immune activation [55, 77].

While encouraging outcomes have been reported in advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with ICI–radiotherapy combinations, data in advanced BC remain inconclusive (See Table 1). This uncertainty complicates the prediction of treatment responsiveness in BC patients [56]. Nevertheless, recent studies have demonstrated that combining PD-1 inhibitors with chemotherapy and hypofractionated radiation therapy in BC yields a favorable safety profile and reduced adverse effects compared with previous studies [78]. The integration of oral agents and advanced radiotherapeutic techniques such as Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy (IMRT) has contributed to minimizing radiation-induced toxicity [54] (See Table 1)

Despite these advancements, late-onset radiation-induced spinal cord injury remains a clinical concern, typically manifesting between 9 and 15 months post-treatment. Symptoms include motor weakness, sensory deficits, and urinary dysfunction, often attributed to microvascular damage. Concurrent chemotherapy may exacerbate this risk by sensitizing neural tissues to radiation injury [79].

Preclinical in vivo models have provided compelling evidence for the efficacy of combined radiotherapy and ICI in BC. Mice treated with this combination exhibited superior survival rates, increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, and signs of an abscopal effect, a phenomenon where localized treatment induces systemic antitumor responses [80]. Clinically, patients ineligible for surgery who underwent Transurethral Resection of Bladder Tumor (TURBT) followed by ICI and radiotherapy achieved complete response rates of up to 81%, although grade 3–4 toxicities were observed in approximately 34% of cases. These findings underscore the potential of radiotherapy to synergize with ICIs, enhancing tumor control and survival outcomes in BC [57].

ICIs + BCG or novel vaccines

BCG is an attenuated strain of Mycobacterium bovis used as an intravesical immunotherapy for BC [81]. It acts by stimulating the immune system to recognize and eliminate malignant cells lining the bladder epithelium. However, BCG may upregulate PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, potentially attenuating its antitumor efficacy [82].

In NMIBC, BCG remains the gold standard of care [83]. Yet, its effectiveness diminishes in patients exhibiting refractoriness or resistance [84]. Combining BCG with ICIs such as pembrolizumab or atezolizumab offers a promising strategy to enhance therapeutic outcomes [45] (See Table 1).

Studies have identified four major BCG strain groups, TICE, RIVM, Pasteur, and Tokyo, with no significant differences in efficacy among them. Nonetheless, maintaining consistency in strain administration throughout the treatment course is recommended to ensure optimal immune activation [85].

The variability in patient response to BCG therapy may stem from diverse clinical and biological factors. Despite technological advancements, BCG efficacy still relies heavily on conventional clinical assessments and invasive procedures like cystoscopy, which are costly and burdensome [86]. A subset of high-risk NMIBC patients demonstrates heterogeneous outcomes when treated with BCG and ICIs. Personalized therapeutic approaches based on individual clinical profiles may improve response rates and overall prognosis [87]. (See Table 1)

Beyond BCG, novel cancer vaccines are being explored to augment immune responses. For instance, the bacterial-vectored oral WT1 vaccine has shown potential in preclinical models to inhibit tumor growth and may offer a new avenue for patients unresponsive to conventional therapies [88, 89]. Such strategies could complement existing modalities like BCG and pave the way for innovative immunotherapeutic interventions in BC [90].

Emerging evidence suggests that the efficacy of immunotherapy involving BCG and ICIs may be influenced by the composition of the gastrointestinal and urinary tract microbiomes [91, 92] These microbial communities can modulate immune responses, either enhancing or impairing treatment success. A deeper understanding of host–microbiome interactions could facilitate the development of more targeted and effective immunotherapeutic strategies [93]. Adverse events and management of ICIs

ICIs have significantly advanced cancer immunotherapy by reactivating suppressed antitumor immune responses. However, their use is associated with irAEs, which can affect multiple organ systems, including dermatologic, endocrine, gastrointestinal, hematologic, cardiovascular, renal, and neurologic domains [15]. Early recognition, evaluation, and management of irAEs are critical, particularly in elderly patients or those with pre-existing conditions, to prevent severe complications [94]. Endocrine toxicities

Thyroid dysfunction is among the most common endocrine irAEs, often presenting initially as acute thyrotoxicosis followed by hypothyroidism [95, 96]. This biphasic progression may necessitate lifelong thyroid hormone replacement, especially in patients receiving combination therapy with anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 agents. Routine monitoring of thyroid function is essential for timely intervention [97, 98].

Dermatologic manifestations

In patients with advanced urothelial carcinoma treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, a significant proportion develop immune-related adverse events, and among these, dermatologic irAEs such as rash and pruritus are among the most frequently reported [99]. Interestingly, the occurrence of skin-related irAEs correlated with improved OS, progression-free survival (PFS), and clinical benefit, suggesting their potential as prognostic biomarkers [100] (See Table 2). Further multicenter studies are warranted to validate these associations and elucidate underlying mechanisms.

Gastrointestinal and hematologic toxicities

Gastrointestinal irAEs, such as ICI-induced colitis, can range from mild to life-threatening. Prompt diagnosis and management with corticosteroids or immunosuppressants are essential to mitigate complications [101]. Hematologic irAEs, though rare, include anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia, with autoimmune hemolytic anemia being the most frequent [102]. These events may occur during or after treatment and require tailored management strategies, including granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), antibiotics, and corticosteroids, especially in elderly or immunocompromised patients [103].

Combination therapy toxicities

A study evaluating nivolumab monotherapy and its combination with ipilimumab alongside chemoradiotherapy in MIBC reported grade 3/4 hematologic and gastrointestinal toxicities, with higher incidence in combination regimens. Late-onset irAEs were common, underscoring the need for continuous monitoring during and after treatment [54].

Cardiovascular and pulmonary events

Cardiovascular irAEs, though less frequent, include venous thromboembolism, myocardial infarction, myocarditis, and heart failure. Accurate identification, often hindered by limitations in ICD coding, can be improved through standardized adjudication methods [104]. Sequential ICI therapy has been linked to increased incidence and severity of irAEs, with skin, endocrine, and pulmonary toxicities being most common [105].

Renal and neurologic complications

Acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) is a serious but uncommon immune-related adverse event (irAE) associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors, most frequently occurring in patients treated with PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade [106]. Early diagnosis and treatment with glucocorticoids, and in some cases mycophenolate, are necessary to preserve renal function. Notably, the development of irAEs has been correlated with improved treatment efficacy and survival, reinforcing the importance of vigilant monitoring [107].

Age-Related considerations

Sequential ICI therapy has demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety in elderly patients, including those with BC, relative to younger individuals [108]. Although older patients may require slightly more corticosteroid support, the frequency and severity of irAEs are largely similar. Individualized management remains essential in this population [109].

Case reports of severe IrAEs

Several case reports highlight the potential severity of irAEs. An 80-year-old woman with BC developed multi-organ irAEs, including myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis, after adjuvant nivolumab, ultimately leading to fatal complications [110]. Another patient developed a rare form of non-Aspergillus allergic bronchopulmonary mycosis (ABPM) following pembrolizumab therapy, emphasizing the need for heightened awareness of atypical pulmonary irAEs [111]. Additionally, an 82-year-old patient treated with ICIs developed fulminant myocarditis and myasthenia gravis, resulting in cardiac and respiratory failure despite standard interventions. These cases underscore the critical importance of early recognition and proactive management of serious irAEs [112].

Identifying predictive biomarkers of response to ICIs in BC is crucial for optimizing patient selection and improving therapeutic outcomes [113, 114]. Among the proposed biomarkers, PD-L1 expression has received considerable attention [115] (See Table 2). In a cohort of Chinese patients with MIBC, 52.4% exhibited high PD-L1 expression, which correlated with increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, indicating active antitumor immunity [116]. These immunologically “hot” tumors, characterized by elevated PD-L1 expression (CPS > 10) and dense immune cell infiltration (CD3⁺, CD8⁺, CD163⁺), are considered more responsive to immunotherapy [117].

Recent data suggest a link between PD-L1 overexpression and high-grade bladder neoplasms, implicating a relationship between PD-L1 levels, tumor invasion depth, and immune activation within the TME [118]. However, the clinical utility of PD-L1 as a predictive biomarker remains limited by several challenges. Tumor heterogeneity, sampling variability, and tissue selection, especially when using reduced-sampling methods like tissue microarray (TMA), can lead to inaccurate assessment of PD-L1 status [117]. Additionally, diagnostic variability across immunohistochemical (IHC) assays contributes to inconsistent results. For example, IHC using the 28 − 8 antibody detected PD-L1 in 42.3% of cases, while SP263 yielded 22.1%, with SP263-positive patients showing improved survival. In contrast, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) detected PD-L1 expression in only 2.1% of cases [119].

To enhance detection accuracy, it is recommended to evaluate PD-L1 expression using primary bladder tumor tissue and assess both CPS and IC Score. This approach identifies more eligible patients compared to isolated lymph node analysis or IC scoring alone [120]. IHC performed on urine cytology blocks has also shown promise, with a 10% cut-off effectively predicting ICI benefit in upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) [121]. Despite these advances, PD-L1 expression alone remains an imperfect biomarker, as responses to ICIs have been observed in patients with low PD-L1 levels, and conversely, treatment failure can occur in those with high expression. This underscores the importance of additional factors such as immune cell composition and activation status in determining therapeutic efficacy [117].

Beyond PD-L1, TMB has emerged as a robust predictive biomarker. High TMB (≥ 10 mutations per megabase) is associated with improved clinical responses to ICIs [122] (See Table 2). Tumors with elevated TMB exhibit increased infiltration of activated CD4⁺, CD8⁺ T cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, reflecting enhanced immunogenicity [123]. Importantly, TMB is independent of PD-L1, and both markers can complement each other in predicting treatment outcomes. A weak correlation between TMB and PD-L1 has been reported, suggesting that their combined assessment may improve response prediction [116, 124].

Another promising biomarker is DDR deficiency. Studies have shown that DDR gene alterations are prevalent in advanced BC. While ATM gene deficiency is associated with poor prognosis, mutations in other DDR genes confer platinum sensitivity and increased likelihood of response to ICIs, making DDR status a valuable marker for therapeutic stratification [125].

Future perspectives and emerging strategies

As the therapeutic landscape of BC continues to evolve, novel strategies are being explored to overcome the limitations of ICIs, particularly treatment resistance and suboptimal patient responsiveness [126]. One of the most pressing challenges is ICI resistance, which has been linked to elevated levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6). Elevated IL-6 can impair cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) function, thereby weakening antitumor immunity and compromising therapeutic efficacy [127]. Additional mechanisms contributing to resistance include defective tumor antigen presentation, disruption of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway, and reduced PD-L1 expression within the tumor microenvironment [128].

To counteract these mechanisms, combination therapies have gained prominence. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that IL-6 inhibition in conjunction with atezolizumab can induce tumor regression [127]. Furthermore, the integration of boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) with ICIs has shown potential in reversing immunotherapy resistance by triggering abscopal effects and systemic immune activation [129]. Engineered oncolytic viruses designed to deliver ICI expression directly into tumor cells are also under investigation, representing a novel approach to enhance immune engagement and therapeutic precision [130].

In parallel, the development of advanced predictive biomarkers is reshaping personalized immunotherapy. Network-based tools such as NetBio have demonstrated superior accuracy in predicting treatment response compared to traditional markers, especially when combined with TMB [131]. Additionally, reduced expression of arginase (ARG) has been associated with increased PD-L1 levels in myeloid cells and improved responsiveness to ICIs [132]. A particularly promising biomarker, Ascore, a four-gene signature, has outperformed both TMB and PD-L1 in predicting OS and immunotherapy outcomes in bladder cancer [133] (See Table 2).

Emerging research also highlights the role of the microbiome in modulating ICI efficacy [134]. The composition of microbial communities in the gastrointestinal and urinary tracts can influence immune activation and treatment success [135]. Microbiome-directed interventions, including fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), probiotics, and prebiotics, are being explored as adjunctive strategies to enhance immunotherapeutic responses [136, 137].

Collectively, these innovations point toward a future in which bladder cancer treatment is increasingly personalized, biomarker-driven, and supported by multimodal therapeutic regimens. Continued research into resistance mechanisms, biomarker refinement, and immune modulation will be essential to fully realize the potential of ICIs in bladder cancer.

Conclusion

ICIs have emerged as a cornerstone of modern BC therapy, offering durable responses in selected patient populations and reshaping contemporary treatment paradigms. This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of their mechanisms of action, therapeutic applications, adverse event profiles, and the evolving importance of predictive biomarkers. Despite their substantial clinical benefits, challenges such as therapeutic resistance and biomarker variability continue to limit their universal applicability.

The integration of combination approaches, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), and novel immunomodulators, has shown promise in enhancing efficacy and overcoming resistance. At the same time, the discovery and validation of reliable biomarkers, such as PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), DNA damage repair (DDR) deficiencies, and emerging tools like NetBio, ARG, and Ascore, are essential for refining patient selection and optimizing therapeutic outcomes.

Looking ahead, the future of bladder cancer immunotherapy is moving toward personalized, biomarker-driven strategies supported by adaptive clinical trial designs and high-throughput multi-omics technologies. Moreover, the incorporation of microbiome-targeted interventions and innovative delivery platforms, including oncolytic viruses, is expanding the therapeutic landscape. Through continued interdisciplinary research and clinical innovation, ICIs are poised to offer more effective, targeted, and durable treatment options for patients with bladder cancer.
