Network-Based Multiomic Nutrient-Associated Predictive Models for Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Authors: Martine Saint-Cyr, Evaniya Shakya, Janet C Siebert, Emily B Hill, Nancy F Krebs, Edwin deZoeten, Sarah J Borengasser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a multifactorial disease involving a complex interplay between host physiology, the gut microbiome, and environmental factors such as diet and nutrition. Multiomic analyses may help to identify potential nutrient-associated omic predictors of IBD, allowing for the design of targeted dietary approaches for disease prevention and management.ObjectivesOur objective was to apply the bioinformatics tool, Consolidated Analysis of Network Topology and Regression Elements (CANTARE), to an integrated multiomics dataset to generate nutrient-associated predictive models for IBD.MethodsWe previously used a published data set of microbiome relative abundance (mb), untargeted metabolomics (met), and microbial-derived enzymes (e) in stool samples from 153 adults (IBD = 111, healthy control = 42) to build a network of cross-omic relationships that differed by IBD status. We now revisit this network to identify diet-associated predictive models of IBD using linear regression via the CANTARE workflow.ResultsThe network included 20 literature-supported nutrient-associated predictors across 3 subnetworks. We created 1 predictive model from each subnetwork. These models (M1, M2, and M3) contained 3, 4, and 11 predictors, respectively. Model performance was high, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.87, 0.90, and 0.95 and pseudo-R2 of 0.42, 0.55, and 0.71 (all permutation P values < 0.001) for M1, M2, and M3, respectively. Some metabolites, such as histamine, were associated with greater odds of IBD, whereas others, such as ascorbate (vitamin C), pyridoxamine (vitamin B6), and choline, were associated with lower odds of IBD.ConclusionsCANTARE provides an unbiased and comprehensive strategy that can integrate multiple omics to identify potential nutrient-associated predictors of IBD. Our models support the generation of hypotheses for follow-up targeted investigation in future dietary interventions for the management of IBD.Keywords: inflammatory bowel disease, nutrition, multiomic, network analysis, predictive modeling, gut microbiome, metabolome

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is an umbrella term for a heterogeneous set of inflammatory diseases affecting the gastrointestinal tract, most commonly ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). The etiology of IBD is not completely understood. It is a multifactorial disease that exhibits a variety of molecular interactions involving a complex interplay between genetics, the gut microbiome, the immune system, and environmental factors [1,2]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that the rise in the incidence of IBD, particularly in countries with previously low-incidence such as southern Europe and Asia, is associated with the consumption of a “Western-style diet” [3]. Other studies have demonstrated that patients with IBD have significant shifts in their gut microbial composition that may be specific to their genetic phenotype but also likely modulated by nongenetic factors, such as diet [4]. Diet is a reported risk factor for the development of IBD, playing a substantial pathogenic role in observed intestinal dysbiosis [[5], [6], [7]]. However, it remains unclear how specific dietary constituents modulate gut microbes and other gut-derived small molecules (e.g., metabolites, enzymes) that may cause, sustain, mitigate, or predict IBD.

Gut metabolomic profiles are comprised of features from the digestion of dietary constituents, endogenous human metabolites, and microbially derived compounds [8]. For example, fiber consumption may directly impact gut-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are metabolites produced in the colon by microbial fermentation [9]. Increased levels of SCFAs are associated with improved gut and cardiometabolic health [10,11]. However, in IBD, dysbiosis disrupts gut homeostasis, leading to decreases in SCFA-producing bacteria and dysregulated intestinal immune responses [1,12,13]. Such disruptions have been investigated, and previous research suggests gut microbiome signatures may predict response to therapy [14]. Therefore, the assessment of gut metabolomics and microbiome profiles during IBD may provide insight into specific mechanisms by which diet could influence the development and progression of the condition or other deleterious effects on health.

Primary nonresponse rates of ≤40% have been observed in clinical trials and real-world data on IBD therapeutics, primarily due to the difficulty in predicting individual patient responses to different treatments [15]. A comprehensive approach integrating nutritional constituents, clinical outcomes, and multiple biological omes such as the gut microbiome and metabolome, may provide new insights into the complexity of IBD etiology and its heterogeneous clinical outcomes. Due to recent advances in bioinformatics tools, such high-dimensional data may be analyzed to detect novel and biologically relevant relationships that generate specific hypotheses for follow-up experiments. For example, Franzosa et al. [16] analyzed the fecal microbiome, microbial metagenome, and metabolome in patients with IBD and controls, and across >8000 measured metabolite features, identifying chemicals and chemical classes that were differentially abundant in IBD. These findings identified perturbations of the gut microbiome–metabolome interface in IBD, including many potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets. However, the unique influence of nutrient-associated factors that may be derived from the diet was not assessed.

In previous work, we developed a novel bioinformatics workflow combining Visualization Of LineAr Regression Elements (VOLARE) and Consolidated Analysis of Network Topology And Regression Elements (CANTARE) that utilizes regression techniques and network topology to build families of multiomic predictive models. CANTARE is a bioinformatics tool that builds interpretable and parsimonious multiomic predictive models with comparable overall performance to random forests and elastic-net penalized regressions. This workflow has several strengths for hypothesis generation, including individual-level visualizations, supporting binary outcomes, and providing quantitative and directional results [17,18]. We used the data published by Franzosa et al. [16] to predict a binary outcome, person with IBD, or healthy control. In the present study, our objective was to predict IBD status using nutrient-associated constituents. We hypothesized that this dataset could be used to generate novel and interpretable multiomic predictive models that would support better understanding of the role nutrition plays in IBD pathogenesis and contribute to the development of a precision nutrition approach for IBD management. The models we describe herein support hypothesis generation for follow-up prospective investigations designed to further evaluate the relationship between diet and IBD prevention and management.

Methods

Analytical workflow

We used a published dataset of healthy controls and patients with IBD described in detail in Franzosa et al. [16] The VOLARE-CANTARE workflow utilizes multiomic datasets to build interactive networks that can be used to develop predictive models, as previously described [18]. In brief, VOLARE generates pairwise regressions from multiomic data and summarizes the resulting “top table” in a network [17]. CANTARE is then used to identify network neighborhoods of interest and build predictive models using standard regression techniques [18].

Regression network, predictive models, and cumulative fit plots

Using a published network [18], 3 subnetworks were chosen based on their size (i.e., having 4 or more nodes) to build predictive models (Figure 1). We used the nutrient-associated predictors in each of 3 subnetworks to fit 1 logistic regression model per subnetwork, characterizing study participants as having IBD or being a healthy control. We used backward selection with Akaike information criterion (R method glm with family = binomial) to generate a reduced model. We included fecal calprotectin (FCAL) as a predictor in each of our models and alone as a positive control. Model performance was measured by AUC scores and pseudo-R2. We refit these models using the R method lrm from the rms package to access specialized diagnostics in the rms package [19]. Using previously described methods [18], we generated cumulative fit plots to visualize probabilities, with 1 path/person in the dataset. Effect sizes for each predictor were expressed as the odds ratio (OR) for IBD associated with the IQR of the predictor. Thus, the IQR effect size is the change in OR associated with a change in a predictor from the first quartile (Q1, 25th percentile) to the third quartile (Q3, 75th percentile), which includes 50% of the data values. We derived permutation P values by permuting the class variable 2500 times, building full and reduced models for the permuted data, and recording the pseudo-R2 of these reduced models [19]. Since not all permuted models converged, we sampled 1000 values from the permuted pseudo-R2 values. We then calculated our P values as the fraction of times pseudo-R2 of the reduced permuted model was greater than that of the observed data.

Each node in the network represents a predictor (e.g., a microbe, metabolite, or enzyme), and each edge represents a relationship between 2 predictors. Nodes are identified by e (enzyme), mb (microbe), or met (metabolite).

Additional analyses compared: 1) models for IBD compared with healthy controls, UC compared with healthy controls, CD compared with healthy controls; and 2) models for IBD compared with healthy controls with and without age as a covariate.

Results

Nutrient-associated network nodes

Previously, we used a published data set of microbiome relative abundance (mb), untargeted metabolomics (met), and microbial-derived gene expression (e) in stool samples from 153 adults (IBD = 111, healthy control = 42) to build a network of cross-omic relationships that differed by IBD status [16,18]. Using network neighborhoods centered on microbes, we identified 20 models with AUC values > 0.8. The 3 best models included 43 omic predictors [18]. Here, we revisit this network to identify nutrient-associated predictive models of IBD. First, we searched the biomedical literature for evidence of diet associations with these 43 predictors, identifying 20 associated with diet. Each of these 20 predictors was located in 1 of 3 subnetworks. Next, we fit a regression model using the nutrient-associated predictors in each of the 3 subnetworks. Figure 1 illustrates the multiomic network across 3 omes: mb, met, and e. Twenty nutrient-associated nodes are shown. The 3 subnetworks containing these nodes are identified by shaded regions. From smallest to largest, these subnetworks include 2, 5, and 13 nutrient-associated nodes, of 4, 26, and 40 total nodes, respectively.

Predictive models

Using the nutrient-associated nodes in each of the subnetworks, together with FCAL (mean = 130 μg/g), a reliable marker of intestinal inflammation with good clinical sensitivity for IBD [20], we created 3 regression models. We call these models M1, M2, and M3, in order of increasing subnetwork size. These models contained 3, 4, and 11 predictors, respectively. AUC scores were 0.87 for M1, 0.90 for M2, and 0.95 for M3. Pseudo-R2 values, which are a form of the R2 appropriate for logistic regression, were 0.42 for M1, 0.55 for M2, and 0.71 for M3, each with permutation P values < 0.001.

M1 is illustrated in Figure 2. In this model, Ruminococcus lactaris abundance is protective, with an IQR OR of 0.76. Both histamine and FCAL are associated with increased risk, with IQR OR of 2.0 and 8.72, respectively.

Positive control model of FCAL

To serve as a positive control and a comparison to our 3 identified predictive models, we evaluated the ability of FCAL alone to predict IBD status. The AUC for this model was 0.80, indicating poorer performance compared with models with a greater number of predictors from our VOLARE subnetwork analysis (Supplemental Figure 1).

Nutrient-associated biomarkers uncorrelated with FCAL

Figure 5 illustrates the relationships between FCAL and histamine (A) and FCAL and DHA (B). Inspection of underlying data identified 13 individuals (12 with IBD) with high histamine (> 90th percentile) but FCAL level of < 250 μ/g. Similarly, 11 individuals (10 with IBD) had high DHA but FCAL < 250 μ/g. Only 2 individuals (both with IBD) had both high histamine and high DHA.

Stratification by IDB diagnosis (UC compared with CD)

The stratified models for CD (n = 65 CD and 42 control) and UC (n = 46 UC and 42 control) in Supplemental Figure 2 show that the performance of all models was similar, with similar sets of predictors.

Exploring age as a covariate

Mean age of study participants was 36.5 y for controls, 44.3 y for those with CD, and 46.4 y for those with UC. After recreating the models with age as an optional covariate, we see that age was retained as a factor in all 3 models (Supplemental Figure 3). The performance of all models was similar, with the greatest improvement in model 2. The metabolite choline was not retained in the version of model 3 that included age (Supplemental Figure 3).

Discussion

Leveraging our analytical frameworks, VOLARE and CANTARE, we utilized an integrated multiomics dataset to generate predictive models linking nutrient-related metabolites to IBD. Though significant progress has been made in our understanding of IBD pathogenesis, translating this knowledge into personalized clinical approaches for disease management has not yet been achieved. Our results suggest that CANTARE provides an unbiased and comprehensive strategy that can integrate multiple omes to identify potential modifiable dietary factors associated with IBD. We identified several nutrient-related metabolites, such as ascorbate, pyridoxamine, choline, and N-acetylglutamate, as well as microbes, R. lactaris, E. hallii, and Subdoligranulum, to be protective factors against IBD. Other small molecules, including azelaic acid, histamine, pyruvate kinase, and DHA, were associated with an increased risk of IBD. Our findings support the investigation of hypothesis-driven nutritional interventions in IBD management.

Two out of the 3 gut microbes identified in our models, E. hallii and Subdoligranulum are well described in the literature as abundant members of a healthy gut microbiome that are often found in decreased abundance in IBD [21,22]. A European multicenter study found that the fluorescent signals for E. hallii were lower in IBD compared with symptomatic controls (P < 0.05) [22]. Both E. hallii and Subdoligranulum are crucial for the production of SCFAs, including butyrate, which is produced through the butyryl-CoA: acetate CoA-transferase pathway [[23], [24], [25]]. These butyrate-producing microbes are important for maintaining an anaerobic gut environment, regulating the immunological aspect of the gut barrier, and tightening the intestinal epithelial cell barrier [25]. Although studies investigating the role of R. lactaris in establishing a healthy gut microbiome are limited, the Ruminococcaceae family also plays an important role in transforming resistant starches into SCFAs [26]. A study comparing patients with ischemic colitis to healthy controls observed a 60.8-fold reduced abundance of R. lactaris in patients with ischemic colitis [26]. In a pilot intervention involving healthy adult men ingesting 0.3 g/kg/d of flaxseeds for 1 wk, a transient increase in the relative abundance of R. lactaris was observed [27]. Prebiotics, such as a polyphenol-rich diet and fiber-enriched yogurt, have also been shown to enhance butyrate-producing bacteria, including Eubacterium and members of the Ruminococcaceae family [25]. Baseline community alpha diversity has been observed to be higher in patients with CD initiating anti-integrin therapy (vedolizumab) who achieved remission at week 14. This microbial composition also persisted among this group at 1 y [14]. Other dietary interventions that promote a healthy microbial composition, combined with existing treatments for IBD, warrant further investigation. The microbes identified in our analysis may be targets for modifying the IBD microbiome toward a healthier composition through the incorporation of specific nutrients that could be consumed in the diet.

The small molecules ascorbate (vitamin C), pyridoxamine (vitamin B6), and choline, which are found in various fruits, vegetables, and grains, have been implicated in decreasing inflammation in IBD in preclinical animal models and patient-derived human samples [[28], [29], [30]]. In a clinical study of 96 healthy volunteers, high-dose delivery of vitamin C to the large intestine compared with a placebo has been associated with an increase in microbial alpha diversity and fecal SCFAs [31]. In animal studies, moderate vitamin B6 supplementation attenuated colonic inflammation in an IBD mouse model [28]. In another study, insufficient dietary choline exacerbated the severity of disease in a Citrobacter rodentium-induced colitis mouse model [30]. Glutamate, an amino acid involved in gut protein metabolism, was protective in our analysis [32]. The role of glutamine, a precursor of glutamate, in intestinal dysfunction is well established due to its involvement in enterocyte proliferation, regulation of tight-junction proteins, and suppression of inflammation [33]. Therefore, these metabolites, which were also identified in our analysis, may serve as potential dietary modifiers that can be targeted through medical nutrition therapy to promote intakes of foods rich in these micronutrients in an effort to manage IBD and prevent disease exacerbation.

IBD is a heterogeneous condition influenced by variations in an individual’s physiology, genes, and environment [34,35]. Individual genetic variations play a role in nutrient metabolism and transport, which may impact the nutrient-related treatment of patients with IBD [36]. Several host genes have been found to contribute to dysbiosis in the gut such as the overexpression of genes encoding proteins involved in postprandial gastrointestinal motility and antibacterial defenses [37]. Additionally, genetic variations may impact absorption of nutrients in individuals with IBD. Some of the observed variations in nutrient-related metabolites may be due to genetic polymorphisms such as variants in vitamin C transporter genes SLC23A1 and SLC23A2 [38]. These polymorphisms may play a role in vitamin C levels among patients with IBD. An increased risk of CD was linked to a genetic variant (rs10063949-G) in the SLC23A1 vitamin C transporter gene [39]. Understanding the genetic influences on the detected metabolic changes in individuals with IBD can be used to inform the design and investigation of individualized nutritional therapies. Our work emphasizes the need for future studies to explore the interplay between dietary intake and additional “omes” such as the genome and proteome in relation to the gut microbiome in IBD. The integration of host genome and diet could inform effective development of personalized nutrition and nutrigenomics-related dietary interventions and guidelines.

In clinical practice, FCAL is a highly sensitive biomarker that is routinely used as a marker of endoscopically active IBD [40]. However, FCAL has some limitations, as within the IBD classification, it does not help differentiate between CD and UC [41]. FCAL is also not specific to IBD, and increases are observed in other gastrointestinal disorders, including colorectal carcinoma and diverticulitis [41]. Thus, exploring alternate biomarkers may be helpful in identifying active IBD. Furthermore, it may also prove useful to conduct additional investigation to determine whether these biomarkers can differentiate between types of IBD. The literature indicates that FCAL levels between 100 and 250 μg/g are highly suggestive of IBD in the appropriate clinical context [42]. Additionally, FCAL between 100 and 250 μg/g is considered a “gray zone” in identifying endoscopic inflammation in IBD [42]. Thus, based on previous literature, we chose FCAL < 250 μg/g as a cutoff [42,43]. In our analysis, histamine and DHA were predictive of an increased risk of IBD; however it was observed that patients with multiple IBD with high histamine and/or DHA levels have FCAL lower than 250 μg/g. Histamine, a common proinflammatory mediator abundant in a variety of foods such as fish products, processed meat, and dairy products, has been observed to be elevated in both CD and UC [44,45]. However, some histamine-rich foods, such as tomatoes, spinach, and fermented foods, are included in dietary treatment for IBD (e.g., Mediterranean diet) [46]; therefore, without dietary data, it is unclear what quality or type of histamine is present or responsible for the observed FCAL. DHA is well established as a protective metabolite with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capacities [47,48]. Among patients with IBD who have high DHA but FCAL under 250 μg/g, the elevated DHA could be reflective of dietary changes, such as adherence to a Mediterranean diet rich in DHA for its anti-inflammatory properties. In this context, the correlation may not indicate disease activity but rather a phenotype influenced by dietary habits. These 2 metabolites require further examination as potential alternative clinical biomarkers for IBD, identifying some patients with a particular subtype of IBD for whom FCAL values are positively affected with certain type and quality of nutrients.

Despite its strengths, this study is not without limitations. Although nutrient-associated metabolites measured in stool were selected as predictors based on published literature, dietary intake data were not available, and thus it was not possible to evaluate the reported intake of specific nutrients, foods, or food groups in this dataset. The lack of stratification by traditional diagnostic categories (UC compared with CD) is a potential limitation of our main analysis. However, based on current literature showing that IBD is a continuum with overlapping presentation of the disease between UC and CD [49,50], we chose to consider IBD as single unstratified outcome. Supplemental Figure 2 shows that IBD as a whole captured predictors for both UC and CD as most significant predictors were the same across primary and stratified analyses. Finally, although we investigated age as a potential covariate in our supplemental analysis, we were limited in examining other potential confounders due to the utilization of existing data.

Our approach has several advantages. The VOLARE-CANTARE workflow provides a way to assess and visualize high-dimensional data when building predictive models and has been applied to “omic” data sets that include diet intake data [51]. This approach may support the development of precision nutrition approaches for certain subgroups of individuals. Such a systems biology perspective will also help with the prediction and interception of the disease, as well as promote personalized treatment for patients with IBD. This comprehensive approach to identifying potential nutritionally relevant modulating factors in IBD development and progression may provide the basis for designing improved dietary strategies for the treatment of patients with IBD as well as discovering clinical biomarkers that can characterize IBD pathogenesis in a subtype-specific manner.

IBD is a complex disease with heterogeneous clinical outcomes. The predictive models presented herein include features associated with reduced and increased odds of IBD. As such, these models may provide valuable insights into identifying noninvasive clinical biomarkers and targets for therapeutic interventions for patients with IBD. These features may also aid in characterizing IBD pathogenesis, etiology, and diagnosis. Although our discussions of biological relevance are speculative, our findings offer some insights into potential nutritional targets for controlled interventions and may identify novel predictors that may provide a foundation for testable hypotheses in future studies.
