Glioblastoma exploits ATP from leading-edge astrocytes to fuel its infiltrative growth revealed by spatially resolved chimeric analysis
Authors: Gaoxia Yang, Xiaodong Niu, Jieying Gan, Tianping Yu, Menghan Li, Peng Xiao, Rui Zhang, Ping He, Yuan Yang, Yi Cui, Xiaoyi Huang, Yuan Wang, Qing Mao, Ran Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) is the deadliest primary brain tumor that frequently infiltrates surrounding brain tissue, causing therapy resistance and recurrence. The molecular characteristics of infiltrating GBM cells and their interactions with brain cells remain poorly understood, partly due to limited spatial tools for distinguishing tumor cells from those in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Here, we introduce Spatially-resolved Chimeric AnalyzeR (SCAR), a computational tool for dissecting tumor-TME gene expression in spatial transcriptomics of human-mouse chimeric cancer models. SCAR reveals spatially distinct characteristics of GBM and their interactions with TME, identifying that infiltrative GBM up-regulates creatine kinase brain type (CKB) at the astrocyte-enriched leading edge compared to the tumor core. Mechanistically, GBM-secreted CKB catalyzes the extracellular conversion of astrocyte-supplied adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and creatine into phosphocreatine, providing metabolic support for infiltrative tumor growth, which can be blocked by cyclocreatine. These results provide proof-of-concept validation of SCAR and demonstrate spatial context–dependent metabolic rewiring of GBM cells, with implications for therapies targeting infiltrative GBM.

INTRODUCTION

GBM is the most common primary brain tumor (1, 2). Although it rarely metastasizes outside the brain (3), GBM cells frequently infiltrate the brain parenchyma (4). These infiltrative cells can adapt and evolve under the selective pressures of standard therapies, resulting in recurrence even after the bulk of the tumor is surgically removed. Thus, understanding the molecular characteristics of these cells and their cross-talk with surrounding brain cells to drive infiltrative growth is crucial for developing more effective targeted therapies.

The investigation of infiltrative tumor cells conventionally relied on low-throughput methods such as immunohistochemistry (5, 6). Combining bulk transcriptomics, in situ hybridization, and histology, the Ivy Glioblastoma Atlas offer a high-throughput characterization of GBM across anatomical regions, including the leading edge and infiltrating areas (7). However, because of the scarcity of tumor cells in these regions, the gene expression profiles often reflect the dominant tumor microenvironment (TME) cells rather than the infiltrative tumor cells. Region-specific sampling combined with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) uncovered the profiles of infiltrative tumor cells (8, 9). However, tissue dissociation disrupts spatial interactions, limiting the understanding of the cellular context within the TME. Recently, cutting-edge technologies like spatially resolved transcriptomics (10–12), proteomics (13), and metabolics (14) have enabled the identification of spatial interactions between tumor and TME cells. Despite these advances, these methods are often restricted by trade-offs between throughput and cellular resolution, the latter of which often results in data representing mixed cell types and states, requiring computational inference (15, 16). Thus, the characterization of infiltrative tumor cells and their interactions with the TME components at the leading edge are restrained by unique technical challenges.

In this study, we aim to address these challenges by combining spatially resolved profiling of human-mouse chimeric GBM models with an improved computational workflow that enables highly specific and sensitive differentiation of tumor and microenvironmental profiles. This computational tool, Spatially-resolved Chimeric AnalyzeR (SCAR), allows us to characterize the infiltrative GBM cells in a spatial context with enhanced resolution. As a proof of concept, we show that CKB is up-regulated by GBM cells at the leading edge, which is then secreted into the extracellular space to exploit astrocyte-supplied adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and creatine (Cr), promoting the infiltrative growth of GBM.

RESULTS

SCAR accurately differentiates malignant from nonmalignant cells in chimeric tumor models

To explore the spatial heterogeneity of malignant and nonmalignant cells in vivo, we performed spatial transcriptomic sequencing with 10X Visium on two types of GBM models: a typical glioma stem cell (GSC) xenograft model (17) (table S1) and our previously established TP53/NF1/PTEN triple knockout (TNP) human neural stem cell (hNSC) xenograft glioma model (18) (Fig. 1A). We acquired data from both the xenograft side and the contralateral side of coronal brain sections, and obtained an average of 2485 spots per sample and 2259 to 6491 median number of genes (4794 to 38,503 mean reads) per spot after quality control (table S2).

These chimeric models offer a unique advantage to distinguish human malignant cells from murine nonmalignant cells on the basis of their species of origin. Because 10X Visium lacks single-cell resolution (on average ~10 cells per spot), it is necessary to distinguish mouse and human gene expression at the individual transcript level. To evaluate the performance of different strategies to distinguish species-specific transcripts, we generated test datasets by our previously reported 3′-biased simulation tool (19) (fig. S1A), along with randomly mixed real human and murine scRNA-seq datasets. The simulation dataset faithfully mirrored actual data distribution with a 3′-bias (fig. S1, B and C). We found that standard gene expression quantification in Space Ranger using a combined mouse/human genome reference resulted in lower accuracy compared to the ground truth (fig. S1, D and E). To overcome this, we refined the quantification pipelines (detailed in Materials and Methods) and incorporated them into an R package named SCAR, which exhibits improved performance (fig. S1, F and G). Additional simulations with varying proportions of mouse versus human cells were conducted to assess the root mean square error (RMSE), and SCAR consistently exhibits superior performance over the standard method on all occasions (fig. S1, H and I).

We then used SCAR to assign individual transcripts to malignant (human) and nonmalignant (mouse) cells, revealing the spatial distribution of tumor cells across brain regions. We observed a strong correlation between the predicted tumor content (measured by the abundance of human reads) and tumor histology in hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)–stained sections (Fig. 1, B and C). Using a clustering-based approach and the expression of mouse genes, we could assign each spot into brain regions such as cortex (CTX), white matter (WM), subventricular zone (SVZ), lateral septum (LSX), hypothalamus (HY), and dorsal/ventral striatum (STRd/STRv) (Fig. 1D and fig. S2, A to C). We then performed deconvolution analysis to determine the composition of murine cells across anatomical regions, using our in-house single-nucleus RNA-seq of the mouse brain as a reference (fig. S2D), and identified region-specific enrichment of different cell types. As expected, excitatory neurons, oligodendrocytes, and inhibitory neurons are enriched in the normal brain regions CTX, WM, and STR, respectively (figs. S2E, S3, and S4).

To investigate the spatial heterogeneity of tumor cells, we performed unsupervised clustering and dimensionality reduction on the basis of tumor gene expression. This analysis identified five distinct clusters from the GSC model, which, on the basis of their spatial distribution and tumor content (fig. S5A), were classified into cellular tumor (cluster 0, CT), a mix of cellular tumor and leading edge (clusters 3 and 4, CT/LE), and leading edge–infiltrative zone (clusters 1 and 2, LE/IZ) (Fig. 1E). Similarly, tumor spots from the TNP model were classified into cellular tumor and LE/IZ (Fig. 1F and fig. S5A). Across these regions, we identified astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and neurons in the TME (fig. S5, B and C). Notably, immune cells such as microglia and endothelial cells were enriched in the cellular tumor in both glioma models (Fig. 1G and fig. S5, B and C), reflecting immune cell infiltration and angiogenesis consistent with a previous report (20). To investigate the molecular characters of infiltrative tumor cells, we next performed differential expression analysis on tumor spots at the CT/LE and LE/IZ versus CT (data S1 and S2). Tumor cells at the leading edge up-regulate known pathways associated with infiltrative growth and inflammatory response such as RNA splicing, focal adhesion, and NF-κB (nuclear factor κB) signaling (Fig. 1H). Axon development and synaptic plasticity were markedly enriched as observed in TNP model (Fig. 1I and data S5), highlighting the critical role of glioma-neuronal interactions in GBM infiltrative growth. Notably, oxidative phosphorylation and ATP metabolism and associated genes were notably enriched (Fig. 1H and data S3 and S4), indicating a high energy demand at the leading edge. Together, these findings demonstrate that SCAR effectively uncovers the distinct molecular and cellular characteristics of tumor cells in a spatial context.

Cellular communication plays a critical role in the tumor ecosystem (21), and SCAR could precisely predict tumor-TME interactions across spatial domains. By incorporating ligand-receptor pairs as input, SCAR calculates potential interaction signals between tumor cells (human) and TME cells (mouse). SCAR identified significantly higher interaction frequencies at the leading edge in both tumor model (Fig. 1J), highlighting frequent interactions between tumor and TME cells as potential drivers of infiltrative growth. As an example, the PPIA-BSG ligand-receptor pair, which has been shown to promote tumor invasive growth (22), exhibit region-specific expression patterns at the leading edge, with TME cells expressing Ppia and tumor cells expressing BSG (fig. S5D). These findings demonstrate that SCAR accurately captures the spatial dynamics of tumor-microenvironment interactions in GBM.

Region-specific up-regulation of CKB at the GBM LE/IZ

To elucidate the infiltrative growth mechanism of GBM cells, we incorporated GSC model and TNP model to identify conserved genes that specifically up-regulated at the LE/IZ. Notably, creatine kinase brain type (CKB), a key enzyme involved in ATP energy metabolism (23), displayed significant up-regulation at the LE/IZ in both the GSC and TNP (Fig. 2A and fig. S5E). Coincidentally, CKB expression was markedly elevated at the LE in the Ivy datasets (Fig. 2B). Whereas CKB expression was not significantly elevated in IT within the Ivy datasets due to the low resolution of bulk RNA-seq, SCAR precisely detected high CKB expression in tumor cells at LE/IZ (Fig. 2C). This highlights the robustness and advantages of our computational tool in accurately characterizing infiltrative GBM cells within a spatial context at enhanced resolution.

Consistent with these observations, immunofluorescence (IF) analysis confirmed enhanced CKB expression at the LE/IT in two GSC models (GSC1 and GSC2) and TNP model (Fig. 2D and fig. S5F). Similarly, GBM cells infiltrated into CC and contralateral CTX regions also exhibited higher CKB expression compared to the cellular tumor in TNP (Fig. 2E). We further analyzed published spatial transcriptomic datasets for GBM by Ravi et al. (10) and found elevated CKB expression at the LE/IT in six of eight samples with LE/IT annotations (Fig. 2F and fig. S6, A to C; P ≤ 0.015, hypergeometric test). Consistently, CKB also exhibited elevated expression at the LE/IT in GBM surgical specimens (Fig. 2G). In addition, across models and human GBM datasets, CKB expression at the LE/IT is consistently higher than in necrotic regions (Fig. 2, D and F). These data suggests that CKB may have a specific role in promoting tumor infiltrative growth at the LE/IT.

CKB is necessary for the infiltrative growth of GBM cells

To determine the function of CKB in GBM cells, we knocked down CKB in GSC1 and GSC2 using two nonoverlapping short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) and confirmed the knockdown (KD) efficiency by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and Western blot (Fig. 3, A and B). CKB KD in both GSCs markedly inhibited cell growth and tumor sphere formation (Fig. 3, C and D, and fig. S7, A to C).

To investigate the role of CKB in vivo, control and CKB KD GSCs were transplanted into immunodeficient mice to initiate tumors. For both GSC models, CKB KD reduced the size of tumors and extended survival compared to controls (Fig. 3, E and F). Subsequently, a significant reduction in invasive tumor cells at the LE/IT in both GSC1 and GSC2 model suggests that CKB may drive the infiltrative growth of GBM cells (Fig. 3, G and H, and fig. S7, D and E). Moreover, CKB KD reduced corpus callosum invasion following irradiation (fig. S8A) and significantly impaired irradiation-induced tumor migration (Fig. 3, I and J), leading to attenuated tumor growth (fig. S8, B and C) and further supporting the role of CKB in driving tumor cell infiltration in vivo. Collectively, these findings highlight an important role for CKB in promoting not only the proliferation and stemness of GSCs in vitro but also their infiltrative growth in vivo.

Astrocytes supply Cr and ATP to GBM cells at the LE/IT facilitating infiltrative growth

CKB catalyzes the reversible conversion of Cr to phosphocreatine (PCr), a reaction for ATP buffering and shuttling (24). However, KD of CKB in GSCs led to a decrease in both intracellular ATP and PCr (Fig. 4, A and B), suggesting additional defects in synthesis or intake of Cr/PCr by GSCs. Cr biosynthesis involves two enzymes, GATM and GAMT, with GATM serving as the key enzyme that catalyzes the rate-limiting step in the synthesis process (Fig. 4C). Overall, GBM cells express low levels of GATM across anatomic regions, suggesting that they have limited capacity to synthesize Cr (Fig. 4D). We also did not observe increased expression of the Cr transporter SLC6A8 at the LE/IT (fig. S9A). A previous study reported that CKB can be secreted to use extracellular ATP and Cr for PCr production, supporting energy demands during metastasis (25). Because ATP and Cr can be released into the extracellular space in the brain (26, 27), we hypothesized that GBM cells at the LE/IT may adopt a similar mechanism to promote their infiltrative growth. To test this, we first confirmed that CKB can be detected in a GSC-conditioned medium, using CKB KD groups as controls (Fig. 4E). To ascertain whether GSCs could use extracellular ATP, we engineered a membrane-bound luciferase sensor of extracellular ATP (28) into GSCs (pME-Luc-CTR-GSCs) (Fig. 4F). Addition of ATP drastically increased the levels of bioluminescence in pME-Luc-CTR-GSCs, confirming that this system could measure extracellular ATP levels (Fig. 4G). We then transplanted pME-Luc-CTR-GSCs and pME-Luc-CKB KD-GSCs into immunocompromised mice, and bioluminescence imaging showed increased extracellular ATP levels in the CKB KD compared to control (Fig. 4H), whereas tumor size remained comparable between the groups (Fig. 4I). Thus, the elevated ATP signal reflects impaired ATP utilization by GBM cells caused by CKB silencing, rather than differences in tumor cell abundance.

Cells often rely on the PCr shuttle to use extracellular ATP (29). Tumor cells at the LE/IT did not show up-regulation of Cr synthesis (Fig. 4D) but robustly expressed the Cr transporter SLC6A8 on the cellular membrane (fig. S9, B and C), whereas the reduction in ATP levels caused by CKB depletion could be restored by PCr supplementation (Fig. 5A). Thus, we first investigated whether TME cells exhibit increased expression of Cr biosynthesis genes. Gatm displayed high expression in areas surrounding the tumor core (Fig. 5B). To determine which TME cell type is the main supplier of Cr and ATP at the LE/IT, we reanalyzed public GBM scRNA-seq and single-nucleus RNA-seq datasets (GBmap) (30), and found high GATM expression in tumor-associated astrocytes and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs), whereas neurons demonstrated elevated expression of GAMT (Fig. 5C). Cell deconvolution and examination of astrocyte markers such as Gfap revealed an increased presence of astrocyte cells at the LE/IT (Fig. 5D and figs. S6D and S10A), whereas the content of OPCs was relatively lower than astrocytes. Consistently, the conditioned medium from cultured astrocytes contains high levels of Cr, in contrast to minimal levels in those from neurons, which were abundant in the TME and expressed GAMT (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, we cultured astrocytes in 13C-labeled flux media and analyzed the metabolic profile of the supernatants, comparing astrocyte-conditioned media to flux media (Fig. 5F). We observed a decrease in M+6-labeled arginine levels in the presence of astrocytes, accompanied by a significant increase in M+1-labeled Cr (Fig. 5G). These findings support the finding that astrocytes can consume extracellular arginine, converting it into Cr and subsequently secreting it into the extracellular space.

Furthermore, we asked whether Cr supplement would promote tumor growth or migration. Cr supplementation is insufficient to enhance either phenotype (fig. S10, B and C). These results are consistent with a previous study that Cr supplementation does not enhance tumor cell viability under normoxic conditions (31), suggesting that Cr alone is insufficient to promote infiltrative growth of GBM cells.

Astrocytes are known to release ATP in response to brain injury (32, 33). Because infiltrating GBM cells induce injury-like responses, we hypothesize that astrocytes at the LE/IT may also supply ATP alongside Cr to support GBM growth. To validate this, we compared ATP release from astrocytes and neurons using mechanical stimulation with glass beads to mimic injury responses. Although ATP levels were initially not significantly different between a conditioned medium from astrocytes and neurons (Fig. 6A), there is a marked increase in ATP release from astrocytes following glass bead stimulation (34). After coculturing with stimulated astrocytes (Fig. 6B), control GSCs but not CKB KD GSCs exhibited significantly increased ATP levels, sphere sizes, and migration, suggesting that GSCs may rely on CKB to use extracellular ATP and Cr from astrocytes to fuel their growth and motility (Fig. 6, C to G, and fig. S11A). GATM KD astrocytes failed to increase the ATP levels in cocultured GSCs compared to controls (Fig. 6H and fig. S11, B and C). Collectively, these findings provide direct proof that astrocyte-derived Cr and ATP contribute to the metabolic support of GSCs.

To assess the therapeutic effects of targeting the PCr shuttle in GBM, we treated GSC-bearing mice with cyclocreatine (cCr) (Fig. 6I), a Cr analog and an efficient substrate for CKB, yet its phosphorylate form is a poor phosphate/ATP donor compared with PCr. Four-week treatment with cCr significantly reduced tumor size and restricted the infiltrative growth in both GSC1 and GSC2 models (Fig. 6J and fig. S11, D to F). Furthermore, we reperformed the cCr treatment experiment on a larger cohort, monitored tumor growth through live imaging (Fig. 6, K and L), and obtained survival data (Fig. 6M), supporting the tumor-inhibiting effects of cCr treatment. Thus, targeting the PCr shuttle with cCr could potentially restrict the infiltrative growth of GBM at the leading edge and prolong the survival of mice bearing GSCs.

DISCUSSION

Accurately dissecting tumor cells and environmental cells interaction at the leading edge of GBM is challenging due to the low cell counts at the LE/IT and the limitations of current technological resolution (35). However, understanding these interactions is crucial for developing previously unknown therapies to improve prognosis. In this study, we leveraged the chimeric model combined with spatial transcriptomic profiles and an advanced computational workflow to characterize and target GBM and TME cells interactions at the leading edge to inhibit the infiltrative growth of GBM cells.

Our computational tool SCAR outperforms standard workflow to more precisely dissect human/mouse-specific transcripts and could be widely useful for a variety of commonly used xenograft models in tumor types beyond GBM. Our study showcases its utility in determining region-specific tumor-TME interactions and identifying tumor dependencies in a spatial context. Notably, a limitation of commonly used xenograft tumor models is that tumor growth occurs in an immunocompromised microenvironment. Therefore, the impact of immune cells needs to be further investigated in humanized mouse models.

During the infiltrative growth of GBM, tumor cells must adapt to environment stress such as nutrient/energy deprivation at the leading edge and make use of alternative nutrient sources or metabolic pathways. In our GSC and TNP models as well as public datasets, GBM cells at the LE/IT exhibit region-specific up-regulation of CKB, suggesting conserved reliance on CKB-regulated Cr pathways in this spatial domain. The GBM-promoting effects of Cr have been highlighted by two recent studies. Rashidi et al. show that, in the necrotic region, tumor-associated myeloid cells up-regulate Cr biosynthesis, whereas GBM cells up-regulate the transporter SLC6A8 to promote Cr import under hypoxia stress (31). However, at the leading edge, the expression of SLC6A8 in GBM cells is not significantly increased and myeloid cells are not the major TME cell types, indicating alternative mechanisms. Moreover, CKB expression is consistently higher in the LE/IT than the perinecrotic regions across in-house and public datasets (Fig. 2, D and F, and fig. S12), supporting region-specific roles of CKB that may not be primarily driven by hypoxia. Another study shows that GSCs, compared to more differentiated glioma cells, up-regulate CKB to overproduce PCr for epigenetic reprogramming (36). However, the source of Cr and ATP at the leading edge remains undetermined.

Through in vitro and in vivo experiments, our study demonstrates that leading-edge astrocytes are the main source of both Cr and ATP, which can be converted by glioma-secreted extracellular CKB into PCr, and consumed by glioma cells to promote their infiltrative growth (fig. S11G). This mechanism of action differs from previous reports and offers valuable insights into the role of Cr and microenvironmental astrocytes on glioma growth in a specific spatial context, with implications for targeted therapy against infiltrative GBM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines and cell culture

hNSCs with introduced mutations in TP53, NF1, and PTEN are referred to as TNP cells. The TNP cells were expanded and maintained in the hNSC maintenance medium containing 1×N2 supplement, 1×B27 supplement (Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; 20 ng/ml). These TNP cells were constructed in our laboratory. Patient-derived GSCs (GSC1 and GSC2) were kindly provided by X. Qi at West Lake University, with the corresponding clinical information listed in table S1. GSCs were cultured in a stem cell medium containing B27 supplement (Gibco, #17504-044), human epidermal growth factor recombinant protein (10 ng/ml; Gibco, #PHG0311), and human bFGF recombinant protein (Gibco, #13256029). The 293T cells were purchased from the Cell Resource Center of the Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). The human astrocyte cell line (NHA) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection cell bank and cultured in medium consisting of 90% DMEM, 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were maintained in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2.

Animals

Female NCG mice, 6 weeks of age, were purchased from Beijing Vital River GemPharmatech Laboratory Animal Technology (Jiangsu, China). The mice were housed in individually ventilated cages (PIV/IVC) within SPF (specific pathogen–free)–grade breeding rooms at the Experimental Animal Center of the State Key Laboratory of Biotherapy, Sichuan University. They were maintained in a conventional barrier facility with a 14-hour light/10-hour dark cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan University (20230227023).

Vectors and shRNAs

Relevant literature was reviewed to collect available shRNA sequences, and higher-rated shRNAs targeting the coding sequence (CDS) region were selected from the Sigma website (https://sigmaaldrich.cn/CN/zh). These shRNA sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and subsequently cloned into the PLKO.1-GFP vector. Lentiviruses were produced in 293T cells using a calcium phosphate precipitation packaging system, which included scrambled sequence (shScr), shCKB#1, shCKB#2, shCKB#3, shCKB#4, shScr-pME-luc, and shCKB#4-pME-luc. The harvested lentivirus was further concentrated (10×) using the Lenti-X Concentration Kit (Takara) to remove excess FBS. GSCs were plated in 24-well plates and infected with lentiviruses for 12 hours. Following infection, the cells were cultured in a fresh medium for 48 hours and then selected in a medium containing puromycin (1 μg/ml). The GSCs were passaged three times, and the targeting efficiency of the shRNAs was evaluated. Last, two of the most effective shRNAs, validated by qPCR and Western blot analyses, were selected for subsequent gene editing experiments. The detailed sequence information used for shRNA design and qPCR experiments is provided in table S3.

Orthotopic xenograft mouse models

Briefly, GSCs were transduced with various plasmids as described in this study using lentiviral infection. Forty-eight hours posttransduction, cells were selected in culture with puromycin (1 μg/ml) for an additional 48 hours. The different infected cell groups—including shScr, shCKB#1, shCKB#4, shScr-pME-luc, shCKB#4-pME-luc, and TNP hNSCs—were dissociated using ACCUTASE Cell Detachment Solution. Single-cell suspensions were prepared in sterile Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS; Gibco) immediately before xenografting. A total of 5 × 104 cells in 4 μl of HBSS were stereotactically injected into the striatum of 6-week-old female NCG mice using a 10-μl microsyringe (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). Stereotactic coordinates used were 0.5 mm anterior to the bregma, 2.45 mm lateral to the midline, and 4.0 mm deep. After suturing the wound, the mice were transferred to clean cages and housed individually once they regained consciousness. Antibiotics were administered for 1 week postoperation, and the mice’s conditions were closely monitored. To investigate the role of CKB in GBM in vivo, CKB function was inhibited using the Cr analog cCr (Sigma-Aldrich, #377627). GSCs transfected with shScr were stereotactically injected into NCG mice to establish an orthotopic GBM model. One week after injection, the treatment group received 1% cCr in drinking water for 3 weeks. After the treatment period, tissue samples were collected to assess the impact of CKB inhibition on tumor progression.

Tissue preparation for histology

GBM tissues were obtained from patients at West China Hospital with informed consent and approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee. For histological analysis, frozen sections were prepared. Mice were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at various time points. The brains were dissected, cut into coronal slices, and processed for frozen sections. Brains were postfixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C and then transferred to 30% sucrose overnight at 4°C. The dehydrated brain tissue was embedded in O.C.T. compound (Tissue-Tek) and frozen on dry ice. For paraffin sections, continuous coronal sections were cut at 5 or 10 μm thickness using a cryostat.

Live imaging

We used a PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III instrument for all live imaging experiments, with the following parameters for image acquisition: open for total bioluminescence, exposure time of 60 s, binning set to medium (8), and f/stop at 1.2. Bioluminescence images were analyzed using Living Image 4.5 software.

GSC1 cells infected with either pME-Luc-CTR or pME-Luc-CKB KD were digested into a single-cell suspension and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After counting the cells in each group, 2 × 105 cells were transferred to a 96-well black plate, with three replicates per group. To each well, 8 μl of d-luciferin (15 mg/ml) sodium salt solution was added, followed by a 3-min incubation. Luminescence was measured using the PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III instrument. ATP was then added at concentrations ranging from 1 μM to 1 mM, and the wells were gently mixed immediately after ATP addition to ensure uniform distribution. Luminescence was measured again, and the data were statistically analyzed on the basis of luminescence values across wells.

For animal experiments, GSC1 cells infected with pME-Luc-CTR or pME-Luc-CKB KD were digested into a single-cell suspension, resuspended in HBSS, and counted. A total of 5 × 105 cells were injected into each mouse, following the previously described orthotopic xenograft mouse model procedure. After recovery, mice were administered an intraperitoneal injection of a luciferin (15 mg/ml) working solution. In vivo imaging analysis was conducted 10 to 20 min postinjection. Mice were continuously monitored for in vivo signals over a 15-day period, with imaging performed until signals were detected. Data were statistically analyzed on the basis of the observed signals.

Ionizing radiation treatment

Mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol and immobilized in a biological x-ray irradiator (Rad Source RS2000pro). Localized brain irradiation was delivered using a high-throughput small animal irradiation system with a defined field size of 3 mm by 3 mm. A single dose of 2-gray x-ray was administered per session, repeated every other day for a total of three sessions over 7 days. Following treatment, body weight was continuously monitored. On day 32 posttumor implantation, brain tissues were harvested as described in the Tissue Preparation section. 4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining (1 μg/ml) was performed, and whole-slide imaging was acquired using the Olympus SLIDEVIEW VS200 research-grade scanner to quantify the maximum tumor cross-sectional area.

Survival of mice treated with cCr

To investigate the in vivo role of CKB in GBM, CKB activity was inhibited using the Cr analog cCr (Sigma-Aldrich, #377627). An orthotopic GBM model was established by stereotactically injecting 5 × 104 shScr-Akaluc–transduced GSCs into the brains of NCG mice. One week postinjection, mice in the treatment group received 1% cCr in their drinking water for a duration of 3 weeks. Body weight was continuously monitored until the onset of disease symptoms. Tissue samples were collected, and overall survival was recorded to evaluate the impact of CKB inhibition on tumor progression.

Immunofluorescence

For IF staining on tissues, frozen brain sections were oven-dried at 42°C for 30 min, then rinsed, and rehydrated with PBS. The sections were treated with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min at room temperature (RT). Subsequently, sections were blocked with 2% goat serum in PBS for 1 hour at RT and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies were visualized using species-specific goat secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor dyes (Alexa 488/555/647, 1:1000, Invitrogen). Afterward, sections were stained with DAPI (1 μg/ml) for 5 min. The slides were then coverslipped and imaged using an Olympus BX51 fluorescent microscope. The antibodies used in this study included the following: CKB (1:100, Proteintech), GFAP (1:2000, Abcam), Ki67 (1:200, BD), MAP2 (1:250, Proteintech), Human Nuclear (1:250, Abcam), GATM (1:200, Proteintech), NeuN (1:250, Abcam), SLC6A8 (1:100, Proteintech), and HIF1A (1:200, Proteintech). To define the LE/IT, we labeled environmental cells such as astrocytes and neurons to identify the outer tumor boundary. From this boundary, we extended inward toward the tumor core until the density of tumor nuclei increased noticeably, representing the zone where tumor cells begin to infiltrate the surrounding tissue.

Construction of the plasmid for extracellular ATP sensing

The plasmid sequence for sensing extracellular ATP is based on the pME-Luc plasmid sequence from the Paolo Pinton research group. Stable cell lines transfected with pME-Luc have been used to monitor extracellular ATP concentrations in various in vivo models. The pME-Luc sequence was fully synthesized by Sangon and subsequently cloned into the pLentiV2T-mCherry (V2TC) vector, which was constructed from the pLentiCRISPR V2 vector by replacing the Cas9 sequence with the reporter gene mCherry.

CCK-8

After preparing the single-cell suspension, different cells were evenly seeded into a 96-well plate, with six parallel wells for each condition. Five to six identical 96-well plates were prepared on the basis of the number of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) detection days. Twenty-four hours after seeding, one 96-well plate was treated by adding 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent to each well, followed by incubation at 37°C for 3 hours. For Cr supplementation experiments, 1 μM Cr was added to the treatment group 24 hours after seeding to initiate stimulation. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm value was measured using a microplate reader. This process was repeated every 24 hours with a different 96-well plate, and the data were collected and analyzed.

Sphere formation and Transwell assay

After preparing the single-cell suspension, different cells were evenly seeded into a 96-well plate. Forty-eight hours after plating, three wells with uniform cell density were selected from each group, and three fields of view were captured under a 4× microscope. Images were taken every 48 hours up to day 6. The number and diameter of tumor cell spheroids were then quantified using ImageJ software.

First, astrocytes were seeded in the lower chamber of the Transwell with DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS. Subsequently, GSCs were seeded in the upper chamber of the Transwell. After 48 hours of coculture, nonmigrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed. Migrated cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed with PFA and stained with crystal violet. Following washing, drying, and mounting, images were captured using an inverted microscope. Migration was quantified by analyzing staining intensity in at least nine fields per group (n = 3) using ImageJ.

Extreme limiting dilution assay

GSCs were trypsinized, washed with PBS, and seeded into 96-well plates at serial dilutions of 200, 150, 100, 50, 25, 12, 6, 3, and 1 cell per well (12 replicates per dose). Because of intrinsic differences between the two cell lines, neurospheres with diameters greater than 120 μm were counted for GSC1 after 5 days, and neurospheres greater than 400 μm were counted for GSC2 after 7 days. Sphere formation frequency was calculated using the ELDA online tool (http://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda).

Isotope tracing assay

A total of 3 × 105 astrocytes were dissociated and seeded into 6-well plates. After adherence, cells were cultured in 13C-arginine–supplemented media (CLM-2265-H-PK, Cambridge lsotope Laboratories) for 24 hours. Supernatants were then collected and analyzed by mass spectrometry, whereas media without cells served as a negative control.

ATP and PCr measurements

ATP and PCr levels were simultaneously measured as previously described with the following modifications (25). Briefly, a total of 1 × 106 cells were harvested and lysed in ATP assay lysis buffer, and the protein concentration of each sample was determined. Fifty microliters of the extract were transferred into a 96-well black plate in triplicate, followed by the addition of 100 μl of ATP assay reagent. The chemiluminescence signal was measured using a microplate reader to determine ATP levels. Subsequently, ADP and Cr phosphokinase were added to the reaction well, allowing the Cr kinase enzymatic reaction to proceed for 10 to 15 min, after which the increase in bioluminescence signal was recorded to measure PCr levels.

Western blot analysis

Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and centrifuged at 14,000g at 4°C for 5 min. Protein samples were analyzed by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies at RT for 1 hour. Signals were detected using ECL or ECL Plus (GE Healthcare) and visualized by film development. The primary antibodies used were as follows: CKB (1:200, Proteintech) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:1000, Cali-Bio).

For supernatant analysis, cultures infected with shScr, shCKB#1, and shCKB#4 were collected, and the supernatants were filtered using a 0.22-μm filter. The filtered supernatant was then added to the upper compartment of a 100K Molecular Weight Cut-Off (MWCO) ultrafiltration tube and centrifuged at 3900 rpm at 4°C for 20 min. The lower fraction was collected and transferred to the upper compartment of a 10K MWCO ultrafiltration tube, followed by centrifugation under the same conditions. The upper fraction was collected, mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, and heated at 100°C for 10 min. Last, the samples were stored at −80°C for subsequent Western blot analysis.

PCR/qPCR

Total RNA was purified from GSCs or astrocytes using the EZB-press RNA purification kit (EZBioscience, #B0004DP-100). For each sample, 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using ExonScript RT SuperMix with dsDNase (Exongen, #A502-02). The cDNA was then prepared with iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) and analyzed using the Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System. The average threshold for each gene was determined and normalized to GAPDH. The primers used for quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) are listed in table S3.

PCr supplementation assay

First, the GSCs stably transfected with shScr, shCKB#1, and shCKB#4 were digested into a single-cell suspension. After counting the cells, they were seeded into 6-well plates, with three parallel replicates for each group. The GSCs were cultured in neuronal medium for 3 days. On the third day, 10 μM Cr phosphate was added to the medium and cultivation was continued. Subsequently, the ATP content was measured in each group using the ATP measurement reagent (Beyotime Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Detection of ATP release from astrocytes stimulated by glass microbeads

After astrocytes are digested into a single-cell suspension, they are counted and seeded into 12-well plates for culture. Following a 24-hour incubation period, the supernatant is removed, and the cells are washed three times with observation saline. Glass microbeads (30 to 50 μm) are gently added to the wells to induce mechanical stimulation. The supernatant is collected at various time points and frozen for later analysis. ATP levels in the supernatant are measured using the ATP measurement reagent (Beyotime Inc.), using a sensitive luciferase-based bioluminescence assay.

ATP and PCr assays for coculture of E18 fetal rat cortical primary neurons and GSCs

Pregnant rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the embryos were transferred to prechilled dissection solution. Using ophthalmic forceps, the fetal brains were carefully dissected, with the olfactory bulbs removed and the cerebral cortex isolated after stripping away the vascular membrane. The cerebral cortex was digested in enzyme solution for 20 min, mechanically dissociated, and filtered through a 40-μm cell strainer for cell counting. Cells were then seeded into 12-well plates precoated with poly-d-lysine, with three parallel wells per group, and maintained in a neuron maintenance medium for 5 days until cells adhered. After 5 days, glass beads were added to the 12-well plates, and Transwell inserts for coculture were placed above the wells. GSCs stably infected with shScr, shCKB#1, or shCKB#4 were digested into a single-cell suspension, counted, and seeded at the appropriate density in the Transwell inserts. After 48 hours of neuron-GSC coculture, the glass beads were gently mixed every 6 hours. Subsequently, GSCs in the upper chamber were collected, and ATP and PCr assays were performed following established protocols.

Sphere formation assay for coculture of E18 fetal rat cortical primary neurons with GSCs

Pregnant rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the embryos were transferred to prechilled dissection solution. Using ophthalmic forceps, fetal brains were carefully dissected, with the olfactory bulbs removed and the cerebral cortex isolated after stripping away the vascular membrane. The cerebral cortex was digested in enzyme solution for 20 min, followed by gentle trituration and filtration through a 40-μm cell strainer for cell counting. Cells were then seeded into poly-d-lysine–coated 12-well plates, with three parallel wells per group. Once the cells adhered, they were maintained in a neuron maintenance medium for 5 days. After 5 days, glass beads were added to the 12-well plates, and Transwell inserts for coculture were placed above the wells. GSCs stably infected with shScr, shCKB#1, or shCKB#4 were digested into single-cell suspensions, counted, and seeded at the appropriate density into the Transwell inserts. The glass beads were gently mixed every 6 hours. Images were captured every 24 hours for a total of 6 days, and data were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Spatial transcriptome library preparation

For spatial gene expression library preparation, frozen sections on Visium Spatial Gene Expression Slides were fixed with prechilled methanol and stained with H&E, following the Tissue Optimization Guide (CG000238 Rev. A, 10X Genomics). Sections were then permeabilized using Permeabilization Enzyme for the optimal duration, after which cDNAs were reverse transcribed, spatially barcoded, and amplified for 10 cycles. Standard spatial transcriptomics libraries were prepared according to the Visium Spatial Gene Expression User Guide (CG0000239 Rev. A, 10X Genomics). A total of 10 μl of amplified cDNA from each sample was used for library construction, including fragmentation, adapter ligation, index PCR, and purification. Each final library underwent quality control using the Bio-Fragment Analyzer Qseq1 (Bioptic Inc.) and was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Novogene, Beijing, China, with a sequencing depth of ~300 million to 400 million read pairs per sample.

3′ tag RNA-seq data simulation and evaluation of SCAR

The simulation datasets followed the generative model described in SCAPE (19). Briefly, the abundance and polyadenylation site of each transcript were provided to the simulation script, which returned the regions of each read derived from specific genomic coordinates. These reads were then merged as a library a chimeric sample, and the read alignment was visualized using Trackplot (37). For the real datasets, we incorporated scRNA-seq from a chimeric model and manually mixed human-derived and mouse-derived reads in different ratios to simulate spots in 10X Visium. The libraries from both simulation and real datasets were subsequently demultiplexed using various approaches to evaluate performance against gene expression quantification results before merging.

Alignment, quantification, and clustering

The quality control and preprocessing of FASTQ files were implemented by fastp with default parameters (38). Before processing the sequencing data with SpaceRanger to obtain gene expression profiles, SCAR was used to demultiplex the human and mouse reads from the raw files. All analysis steps were seamlessly coordinated using Snakemake (39). The separated mouse- and human-derived fastq files were then processed with SpaceRanger to generate gene expression data. For quality control, low-quality spots—defined as those with fewer than 100 gene counts or a mitochondrial gene ratio greater than 25%—were removed. The expression matrix of the high-quality spots was then normalized and scaled, and highly variable genes were selected using SCTransform. Principal components analysis was performed on the scaled matrix with default parameters. Shared nearest-neighbor graph construction and clustering were then performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions in Seurat (40), respectively. The anatomical regions of each slide were defined on the basis of clustering results integrated with the Allen Brain Atlas map (41) using the online tool ShinySRT (42).

Deconvolution and gene enrichment

The scRNA-seq datasets from GL261-bearing mice were used as the reference dataset. We applied Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) to deconvolute the transcriptome of each spot, identifying the likely constituent cell types (43). For gene enrichment analysis, gene sets from MSigDB (44) were downloaded and subjected to the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) function in clusterProfiler (45) to perform the enrichment analysis.

Cell-cell interaction analysis

The ligand-receptor interaction data were obtained from CellChat (46). Subsequently, the ligand and receptor of each interaction pair were converted to mouse gene symbols to enable the analysis of interactions between tumor cells (human genes) and the TME (mouse genes). Spatially resolved cell-cell interaction analysis was then conducted using CellChat with default parameters, integrated into the SCAR package.

Analysis of public glioma spatial RNA-seq datasets

For Ravi’s datasets, the data were downloaded from SPATAData (10), and the author-provided annotations were used for downstream analysis. For the IVY datasets, the FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) expression values and annotations for each sample were downloaded from the Allen Institute Glioblastoma portal (https://glioblastoma.alleninstitute.org/). Region-specific expressed genes were calculated using limma (47), with a log2 fold change of >0.5 and a P value of ≤0.01 considered as significantly differentially expressed genes. These genes were visualized by ComplexHeatmap (v2.0.0) (48).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 and GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Data were presented as means ± SD with individual data points. Significance values are indicated in figures or marked as *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001, and ****P ≤ 0.0001.
