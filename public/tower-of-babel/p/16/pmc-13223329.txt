Green finance and environmental sustainability: evaluating the role of urbanization, energy, and institutional quality in Asia’s low-carbon transition
Authors: Qiheng Wang, Sareer Ahmad, Nigah Zehra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Asia is a fast-growing emerging market with investment potential, making it essential to investigate the mechanisms by which green finance influences environmental sustainability. For the empirical analysis using data from 2000 to 2022 on twenty-two Asian economies, we applied the fixed- and random-effects model. First, we have verified the model’s feasibility and employed diagnostic tests and robustness, which show that the model is appropriate. The key findings of the fixed effect model, suggested by the Hausman test, indicate that there is a negative association between Green Finance and CO2 emissions. The green finance coefficient is − 0.0107, which implies that a 1% increase in green finance leads to a reduction in carbon emissions by 0.0107%. Likewise, population density exhibits a negative relationship with CO2 emissions. The coefficient of population density is − 0.5373, implying that a 1% increase in population density diminishes carbon emissions by 0.5373%, which supports the ecological efficiency theory. The Causality test shows unidirectional causality among the variables. Based on evidence, this study suggests that policymakers of the Asian economies should focus on green finance initiatives to support environmentally sustainable investments, which leads to a decline in CO₂ emissions.

The term green itself refers to activities that reduce environmental pollution, save natural resources, and foster sustainable development3.

Green finance plays an important role as a driving force in changing environmental development, mainly in two ways. First, it promotes entrepreneurs who engage in producing eco-friendly products and environmentally friendly practices, while secondly, it helps to decrease pollution levels by fostering the efficient use of energy-efficient technologies, replacing high-energy- consuming equipment. As well, green finance development contributes to reducing poverty through awareness among farmers about green products. Farmers must be motivated and stimulated to grow organic crops without using any ingredients that cause environmental distortion4. By upgrading rural regeneration, green finance needs to be injected into environmentally friendly projects. Therefore, the stakeholders or governments should enhance the environmental quality and focus all the sectors to use the efficient financial tools that foster the green development10. Hanif11 reported that the consumption pattern of both fossil fuels and solid fuels has a negative impact on life expectancy (LE) by increasing the mortality rate (MR) in the region of Sub-Saharan African economies.

Literature review

Studies on green financing and its impact have received significant attention in recent years. Existing literature gives a rich archival record in this regard. Keeping our research objectives under consideration, we have accumulated a summary of existing research across our major themes, i.e., green financing and environmental sustainability. It provides a glimpse of the existing body of knowledge on the selected areas and also helps us to identify potential research gaps.

Green finance, economic development, and sustainability

According to Liu et al.28, who investigated the nexus between green finance and green growth in China, they collected data from 30 provinces from 2007 to 2016. This study concludes that green finance and green growth are interconnected, indicating that green finance leads to enhanced green growth in China. However, Zhang and Wang29 analyzed the impact of green finance on energy sustainable development in China using data from 2004 to 2017. The findings show that sustainable energy development can be improved through green financing development using different tools for measuring, such as economic development, environmental development, and financial development. Nawaz et al.30 explored the effect of green financial development on growth using data from Pakistan. The empirical analysis using the ARDL model found that green credit, green investment, and securities have a positive impact on the economic growth of Pakistan. Additionally, the outcome of the study shows that FDI and green insurance have a positive but insignificant impact on growth. Yasmeen and Shah31 found that forests show a positive influence on the climate conditions and improve their efficiency. Additionally, they also reported the presence of a U-shaped environmental Kuznets curve.

Xu et al.32 observed that trade openness reduces ecosystem quality, while renewable energy development and institutional quality have a positive influence on green financing. Khan et al.33 found that green energy consumption leads to environmental sustainability. Moreover, Khan et al.23,24 found that green finance and renewable energy are the main sources of environmental sustainability. They argue that renewable energy consumption, research and development, and public-private partnerships help in reducing CO2 emissions. Dong et al.34 conducted a research study to determine whether green finance & innovation enhance climate sustainability in China using the Quantile ARDL model. The findings confirm that green finance and green innovation significantly impacted the environment. The evidence shows that GDP growth significantly enhances CO2 emissions, while renewable energy consumption enhances environmental sustainability. The study suggested that policymakers focus on green investment, launching green investment initiatives that enhance environmental quality. Cheng et al.35 argue that we should focus on innovative technology and proper utilization of resources that reduce carbon emissions. Country-level effective governance plays a key role in prosperity. Effective governance attracts foreign investors to the country that can invest in green technology, which will lead to enhancing the green economy. The study is consistent with Bhutta et al.36. Effective governance has a positive impact on economic growth as well as environmental sustainability. Lv et al.37 highlight the key findings that improvement in resource allocation and green technology enhances environmental sustainability in China. Additionally, they have argued that the Chinese economy transformed from low-income to upper-middle-income due to structural reforms.

Gap of the study

Despite the existing literature on the rapid expansion of green finance initiatives globally, there is a lack of inclusive regional-level empirical analysis on how green finance influences environmental sustainability in Asian countries. As per our knowledge, how does this study differ from the previous studies? First, most of the studies available on developed economies, OECD economies, or individual Asian economies ignore the heterogeneity across Asian economies in terms of institutional quality, climate policies, and financial development frameworks. This diversity makes Asian countries ideal for investigating the channel through which green finance enhances environmental sustainability under different developmental trajectories. Secondly, in the existing literature, most of the study treats green finance as a homogenous while this study investigates its role under heterogeneity in terms of climate policy, Institutional quality, and financial framework. Thirdly, previous studies rely on short term span and static models, but in the existing study, we have applied the advanced econometric techniques to capture the cross-sectional dependency and heterogeneity across countries. Fourth, past studies conducted before 2015, this study incorporated the post-Paris Agreement period, capturing the effects of newly announced green finance instruments such as green bonds and climate finance initiatives, sustainable investment mechanisms, and green banking guidelines following the Paris Agreement. The effectiveness of these financial initiatives and their central role in sustainability through the green finance channel has been largely unexplored at the regional level. Thus, we will investigate the impact of green finance on environmental sustainability in Asian economies and fill the gap in the existing literature.

Empirical model and methodology

In this study, we have targeted Asian economies that are highly vulnerable in the context of climate change and have investigated the impact of green finance on environmental sustainability. For the empirical analysis using data from 2000 to 2022, we applied the fixed and random effect models to capture the effects across the country. This period captures major developments in green finance initiatives, renewable energy expansion, and environmental policy reforms across the regions. For robustness, we have applied the different diagnostic tests to verify the validity of the data.

Econometric model and techniques

We have used the panel data analysis, and its advantage is that it controls the unobserved heterogeneity and gives more reliable outcomes. In the literature, using Pooled Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), fixed and random effect models. If we assume that the sample socio-economic characteristics are the same, then we move to Pooled Ordinary Least Squares techniques, but if we assume that the data dynamics are different, their socio-economic characteristics, then we apply fixed and random effects. The decision of the fixed and random model based on the Hausman test.

Fixed effect model (FEM)

The main reason of applying fixed effect is to capture the unobserved heterogeneity across countries such as socio-economic characteristics, geography, climate variation and institutional quality that contribute in the CO2 emissions may be positive or negative. If we ignore these factors can give us inconsistent and biased results. Fixed effect focuses on the country-specific variation over time and remove these unobserved effects from the model. It allows us to capture the true impact of green finance and other control variables on environmental degradation. Fixed effect is important because the Asian panel has mixed countries (Higher, middle, and lower income), which have different financial structures, economic backgrounds, and environmental policies. Thus, it reduces the omitted variable bias problem and provides more reliable and consistent outcomes as compared to Pooled OLS. Two-stage least squares (2SLS) model

The Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) technique is used to address the potential endogeneity in the explanatory variables. It first regresses the endogenous variables on suitable instruments and then uses the predicted values in the main regression. We have applied this method to address the endogeneity problem.

The feasible generalized least squares (FGLS) model

The Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) method corrects for autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity in the panel data and provides more efficient and reliable estimates compared to ordinary least squares when these issues are present. We have employed the FGLS in our study.

The panel-corrected standard errors (PCSE) model

The Panel-Corrected Standard Errors (PCSE) approach is used to tackle the problem of heteroscedasticity and cross-sectional dependence across countries, making it mainly suitable for multiple panels observed over time, such as our dataset of 22 Asian economies for the period 2000 to 2022. We have also applied the PCSE in our study.

Hausman test

To ensure the model’s validity, determine whether the fixed effect model is appropriate or the random effect model. The Hausman test helps in determining whether unobserved country-specific factors are correlated with the independent variables. If we apply the random and fixed model without applying the Husman test, the result will be inconsistent and biased. For the credibility and robustness of the findings, this test has an important implication. The construction of the hypothesis is given below:

Random effects are efficient and consistent

H1

Fixed effects are efficient and consistent

If we reject the null hypothesis and accept the alternative hypothesis, we suggest that the fixed effect model is appropriate.

Data and variables

The study creates a rich theoretical approach to analyzing the questions of whether green finance has the potential to provide environmental sustainability in the Asian economies based on the Ecological Efficiency Theory, Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis, and Governance Efficiency Theory. The framework describes the interaction between economic, demographic, institutional, and energy-related factors to affect CO2 emission, which is a proxy of environmental sustainability.

In this study, we used CO2 emissions as our core dependent variable, which is widely accepted as a proxy for environmental sustainability. From a green finance perspective, we aim to reduce environmental degradation by financing a low-carbon future, green energy projects, and energy efficiency. Asian countries contribute to global economic growth and have high demand for energy consumption and environmental stress, contributing to global environmental degradation. At the same time, many Asian economies have recently started green banking, green bonds, and green investment initiatives. Thus, we used CO2 emissions to evaluate how these initiatives effectively mitigate the environmental pressure. Likewise, carbon emission data is consistent, comparable, and easily available for all countries over time. Same proxy used by Fan et al59, Choudhary et al60. Green Finance is the main focus independent variable and plays a key role in promoting environmental sustainability and low-carbon economic activities. Green finance demonstrates the efficient allocation of financial resources through banks, capital markets, and investment instruments to innovative projects or activities that promote a sustainable environment, including green technologies, green bonds, sustainable resource management, climate policy, and renewable energy, which support long-term green growth and stability. Green finance is based on the Ecological Efficiency Theory, which increases resource efficiency and encourages cleaner production methods, which decrease carbon emissions. We have taken data for green finance using proxy public investments in renewable energy (2019 million USD), from the IRENA Public Finance Database. Similar proxies were used by Cui61, Peng and Zheng2, and Shan et al.3 for green financing. Population is the control variable showing the growth and size of the country’s population. We have included it as a control variable because when the population increases, it leads to an increase in the energy consumption demand, economic activities, and urban pressure, which directly contribute to environmental sustainability and CO2 emissions. Theoretically, the sign of population growth will be positive, but it was reported as negative in a study by Rehman et al.62. The positive and negative sign of the population depends on the region and developmental differences. Due to economies of scale may the higher population density may contribute to the effective utilization of resources, which decreases the per capita energy consumption. Urbanization means the share of the population living in urban areas because people migrated for opportunities and better living standards. Thus, urbanization significantly influences the energy consumption demand, industrial activities, transportation trajectories, and environmental quality. We used the measurement unit for urbanization, which is the percentage of the total population. The sign of the coefficient (positive/negative) depends on the economic trajectories of a country. In the literature, reports of negative and positive signs regarding carbon emissions. The income of a country plays an important role in its economic prosperity. To measure income, we used the GDP at constant US dollars, and data were taken from World Development Indicators. Higher-income economies initially have higher demand for energy consumption, which leads to environmental degradation, but with the passage of time, due to strong institutions, economies have transformed to green and clean technologies, which have enhanced environmental sustainability, supporting the environmental Kuznets curve. Its sign depends upon the structure of economies; a positive sign indicates that higher income leads to growth-driven pollution, while a negative sign indicates that higher income gains green technologies, which contribute to a clean environment. A similar proxy was used by63. Institutional quality is an important variable on the policy side when we evaluate environmental sustainability. Institutional quality measures the effectiveness of the governance system of a country. We have constructed the Institutional Quality Index through Principal component analysis, which is the combination of six governance indicators. Strong institutions are important because they ensure the effective implementation of green financing policies, improve transparency, and accountability. The green finance and the environmental outcomes have an intermediate level of institutional quality (IQ). The Governance Efficiency Theory holds that good institutions enhance the enforcement of policies, regulatory adherence, and transparency, which may ensure that green financial instruments are effective in supporting sustainable development objectives. On the contrary, poor quality of institutions can undermine the environmental gains of green finance. Therefore, improved institutional systems increase the emission-cutting effect of green finance.

Energy consumption in our control variable is measured in “kg of oil equivalent per capita,” and data have been taken from WDI. It measures the total use of energy consumption in an economy, and it is the key driver of carbon emissions and environmental degradation. Most of the Asian economies still rely on fossil fuels, which significantly contribute to environmental degradation. The expected sign of energy consumption with carbon emissions is positive, as reported in the economic literature64.

The renewable electricity output measure is the share of energy generated from renewable sources, which demonstrates the transition towards clean energy or a sustainable environment. We measured this variable as % of total electricity output, and the data have been taken from the WDI source. Why is Renewable electricity output important? Because it reduces the reliance on fuels, which is the main source of carbon emissions. Its expected sign is negative with CO2 emissions and supports the ecological efficiency theory. The above Table 10 explains the findings of the 2SLS econometric techniques. The instrument variable is introduced and regressed 2SLS regression to tackle the issue of endogeneity. Eco-innovation is used as an instrument for green finance based on relevance and exogeneity considerations. Eco-innovation reflects the capacity of an economy to develop environmentally friendly technologies that require considerable financial resources for large-scale implementation, thus increasing the demand for green finance. Conditional on income, energy consumption, renewable energy use, institutional quality, and urbanization, eco-innovation does not directly affect carbon emissions; its impact operates primarily through green finance that facilitates the adoption and diffusion of green technologies. Hence, eco-innovation satisfies the exclusion restriction and constitutes a valid instrument for green finance. The variable GF has a significantly negative relationship with carbon emissions. GF coefficient is − 0.0290, meaning that with 1% increase in GF, CO2 emission will decrease by − 0.0290%. Our findings are consistent with economic literature. Another variable population with a coefficient is − 0.0844, which has a negative association with carbon emissions. This suggests that population increase leads to a decline in carbon emissions. The results can be counterintuitive, as we expect that population increases, carbon emissions also increase due to transportation needs, industrial activities, and energy consumption. But in literature, we can interpret the results in several ways depending upon the context of the study. In urban areas, a larger population might benefit from economies of scale, better public transport systems, and more efficient infrastructure, leading to lower carbon consumption per capita and more stringent environmental regulations, resulting in lower per capita emissions. Regions with expanding populations may potentially see faster technological developments and better deployment of green technologies. This has the potential to improve energy efficiency & reduce CO2 emissions with population growth. EC also reported a positive link with CO2 emissions, with a 1 percent increase in EC resulting in 0.9156% rise in CO2 emissions. CO2 is positively impacted by income as well.

Conclusion and policy implications

This study aimed to investigate whether green finance can help ensure environmental sustainability by decreasing the level of carbon emissions in Asian economies. In this study, using a panel data set of 22 Asian economies over the years 2000 to 2022, the effect of green finance, in after controlling for some macroeconomic determinants such as population, urbanization, income, energy consumption, renewable electricity output, and institutional quality, on CO₂ emissions has been examined. For estimation purposes and robustness of findings, this study has employed different econometric models such as fixed and random effects, two-stage least squares, feasible generalized least squares, and panel-corrected standard errors. The empirical findings revealed that green finance significantly reduces carbon emissions in the Asian economies. The finding strongly supports the statement that green finance is an effective tool in ensuring that sustainable environments are achieved in the Asian economies.

Among the control variables, population growth exerts a negative association with CO2 emissions, which could be a result of improvements in energy efficiency, technological diffusion, or demographic changes in some Asian economies. Also, urbanization is seen to enhance overall carbon emissions, which implies that fast-moving urbanization may present serious environmental issues if not managed properly. Income growth is also positively related to overall carbon emissions, which implies that overall income growth in Asia remains energy and carbon-intensive, thereby reinforcing the need for greener growth strategies. Moreover, the energy consumption significantly increases CO₂ emissions, showing that the dependence on fossil fuels is still dominant within the economies of Asia. The result for renewable electricity output also positively affects CO₂ emissions, implying that the scale and method of generation for renewable energy, perhaps working simultaneously with existing energy infrastructure, still have some effects that lead to higher CO₂ emissions. Institutional quality has significant positive effects on environmental performance, demonstrating that effective governance and regulatory frameworks enhance the effectiveness of environmental and green finance policies.

This paper makes a contribution to the existing literature by providing strong empirical evidence on the role of green finance in mitigating carbon emissions in the context of Asian economies, which have received relatively less attention in the existing literature. By using a variety of econometric methods such as 2SLS to account for endogeneity as well as FGLS and PCSE to control for possible heteroscedasticity, autocorrelation, and cross-sectional dependence, further enhances confidence in their validity. The results provide strong support for the causal role of green finance in promoting environmental sustainability. At the end, the sensitivity analysis and panel Granger causality test have been conducted. The sensitivity analysis revealed that the coefficient for green finance remained negative and statistically more significant when one independent variable was excluded, indicating that the main result is not driven by a single explanatory variable. When it comes to Granger causality, our results explored the unidirectional causality.

Policy implications

Based on the above findings, the following policy recommendations are proposed. The empirical evidence emphasizes the need for Asian economies to increase and deepen green finance initiatives to support environmentally sustainable investments, as it leads to a decline in CO₂ emissions. As energy consumption continues to increase, environmental pressures, policies that encourage energy efficiency, and decrease dependence on fossil fuels are imperative. The positive association between renewable electricity output and emissions reflects transitional challenges, highlighting the importance of enhancing the efficiency and systemic integration of renewable energy technologies. Furthermore, improvements in institutional quality with the help of strong governance and regulatory frameworks can strengthen the effectiveness of green finance and environmental policies, in conjunction with the adoption of sustainable urban development strategies.

Drawbacks or limitations of the study

During the conduct of this study, we faced several drawbacks (limitations). First, we have faced the issue of missing data for the countries for many years; we have excluded those countries from the sample. Secondly, we have missed the regional evidence gap, to investigate the data on the basis of income classification (Higher, middle, and lower income) countries to capture the heterogeneity effect. We have faced the issue of aggregation bias because we analyzed the data of the Asian economies as a whole. Third, due to data availability constraints, this study uses a weak proxy for green finance, which is public investments in renewable energy and environmental sustainability, which is CO2 emissions, a single environmental indicator, and misses the multidimensional nature of sustainability proxies such as Ecological indicators, economic resilience, and institutional quality, etc. Fourth, the sample is limited to Asian countries, which may restrict the generalizability of the results beyond the Asian region.
