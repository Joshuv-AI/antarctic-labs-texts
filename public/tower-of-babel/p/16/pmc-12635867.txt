Overnight Dexamethasone in Primary Aldosteronism Screening in Patients on Interfering Therapy (ODEPRASC): A Diagnostic Interpretability Study Protocol
Authors: Piotr Kmieć, Dominika Okroj, Małgorzata Zdrojewska, Jowita Fiszer, Sonia Zembrzuska, Renata Świątkowska‐Stodulska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractScreening for primary aldosteronism (PA) remains exceedingly low, despite the fact that the disorder contributes to or underlies hypertension (HT) in as many as 20% of unselected patients. Conventionally, withdrawal of medications interfering with the renin‒angiotensin‒aldosterone system (RAAS) has been recommended before PA work‐up. Previous research showed that combining objective thresholds and 2‐day cosyntropin suppression was highly accurate in diagnosing PA among HT patients off interfering drugs. Here, we present the protocol of a study designed to generate and temporally validate aldosterone‐to‐renin ratio (ARR) thresholds following overnight cosyntropin suppression in PA screening on interfering medications. We hypothesize that overnight cosyntropin suppression with 1 mg dexamethasone will result in 25% higher diagnostic interpretability compared to conventional ARR testing. This single‐center study consists of a development and confirmation cohort (both n = 80). Patients with an adrenal incidentaloma are enrolled in a 1‐day clinic. Aldosterone‐to‐renin ratios (ARRs) are determined before and after overnight intake of 1 mg dexamethasone (DXM) on, partially off, and off medications interfering with the RAAS. Emphasis on screening and limitation of PA confirmatory (suppression) tests have been included in the current Endocrine Society guideline on PA due to low evidence of benefits of the latter in diagnosing the disorder. In light of poor PA screening rates, the ODEPRASC study may provide a rationale for an optimized diagnostic approach. Background

Taking into account its overt and mild (not meeting conventional diagnostic criteria) forms, primary aldosteronism (PA) underlies or contributes to hypertension (HT) in up to 20% of unselected patients [1, 2, 3, 4]. Cardiovascular (CV) and nephrogenic complications occur markedly more often in PA patients than in those with primary HT despite comparable blood pressure (BP) [5, 6]. Aldosterone (Ald) exerts the majority of its biological effects by binding to the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR), which can be countered with an MR antagonist (MRA). In some patients, lateralization of Ald secretion allows for curative adrenalectomy. Both surgical and pharmacological treatment mitigate the increased CV risk in PA [7, 8].

In spite of high prevalence, significant health burden associated with it, and availability of targeted therapy, PA is vastly underdiagnosed. While established indications for PA screening comprise approximately 50% of all patients with HT, fewer than 5% are tested [9, 10]. Recently, a recommendation to consider testing all adult patients with confirmed HT for PA was included in the European Society of Cardiology guideline on the management of high blood pressure and HT [11], which was also suggested in the 2025 Endocrine Society guideline [12].

The diagnostic approach toward PA in line with the 2016 Endocrine Society guideline (and the European Society of Hypertension's 2020 consensus statement) consists of three steps: screening by determining the Ald‐to‐renin ratio (ARR), confirmatory testing, and subtype differentiation [7, 13]. These steps are optimally undertaken upon complete withdrawal of RAAS‐interfering. Although in some PA patients, renin suppression and abnormally high Ald persist despite the use of renin‐stimulating medications (e.g., diuretics, angiotensin II receptor antagonists, ARBs, and angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, ACE‐Is), for the majority of patients with HT, the ARR on interfering drugs has been shown to approximately double upon their withdrawal [14, 15]. Indeed, the above‐mentioned ESC guideline lists “best practice to screen and manage” PA as one of the specific “gaps in the evidence” [11]. The 2025 Endocrine Society guideline suggested limiting confirmatory PA tests to patients with an intermediate probability of a lateralizing disorder subtype [12].

Furthermore, similar to other endocrine disorders, accumulated evidence demonstrates that the PA spectrum encompasses a wide range of clinical and biochemical presentations, from normotension to severe HT, normo‐ (usually) to severe hypokalaemia, and negative screening but positive confirmatory test results, and vice versa [4, 16, 17, 18, 19]. This contrasts with binary categorization of ARR and PA confirmatory test results.

One of the approaches to improve the diagnostic accuracy is to use 97.5th‐percentile Ald and ARR thresholds derived from normotensive controls with normal adrenal morphology. Another is the exclusion of the stimulatory effect of corticotropin (ACTH) on Ald release in PA testing. These two modifications of the conventional work‐up were successfully applied by GP Piaditis, Markou et al., who, among others, demonstrated that PA screening and confirmatory tests performed after applying 2‐day 2 mg/day dexamethasone (DXM) intake allowed for accurately diagnosing the disorder [18, 20, 21, 22]. While accounting for ACTH stimulation in Ald secretion is not standard in PA diagnostic work‐up (at least not in screening), it is highly relevant in explaining the individual variability of mineralocorticoid's concentrations, which can underlie PA underdiagnosis [3, 23]. In healthy volunteers, Ald secretion over 24 h strongly correlated with cortisol in some patients, but in others, a pulsatile pattern was recorded [24].

The “Overnight Dexamethasone in PA Screening in Patients on Interfering Therapy” (ODEPRASC) study aims to provide an optimized approach to PA screening by suppressing corticotropin without (or with only partial) modification of medications interfering with the RAAS. The primary objective is to develop and temporally validate post‐DXM ARR thresholds designed to lower the equivocal screening rate by 25% compared with conventional ARR determination. The secondary objective of this study is to investigate the associations between post‐DXM ARR levels and urinary 24‐h Ald excretion in the oral salt loading test (OSLT) off RAAS‐interfering medications. The study protocol adheres to the SPIRIT guidelines [25].

Patients and Methods

Setting

The ODEPRASC study is conducted at a tertiary endocrine center in a university hospital. Participant recruitment commenced on October 22, 2024, and its completion is anticipated in October 2026.

Trial Design

ODEPRASC is a cross‐sectional study investigating the diagnostic interpretability of post‐DXM ARR determination in PA screening. Post‐DXM ARR thresholds will be generated and temporally validated among patients with HT on chronic, RAAS‐interfering therapy.

The study combines baseline (conventional) ARR determinations and PA confirmatory tests (the seated saline infusion test, or SIT, or captopril challenge test, CCT) that are clinically indicated with post‐DXM ARR measurements for research purposes. The oral salt loading test (OSLT) has been planned as a research‐specific activity.

Therapy modifications will generate data for different medication constellations. Each participant will be tested several times (pre‐ and post‐DXM, as well as on, partially off, and off RAAS‐interfering therapy).

In the course of the study, permissive ARR thresholds are used. In patients off interfering medications, ARR <10 ng/IU (or <20 ng/dL per ng/mL/h) is considered negative, ARR 10–20 ng/mIU equivocal, and an ARR ≥20 ng/mIU positive, provided Ald ≥6 ng/dL (otherwise equivocal).

A study by Li et al. showed that an ARR 10 ng/mIU in patients on RAAS‐interfering medications was equivalent to 20 ng/mIU in those switched to non‐interfering drugs [15]. Also, studies by others showed beta‐blockers led to a two‐to‐three‐fold increase in ARR due to renin suppression [26, 27]. Taking these data into account, in the ODEPRASC study, in patients on beta‐blocker monotherapy, a positive ARR is defined as ≥20 ng/mIU, provided Ald ≥6 ng/dL (otherwise negative). In patients on medication regimens that include renin‐stimulating drugs (including diuretics), an ARR <8 ng/mIU is considered negative, 8–20 ng/mIU equivocal, and ≥20 ng/mIU positive, provided Ald ≥6 ng/dL (otherwise equivocal). The low ARR threshold in patients on combined renin‐inhibiting and ‐stimulating therapy (e.g., beta‐blocker and an ACE‐I or diuretic) prioritizes sensitivity for initial PA screening. Although discontinuing beta‐blockers typically elevates plasma renin and yields a negative ARR upon retesting, this is not universal.

Objectives and Endpoints

The primary objective of the study is the development and temporal validation of post‐DXM ARR thresholds that a 25% reduction in equivocal rate compared with conventional (without overnight 1‐mg DXM intake) ARR determination. Endpoints that are required to meet this objective are two to three sets of pre‐ and post‐ARR determinations for each patient: without, with partial, and with complete withdrawal of RAAS‐interfering medications, both in the development and confirmatory cohorts.

The secondary objective is investigating an association between post‐DXM ARR and urinary 24‐h Ald excretion in the oral salt loading test performed off interfering medications. Post‐DXM ARRs on different medications will be investigated for correlations or other associations with urinary aldosteronuria in the OSLT.

Patients Characteristics, Inclusion, and Exclusion Criteria

In this study, a total of 240 patients will be recruited, a 33% dropout rate is anticipated, and the development and confirmation cohorts will comprise 80 patients each. Participants are enrolled in a 1‐day endocrine clinic among patients admitted due to the presence of an adrenal incidentaloma (AI) with radiologic features of an adrenocortical adenoma (ACA) or hyperplasia (ACH). Informed consent for participation is obtained in writing from every subject, which stipulates that withdrawal from the study is possible at any time.

To be eligible to participate in the ODEPRASC study, participants must be aged between 40 and 75 years, suffer from (suspected or diagnosed) HT, have available Ald and renin results at the time of the 1‐day clinic stay, and be scheduled to undergo the 1‐mg‐DXM test (indicated for possible mild autonomous cortisol secretion, MACS, defined as 8 am. cortisolemia of at least 50 nmol/L in the overnight 1‐mg DXM test without the presence of overt clinical Cushing's syndrome features).

Exclusion criteria include the following: baseline (pre‐DXM) hyperreninemia (renin exceeding the upper limit of normal at the study site) and/or Ald<4 ng/dL; overt clinical and/or biochemical features of adrenal hormone deficiency or excess apart from MACS defined as cortisolemia in the 50–140 nmol/L range in the 1‐mg DXM test; therapy with glucocorticoids, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, hormonal replacement therapy, hormonal contraceptive therapy, and/or liquorice intake; established or suspected secondary cause of HT other than possible/suspected PA; poor physical condition (frailty), as well as comorbidities affecting the feasibility of chronic medication modification.

Sample Size Calculation

The sizes of the post‐DXM ARR development and confirmatory cohorts are based on the pilot data included below. A 25% increase in ARR interpretability in PA screening is expected with overnight 1 mg DXM intake prior to determination. In other words, the post‐DXM ARR is anticipated to reduce the rate of indeterminate ARR by 25% compared with conventional ARR measurement.

The target sample size n = 80 for both cohorts is inflated from n = 77, i.e., the result of the test of paired proportions of diagnostic interpretability (the ratio of clearly categorized screening result to the total number of determinations) as calculated in RStudio 2023.06.1 Build 524, i.e., McNemar test with a correlation factored to adjust the effect size: 0.4 (interpretability of conventional ARR) and 0.65 (interpretability of post‐DXM ARR), a correlation of 0.4. A Bonferroni‐method adjusted alpha level of 0.0625 (unadjusted 0.05, up to eight subgroups due to multiple medication combinations planned), and 90% power.

Preliminary Data

An ARR threshold of 20 ng/mIU (combined with Ald>6 ng/dL) was set for a positive screening result in patients on interfering medications. This cutoff balances sensitivity and specificity in the case of beta‐blocker therapy, which typically inflates the ARR two‐ to three‐fold, as previously reported [26, 27]. Admittedly, in patients on renin‐stimulating medications other than diuretics (e.g., ACE inhibitors or ARBs alone), a lower threshold could enhance sensitivity [15]. Accordingly, ARR values between 10 and 20 ng/mIU were classified as equivocal. No specific negative ARR threshold was applied for patients on diuretics.

Preliminary data from 19 patients revealed conventional ARR screening was negative (<10 ng/mIU off diuretics) in five, positive in three, and equivocal in 11 patients. Post‐DXM ARR clearly classified ARR as positive (>20 ng/mIU with Ald>6 ng/dL) in six equivocal cases, which translated to a 31% higher interpretability (42% pre‐DXM vs. 73% post‐DXM). Specifically, equivocal rate decreased due to the following: in two patients receiving beta‐blockers, Ald was below and above 6 ng/dL, respectively pre‐ and post‐DXM, in three others, renin was at least 30% lower post‐DXM, and in the sixth case Ald was approximately 20% higher post‐DXM, while renin was relatively constant (4.9 and 5.2 mIU/L).

Study Schema and Participant Timeline

The study will follow the schema presented below (Figure 1). The same activities have been planned for both the development and confirmatory cohorts.

Study schema.

Participant timeline in each cohort will be 4 to 6 months (Figure 2). It includes an ARR assessment on chronic medications in the 1‐day clinic, three to four outpatient visits. Patients are advised to discontinue RAAS‐interfering medications and are prescribed doxazosin and/or verapamil. Minimum washout periods of 2 weeks for beta‐blockers, ACE inhibitors, and ARBs, and 4 weeks for diuretics were established to accommodate participants unable or unwilling to extend medication withdrawal. In practice, as shown in the individual participant timeline (Figure 2), washout has been planned to extend beyond the minimum periods and ARR re‐testing has been taking place 3 weeks off beta‐blockers, 4 weeks off ACE‐Is and ARBs, and typically 6‐week off diuretics. Additional outpatient clinic visits will be provided if required to verify HT control on modified medications (in‐office and with 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring).

Data Collection, Validation, Monitoring, and Management

A database containing baseline and follow‐up characteristics was created. Only study investigators have access to the data. The data are monitored for quality by the investigators.

Laboratory Examinations

Laboratory examinations are performed in the Central Diagnostic Laboratory of the University Clinical Center of the Medical University of Gdańsk, which applies standard and validated methodology, particularly Liaison Diasorin assays for plasma direct renin and serum Ald concentrations.

Statistical Analysis

The primary objective will be achieved on the basis of PA confirmatory test results, one of which (the OSLT) has been planned for all participants. Transformations of post‐DXM ARRs on different medications will be applied to generate a single threshold with AUROC analysis to optimize sensitivity and specificity. The continuous nature of PA renders it impossible to determine a single threshold to exclude the disorder; however, the current (conventional) diagnostic criteria can be applied for this purpose. Missing data will be inferred via maximum likelihood estimation.

Full Study Protocol

The full study protocol is freely accessible at the following website: https://www.protocols.io/private/63CAB389BA1611EF95DA0A58A9FEAC02/. Although the ODEPRASC study is observational in nature, the protocol follows the 2013 “Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials” (SPIRIT) checklist [28].

Discussion

A significant shift in the understanding of PA has occurred in recent years: the conventional binary approach, which is evident in (1) eligibility for screening, (2) interpretation of the ARR, (3) confirmatory tests, and (4) the presence of unilaterality of Ald secretion, was proven obsolete in lieu of a continuous spectrum of renin‐independent Ald excess. Clinical data are supported by discoveries on the cellular and tissue pathology in PA. The implementation of Ald synthase (CYP11B2) immunohistochemistry and next‐generation sequencing (pinpointing Ald‐driving mutations) vastly broadened the understanding of possible lesions [29]. The concomitance of different lesions in one patient is frequent [30, 31].

Appropriate interpretation of the ARR affords even more weight than just for screening if one considers that confirmatory PA tests provide little diagnostic benefit and poorly predict even surgically treatable cases; this has been documented in the 2025 Endocrine Society guideline [12, 14, 32, 33, 34].

The ODEPRASC study will most likely not generate all‐encompassing diagnostic criteria; however, it is anticipated to provide evidence for simplified (by avoiding medication modification), yet more useful PA testing. The potential impact of the study depends on successful recruitment and repeated ARR determinations in the enrolled participants. While this can be problematic, subjects will undergo clinically indicated testing, only complemented by post‐DXM assessments. Additionally, internal temporal validation of post‐DXM ARR thresholds has been planned, which will require further studies prior to potential universal clinical implementation of the proposed approach to PA screening.
