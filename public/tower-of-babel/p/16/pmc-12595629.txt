Exploring the retirees’ needs and determinants of their adjustment during retirement: a qualitative study guided by Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model
Authors: Elaheh Shoushtari-Moghaddam, Mohammad Hossein Kaveh, Masoud Karimi, Mohammad Javad Tarrahi, Esmail Soltani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRetirement is a major life transition that introduces various bio-psycho-socio-spiritual challenges, requiring individuals to adapt to new financial, social, and existential realities. While previous studies have examined specific aspects of retirees’ needs, a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing retirement adjustment remains limited. This study explores retirees’ needs and determinants of adjustment using Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model as a guiding framework.MethodsThis qualitative study employed a directed qualitative content analysis approach, utilizing semi-structured in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with 42 key informants. Data were analyzed using Graneheim and Lundman’s qualitative content analysis framework, identifying key themes and categories.ResultsA total of 152 codes were categorized into Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model levels. At the microsystem level, retirees focus on financial stability, health, and social well-being. The mesosystem highlighted the role of family, social groups, and community support. The exosystem involved institutions such as retirement offices, healthcare providers, and media in shaping retirees’ experiences. The macrosystem reflected societal norms, economic policies, and cultural expectations influencing adjustment. Finally, the chronosystem captured the impact of life transitions, technological changes, and evolving social roles on long-term adaptation.ConclusionsFew studies using ecological systems theories, such as Bronfenbrenner’s model, have explored the interrelationships of determinants at different levels to improve retirees’ health, likely due to gaps in understanding the context and methodological approaches. This study paves the way for more systematic and operational research based on an ecological approach by examining the determinants of retirement adjustment and their interrelated relationships across levels.Keywords: Retirement adjustment, Psychological well-being, Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model, Older adults, Qualitative study, Social engagement

Background

The global decline in birth and fertility rates has contributed to a growing aging population, particularly among retirees [1]. This demographic shift is not only due to declining fertility rates but also stems from improved healthcare, decreased mortality, and longer life expectancy [2]. According to national statistics, Iran is experiencing rapid population aging, with projections indicating that more than 20% of the population will be over 60 years old by 2041. This demographic shift presents major challenges for healthcare, pension systems, and age-friendly infrastructure development [3]. Retirement represents a pivotal transition into older adulthood, where well-being during this phase plays a key role in shaping health and quality of life in later years [4]. Therefore, safeguarding and enhancing health in retirement is a critical public health priority to support a high quality of life in old age [5]. Developing effective policies and health promotion initiatives requires a thorough understanding of the target population’s needs, concerns, and challenges [6]. Gaining insights into their perceptions and lived experiences is essential for achieving this understanding [7].

Retirement involves a three-stage transition—pre-retirement, transition, and post-retirement [8]—during which individuals face substantial changes in personal (physical, mental, and spiritual), financial, and social dimensions [9]. These shifts may challenge retirees’ ability to adapt and maintain their desired health and quality of life in later years [10]. Retirement adjustment refers to the process of adapting to these changes and is defined as an individual’s psychological ease and satisfaction with retirement life [11, 12].

While many retirees navigate this transition smoothly, preserving their personal and social functioning [13], others encounter difficulties that can lead to reduced performance [14] and health problems that diminish life satisfaction [15]. The impact of retirement on health and lifestyle varies: some studies report positive outcomes such as improved self-control, increased physical activity, and more frequent social engagement [10, 13], while others associate retirement with negative outcomes like smoking, inactivity, obesity [16], depression [17], social isolation, and even suicide [18]. These differences may be influenced by demographic, socioeconomic, occupational, and contextual factors.

In Iran, public sector employees—especially in the Ministries of Education and Petroleum—are covered by institutional retirement systems that provide pensions and post-retirement support. These state-funded systems offer healthcare access and limited welfare services [19]. Retirement centers affiliated with these ministries typically provide recreational, cultural, and limited medical services, though their availability and quality vary by institution and region. Compared to countries such as the UK, where retirees benefit from a wider and more integrated array of public and private support mechanisms—including housing assistance, community-based health and social care, and local engagement programs [20, 21]. Although the types of services provided in Iran and Western countries may be similar in form, there are often substantial differences in their quantity, quality, and accessibility [19, 22].

In recent years, Iran has also introduced policy reforms in response to population aging and labor force decline. A new law has increased the statutory retirement age, and individuals with higher education (master’s degree or higher) may extend their working years [23]. At the same time, economic recession and the widening gap between income and expenses after retirement have led many retirees to seek secondary employment, reflecting a form of “unretiring” to supplement household income [24]. These developments highlight the policy and economic backdrop in which retirement experiences unfold.

However, research on retirement adjustment and related challenges in Iran remains limited. For example, Alavi et al. examined psychosocial issues among retirees in the education sector using a qualitative approach [25]. Another study explored key adjustment factors among retired nurses [26]. A quantitative study by Kaveh et al. found a positive correlation between retirement resources and adjustment, emphasizing the role of financial and mental resources [27].

Several factors are thought to influence adjustment to retirement and overall well-being, including personal attributes (e.g., mental competence, physical health), pre- and post-retirement resources (e.g., finances, social networks), coping mechanisms, and situational variables [28]. Wang and Hesketh (2012) conceptualized well-being as a multidimensional construct encompassing fiscal, physical, social, and psychological dimensions [29]. The adjustment process is often complex, with studies reporting diverse outcomes—positive, negative, or neutral [30–32]—depending on the availability of resources and individual context. For instance, pre-retirement physical health and access to social and financial resources have been consistently linked to positive adjustment outcomes [15, 33, 34]. Gender-related roles and differences in work and family experiences also shape retirement transitions differently for men and women [35]. As Van Solinge and Henkens noted, retirement satisfaction is largely determined by health, finances, and social relationships [11].

The diverse health needs and varying adjustment experiences during retirement underscore the importance of community-based participatory research guided by a strong theoretical foundation. Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model offers such a framework by capturing the dynamic interplay of personal, social, cultural, economic, and political factors that shape health and well-being [36–38]. It enables a comprehensive understanding of how individuals interact with multiple layers of their environment when facing life transitions such as retirement [39]. The model outlines five interrelated systems—microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, macrosystem, and chronosystem—each reflecting a level of influence on individual outcomes [40]. By addressing these interconnected levels, the model supports a holistic perspective that incorporates both individual needs and broader contextual influences, from family and social networks to institutional and cultural structures [41]. Guided by Bronfenbrenner’s ecological theory, this study aims to investigate the challenges and needs of retirees in Iran and identify the factors that influence retirement adjustment.

Two key research questions were as follows

-What are the needs and challenges during retirement, as perceived or experienced by Informants?

-What are the needs and challenges during retirement, as perceived or experienced by Informants?

-What factors in multiple levels(based on Bronfenbrenner’s model) influence retirement adjustment, as perceived by Informants?

-What factors in multiple levels(based on Bronfenbrenner’s model) influence retirement adjustment, as perceived by Informants?

Methods

Study design

This investigation utilized a directed qualitative content analysis framework as delineated by Graneheim and Lundman. This methodological approach has the potential to enhance the robustness of qualitative data analysis, facilitate the comparative analysis of findings across diverse studies, and produce actionable outcomes [42]. To ensure comprehensive reporting of the study findings, the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines were adhered to [43].

Study settings and population

We selected retirement centers in Shiraz, the third most populous city in southern Iran, as the setting for this study. The research focused on retirees affiliated with the Ministry of Education and the Ministry of Petroleum, two of the country’s largest public sector employers. The Ministry of Education is the largest in terms of workforce size nationwide [44], while the Ministry of Petroleum, ranked among the top six, has a significant concentration of employees in Fars Province.

Shiraz is a preferred post-retirement destination for many petroleum sector employees who previously worked in the hotter southern regions of Iran, due to its more temperate climate and urban amenities. This made it a practical location for accessing a large and diverse group of retirees from both sectors.

Each ministry operates its structured retirement system with dedicated service centers and administrative bodies. These differences in organizational structure and coverage provided valuable variation in the types of services, retirement experiences, and adjustment challenges faced by retirees—making them suitable for inclusion in this ecological study.

Sampling and participant

This study employed a purposive sampling strategy, supported by theoretical sampling, to ensure diversity in participant perspectives and conceptual depth. Participants included retirees, family members (especially spouses), and employees of retirement organizations—key informants selected for their ability to provide rich insight into the processes and challenges of retirement adjustment.

Purposive sampling was used to recruit informants with diverse characteristics—such as age, gender, type of retirement, and institutional affiliation—to maximize variation and capture a broad range of experiences relevant to the research questions [45]. This strategy is widely used in qualitative research to identify those who can meaningfully contribute to in-depth analysis.

In addition, we used theoretical sampling, meaning that ongoing data analysis informed the selection of additional informants and refinement of follow-up questions, based on emerging themes and conceptual gaps [46]. This iterative approach allowed the research to evolve organically and ensured that participant selection remained grounded in the evolving needs of the study.

To ensure ethical recruitment, we did not contact participants directly. Instead, a designated staff member at each retirement center acted as a gatekeeper, referring individuals who met our inclusion criteria. These gatekeepers shared information about the study via phone or mobile-based platforms, distributed invitations, and referred those who consented to participate to the research team for scheduling.

All informants received detailed information about the study’s purpose, confidentiality measures, and their right to withdraw at any time. Written informed consent was obtained before interviews. Interviews were conducted in private rooms within the retirement centers, ensuring a comfortable and confidential setting.

We monitored data saturation continuously throughout the study, which was reached when no new themes or codes emerged during analysis. Saturation occurred with 42 participants (see Table 1), consistent with methodological guidance suggesting 15–25 interviews are typically sufficient for semi-structured qualitative studies [47, 48].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for selecting key informants were: having at least a high school diploma, the ability to communicate effectively in a way that provides relevant answers to the research questions, expressing consent and signing an informed consent form to participate in the study, and having at least two years of retirement experience or work experience in a retirement center/office (for retirees and employees).

Regarding educational levels, national data indicate that approximately 21% of retirees in Iran have less than a high school diploma (https://www.mizanonline.ir/fa/news/4576722/). However, based on an unpublished report by administrative experts and a review of retiree records in the study setting, this proportion is significantly lower among retirees in the education and oil sectors—estimated at less than 1% and 5%, respectively. Retirees with lower education levels typically retired many years ago, are now over 75 years of age, and are difficult to access for in-depth interviews. Therefore, a high school diploma or higher was set as an inclusion criterion to ensure relevance, accessibility, and the quality of data collection.

Individuals who provided highly incomplete or irrelevant information or withdrew from participation were excluded from the study.

Data collection

Semi-structured (face-to-face) in-depth interviews (lasting 30–60 min) were conducted by the researcher (first author), under the supervision of the research team. Additionally, to complement the information gathered from these interviews, two sessions of focused group discussions (each lasting 1 h and 30 min) were facilitated by the researchers (first author and corresponding author). These discussions were guided by a set of predetermined questions or topics aimed at ensuring systematic and comprehensive data collection while maintaining focus on the research objectives [48]. Both focus group discussions and individual interviews allow participants to express themselves in their own words, facilitating a deeper understanding of their perspectives [49].

Two separate guides were utilized for conducting the interviews and focus groups, which are available as appendices in the study protocol article [50]. Interviews were recorded only with participants’ consent and subsequently transcribed verbatim. It’s worth noting that the interviews were conducted in Persian and later translated into English by a bilingual translator for publication purposes.

Data analysis

The qualitative data were analyzed using the content analysis method described by Graneheim and Lundman [51]. The first author audio-recorded all interviews and transcribed them verbatim in Persian. The transcripts were carefully read several times to achieve immersion and a holistic understanding of participants’ perspectives. Meaning units were then identified, condensed, and coded.

The first and second authors independently identified initial codes and organized them into categories and subcategories. They then compared these results and discussed any discrepancies until reaching a consensus. The final decision was made in collaboration with the broader research team, especially when agreement was difficult. This iterative process helped ensure the rigor, reflexivity, and credibility of the coding framework [52].

Although Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model informed the overall analytic lens, the coding itself was inductive and data-driven. Themes were first derived directly from participants’ narratives and then interpreted within the ecological model to highlight how experiences of retirement adjustment are shaped across different environmental levels.

Data trustworthiness

Lincoln and Guba proposed four criteria of Credibility, Dependability, Confirmability, and Transferability as criteria for scientific accuracy in qualitative research [52].

Credibility

Participants from diverse backgrounds - retirees, family members, and retirement center agents - were carefully selected, ensuring gender, age, and retirement history diversity. Sufficient time was dedicated to data collection and analysis, fostering a comprehensive understanding. Disagreements among the research team were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. Researchers engaged in iterative data collection and analysis, and interviewees validated findings.

Dependability

Data reliability was ensured through a thorough review of interview texts and immersion in the data, following a consistent analysis procedure.

Confirmability

Verifiability was maintained through meticulous documentation at all stages and consultation with knowledgeable individuals in the research field.

Transferability

Research details, including demographic characteristics, study platform, participant selection, data collection methods, and analysis process, were elucidated to facilitate replication. The research team sought consultation with other qualitative research experts when necessary.

Results

A total of 42 people participated in this study, including 33 retirees, 4 family members, and 5 employees working in retirement centers. Of the 50 individuals initially approached, 8 were excluded—5 due to lack of relevant experience and 3 due to withdrawal before or during interviews. The mean age of the participants was 66, most of them (81%) were male, and more than half (52.4%) had higher education. The participants were on average in 7(SD: 4.1) years of retirement (range:1–15 years) (Table 1).

In response to the first central research question, we identified participants’ needs and challenges across four major thematic categories: financial management, preserving health and well-being, sustaining familial and societal bonds, and seeking existential meaning. Participants also described their efforts to address these needs using personal strategies and by interacting with their immediate environment. These adaptive responses often occurred within what Bronfenbrenner’s model describes as the microsystem, where individuals directly engage with family members, peers, and local structures such as community centers. In some cases, these interactions extended to the mesosystem, reflecting the links among multiple microsystems, such as between family and neighborhood-based institutions. Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model helped to frame and interpret these findings and illustrate how different layers of the environment influence retirement adjustment.

Managing finances during retirement was a central concern, even for participants who had previously planned for this transition. One retiree explained, “My past experiences taught me to plan financially, but I still feel unprepared for the rising healthcare costs” (Participant 11, retired, 66 years old, male, 7 years of retirement). Familial disconnect, stemming from years of work-related absences, was also evident: “I struggle to maintain proper verbal and emotional connections with my wife and children. Because I was often away from them during the years of working, it feels like we’re strangers” (Participant 6, retired, 69 years old, male, 8 years of retirement).

Spiritual practices provided resilience for some, with one noting, “My daily prayer and reflection habits give me the strength to face new challenges in retirement” (Participant 19, retired, 70 years old, female, 10 years of retirement). Others viewed retirement as a time for introspection: “Retirement is a phase where one can introspect, to understand one’s origin, purpose, and destiny. In the words of Hazrat Rumi: ‘Where did I come from? What was my purpose? Where am I headed? Show me not my homeland” (Participant 3, retired, 68 years old, male, 5 years of retirement). Positive lifestyle changes also emerged, as one retiree shared, “I’ve become healthier since retiring because I have more free time to go to the mountains, hike, eat healthy food, and spend time with my family” (Participant 15, retired, 65 years old, male, 4 years of retirement). However, for others, the lack of structure led to aimlessness: “Ever since I retired, I wake up aimlessly in the morning and don’t know how to spend my time until night” (Participant 41, retired, 63 years old, male, 2 years of retirement). Societal undervaluation was another challenge: “People in society don’t value me like before when I was going to work. They don’t value me and my abilities; they look at me like a disabled person” (Participant 29, retired, 67 years old, male, 7 years of retirement).

In response to the second central research question, participants described the roles and interactions of peer groups and existing social structures—such as family networks, mosques, cultural centers, and recreational clubs—in meeting retirees’ evolving needs. These local and immediate environments were often seen as potential sources of emotional, social, and even spiritual support. As one retiree suggested, “Local community centers like cultural centers and mosques could collaborate to plan for retirees’ leisure time” (Participant 22, retired, 60 years old, male, 7 years of retirement). Another emphasized the emotional value of religious spaces: “The mosque is more than a place of prayer—where I find companionship and shared understanding” (Participant 14, retired, 66 years old, male, 9 years of retirement). Organized activities were appreciated but underutilized. One retiree suggested, “We should form groups and come together to uplift our spirits, like book clubs, storytelling, and poetry reading groups. Fortunately, our retirement center has suitable spaces for such gatherings, but many retirees may not be aware, or we need to educate them to embrace these activities” (Participant 35, retired, 62 years old, female, 3 years of retirement).

Family members confirmed these gaps. As one daughter shared, “There are programs available, but my father didn’t know about them until I helped him register” (Participant 36, family member, 40 years old, female). Retirees also emphasized their capacity to contribute: “Retirees are repositories of knowledge and experience, having spent our lives in various industries. Now that we are retired, we can impart knowledge to the younger workforce. Opportunities should be provided for us to engage with these young individuals” (Participant 32, retired, 69 years old, male, 11 years of retirement).

Other participants’ comments pointed to the roles of larger social organizations in urban environments that support retirees directly or through collaboration. As one retirement center employee noted, “Many retirees don’t access what’s available—we need more proactive engagement” (Participant 44, retirement center staff, 52 years old, female). Another employee added, “We’re seeing more retirees asking for digital literacy training—this trend will grow” (Participant 45, retirement center staff, 55 years old, male).

Access to health care was another key institutional concern. As explained by a retirement center staff member reflecting on retiree needs: “Access to regular check-ups has been crucial for managing my health, but clinic waiting times can be frustrating” (Participant 26, retirement center staff, 50 years old, female). Similarly, a staff member noted, “The retirement office helped me with my pension paperwork, but I still struggle to understand all the policies—they don’t explain things clearly” (Participant 13, retirement center staff, 57 years old, male). Another remarked, “We only hear from them when there’s an issue—why not regular updates on programs or services we could benefit from?” (Participant 20, retirement center staff, 54 years old, female). Family members also expressed concern: “Now that we are retired, our age has increased and we face various diseases, but we should not worry about medical expenses. Insurance should support us more at this age with these conditions” (Participant 5, family member/spouse, 63 years old, female).

Urban systems also shaped daily experiences. As a family member explained, “I stopped going out as much because crossing busy streets is risky, and many buildings don’t have elevators” (Participant 18, family member/daughter, 42 years old, female). The media’s influence was another recurring issue. “There’s too much negative news—it would help if programs focused on retirees, showcasing success stories or providing practical advice” (Participant 24, retired, 64 years old, male, 6 years of retirement).

Participants also referred to broader systems that affect retirement adaptation. One retiree shared, “Government policies on pensions don’t account for inflation, making it harder to make ends meet” (Participant 2, retired, 70 years old, male, 12 years of retirement). Another stated, “The lack of long-term planning in national retirement schemes means we’re always worried about whether our benefits will keep pace with rising costs” (Participant 4, retired, 65 years old, female, 8 years of retirement). Economic instability compounded these issues: “Rising prices and economic instability have made it difficult to plan for the future” (Participant 16, retired, 65 years old, female, 8 years of retirement). Cultural norms added burdens: “In our culture, children expect us to support them in every way at this age, while they should become independent and stand on their own feet” (Participant 8, retired, 68 years old, male, 10 years of retirement). Another noted, “The traditional view that older parents should always be a fallback for their adult children often leaves us feeling overburdened when we should focus on our well-being” (Participant 28, retired, 67 years old, male, 7 years of retirement).

Participants also referred to changes in needs over time, consistent with Bronfenbrenner’s chronosystem. “I was very confused at the beginning of retirement, but over time I was able to cope with the conditions of retirement” (Participant 11, retired, 66 years old, male, 7 years of retirement). Technology played a transformative role: “Keeping up with new technology has been both a challenge and a blessing. While it’s hard to learn, using smartphones and social media helps me stay connected with my children and friends” (Participant 15, retired, 64 years old, female, 7 years of retirement). Another participant highlighted its convenience: “Previously, it was necessary to go in person to do everything, but today, with the provision of new communication and information technologies, it is possible to do many things without going in person” (Participant 21, retired, 65 years old, male, 6 years of retirement). The need for lifelong learning was emphasized: “We must update ourselves through education. Keeping up with these changes is necessary and empowering” (Participant 5, family member/spouse, 63 years old, female).

The findings showed interactions both within and across the ecological levels. One retiree noted that retirement organizations (exosystem) could facilitate access to neighborhood services (microsystem): “If retirement organizations signed agreements with local health centers, we wouldn’t need to travel far for services” (Participant 1, retired, 67 years old, male, 8 years of retirement). Another stated, “We have to travel a long distance to our organization to participate in training courses, which is time-consuming and expensive for us. They can provide some training for retirees through cultural centers in the local community after agreeing” (Participant 1, retired, 67 years old, male, 8 years of retirement).

Discussion

This study explored retirement adjustment in Iran using Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model, revealing an intricate network of personal, interpersonal, institutional, and societal influences shaping retirees’ adaptation experiences. The findings highlight four core areas of need and challenges: financial management, health and well-being, familial and social connectedness, and existential meaning. While similar themes are observed in Western contexts [8, 9]. Differences emerge in how these needs are perceived, prioritized, and addressed due to cultural, structural, and policy factors [3, 15].

At the microsystem level, participants voiced concerns about financial insecurity, self-care, emotional regulation, and maintaining social identity. Financial distress, a commonly cited factor in both Western and Eastern studies [4, 11], appeared heightened due to economic instability and limited pension adjustments in Iran [19]. Health maintenance was not only viewed in biomedical terms but also intertwined with cultural values on independence and dignity [5, 10]. Many also emphasized spiritual coping and existential reflection—a dimension particularly strong in this context, shaped by religious and philosophical traditions [53].

These shared themes align with global findings [33, 54], yet diverge in content and urgency. For instance, while retirees in many Western contexts may prioritize leisure and lifestyle design [55]. Participants in this study emphasized basic financial survival, access to primary healthcare, and spiritual continuity. Such contrasts underscore the importance of culturally contextualized interventions [56, 57].

The mesosystem findings reflect the central role of families, peers, religious spaces, and local centers in shaping retirement experiences. Consistent with international evidence, social support is a buffer against isolation and psychological decline [58, 59]. However, the expectations of multigenerational reciprocity, religious community involvement, and informal caregiving norms carry unique weight [3, 60]. Participants valued mosques and cultural centers as sources of connection and meaning, yet noted that many of these institutions lacked targeted, inclusive programming [61].This is in line with previous studies showing that social engagement in later life depends on both personal and structural factors [58].

At the exosystem level, the roles of retirement organizations, healthcare services, and local governments were mixed. Participants described inconsistent service delivery, administrative inefficiencies, and limited communication—barriers also documented in middle-income settings [6, 62]. For example, they shared difficulties with insurance access, long clinic wait times, and uncoordinated social support schemes. These findings mirror structural deficits in other rapidly aging societies [63, 64].

In terms of physical environments, participants encountered significant age-unfriendly features: low benches, dangerous street crossings, and inaccessible buildings. These findings reinforce global calls for inclusive urban design [61, 65]. Inactivity and disengagement linked to unsafe public spaces echo the importance of environmental safety for health and participation in later life [1, 7].

Macrosystem-level data revealed policy shortcomings and cultural expectations that affect retirement adaptation. Many criticized inadequate pension schemes and inflation-insensitive benefits [3, 20]. Cultural pressures to support adult children, while being seen as passive or burdensome by society, contributed to psychological strain [66, 67]. These narratives highlight how aging policies, economic planning, and societal values converge to shape lived experiences [9, 14].

The chronosystem findings emphasized adjustment as a temporal, evolving process. Many described confusion and disorientation early on, followed by gradual adaptation through digital inclusion, community participation, and personal growth.

Interestingly, the data also suggested differences between those recently retired and those with longer retirement experience. Newly retired individuals often reported uncertainty, identity loss, and heightened financial anxiety, whereas those further along in the transition described gradual coping, new routines, and greater reliance on social and community resources.

This indicates that interactions with ecological systems may shift over time—for example, the microsystem (family) may be more critical in the early stages of retirement, while the exosystem (institutions and services) becomes more salient as retirees gain experience navigating support structures.

Digital tools were also reported—depending on users’ literacy and access—both as enablers and as barriers, a pattern also found in the studies by Sen et al. and Lythreatis et al. [68, 69]. Taken together, these findings support the conceptualization of retirement as a developmental transition rather than a single event [28, 32].

Overall, this study confirms Bronfenbrenner’s premise that development is nested within interconnected systems [36, 38]. However, few retirement studies have operationalized this framework comprehensively, particularly in non-Western contexts [39, 40]. The present context, with its complex interplay of policy gaps, cultural richness, and institutional fragmentation, offers a compelling case for using ecological models in planning interventions.

Although Bronfenbrenner’s model provides a useful framework, applying it to later life involves methodological challenges. Theoretical sampling and multi-actor interviews helped capture layered perspectives, yet further research is needed to refine analytical tools for mapping dynamic inter-system interactions [37, 42].

Strengths and limitations

This study employs Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model, offering a comprehensive and systematic framework to examine the multi-layered determinants of retirement adjustment. By including retirees, family members, and retirement professionals, the study provides a diverse and holistic perspective on retirement experiences. The use of rigorous qualitative content analysis ensures credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability through systematic coding and thematic analysis. The findings have practical implications for policymakers, retirement organizations, and healthcare professionals, guiding the development of targeted interventions to support retirees.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in an urban setting and focused on retirees affiliated with formal institutions. In Iran, as in many Western countries, a large portion of public sector retirees reside in cities, whereas rural retirees—many of whom are self-employed or previously worked in agriculture and animal husbandry—are typically served by the Social Security Insurance Organization and have more limited access to structured retirement services, healthcare, and welfare support. Thus, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of retirees in rural areas, and generalizing the results to this population should be done cautiously. Second, although a small proportion of retirees in the two education and the petroleum sectors had less than a high school diploma, generalizing the findings of this study should be done with caution. Third, the sample was predominantly male (81%), potentially underrepresenting the needs and views of female retirees. Fourth, as with most qualitative research, the findings are not statistically generalizable, though they provide in-depth, context-rich insights. Finally, self-reported data may have been influenced by recall or social desirability bias.

Conclusion

Few studies have applied the Bronfenbrenner model to different populations, leaving a gap in operational and methodological evidence for designing effective health promotion interventions for retirees. Additionally, the application of Ecological Systems Theory (EST) presents challenges, particularly in untangling interwoven relationships across systems. Additionally, measuring the impact of interventions at different levels requires multidisciplinary and transdisciplinary collaboration, demanding expertise from diverse fields. Most existing interventions remain static and fragmented, focusing primarily on intra-individual and, to a lesser extent, interpersonal factors.

This study highlights the complex nature of retirement adjustment and the need for tailored empowerment programs that address financial security, health maintenance, social engagement, and existential fulfillment. Based on Bronfenbrenner’s model, retirement adjustment determinants interact at intra- and inter-levels, evolving. This underscores the importance of ecological and dynamic approaches in developing retirement health promotion programs.

Our findings provide concrete examples of these connections, offering valuable guidance for health promotion planners. A comprehensive, multi-level strategy is essential to ensure holistic and sustainable support for retirees. Further research is needed to clarify methodological approaches and derive practical guidelines for designing and implementing interventions that target the interaction of influential structures at different levels. Future research should also assess the effectiveness of integrated interventions, enhancing retirement adaptation and overall quality of life.
