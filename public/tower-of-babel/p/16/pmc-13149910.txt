Proteomic profiling reveals the mechanism of ginsenoside synthesis in Panax notoginseng
Authors: Zheng-Wei Liang, Shi-Cheng Tang, Yan Ma, Teng Wang, Zheng Lv, Yan-Hui Guan, Jun-Wen Chen, Ming Zhao, Sheng-Chao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPanax notoginseng, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, holds significant historical and clinical value due to its ability to promote blood circulation, alleviate blood stasis, and provide neuroprotective effects. The primary bioactive constituents of this herb are P. notoginseng saponins (PNS). However, the mechanisms underlying the biosynthesis and regulation of PNS remain inadequately understood.MethodsThe contents of notoginsenoside R1, ginsenosides Rg1, Re, Rb1, and Rd in P. notoginseng roots were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography. The protein composition of P. notoginseng roots was digested and labeled by tandem mass tags, and high pH reverse phase separation, analyzed using low pH nano high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (pH nano-HPLC-MS/MS) analysis system. Key protein data and regulatory pathways in the roots of P. notoginseng were analyzed to identify the essential enzymes and their corresponding genes involved in ginsenoside synthesis.ResultsA total of 7578 proteins were identified, among which 252 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were characterized. Specifically, 155 were found to be downregulated, while 93 were upregulated in the comparison of high to low (H/L) ginsenoside content. The DEPs were primarily associated with biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components, as classified by Gene Ontology. Additionally, Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes pathway enrichment analysis revealed a significant involvement of the ribosomal pathway. Notably, 19 enzymes identified among the DEPs are linked to ginsenoside biosynthesis.ConclusionsThis study analyzed the differences in the content of five major ginsenosides in P. notoginseng roots and identified the key proteins (enzymes) involved in regulating ginsenoside synthesis. The findings highlight the regulatory roles of the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) and UDP-glycosyltransferase (UGT) enzyme families in the biosynthetic pathways of secondary metabolites, providing valuable insights into the biosynthesis of the active medicinal components in P. notoginseng.

Introduction

Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F. H. Chen, a traditional Chinese medicine, has a long history of significant clinical value, particularly due to the use of its dried rhizomes as herbal medicine [1]. It is regarded as one of the most well-known Chinese medicinal herbs, characterized by its warm, sweet, and slightly bitter properties [2]. In China, Notoginseng Radix, the dried roots of P. notoginseng, have been traditionally used to eliminate stasis, reduce bleeding and swelling, and alleviate pain [3]. Notoginseng Radix has been used for over 430 years, with its earliest documentation found in the Ben Cao Gang Mu (⟪本草纲目⟫Compendium of Materia Medica) during the Ming dynasty [4]. Modern pharmacological research have demonstrated that P. notoginseng exhibits various biological activities, such as anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidation, regulation of blood glucose and blood pressure, and neuronal protection [5], and the main bioacive ingredients in P. notoginseng are triterpenoid saponins (PNS) [6].

To date, a variety of active chemical components have been isolated from P. notoginseng, including saponins, flavonoids, cyclopeptides, sterols, polyacetylenes, volatile oil, amino acids, and polysaccharides [7]. Triterpenoid saponins are the key active components, which have a wide range of pharmacological activities in the cardiovascular systems, they also show anti-diabetic, anti-viral, anti-cancer, antioxidant, and other beneficial effects [8,9]. Ginsenosides are important triterpenoid saponins biosynthesized from 2,3-oxidosqualene using the universal precursors dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP) and isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) [10]. Generally, two pathways contribute to biosynthesis of universal precursors, namely, the mevalonate pathway (MVA) in cytosol [6] and the methylerythritol phosphate pathway (MEP) in plastid [11], which can coordinate with each other to synthesize a variety of metabolites. The biosynthesis of ginsenosides involves three primary processes: the formation of ginsenoside skeletons [12], the synthesis of sugar donors [13], and the skeletons modification processes [14]. The upstream pathways and key enzymes of ginsenoside biosynthesis in P. notoginseng have been fully studied, such as squalene epoxidases (SEs) [15], oxidosqualene cyclases (OSCs) [16,17], and dammarenediol synthase (DDs) [18,19], which play an important role in the formation of ginsenoside skeleton [6]. However, there is still a lack of research on the key enzymes and associated proteins in the downstream pathway of saponin biosynthesis in P. notoginseng.

Researchers have reported a high-quality reference genome for P. notoginseng, characterized by a genome size of approximately 2.66 Gb and a contig N50 of 1.12 Mb [20]. They assessed the implications for ginsenoside biosynthesis, and a comprehensive transcriptional analysis of P. notoginseng was conducted to elucidate the gene-level mechanisms governing the formation of its distinctive tubercles [20]. However, due to the universality of life phenomena such as transcriptional regulation [21], translational regulation, and post-translational modification [22], it is not possible to systematically and comprehensively reveal complex biological activities solely from the perspective of the genome or transcriptome. Recent advancements in proteomics have significantly contributed to the analysis and identification of proteins associated with the ginsenoside biosynthetic pathway. This field has proven instrumental in elucidating the functional roles of proteins within the medicinal plant Panax [23,24]. The metabolites of P. notoginseng roots play a crucial role in its pharmacological effects and include ginsenosides, organic acids, esters, polysaccharides, amino acids, sterols, and flavonoids [25]. According to traditional medicinal knowledge, the optimal harvest period for P. notoginseng roots spans from sowing until the end of the third year, during which triterpenoid saponins, such as ginsenoside Rg1, Re, Rb1, Rd, and notoginsenoside R1, accumulate significantly. Transcriptome analysis has shown that PnPHL8 specifically binds to the promoters of PnGAP, PnCYP716A47, and PnGGPPS3, promoting the rapid accumulation of ginsenoside Rb1 [26].

In this study, innovatively applies quantitative proteomic analysis using isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantification by tandem mass tags (TMT) labeling to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) involved in the downstream pathway of saponin biosynthesis in P. notoginseng, addressing a gap left by previous genomic and transcriptomic studies. By focusing on protein-level regulation, this research provides new insights into key enzymes and associated proteins in ginsenoside biosynthesis, offering valuable data to improve the quality and production of pharmacologically active saponins in P. notoginseng.

To date, proteomic profiling of P. notoginseng roots has been limited to global protein expression analyses, with no systematic investigation specifically targeting the downstream saponin biosynthetic pathway at the protein level. This study represents the first comprehensive proteomic analysis aimed at identifying key enzymes, such as CYP450s and UGTs, as well as regulatory proteins, including auxin influx carriers, that are specifically involved in the terminal steps of ginsenoside synthesis.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

P. notoginseng roots samples were collected from Yanshan County, Yunnan Province (23°39′ 60″N, 104° 27′ 48″E). Briefly, a total of one hundred samples of P. notoginseng roots were randomly selected for the experiment, all of which were three years old. The root tissue from each plant was collected, cleaned, quickly sliced, and evenly divided into two equal portions. Each portion was labeled and placed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube. One portion was stored in a liquid nitrogen tank, while the other was placed in a ziplock bag and kept in a 60 °C oven until it reached a constant weight.

Ginsenosides content of P. notoginseng roots

The dried roots of P. notoginseng were ground into a fine powder. A total of 30 mg of the sample was extracted with 25 mL of 70% methanol for 30 min, with shaking occurring every 10 min, followed by ultrasonication for an additional 30 min. The extraction was then filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane for analysis. Chromatographic experiments were conducted using an Agilent 1200 series high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Agilent, USA). The separation was performed at 25 °C on an Agilent Poroshell C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) with a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. The injection volume was 10 μL, and the detection wavelength was set at 203 nm. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (acetonitrile) and solvent B (water). The gradient was applied as follows: from 0 to 20 min, 20% A; from 20 to 45 min, 20% to 46% A; from 45 to 55 min, 46% to 55% A; and from 55 to 60 min, 55% A. The identification and quantification of five ginsenosides including notoginsenoside R1, ginsenosides Rg1, Re, Rb1 and Rd were reference components. Based on the content of five major ginsenosides in P. notoginseng roots, normality and lognormality tests were performed. The Anderson-Darling test, D'Agostino-Pearson test, Shapiro-Wilk test, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and nonlinear fitting of the histogram for the total content of the five ginsenosides were performed to select the most representative P. notoginseng roots samples for proteomic analysis. These samples were pooled and categorized into two groups: the low (L) group and the high (H) group. From these groups, a total of six samples-L1, L2, L3, and H1, H2, H3-were collected. The results are presented as histograms that conform to a normal distribution. Notoginsenoside R1, ginsenosides Rg1, Re, Rb1, and Rd were purchased from Chengdu DeSiTe Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China).

Protein extraction, enzymatic hydrolysis and TMT labelling

The P. notoginseng roots samples were rapidly ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen. The resulting powder was subsequently extracted with 2 mL of lysate composed of 8M urea, 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and a 1 × Protease Inhibitor Cocktail. The mixture was sonicated on ice for 10 min and then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a new tube, to which five volumes of 0.1 M pre-cooled acetone were added. This mixture was then incubated −20 °C overnight centrifuged subjected to centrifugation 13,000 rpm for an additional 10 min. The precipitation was cleaned three times with 50% ethanol and 50% acetone. The 50 μg pellet was reconstituted with 100 μL denaturant (8 M urea in 0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH = 8.0) and 11 μL of 1 M DTT shaking 1 h at 37 °C. The protein solutions were alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide for 20 min at room temperature in darkness, and 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) was used as exchange buffer. Then sequence-grade modified trypsin (Promega, WI, USA) was added for digestion. The peptide mixture were labeled according to the TMT kit (Pierce Biotechnology, IL, USA) instructions and then freeze-dried under vacuum.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Before mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, the tryptic peptides were fractionated into fractions by high pH using Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo Fisher scientific, MA, USA) connected to a reverse phase column (XBridge C18 column, 4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). The linear gradient for the peptide was established at 5% to 45% B, where B represents a solution of 20 mM ammonium formate in 80% acetonitrile (ACN) at pH 10.0, adjusted with ammonium hydroxide. A total of 12 fractions were collected over a duration of 40 min. Each fractionated sample was subsequently dried completely using a speed vacuum.

The peptides were solubilized in 30 μL of solvent C, which consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and subsequently analyzed using a nano-electrospray ionization liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (nano-ESI-LC-MS/MS) system equipped with a C18 column (Thermo Scientific Acclaim PepMap C18, 100 μm × 2 cm). The sample size was 5 μL and the flow rate was set at 10 μL/min for 3 min, followed by linear gradient separation 2%D (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) to 40%D for 100 min on the analysis column (C18, 75 μm × 15 cm). The analysis column was equilibrated for 15 min under initial conditions. The flow rate was 300 nL/min, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the mass spectrum inlet voltage was set at 1.9 kV.

Data search and identification criteria of P. notoginseng roots protein

Raw data from tandem mass spectrometry were imported into Mascot software (Matrix Science, London, UK; version 2.5.1). The selected database was the P. notoginseng transcriptome database of the laboratory research group, and version 4.7.2 of Scaffold software was used to correct the identified peptides and proteins. Peptides with less than 1.0% false discovery rate (FDR) and proteins containing at least 2 peptides were screened by Scaffold software.

Statistical analysis

The Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation of proteins by utilizing STRING v10 (www.string-db.org), and the metabolic pathways will be identified by using KOBAS 2.0 (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn). The statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.3. The data are presented as the mean ± SD, unless otherwise specified. The Pearson correlation analysis was employed to identify significant relationships between the content of ginsenosides and key enzymes, as well as associated proteins (p < 0.05). Each experiment is conducted in parallel twice, with each parallel being repeated three times.

Results and discussion

Content of five ginsenosides in P. notoginseng roots

The contents of notoginsenoside R1 (1), ginsenosides Rg1 (2), Re (3), Rb1 (4) and Rd (5) in 100 samples of P. notoginseng roots were detected by HPLC, and they exhibited great variation among individual plants (Fig. 1A). The contents of notoginsenoside R1 (1) and ginsenosides Rg1 (2), Re (3), Rb1 (4), and Rd (5) ranged from 0.22% to 0.67%, 1.07% to 8.58%, 0.00% to 3.25%, 1.27% to 10.27%, and 0.32% to 2.33%, respectively. The coefficients of variation (CV) for these five ginsenosides were 72.09%, 34.35%, 70.07%, 41.39%, and 40.09%, respectively. Additionally, the average contents of notoginsenoside R1, ginsenosides Rg1, Re, Rb1, and Rd were 1.58% ± 1.14%, 4.46% ± 1.53%, 0.74% ± 0.52%, 4.93% ± 2.04%, and 1.11% ± 0.44% respectively. The total content of these five ginsenosides was 12.80% ± 3.65% (Fig. 1B). Previous research has reported that the concentrations of notoginsenoside R1 (1), ginsenosides Rg1 (2), Re (3), Rb1 (4), and Rd (5) in the roots of P. notoginseng range from 0.59% to 3.58% for notoginsenoside R1, 1.34% to 3.82% for ginsenoside Rg1, 0.08% to 0.85% for ginsenoside Re, 1.26% to 5.35% for ginsenoside Rb1, and 0.25% to 1.19% for ginsenoside Rd [27], respectively. The total concentrations of the five ginsenosides in P. notoginseng roots range from 5.98% to 16.54% [28]. Protein profiling

In this experiment, a total of 468,777 spectra were detected from the H and L group P. notoginseng roots samples, among which 140,834 spectra were quantified. Subsequently, 7578 proteins were identified by these spectra, with a spectral identification rate of 30%. Here, PCA was used to analyze the differential proteins in the samples from the two H and L groups. The combined variance explained by PC1 and PC2 was 70.10%, exceeding the 68% confidence interval. This indicates significant differences in protein expression between the two groups, with a high degree of separation (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, Partial least squares discrimination analysis (PLS-DA) indicated a significant separation in the relative protein content between the H and L groups of P. notoginseng roots samples, demonstrating notable differences (Figure 2B). The analysis, which employed a fold-change threshold greater than 1.5 and a p-value less than 0.05, identified a total of 248 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in the H/L comparison. Among these, 155 proteins were significantly down-regulated, including wound-induced basic protein (Unigene0034927), protein FAM135B-like (Unigene0010666), adenylate kinase 1, chloroplastic-like (Unigene0013521), and gypsy retrotransposon integrase-like protein 1-like (Unigene0010733). Conversely, 93 proteins were significantly up-regulated, including minus strand tumor-related protein (Unigene0012188), pathogenesis-related protein 2 (Unigene0003687), cysteine protease (Unigene0019376), and auxin influx carrier protein (Unigene0002907), among others (Fig. 2C–Table S1).Fig. 2Analysis of differential proteins in root samples of Panax notoginseng from two groups, H and L. Finally, hierarchical analysis of differential protein clustering (HCA) revealed significant differences between H and L groups. In the H group of P. notoginseng roots, 62.5% of the differential proteins exhibited downregulation, including auxin influx carrier protein (Unigene0002907), xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase (Unigene0015440), and ripening-related protein-like (Unigene0012884) etc. The auxin influx carrier proteins play a critical role in plant growth, with each member of the AUX/LAX family functioning as an active auxin influx carrier that mediates auxin-related developmental processes across various organs and tissues [29]. Specifically, AUX1 plays a critical role in the regulation of root gravitropism, root hair formation, and leaf phyllotaxy. Structurally, AUX1 is oriented with its intracellular domain exposed and consists of 11 transmembrane helices, displaying the characteristic LeuT-fold architecture [30]. Additionally, Both AUX1 and LAX3 have been associated with lateral root development and apical hook formation [31]. This protein mediates the inward transport of auxin and influences plant growth by responding to the proton concentration gradient. Research has demonstrated that xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase are integral to plant morphogenesis, as they mediate responses to diverse plant hormones and environmental stimuli [32], thereby modulating plant growth, development, and stress resistance. Notably, ripening-associated genes such as ACS2, ACS4, ACO1, PSY1, PL, RIN, and NOR exhibited increased expression levels in SlLHP1b-RNAi fruits throughout the ripening process, whereas their expression was diminished in SlLHP1b-OE fruits [33]. Thus, the ripening-related protein-like factor serves as a crucial regulatory function during the late developmental stages of plants, particularly at maturity.

Besides, there are 37.5% of the differential proteins were upregulated, comprising dehydroquinate dehydratase (Unigene0032289), cytochrome C-1 (Unigene0009815), 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (Unigene0023987) in the P. notoginseng roots (Fig. 2D). As previously reported, dehydroquinate dehydratase catalyzes the conversion of 3-dehydroquinic acid to 3-dehydroshikimic acid during the intermediate phase of the shikimate pathway [34], a critical process for the biosynthesis of aromatic amino acids and folates. In the P. notoginseng roots, the biosynthesis and metabolism of B vitamins, particularly folates, as well as the generation of aromatic components specific to Panax genus, are likely linked to this metabolic pathway. Besides, the levels of cytochrome C connect mitochondrial functionality to plant development and stress responses by modulating the activity of the sucrose nonfermenting 1-related protein kinase 1 (SnRK1) signaling pathway [35]. Thus, this regulation positively influences the stress response triggered by environmental changes during the growth of P. notoginseng and plays a crucial role in promoting the accumulation of secondary metabolites, such as triterpenoid saponins. 1-Aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate is a novel and potent chemoattractant for the plant-beneficial rhizobacterium Pseudomonas putida UW4 [36], which enhancing the enrichment of plant growth–promoting rhizobacteria in the rhizosphere and improving the medicinal quality of P. notoginseng.

In contrast to prior transcriptomic investigations that primarily concentrated on upstream pathway genes, including SEs, OSCs, and DDS, our proteomic methodology distinctly elucidates post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms within the downstream pathway. Notably, we identified differential expressions of auxin influx carrier proteins, exemplified by Unigene0002907, as well as ripening-associated proteins such as Unigene0012884, in roots exhibiting elevated ginsenoside levels. These findings imply a hitherto unrecognized connection between plant hormone signaling and secondary metabolic processes.

Differentially expressed proteins analysis

GO enrichment of differentially expressed proteins

Based on the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), Gene Ontology classification and enrichment analysis were conducted in this experiment, with a false discovery rate of less than 0.05 (Fig. 3A). In the biological process category, DEPs mainly enriched into isoprenoid biosynthetic process (10 DEPs, p-value = 5.62 × 10−7), single-organism biosynthetic process (38 DEPs, p-value = 1.78 × 10 −6), single-organism metabolic process (85 DEPs, p-value = 1.99 × 10−6) and metabolic process (147 DEPs, p-value = 3.10 × 10−6). In molecular function, DEPs mainly enriched into structural constituent of ribosome (16 DEPs, p-value = 1.60 × 10−6), structural molecule activity (16 DEPs, p-value = 3.07 × 10−5), dimethylallyltranstransferase activity (3 DEPs, p-value = 4.20 × 10−5) and catalytic activity (133 DEPs, p-value = 1.47 × 10−4). In terms of cellular component, DEPs enriched in cytoplasmic part (66 DEPs, p-value = 3.73 × 10−2), nuclear nucleosome (4 DEPs, p-value = 4.81 × 10−7), ribosome (20 DEPs, p-value = 3.83 × 10−6), ribosomal subunit (12 DEPs, p-value = 3.59 × 10−6) and cytosolic ribosome (11 DEPs, p-value = 7.36 × 10−6) (Fig. 3A–Table S2).Fig. 3GO enrichment (A) and KEGG enrichment (B) analysis of DEPs.Fig. 3

For the CYP716 family, cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP716A53v2 catalyzes the formation of protopanaxatriol from protopanaxadiol during ginsenoside biosynthesis in P. ginseng [37,38]. These results suggest that metabolic processes and catalytic functions within the cytoplasm play a significant role in the difference of ginsenoside content in P. notoginseng roots. They also demonstrate that the catalytic enzymes and regulatory proteins involved in ginsenoside synthesis are primarily localized in the cytoplasm. The observed enrichment of the category 'structural constituent of ribosome' (comprising 16 differentially expressed proteins) suggests a regulatory mechanism at the translational level affecting key enzymes, such as CYP716A53v2, in roots exhibiting elevated ginsenoside content. This observation is consistent with our findings that ribosomal proteins are co-localized with genes involved in terpenoid biosynthesis within specific subcellular compartments.

KEGG pathway enrichment of DEPs

Herein, a total of 248 DEPs were further investigated using the Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) database. The DEPs identified in the KEGG pathway were predominantly enriched in terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, ribosomal function, and carbon fixation processes in photosynthetic organisms. Among the 248 DEPs, the enrichment pathway of DEPs obtained in the ribosome is the most abundant (Fig. 3B). In accordance with the findings related to P. ginseng, the pathway exhibiting the highest frequency of protein involvement was identified as ribosome and carbon metabolism [23]. In Terpenoid backbone biosynthesis pathway, precursor (S)-2,3-epoxysqualene is involved in the biosynthesis of dammarenediol II synthase (DDS), dammarenediol 12-hydroxylase (CYP716A47), ginsenoside 20-O-glucosyltransferase (UGTPg1), and protopanaxadiol-type ginsenoside 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UGT74AE2). Ginsenoside Rd is synthesized through the catalytic action of protopanaxadiol-type ginsenoside 3-O-glucoside 2-O-glucosyltransferase (UGT94Q2). Subsequently, ginsenoside Rb1 is further synthesized under the catalysis of UDP-glucose [6]. This was succeeded by pathways associated with the biosynthesis and metabolism of amino acids, which aligns with the results obtained from GO analysis.

Additionally, we identified seven Acetyl-CoA-related pathways directly linked to ginsenoside synthesis. These include glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), starch and sucrose metabolism, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms (CFPO), pyruvate metabolism, the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), and propanoate metabolism. Notably, PPP and CFPO pathways are essential as they provide the necessary carbon chain backbone and NADPH for ginsenoside synthesis, thereby significantly influencing the accumulation of ginsenosides [39]. Furthermore, three pathways associated with the downstream processes of terpenoid backbone synthesis were found to be enriched. These include steroid biosynthesis, sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, as well as monoterpenoid biosynthesis.

The elevated expression levels of PPP enzymes, such as glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH) and 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (6PGDH), observed in roots with high ginsenoside content, imply an augmented production of NADPH to support terpenoid reduction reactions. Concurrently, the upregulation of CFPO enzymes, including ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO) and sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphatase (SBPase), indicates that carbon fixation via photosynthesis is preferentially directed towards ginsenoside biosynthesis during periods of active growth.

Differential expression enzymes involved in ginsenoside skeleton formation

This experiment focused on the analysis of the terpenoid backbone biosynthesis pathway, which is the most relevant to ginsenoside synthesis, and redrew the pathway based on DEPs (Fig. 4). In the mevalonate (MVA) pathway, we only identified one DEP, while in the deoxyxylulose 5-phosphate/methylerythritol 4-phosphate (MEP/DOXP) pathway, five DEPs were identified and all were down-regulated, indicating that P. notoginseng roots is rarely synthesized by the MEP/DOXP pathway, completed mainly by the MVA pathway. The observed downregulation of enzymes involved in the MEP pathway, such as DXS and HDR, within roots exhibiting elevated ginsenoside levels, indicates a metabolic flux shift favoring the MVA pathway. In P. notoginseng roots, the de novo synthesis of isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl pyrophosphate (DMAPP) occurs through two distinct pathways: the traditional acetate/mevalonate pathway within the cytosol and the pyruvate/glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate pathway located in the plastid [40]. These two pathways commonly form IPP, which then reacted into farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) [11]. FPP should have been catalyzed by squalene synthase to form squalene; however, in this experiment, we did not observe any significant differential expression of key enzymes such as squalene synthase (SS), squalene epoxidase (SE), and dammarenediol-Ⅱ synthase (DS). It is speculated that the samples with high ginsenoside content may be in a phase of slower ginsenoside synthesis. Additionally, we hypothesize that the terpenoid synthesis precursors in the samples with low ginsenoside content may be utilized more in the metabolic pathways of other terpenes, resulting in a decrease in the content of triterpenoid ginsenosides.

UDP-glycosyltransferase involved in ginsenoside biosynthesis

Glycosyltransferases constitute a substantial protein family; however, this study focused exclusively on three glycosyltransferases derived from DEPs, including Unigene0023403, Unigene0005717 and Unigene0044620 (Table S4). UDP-glucose flavonoid-3-O-glycosyltransferase 6 (Unigene0023403) mainly catalyzes the glycosylation reaction on the oxygen atom 3 of anthocyanins, converting anthocyanidins to anthocyanins, thereby increasing the stability of anthocyanins and decreasing their reactivity [45]. UGT94E5 (β-D-glucose crocetin β-1,6-glucotransferase like, Unigene0044620) is mainly manifested by quercetin 3-O-glucosyltransferase activity and quercetin 7-O-glucosyltransferase activity, which is mainly involved in the glycosylation reaction in the flavonoid metabolic pathway [46]. Moreover, there is a glycosyltransferase UGT3 (Unigene0005717) found in Bupleurum chinensis that is involved in flavonoid metabolic pathways [47]. Our previous research found that the total saponin content in purple-green roots of P. notoginseng was significantly higher than in pure yellow roots [48]. Additionally, variations in the expression levels of GmCYP93A1 and GmCYP93A2 significantly influence the isoflavonoid metabolic pathway in soybeans [43]. In this experiment, the purple pigment of P. notoginseng root was identified as a flavonoid. Meanwhile, the glycosyltransferases associated with flavonoid synthesis in the high ginsenoside samples were down-regulated, indicating that the site of ginsenoside synthesis was not located in the taproot at this time.

Correlation analysis between DEPs and ginsenoside content

In this experiment, Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the expression of DEPs and the content of five ginsenosides. The results indicated that notoginseng R1 was significantly positively correlated with 3,9-dihydroxypterocarpan 6A-monooxygenase (CYP93A1). The study demonstrated that the strong induction of CYP93A1 expression in mechanically damaged soybean stems indicates that the CYP93A1 enzyme plays a crucial role in the defense response [49]. This role is closely associated with the synthesis of notoginsenoside R1, a defensive secondary metabolite. The positive correlation between CYP93A1 (r = 0.72) and notoginsenoside R1 hints at its potential role in defense-related ginsenoside biosynthesis (Fig. S1). This aligns with previous reports linking jasmonic acid signaling to triterpenoid accumulation in plants, opening avenues for elicitor-based production strategies.

Additionally, ginsenoside Rg1 showed significant correlations with 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol-2,4-cyclic phosphate synthetase (MCS), HMBPP synthetase (HDS), Acetyl-CoA C-acetyltransferase (AACT), Geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate synthase 2 (GGPS2), heading date repressor 1 (HDR1), CYP450, and cytochrome P450 monooxygenase. Furthermore, ginsenoside Re was significantly correlated with CYP93A1, CYP72A219, and cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (Fig. S1). In addition, through the correlation analysis of DEPs and ginsenosides, we found that among the DEPs related to terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, six exhibited a highly significant negative correlation. These include MCS, CDP-ME kinase (CMK), HDS, Acyl coenzyme A-cholesterol acyltransferase (ACAT), GGPS2, and HDR1. Among the six differentially expressed CYP450 enzymes, five exhibited significant correlations. This included negatively correlated CYP450 and positively correlated CYP82D47, CYP93A1, CYP72A19, and CYP450 monooxygenase. Among the three differentially expressed glycosyltransferases, two showed significant negative correlations: UDP-glucose-flavonoid-3-O-glycosyltransferase and glycosyltransferase UGT3.

Limitations of the study

This study investigated the protein regulation of five major ginsenosides in P. notoginseng roots and found that CYP450 enzymes play a negative role in ginsenoside synthesis. Specifically, CYP82D47, CYP93A1, CYP72A19, and CYP450 monooxygenases promote the synthesis and accumulation of ginsenosides in P. notoginseng roots, a finding partially supported by previous research [49]. However, the synthesis of plant secondary metabolites is influenced by environmental and polygenic factors. Due to limitations in the authors' resources, this study lacked verification experiments for key regulatory proteins. Future research will integrate transcriptomics, qPCR validation, protein fluorescence quantification, and other techniques to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of key genes and proteins involved in ginsenoside biosynthesis in P. notoginseng roots. These efforts will contribute to the biological breeding of P. notoginseng, an important medicinal plant.

Conclusion

In this research, we conducted a proteomic analysis on two distinct groups of P. notoginseng roots that exhibited notable variations in ginsenoside content. Our primary objective was to investigate the key enzymes involved in the downstream pathways of ginsenoside biosynthesis in P. notoginseng roots. Our research indicates that the content of ginsenosides is associated with the expression and regulation of cytochrome P450 enzymes and uridine diphosphate-glucuronosyltransferases. Furthermore, it appears that certain CYPs and UGTs may not be directly involved in the ginsenoside biosynthetic pathway; rather, they may play an indirect role in ginsenoside synthesis. Specifically, enzymes such as MCS, CMK, HDS, ACAT, GGPS2, HDR1, CYP82D47, CYP93A1, CYP72A19, as well as CPY450 monooxygenase, UDP-glucose-flavonoid-3-O-glycosyltransferase, and glycosyltransferase UGT3 contribute to elucidating the mechanisms underlying ginsenoside biosynthesis in P. notoginseng roots. Our identification of CYP716A53v2 and UGT94Q2 as key regulators of ginsenoside skeleton modification and glycosylation provides actionable targets for metabolic engineering. For instance, overexpressing CYP716A53v2 in engineered yeast could enhance protopanaxatriol production. Additionally, the correlation between auxin influx carriers Unigene0002907 and ginsenoside accumulation suggests auxin signaling as a novel breeding marker for selecting high-yield cultivars.
