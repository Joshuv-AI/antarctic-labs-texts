Community-based, supervised, group exercise for people living with chronic conditions and multimorbidity: a cohort study
Authors: Muireann O’Shea, Paul Doody, Noel McCaffrey, Aodhaghan O’Deashunaigh, Fiona Skelly, Lisa Kelly, Sean P Kennelly, Noel McCarthy, Susan M Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExercise is a safe, effective intervention for improving health-related outcomes in people living with chronic conditions and multimorbidity. ExWell Medical is a community-based, medically designed group exercise programme for people living with chronic conditions in Ireland.AimsWe aimed to evaluate the longitudinal association of ExWell with health-related outcomes and to explore the association of these outcomes with programme adherence.MethodsWe employed a prospective cohort study design. All participants attending an ExWell Medical induction in the Dublin/Kildare regions January–September 2023 were invited to participate. At baseline, 12 and 24 weeks we collected data on socio-demographics; health status, health behaviours; measures of physical function, patient-reported outcomes.ResultsOf the 2219 patients referred to ExWell, 1364 attended an induction and 1289 agreed to participate, mean age 69 years (SD: 10.90 CI: 68.84–70.06), 60% female, living with a mean of 3.29 (SD: 2.17 CI: 3.17–3.40) chronic conditions. 725 (56%) completed assessments at the primary endpoint (24 weeks). There were significant improvements in body mass index, gait speed, a range of timed tests, health related quality of life, self-efficacy, stages of adopting exercise, a modest reduction in alcohol consumption, no significant change in smoking, an increase in physical activity but not sufficient to meet current guidelines, with programme adherence significantly associated with better outcomes. There were no improvements in grip strength, fear of falling, pain, smoking prevalence.ConclusionParticipation in the ExWell programme was associated with significant improvements in some health-related outcomes in community-dwelling adults living with one or more chronic conditions.

Introduction

The prevalence of chronic conditions, illnesses or disabilities of long duration increases with age. It is anticipated that by 2030 chronic conditions will account for 57% of global disease burden and 69% of deaths [1]. The World Health Organization has identified noncommunicable diseases as the leading cause of morbidity and mortality burden globally [2, 3] recognizing the substantial impact of inadequate physical activity on morbidity and premature mortality [3].

Multimorbidity is the presence of two or more chronic conditions in an individual; prevalence increases with age, and is higher in females [4]. Multimorbidity is associated with increased disability [5, 6], mortality [7], functional decline [8], reduced health related quality of life [9], psychological distress [5, 10] and polypharmacy [11–13].

Physical inactivity is a modifiable risk factor for many chronic conditions and multimorbidity. [14–16]. The WHO recognizes the significant contribution of inadequate physical activity to global chronic condition burden, premature morbidity and mortality [3, 17] and has clear guidelines on recommended physical activity levels for health [17].

Community based group exercise programmes are a safe [18] and effective mode of delivery for exercise rehabilitation [18, 19] allowing for their delivery to be moved from secondary to primary care settings.

Although exercise interventions have been shown to be effective in improving health outcomes and quality of life in adults with a range of different chronic conditions [20] and multimorbidity [16, 21, 22], there is still a need for further research focusing on exercise interventions addressing multimorbidity [23, 24]. Recent systematic reviews of exercise and multimorbidity suggest that gaps remain in examining the feasibility of multimorbidity rehabilitation programmes, the effect of programme frequency and the outcomes achieved [16, 23]. Recommendations have been made that future focus should include interventions targeting health behaviours [24], and that health systems widen their available exercise programmes to include those with multimorbidity [23].

The ExWell programme is a not for profit, community-based exercise rehabilitation (CBER) programme for adults with chronic conditions and multimorbidity. This study aims to evaluate the association between a preexisting CBER programme (ExWell) and participant’s health behaviours, physical function; and to explore these outcomes based on programme uptake and adherence.

Methods

Study design

This was a prospective cohort study of the ExWell CBER programme, reported using the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist (Supplementary Data Appendix 1) [25]. ExWell is a medically designed, community-based exercise rehabilitation programme for individuals living with chronic conditions and multimorbidity in Ireland. During the study, ExWell classes were funded by the Health Service Executive of Ireland. Ethical approval was obtained from Trinity College Dublin’s Research Ethics Committee (reference 221002). This paper reports the quantitative findings relating to participant health.

ExWell intervention

The intervention was a 24-week, medically designed, twice weekly, community-based supervised group exercise programme located in community fitness centres. Intended flow of study participants for this research project is illustrated in Figure 1. Detailed intervention description is included in Supplementary Data Appendix 4.

Intended flow of study participants.

Setting and sampling

Potential participants were referred to an ExWell class within the study area by a healthcare professional, were contacted by an administrator, and invited to attend an induction session. All patients who were referred to ExWell and attended an induction in the study area between 30 January and 22 September 2023 were invited to participate. Potential participants were provided with a link to a participant information leaflet prior to attending their scheduled induction session. Participation in the study was voluntary and those declining to join the research project continued to participate in the ExWell programme. All participants provided written informed consent.

Population

Eligible participants were aged >18 years, living with at least one diagnosed chronic condition and referred to ExWell by a healthcare professional. We excluded those unable to participate in the programme due to disabling physical conditions (e.g. uncontrolled cardiac conditions) or with mental health conditions, cognitive impairments, or language restrictions that restricted communication or capacity to consent.

Data collection

Data were collected at baseline (induction session), 12 and 24 weeks (reassessment sessions) by trained members of the ExWell team. Each participant was provided with a paper booklet with a questionnaire that was designed by the study team to collect the baseline data (Supplementary Data Appendix 5). Blinding of outcome assessors was not possible. Participants were invited to complete assessments regardless of how many classes they had attended. Study participants no longer attending the ExWell programme were invited by phone call from the study team to return for a specific 24-week reassessment session. For those unable to attend, telephone interviews were conducted to complete patient reported outcome measures (PROMs) where possible and capture reasons for non-attendance. Participant recruitment, retention and individual participant class attendance were also recorded.

Participant characteristics

A range of participant characteristics were recorded at baseline including age, sex, marital status, number and type of chronic conditions, healthcare services attended, smoking and alcohol use, physical activity levels, physical measures and patient reported outcome measures (PROMs). Frailty status was recorded by trained clinicians using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) [26]. Smoking and alcohol use, measures of physical function and PROMs were reassessed after 12 and 24 weeks.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline demographic details of enrolled participants. Summary statistics were calculated for each outcome measure at baseline, 12 weeks and 24 weeks for all participants. Paired t-tests were utilized to compare means for normally distributed data, Wilcoxon signed rank test for not normally distributed data and McNemar’s chi square test for paired nominal data. Analyses were performed between baseline and 12 weeks to assess for early intervention impact. The primary analysis of the impact of the programme at the pre-defined study endpoint (24 weeks) was conducted as a complete case analysis and included the participants with data available at both time points. Last observation carried forward analysis (LOCF) was completed on select variables to assess the effect of missing longitudinal data on the results (Supplementary Data Appendix 3 3.2.4–3.2.5).

Multiple regression analyses were conducted to assess whether age and intervention adherence (number of classes attended) were associated with improvements in the six-minute time trial, timed up and go and gait speed results. Ordinal logistic regression was conducted to evaluate if age and referral source were associated with programme uptake and class adherence. All statistical analyses were performed with STATA [34].

Results

Recruitment

From 30 January to 22 September 2023, 2219 patients were referred to ExWell; 1364 attended an induction session and were invited to participate, 1290 (95% of those invited) agreed to participate, of whom 807 (63%) were followed up at 12 weeks and 725 (56%) at 24 weeks. Study recruitment and retention is presented in Figure 2.

Baseline characteristics

Demographic and baseline health behaviour data is presented in Table 1. Of the 1290 participants enrolled in the study mean age was 69 (SD:10.90) years; 60% were female and 58% married. The mean number of healthcare services attended was 1.6 (SD:1.44) and mean number of chronic conditions was 3.3 (SD:2.17). Sixty-two percent of participants at baseline were assessed as being either very fit, well or managing well, 23% were vulnerable and 15% frail according to the CFS. The baseline characteristics of the 725 participants who completed 24-week assessments indicated that they were majority female, married and living with multimorbidity. Baseline characteristics of those lost to follow up are presented in Supplementary Data Appendix 3 3.1.1 and indicates that they were younger, had a higher number of conditions, a higher number of services attended, and were less physically active.

Health behaviours

There was a significant difference in reported physical activity levels between baseline and 24-week (P < .001) assessment. Further, there was a significant reduction in the percentage of participants who reported engaging in zero days of 30 minutes of moderate physical activity per week (26% at induction to 9% at 24-week reassessment (P < .001). However, there was no significant improvement in meeting physical activity guidelines of five days of greater than 30 minutes of moderate physical activity per week between baseline and 24 weeks. There was a modest reduction in reported alcohol intake (P = .01). No significant reductions were observed in smoking (P = .30).

Measures of physical function

There was a significant reduction in BMI from 30.2 kg/m2 (SD 6.58) to 29.8 kg/m2 (SD 6.21) at 24 weeks (P = .005). There were significant improvements at reassessment in the six-minute walk test (+45 m P < .001), 5 sit-to-stand test (−1.3 sec P < .001), 10 sit-to-stand test (−1.9 sec P < .001), timed up and go test (−0.52 sec P = .008), timed up and go dual task test (−1.2 sec P < .001), gait speed (+0.1 m/s P < .001) and gait speed dual task tests (+0.12 P < .001). All these measures showed a significant early intervention impact with improvements at 12 weeks which at 24 weeks were either maintained (5 sit-to-stand, timed up and go dual task, gait speed), further improved (ten sit to stand), or had a lesser (but still significant) improvement (six-minute walk test, timed up and go test, gait speed dual task). There was no significant change in handgrip strength (kg) at any timepoint.

Patient reported outcome measures

There was a significant increase at 24-week follow up in the EQVAS (+3.17 P < .001) and EQ5D5L index scores (+0.026 P = .002), the modified Stages of Adopting Exercise score (+1.27 P < .001), Stanford 6-item Self-Efficacy Score (+0.19 P = .01) and Fear of Falling Limiting Activity (−5.2% P = .006 x2 7.50). However, there was no statistically significant change in the Numeric Pain Scale Score, Short Falls Efficacy Scale (FES) score and Fear of Falling.

All measures that improved, except the Stanford Self Efficacy scale, showed a significant early intervention impact at 12 weeks and further improvements at 24 weeks.

Participants who did not complete the programme

Of the 1290 participants recruited, 565 (44%) disengaged from the programme (i.e. stopped attending classes); 31 of these agreed to attend for a 24 week reassessment. Reasons for disengagement will be reported in the process evaluation. An analysis comparing completers and noncompleters (see Supplementary Data Appendix 3 Table 5) indicated that non-completers did not have the improvements documented in the completer group.

Class attendance

The mean number of classes attended was 10 and 17 at 12 and 24 weeks respectively; 35% of participants (n = 444) attended eight classes or less over the 24 weeks and 8% of participants did not attend any classes after the induction session (n = 103). 32% of participants attended greater than 24 classes (see Supplementary Data Appendix 3 Table 9).

Regression analyses

Neither age nor sex was significantly associated with gait speed or timed up and go at follow up. Increasing age was significantly associated with a lower six-minute walk test distance at follow up. Adherence, measured by class count, was significantly associated with an improvement in the six-minute time trial (coeff 2.067 P < .001 AdjR2 = 0.087 CI:1.42–2.715), gait speed (B = 0.006 P < .001 AdjR2 = 0.037 CI: .004–.009) and timed up and go (B -0.042, P = .029 AdjR2 = 0.012 CI: −.079- -.004).

Ordinal logistic regression results demonstrated that participant’s being older in age at enrolment had a small but statistically significant effect on increasing class attendance (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03, P < .001 R2 = 0.0064). Class attendance was not associated with gender or number of chronic conditions.

Discussion

This cohort study found a positive health-related association of participation in the ExWell programme in those who attended following referral among community dwelling adults living with one or more chronic conditions, the majority of whom had multimorbidity. Improvements were observed in measures of physical function, quality of life, self-efficacy, exercise adoption and fear of falling limiting daily activities in those who completed the programme. These improvements were associated with programme adherence, measured by number of classes attended. There were no changes in measures of upper limb strength, overall fear of falling, or pain.

Our results are consistent with previous systematic reviews of exercise interventions both in single conditions and in multimorbidity [14, 15, 20, 21, 23, 24]. Our results are also consistent with emerging evidence from a recent randomised controlled trial (MOBILIZE) of a 12-week exercise and self-management programme targeting people with multimorbidity, which reported significant improvements in health-related quality of life (measured using the EQ5D5L), the six-minute walk test, the 30 second chair stand test, burden of illness, depression and the WHO disability assessment schedule [22].

Health related quality of life has been identified as a core outcome in assessing interventions targeting multimorbidity [35]. We found an improvement in health-related quality of life measured using both the EQ5D5L index score and the visual analogue scale (VAS) from baseline to 24 weeks. However, this improvement is below the agreed parameters for a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) [36], so the clinical significance of our results remains unclear. Nonetheless, previous multimorbidity interventions have failed to show any improvements in EQ5D so our results are encouraging [37]. In our study, the majority (79%), but not all, had multimorbidity. The heterogeneity of the sample may therefore have affected the MCID. By comparison, the recent MOBILIZE study reported a statistically significant improvement in EQ5D5L index score of +0.064 and VAS of 6.9 [22].

We also found a significant improvement in self-efficacy for chronic disease management, which has also been identified as a core outcome in multimorbidity research [35]. While this is a modest improvement, previous studies of interventions to improve self-efficacy in chronic disease have not demonstrated any significant improvements in self-efficacy [22, 24, 38]. Although the improvements in health-related quality of life and self-efficacy were small, even modest improvements in this group with chronic conditions/multimorbidity could be clinically significant and result in meaningful benefits in daily functioning.

Falls risk is a serious concern in ageing populations [39, 40] and, while we did not assess number of falls, our participants reported a significant reduction in fear of falling limiting their daily activities. However, there was no significant improvement in overall fear of falling. Avoidance of activities due to fear of falling has important implications including reduced physical functioning, social isolation and reduced quality of life [41], an improvement in this parameter alone in the study population is encouraging.

Overall, the cohort became more physically active, but there was no increase in the number of participants achieving 150 minutes of moderate activity per week. This may imply the programme encouraged the least active participants to engage in a moderate level of activity, but not a sufficient amount to achieve compliance with national and international recommendations [42, 43]. This may reflect that these recommendations are challenging for older adults living with chronic conditions and multimorbidity.

The significant reduction in BMI from 30.2 to 29.8 at 24-week reassessment is also encouraging. While the improvement was modest and may not be considered clinically important at an individual level, it may be significant at a population level [46]. Additionally, this change was observed at 24 weeks and offers a potential trajectory for more substantial change supporting studies with longer term follow up of BMI following exercise interventions.

The results of our study showed significant improvements across a range of measures of physical function including gait speed, which has been described as the ‘6th vital sign’ [47] and is predictive of a range of outcomes including physical function, disability and mortality [48]. Gait speed improved by 0.1 m/s (P < .001) and significant improvements in morbidity and mortality have been reported with incremental gait speed improvements of 0.1 m/s in previous studies [48, 49]. Our results are also consistent with improvements in physical measures in the MOBILIZE trial [22].

As evidence continues to build for the effectiveness of exercise as an intervention for multiple chronic conditions, one of the key remaining questions is about recruitment and retention in these types of programmes. Recruitment to the study was successful, however, the attrition rate was substantial following enrolment; 44% of study participants did not proceed to complete the 24-week reassessment and 8% of study participants did not attend any ExWell class after the induction session. A systematic review of recruitment and retention rates for RCTs of exercise therapies in multimorbidity identified an average recruitment rate of 75% and retention of 90%, though this was in trial settings [50]. Regression analysis demonstrated that programme adherence was significantly associated with improved outcomes although the regression results with modest R2values indicate that additional factors beyond programme adherence likely played a significant role.

Strengths and limitations

The study was conducted within a preexisting CBER programme, so its findings reflect real world implementation and took account of the real-world logistical challenges involved in delivery of such a programme. It was conducted and reported in line with the STROBE reporting guidelines [25]. Recording of the number of classes attended by each participant provided insight into programme adherence and allowed for analysis of intervention exposure.

The lack of a control/usual care group is a limitation of the study and may lead to over estimation of results, a limitation common to all cohort studies. The substantial loss to follow up increases the risk of attrition bias and may limit the generalizability of the results. It is possible that those retained within the programme were the participants most motivated to improve their health. Comparison between complete case analysis and LOCF results demonstrated larger effect size in the complete case analysis by 22%–30%. This suggests that attrition impacted the magnitude of the effect size, however, the direction of effect was consistent across both analyses indicating that findings overall are robust.

Measures were taken to contact those who were lost to follow up to explore reasons for disengagement and to invite them to attend for reassessment. This provided us with data on a small group of study ‘noncompleters’. A further limitation relates to the lack of blinding of outcome assessors, which was not possible in this real-world setting and within our study budget. Inter rater and test–retest reliability were not formally assessed as part of the study however the outcome measures utilized are well validated and reliable measures [51–54].

Implications for policy and practice

This study demonstrates that participation in a CBER programme for individuals with chronic conditions/multimorbidity is associated with substantial improvements in measures of physical function and PROMs. It supports the evidence base for CBER programmes in the management of single chronic conditions [20] and addresses gaps in the evidence for exercise as an intervention to manage multimorbidity relating to programme feasibility, the effect of programme exposure and outcomes achieved [16, 23]. It provides information to support recent recommendations for future interventions to target health behaviours and for health systems to expand access to exercise programmes to include those with multimorbidity [55, 56], and may indicate a need to focus on strategies for engagement and return to programme. Our preliminary evidence justifies future research examining the clinical and cost effectiveness of large-scale CBER programmes for this population, ideally using individually randomised controlled trials including incorporation of implementation perspectives.

Conclusion

While evidence supports the efficacy of exercise in improving health outcomes in people living with various chronic conditions and multimorbidity, there is limited evidence regarding established real-world, community-based exercise programmes designed for people living with various chronic conditions and multimorbidity. This prospective observational cohort study provides evidence that such programmes are associated with improvements in physical and patient reported outcome measures in those attending outside the context of study specific interventions, but further work is needed to determine the definitive clinical and cost-effectiveness of such interventions.
