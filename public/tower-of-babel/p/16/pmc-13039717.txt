Enhanced Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer for engineering optimization problems
Authors: Haobo Wang, Zhentao Xin, Xuekui Qi, Qiang Zhou, Jiantao Liu, Zhe Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The design of efficient and robust metaheuristic algorithms remains a fundamental challenge in addressing complex optimization problems, particularly those involving high dimensionality, nonlinearity, and multimodal landscapes where traditional methods often struggle. To overcome these difficulties and enhance search effectiveness and adaptability, this paper proposes the Enhanced Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer (ERBMO), an improved variant of the recently introduced Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer (RBMO). ERBMO integrates three synergistic enhancement mechanisms: Diversity-adaptive weight updating, Periodic pattern search, and Evolutionary probabilistic combinatorial mutation. These components are specifically designed to strengthen population diversity, mitigate premature convergence, and achieve a dynamic balance between exploration and exploitation throughout the optimization process. Comprehensive evaluations demonstrate the superior performance of ERBMO. On the CEC2017 benchmark suite, ERBMO achieves the highest Friedman rankings across all tested dimensions, with average ranks of 1.667 (30D), 1.433 (50D), and 1.133 (100D), consistently outperforming the original RBMO which ranked second. On the CEC2022 benchmark suite, ERBMO again secures the top overall rankings for both 10D (1.833) and 20D (1.833), surpassing nine state-of-the-art algorithms including ESC and RBMO. Ablation study results confirm the effectiveness of each proposed strategy, as the complete ERBMO achieves superior Friedman rankings (2.282 for 10D, 2.140 for 20D) compared to variants with any single strategy removed. Furthermore, when compared against classical algorithms (PSO, DE, CMA-ES) and their advanced variants (SaDE, LSHADE) on CEC2022, ERBMO obtains the best overall rankings (3.550 for 10D, 3.089 for 20D). When applied to four real-world engineering design problems—speed reducer, pressure vessel, step-cone pulley, and hydrostatic thrust bearing—ERBMO consistently ranks first, achieving optimal or near-optimal solutions with superior robustness. The superior performance across both benchmark and practical problems highlights the effectiveness and reliability of the proposed improvements. This work presents a framework for engineering optimization and metaheuristic algorithm design. The source code of ERBMO is publicly available at: https://github.com/x5865/Enhanced-Red-billed-Blue-Magpie-Optimizer-ERBMO-.

Introduction

In the realm of science and engineering, optimization problems are ubiquitous, ranging from structural design and mechanical systems to energy management and industrial scheduling. These real-world challenges often exhibit high dimensionality, nonlinearity, multimodality, and constraint complexity, rendering traditional gradient-based and deterministic methods inefficient or inapplicable—particularly in rugged, discontinuous, or high-dimensional search spaces1. Consequently, there is a pressing need for intelligent optimizers capable of exploring complex design landscapes without relying on gradient information or extensive parameter tuning. This demand has catalyzed the rapid development of metaheuristic algorithms, which draw inspiration from natural phenomena, biological behaviors, and evolutionary processes to achieve global optimization with remarkable flexibility and resilience2–5.

Driven by the need for robust, assumption-free search, metaheuristics have evolved from simple stochastic operators into sophisticated, nature-inspired frameworks that coordinate population dynamics through adaptive exploration and exploitation strategies. Metaheuristic algorithms can be categorized into four types: evolutionary algorithms, swarm intelligence algorithms, physics-based algorithms, and human behavior-based algorithms. Evolutionary algorithms include Genetic Algorithms (GA)6, Differential Evolution (DE)7, Evolution Strategy with Covariance Matrix Adaptation (CMA-ES)8, and the recently emerged Graph Neural Evolution (GNE)9,10. Swarm intelligence algorithms include classic algorithms such as the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA)11, Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO)12, and Slime Mould Algorithm (SMA)13, as well as recently emerged ones like Bighorn Sheep Optimization14, and the Cape Lynx Optimizer15. Physics-based algorithms include RIME Optimization Algorithm(RIME)16, Fata Morgana Algorithm (FATA)17, Polar Lights Optimizer(PLO)18, and Wave Optics Optimizer(WOO)19. Human behavior-based algorithms include Teaching–learning-based Optimization (TLBO)20, Escape (ESC)21, and Love Evolution Algorithm (LEA)22. In addition to these, mathematically inspired algorithms can also be considered another category23,24. As a class of simple and easy-to-implement black-box optimization methods, metaheuristic algorithms are also employed to solve a wide range of real-world application problems, including engineering design optimization, photovoltaic parameter identification, UAV path planning, and signal processing25–31.

The No Free Lunch Theorem32 asserts that no single algorithm can universally outperform all others across all possible problem domains, implying that performance gains on one class of problems come at the expense of losses on another. This fundamental principle underscores the necessity of continuously refining metaheuristics to enhance their adaptability and robustness in complex scenarios. In this context, the Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer (RBMO)33, a nature-inspired algorithm based on the foraging behavior of Urocissa erythroryncha, has emerged as a promising heuristic. It has demonstrated competitive performance on benchmark suites such as CEC2014 and CEC2017, as well as in engineering design and UAV path planning. Since its introduction, RBMO has attracted growing research interest and has been applied to diverse real-world tasks, including high-efficiency solar thermal design, sales forecasting, energy supply-demand matching, trajectory tracking control for USVs and multi-target path planning28,34–37. To address these limitations and further enhance performance in demanding applications, this study proposes an Enhanced Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer (ERBMO), incorporating three core innovations:

Diversity-adaptive weight updating: A dynamic weight adjustment mechanism that balances exploration and exploitation based on both population diversity and iterative performance. This strategy promotes exploration when the algorithm’s improvement is insufficient or population diversity is high, and encourages more exploitation otherwise, thereby adaptively responding to stagnation risks.Periodic pattern search: A periodically activated search module that enhances algorithmic adaptability by switching between different search patterns. By introducing structured variation at regular intervals, it improves exploitation efficiency without compromising global exploration capability.Evolutionary probabilistic combinatorial mutation: A hybrid mutation strategy that combines Gaussian mutation and uniform mutation with dynamically adjusted mutation rates. Enhanced Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer

Original Red-billed Blue Magpie optimizer

The Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer (RBMO) is a metaheuristic algorithm inspired by the social behavior and foraging strategies of red-billed blue magpies. These birds exhibit sophisticated group behaviors, including cooperative foraging, adaptive predation, and food caching, which are abstracted into mathematical operators to solve complex optimization problems. The core philosophy of RBMO lies in balancing global search and exploitation through simulating the dynamic transition between small-group and large-group behaviors.

The operational framework of RBMO consists of four sequential phases: population initialization, food searching, prey attacking, and food caching.

The mathematical model of the proposed enhanced Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer

To address the potential limitations of the original RBMO, such as insufficient adaptability to complex optimization landscapes and over-reliance on iteration progress for parameter adjustment, three improved strategies are integrated into the algorithm framework. These strategies—diversity-adaptive weight updating, periodic pattern search, and evolutionary probabilistic combinatorial mutation—are designed to enhance the balance between exploration and exploitation, improve the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima, and strengthen its robustness in solving engineering optimization problems. Each strategy is mathematically modeled to complement the original RBMO’s mechanisms, as detailed in the following subsections.

Periodic pattern search

A critical challenge in metaheuristic algorithms lies in avoiding stagnation in local optima, which often stems from fixed search patterns that fail to adapt to the dynamic characteristics of complex optimization landscapes. In the original RBMO, the food searching and prey attacking phases rely on guidance from group-average positions, leading to a tendency for search agents to converge prematurely to local optimal regions. To address this limitation, a periodic pattern search strategy is proposed, which modifies the search mode at fixed iteration intervals to enhance the algorithm’s ability to explore unvisited regions and escape local optima.

This strategy is triggered periodically: specifically, it is executed when the current iteration count \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} satisfies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$tmod100=0 and t\ne0$$\end{document} (i.e., every 100 iterations). Its core logic consists of three sequential steps—exploratory moves, pattern moves, and step size adjustment—each mathematically modeled to adapt to the local landscape of the objective function, as elaborated below.

First, exploratory moves are designed to identify promising search directions through probing along the coordinate axes of the solution space. Evolutionary probabilistic combinatorial mutation

For complex optimization problems with multiple local optima, a single mutation strategy in metaheuristic algorithms often fails to provide sufficient diversity for comprehensive exploration of the solution space, leading to premature convergence to suboptimal regions. To address this limitation of the original RBMO, an evolutionary probabilistic combinatorial mutation strategy is proposed. This strategy synergistically combines Gaussian mutation and uniform mutation within a dynamically adjusted probabilistic framework, thereby balancing global exploration and local exploitation throughout the evolutionary process.

The mutation probability is adaptively increased over iterations to shift the search emphasis from exploration in the early stages to exploitation in the later stages. Specifically, the current mutation probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{current}$$\end{document} is updated as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{current}=min \left({p}_{initial}+\varDelta p \times \left(t-1\right),{p}_{max}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{initial}=\mathrm{0.1}$$\end{document} is the initial mutation rate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varDelta p=0.001$$\end{document} is the incremental step per generation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{max}=0.5$$\end{document} is the maximum allowable mutation rate, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} denotes the current iteration number. The increasing trend ensures a higher degree of randomness and diversity in the initial phase, while gradually focusing on fine-tuning promising regions as the algorithm converges.

For each individual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i$$\end{document} in the population, a random number is generated. If this value is less than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{current}$$\end{document}, a mutation operation is executed. Within the mutation process, Gaussian mutation is applied with probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{\mathrm{G}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{n}}=0.6$$\end{document}, defined as:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{i}^{new}={X}_{i}+\sigma \cdot rand n \left(1,D\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma=0.1$$\end{document} is the standard deviation, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$rand n\left(1,D\right)$$\end{document} generates a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D-dimensional$$\end{document} vector of random numbers drawn from a standard normal distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N\left(\mathrm{0,1}\right)$$\end{document}. Conversely, with probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-{p}_{\mathrm{G}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{n}}$$\end{document}, uniform mutation is performed:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{i}^{{new}} = X_{{\min }} + \left( {X_{{\max }} - X_{{\min }} } \right) \cdot rand\left( {1,D} \right)$$\end{document}

Experimental results analysis on benchmark functions

To validate the performance of the proposed ERBMO, extensive experiments are conducted on standardized benchmark suites, comparing ERBMO against several high-performing metaheuristic algorithms. The evaluation focuses on solution accuracy and robustness across optimization problems with diverse characteristics and varying dimensionalities. For this purpose, a rigorous statistical analysis is carried out using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test for pairwise comparisons and the Friedman mean rank test for overall algorithm ranking, ensuring an objective and comprehensive assessment of comparative performance.

Experimental setup

To evaluate the performance of the proposed ERBMO, two widely used benchmark suites, CEC 201744 and CEC 202245, are selected for experimental testing. These test functions are commonly adopted in the evolutionary computation community to assess the effectiveness of optimization algorithms. Experiments are conducted on CEC 2017 functions F1–F30 with dimensions 30, 50, and 100, and on CEC 2022 functions F1–F12 with dimensions 10 and 20. For each function, all algorithms are independently run 30 times with a maximum of 500 iterations per run to ensure consistent and comparable results.

ERBMO is compared with nine well-known or recently developed metaheuristic algorithms. These include Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO)46, Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA)11, Dung Beetle Optimizer (DBO)47, Young’s Double-Slit Experiment Optimizer (YDSE)48, Great Wall Construction Algorithm (GWCA)49, Newton-Raphson-based Optimizer (NBRO)50, Beluga Whale Optimization (BWO)51, Escape Algorithm (ESC)21, and the original Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer (RBMO)33. The parameter settings for all algorithms are listed in Table 4, using default or recommended values from their original studies to ensure a fair comparison. The parameters of ERBMO are empirically set during the tuning process. Among them, α, β, and γ are adaptive weights that control the update process of CF. A larger α tends to keep CF closer to its original value, a larger β increases the degree to which fitness improvement reduces CF, and a larger γ amplifies the increase of CF when population diversity improves. Additionally, larger values of δ, b, and a enhance the exploration capability of ERBMO during its periodic pattern search.

All experiments are implemented in MATLAB R2023a, running on a laptop equipped with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-12700 H processor (2.30 GHz) and 16 GB of RAM.

Experimental results analysis on the CEC2017 benchmark suite

Mean and variance analysis of the CEC2017 benchmark suite

The performance of the proposed ERBMO is evaluated against nine competing algorithms on the CEC 2017 benchmark suite across dimensions of 30, 50, and 100. The mean and standard deviation (Std) of the final fitness values, obtained from 30 independent runs with a maximum of 500 iterations per run, are recorded and presented in Tables 5, 6 and 7, respectively. The best result for each test instance is highlighted in bold.

Analysis of the mean values demonstrates the strong optimization capability of ERBMO. Specifically, ERBMO achieves the best mean performance on 16 out of 30 test functions in the 30-dimensional case. This advantage grows with increasing dimensionality: ERBMO obtains the top mean value on 21 functions at 50 dimensions and on 27 functions at 100 dimensions. This trend indicates that ERBMO scales effectively to higher-dimensional spaces and consistently locates high-quality solutions in complex and challenging search landscapes.

The standard deviation results further confirm the algorithm’s stability. As shown in Fig. 3, which displays boxplots of the final results for a representative subset of functions, ERBMO exhibits the narrowest interquartile ranges and the least dispersion of data points in most cases. This low variance across independent runs indicates that ERBMO produces highly stable outcomes, confirming its strong reliability and robustness.

Furthermore, convergence speed is assessed by comparing the fitness evolution curves in Fig. 4. The results show that ERBMO converges faster than the competing algorithms and consistently reaches superior solutions. In many cases, ERBMO approaches or achieves optimal performance within a relatively small number of iterations, demonstrating an efficient search mechanism. This rapid convergence is particularly beneficial for optimization tasks with limited computational budgets.

Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the CEC2017 benchmark suite

To rigorously assess the performance differences observed in Sect. 4.2.1, the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test, also known as the Mann-Whitney U test, is conducted at a 5% significance level52. This test evaluates whether ERBMO’s superior performance, as reflected in the mean fitness values, is significant when compared to each of the nine competing algorithms on the CEC 2017 benchmark functions in 30, 50, and 100 dimensions.

The test results are summarized in Table 8, with each entry formatted as +/−/=, indicating the number of functions for which ERBMO is significantly better (+), significantly worse (−), or statistically equivalent (=) to the corresponding algorithm.

In the 30-dimensional case, ERBMO shows a significant advantage over HHO, WOA, DBO, YDSE, NBRO, and BWO on all 30 functions, with no instances of significantly worse performance. Compared to GWCA and ESC, ERBMO performs significantly better on 29 and 20 functions, respectively, while being significantly worse on only 1 and 3 functions. When compared to its predecessor RBMO, ERBMO achieves significantly better results on 13 functions and is outperformed on 16, with one function showing no significant difference. At this dimension, although ERBMO wins fewer times than RBMO, the difference between them is small. Meanwhile, the analysis in Sect. 4.2.3 shows that ERBMO still achieves a higher average Friedman ranking, demonstrating that it remains highly competitive at this dimension as well.

As the dimensionality increases, the superiority of ERBMO becomes increasingly evident. In the 50-dimensional scenario, ERBMO maintains strong performance against all competitors. Notably, it outperforms RBMO on 17 functions and is outperformed on 11, with two functions showing equivalent results—marking a shift toward improved competitiveness.

The performance advantage of ERBMO is most pronounced in the 100-dimensional case. ERBMO demonstrates near-universal superiority, achieving significantly better results on 25 out of 30 functions against ESC and 26 out of 30 against RBMO, with only 3 and 1 significantly worse outcomes, respectively. Against all other algorithms, ERBMO is significantly better on either all 30 functions or 29 (in the case of GWCA). Aggregating the results across all dimensions and functions, the overall comparison yields a total of 260/4/6(+/−/=). This result provides conclusive evidence that ERBMO outperforms the competing algorithms on the CEC 2017 benchmark suite, with its advantage growing substantially in high-dimensional search spaces.

The results of the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the CEC2022 benchmark suite are summarized in Table 12. The test compares the performance of ERBMO against nine competing algorithms across 12 functions in both 10 and 20 dimensions.

In the 20-dimensional case, ERBMO maintains strong performance, consistently outperforming HHO, WOA, DBO, YDSE, GWCA, NBRO, and BWO across nearly all functions. Compared to RBMO, ERBMO demonstrates superior performance on 8 functions, inferior performance on only 1, and equivalent results on 3. Against ESC, ERBMO is superior on 7 functions, inferior on 4, and equivalent on 1, reflecting a favorable outcome despite close competition.

Overall, ERBMO achieves better performance in 88 out of 108 comparisons in 10 dimensions and 97 out of 108 in 20 dimensions, with only 5 and 6 comparisons, respectively, showing inferior results. This can be attributed to the algorithm’s design, which emphasizes exploitation. While beneficial for complex landscapes, this strong exploitation bias may limit the algorithm’s exploratory capabilities. On lower-dimensional problems, where the function landscape is relatively simple, the reduced need for exploration curtails the algorithm’s opportunity for trial and error. This lack of exploration can increase the risk of premature convergence, thereby limiting ERBMO’s performance gains on these simpler functions.

Runtime analysis of different algorithms

In this section, we analyze the runtime comparison of ERBMO and its competitors in the CEC2022 experiments with dimensions of 10 and 20 in Sect. 4.3.

These results indicate that while the incorporation of multiple enhancement strategies inevitably increases computational cost, ERBMO maintains a reasonable trade-off between solution quality and computational efficiency, achieving state-of-the-art optimization performance at the expense of moderate additional runtime.

Applications in engineering optimization problems

In this section, ERBMO is applied to four engineering design optimization problems to evaluate its practical effectiveness. The selected problems include the weight minimization of a speed reducer62, pressure vessel design63, step-cone pulley design64, and hydrostatic thrust bearing design65, which are characterized by nonlinear objective functions, multiple inequality and equality constraints, and mixed variable types (continuous, discrete, and integer). The results are used to assess ERBMO’s ability to handle complex constraints and produce high-quality solutions in realistic engineering scenarios. This study employs a penalty term method to handle equality and inequality constraints.

Limitations

Although ERBMO demonstrates excellent performance, it still has some limitations. For instance, its superior performance comes at the cost of longer runtime. Additionally, the algorithm involves numerous parameters, making fine-tuning for specific problems relatively complex. On the other hand, when dealing with functions characterized by complex multimodal landscapes, ERBMO occasionally underperforms compared to RBMO and ESC, which can be attributed to excessive exploitation at the expense of sufficient exploration within its mechanism. To address these issues in future work, we plan to introduce strategies such as adaptive population decay to reduce runtime, conduct comprehensive parameter sensitivity analysis, and consider implementing adaptive parameter adjustment mechanisms to enhance the algorithm’s exploration capability.

Conclusion

This paper proposes an Enhanced Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer (ERBMO), an improved variant of the original RBMO. By integrating three key strategies—Diversity-Adaptive Weight Updating, Periodic Pattern Search, and Evolutionary Probabilistic Combinatorial Mutation—ERBMO aims to enhance solving performance for complex engineering optimization problems. The effectiveness of ERBMO is systematically validated through experiments on the CEC2017 and CEC2022 benchmark suites, as well as four classical engineering design problems.

First, in the experiments on the CEC2017 benchmark functions, ERBMO achieved the best average Friedman rankings across all tested dimensions (30D, 50D, and 100D), with scores of 1.667, 1.433, and 1.133, respectively, consistently outperforming the original RBMO which ranked second. This result clearly demonstrates the significant performance advantage of ERBMO over its base version and other compared algorithms such as HHO, WOA, and BWO. In the tests on the CEC2022 benchmark functions, ERBMO similarly exhibited excellent performance, obtaining the highest overall Friedman rankings for both the 10D and 20D problems (1.833 and 1.833). It not only surpassed advanced algorithms like ESC and RBMO but also secured the top position when compared with classical algorithms such as PSO, DE, and CMA-ES, and their advanced variants like SaDE and LSHADE. This further confirms the stable superiority of ERBMO across problems of varying dimensions.

Second, the results of the ablation study quantify the contribution of each improvement strategy. The experimental data show that the complete ERBMO achieved superior Friedman rankings on the CEC2022 10D and 20D problems (2.282 and 2.140) compared to all algorithm variants where a single strategy was removed (ERBMO-1, ERBMO-2, ERBMO-3). This strongly demonstrates that each of the three proposed strategies plays an indispensable and positive role in the overall performance of the algorithm.

Finally, when applied to four real-world engineering optimization problems with complex constraints, ERBMO consistently demonstrated superior optimization capabilities. For the Speed Reducer Design problem, ERBMO achieved the lowest mean cost (2.994E + 03). In the Pressure Vessel Design problem, ERBMO successfully found the global optimum (6.060E + 03) and exhibited the smallest standard deviation (5.693E + 01), indicating high solution accuracy and robustness. For the Step-Cone Pulley and Hydrostatic Thrust Bearing Design problems, ERBMO also obtained the best Friedman ranking among all compared algorithms. These results fully demonstrate the strong practical applicability and solution quality of ERBMO when handling complex, constrained real-world problems.

Future research will focus on enhancing the adaptability of ERBMO for a broader range of complex optimization scenarios. Key directions include: (1) Extending the algorithm to handle multi-objective and large-scale optimization problems to further assess its search behavior; (2) Exploring hybridization with local search or knowledge-based operators to strengthen its exploitation capability and accelerate convergence; and (3) Evaluating ERBMO’s performance in dynamic and constrained environments, particularly in emerging application domains such as renewable energy system design, smart manufacturing, and intelligent transportation systems, to comprehensively validate its robustness and practical potential in modern engineering contexts.
