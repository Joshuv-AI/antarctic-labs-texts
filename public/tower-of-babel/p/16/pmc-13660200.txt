Characterization of physical health variables across fall risk appraisal groups: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Chitra Banarjee, Kworweinski Lafontant, Jethro Raphael Suarez, Janet Lopez, Zengyan Wang, Carla Leinbach, Rui Xie, Ladda Thiamwong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFall risk appraisal (FRA) integrates perceived fall risk (levels of fear of falling) and physiological fall risk (static balance). Established literature has defined cut-offs for four groups (congruent, incongruent, irrational, and rational), but characterization of these groups is limited to separate studies evaluating physical activity, body composition, and fall history. This study aims to characterize physical health measures in a large sample of older adults and evaluate differences between each maladaptive group (congruent, incongruent, irrational) and the adaptive (rational) group.MethodsThe sample included 381 low-income older adults (age = 74.3 ± 7.1 years; 87.7% female) from diverse backgrounds. Age, body mass index (BMI), physical activity measured through accelerometry, dynamic balance measured through the Timed Up and Go test, lower limb power measured through the 30-second chair stand test, and handgrip strength were compared across groups distinguished by perceived and physiological fall risk.ResultsThe characterization of a large and diverse sample of low-income older adults revealed a majority with maladaptive FRA. Those in the congruent group presented with greater age, BMI, and decreased physical activity, dynamic balance, lower limb power, and handgrip strength. However, those with physiological deficits but low perceived fall risk (incongruent) outperformed in physical health measures than those without physiological deficits and high fear of falling (irrational). Older adults with greater fear of falling and better static balance (congruent) show worse physical health than older adults with less fear of falling and worse static balance.ConclusionsThis study examines the physical health of a large group of community-dwelling older adults based on differences in fear of falling and static balance. The results reveal the greater significance of fear of falling in affecting physical health than the presence of physiological balance deficits. It further validates that FRA differentiates older adults with differences in physiological physical health and reveals the significance of applying fall risk appraisal to practice and providing tailored recommendations that decrease fall risk. Additionally, it strengthens the argument for tailored interventions to address perceived fall risk alongside physiological exercise and physical activity.Trial registrationThis study used data collected from a clinical trial registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on March 21, 2023 (NCT05778604).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07745-8.Keywords: Static balance, Fear of falling, Dynamic balance, Physical function, Physical activity

Background

Falls are one of the leading causes of injury and hospitalization in older adults [1]. There are a variety of assessment methods that provide older adults and clinicians alike with information about fall risk factors [2]. Fall risk appraisal (FRA) is a method that evaluates mismatches between physiological and perceived fall risk. In the FRA framework, physiological and perceived fall risk are presented as static balance performance and fear of falling (FoF), respectively. Older adults who have maladaptive FRA – a discrepancy between perceived and physiological fall risk – show reduced physical activity (PA), exacerbating fall risk [3, 4]. Thiamwong et al. 2020 introduced the FRA matrix, a graphical framework (Fig. 1) that categorizes combinations of FoF and balance levels into four FRA quadrants: (1) rational (low FoF and normal balance), (2) incongruent (low FoF despite poor static balance), (3) irrational (high FoF despite normal balance), and (4) congruent (high FoF and poor static balance). This study expands the characterization of the FRA groups to evaluate the prevalence of maladaptive FRA and characterize objective physical function in each FRA group in a large (n = 381) sample of older adults.

Distribution of FRA groups

Since its development, FRA has been used as a tool within interventions to facilitate cognitive reframing, determine drivers of longitudinal change, and compare groups [5]. The four groups can be further categorized into maladaptive (incongruent and irrational groups with mismatched balance and FoF) and adaptive FRA groups. The adaptive FRA groups are either positive adaptive (rational with normal balance and low FoF) or negative adaptive (congruent with poor balance and high FoF). Because the negative adaptive congruent group has been shown to be at higher risk for falls and adverse outcomes, this group is considered maladaptive as well [4]. Early work in a group of 102 older adults showed that over 40% of older adults presented with maladaptive FRA, revealing the prevalence of the problem [4]. For older adults with maladaptive or negative adaptive FRA, presenting and discussing the FRA Matrix was used as part of the Physio-Feedback Exercise Program (PEER) fall prevention intervention for cognitive reframing of mismatched perceived fall risk [5].

Physical health is a major contributor to fall risk. Risk factors relating to physical health include age, body composition, physical function, physical activity (PA), and grip strength [6–8]. FRA groups have been evaluated in the context of several of these physical health measures. For example, FRA has been quantified as a predictor of longitudinal change in dynamic balance performance [9]. Comparison across groups revealed that the FRA groups differed in their PA and fall history [10]. Similarly, body mass index (BMI) and measures of body composition differed across FRA groups [11]. However, the FRA groups have not yet been characterized at a larger scale by evaluating other physical function measures. Additionally, it remains unclear whether there are patterns in the differences between groups across these different measures.

No fall risk assessment fully captures all domains of fall risk, with best practices recommending multifactorial assessments [2]. The study evaluates how FRA groups differ based on objective measures, including physical activity, dynamic balance, lower limb power, and handgrip strength. Previous work has established group differences in physical activity and body composition, but other physical function measures are underexplored in the context of FRA [9, 12]. By further characterizing these measures, future interventions can provide tailored recommendations based on the group-level differences in physical function.

Methods

This study was conducted as part of a larger study that is federally funded by the National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities (R01MD018025). The sample consisted of 381 low-income community-dwelling older adults aged 60 to 97 years in Orlando, Florida. Recruitment protocols, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and assessment protocols have been described previously [5, 9, 10]. Upon enrolling in the study, participants completed a demographics survey including self-reported fall history within the past year and fitted with an accelerometer worn on the non-dominant wrist for 7 days. Given that some studies have shown higher proportion of women in the older adult population frequenting community centers [13], the study sample exhibited a sex-based bias.

Measures

Fear of falling

FoF was evaluated using the Short- Falls Efficacy Scale – International (Short FES-I), a 7-item self-administered tool measuring concern about falling while performing daily activities on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all concerned to 4 = very concerned) with total scores ranging from 7 to 28. The Short FES-I questionnaire has been validated against the long form FES-I and demonstrated utility as an FRA tool in older adults for reliability and predictive validity in psychological fall risk and balance ability [12, 14].

Static balance

Static balance (postural sway) was used to quantify physiological fall risk using the BTrackS (Balance Tracking Systems, Inc. San Diego, CA), consisting of a balance plate and BTrackS software (version 7.5.5-SP). Static balance was selected due to its predictive validity for fall risk and has been validated for reliability and validity [9, 15–18]. Participants were instructed to stand on the balance plate with their eyes closed, feet shoulder width apart, hands on their hips for 20 s. After an initial familiarization trial, postural sway (center of pressure displacement) was collected and analyzed for three trials.

FRA group

Based on previous work, participants were categorized into four subgroups (rational, congruent, irrational, incongruent) based on physiological fall risk (i.e., BTrackS score) and perceived fall risk (i.e., Short FES-I score), and used cutoff values for high and low risk as follows: (1) rational (low physiological fall risk [BTrackS ≤ 30 cm] and with low perceived fall risk [Short FES-I ≤ 10]); (2) congruent (high physiological fall risk [BTrackS > 30 cm] and high perceived fall risk [Short FES-I > 10]); (3) irrational (low physiological fall risk [BTrackS ≤ 30 cm] and high perceived fall risk [Short FES-I > 10]); and (4) incongruent (high physiological fall risk [BTrackS > 30 cm] and low perceived fall risk [Short FES-I ≤ 10]) [19]. To ensure consistent classification of borderline values, strict threshold criteria was applied for group assignment. FoF was considered ‘high’ only when FES-I scores exceeded 10, and balance impairment was considered ‘poor’ only when BTrackS sway scores exceeded 30. Values equal to the cutoff (e.g., FoF = 10 or BTrackS = 30) were therefore categorized as low FoF or normal balance. This approach prevented ambiguous cases and maintained reproducibility in group assignment.

Accelerometer-based physical activity

ActiGraph GT9X Link and LEAP (ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, FL, USA) were used to measure PA levels in participants at each timepoint, following the procedures described in prior work [9]. Briefly, participants wore a wrist-worn triaxial accelerometer (ActiGraph GT9X Link; ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, FL, USA) on their nondominant wrist for 7 consecutive days, removing it only for water-based activities or medical imaging. Device initialization and data collection protocols have been detailed previously [20, 21]. The ActiGraph LEAP device is a newer model of the GT9X, which expands upon the framework of the GT9X to minimize participant burden and maximize adherence. The LEAP was utilized in conjunction with the GT9X Link due to the discontinuation of the production of the GT9X Link and the number of participants participating in the study at once. For data analysis, only participants who had at least 4 days with 10 h each day recorded minimum were included. Raw acceleration data was downloaded using ActiLife software (GT9X, version 6.13.6) and the CentrePointe web-based program (LEAP) and analyzed using R statistical software (Version 4.5.1, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) to process the “.csv” files. To minimize variability between the devices, Monitor Independent Movement Summary (MIMS) units were calculated at the one-second level using the “MIMSunit” open-source R-package [22–24]. The data was aggregated at the minute-level. Nonwear time was determined using a preexisting algorithm that was recreated in R [25]. Out of the total valid wear time, the percentage of time spent in moderate-to-vigorous PA (%MVPA) was calculated based on the cut-off point of MVPA ≥ 19.614 MIMS [26–29]. This percentage was then averaged across the period that the accelerometer was worn at each timepoint.

Physical function tests

Timed Up and Go test

Dynamic balance was assessed using the Timed Up and Go (TUGT) test. Before participants completed the TUG test, experimenters provided standardized instructions and demonstrated the assessment, as described in previous work [9]. Participants were instructed to stand up from a chair, walk 3 m to a line marked on the floor with visible tape, turn around, walk back to the chair, and sit down again. The measure of dynamic balance was recorded as the time in seconds that the participants take to complete the assessment [7].

30-second chair stand test

Lower limb power was assessed using the 30-second Chair Stand test (30CST). As with the TUG, research team members instructed and demonstrated the assessment. Participants started in a seated position in a sturdy chair with their arms crossed on their chest, hands placed on each opposite shoulder, and their feet flat on the floor with knees bent at approximately 90°. Participants were instructed to complete as many sit-to-stand repetitions as possible in 30 seconds [30].

Handgrip strength

Handgrip strength (HGS) was assessed using a calibrated digital dynamometer (JAMAR Plus+, JLW Instruments, Chicago, IL, USA) with the dominant hand. For each assessment, participants sat in a sturdy chair with the dominant arm’s elbow flexed to approximately 90°, elbow unsupported, and flat middle phalanges on the 1st and 2nd digits. Participants were instructed to squeeze their dominant hand as hard as possible for three trials until peak force output was achieved (each trial lasting approximately 2–5 s) and with 30 s of rest between trials. The maximum recorded value between the three trials was used for analysis [31].

Statistical analysis

All data were stored in a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database managed by the University of Central Florida [32, 33]. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (Version 4.5.1, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics presenting participant characteristics, with means and standard deviations reported for continuous variables (e.g., Age, BMI, %MVPA, TUG, 30CST, HGS) and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables (e.g., sex). Group comparisons were conducted using cross-tabulations and Pearson chi-square tests for categorical variables, while continuous variables were assessed using ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests depending on distributional assumptions. Post-hoc pairwise t-tests were performed to identify specific group differences in continuous measures, with p-values adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. Missing data were addressed using listwise deletion, such that any case with incomplete values on the variables of interest was excluded from the analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and the threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Of the 401 participants enrolled, 381 were included in the analysis, excluding participants with missing FRA group. Descriptive measures of the entire sample and FRA group are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Participants were categorized (Fig. 2) into congruent (22.31%), incongruent (22.57%), irrational (14.17%), and rational (40.94%). Post-Hoc pairwise differences

The rational group (low perceived and physiological fall risk) was used as the reference variable to facilitate interpretation of continuous variables (Table 2). The congruent group differed from the rational group across all of the variables, with older age, decreased %MVPA, worse dynamic balance (greater TUG scores), decreased lower limb power (30CST), and decreased handgrip strength. Discussion

This study aimed to characterize FRA group characteristics in a large sample of low-income older adults. The results build upon previous work to present differences in physical function and activity across the four FRA groups (congruent, incongruent, irrational, and rational), which vary in their appraisal of perceived and physiological fall risk. Across a large and diverse sample of low-income older adults, fear of falling (FoF) exerted a stronger and more consistent influence on physical health than physiological balance deficits alone. This pattern was evident in the irrational group, who demonstrated poorer physical function despite normal static balance, and in the incongruent group, whose low FoF appeared to buffer functional decline despite physiological deficits and decreased physical activity.

Older adults with mismatched perceived and physiological fall risk are considered to have maladaptive FRA, which contributes to increase fall risk. Previous studies investigating FRA have shown differences in PA, body composition, and dynamic balance [9, 11, 34]. The sample included 381 older adults of various ethnic and racial backgrounds and ages (61 to 96 years). Participants were predominantly female, driven by the nature of the recruitment at community and senior centers, which may be utilized more frequently by older women. Due to the highly skewed gender distribution, sex differences were not included. Of the sample, over half the participants (59.06%) presented with maladaptive FRA (Fig. 1).

Evaluation of group differences showed significant differences across all variables (Table 1). To further evaluate these differences between the adaptive (rational) group and the maladaptive FRA groups, pairwise comparisons (Table 2) utilized the rational group as the reference group to which each of the maladaptive groups were compared. When comparing the groups, the results show that older adults in the congruent FRA (high perceived and physiological fall risk) group performed significantly worse than the rational group across all variables, which include age, %MVPA, dynamic balance, lower limb power, and handgrip strength (Table 2; Fig. 2). Given both increased perceived and physiological fall risk, these results align with previous literature that FoF and static balance are predictive of physical function [35–37].

The irrational (high perceived fall risk, but low physiological fall risk) group showed similar behaviors to the congruent in all variables except age and BMI. This group, despite good performance in static balance assessment, demonstrated poor physical function, which aligns with studies that have shown that worse TUG and CST performance were associated with high FoF [38, 39]. Similarly, handgrip strength, a marker of frailty, has been shown to be decreased in older adults with high FoF [40, 41]. Notably, engagement in physical activity was limited in this group, which may drive some of the functional deficits rather than the elevated FoF [42, 43]. Avoidance of physical activity in conjunction with elevated FoF may lead older adults to maladaptive patterns that impair physical function even in the absence of physiological deficits [44].

High physiological fall risk in the incongruent group did not correspond with similar physical function deficits as the congruent and irrational groups, which may be a characteristic of their low perceived fall risk. The low FoF in this group may contribute to better physical function measures by allowing individuals to remain active and functionally capable despite underlying deficits [10, 36, 45]. Although this group demonstrated reduced %MVPA compared to the rational group, their performance on the other physical health measures remained comparable, suggesting that lower activity levels may be an early behavioral manifestation of their physiological impairment. Importantly, the absence of broader functional deficits despite poor static balance contrasted sharply with the irrational group, who exhibited similar reductions in %MVPA but worse physical function. This divergence implies that FoF, rather than physical activity alone, may play a more central role in shaping functional outcomes. In this context, the incongruent group may represent a unique phenotype driven by maladaptive FRA, individuals with physiological deficits who maintain functional capacity through preserved confidence.

Implications

Both FoF and a lack of awareness of physiological fall risk deficits play a role in physical activity. The results from these two groups suggest the significance of FoF in not only limiting physical activity and worsening body composition, as previously established, but also exacerbating other physical function measures [11, 34]. Due to the cross-sectional design of this study, causal relationships cannot be inferred between FRA groups and physical health; it is equally plausible that lower physical activity contributes to maladaptive FRA or that maladaptive FRA restricts activity. However, the improved physical function despite decreased physical activity in the incongruent group suggests that FoF may play a greater role than physiological fall risk. Taken together, the findings support the significance of tailored interventions, such as physiofeedback and cognitive reframing, that adjust for perceived and physiological fall risk. By reframing FoF that older adults face and providing feedback about balance performance, these interventions can overcome self-established limitations on physical activity and decrease fall risk.

Limitations

The generalizability of the study is limited by the recruitment of older adults from older adult community centers in Central Florida, which may not be representative of the greater population. The predominantly female sample limited the ability to further investigate potential differences between male and female older adults.

Additionally, minimal differences between the incongruent and rational groups may reflect limitations in the included physical function assessments. Although HGS is a widely used marker of overall strength and frailty, it does not fully represent lower extremity strength or functional capacity and may be influenced by hand pain, arthritis, or motivational factors [46, 47]. These limitations should temper interpretation of handgrip strength differences across FRA groups. Similarly, the TUG is a practical and validated measure of mobility and dynamic balance, but it demonstrates ceiling effects in higher-functioning older adults, reducing its sensitivity to detect mild dynamic balance deficits [48]. Additionally, it captures only a subset of dynamic balance components and may not reflect more complex balance demands encountered in daily life [49]. While the variables included capture key domains of physical health, they do not encompass cognitive, sensory, or environmental contributors to fall risk. Future work should integrate cognitive testing, gait variability, and real‑world mobility monitoring to more comprehensively characterize FRA subgroups.

Conclusions

This study presents evidence of distinct patterns in the physical health of low-income older adults in different FRA groups. Over half of the sample exhibited maladaptive FRA, whether driven by over- or under-estimation of FoF, highlighting the prevalence of this problem and how it relates to physical health. Findings from the congruent group aligned with previous literature, but the characterization of incongruent and irrational groups underscored the need for future research targeting both perceived and physiological deficits. The incongruent group with worse static balance performed similarly to the rational group despite lower physical activity, an early indicator of their worsened physiological fall risk. The contrasting results between the incongruent and the irrational group suggests the protective effect of a reduced FoF. Interventions should harness FRA grouping as a tool that integrates physical training with psychological support to promote adaptive FRA, reduce fall risk, and enhance mobility in diverse, underserved older adult populations.
