Model Peptides Enable Comparisons of Substrate Binding Modes of Hsp70 Molecular Chaperones
Authors: Karishma Bhasne, Lea Dörries, Akshitha Maqtedar, Sydney T. Mager, Eugenia M. Clerico, Lila M. Gierasch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hsp70 molecular chaperones play a wide array of essential roles in the cell by exploiting their ability to bind incompletely folded client proteins. Our past study of the Escherichia coli Hsp70, DnaK, bound to short peptides revealed many details of the interaction between a model substrate and the “pockets” of the canonical binding cleft within the chaperone substrate binding domain (SBD). A major finding was that peptides bound the SBD cleft in either N- to C- or C- to N- orientations with nearly equal frequencies. The current study asks key questions: What determines preferred orientation in the bound substrate? Also, are the binding behaviors observed for DnaK also found for mammalian homologues? Here, we use NMR and cross-linking methods developed in our past study and compare modes of binding to DnaK and the two major human cytoplasmic Hsp70s, Hsc70 and HspA1. Model peptides examined include a “palindromic” peptide, central residue variant peptides, and examples of naturally occurring Hsp70 clients. The results reveal that DnaK is agnostic to backbone orientation of the bound peptide, while Hsc70 modestly favors the C- to N- orientation, and HspA1 strongly favors the N- to C- orientation. Moreover, substrate affinities in general are similar for DnaK and Hsc70 and weaker for HspA1. The binding energy landscapes of these chaperones also differ, as the data suggest deeper energy wells for bound states in DnaK and HspA1 and a more rugged landscape for Hsc70. We speculate that these properties enable the chaperones to perform their physiological functions more effectively.

Introduction

The 70 kDa heat shock proteins (Hsp70s) are ubiquitous chaperone proteins in prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Their mechanism of action is highly conserved, as are their sequences and structures. Hsp70s play essential roles in the maintenance of cellular protein homeostasis that include supporting (un)­folding, preventing aggregation, and promoting disaggregation of proteins. , Hsp70s are two-domain proteins composed of a nucleotide binding domain (NBD) and a substrate binding domain (SBD). Ligand binding and release in either domain leads to global conformational changes in the chaperone, influencing its action on substrate proteins. , Binding affinity (on and off rates) of clients to the SBD is modulated by nucleotide binding to the NBD: When ATP is bound, substrates bind with low affinity and rapid on/off rates. When ADP is bound to the NBD, substrates bind to the SBD with high affinity and slow on/off kinetics.

Hsp70s are involved in a wide variety of cellular processes, and a central aspect of all of their functions is their ability to recognize and interact with their substrates in a manner that enables them to discriminate incompletely folded substrates from those that are fully folded. While Hsp70s bind a wide array of substrates, they show some selectivity in the bound sequences as well as the state of the bound client protein. − We describe this binding behavior as “selective promiscuity”. The way this binding is achieved is a fascinating question in molecular recognition.

The SBD structure , is made up of a twisted β-sheet subdomain that harbors the substrate binding cleft, and an α-helical lid subdomain that can close fully or partially on top of the β-sheet when a substrate is bound. , The canonical substrate binding site is composed of five shallow binding regions (here called “pockets”) that are the major binding components with some contribution from the next sites out (−3 and +3). The central pocket, also known as the 0th pocket, represents the primary site of hydrophobic side chain engagement, and this pocket is the most stringent binder, sterically. Moving away from the central pocket are subsites referred to as −3, −2, −1, + 1, +2, and +3. , Because the binding of substrates relies primarily on short, extended segments of polypeptide chain, the characteristics of binding have been productively explored by our lab and others using model peptides and peptide arrays (for a review of this work, see). These approaches have revealed that Hsp70s exhibit a strong binding preference for short stretches of hydrophobic amino acids often flanked by positively charged residues with a sparsity of negatively charged amino acids. − , However, there is no specific consensus sequence for substrate binding. These behaviors reflect the physiological roles of Hsp70 chaperones, which require them to interact with a wide array of client proteins. ,,

Our lab initially focused on the Escherichia coli Hsp70, DnaK, in our efforts to understand the binding preferences of Hsp70s for their substrates. We found that DnaK engages unfolded substrates in both N- to C- and C- to N- orientations, relative to the SBD binding cleft, with no strong directional bias. This finding indicates that productive binding of model peptides to DnaK does not depend on polypeptide polarity and that bidirectional substrate binding is an intrinsic property of this Hsp70's substrate interaction. By contrast with the rich data available on DnaK substrate interactions, there is a paucity of information on the substrate recognition properties of the canonical human Hsp70s (hHsp70). Hsc70 (also known as HspA8) is the major constitutively expressed Hsp70 chaperone in the cytosol that performs various housekeeping functions and therefore is essential to protein homeostasis in humans. The other major cytoplasmic mammalian Hsp70 is HspA1 (also known as Hsp72), and its expression is upregulated by stress. For all Hsp70s, the major contributor to substrate recognition is within the SBD, and the structures of this domain are highly conserved (Figure A). Specifically, the SBD β-sandwich subdomain contains the substrate binding cleft and represents the primary determinant of substrate selection. While full-length Hsc70 and HspA1 share approximately 85.8% sequence identity with one another and retain 48.2 and 49.6% sequence identity with full-length E. coli DnaK, respectively, the sequence identity within the SBD β-sandwich subdomain is higher than that of the full-length chaperones (Figure B). Strikingly, Hsc70 and HspA1 share 91.2% sequence identity within the SBD β-sandwich subdomain, with only 10 residues differing between them. Of these, three lie proximal to the substrate binding cleft: I403/L, T427/I, and K458/R (Hsc70/HspA1) (shown in Figure C). Among these, residue 427 represents a nonconservative substitution and is also distinct from the corresponding residue in DnaK (V425). The presence of a threonine at this position in Hsc70 introduces a hydroxyl group that could enable additional polar interactions with bound substrates. These differences may lead to differences in substrate selectivity. Consistent with this idea, previous reports have noted differences in their substrate binding and differing functional client interactions. ,, Taken together, these observations highlight a central feature of the Hsp70 family: Although overall structure has been strongly conserved, relatively subtle sequence differences within the substrate binding cleft are sufficient to give rise to distinct substrate binding outcomes.

The goal of the present study is to use model peptides and an array of biophysical methods to explore in depth the comparative substrate recognition preferences of the two major human cytoplasmic Hsp70s, using the well-studied E. coli Hsp70, DnaK, as reference. Based on our past work, one of the fundamental differences in substrate binding properties may be the orientation of the substrate in the binding cleft and the preferred occupancy of the chaperone side chain pockets by residues from the substrate. The long-term goal of this work is to decipher the molecular origins of the balance between binding specificity and the capacity of each chaperone to bind many sequences, i.e., promiscuity. Ideally, our findings will elucidate each chaperone’s properties that enable them to interact with the array of clients presented to them to meet physiological needs.

Materials and Methods

Protein Expression and Purification

The gene encoding wild-type E. coli DnaK SBD (residues 389–607 cloned into the pSCG plasmid carrying an N-terminal His6-tag followed by Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease digestion sequence) was used to overexpress proteins in E. coli BL21 (DE3). DnaK SBD (389–607) variants carrying cysteine residues at positions 425 (DnaK SBD425C) and 458 (DnaK SBD458C) were created using site-directed mutagenesis of the wild-type gene. Protein expression and purification of the unlabeled and 1H,13C-labeled protein at Ile d1-methyl (in a 2H, 12C background) for NMR was performed as previously described. The only modification to the 13C-labeling protocol was the use of sodium salts of α-ketobutyric acid (methyl 13C, 3,3-D2) to achieve selective labeling of Ile δ1-methyl groups. The gene encoding wild-type Homo sapiens Hsc70 SBD and HspA1 SBD (residues 395–616 cloned into the pMSCG7 vector carrying N-terminal His6-tag followed by the TEV digestion sequence) were cloned and overexpressed in E. coli Rosetta BL21 (DE3) competent cells. Hsc70 SBD (395–616) and HspA1 SBD (395–616) variants containing cysteine mutations at positions 427 and 460 (Hsc70 SBD427C, Hsc70 SBD460C, HspA1 SBD427C, HspA1 SBD460C) were synthesized from GenScript (Piscataway, New Jersey) in the pMSCG7 vector carrying the N-terminal His6-tag and TEV digestion sequence. All Hsc70 and HspA1 SBD expression and purification were performed as previously described for DnaK, except that during purification, the Ni-NTA-bound SBD was supplemented with 2 M urea to partially unfold the SBD and remove any bound substrates, urea removed after the affinity column. 1H,13C- δ1-methyl Ile Hsc70 SBD and HspA1 SBD protein expression was performed exactly as described above for DnaK SBD. For all Hsp70 SBDs, protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE and concentration determined by UV absorption and amino acid analysis. Cysteine variant proteins were assessed for WT-like secondary structure by CD (data not shown).

Peptides

All peptides used for this work (Table ) were purchased from Synpeptide (Nanjing, China). With the exception of two of the naturally occurring peptides (QL) and SNAP-25 peptide (SNN), all peptides contain an N-terminal cysteine residue. Purity was checked by reverse-phase HPLC, concentration by amino acid analysis at the Texas A&M Protein Chemistry Laboratory (College Station, Texas), and identity by mass spectrometry.

Disulfide Cross-linking Experiments

The experimental design behind the disulfide cross-linking between SBD and peptides was previously described. The experimental procedure for all Hsp70s was the same; namely, 10 μM SBD425C/427C and SBD458C/460C were incubated with 20 μM Cys-peptide for 1 h at 22 °C in HMK buffer with 1 mM DTT. Excess peptide and DTT were removed by loading 50 μL of the sample in a desalting Zeba spin column from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, Massachusetts), eluting the protein at the final step with a 30 s spin at 1000g. The samples were kept at 22 °C for 10 min to allow the cysteine in the bound peptide to form a disulfide bond with the cysteine in the SBD (with Cys425/427 if the peptide is bound on the N- to C- orientation, and with Cys458/460 if it is bound in the C- to N- orientation). The samples were then supplemented with iodoacetamide purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific at a concentration of 100 mM to block the unreacted thiol groups and kept at 22 °C for 10 min followed by addition of 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer (without reducing agent). The results were visualized by SDS-PAGE using 12% acrylamide gels purchased from GenScript (Piscataway, New Jersey).

NMR Spectroscopy

All NMR experiments were performed as previously described, with the exception that all SBDs contained only an Ile-13CH3 label due to our focus on the Ile chemical shift reporter.

Binding Assays

The apparent affinities of the DnaK SBD, Hsc70 SBD, and HspA1 SBDs for all peptides were measured by fluorescence anisotropy using competition assays at 25 °C in a BioTek Synergy 2 micro plate reader (Biotek, Winooski, Vermont), with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 516 nm. The affinity of the SBD for fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled peptide p5 (ALLLSAPRR) was measured first by direct binding as previously reported. , In the present study, 0–200 μM of each model substrate peptide was added to 35 nM FITC-p5 in 20 mM Hepes pH 7.6, 10 mM MgCl2, and 100 mM KCl (HMK buffer) in the presence of 0.12 μM DnaK SBD, 0.4 μM Hsc70 SBD, or 0.4 μM HspA1 SBD. The mixture was then incubated in 384-well plates for 1 h at 25 °C for DnaK SBD and 2 h for Hsc70 and HspA1 SBD, after which the fluorescence anisotropy was measured. Assays were carried out in triplicate. The data were analyzed as described previously.

Results

Experimental Strategy

To systematically dissect the specificity and promiscuity of substrate recognition at the canonical binding site of the human Hsp70s, Hsc70 (constitutively expressed), and HspA1 (heat shock–inducible), we utilized isolated SBDs as we have in our past studies of DnaK. This reductionist approach allows us to intentionally decouple substrate recognition from the allosteric chaperone cycle, focusing instead on intrinsic binding modes and sequence preferences of the canonical substrate binding site. We assembled a set of peptides as model substrates, including both designed and naturally occurring sequences (Table ). The designed peptides probe both residue preferences at the central binding pocket of the Hsp70 SBDs and how substitutions at this position affect the preferred orientation of the bound peptide.

The apparent affinities of each peptide were determined for each Hsp70 SBD using fluorescence anisotropy competition assay, as previously described. In addition, we deployed two methods to assess the mode of binding of the model peptides to the chaperone SBDs. Methyl NMR spectroscopy was used to define peptide binding modes at residue-level resolution. ,− As described in our previous work, we monitored δ1-methyl positions of key Ile residues lining the Hsp70 central pocket. In DnaK, the 13C δ1-methyl chemical shifts of Ile438 and Ile401 exhibit characteristic changes depending on the identity of the residue occupying the 0th pocket. Analogous reporter residues in the human chaperones (Ile440 and Ile403 in Hsc70; and Ile440 in HspA1), enable direct extension of this approach. The methyl NMR maps of I438 or I440 (as Ile 403 is not present in HspA1) were used to determine, for all peptides and all SBDs, both the identity of the residue occupying the central pocket and the orientation of the bound peptide. [For a summary of the results of NMR analysis and binding affinities for all peptides studied, see Table S1.] When possible, we supported the NMR-based interpretation of preferred binding orientation using disulfide-mediated cross-linking, as previously described for DnaK. Each SBD was mutated to create two variants, each containing a single cysteine on either side of the substrate binding groove (Cys458 or Cys425 for DnaK; Cys460 or Cys427 for Hsc70 and HspA1) and all peptides carried an N-terminal Cys, except the clathrin model (QL) and the SNAP-25 peptide (SNN), both of which were synthesized for previous studies that did not utilize cross-linking. , Cross-linking reactions were performed in the absence of reducing agents, allowing disulfide bond formation between the peptide and the SBD Cys positioned proximal to the peptide N-terminus. Observation of a cross-linked product for either or both Cys-containing SBDs supported the orientation of the peptide that placed its Cys residue proximal to that in the SBD variant. The cross-linking experiment consistently yielded clear and interpretable results for DnaK, but not so for the human Hsp70s. We concluded that the Cys residue substitutions in the human Hsp70s, and in several instances, lower peptide affinities (which led to higher free peptide concentrations), resulted in competition from intermolecular cross-links and thus confounded interpretation.

A Palindromic Peptide Reports on Orientation Preference

Our past work and that of others , established an intriguing aspect of the binding behavior of Hsp70s: Both an N- to C- orientation and the reverse, C- to N- orientation, can be accommodated in the binding pocket. In both cases, the bound peptide is in a gently twisted extended conformation and forms five hydrogen bonds with the chaperone. In the N- to C- binding mode, the peptide interacts with a strand in the SBD in a parallel fashion (Figure A), while in the C- to N-binding mode, the peptide interacts in an antiparallel mode (Figure B). The “rules” determining which orientation is favored and the relative energy of binding for each are a mystery. Thus, our initial experiment involved the design of a “palindromic” peptide (PalP), CRSGQKALAKQGSR (Table ), in which the Leu residue was anticipated to bind in the 0th pocket and the remaining side chains would be arranged symmetrically around it. This symmetric arrangement ensures that, irrespective of the peptide orientation in the SBD binding pocket, the same side chains occupy the −3 to +3 SBD subsites.

Hsp70 SBDs can accommodate peptides in an N- to C-, and the reverse, C- to N- orientation, depicted in the structure of the peptide-bound E. coli SBD. (A) Peptides bound in an N- to C- orientation interact with a strand in the SBD in a parallel fashion. (B) Peptides bound in a C- to N- orientation interact in an antiparallel mode. In both cases, Ile438 (440 for human Hsc70 and HspA1) is in the immediate vicinity of the peptide residue bound in the central binding pocket (*).

For DnaK, methyl-TROSY NMR revealed two distinct resonances for the 13C δ1-methyl group of Ile438, each with approximately equal intensities (Figure A), indicating nearly equal populations of the two different bound orientations. Based on the characterization of the Ile438 resonances as a function of the residue occupying the central pocket, PalP binding places a leucine residue in the central pocket of DnaK, as anticipated in the design. We therefore assigned these two bound forms to N- to C- and C- to N- orientations, and indeed disulfide-mediated cross-linking using the Cys-engineered DnaK SBD variants showed comparable populations of peptide bound in both orientations (Figure B). Together, these results support the conclusion that the DnaK SBD is agnostic to the direction of the peptide backbone (Figure C), provided that the same side chains bind to the pockets within the SBD in either orientation.

While the DnaK result was compelling and yielded a clear conclusion, the results for the two hHsp70s were more intriguing and pointed to striking differences in their binding behavior despite their high sequence homology. The methyl-TROSY NMR spectrum for the palindromic peptide bound to Hsc70 SBD reveals two distinct populations of bound peptide with chemical shifts for Ile440 (Hsc70 numbering) indicating placement of leucine in the 0th binding pocket in both cases (Figure A). The Ile440 δ1-methyl chemical shifts for these two species correspond to ∼80% of the population binding in the C- to N- and ∼20% in the N- to C- orientation. While clearly the C- to N- state is energetically favored, it is important to recognize that an 80:20 ratio corresponds to an energy difference of less than 1 kcal/mol, which demonstrates that a symmetric sequence surrounding the central leucine does not impose a strong directional bias on substrate binding in Hsc70. In contrast, the methyl NMR spectrum for HspA1 displays a single dominant bound population under the same conditions (Figure A), consistent with an N- to C- orientation based on Ile methyl chemical shifts with respect to their positions in DnaK. These results indicate striking differences among the chaperones: DnaK is agnostic about peptide orientation when sequences upstream and downstream of the central residue are symmetric, Hsc70 prefers that the peptide is bound C- to N- when sequence symmetry is offered, and in striking contrast, HspA1 exhibits an intrinsic orientation preference for the N- to C- orientation of its bound substrate when side chain/pocket occupancy is comparable (Figure B).

Role of the Central Residue in Determining the Mode of Binding

Based on prior analyses of DnaK substrate recognition, ,, synthetic peptide sequences were designed such that binding was not likely to be strongly influenced by interactions with sites in the −3, −2, −1, +1, +2, or +3 positions, but rather emphasized the importance of the residue occupying the central binding pocket. Here, in each peptide, the sequence was specifically designed to favor a binding mode in which one residue occupied the central pocket of the SBD, and this residue was varied among Leu, Ile, Val, or Pro, the residues that past work showed were favorably accommodated in the 0th pocket. ,,

The resulting series of model sequences are dubbed “central residue variant peptides (CRVPs)”: CRVP-L (CRYSGKALTNGSG), CRVP-I (CRYSGKAITNGSG), CRVP-V (CRYSGKAVTNGSG), and CRVP-P (CRYSGKAPANGSG) (Table ). With the exception of the Pro-containing peptide, all flanking residues were held constant and selected to support binding without competing for the central pocket, allowing isolation of the contribution of the zeroth-position residue to binding mode. The CRVP-P peptide contains an Ala in place of the Thr in the other sequences because initial studies revealed some ambiguity in interpretation due to the poor binding of Pro to the central pocket.

Methyl-TROSY NMR (Figure A) and disulfide-mediated cross-linking (Figure B) revealed that DnaK binds CRVP-L in both N- to C- and C- to N- orientations, with a significant preference for N- to C- mode. The Ile-containing peptide displayed a similar behavior, populating both orientations with a strong bias for the N- to C- binding orientation in both NMR and cross-linking experiments (Figure ). The Val-containing peptide bound equally in the N- to C- and C- to N- orientations, indicating no measurable orientation preference (Figure ). The Pro-containing peptide exhibited very low affinity (see below), and while NMR detected occupancy of the central pocket, binding was largely restricted to the C- to N- orientation by cross-linking (Figure B), likely reflecting the conformational constraints of proline. These results indicate that the DnaK SBD can accommodate the peptide backbone in either orientation with comparable binding energy and that the apparent orientation preferences of the CRVPs can be significantly influenced by the identity of the residue occupying the central binding pocket.

We next expanded our analysis to the binding preferences of CRVPs to the hHsp70s (Figure ). We found that the Leu-containing peptide binds in the N- to C- orientation in both Hsc70 and HspA1. The Val-containing peptide yields chemical shifts that are in a gray zone, so that it is not possible to conclude which orientation is preferred. Surprisingly, the Ile-containing peptide exhibits preferences for the C- to N- orientation in both hHsp70s, illustrating the impact of the central residue on binding mode. This orientation bias demonstrates that the central residue identity influences the preferred binding mode, with the proviso that the surrounding sequence undoubtedly plays a role. The exciting aspect of our findings with this series of peptides is how different the binding preferences are for these closely related chaperones. We note that in addition to differences in binding orientation, these peptides display substantial differences in binding strength (Figure ). Among the variants tested, the Leu-containing peptide binds both hHsp70 chaperones most strongly, followed by the Ile- and Val-containing peptides, as shown by binding assays. Notably, the apparent binding affinities are higher overall for Hsc70 than for HspA1.

In contrast, the Pro-containing peptide exhibits weak to no binding to both Hsc70 and HspA1, as evidenced by methyl-TROSY NMR and binding assays. We observe a weak peak for CRVP-P binding to Hsc70 SBD (we assign this peak to a C- to N- orientation based on the position of the corresponding resonance in DnaK where we can conclude the orientation is C- to N- based on cross-linking results), but the presence of peaks overlapping with the empty SBD clearly shows that CRVP-P does not lead to full occupancy of the Hsc70 SBD binding site. This observation suggests that, although proline can be accommodated geometrically within the central binding pocket, its presence alone may not be sufficient to support stable substrate engagement in this simplified peptide context. Taken together, these results indicate that while central pocket interactions are important, local sequence context and overall binding energetics also contribute to substrate engagement by human Hsp70s.

Does the Binding of the Naturally Occurring Hsp70 Substrates Parallel That of the Model Peptides?

The chaperones we have studied interact in the physiological context with a set of proteins from the expressed proteomes of the corresponding organism. While interrogating the entire clientele of each Hsp70 is beyond the scope of this study, we did want to see whether sequences that are derived from naturally occurring substrates of bacterial and human Hsp70s behaved in a similar fashion to what we have observed for the families of designed peptides we have described above. We selected Peptide D (CAKKEGNTLVIVTADH), a sequence derived from proPhoA, which is an E. coli protein reported to be a DnaK substrate; ,, a peptide derived from the N-terminal domain of human SNAP-25, SNN (RRMLQLVEESK); a peptide corresponding to the reported Hsc70-binding motif of clathrin, QL (QLMLTAG); and a peptide corresponding to the Hsc70-binding site in the glucocorticoid receptor ligand binding domain, GR (CGNKTIVPATLPQ) (Table ). We must emphasize that the picture emerging from these comparisons is complex. Still, we have learned that the same substrates can be bound in distinct ways by closely related Hsp70 family members.

When presented with the proPhoA-D peptide, which contains a hydrophobic sequence LVIV, DnaK binds in only one mode with the substrate residue Leu in the central pocket and a C- to N- orientation (Figure ). Clearly, placing a Leu in the central pocket is favored over the other hydrophobic residues, and this preference is shown for all three chaperones. In our past study, this DnaK SBD-bound state for proPhoA-D was also seen in a crystal structure (PDB 7JN8). By comparison, Hsc70 binds this peptide in multiple manners (Figure ). The C- to N- orientation with Leu in the central pocket is observed with approximately 50% population. In addition, two other bound forms are seen at about the same intensity (25%) given the limits of NMR to quantitate these populations. We conclude that one of the more minor states fits an interpretation of Leu in the central pocket and an N- to C- orientation. The other displays an Ile440 methyl signal at an unusual chemical shift that does not correspond to positioning either Ile or Val in the central pocket and which we propose may arise from the Leu in the sequence that is adjacent to a phenylalanine and may thus be affected by ring current perturbations. Intriguingly, only one signal, which has a doublet character and may arise from closely spaced signals for two species, perhaps rotameric variants, is observed for the methyl of Ile440 when proPhoA-D binds to HspA1 (Figure ). The position of this resonance is consistent with a dominant species with Leu in the central pocket and an N- to C- orientation. Even this simple comparison of the binding of DnaK, Hsc70, and HspA1 with the peptide derived from a bacterial protein illustrates how divergent the binding modes for the three chaperones are.

The SNN peptide derived from the N-terminal domain of the human SNAP-25 protein, a known Hsc70 client, contains both a Leu and a Val, either of which could be potential central pocket residues based on the sequence. The methyl-TROSY NMR spectrum of this peptide in the presence of the DnaK SBD shows that placing the Leu residue in the central pocket is preferred, with an occupancy of approximately 90% and a chemical shift indicative of the C- to N- orientation (Figure ). There is an additional clear signal making up ∼10% of the population for the Leu in the central pocket in the N- to C- orientation. The Hsc70 and HspA1 SBDs also prefer to position leucine in the 0th binding pocket (Figure ). Hsc70 has an even stronger preference for one state than seen for DnaK, and by comparison with Ile methyl chemical shifts among all the models, we assign this state to a C- to N- orientation. In a surprisingly distinct behavior, despite its high homology to Hsc70, HspA1 favors one bound state with SNN binding via a Leu in the central pocket but with an N- to C- orientation. Consistent with results from the central residue variants, binding assays show higher affinities for this peptide with DnaK and Hsc70 than with HspA1 (Figure ).

We next analyzed a clathrin-derived peptide (QL), which has a physiologically well-characterized interaction with Hsc70. Interestingly, there are two Leu residues in this peptide, and we were keen to see whether the chaperones could bind to either or both. The binding of this peptide to DnaK indeed yielded two populated states with Leu in the central pocket. The positions of these reflected ∼60% population of a C- to N- orientation for the bound peptide and ∼40% for the N- to C- orientation. We cannot conclude which Leu is binding or whether perhaps a switch from one Leu in the central pocket to the other occurs. The interaction of QL with Hsc70 was essentially the same as that with DnaK: Leu in the central pocket, ∼60% C- to N- and ∼40% N- to C-. However, for HspA1, while we see two states populated, the Ile440 methyl resonances are close to one another, and both are in the region we associate with N- to C-binding. We conjecture that this chaperone may form complexes with the QL peptide using either Leu residue in the central pocket.

The GR peptide, which corresponds to the Hsc70-binding region of the glucocorticoid receptor ligand binding domain, binds DnaK placing a Pro residue in the central pocket (∼60% population) and shows a ∼40% population of a bound state with Ile in the central pocket. We tentatively assign the state with Pro in the central pocket to a C- to N- orientation based on our earlier observations with the CRVP-P peptide. The state with Ile in the central pocket also fits a chemical shift for a C- to N- orientation. By contrast, the native chaperone that binds GR, Hsc70, places the Ile in the central pocket as the only bound state. The orientation indicated by the Ile440 chemical shift is C- to N-. We conclude from the observed chemical shift of Ile440 that HspA1 binds to GR in only one state as well, with Ile in the central pocket and an N- to C- backbone orientation. It is of interest that the cryoEM structure of Hsc70 in complex with the ligand binding domain of the glucocorticoid receptor, Hsp40 (the cochaperone that mediates transfer of a substrate from Hsc70 to Hsp90), and Hsp90 (along with an additional Hsc70 not interacting with the polypeptide as it is presented to Hsp90) found the segment of chain bound to Hsc70 to be C- to N- in orientation, but with a Pro in the central pocket. We point out that the formation of the multichaperone complex and presentation of the substrate for transfer from Hsc70 to Hsp90 confers substantial constraint on the interaction between Hsc70 and the substrate. In our hands, a preference is observed for Hsc70 to place an Ile in the central pocket, but the finding that DnaK may place either Pro or Ile in this position emphasizes that there is room for different modes of binding depending on overall architecture of a complex. Importantly, we see for Hsc70, the endogenous GR binding Hsp70, C- to N- binding orientation regardless of central residue bound, which may reflect that this is the only orientation possible given the constraints of the loading complex of GR with Hsc70, Hsp90, and HOP.

Discussion

Molecular chaperones demonstrate binding behavior that is far from simple “lock-and-key”-type modes of interaction, and as a consequence, it challenges any effort to dissect it in molecular detail. Importantly, because of their functions, they must bind many sequences, they must not bind substrates too tightly, and they must discriminate folded substrates from those that are incompletely folded. These characteristics are shared by all Hsp70s. Nonetheless, the subproteomes that comprise the substrates for each Hsp70, whether from different species or in eukaryotes, from different cellular locations, are divergent. Thus, we anticipated when we began this study that our comparative analysis of substrate interactions by the bacterial Hsp70, DnaK, and the two major human cytoplasmic Hsp70s, Hsc70 (constitutively expressed, housekeeping), and HspA1 (stress-induced, protective under stress), would reveal differences in their mode of recognition of substrates. Indeed, this turned out to be the case.

Specific “rules” for the binding of substrate models to the Hsp70s we have studied cannot be extracted from the limited number of cases we examined. Nonetheless, we have observed several compelling behaviors that are characteristic of each chaperone. An overarching finding is that the affinities of the chaperones for these model peptides differ substantially, with DnaK and Hsc70 being generally similar, and HspA1 displaying the lowest affinities. In addition to the impact of these differing affinities on our interpretation of data, particularly from NMR, we can speculate on how these observations may relate to the cellular functions of the different Hsp70s. We want to emphasize up front that we are comparing behaviors of the SBDs without their NBDs, which modulate effective affinities in the physiological context. DnaK is established to be part of the “greeting committee” for nascent chains in E. coli, and it hands its substrates to its downstream partners, generally Hsp90, which facilitates substrate release. Also, DnaK is called upon under stress conditions, when the ATP concentration would be low and its job is to hold incompletely folded substrates to prevent their aggregation and degradation. Thus, high-affinity binding is consistent with the cellular roles of DnaK. The two cytoplasmic hHsp70s have quite different overall affinities. We speculate that in addition to the interactions with partner chaperones (like those mentioned for DnaK) and the sensitivity to ATP concentration in the full-length chaperones, the lower affinity of HspA1 for model substrates may be consistent with the distinct physiological roles of the two proteins, as Hsc70 primarily supports housekeeping functions, whereas the stress-inducible HspA1 is thought to engage in more transient substrate interactions that facilitate rapid turnover.

Digging into general findings for the differing modes of binding to these model peptides reveals striking distinctions. DnaK is agnostic about bound peptide orientation, given sequences that offer the same side-chain interactions away from the central residue. Interestingly, in situations encountered in several of the naturally occurring peptide sequences where multiple potential central residue binders are present and flanking sequences upstream and downstream of the central residue differ, DnaK often populates one major state. This DnaK behavior invites follow-up studies of the energetic differences in specific residue occupancy of upstream and downstream pockets. Similarly, Hsc70 often populates one major state, and in some cases another minor state, where bound orientation varies depending on the substrate. By contrast, HspA1 has a strong preference to bind substrates in an N- to C- orientation and typically populates a single binding mode, although its weak binding affinities limit our ability to see sparsely populated states. We interpret these differences as reflections on the nature of the binding energy landscape for each chaperone, with relatively deeper wells for DnaK and HspA1 than for Hsc70, which appears to have a more rugged binding energy landscape. Of course, what we are seeing is the result of the sum of many small energetic contributions to binding and must be cautiously interpreted.

This study relied heavily on the use of model peptides of relatively short length. This approach is reasonable because of the nature of the Hsp70 SBD substrate binding cleft, which accommodates 5 to 7 amino acids in an extended conformation, as elucidated from the first structure reported and supported by many other structures solved since then. Nonetheless, it is crucial to recognize that the Hsp70 chaperones act on proteins in their physiological roles and in some cases are components of multiprotein complexes in their action on substrates. Efforts have been made to study protein binding by Hsp70s. ,, An important aspect of the binding of protein substrates is the ability of the α-helical lid to adjust how open the substrate binding site in the SBD can be. Here, we did not explore this aspect as all of the peptide substrate models studied here are of approximately the same size. An additional aspect that we find fascinating is how the Hsp70 “shops” for sites in a full-length protein. We explored this in our previous study using proPhoA and found for this fully unfolded substrate that the DnaK SBD bound to the protein as though it presented its potential sites as peptides. The binding hierarchy and binding modes in full-length proPhoA were largely recapitulated by the peptide models. Of course, if Hsp70s are presented with incompletely folded substrates, then the accessibility of sites will contribute in a major way to the selection of binding sequences.

There have been many predictive algorithms developed for Hsp70 sequence preferences. As mentioned above, this is a challenging effort as their binding is intrinsically complex, abundantly illustrated by the present study. Still, the efforts are worthwhile as researchers seek to identify Hsp70 binding sites in a given protein. As we did in the algorithm we developed, we want to ensure that the possibility of binding in either N- to C- or C- to N- orientations is included in any predictive study.

There are many remaining questions in this fascinating world of Hsp70 substrate interaction. We suggest that challenging chaperones with full-length proteins is an essential next step. In our study of full-length proPhoA, not only was the protein fully unfolded so that accessibility was a nonissue, the binding sites in proPhoA are well separated from one another. What happens if there are steric conflicts between the binding sites? Also, in looking at the comparative behaviors of the three Hsp70s we have studied, can we now seek to understand the molecular origins of the differences? The highly homologous hHsp70s are clearly candidates for site-directed mutagenesis to explore residue differences (only 10!) that create the different behaviors. We are particularly interested in the residues that are nearest to the binding site, such as I403/L, T427/I, and K458/R (Hsc70/HspA1), as they may be anticipated to influence preferred backbone orientation as well as the nature of the binding energy landscape. Lastly, this study intentionally focused on how the SBD binds substrates. In future work, the impact of the observed binding behaviors on the allosteric conformational changes in the Hsp70s and consequent coupling to ATPase rates in the NBD, and conversely how ATP binding modulates the substrate interactions, must be examined. It is formally possible that initial recognition of binding sites occurs when the Hsp70 is ATP-bound and thus in the low-affinity state. The SBD in this state is structurally different from the state our isolated SBDs mimic. To what extent that initial encounter impacts the eventual binding to sites is not obvious. Finally, a final aspirational goal is to move this kind of study into the cellular context and to test the relationship of our conclusions to physiological situations.
