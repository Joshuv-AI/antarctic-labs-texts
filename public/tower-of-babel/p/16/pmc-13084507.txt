Pore Structure and Controlling Factors of Continental Shale in the Xiagou Formation, Qingxi Sag, Jiuxi Basin
Authors: Shiming Zhang, Shuxin Pan, Tao Li, Tingjing Zhang, Guodong Wang, Dazhong Ren, Xiaojun Zhang, Liwen Long, Bochao Yuan, Deqiang Wei, Siyu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To clarify the development characteristics and controlling factors of nanomicron pores in lacustrine shale, this study examines the Cretaceous Xiagou Formation shale reservoir in the Qingxi Sag of the Jiuxi Basin. The rock composition, pore types, and pore structure of continental shale were analyzed using whole-rock X-ray diffraction, laser confocal microscopy, nitrogen adsorption, nuclear magnetic resonance, and high-pressure mercury injection. The different pore reservoir properties were compared, and the factors influencing reservoir development were investigated. The findings indicate that the Xiagou Formation shale comprises a mixture of carbonate and terrigenous clasts, with the carbonate primarily consisting of micritic microcrystalline dolomite. The shale contains various pore types, including nanometer-scale intercrystalline pores, organic matter pores, clay mineral pores, micrometre-scale brittle mineral intergranular pores, millimeter-scale brittle mineral intergranular dissolution pores, and fractures. Organic matter pores exhibit a dispersed distribution and significant heterogeneity. Based on pore size distribution, continental shale pores are classified into three types: (1) isolated nanopores, (2) relatively concentrated nanopores, and (3) nanomicron multiscale composite pores, with the third type exhibiting the most favorable reservoir properties. The study concludes that high-quality reservoir pores in continental shale are primarily influenced by dolomitization and the dissolution of brittle mineral pores by organic acids.

Introduction

The large-scale development of marine shale oil and gas in China has been achieved by leveraging exploration experience from North America and advancing specialized theories and technologies. Continental lacustrine basins are widely distributed across China, offering significant exploration potential for shale oil. However, the overall exploration rate remains relatively low. Accelerating the exploration of terrestrial shale oil and gas has become a key strategy for ensuring the sustainable development of oil and gas resources. − In North America, marine shale oil reservoirs are typically characterized by carbonate laminae or interlayers. − Productive shale gas layers mainly consist of feldspathic-clayey shale lithologies. − In China, terrestrial shale formations are classified into interbedded/interlayered, mixed sedimentary and argillaceous types. − In recent years, significant progress has been made in shale oil and gas exploration within formations such as the middle and lower Jurassic of the Sichuan Basin, the Lucaogou Formation in the Jimusar Sag of the Junggar Basin, the Shahejie Formation in the Jiyang Depression of the Bohai Bay Basin, the Qingshankou Formation in the Gulong Sag of the Songliao Basin and the seventh member of the Yanchang Formation in the Ordos Basin. − However, continental shale oil differs from marine shale in terms of sedimentary environment, lithofacies assemblage, organic matter types and thermal evolution. As a result, theoretical insights derived from marine shale exploration cannot be directly applied to continental shale. − Additionally, continental shale is characterized by frequent changes in sedimentary facies belts, limited spatial distribution, predominantly mixed sedimentary lithology and a low degree of organic matter evolution. These factors complicate research on terrestrial shale formation mechanisms and enrichment patterns. −

The Cretaceous system in the Qingxi Sag serves as the primary hydrocarbon source rock in the basin. Researchers have systematically studied the maceral composition, abundance and hydrocarbon generation potential of organic matter. Their findings indicate that the main hydrocarbon-generating components can be classified into five types: sapropelic amorphous, humic amorphous and planktonic alginate. Organic matter is primarily found within algal laminae, micritic dolomite laminae and clay laminae. − Studies on the sedimentary characteristics, lithofacies assemblages and genesis of Cretaceous lacustrine deposits suggest that the Lower Gou Formation of the Cretaceous represents a semideep lacustrine deposit, where laminated and massive mixed sediments of terrigenous clastics and carbonates are widely developed. − The predominant lithologies include argillaceous dolomite, dolomitic mudstone and calcareous mudstone. − Additionally, some researchers classify the Xiagou Formation as a tight oil system within a mixed-depositional environment and have analyzed factors such as the driving mechanism for tight oil migration, types of tight oil reservoirs and reservoir formation processes. − However, research on shale oil in the Xiagou Formation remains limited, and the overall level of study is relatively low.

The author suggests that the Xiagou Formation is characterized by well-developed micron- to millimeter-scale laminae, organic matter enrichment and a relatively high degree of thermal evolution in some samples, aligning with the definition of continental shale oil. The laminated mixed shale constitutes the primary component of shale oil in the Xiagou Formation, with the interbedded source-reservoir combination and the integrated source-reservoir combination being the dominant reservoir models. This study utilized core samples of mixed shale from the Xiagou Formation. Laser confocal microscopy and field emission scanning electron microscopy were employed to qualitatively analyze the types and distribution of pores in the samples. Additionally, nitrogen adsorption, nuclear magnetic resonance and high-pressure mercury injection were used to quantitatively assess pore-throat structures and morphologies. By integrating these test results with data on rock mineral composition, total organic carbon content and organic carbon thermal maturity, the study examined the factors influencing microscopic pore development in the Xiagou Formation. The findings identify the characteristics and controlling factors of favorable reservoir spaces in lacustrine shales.

Regional Geological Background

The Jiuquan Basin is situated at the intersection of the Altyn Tagh Fault, the Alxa Block and the North Qilian Orogenic Belt. It is subdivided into the Western Jiuquan Depression, the Jiayuguan Uplift and the Eastern Jiuquan Depression from west to east − (Figure a). The basin is underlain by Paleozoic fold structures and has undergone two distinct evolutionary stages: an extensional fault depression during the Early Cretaceous and a compressional depression in the Paleogene. During the Early Cretaceous, localized tensile stress influenced the basin, resulting in the formation of NE- and NNE-trending transtensional faults and a faulted lacustrine basin. − The Qingxi Sag is located in the southeastern part of the Western Jiuquan Basin and contains two key structural features: the Kulongshan Anticline and the Yaerxia large nose-shaped structure. − The Lower Cretaceous sequence consists of deposits formed in a semideep to deep lacustrine saline environment, with local occurrences of braided river delta and fluvial facies. The base of the sequence unconformably overlies Jurassic or Archean strata, while the top is in unconformable contact with overlying Cenozoic strata. From bottom to top, the formations include the Chijinbao Formation (K1c), the Xiagou Formation (K1g) and the Zhonggou Formation (K1z), with the Chijinbao and Xiagou Formations serving as the primary regional hydrocarbon source rocks (Figure b,c). −

Samples and Methods

The samples collected in this study are mainly from drilling cores of wells including Li9, Li8, Li10, L3, and L105. Sample selection was based on core description records and logging data of drilled wells, prioritizing intervals with complete coring (continuous coring length >10 m) in the Xiagou Formation. Priority was given to intervals dominated by organic-rich shale, while additional samples of mudstone, siltstone, and argillaceous dolomite interbedded with organic-rich shale were also collected. Notably, wells with significant oil and gas shows (e.g., Li9) were incorporated into the sampling scheme. Furthermore, based on sedimentary facies analysis results of samples from different wells, samples deposited in shore-shallow lake to semideep lake facies were selected. Horizontally, considering the sedimentary facies map, samples were collected from wells in different positions of the lake basin, including the lake basin center (Li10) and marginal areas (Wells Li8 and Li10 in the southeastern lake basin, and Wells L3 and L105 in the western lake basin). Vertically, for intervals dominated by organic-rich shale, one sample was collected every 1.5–2 m. For rocks interbedded or transitional with organic-rich shale, one sample was collected at the lithological transition zone and another from the internal part of the interval. In summary, sample selection took into account multiple factors, including sedimentary facies, lithological variations, and different positions from the lake basin center to the margin. Parallel samples were tested for organic geochemistry and rock composition to determine the fundamental petrological and mineralogical characteristics. The pore types and their distribution in the samples were examined using laser confocal microscopy and scanning electron microscopy. , To analyze the nano- to micron-scale pore-throat structure, nitrogen adsorption, high-pressure mercury injection and nuclear magnetic resonance techniques were employed. − The key characteristics and primary controlling factors of high-quality reservoirs were assessed by integrating test results on rock chemistry and basic physical properties.

Rock and Mineral Analysis

The structural and compositional characteristics of the samples were identified using an optical microscope. Thin-section samples were polished on both sides to a thickness of 0.03 mm for observation. A ZEISS Axio Scope A1 microscope was used to examine the mineral composition and microstructure of the rock thin sections. The relative mineral content in the samples was determined using a PANalytical Empyrean X-ray diffractometer. For this analysis, rock samples were ground into a powder with a particle size of less than 40 μm. A 2-g portion of the powder was weighed and pressed into a test piece. During the analysis, the working voltage was set at 40 kV, the working current at 40 mA, the scanning speed at 2° (2θ)/min and the sampling step width at 0.02° (2θ). The mineral content percentages were calculated based on the integrated intensity of the diffraction peaks. , The total organic carbon content in the samples was measured using a CS-230 carbon and sulfur analyzer. Prior to testing, the samples were ground to a particle size of approximately 0.15 mm. To remove carbonate minerals, the samples were treated with dilute hydrochloric acid, then rinsed with distilled water and dried at 80 °C for 12 h before analysis. ,

Observation of Pore Morphology

A Hitachi IM4000 ion milling machine and a Quanta 450 FEG field emission environmental scanning electron microscope (SEM) from FEI Company were used to observe mineral distribution and the morphological characteristics of nanopore throats on both fresh and polished sample surfaces. For fresh surface observations under the SEM, samples were cut into cubes or irregular blocks with a volume of approximately 1 cm3. For polished surface observations, small samples (8 mm × 8 mm × 5 mm) were first cut using mechanical methods, and the target surfaces were ground into smooth planes suitable for argon ion polishing. The samples were the placed in an argon ion polisher, where argon ions bombarded the surfaces to achieve polishing. A thin layer of conductive metal (Au) was coated on the observation surfaces and the samples were affixed to the sample stage with conductive glue to ensure electrical connectivity. The prepared samples were then placed in the SEM sample chamber for observation.

The SEM operates by directing a finely focused electron beam onto the sample surface. It analyses the surface morphology using secondary electrons, backscattered electrons and other signals generated from the interaction between electrons and the sample. , The working voltage was set at 20 kV, with an incident electron beam current ranging from 3 × 10–10 to 4 × 10–10 A. Additionally, the oil-bearing properties and pore characteristics of rock thin sections were observed and statistically analyzed using Leica’s TCS-SP5II laser scanning confocal microscope.

Multiscale Pore Structure Testing

Nitrogen Adsorption Testing

Nitrogen adsorption tests were conducted using an ASAP 2460 specific surface area and porosity adsorption analyzer to measure the nitrogen adsorption capacity and isothermal adsorption curves of the samples. This method allows for the characterization of pore distribution within the 50 nm range. During testing, the adsorption capacity and the shape of the adsorption–desorption curves provide insights into the quantity, shape and distribution of pores. The specific surface area and pore size distribution were determined using the BET and BJH models, respectively. − The samples were crushed to a particle size of 0.25–0.425 mm. A quantity of 5–20 g of the crushed material was dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The samples were then degassed under a high vacuum at 110 °C and <10 mmHg for 12 h to completely remove moisture and volatile hydrocarbons. High-purity helium (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas, high-purity nitrogen (99.999%) served as the adsorbate. Gas adsorption experiments were conducted at −195.8 °C under varying pressures, generating isothermal adsorption and desorption curves within a relative pressure range of 0.005–0.996. ,

High-Pressure Mercury Intrusion Testing

The pore-throat distribution and pore structure of samples with pore sizes exceeding 3 nm were analyzed using the AutoPore IV 9510 high-pressure mercury intrusion porosimeter. The test samples were required to be block-shaped, with a maximum length not exceeding 2.5 cm and subjected to a maximum experimental pressure of 200 MPa. , Prior to testing, the samples were dried at 105 °C until a constant weight was achieved. The mercury intrusion experiment involved two phases: a mercury injection phase under increasing pressure and a mercury extrusion phase under decreasing pressure. During testing, pressure was first increased to the maximum experimental value and then reduced back to the initial pressure. Based on Washburn’s theory, the pore-throat size distribution was determined from the mercury injection curve.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Testing

The MR12–060H–I nuclear magnetic resonance imaging analyzer and a high-speed centrifuge were used to examine the nanomicron pore throats and fluid distribution characteristics of the samples. Nuclear magnetic resonance experiments conducted before and after centrifugation provided insights into pore connectivity. When a water-saturated rock sample is placed within a uniform static magnetic field, the hydrogen nuclei (1H) in the fluid become polarized by the field, generating a macroscopic magnetization vector. Applying a radiofrequency (RF) field of a specific frequency induces nuclear magnetic resonance. Once the RF field is removed, an exponentially decaying signal is detected. The signal decay rate is characterized by two parameters: longitudinal relaxation time (T 1) and transverse relaxation time (T 2). − The transverse relaxation time is approximately equal to the surface relaxation time, which is positively correlated with the sample’s specific surface area. Since the specific surface area is related to pore radius, a conversion relationship exists between the T 2 relaxation time and the pore-throat radius. −

The saturated rock core underwent high-speed centrifugation, a process that can be considered analogous to gas displacement. As the centrifugation speed increases, the pore throats through which air can expel movable fluids become progressively smaller. In the final stage of centrifugation, only bound fluid remains within the pores. By comparing the nuclear magnetic relaxation spectra before and after centrifugation, the relative proportion of movable fluid and its distribution characteristics within the pore throats can be determined. ,

Before testing, the core was cleaned using a mixture of alcohol and benzene in an oil-washing apparatus for approximately 1 week, until the solution’s color remained unchanged. It was then removed and dried. The core plug’s length was measured and its weight recorded. Gas porosity and permeability were measured before proceeding with the nuclear magnetic centrifugation test. The sample was saturated with distilled water using a vacuum-pressurized saturation device. It was subjected to vacuum conditions for 4 h, followed by pressurized saturation at 20 MPa for 20 h. After saturation, the samples were removed, wrapped in polytetrafluoroethylene film (which does not generate a nuclear magnetic resonance signal) and then placed in a high-temperature and high-pressure nuclear magnetic resonance holder for measurement. Multiple centrifugation-nuclear magnetic resonance tests were performed until the movable fluid in the T 2 relaxation spectrum remained unchanged before and after the final test. A nuclear magnetic resonance test was then conducted under bound water conditions. After each T 2 relaxation spectrum test, a two-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance T 1-T 2 detection was carried out. The maximum centrifugal rotation speed used in the experiment was 8000 rpm (r/min). When the core length was 3.5 cm, the corresponding centrifugal pressure was approximately 2.88 MPa. The testing equipment used was the MesoMR12–060H–I nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer, operating at a resonance frequency of approximately 12 MHz with a magnetic field strength of 0.5 T. The minimum test echo (TE) spacing was about 0.06 ms. For the T 1-T 2 spectrum test, the SR-CPMG sequence was applied, with the following parameters: waiting time (TE) of 10 ms, stacking frequency (NS) of 32 and echo spacing of 0.07 ms. The high-speed centrifuge model used was the CSC-12, which had a maximum rotation speed of 10,000 r/min, a maximum centrifugal force of 21,250g and an operating temperature range of – 20 to +40 °C.

Petrological Characteristics

The rocks are generally fine-grained, with grain sizes at the silt-grade or finer. Their colors are predominantly gray and dark gray. The composition is mixed, with carbonate and argillaceous materials visible to the naked eye. Core observations indicate that the primary components include terrigenous clastics, carbonates (micrite-, microcrystalline calcite and dolomite), salt minerals and rock fragments. The rocks primarily exhibit massive and horizontal bedding, with some occurrences of deformed bedding. In the logging cores, most oil-bearing intervals display fluorescence, while a small portion shows only oil traces.

Rock Composition

Terrigenous Clastics

The primary terrigenous clastic components consist of quartz, feldspar and clay minerals, with minor amounts of rock fragments, mainly derived from intermediate to acidic igneous rocks. Hematite and analcime are also present. Quartz content ranges from 0.6 to 54.8%, with an average of 14.36%. Under the microscope, quartz appears as irregular and subangular grains with good sorting. Feldspar content varies from 0 to 60.2%, averaging 22.84%, with plagioclase as the dominant type (ranging from 2.4 to 58.2%, averaging 21.05%). Overall, terrestrial debris contains a relatively high proportion of stable components such as quartz and plagioclase.

Clay Minerals

Clay mineral content in the rock varies from 3.2 to 58%, averaging 19.18%. Based on the relative content analysis of 38 samples, mixed-layer Illite/montmorillonite (56.13%) and Illite (39.61% on average) are the predominant clay minerals. Small amounts of kaolinite (4.28% on average) and chlorite (3.54% on average) are also present, while montmorillonite is absent. The formation of these clay minerals is primarily attributed to physical weathering in an alkaline diagenetic environment.

Carbonates

Carbonates are interspersed with terrestrial clastics and primarily consist of micritic microcrystalline calcite and dolomite. These carbonates are often mixed with clay minerals and occur in a matrix or as layered and strip-like formations, interbedded with silt to form horizontal bedding. Overall, carbonate components are widely distributed. X-ray diffraction analysis shows that carbonate content ranges from 0 to 71.8%, with an average of 33.19%. Dolomite content varies from 0 to 71.8%, averaging 29.71%. Micritic-crystalline dolomite accounts for 22.76–100% of the carbonate content, with an average of 91.18%. Additionally, small amounts of ankerite, associated with hydrothermal activity, appear in banded or massive form.

Salt Minerals

Salt minerals primarily consist of gypsum, mirabilite and glauberite. These minerals mainly form through evaporation, though some are influenced by hydrothermal fluids. Gypsum and anhydrite occur as massive or nodular structures within the mud and micritic carbonate matrix, with some appearing in fibrous form or filling fractures. Mirabilite frequently coexists with gypsum. The overall salt mineral content ranges from 0 to 27.2%, averaging 6.17%. Glauberite content varies from 0.4 to 23.3%, with an average of 7.83%, while mirabilite ranges from 0.2 to 23.7%, averaging 3.20%. Gypsum content falls between 0 and 9.3%, with an average of 2.71%. The distribution of salt minerals is uneven in the vertical profile.

Rock Types and Characteristics

The sample exhibits a combination of detrital and carbonate sedimentary features, with a certain proportion of salt minerals. The primary components are dolomite and detrital material with particle sizes below the silt-grade. Based on rock texture, microscopic thin-section characteristics and diffraction mineral composition, the samples from the study area are categorized as follows: First, they are classified into shale, massive dolomitic mudstone, massive dolomitic siltstone, massive silty dolomite and massive argillaceous dolomite, depending on the presence or absence of laminations. Shale is further subdivided according to the mineral composition characteristics of its laminae.

The first category is argillaceous silty shale, where the laminations primarily consist of interbedded silt-grade clastic particles and argillaceous material. The silt-grade laminations are generally thicker than the argillaceous laminations, with clay and clastic components being predominant (Figure a1–a3). The second category is argillaceous dolomitic shale, characterized by laminations composed mainly of interbedded micritic dolomite and argillaceous layers. The dolomite laminations are relatively thicker than the argillaceous ones, and silty bands are occasionally present (Figure b1–b3). The third category is argillaceous siltstone, consisting of silt-grade clastic particles within an argillaceous matrix. The clastic particles are not in direct contact with each other, displaying characteristics of mixed deposition involving clay, silt and carbonate in a relatively homogeneous distribution (Figure c1–c3). The fourth category is silty dolomite, composed of silt-grade clastic particles within a micritic dolomite matrix. The clastic particles are scattered, and silty bands are occasionally observed (Figure d1–d3). The fifth category is mixed sedimentary rock, which contains silt- to fine-sand-grade clastic particles, calcareous-argillaceous rock fragments, carbonaceous fragments and a minor proportion of magmatic rock fragments. It has relatively low structural and compositional maturity (Figure e1–e3).

Characteristics of Pores

Pore Types

The formation, classification and distribution of pores in shale are key factors influencing the effectiveness of shale oil and gas reservoirs and the occurrence phases of hydrocarbons. These characteristics directly impact the assessment of shale oil and gas resources. Currently, there is no universally accepted classification system for shale pores. The most common classification methods are based on pore genesis, morphology, distribution and their relationship with rock particles. Shale pores are predominantly in the nanoscale to micron-scale range, with fractures playing a crucial role in connecting microscopic matrix pores and facilitating fluid seepage. − Techniques such as SEM, transmission electron microscopy and CT imaging have been employed to observe pore-bearing minerals, pore morphology and hydrocarbon content in pores. One of the most widely used classification frameworks is the scheme proposed by Loucks et al., which categorizes pores into three main types: intergranular, intragranular and organic matter pores. Other researchers classify shale pores based on the minerals present, dividing them into organic matter pores, brittle mineral pores and clay mineral pores. Some studies have expanded on Loucks’ classification by incorporating additional pore types, such as fossil cavities and mold pores. There are various classification systems for pore-throat size, primarily based on experimental measurements of pore-throat distribution. These classification schemes differ significantly across research fields. To maintain consistency and facilitate comparisons, shale pore size classification generally follows the IUPAC standard: micropores are those smaller than 2 nm, mesopores range from 2 to 50 nm, and macro-pores are those larger than 50 nm.

In this study, based on existing pore classification schemes, the pores in continental shale samples from the study area are categorized into four types according to their origin: brittle mineral particle pores, organic matter pores, clay mineral pores and intercrystalline mineral pores.

Brittle mineral pores are formed through diagenetic processes such as accumulation, dissolution and cementation of brittle minerals. These include intergranular pores, which consist of residual intergranular pores and intergranular dissolved pores, pore size distribution in brittle minerals typically ranges from tens of nanometers to several micrometers, corresponding to macropores (and larger) in IUPAC classification and interparticle pores in Loucks shale pore classification. Organic matter pores originate within organic matter and form as a result of hydrocarbon generation and expulsion, pore size of organic matter pores corresponds to micropores and mesopores in the IUPAC classification, with mesopores being dominant. Some pores can also reach the micrometer scale, corresponding to organic matter pores in Loucks’ shale pore classification. There are significant differences between terrestrial and marine shales in this regard. Marine shales typically have a stable burial environment with widely distributed organic matter, primarily consisting of high-quality Type I and Type II kerogen. The thermal evolution of organic matter in marine shales is relatively high. − In contrast, lacustrine shales exhibit a highly heterogeneous distribution of organic matter, with lower hydrocarbon generation potential and a lower degree of thermal evolution. As a result, organic pores are less dominant in terrestrial shales compared to marine shales. , Clay mineral pores are the spaces between clay mineral particles, primarily formed due to clay mineral transformation and the release of bound water. These pores are predominantly nanoscale in size. Intercrystalline mineral pores are micropores located between mineral crystal particles. In terrestrial shales, pyrite intercrystalline and dolomite intercrystalline pores are well-developed. The dolomitisation degree in terrestrial shales is relatively low, with dolomite particles primarily in the micritic to fine-grained range. Despite the small crystal size, dolomite intercrystalline pores are widely distributed and relatively homogeneous. The presence of nano- to micron-scale intercrystalline pores contributes to the formation of effective reservoir spaces (Figure ). Pore size of intercrystalline pores typically ranges from tens of nanometers to hundreds of nanometers, mainly corresponding to macropores and larger-scale pores in the IUPAC classification, and Intraparticle Pores in Loucks’ shale pore classification.

Brittle Mineral Pores

In the study area, mixed shale, mixed sedimentary rock and argillaceous siltstone account for approximately 10% of the rock types. These rocks contain a relatively high proportion of terrigenous clastic particles (20–50%), predominantly composed of quartz, feldspar and igneous rock fragments. The content of quartz and igneous rock fragments vary significantly, while feldspar is mainly plagioclase. Calcite cementation is present within intergranular pores, with residual intergranular pores primarily occurring in well-sorted silty masses or bands. The pore shapes are mainly triangular, elongated or irregularly polygonal, with sizes ranging from 2 to 30 μm, most commonly around 10 μm. Both intergranular dissolved pores and a small number of intragranular dissolved pores are observed. Intergranular dissolved pores are primarily found in mixed rocks, organic-rich shales and argillaceous dolomites. In mixed rocks, these pores are mainly formed through the action of acidic fluids, leading to the dissolution and expansion of primary intergranular pores. The debris particles in mixed rock samples from the study area exhibit poor structural and compositional sorting, with soluble micritic carbonate rock fragments and igneous rock fragments providing favorable conditions for the development of intergranular dissolution pores. In organic-rich shales, the laminae primarily consist of organic-bearing argillaceous material, carbonate and argillaceous silt. Intergranular dissolved pores develop at points of contact with organic matter laminae or in areas where fractures connect to these laminae.

Organic Matter Pores

Organic pores are a significant pore type in shale and serve as the primary sites for adsorbed shale oil. , In continental shale, the distribution of organic matter pores is highly heterogeneous. In shale samples from the Xiagou Formation, organic matter is mainly concentrated within the argillaceous laminae, where it occurs in elongated strips, irregular filaments and ellipsoidal shapes. Within the argillaceous matrix, organic matter is typically distributed in similar elongated or ellipsoidal forms. Organic pores are found either within the organic matter itself or at the interface between organic matter and minerals. The organic pores inside organic matter can be classified into two types: The first type consists of pores in organic matter with a lower degree of thermal evolution, which appear as strip-shaped or ellipsoidal voids. These are primarily framework pores associated with the original structure of the organic matter. The second type consists of pores distributed as dot-like formations within filamentous or irregular strip-shaped organic matter or as triangular or irregular microcracks along the edges of organic matter particles. These pores result from the thermal evolution of organic matter, forming due to volume shrinkage or the expulsion of hydrocarbons.

Intercrystalline Pores

In study area, dolomite is present in relatively high amounts, with micritic dolomite being the predominant type. In certain locations, well-crystallized microcrystalline dolomite aggregates can be observed. The crystal sizes of microcrystalline dolomite range from 2 to 10 μm, with most dolomite crystals exhibiting rhombohedral or cubic shapes. The intercrystalline pores within microcrystalline dolomite are primarily triangular or irregular quadrilateral in shape, with pore-throat radii ranging from several dozen nanometers to a few micrometers. SEM analysis reveals that clay mineral pores in the study area mainly develop between Illite and mixed-layer Illite/montmorillonite. It is generally understood that with increasing burial depth, changes in mineral particle volume during clay mineral transformations contribute to the formation of clay mineral pores. The crystal morphology of different clay minerals influences the shape and scale of these pores. Pores within mixed-layer Illite/montmorillonite are typically linear or strip-shaped. Additionally, narrow slit-shaped or triangular pores are occasionally observed at the contact points between clay minerals and detrital particles.

Pore Combinations

The preservation of primary pores and the development of secondary pores are influenced by the rock’s mineral composition, fabric and diagenetic evolution. After diagenetic processes, the pores in different lithofacies typically consist of multiple pore types. To objectively evaluate the distribution and connectivity of pores in the samples, this study classifies pore combinations based on lithofacies and diagenetic characteristics:1)Clay mineral pore–residual intergranular pore combination: This combination is primarily found in argillaceous and silty shale, which contain a relatively high proportion of clay minerals. The dominant pore types include clay mineral pores, along with a small number of residual intergranular pores within silt particles. These pores have not undergone significant dissolution.2)Dolomite intercrystalline pore–clay mineral pore combination: This combination mainly occurs in argillaceous dolomitic shale, which consists of alternating dolomite and argillaceous laminae. The dolomite laminae are typically 5–10 times thicker than the argillaceous laminae. The primary pores in this combination are intercrystalline pores between dolomite crystals, with a smaller proportion of clay mineral pores.3)Organic pore–clay mineral pore–intergranular pore combination: This combination occurs in silty shale with well-developed organic matter laminae. The organic matter is present along bedding planes in laminar or irregular filamentous forms. In addition to organic matter laminae, silty and minor carbonate laminae are also present. Organic pores are primarily located at the edges of organic matter, with some organic pores forming within the organic material itself. Intergranular pores develop between detrital grains in sandy masses or bands. Dissolution pores, formed by organic acid dissolution, are observed mainly along interlayer fractures. Pore Size Distribution Characteristics Based on High-Pressure Mercury Intrusion Experiment

Based on the characteristics of mercury injection and withdrawal curves, as well as of variations in mercury injection volume per unit mass, the mercury injection curves can be classified into three types. The first type of mercury injection curve consists of two segments: one relatively gentle and the other relatively steep. The mercury withdrawal curve is a smooth curve with a consistent slope. In the low-pressure stage (≤30 MPa), the mercury injection curve exhibits a gentle slope, with the mercury injection volume ranging between 0.0015 and 0.00029 mL g–1. In the medium- and high-pressure stages, the curve steepens, with the mercury injection volume ranging from 0.00367 to 0.00706 mL g–1. The transition point where the mercury injection volume changes occurs at 35 MPa, corresponding to a pore-throat radius of 21 nm. This type of mercury injection curve indicates that the pore-throat radius in the sample’s high-volume mercury injection section ranges from 5 to 21 nm. The pores are predominantly nanoscale, exhibiting some degree of connectivity, though overall connectivity is relatively poor (Figure a1). The mercury intrusion pore throat distribution curve shows that the mercury intrusion per unit mass of the sample exhibits a unimodal distribution, with the peak mainly within 10 nm. The adsorption curves of 15 samples from the study area exhibit an inverse S-shape. At low relative pressure, gas adsorption volume increases rapidly as pressure rises. In the medium relative pressure stage, the adsorption volume grows more gradually. At high relative pressure, adsorption volume again rises sharply, and the curve eventually becomes perpendicular to the x-axis without showing signs of saturation. Comparison of Pore Characterization Results from Different Testing Methods

The samples in this study develop nano- to micrometer-scale pores. Generally, nitrogen adsorption is the most effective experimental method for characterizing nanoscale pore structures, which can be used to determine the pore distribution characteristics of micropores (<2 nm) and mesopores (2–50 nm). High-pressure mercury intrusion (HPMI) can characterize pores ranging from several nanometers to hundreds of micrometers, compensating for the limitation of nitrogen adsorption in characterizing macropores and larger-scale pores. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is capable of testing pores from several nanometers to hundreds of micrometers, serving as a full-scale pore characterization method.

Comparing the results of HPMI and nitrogen adsorption, the two methods show consistent pore throat distribution ranges when characterizing micropores and mesopores. The consistency between HPMI and nitrogen adsorption is better in samples with well-developed nanoscale pores. For pores smaller than 20 nm, nitrogen adsorption can more accurately characterize the pore distribution characteristics.

Results and Discussion

The micrometer-scale pores in the samples primarily include fractures, detrital grain dissolution pores, carbonate dissolution pores and residual intergranular pores. The fractures can be classified into three types: diagenetic fractures (S1, S7), hydrocarbon expulsion overpressure fractures (S2, S3) and interlayer fractures (S4). Most dissolution pores are distributed along fractures. CT scanning results and nuclear magnetic resonance relaxation time spectra indicate that fractures and the dissolution pores connected to them significantly enhance the storage capacity of fractures. Samples with well-developed interlayer fractures often exhibit intense yellow fluorescence. Organic-rich laminae serve as the primary sites for in situ hydrocarbon accumulation in shale. Additionally, interlayer fractures play a crucial role in the initial migration of hydrocarbons within organic laminae. The nuclear magnetic resonance relaxation spectrum confirms that fractures exhibit strong connectivity. Moreover, fractures often codevelop with organic pores and dissolution pores, facilitating shale oil flow.

The dissolution of organic acids is the primary process responsible for the formation of detrital grain dissolution pores. These pores are predominantly found in regions with high feldspar and carbonate fragment content and well-developed organic matter (S5, P5, S8). CT slice analysis reveals that dissolution significantly enhances the pore size and connectivity of carbonate matrix micropores. For instance, in samples with well-developed dissolution pores (e.g., N5, N8), the average connected pore volume is 25.45%, with a range of 9.19 to 54.22% (N = 9). Conversely, in samples with minimal dissolution (e.g., N6), the average connected pore volume is only 2.74%, ranging from 0.17 to 6.72% (N = 3) (Figure ).

Influence of Organic Matter Content and Thermal Maturity on Micronano Pores

Studies on marine shales indicate that organic pore development is primarily controlled by total organic carbon (TOC) content, organic matter type and thermal maturity. − The amount of organic matter provides the foundation for organic pore formation. In general, marine sedimentary environments exhibit stable organic matter deposition. , In contrast, lacustrine organic matter content is highly variable, displaying significant heterogeneity. Additionally, continental environments are strongly influenced by terrigenous clastics, which hinders organic matter deposition. As a result, organic pores in lacustrine shales tend to be dispersed and heterogeneous. − For organic pores to develop effectively, organic matter must reach a certain level of maturity. In low-maturity shales, honeycomb-like organic pores are generally absent. Medium-maturity shales enter the active hydrocarbon generation stage, where organic pores form due to organic matter evolution and hydrocarbon expulsion. Furthermore, organic acids and overpressure generated during hydrocarbon formation help preserve organic pores. However, in high-maturity shales, organic pores undergo compaction due to a decrease in internal pressure following hydrocarbon expulsion. −

SEM observations reveal that organic matter in the samples appears in dot-like, fibrous and banded forms. Organic pores primarily develop along the edges of the organic matter or as elongated slits within it. Their formation is strongly influenced by the primary structure of the organic matter. Throughout organic matter evolution, the number of pores generated by hydrocarbon formation and expulsion is relatively low (Figure ).

The development of organic pores I” als’ linked to kerogen type. Kerogen maceral analysis indicates that the sapropelinite content is relatively low, with most samples containing less than 30%. The primary components are exinite (ranging from 20.4 to 67.3%, averaging 44.7%) and vitrinite (ranging from 14.1 to 68.4%, averaging 30.7%), classifying the kerogen predominantly as Type II. Generally, Type I kerogen, which has a high sapropelinite content, is more favorable for organic pore development. However, the kerogen in the study area is less conducive to organic pore formation.

Organic geochemical analysis data indicate a positive correlation between TOC content and both chloroform bitumen A and pyrolysis S1. This suggests that higher organic carbon content corresponds to greater free-state shale oil content. Additionally, the formation of free-state shale oil is accompanied by the development of more organic pores. The vitrinite reflectance (Ro) of the samples (ranging from 0.56 to 1.08, with an average of 0.75, N = 7) also shows a positive correlation with both specific surface area and total pore volume. This indicates that higher organic matter maturity enhances hydrocarbon generation, leading to an increase in adsorption and storage capacity within nanoscale micropores.

Across the entire well section, TOC values are mainly within the 2–4% range, and organic pores are present throughout. Above 4900 m, the pyrolysis S2 content is relatively high, suggesting that most organic matter has not undergone significant hydrocarbon generation, with Ro < 0.75. Below 4900 m, Ro increases with depth, indicating that organic matter has reached maturity, leading to a significant increase in free hydrocarbon content (Figure ).

The thermal maturity of organic matter influences organic pore formation. Above 4900 m, hydrocarbon expulsion has not been significant and organic pores primarily develop at the edges of organic matter. However, no distinct organic pores are formed within the organic matter due to hydrocarbon generation. Below 4900 m, as free hydrocarbons form, organic pores begin to develop within the organic matter. These pores are oil-filled and retain some organic material. Additionally, organic-rich laminae generate interlayer fractures due to free hydrocarbon expulsion. Clay mineral pores and interlayer fractures also form within clay minerals interbedded with organic matter (Figure ).

Influence of Clay Mineral Content on Micronano Pores

The type, content and crystallinity of clay minerals are closely linked to the development of clay mineral pores. Based on SEM observations and X-ray diffraction analysis, the clay mineral content varies widely, ranging from 3.20 to 58.00%, with an average of 19.18%. Among the samples, seven exhibit a relatively high chlorite content (14.00–22.00%). Chlorite appears as irregular flakes and is distributed along the laminae with Illite and mixed-layer Illite/montmorillonite, though no distinct crystal structures are observed. These samples develop elongated clay mineral micropores. Some samples contain kaolinite (11.00–15.00%, averaging 13.8% in five samples), coexisting with Illite and mixed-layer Illite/montmorillonite. However, kaolinite’s characteristic book-like pores are absent. In most samples, mixed-layer Illite/montmorillonite and Illite are the dominant clay minerals. Mixed-layer Illite/montmorillonite is the most abundant, while the content of other clay minerals does not exceed 10%. As unstable transitional minerals, mixed-layer Illite/montmorillonite lack prominent crystal structures and develop fewer micropores than other clay minerals.

A positive correlation has been observed between clay mineral content and both nitrogen adsorption-specific surface area and pore volume (Figure a,b). This suggests that clay minerals play a role in the development of pores smaller than 50 nm. First, organic matter primarily precipitates alongside clay minerals, which influences the formation of organic pores. Second, clay minerals themselves develop nanoscale pores. The clay mineral content also shows a strong positive correlation with the specific surface area and pore volume of pores in the 2–10 nm and >50 nm ranges (Figure c,d). Pores in the 2–10 nm range are primarily clay mineral pores, while pores larger than 50 nm are intergranular pores formed through the interaction of clay minerals and detrital particles.

Influence of Diagenesis on Micron-Scale Pores

The porosity and permeability of the samples are generally low, with most porosity values below 5% and permeability mostly under 0.1 mD. A correlation has been identified between porosity, mineral content and organic geochemical data along the vertical profile. The lack of a consistent relationship between porosity and depth, along with the occurrence of localized high-porosity zones, suggests that pore development is not solely controlled by compaction. These high-porosity zones are closely linked to diagenetic processes or favorable sedimentary facies. Vertically, high-porosity zones correspond well with areas of high feldspar content, and there is a positive correlation between porosity and feldspar content. This indicates that the formation of high-porosity zones are primarily influenced by the preservation of intergranular pores among detrital grains and the dissolution of easily soluble minerals like feldspar (Figure a,b).

Relationship between feldspar content and reservoir physical parameters. (a) Correlation between feldspar content and porosity and (b) correlation between feldspar content and permeability.

The samples contain nano- to microscale pores, including clay mineral pores, dolomite intercrystalline pores and organic matter pores. Vertically, clay, dolomite and organic carbon contents do not show a clear correlation with porosity, nor is there an apparent relationship between these mineral contents and porosity. This lack of correlation is attributed to the fact that the development of clay mineral pores and dolomite intercrystalline pores depends not only on mineral content but also on crystallinity, mineral stacking patterns and other structural factors. Additionally, these micropores are dispersed throughout the samples, with no distinct zones of prominent pore development. The distribution of organic pores is mainly influenced by the organic matter type and its thermal evolution. A strong vertical correlation has been observed between vitrinite reflectance and nitrogen adsorption-specific surface area as well as total pore volume. Furthermore, TOC content is positively correlated with specific surface area and total pore volume, and a similar positive correlation exists between vitrinite reflectance, specific surface area and total pore volume. This suggests that when organic matter types are similar; the development of organic pores is controlled by organic carbon content and thermal evolution (Figure a,b).

The samples exhibit low sapropel content and a low degree of organic matter thermal evolution. Additionally, organic matter in the continental sedimentary environment is highly heterogeneous and dispersed. Consequently, the conditions necessary for the large-scale development of organic pores are not present in the study area.

Factors Influencing the Formation of High-Porosity Zones

High-porosity zones are primarily found at depths of 4400–4500 m, with porosities typically ranging from 10 to 15%. Laser confocal imaging reveals that the dominant pore types in these high-porosity zones are residual intergranular pores and intergranular dissolution pores.

The rock fabric with well-developed dissolution pores can be categorized into three types. The first category is the silt clumps or bands in argillaceous shale. These silt bands are well-sorted, with an argillaceous matrix filling the spaces between grains. High-porosity in these bands is mainly attributed to retention of primary intergranular pores among detrital grains; low argillaceous matrix content between grains; microfractures at the interface between the argillaceous matrix and silt bands, formed during diagenesis; and acidic fluids migrating through these microfractures, leading to dissolution of silt grains and the enlargement of intergranular pores.

The second category is the shale with argillaceous and fine-sand laminations. The detrital grains within the fine-sand laminations have low structural and compositional maturity. Some primary intergranular pores remain intact and dissolution of feldspar and carbonate rock fragments contributes to the formation of intergranular dissolved pores and intragranular dissolved pores.

The third type consists of medium sand-sized carbonate rock fragments within the mixed rock. These fragments are irregularly elliptical and embedded in the argillaceous matrix. The dissolution of carbonate rock fragments leads to the formation of moldic pores. Additionally, detrital grains and plant fragments are present, with strong fluorescence observed. The dissolution of carbonate rock fragments is primarily driven by organic acids.

Fractures and associated dissolution pores in the carbonate matrix also contribute to higher porosity and permeability. Interlayer fractures commonly develop at the interfaces between argillaceous laminations and silt laminations, organic-rich laminations and silt laminations and micritic carbonates and silt laminations. Both micritic carbonates and silt clastic particles adjacent to interlayer fractures are susceptible to dissolution. Areas where interlayer fractures intersect with overpressure fractures and structural fractures are characterized by well-developed dissolution pores in silt clastic particles and the micritic carbonate matrix. Interlayer fractures are widespread in continental shales. These open fractures provide storage and migration pathways for fluids such as hydrocarbons and organic acids. The organic acids dissolve easily soluble components on both sides of the fractures, expanding the reservoir space.

The vitrinite reflectance (Ro) of the samples in the study area reaches 0.5% at the burial depth of 4000 m, which indicates that the samples have entered the oil generation window since then, accompanied by the generation of organic acids in the process of hydrocarbon generation. Among them, 4400–4500 m and 4900–5000 m are intervals with high total organic carbon (TOC) content, and the interval of 4400–4500 m also has high free hydrocarbon content, indicating that this interval not only has high hydrocarbon generation intensity, but also has high content of accompanying organic acids.

It is worth noting that the feldspar content in the 4400–4500 m interval (average: 32.41%, range: 8.1–60.2%) is not only higher than the average feldspar content of the entire 4000–5000 m interval (25.2%, range: 0–60.2%) but also higher than that of the other high-TOC interval (4900–5000 m, average: 22.55%, range: 8.7–42.2%). The high organic acid content coupled with the high feldspar content in the 4400–4500 m interval confirms that this interval is a preferential development zone for dissolution pores.
