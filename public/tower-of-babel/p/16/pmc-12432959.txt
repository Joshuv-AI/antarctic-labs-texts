Clinical significance of transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunting for hepatocellular carcinoma complicated with portal hypertension
Authors: Feng Dai, Chunhan Pan, Chunyang Xu, Yu Yao, Mu Su, Jianwu Ren, Zhiyuan Qiu, Mario Capasso, Xiuming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPortal hypertension (PHT) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) often appear concurrently in clinic, and PHT variceal bleeding arising due to systemic treatment of HCC remains a clinical issue that urgently requires a solution. This study aimed to examine the therapeutic effects and clinical significance of transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunting (TIPS) in patients with HCC complicated with PHT.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 21 patients with HCC complicated with PHT who were admitted to The Second Hospital of Nanjing between June 2018 and June 2023 were included. TIPS was performed, and liver and kidney function, blood routine, and biochemical indicators such as ammonia were examined preoperatively, at 1 week postoperatively, and at 1 month postoperatively. To determine the improvements in stent blood flow, ascites, and esophageal and gastric varices, ultrasound and/or enhanced computed tomography were performed. Treatment was evaluated based on the Modified Solid Tumor Efficacy Evaluation Criteria. Parameters such as portal vein pressure and portal blood flow before and after TIPS were analyzed using paired-sample t-tests.ResultsPostoperatively, the direct pressure of the portal vein of patients decreased significantly from 28.33±7.15 to 19.27±3.10 mmHg (P<0.05). The amount of ascites also significantly decreased, whereas esophageal and gastric varicose veins significantly improved. Meanwhile, no significant differences were observed in liver or kidney function indicators, including bilirubin, transaminase, and creatinine, from the preoperative to the postoperative period.ConclusionsTIPS can effectively improve the various symptoms of PHT without increasing the incidence of liver function damage or other complications in patients with HCC complicated with PHT.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunting (TIPS) can effectively improve various symptoms of portal hypertension (PHT) without increasing the incidence of liver function damage and other complications in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) complicated with PHT.

What is known and what is new?

• PHT and HCC are considered to be the two major complications of cirrhosis in clinical settings. PHT variceal bleeding, a complication of PHT, can increase the mortality rate of patients with HCC and may occur due to systemic treatments for HCC. TIPS is an effective method for treating PHT and alleviating its complications, but it has not been widely applied in patients with HCC complicated with PHT owing to concerns regarding liver failure and tumor metastasis. Early guidelines for treating PHT include HCC complicated with PHT as a contraindication to TIPS.

• TIPS can be used as an early intervention therapy for HCC or as a comprehensive treatment strategy for portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT) in advanced HCC. TIPS neither increases the occurrence of serious complications, including abdominal bleeding and tumor rupture, nor leads to distant metastasis of HCC and associated liver function deterioration and failure. With the development of TIPS, the safety of TIPS is gradually improving for patients with complex HCC accompanied by PVTT. Furthermore, it can act as a bridging method for liver transplantation and provide the treatment time and opportunities for treating advanced HCC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Future studies should focus on how to establish personalized clinical therapy based on the PHT situation in the early phase of treatment for patients with different HCC stages.

Introduction

Portal hypertension (PHT) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are considered to be two key complications of cirrhosis in clinical settings. These two conditions often coexist and interact with one another, seriously lowering the survival expectations of cirrhotic patients (1).

As an important complication of liver cirrhosis, PHT frequently manifests as esophageal and gastric variceal bleeding, recurrent ascites, and hepatorenal syndrome. PHT not only exacerbates the mortality rate of patients with liver cirrhosis but also adversely affects their prognosis (2). One of the effective treatments for managing PHT-related complications is transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunting (TIPS), which forms a direct channel between the hepatic and portal veins via intervention, decreasing portal vein pressure (3).

HCC is considered a common malignant tumor in China. It is closely associated with post-hepatitic cirrhosis. Therefore, although new systemic therapies have significantly improved the survival of patients with advanced HCC, the coexistence of clinical PHT remains a significant issue, with the potential for variceal bleeding requiring an effective therapeutic approach. In this present study, we assessed the therapeutic value and clinical significance of TIPS in treating PHT among patients with HCC. Based on the relevant TIPS data from patients with HCC and PHT at The Second Hospital of Nanjing, we determined the safety and efficacy of TIPS and discussed the related issues. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jgo.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jgo-2025-365/rc).

Methods

Clinical data

In this study, 21 patients with HCC complicated with PHT who underwent TIPS at The Second Hospital of Nanjing between June 2018 and June 2023 were retrospectively analyzed. The clinical data were as follows: (I) All patients were diagnosed based on the diagnostic criteria of the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging classification. (II) All patients with HCC had a history of local ablation and/or transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE), including 1 case of right hepatectomy. (III) All patients exhibited signs and symptoms of gastrointestinal bleeding and recurrent ascites, with relevant endoscopic and imaging data supporting a PHT diagnosis (Table 1).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) a Child-Pugh rating of C for liver function, (II) distant organ metastasis and a physical Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group score of >2, and (III) hepatic encephalopathy appearing before the occurrence of PHT-related complications.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the ethics committee of The Second Hospital of Nanjing (No. 2025-LS-004) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Clinical treatment

All patients with concurrent PHT were required to undergo corresponding gastroscopy and/or internal hemostatic treatment for gastrointestinal bleeding before undergoing TIPS. Symptomatic treatment such as percutaneous puncture drainage, diuresis, and albumin infusion was administered for patients with recurrent ascites. Twenty-one patients with PHT underwent TIPS after tumor tissues and surgical contraindications to the TIPS surgical puncture channel were excluded.

TIPS was performed as follows: (I) Surgical contraindications were eliminated, various examinations were improved, and the puncture path was analyzed based on imaging data. (II) After routine local anesthesia was administered to the right side of the neck, the right internal jugular vein was punctured. Subsequently, the RUPS-100 puncture kit (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA) was placed at the opening of the right hepatic vein to puncture the portal vein. (III) After a successful puncture, a 5-F pigtail catheter was introduced, and pressure was measured. After portal vein angiography, a mixture of spring coils and tissue glue was used to embolize the corresponding varicose veins. The Viatorr stent (Gore Medical, Newark, DE, USA) with an appropriate length and a diameter of 8 mm was selected based on the puncture channel, and an 8 mm × 60 mm balloon was used for posterior expansion. (IV) An 8-mm bare stent system was placed for patients with portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT) or extensive thrombosis to maintain uninterrupted blood flow at the stent inlet. (V) Angiography was stopped, the stent was checked to ensure it was unfolded and in a good position, and the shunted portal vein pressure was measured. After surgery, routine symptomatic supportive treatments such as liver protection, diuresis, and anticoagulation were administered.

Local ablation and TACE for HCC were applied as follows: based on the diagnosis and treatment standards for patients with HCC, patients with different HCC stages were guided to receive on-demand TACE and/or local ablation. In TACE, super-selective intubation embolization was performed to achieve precise embolization and protect liver function. Systemic treatment (targeted and/or immunotherapy) was administered to patients with stages B and C HCC.

Efficacy criteria and follow-up

Liver and kidney function, blood routine, and biochemical indicators such as blood ammonia were measured at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively to investigate improvements and relief of clinical PHT-related gastrointestinal bleeding and ascites. Furthermore, to evaluate improvements in portal vein pressure, ascites, and esophageal and gastric varices, ultrasound and/or enhanced computed tomography were performed. Treatment was evaluated based on the Modified Solid Tumor Efficacy Evaluation Criteria. The efficacy of HCC treatment was classified as follows: complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Dose data with a normal distribution are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Parameters such as portal vein pressure and portal blood flow before and after TIPS were evaluated using paired-sample t-tests. A P value of <0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

The study cohort included 14 males and 7 females, with an age range of 37–67 years and average age of 54.12±10.83 years. Among them, according to BCLC, 6 patients were stage A, 10 stage B, and 5 stage C. A total of 21 patients underwent TIPS with 100% success. Furthermore, 20 patients underwent direct portal vein puncture via the hepatic vein (Figure 1). One patient with stage C HCC underwent TACE, radiotherapy, and systemic treatment for PVTT with cavernous degeneration. First, to open the portal vein and guide the puncture, percutaneous transhepatic portal vein puncture was performed, followed by portal vein puncture via the hepatic vein (Figure 2). The 8-mm Viatorr stent was implanted into all patients with HCC who underwent TIPS. If PVTT reached the main portal vein and affected liver blood flow, a corresponding 8-mm bare stent was implanted to ensure uninterrupted blood flow at the stent inlet. After TIPS, PHT symptoms were significantly relieved in all patients. However, two patients with stage A-B HCC developed hepatic encephalopathy, which improved after symptomatic treatment with drugs such as rifaximin, lactulose, and ornithine aspartate.

TACE combined with systemic treatment for a patient with HCC (BCLC-stage C) and gastrointestinal bleeding treated with TIPS. The patient was admitted with gastrointestinal bleeding in February 2021. A mass was noted in the posterior liver lobe, with portal vein invasion. Based on imaging findings, AFP levels, and medical history the patient was diagnosed with primary HCC (BCLC-stage C). After gastroscopy, ligation and sclerotherapy were performed to treat PHT-related bleeding, TACE was performed twice (March and April 2021), and systemic treatment with lenvatinib was administered. During this period, the tumor was evaluated as partial remission. However, during the treatment period, repeated gastrointestinal bleeding was observed. TIPS was performed in February 2022, which ameliorated the postoperative bleeding, and systemic treatment was continued. Discussion

PHT and HCC are considered the most common complications of liver cirrhosis and often occur simultaneously. Depending on the tumor stage, HCC treatment regimen includes radiofrequency ablation (RFA), TACE, and surgery. Oral nonselective β-receptor blocker therapy, gastroscopic ligation and sclerotherapy, and surgical disconnection are the treatment modalities for PHT management. Meanwhile, TIPS is commonly used to treat PHT due to its safety and efficacy (4,5).

Both HCC and PHT lead to multiple adverse disease outcomes in patients with liver cirrhosis. Monitoring and treating PHT are critical to preventing complications in patients with HCC (6). Compared with those of HCC, PHT-related complications are more urgent and can endanger a patient’s life. Gastrointestinal bleeding caused by PHT was one of the main causes of death in HCC patients, and repeated gastrointestinal bleeding can induce hepatic encephalopathy. Therefore, the alleviation of PHT-related symptoms forms the basis of HCC treatment, as it can facilitate subsequent HCC treatment (7). Researchers have used clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH) as a vital indicator to guide HCC treatment in clinical settings. CSPH can predict HCC occurrence and liver failure following liver resection (8). In patients with early-stage HCC, the incidence of PHT is 35–52%; however, in patients with intermediate-to-late-stage HCC, the proportion of PHT may be higher. This may be attributed to several factors, including severely decompensated liver cirrhosis and portal vein invasion. The hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) is increased in patients with HCC due to arteriovenous fistula and the local remodeling of liver cell structure (9,10). Therefore, in clinical settings, examining PHT in patients with HCC is vital because it can help prevent and treat PHT-related esophageal and gastric variceal bleeding events.

Over the past two decades, owing to the promotion of TIPS, the prognosis of patients in emergency care with esophageal and gastric variceal bleeding has markedly improved. However, the application of TIPS has not been widely applied in patients with HCC complicated with PHT due to concerns regarding postoperative liver failure and tumor metastasis. The most recent guidelines for treating PHT included HCC complicated with PHT as a contraindication for TIPS. Through extensive clinical research, it has now been confirmed that TIPS neither increases the occurrence of serious complications, including intra-abdominal bleeding and tumor rupture, nor leads to distant metastasis of HCC and associated liver function deterioration and failure. In contrast, it establishes conditions for liver transplantation and local treatment, assuming a bridging role (11). In our study, one of the twenty-one HCC patients was admitted to hospital due to gastrointestinal bleeding and was diagnosed with caudate lobe HCC (BCLC-stage A). Liver transplantation was planned for this patient, and thus TIPS was first performed to control bleeding and was followed by TACE and lenvatinib to treat the local tumor as a bridge therapy to liver transplantation. The patient underwent liver transplantation 3 months after maintenance therapy, with no tumor recurrence during the 1-year follow-up period. This case illustrates the possible bridging effect of TIPS in liver transplantation. Indeed, it’s important to notice that the role of TIPS in reducing decompensation events may help prevent worsening of the Child-Pugh class, thereby serving as a bridge to allow patients access to a broader range of treatments or to more prolonged therapeutic courses (12). Larrey et al. (13) reported that TIPS can effectively control PHT-related acute variceal bleeding and recurrent ascites, and when combined with local tumor reduction methods such as RFA and TACE, it can prolong the waiting period for liver transplantation and increase the probability of transplantation. Combining TIPS with subsequent antitumor therapy is feasible and effective for HCC patients with refractory ascites, providing ascites control in 86.8% of cases and enabling continuation of antitumor therapy in 92.6% of patients (14). In a retrospective study (15), it was found that the combination therapy of TIPS and TACE can improve patients’ liver function and prolong survival time. The latest Baveno VII guidelines also include recommendations related to the prevention and treatment of PHT-related complications in patients with advanced HCC (16,17), it is consistent with the purpose of this study.

Since 2007, when sorafenib was used for the first time as a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) for systemic HCC treatment, first- and second-line drugs such as lenvatinib and regorafenib have come to be universally applied in clinical settings. TKIs target vascular endothelial growth factor receptors, possibly increasing the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding. Moreover, in the IMbrave 150 study, compared with the sorafenib group, the bevacizumab–atezolizumab combination group had a higher bleeding risk (25.2% vs. 17.3%) and a higher risk of gastrointestinal bleeding (7% vs. 4.5%) (18). Therefore, although TKIs and immune checkpoint inhibitors can significantly improve survival rate of individuals with intermediate-to-advanced-stage HCC, they may intensify the degree of PHT, lead to variceal bleeding, and restrict systemic treatment. In a small sample study (19) on the combination of TIPS with multiple anti-tumor methods, anti-tumor treatment methods included TACE, microwave ablation, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and combination therapy. The results showed that not only was TACE used in combination, but TIPS combined with other anti-tumor treatments could also improve patient prognosis. This indicates that the combination of TIPS in the treatment of PHT could provide opportunities for further anti-tumor treatment. The patients enrolled in our study who underwent TIPS were able to receive effective anti-tumor treatment in the later stages.

Portal vein invasion is often a manifestation in patients with intermediate-to-advanced-stage HCC, resulting in PVTT and further resulting in complications such as bleeding and ascites. One study indicated that the median survival of patients with HCC and PVTT in the absence of active treatment intervention is only 3 months (20). The above-mentioned phenomena can be primarily attributed to acute bleeding, refractory ascites, and PHT-related liver failure, which prevent patients from undergoing tumor treatment. Therefore, in clinical settings, PHT has become the key to improving the overall survival rate of patients with HCC (21).

Owing to the dual blood supply characteristics of the liver, a mutual regulatory mechanism exists between the hepatic artery and portal vein system. TIPS decreases portal vein blood flow into the liver but increases reactive hepatic artery blood flow to maintain liver perfusion. Therefore, some researchers believe that TACE or transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) after TIPS may exacerbate hepatic ischemic injury. Qiu et al. (11) subjected 261 patients with HCC and PHT to TIPS combined with the local treatments of TACE, TAE, and RFA and observed a significant decrease in PHT-related complications: the 1-, 2-, 3-, 4-, and 5-year survival rates were 91.4%, 84.7%, 71.8%, 51.2%, and 38.8%, respectively. Therefore, TIPS combined with local treatment is safe and effective for HCC, considerably prolonging patient survival. In our study, patients with PHT-related variceal bleeding and recurrent ascites underwent TIPS. During this period, local treatment such as TACE and RFA did not worsen liver damage in patients with HCC. A retrospective study revealed that TIPS combined with TACE is superior to endoscopy combined with TACE, with a median survival of 13.75 months compared with 8.5 months, respectively (15). In our study, all patients underwent TACE, some of them combined with RFA. All patients underwent TACE using “super-selection” technology and precise embolization, without any liver function damage or other complications. Therefore, TIPS combined with TACE and/or RFA can provide a better quality of life for patients with HCC in subsequent treatment while controlling PHT-related complications such as bleeding and ascites.

PVTT is a marker of intermediate-to-advanced-stage HCC that not only serves as a key factor of intrahepatic tumor metastasis but also exacerbates PHT, resulting in variceal bleeding, ascites, and liver function damage. Recently, Wu et al. demonstrated that in HCC patients with PVTT and variceal bleeding, TIPS reduced early death with a better short-term survival than standard treatment (68 vs. 43 days) (22). At present, the clinical treatment methods for PVTT include thrombectomy during surgical tumor resection, radiotherapy, I125 particle portal vein therapy, and systemic therapy, but these all have unsatisfactory efficacy. Alternatively, partial treatment of PVTT may result in mechanization, whereas incomplete establishment of cavernous vessels worsens acute hemorrhage in PHT. Therefore, TIPS is more commonly used to treat PVTT (23), but the combination of TIPS with I125 particles is occasionally used to provide local radiotherapy for cancer thrombi during shunting. TIPS can significantly decrease portal vein pressure, with high patency and success rates (24,25). Two patients with PVTT from our study can serve as an illustrative example. One patient (Figure 1) received TACE combined with systemic treatment for right PVTT and achieved PR in early HCC treatment. However, during the treatment period, PHT resulted in multiple episodes of digestive bleeding, affecting the local and systemic treatment of conventional tumors. Subsequently, TIPS was performed. The other one (Figure 2) with HCC who achieved CR after multiple local and systemic combination treatments for PVTT. After PVTT organization and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding occurred, TIPS was performed to improve PHT. From a technical perspective, the difficulty in performing TIPS for patients with PVTT has slightly increased. For early cancer thrombi, repeated exploration via a guide wire after portal vein puncture can easily result in the guide wire passing through the cancer thrombus segment. For the occurrence of cavernous degeneration owing to prolonged occlusion, combination with methods such as percutaneous transhepatic puncture to open the portal vein is critical. In our study, all patients with PVTT successfully underwent TIPS, which effectively improved the symptoms of gastrointestinal bleeding and ascites and established a sound foundation for subsequent HCC treatment. It was confirmed that TIPS was technically safe and feasible for treating such patients, and could effectively control symptoms related to PHT. As a transitional treatment, it provided opportunities for the implementation of other tumor and PVTT related treatments.

Due to the small sample size of our study, the generalizability of the research results was not sufficient, and a strong response couldn’t be given to the controversies surrounding the application of TIPS in HCC. For example, during TIPS, the puncture needle or diversion channel passing through the tumor may pose risks of tumor rupture and bleeding, as well as implant metastasis of the tumor puncture channel. However, none of the patients included in our study experienced this phenomenon. Therefore, strict adherence to indications and selection of appropriate puncture and diversion channels before surgery was key to solving this problem. As the sample size increase, it was worth further exploring whether the probability of postoperative complications in TIPS patients with large tumor burden HCC combined with PHT was higher than that of PHT alone.

Conclusions

Patients with liver cirrhosis and both PHT and HCC frequently have a poor overall prognosis. Monitoring and treating PHT is the key to preventing and treating HCC, with gastroscopy and HVPG being the most common methods. TIPS is an effective treatment method for improving PHT, whether used as an early intervention therapy for HCC or as a comprehensive treatment strategy for PVTT in advanced HCC. Furthermore, it can act as a bridge to liver transplantation and expand the treatment time and opportunities for patients with advanced HCC. Future research should focus on how to establish early-stage personalized treatment regimens in clinic based on the PHT situation among patients with different HCC stages.
