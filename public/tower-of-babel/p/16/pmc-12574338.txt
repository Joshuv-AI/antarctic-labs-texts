Synchronous tele-interpersonal psychotherapy versus tele-cognitive behavioural therapy for adults: which works better? Results from a randomised clinical trial
Authors: Luiza Silveira Lucas, Bruno Lo Iacono Borba, Bruno Martini de Azevedo, Alexandro Cagliari, Andreia Rosane de Moura Valim, Edna Linhares Garcia, Silvia Virginia Coutinho Areosa, Alessandra Menezes Morelle, Marzie Rita Alves Damin, Simone Stulp, Alana Castro Panzenhagen, Flávio Milman Shansis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTele-cognitive behavioural therapy (t-CBT) is the most studied remote therapy, and evidence supports its efficacy in treating depression and anxiety symptoms.AimsTo compare the effectiveness of tele-interpersonal psychotherapy (t-IPT) to that of t-CBT. We hypothesise that t-IPT is as effective as t-CBT.MethodsWe conducted a randomised clinical trial with two parallel arms and equal randomisation. The allocation was on a 1:1 ratio based on a computerised randomisation sequence of permuted blocks of 50. Interventions and assessments were done via a website designed specifically for the trial. Participants were community-based adults with symptoms of anxiety, depression or irritability who received four sessions of t-CBT or t-IPT. The main outcome measures were the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 for depressive symptoms, Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 for anxiety symptoms and Affective Reactivity Index for irritability.Results149 individuals with a mean (standard deviation) age of 32.51 (10.73) years were randomised to receive t-CBT (n=73) or t-IPT (n=76). Seven participants withdrew from the interventions (t-CBT, n=4; t-IPT, n=3), and 20 participants completed the interventions but did not complete the follow-up questionnaires (t-CBT, n=9; t-IPT, n=11). Analysis was conducted by intention-to-treat. There was a significant overall reduction in symptoms of depression, anxiety and irritability (p<0.001) in both treatment arms; neither modality was superior to the other. Effectiveness analysis showed that the two interventions were equivalent.ConclusionsIn community adults, t-IPT is as effective as t-CBT in treating symptoms of anxiety, depression or irritability.Keywords: Psychotherapy; Anxiety; Depression; Randomized Controlled Trials as Topic; Psychotherapy, Brief

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Remote tele-cognitive behavioural therapy (t-CBT) is widely studied and recognised as effective for depression and anxiety. While interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) shows comparable efficacy to cognitive behavioural therapy in face-to-face settings for depression, its effectiveness via synchronous video delivery remains understudied.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This is the first randomised clinical trial to demonstrate that brief, synchronous video-delivered tele-IPT (t-IPT) is as effective as t-CBT in reducing depression, anxiety and irritability symptoms in a community-based adult sample. Both four-session interventions resulted in significant symptom reductions (p<0.001), with no difference in effect sizes between the interventions.

Both four-session interventions resulted in significant symptom reductions (p<0.001), with no difference in effect sizes between the interventions.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings support t-IPT as a viable alternative to t-CBT and underscore the potential of implementing brief, evidence-based remote interventions in public mental health services.

Introduction

Depression and anxiety are the two most disabling mental disorders, ranking in the top 25 leading disease burdens worldwide in the Global Burden of Disease Study.1 2 This situation has worsened since the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, which resulted in an estimated 25% increase in the global prevalence of anxiety and depressive disorders.3,5 The United Nations has called for action to minimise the mental health consequences of COVID-19 by investing in mental health interventions that could be delivered remotely.6

Telepsychotherapy is defined as psychotherapy delivered using remote technologies.7 It has many advantages over in-person treatment, such as convenience (fewer time constraints), accessibility (elimination of transportation or geographical barriers), reduced perceived stigma8 and, especially in health emergencies like the COVID-19 pandemic, the ability to mitigate the spread of the disease.9 Telepsychotherapy can be delivered synchronously or asynchronously.7 In synchronous telepsychotherapy, the patient–therapist interaction happens in real-time via telephone or video conference.8 Meta-analyses comparing the effectiveness of video-delivered psychotherapy to in-person treatments showed that telepsychotherapy is no less efficacious.8 10

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) aims to help individuals identify and modify maladaptive patterns of thinking and behaviour, and it is recommended by the National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence as first-line treatment for depression and anxiety.11 12 Several meta-analyses have reported robust evidence for remotely delivered CBT in treating both depressive and anxiety symptoms, with similar efficacy and acceptability as in-person CBT.810 13,15

Despite a lack of evidence, during the COVID-19 pandemic, other types of in-person psychotherapies were rapidly switched to remote delivery.9 Therefore, it is essential to investigate and compare the effectiveness of various psychotherapies for these conditions when delivered remotely.

Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) focuses on helping the patient improve their social functioning and interpersonal relationships, and it may be particularly suitable for individuals whose primary concerns revolve around four specific areas: role disputes, role transitions, grief and interpersonal deficits.16 17 IPT has consistent supportive evidence for treating depression,18 and in-person IPT has similar effectiveness to in-person CBT in reducing depressive symptoms.19 Conversely, the evidence for treating anxiety disorders with in-person IPT is conflicting and scarce.20 To our knowledge, only two studies have investigated the efficacy of remote synchronous IPT for depressive symptoms. In both, the intervention was delivered via telephone and focused on specific populations.21 22 No study has investigated the effectiveness of remote, synchronous, video-delivered IPT for symptoms of anxiety or depression.

This study aims to compare the efficacy of two brief, synchronous, video-delivered interventions—tele-CBT (t-CBT) versus tele-IPT (t-IPT)—for adults with symptoms of depression, anxiety or irritability. We hypothesise that t-IPT would be equally effective as t-CBT. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first randomised clinical trial (RCT) to compare synchronous video-delivered t-CBT versus t-IPT.

Methods

Trial design

We conducted an RCT with two parallel arms and equal randomisation to compare synchronous t-IPT versus t-CBT. Participants were eligible if they were: (1) feeling depressed, anxious, irritable, stressed or emotionally exhausted; (2) aged 18 or older; (3) able to understand the self-administered questionnaires; and (4) not currently enrolled in psychotherapy. Only participants residing in the Vale do Taquari or Rio Pardo regions of the State of Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil, were included. Potential participants found to be at a moderate or high risk of suicide or who were experiencing psychotic and delusional symptoms were excluded from the study.

The trial protocol was approved by the Universidade do Vale do Taquari (UNIVATES) Research Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. The reporting of this trial complies with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 guidelines.23

Participants were recruited between March and November 2021 through an advertisement placed on local radio, a local newspaper and social media. The ad included the address of the study website, which provided general information and instructions on how to participate. All individuals interested in participating could register directly on the website. After registration, subjects signed an online informed consent form (ICF). They were then instructed to complete sociodemographic data and baseline self-administered questionnaires that assessed symptoms of depression, anxiety, irritability, hopelessness and suicidal ideation.

A teleconsultation with a psychiatrist was then scheduled for screening. This teleconsultation was conducted on the study website itself. It was based on the TelePSI project, a Brazilian Ministry of Health-funded initiative in collaboration with several academic institutions aimed at reducing the impact of COVID-19-related distress.24 The psychiatrist assessed individuals for eligibility by applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as the Columbia Suicide Risk Assessment Scale to stratify the risk of suicide as absent, low, moderate or high.25 Those who met the inclusion criteria were assigned to the clinical trial and underwent randomisation. Interventions

The included participants received four sessions of either t-CBT or t-IPT, with a weekly interval between sessions. These sessions were conducted by three clinical psychologists who received training and certification on both t-CBT and t-IPT through the TelePSI programme prior to this study.24 26

The t-CBT and t-IPT sessions were performed according to the TelePSI manuals. These manuals were primarily designed by the TelePSI team to support healthcare workers in managing emotional distress during the COVID-19 pandemic and are described elsewhere.26 27 The t-CBT manual follows a transdiagnostic approach adapted for the COVID-19 context from the unified protocol for transdiagnostic treatment. This cognitive behavioural intervention was designed directly to address neuroticism, that is, the core temperament vulnerable to anxiety and depressive disorders.26 28 29 Additionally, this t-CBT manual includes weekly tasks in which the participant receives videos addressing topics based on the cognitive and behavioural model and teaching techniques such as mindfulness and diaphragmatic breathing.26 The t-IPT manual, in turn, is an adaptation of the Edinburgh Early Intervention Model Adapted for COVID-19.30 This manual was compiled by the TelePSI team and includes an authorised cross-cultural translation and adaptation of the model. The TelePSI team also incorporated general elements of psychoeducation, including parts of the Interpersonal Counselling for Primary Care manual.31 32 It provided supporting materials to be sent to participants between sessions (videos for psychoeducation and reinforcement of t-IPT techniques).26

Each psychotherapy session lasted approximately 50 min and took place on a web-based platform designed specifically for the study. Weekly group supervision was conducted by an experienced supervisor (ELG) throughout the course of this study. During these 2-hour meetings, the three therapists and the supervisor discussed the online sessions, with an emphasis on treatment.

Outcome measures

The outcome variables were defined a priori. The principal outcome measures were the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depressive symptoms, the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) for anxiety symptoms and the Affective Reactivity Index (ARI) for irritability symptoms.

The PHQ-9 is a brief self-administered instrument for the assessment of depressive symptoms. It consists of nine items, answered on a 4-point scale, ranging from 0 (never) to 3 (almost every day). The sum score, ranging from 0 to 27, assesses the frequency of signs and symptoms of depression in the previous 2 weeks; scores greater than or equal to 10 are considered indicative of major depression. Regarding severity, PHQ-9 comprises five categories, where a cut-off point of 0–4 indicates no depressive symptoms, 5–9 indicates mild depressive symptoms, 10–14 indicates moderate depressive symptoms, 15–19 indicates moderately severe depressive symptoms and 20–27 indicates severe depressive symptoms.33 34 The PHQ-9 has been translated into Brazilian Portuguese and validated in Brazil.35

The GAD-7 is a brief self-administered instrument for the assessment of anxiety symptoms. It consists of seven items, also answered on a 4-point scale of 0 (never) to 3 (almost every day), with sum scores ranging from 0 to 21. It measures the frequency of signs and symptoms of anxiety in the previous 2 weeks. As in the PHQ-9, scores equal to or greater than 10 are considered indicative of anxiety. The cut-off points for mild, moderate and severe anxiety are 5–9, 10–14, and greater than or equal to 15, respectively.36 37 The GAD-7 has also been validated in Brazilian Portuguese.38

Finally, the ARI is a brief self-administered instrument for the assessment of symptoms of irritability. The instrument consists of six items assessing feelings and behaviours related to irritability, as well as one item assessing impairment due to irritability. Respondents rate each item on a 3-point scale (0=false; 1=somewhat true; 2=certainly true). Total ARI scores refer to the first six items and range from 0 to 12, with higher scores reflecting higher levels of irritability. There are no cut-off points. The ARI scale was initially developed for use in childhood and adolescence, but its validity in adults was subsequently demonstrated.39 The ARI scale has been validated in Brazilian Portuguese.40

All outcome assessments were conducted remotely using these self-report instruments, which were completed through the secure online platform specifically designed for the study. Participants filled out the scales at baseline (within 2 weeks preceding the start of the intervention) and again up to 14 days after completion of the intervention. This online setting ensured uniformity of data collection across both treatment arms (t-CBT and t-IPT).

Sample size

A minimum sample size of 170 subjects (85 subjects in each group) was calculated, considering a statistical power of 90%, a significance level of 5% and an effect size of less than 0.5 for non-inferiority. The sample size calculation was performed using WinPEPI (Programs for Epidemiologists for Windows) V.11.65.

Random allocation and blinding

Participants were randomly allocated to two groups (t-CBT and t-IPT) in a 1:1 ratio, based on a computerised random-number table randomisation sequence of permuted blocks of 50. The random allocation sequence was generated by a researcher who was not directly involved in the intervention (BLIB). Blinding after allocation was not possible for the clinical psychologists who administered the interventions. Participants also knew the type of intervention that would be used from the first session, as the type of technique to be used was explained during psychoeducation. Outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation throughout.

Statistical analyses

Data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics. Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, V.28.0 (IBM, 2021), with a significance level of 5% (p<0.05). Continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation (SD)), and categorical variables as absolute and relative frequencies. The distribution of numerical variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Student’s t-test was applied for comparison of means, and Pearson’s χ² test for comparing proportions. To compare the scores of the PHQ-9, GAD-7 and ARI scales over time according to group allocation, the generalised estimating equations (GEE) model supplemented by the least significant difference test was applied. Due to the asymmetry of the scales, the gamma model was used in the analyses. The effect size of each intervention (type of therapy) was evaluated using Cohen’s d, where values below 0.5 represent a weak effect, between 0.5 and 0.8 a medium effect and above 0.8 a strong effect size.41 The McNemar test was used to compare preintervention and postintervention levels of anxiety and depression in each arm.

The analysis was conducted using the intention-to-treat (ITT) approach, that is, all individuals who participated in the trial, including those who dropped out or did not complete the assessments, were included in the statistics, based on the group to which they were initially allocated. ITT analysis assesses clinical effectiveness.42 We performed post hoc subgroup analyses to test whether t-IPT remained as effective as t-CBT in participants with GAD-7 scores of 10 or higher (subgroup analysis #1) and in participants with PHQ-9 scores of 10 or higher (subgroup analysis #2). The results of these analyses are presented in the online supplemental tables 1 and 2.

Results

Characteristics of the study sample

A summary diagram based on the CONSORT statement23 is presented in figure 1. Between March and November 2021, 495 individuals registered on the website and signed the ICF. Of these, 229 underwent teleconsultation with a psychiatrist for assessment of eligibility. Finally, 149 individuals were included in the trial and randomised. Of these, 73 were randomly assigned to t-CBT (49%) and 76 to t-IPT (51%). Data collection ended in January 2022.

Seven participants withdrew from interventions: four from the t-CBT arm and three from the t-IPT arm. Of these, five individuals had completed one session (four in the t-CBT arm and one in the t-IPT arm), one individual from the t-IPT arm had completed two sessions and one individual from the t-IPT arm had completed three sessions before withdrawal. 20 participants completed the interventions but not the follow-up questionnaires—9 from the t-CBT arm and 11 from the t-IPT arm. Nevertheless, all 149 participants who started the interventions were included in the statistical analysis (ITT).

The profiles of these patient groups are shown in table 1. The mean (SD) age was 32.51 (10.73) years, and there was a female predominance (73.80%). Most of the participants were single, had no children and had started, but not completed, a university degree. Less than 20% of participants were taking psychotropic drugs; this proportion was similar in both arms. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two intervention arms (t-CBT and t-IPT).

Outcomes measures

Table 2 presents the results on the outcome measures, including change scores with 95% CIs, as well as a comparison between t-CBT and t-IPT. The GEE analysis of PHQ-9, GAD-7 and ARI scores showed an overall decrease in symptoms of depression, anxiety and irritability, respectively (χ² Wald (1) ≥4.32; p<0.001), in both treatment arms. This improvement was similar for t-CBT and t-IPT, with neither intervention showing superiority. The baseline assessment was completed on average 18 days before the interventions commenced, and the follow-up assessment was completed on average 7 days after the intervention was completed. Moderator analyses were conducted to examine whether treatment efficacy differed by sex or age group. For sex, the three-way interaction term (sex×therapy × time) was not significant for anxiety symptoms (χ²Wald (1) = 0.01; p=0.942), depressive symptoms (χ²Wald (1) = 2.61; p=0.106) or irritability (χ²Wald (1) = 0.14; p=0.704). For age, the three-way interaction term (age group×therapy × time) was likewise non-significant for anxiety symptoms (χ²Wald (1) = 0.97; p=0.325), depressive symptoms (χ²Wald (1) = 0.03; p=0.872) and irritability (χ²Wald (1) = 0.02; p=0.895). These results suggest that treatment effects did not differ meaningfully according to sex or age category. Further details are available in the online supplemental material.

Comparative effectiveness

The effect size of the interventions, calculated using Cohen’s d statistic,41 ranged from −0.19 to 0.24, indicating that the type of psychotherapy had little effect on the results (table 2). This suggests that t-IPT is equivalent to t-CBT in lowering anxiety, depression and irritability symptoms.

Dropouts

Dropout rates were low in both groups, with only seven participants discontinuing the intervention (t-CBT, n=4; t-IPT, n=3). Additionally, 20 participants completed the sessions but did not provide post-treatment data (t-CBT, n=9; t-IPT, n=11).

Harmful effects

Participants reported no adverse events or harm resulting from their participation in the study. However, potential harmful effects were not systematically assessed.

Discussion

Main findings

This was the first RCT to compare synchronous, video-delivered t-IPT to t-CBT for community adults with symptoms of depression, anxiety or irritability. Both interventions significantly reduced symptom scores on the PHQ-9, GAD-7 and ARI (χ² Wald (1) ≥4.32; p<0.001), with no statistically significant difference in effectiveness between the treatment arms. Effect sizes across outcomes were small (ranging from –0.19 to 0.24), indicating equivalence between interventions. Our findings confirm the efficacy of t-CBT, aligning with several meta-analyses.8 10 13 14 Furthermore, the symptom reduction observed in our study is comparable to the average effect sizes reported in large network meta-analyses of remote CBT interventions,15 43 which generally show moderate symptom reduction with both guided asynchronous and synchronous formats.

Recent evidence from a large RCT conducted in Brazil during the COVID-19 pandemic also supports the equivalence between brief remote CBT and IPT.27 In that preprint study, Salum et al27 compared the same four-session t-CBT and t-IPT protocols used in the present trial, applying them to a distinct population (healthcare workers experiencing emotional distress). The study found substantial symptom reduction in all treatment arms, including an enhanced single-session psychoeducation group, with no significant differences between interventions. These findings reinforce our results by demonstrating that brief synchronous t-CBT and t-IPT can be equally effective across different populations and contexts, including high-stress settings such as a health crisis.

In contrast to previous studies that evaluated IPT delivered by telephone and in specific subgroups (eg, human immunodeficiency virus-positive individuals or postpartum women),21 22 and to the aforementioned randomised trial comparing t-CBT and t-IPT for healthcare workers,27 our results suggest that synchronous video-delivered t-IPT can achieve similar outcomes in a more diverse, community-based adult sample. Both of our interventions were based on brief treatments (only four sessions, with a short follow-up period), which differ from the standard protocols of in-person CBT and IPT, which comprise at least 12 sessions.17 44 It is important to note that an established alternative to IPT is interpersonal counselling (IPC), a brief six-session intervention that follows structured scripts and was originally designed to train non–mental health professionals to treat patients with depressive symptoms in primary care.32 The t-IPT manual partially incorporates the IPC paradigm.26 31 Even though our protocol consisted of fewer sessions than standard IPT or CBT protocols and two fewer sessions than IPC, our interventions were effective in reducing symptoms of anxiety, depression and irritability. This is consistent with a recent systematic review investigating the dose-response phenomenon in psychotherapies.45 Dose-response therein is defined as ‘the relationship between the dose (eg, length, frequency) of treatment and the subsequent probability of improvement’. Robinson et al found that the optimal doses of psychotherapy range between 4 and 26 sessions.45 They also found that weekly therapy, which was the frequency used in our study, ‘seems to accelerate the rate of improvement compared to less frequent schedules’.45 Importantly, dropout rates in our study were remarkably low in both arms (<6%)—notably lower than those reported in other trials of asynchronous internet-based interventions, which often exceeded 30%–70%.15 46 This further supports the acceptability and feasibility of synchronous telepsychotherapy.

From a theoretical perspective, the comparable efficacy of t-IPT and t-CBT may be understood in light of their distinct but possibly related mechanisms of action. t-IPT is thought to enhance interpersonal functioning, increase perceived social support and promote adaptive emotional regulation.17 18 Meanwhile, t-CBT aims to identify, challenge and restructure dysfunctional thought patterns, thereby facilitating behavioural activation and reducing emotional distress.44 At a neurobiological level, however, both modalities may exert similar effects. It has been shown in the literature that both therapies seem to be associated with the normalisation of activity within corticolimbic networks involved in mood regulation,47 modulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis48 and attenuation of inflammatory processes linked to chronic stress.48 Therefore, shared underlying biological mechanisms might provide a plausible explanatory framework for the observed comparable clinical improvements.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged when interpreting its findings. First, the sample was composed predominantly of young, female and well-educated participants, which may limit the generalisability of the results to the broader population, particularly to older adults, men or individuals with lower educational attainment. As such, extrapolation of these findings to more diverse demographic groups must be done with caution. Second, participants were recruited from the general community and included based on self-reported symptoms of anxiety, depression or irritability, without requiring a formal clinical diagnosis at baseline. This was done to facilitate access to the intervention for all interested individuals of the general population, considering the COVID-19 scenario. Similarly broad inclusion criteria were used in another mental health RCT designed during the pandemic.49 Although two subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate outcomes specifically in those who met criteria for GAD or major depressive disorder based on cut-off scores (see online supplemental material), the absence of structured diagnostic interviews reduces the clinical precision of the sample characterisation. Third, the interventions were brief—only four sessions per participant—and, although they were based on validated short protocols and supported by systematic reviews on dose-response (eg, Robinson et al), this brevity differs from standard CBT and IPT protocols, which are typically composed of at least 12 sessions. The short follow-up window (average of 7 days after the final session) also limits conclusions about the durability of treatment effects over time.

Fourth, the absence of a passive control group such as ‘treatment as usual’ (TAU) or a waitlist condition precludes inferences about absolute treatment efficacy. Nevertheless, previous meta-analyses have questioned the ethical and methodological adequacy of such control groups, particularly due to potential nocebo effects.50 Munder and colleagues focused on the inexistence of a single TAU and analysed the impact of the intensity of TAU on the effects of face-to-face and internet-based psychotherapy.51 Finally, although no harm or adverse events were reported, the study did not systematically assess negative effects or unwanted events. This may underestimate potential harm related to brief telepsychotherapy interventions. Since dropout rates did not differ between intervention arms, we believe this potential source of bias does not greatly influence the validity of the results. Moreover, to further reduce biases, all analyses were conducted using an ITT approach.

Implications

The equivalence of t-IPT and t-CBT in this trial suggests that either approach may be considered a valid first-line option for individuals with affective symptoms, supporting flexibility in clinical decision-making. This means that treatment selection can be guided by factors such as patient preference, therapeutic alliance, therapist expertise and logistical considerations, including the availability of trained providers and scheduling constraints. Given that IPT is often perceived as more relational and emotionally focused,17 it may be preferred when the cognitive restructuring techniques of CBT are not suitable or desired. This flexibility is particularly relevant in telepsychotherapy settings, where optimising accessibility and maintaining patient engagement are critical to achieving positive outcomes. From a health policy perspective, these findings support the diversification of evidence-based telepsychotherapy options in public mental health systems. Implementing both t-CBT and t-IPT could help address the shortage of mental health professionals and improve access to care in geographically remote or underserved populations. Future studies should evaluate cost-effectiveness and long-term outcomes of brief synchronous telepsychotherapies to inform scalable and sustainable mental health strategies.

In 2023, she earned a master’s degree in Medical Sciences (with a concentration in Behavioural Sciences) from the University of Vale do Taquari (UNIVATES), Brazil. Since 2020, she has worked in private psychiatric practice and has served as a supervisor in the Psychiatry Residency Program at the Divina Providência Health Network, where she currently coordinates both the inpatient psychiatric unit and the outpatient anxiety clinic. Since 2024, she has been a professor at the UNIVATES School of Medicine. Her current research interests include the treatment of anxiety and depression, psychotherapies and telepsychiatry / telepsychotherapy.
