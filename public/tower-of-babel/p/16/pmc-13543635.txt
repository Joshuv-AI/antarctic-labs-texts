Association of potentially inappropriate prescribing with drug–drug interactions and medication regimen complexity in geriatric patients: a cross-sectional analytical study
Authors: Ahmet Çakır, Ahsen Telli̇, Yunus Emre Ayhan, Hasan Memi̇ş, Muhammed Yunus Bektay, Funda Datlı Yakaryılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPotentially inappropriate prescribing (PIP), potential drug–drug interactions (pDDIs), and elevated medication regimen complexity co-occur frequently in older adults and collectively constitute the “iatrogenic triad” of geriatric pharmacotherapy.MethodsThis cross-sectional analytical observational study with prospective recruitment included patients aged 65 years and older attending the Geriatrics Outpatient Clinic. Patients were screened during routine visits using the 2023 American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria and classified by the presence or absence of at least one PIP. Enrollment continued until 40 patients per group were included; therefore, the study was not designed to estimate PIP prevalence. Clinically significant pDDIs were identified using Micromedex® and UpToDate®. Medication regimen complexity was assessed with the Medication Regimen Complexity Index (MRCI), and independent factors associated with PIP were evaluated using binary logistic regression.ResultsEighty patients were analyzed: 40 with PIP and 40 without PIP. Overall, 48.8% were women, the median age was 71 years (IQR: 67–76), 25% reported a fall within the previous year, and 65% had polypharmacy. Micromedex® detected clinically significant pDDIs in 75% of patients with PIP versus 37.5% without PIP (odds ratio [OR] = 5; p = 0.002) and UpToDate® in 47.5% and 15% (OR = 5.13; p = 0.004). In total, 215 clinically significant pDDIs were identified: 172 major interactions by Micromedex® and 43 D- or X-category interactions by UpToDate®. Total MRCI scores were significantly higher in Group A than in Group B (median 22.8 vs. 15.0; p = 0.005). Polypharmacy (OR = 9.91; p = 0.001) and Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden score (OR = 1.87; p = 0.031) were independently associated with the presence of PIP.ConclusionIn this equal-group outpatient cohort, PIP was associated with higher pDDI burden and greater medication regimen complexity. Polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden were the main modifiable factors associated with PIP. These findings suggest that systematic pharmacist-led medication review may represent a useful strategy for identifying interconnected medication-related risks in geriatric outpatient settings.Keywords: Potentially inappropriate prescribing, Drug–drug interactions, Medication regimen complexity, Polypharmacy, Geriatrics

Introduction

The global aging of the population has created an unprecedented challenge in clinical pharmacotherapy. Adults aged 65 years and older constitute the fastest-growing demographic worldwide, and their high burden of chronic comorbidities makes polypharmacy, defined as the simultaneous use of multiple medications, virtually unavoidable. The prevalence of polypharmacy among community-dwelling older adults has been reported to exceed 40% in many countries, with substantially higher rates observed in hospital and geriatric outpatient settings [1, 2]. Polypharmacy in older adults is not a merely numerical phenomenon; it is a clinically significant condition linked to functional deterioration, falls, hospitalization, and increased mortality rates [2, 3]. Among the most consequential medication-related problems arising in this context are potentially inappropriate prescribing (PIP), potential drug–drug interactions (pDDIs), and excessive medication regimen complexity. These three problems frequently co-occur and reinforce one another, forming what has been described as the “iatrogenic triad” of geriatric pharmacotherapy [4].

PIP is generally characterized as the administration of a medicine for which the risks outweigh the benefits, or for which there is no clear therapeutic justification in a given patient [5]. The American Geriatrics Society (AGS) Beers Criteria represent the most widely employed and regularly updated explicit tool for identifying PIP in older adults [6]. A 2023 global systematic review and meta-analysis found that the prevalence of PIP among older adults worldwide ranged from approximately 20% to over 50%, depending on clinical setting and screening instrument applied [7]. In Türkiye, PIP prevalence rates reported in outpatient and community pharmacy settings have been considerably higher, ranging from 63.5% to 83.5% among patients aged 65 years and older [8–10]. These higher rates likely reflect the specific characteristics of Turkish study populations, including older patients with substantial multimorbidity, the predominant use of specialized clinic settings, and the application of different screening criteria (TIME criteria and Beers Criteria 2015, 2019, and 2023 updates), rather than indicating a uniformly higher PIP burden in the Turkish geriatric population overall.

A critical but underexplored consequence of PIP is its potential to amplify the risk of clinically significant pDDIs. The relationship between PIP and pDDI is bidirectional and synergistic: medications flagged by the Beers Criteria frequently introduce pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic interactions with the patient’s existing drug regimen [11]. A study has shown that the prevalence of pDDI in a geriatric patient group ranges widely from 2.8% to 90.1%; polypharmacy and prolonged hospital stays were identified as factors associated with pDDI [12]. Moreover, Jang et al. (2021) demonstrated that PIP use and pDDI presence were significantly associated, with polypharmacy acting as a shared predisposing factor. It is important to acknowledge that the observed co-occurrence of PIP and pDDI may partly reflect their shared association with polypharmacy rather than a direct causal relationship between the two phenomena [13].

Beyond interactions, PIP may be associated with greater medication regimen complexity. A study conducted by Albayrak et al. found that patients with PIP had higher Medication Regimen Complexity Index (MRCI) scores compared to patients without PIP [8]. In a study conducted by Ferreira et al. (2015), high MRCI scores were found to be associated with age, the presence of at least one potential drug-related problem, the number of medications, and polypharmacy [14].

Despite growing recognition of these interrelated problems, studies simultaneously evaluating PIP, pDDIs, and MRCI in geriatric outpatient populations are scarce. A recent cross-sectional study from Eritrea concurrently assessed polypharmacy, PIP, pDDIs, and MRCI among older adults attending referral hospitals and reported substantial rates of clinically significant pDDIs and PIP, emphasizing the clinical relevance of comprehensive medication-related risk assessment in geriatric populations [15]. To our knowledge, no published study from Türkiye has concurrently evaluated PIP, clinically significant pDDIs using two interaction databases, and MRCI in a dedicated geriatric outpatient clinic.

The primary aim of this study was to compare the rate and number of clinically significant pDDIs and the degree of medication regimen complexity between geriatric outpatients with and without PIP, as identified by the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria [6]. The secondary aims were: (1) to describe the distribution of the number of Beers Criteria–identified PIPs per patient; (2) to compare pDDI rates and per-patient interaction counts identified by Micromedex® and UpToDate®; (3) to characterize the severity distribution of detected interactions; (4) to assess inter-database agreement using Cohen’s kappa; (5) to compare MRCI subscores between groups; and (6) to identify patient-level factors independently associated with PIP.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a cross-sectional analytical observational study with prospective recruitment conducted between December 15, 2025, and May 15, 2026, at the Geriatrics Outpatient Clinic of the Department of Internal Medicine at XXX Medical Center, XXX University, in Türkiye. Patients attending routine outpatient visits were consecutively screened for eligibility and classified according to the presence or absence of PIP, as determined by the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria [6]. Group A comprised patients with at least one PIP (n = 40), whereas Group B comprised patients without PIP (n = 40). Enrollment continued until the predefined target of 40 patients in each group had been reached. Therefore, the study employed a pre-specified equal-group recruitment strategy rather than true consecutive sampling of the clinic population. No randomization was performed, and group allocation was based on the clinical assessment of each patient’s medication profile.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients aged 65 and older who visited the Geriatrics Outpatient Clinic of the Department of Internal Medicine, were taking at least two medications, and provided written informed consent to participate in the study were included in the study. Patients who refused to provide written informed consent, had cognitive impairment that hindered effective communication, or experienced significant communication difficulties were excluded. Additionally, patients with incomplete data were excluded from the final analysis.

Data collection

Patient data were collected using a structured patient record form. Demographic characteristics including age, sex, body mass index, hospitalization during the previous 12 months, and the reason for outpatient visit were recorded. The reason for outpatient visit was categorized as medical or non-medical according to the primary reason documented in the hospital information management system. Medical visits included consultations or follow-up visits related to chronic diseases, geriatric syndromes, medication management, or other internal medicine-related conditions. Non-medical visits included visits primarily related to non-clinical consultations. Clinical data including comorbidities, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), history of falls and hospitalization within the last 12 months, adverse drug reaction history, and functional status were evaluated. Because cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms may influence medication management and have previously been associated with inappropriate prescribing, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) scores were collected as clinically relevant covariates and evaluated as potential factors associated with PIP. Medication-related variables including all prescribed medications, dosage, administration route, indication, polypharmacy status, MRCI, anticholinergic burden, and pDDIs were assessed. Laboratory parameters including renal and liver function tests, lipid profile, hematological parameters, and serum albumin levels were evaluated and used during the assessment of PIP. All data were obtained from the hospital information management system. All study variables were cross-checked during data collection, and no missing data were identified; therefore, no imputation procedures were required.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was to assess whether patients with PIP (Group A) had a significantly higher rate of clinically significant pDDIs and greater medication regimen complexity compared to patients without PIP (Group B).

Secondary outcomes included comparison of per-patient pDDI counts between groups; severity distribution of detected interactions (major vs. contraindicated for Micromedex®; D- vs. X-category for UpToDate®); inter-database agreement between Micromedex® and UpToDate®; comparison of MRCI subscores; description of the number of Beers Criteria–identified PIPs per patient; and identification of independent predictors of PIP.

Definitions

PIP was identified jointly by a geriatrician and a clinical pharmacist using the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria [6]. Assessments were performed collaboratively during the evaluation of each patient’s medication profile, and classifications were determined by consensus at the time of assessment; therefore, no formal disagreement resolution process was required. Each detected PIP was recorded with its corresponding criterion, drug class, and clinical rationale.

pDDIs were screened using two independent clinical drug information databases: Micromedex® Drug Information (Merative) and UpToDate® (Wolters Kluwer Health). For Micromedex®, interactions classified as contraindicated or major severity were considered clinically significant. For UpToDate®, X-category (avoid combination) and D-category (consider therapy modification) interactions were considered clinically significant. For each database, a binary variable (present/absent) was coded per patient, and the total number of clinically significant interactions per patient was recorded. The severity category of each detected interaction was documented separately (major vs. contraindicated for Micromedex®; D- vs. X-category for UpToDate®). Because UpToDate® applies more stringent criteria for clinically significant interactions (categories X and D), per-patient interaction counts for UpToDate® were expected to be lower than those for Micromedex® and may have a median value of zero in subgroups with limited interaction burden.

Medication regimen complexity was assessed using the MRCI [16, 17], a validated 65-item tool comprising three domains: Section A (dosage form), Section B (dosing frequency), and Section C (additional administration directions). Section A assigns weighted scores based on the dosage form and route of administration, with more complex formulations receiving higher scores. For example, an oral tablet is assigned 1 point, whereas a nebulized medication receives 5 points. Section B evaluates dosing frequency, with higher scores assigned to more frequent administration schedules (e.g., once daily = 1 point; every 12 h = 2.5 points; every 8 h = 3.5 points). Section C includes additional instructions that may increase regimen complexity, such as administration at a specific time of day, meal-related dosing requirements, use with a specific fluid, dose titration, or tablet manipulation. For each prescribed medication, scores from Sections A, B, and C were calculated and summed. Patient-level MRCI scores were obtained by summing up the scores of all prescribed medications. The total MRCI score ranges from 0 for patients not receiving medications and has no upper limit, with higher scores indicating greater medication regimen complexity. Higher total scores indicate greater complexity. Written permission was obtained from the original authors.

Comorbidity burden was assessed using the CCI, calculated via MDCalc (https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/3917). The CCI assigns weighted scores to 17 comorbid conditions and estimates 10-year survival probability.

Anticholinergic burden was evaluated using the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) Scale (https://www.acbcalc.com/). Medications were scored between 1 and 3 according to their anticholinergic cognitive effects, and a total ACB score ≥ 3 was considered indicative of high anticholinergic burden.

Geriatric assessment instruments included the 15-item GDS-15, with scores ≥ 5 indicating probable depression; the MoCA, with scores ≥ 21 considered normal and scores < 21 indicating mild cognitive impairment; the Katz Index of Activities of Daily Living (ADL), with scores ≥ 6 representing full independence; and the Aachen Falls Prevention Scale, with scores ≥ 5 indicating increased fall risk.

In this study, polypharmacy was identified if a patient was prescribed five or more chronic medications concurrently during the study period [18].

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on data from the study by Albayrak et al. (2023) [8], using the expected difference in total MRCI scores between patients with and without PIP (mean MRCI: 13.18 ± 5.98 vs. 8.68 ± 4.98). An a priori power analysis for independent samples was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Dusseldorf, Germany). Assuming a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 95%, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 40 patients per group (total n = 80). Post hoc power analysis confirmed that the achieved sample size provided > 99% power to detect the observed difference in total MRCI scores between groups (effect size d ≈ 0.74).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and compared using the independent samples t-test; height was the only variable meeting normality criteria. All remaining continuous variables — including per-patient pDDI counts, which were non-normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk p < 0.001 for all) — are presented as median (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

In addition to p-values, effect sizes were calculated to quantify the magnitude and clinical relevance of between-group differences. Rank-biserial correlation coefficients (r) were used for non-parametric comparisons, while Cramer’s V or Phi (φ) coefficients were used for categorical variables. Effect sizes were interpreted according to Cohen’s conventions as negligible (< 0.10), small (0.10–0.29), medium (0.30–0.49), and large (≥ 0.50).

For the primary outcomes, binary pDDI rates were compared between groups using the chi-square test with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs); per-patient pDDI counts were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test; and MRCI total scores were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Inter-database agreement between Micromedex® and UpToDate® for binary pDDI detection was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ), interpreted according to Landis and Koch (1977) [19]: < 0.20 slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, > 0.80 almost perfect.

Binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of PIP. Univariable logistic regression analyses were first conducted for candidate predictors. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable model. Multicollinearity among variables included in the multivariable logistic regression model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values; VIF values below 5 were considered to indicate the absence of relevant multicollinearity. The final multivariable model included six predictors, corresponding to an events-per-variable ratio of approximately 6.7 (40 PIP-positive cases / 6 predictors). Although this ratio is below the conventional threshold of 10 events per variable, all clinically and statistically relevant covariates were retained in the model. The potential risk of overfitting was therefore acknowledged as a study limitation. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 80 patients were enrolled and equally distributed between Group A (PIP present, n = 40) and Group B (PIP absent, n = 40). The median age of the overall study population was 71 years (IQR: 67–76). Patients in Group A had a significantly higher comorbidity burden compared to Group B, as reflected by CCI scores (median: 7.0 [IQR: 5.0–8.2] vs. 5.0 [4.8–7.0]; p = 0.020, r = 0.30 [medium effect]). Polypharmacy was present in 92.5% of patients in Group A compared to 37.5% in Group B (χ² = 24.23, p < 0.001, φ = 0.550 [large effect]), representing the most striking between-group difference in baseline characteristics. Anticholinergic medication burden was also significantly higher in Group A (median ACB: 2.0 [IQR: 1.0–4.0] vs. 1.0 [0.0–1.0]; p < 0.001, r = 0.46 [large effect]). Medication regimen complexity

Total MRCI scores were significantly higher in Group A than in Group B (median: 22.8 [IQR: 14.4–29.0] vs. 15.0 [11.0–19.2]; p = 0.005, r = 0.37 [medium effect]), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Subscale analysis revealed that the between-group difference was primarily driven by Section B (dosing frequency; median: 11.0 [IQR: 6.9–14.2] vs. 6.0 [3.8–8.2]; p < 0.001, r = 0.51 [large effect]) and Section C (additional administration directions; median: 7.0 [IQR: 5.0–9.2] vs. 5.0 [3.0–6.0]; p = 0.002, r = 0.40 [medium effect]). Potential drug–drug interactions

pDDIs were detected in 45 of 80 patients (56.2%) by Micromedex® and in 25 of 80 patients (31.2%) by UpToDate® overall. Screening with Micromedex® identified at least one clinically significant interaction in 30 patients in Group A (75%) compared with 15 in Group B (37.5%) (OR = 5.00, 95% CI: 1.91–13.06; χ² = 9.32, p = 0.002, φ = 0.343 [medium effect]). UpToDate® screening identified clinically significant interactions in 19 patients in Group A (47.5%) versus 6 in Group B (15%) (OR = 5.13, 95% CI: 1.76–14.90; χ² = 8.38, p = 0.004, φ = 0.324 [medium effect]). These findings are summarized in Fig. 2. Regarding severity classification, all 172 interactions detected by Micromedex® were of major severity; no contraindicated interactions were identified in either group. Of the 43 interactions detected by UpToDate®, 39 were D-category (consider therapy modification) and four were X-category (avoid combination). The four X-category interactions were: aspirin–dexketoprofen, piribedil–risperidone, flurbiprofen–diclofenac, and dexketoprofen–diclofenac potassium, all identified in Group A patients.

Beyond binary detection, per-patient interaction counts were compared quantitatively. Micromedex® identified a median of 2.0 (IQR: 0.8–4.2) major-severity interactions per patient in Group A compared to 0.0 (IQR: 0.0–1.0) in Group B (p < 0.001, r = 0.46 [large effect]). UpToDate® identified a median of 0.0 (IQR: 0.0–1.0) interactions per patient in Group A versus 0.0 (IQR: 0.0–0.0) in Group B (p = 0.002, r = 0.33 [medium effect]). Inter-database agreement for binary pDDI detection was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. Of the 80 patients, 31 were negative on both databases and 21 were positive on both (observed agreement: 65%). Twenty-four patients were positive on Micromedex® but negative on UpToDate®, while four showed the reverse pattern. Overall inter-database agreement was fair (κ = 0.331, 95% CI: 0.132–0.531; p = 0.001).

A total of 215 clinically significant drug interaction events were identified (172 by Micromedex® and 43 by UpToDate®) across 159 unique drug pairs. The ten most frequently detected drug pairs are presented in Table 2. The tramadol–scopolamine combination was the most commonly observed interaction (n = 6, 7.5% of all patients), detected by both databases. Aspirin–metformin was the second most frequent interaction (n = 5, 6.3%), identified exclusively by Micromedex®.

Distribution of beers criteria violations and factors associated with potentially inappropriate prescribing

Among the 40 patients in Group A, 17 (42.5%) had a single Beers-flagged PIP, 15 (37.5%) had two PIPs, six (15%) had three PIPs, and two (5%) had five PIPs. Polypharmacy co-existed in 37 of 40 PIP-positive patients (92.5%), indicating a high degree of overlap between PIP and polypharmacy in this group. Univariable logistic regression analysis identified polypharmacy (OR = 20.56, 95% CI: 5.43–77.77; p < 0.001), ACB score (OR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.39–3.92; p < 0.001), CCI score (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.03–1.57; p = 0.020), and Aachen Falls Prevention Scale risk category (OR = 2.54, 95% CI: 1.03–6.26; p = 0.043) as statistically significant predictors of PIP. GDS-15 score (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.98–1.30; p = 0.071) and reason for outpatient visit (OR = 8.27, 95% CI: 0.97–70.67; p = 0.062) were borderline significant and were also included in the multivariable model. In the multivariable model, polypharmacy (OR = 9.91, 95% CI: 2.42–40.66; p = 0.001) and ACB score (OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.06–3.29; p = 0.031) remained independently associated with PIP after adjustment for covariates. GDS-15 score did not retain independent significance (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.97–1.41; p = 0.109). CCI score (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.84–1.41; p = 0.533), Aachen Falls Prevention Scale risk category (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.27–2.95; p = 0.845), and reason for outpatient visit (OR = 3.27, 95% CI: 0.30–35.78; p = 0.333) were also non-significant in the multivariable analysis. Multicollinearity diagnostics did not indicate relevant collinearity among variables included in the multivariable model. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the interrelationship between PIP, clinically significant pDDIs, and medication regimen complexity in a geriatric outpatient population. Our findings demonstrate that older adults with PIP exhibit substantially higher rates and greater numbers of clinically significant pDDIs, as well as significantly increased medication regimen complexity. Notably, polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden emerged as the strongest independent predictors of PIP, highlighting their central role in medication-related risk accumulation in this population. These findings are consistent with the concept of an “iatrogenic triad” in geriatric pharmacotherapy.

Studies have revealed a wide prevalence of PIP (22–79%), with the significant variation attributable to the various patient populations evaluated and the assessment tools employed [20]. In the present study, the equal distribution of patients between the PIP-present and PIP-absent groups was predefined by the study design; therefore, the observed PIP rate should not be interpreted as a population-based prevalence estimate. Rather, the principal contribution of this study lies in characterizing the clinical and pharmacotherapeutic factors associated with PIP.

Studies have shown that the prevalence of polypharmacy among older adults living in the community ranges from 14.5% [21] to 17% [22]. In our study, the overall rate of polypharmacy was notably higher (65%), which may be explained by the inclusion of older adults attending a specialized geriatric outpatient clinic with substantial multimorbidity and medication burden. Similarly, studies conducted in Türkiye have reported polypharmacy rates of 69% in community pharmacy settings [10] and 62.3% in primary care populations [23], supporting the high prevalence of medication burden among older Turkish adults. Consistent with these findings, polypharmacy was markedly more common among patients with PIP in our cohort and remained the strongest independent predictor of PIP in multivariable analysis.

Consistent with previous findings [24, 25], polypharmacy emerged as the strongest independent predictor of PIP in our study, with more than a 20-fold univariable risk and a near 10-fold adjusted risk. An increasing number of medications may amplify prescribing complexity and the likelihood of drug–drug interactions, therapeutic duplication, and prescribing cascades. These mechanisms may be associated with a higher likelihood of inappropriate prescribing in older adults. In contrast, although CCI score was significantly associated with PIP in the univariable analysis, this association was no longer significant after adjustment for other variables. This finding may indicate that the effect of comorbidity burden on PIP is largely mediated through increased medication use. Similarly, the borderline associations observed for GDS-15 score and Aachen Falls Prevention Scale risk category were not maintained in the multivariable model. The purpose of assessing cognitive function and depressive symptoms is that these are common geriatric syndromes that may affect medication management and the quality of prescribing. However, in this study, neither the MoCA nor the GDS-15 scores showed an independent association with PIP. The persistence of polypharmacy and ACB score as independent predictors suggests that medication-related factors may play a more direct role in the occurrence of PIP than patient-related clinical characteristics. Anticholinergic burden is known to be bidirectionally associated with polypharmacy [26], and the independent association between ACB score and PIP observed here implies that prescribing quality, in addition to medication count, may be associated with inappropriate prescribing in older adults.

Increasing medication use substantially raises the risk of pDDIs, which are among the most common forms of PIP in older adults [27] and may contribute to adverse outcomes including hospitalization and reduced quality of life. In our cohort, pDDIs were significantly more frequent among patients with PIP: 75% versus 37.5% by Micromedex® and category D/X interactions were also more common in the PIP group by UpToDate®. Reported pDDI rates in older adults vary considerably across settings—from 20.9% to 65%—depending on study population, prescribing patterns, and detection methodology [15, 28, 29]. The discrepancy between their findings and ours may be explained by differences in study populations, prescribing patterns, and methods used to identify interactions. In addition, only fair agreement was observed between Micromedex® and UpToDate® for binary pDDI detection in our cohort, with Micromedex® identifying substantially more interactions than UpToDate®. This finding likely reflects differences in interaction classification systems and severity thresholds across databases and suggests that reliance on a single screening tool may underestimate the true burden of clinically relevant pDDIs. Although reassuring, the absence of Micromedex®-classified contraindicated interactions in our cohort may be attributable either to appropriate prescribing practices or to the inherent characteristics of an outpatient setting, where life-threatening drug combinations are less prevalent than in inpatient cohorts. These findings are consistent with previous evidence suggesting that pDDIs occurring in the presence of potentially inappropriate medications may further increase medication-related risks in older adults [30]. Together, the coexistence of polypharmacy, anticholinergic burden, and clinically significant pDDIs among patients with PIP provides support for the concept of an iatrogenic triad in geriatric pharmacotherapy.

Beyond these factors, medication regimen complexity constitutes an additional and often overlooked aspect of pharmacotherapy burden. In the present study, patients with PIP exhibited significantly higher MRCI scores, suggesting that inappropriate prescribing may be associated not only with the number of medications prescribed but also with the complexity of medication administration. In a recent study involving older adults presenting to the emergency department, the mean MRCI score was found to be 23.8 ± 13.5; regression analyses revealed that higher MRCI scores were significantly associated with polypharmacy and PIP exposure [31]. A Brazilian study demonstrated a significant association between polypharmacy and a high MRCI, which was associated with drug interactions, PIPs, and therapeutic duplicity [32]. The mean MRCI score of hospitalized elderly patients in Korea was 28.2 ± 14.2, and the MRCI A, B, and C scores were 2.4 ± 1.7, 11.8 ± 6.0, and 14.0 ± 8.1, respectively [33]. Dosing frequency and additional administration instructions also contributed most to regimen complexity in the Korean cohort, supporting our finding that Sections B and C contributed to MRCI differences rather than dosage form complexity. In a study involving elderly patients who visited community pharmacies in Türkiye, median MRCI score of 12.5, with MRCI A, B, and C scores of 3 (1–5), 8.0 (5–13), and 1 (0–3), respectively [10]. An additional finding of our study was that the observed difference in overall MRCI scores was primarily driven by dosing frequency and additional administration instructions, whereas dosage form complexity did not differ between groups. Although previous studies have reported total MRCI scores and, in some cases, the contribution of individual MRCI components, few studies have explored which specific domains of regimen complexity are most strongly associated with PIP. Our findings suggest that regimen complexity related to medication scheduling and administration requirements may be more closely associated with inappropriate prescribing than dosage form complexity alone. This observation may have practical implications, as simplifying dosing schedules and reducing unnecessary administration instructions could potentially reduce pharmacotherapy burden and medication-related risks in older adults. Although direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in patient populations and healthcare settings, these findings collectively suggest that medication regimen complexity is a prevalent challenge among older adults and may be associated with medication-related risks beyond medication count alone.

This study has several limitations. First, the pre-specified equal group allocation was intentionally used to facilitate comparisons between patients with and without PIP. However, this approach introduces selection bias, precludes valid estimation of PIP prevalence in the broader geriatric outpatient population, and limits the generalizability of the findings. Consequently, the current findings should be regarded mostly as estimations of relationships rather than assessments of population prevalence. Second, polypharmacy was defined solely according to the number of concurrently prescribed medications, and no distinction was made between appropriate and inappropriate polypharmacy. Consequently, our findings regarding polypharmacy should be interpreted as reflecting medication burden rather than the clinical appropriateness of multidrug therapy. Third, the single-centre design and small sample size restrict generalizability and increase the risk of type II error for secondary outcomes. Fourth, while per-patient interaction counts were recorded as a secondary analysis, the primary pDDI outcome was binary; future studies should incorporate pDDI severity scores rather than binary classification. Fifth, database-derived pDDIs may overestimate clinical risk because they do not fully account for dose, duration, laboratory values, monitoring, and individual patient context. Sixth, although multicollinearity diagnostics did not indicate relevant collinearity among the predictors, the events-per-variable ratio in the multivariable logistic regression model was approximately 6.7, below the conventional threshold of 10, raising the possibility of model overfitting; the wide confidence intervals observed for some predictors (e.g., reason for outpatient visit, 0.30–35.78) are consistent with this concern. Finally, this study captures medication-related surrogate endpoints rather than clinical outcomes such as adverse drug events or hospitalization.

Conclusion

Geriatric outpatients with PIP exhibited significantly higher rates and greater numbers of clinically significant pDDIs, as well as greater medication regimen complexity, compared with those without PIP. Polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden were the key modifiable factors independently associated with PIP. The fair inter-database agreement between Micromedex® and UpToDate® suggests that using more than one interaction database may provide a more comprehensive assessment of clinically significant pDDIs in older adults with complex medication regimens. These findings suggest that systematic, pharmacist-led medication review may represent a potentially valuable approach for identifying medication-related risks in geriatric outpatient clinics. However, interventional studies evaluating clinical outcomes are needed to confirm its effectiveness. Future studies with larger, population-representative samples and clinical outcome measures are warranted.
