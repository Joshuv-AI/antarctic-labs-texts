Communication and Medication‐Related Deprescribing for Healthcare Professionals: A Rapid Review of the Literature
Authors: Beheshta Momand, Winnie Sun, Jennifer Abbass‐Dick, Adam Dubrowski, Efrosini Papaconstantinou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSignificance/BackgroundPolypharmacy, a rising concern in the older adult population, is associated with significant risks, including adverse drug reactions and inappropriate medication use. Deprescribing, which is supported by effective communication between healthcare professionals and patients, has emerged as an important strategy to reduce potentially inappropriate medications. While numerous frameworks, guidelines, and tools exist to support healthcare professionals in deprescribing, many lack explicit integration of communication strategies, despite their critical role in shared decision‐making and patient engagement.AimThe aim of this rapid review was to synthesize and describe the existing deprescribing frameworks, guidelines, and tools used by healthcare professionals, with a focus on how communication is represented within them. The secondary objective was to extract communication elements from each of the deprescribing frameworks, guidelines, and tools.MethodsWe used rapid review methodology recommended by the World Health Organization. The protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework and reported according to the PRISMA statement. CINAHL, Ovid Medline, and Scopus were searched from January 2003 to July 2024. Eligible studies focused on deprescribing frameworks, guidelines, or tools used by healthcare professionals caring for older adults (≥ 65 years). A qualitative synthesis of the evidence was conducted.Results/FindingsThe search retrieved 5177 articles. After removing 1704 duplicates, 3473 citations were screened for eligibility. Of those, 343 were reviewed in full, and 18 were included in the final synthesis. We identified three frameworks, two guidelines, and seven tools. Frameworks such as A‐TAPER, TAPER, and the 10‐Step Conceptual Framework emphasized patient‐centered care but varied in approach. Communication strategies, shared decision‐making, active listening, feedback, communication adaptation, and encouraging participation were present but not explicit. Most frameworks targeted physicians and pharmacists, with minimal involvement of nurses.Linking Evidence to ActionFuture deprescribing frameworks should explicitly integrate communication strategies and include nurses in their development. Building on these findings, our next step is to engage nurses to identify the most important communication characteristics for effective deprescribing conversations. These insights can guide the development of future frameworks, guidelines, and tools to support structured, patient‐centered communication and improve deprescribing outcomes. This has important implications for clinical practice, education, and policy aimed at optimizing care for older adults.

Introduction

Polypharmacy, the use of five or more medications, is a growing concern among older adults, increasing the risk of adverse drug events (ADEs) such as drug reactions, injury, Potentially Inappropriate Medication (PIM), and hospitalizations (Vasudev et al. 2017). In Canada, over half of institutionalized older adults and 13% in private households experience polypharmacy, contributing to an $11 billion annual economic burden (Church and MacKinnon 2010). Deprescribing, the planned reduction or cessation of unnecessary medications, has emerged as a strategy to manage polypharmacy and improve outcomes (Reeve et al. 2015). While deprescribing initiatives like Assess, Talk, Alternatives, Plan, Engage, Reduce (A‐TAPER) and initiatives such as the Eliminating Medications Through Patient Ownership of End Results (EMPOWER) brochure emphasize patient‐centered care, they often exclude explicit communication strategies (Armistead et al. 2022; Tannenbaum et al. 2014). In addition, they are also primarily targeted at physicians and pharmacists and neglect other healthcare professionals, such as nurses, despite their critical role in patient care. Studies indicate that nurses on average spend around 15% to 19% more time with older adults than physicians (Butler et al. 2018). Nurses are also actively involved in developing healthcare plans based on an individual's needs, educating patients and families, and facilitating care across settings. Therefore, nurses are uniquely positioned to engage and initiate deprescribing conversations, guide families, and monitor outcomes.

Although nurses are uniquely positioned to initiate deprescribing conversations with older adults and should be used more actively in the deprescribing process, they are not always adequately trained in how to communicate effectively during these discussions (Naughton and Hayes 2017). To support their role and to effectively engage in deprescribing, nurses require structured communication frameworks, guidelines, and tools. Providing nurses with effective communication strategies and tools is crucial for fostering meaningful conversations about deprescribing with older adults, ultimately improving adherence and enhancing safety. However, despite the existence of numerous deprescribing frameworks, tools, and guidelines, none explicitly focus on communication. Given that the clinical act of deprescribing is often undertaken by physicians, pharmacists, or other prescribers depending on the healthcare system, this review instead focuses on the communication processes that support deprescribing, with particular emphasis on the role of nurses in facilitating discussions, patient education, and shared decision making.

Aims

The aim of this study was to synthesize the various deprescribing frameworks, guidelines, and tools currently used by health care professionals working with the older adult population. Methods

We conducted a rapid review of the literature. A rapid review is a type of knowledge synthesis in which components of the systematic review process are simplified or omitted to produce information in a short period of time (Lagisz et al. 2022). We followed the methodology recommended by the World Health Organization (Tricco et al. 2017).

Protocol and Registration

We reported our review according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) checklist (Moher et al. 2010). We registered our review with Open Science Framework (OSF) Registry (#s3uvb).

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible studies met the following inclusion criteria: (1) included a deprescribing framework, guideline, or tool; (2) cross‐sectional, case–control, or cohort studies published in a peer‐reviewed journal (applicable to objective 3 only); (3) English only; and (4) investigated adults aged > 65 years. We excluded editorials, commentaries, books, conference documents, lectures, and expert opinion. It's important to note that the scope of this review does not assess the legal capacity of nurses to independently deprescribe. Rather, it examines their communication roles within the deprescribing process, regardless of whether they act as primary decision‐makers or as part of an interprofessional team.

Population

The target population was adults aged 65 years and older. We included this age group based on Statistics Canada (Martin and Grant 2007) definition of older adult. We also limited to older adults that received any components of the deprescribing process by a healthcare professional. Examples of healthcare professionals included physicians (including gerontologists), pharmacists, and nurses. In this review, the term nurses refers to nurse practitioners (NPs), registered nurses (RNs), registered practical nurses (RPNs), and other regulated nursing professionals. While the scope of nursing practice in deprescribing varies internationally, this review is situated in the Canadian context, where nurses most often contribute through communication, patient education, and care coordination within interprofessional teams.

Exposure

Any frameworks, tools, guidelines that focus on deprescribing.

Comparison

We explored all deprescribing frameworks, tools, guidelines in both conceptual and research literature (i.e., cohort studies and Randomized Control Trial) to describe and synthesize all frameworks (Objective 1). Elements of communication within each of the deprescribing frameworks, tools, and/or guidelines identified were extracted (Objective 2).

Outcomes

Deprescribing outcomes included but were not limited to identifying inappropriate medication, altering patients' medication, reducing medication, stopping medication, and/or tapering medication. Various elements of communication within each of the frameworks identified were extracted. Outcomes of communication include initiating deprescribing communication, frequency of communication, patient engagement and involvement, and other communication strategies.

Information Sources

We developed a search strategy in consultation with a health sciences librarian. During rapid reviews, it is suggested to focus on two to three search engines (King et al. 2022). Therefore, for the purposes of this rapid review, we systematically searched three databases believed to generate and capture the most relevant studies focused on the research question, from January 2003–July 2024. The Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Ovid Medline, and Scopus were searched (for the full search strategy, please refer to Appendix A in the Supporting Information).

Study Selection

A two‐phase screening process was used to identify eligible studies. In phase one screening, titles and abstracts were screened and classified as irrelevant or possibly relevant. During phase two screening, the full text of possibly relevant articles was screened for final determination of eligibility. The student lead (BM) was trained to conduct all the screening. The student who conducted the screening had a health sciences background and had received prior training in conducting scoping reviews. The process was supervised by a committee member with expertise in this methodology, and any uncertainties were resolved through discussion. Prior to phase one screening, the quality of screening by BM was validated. Five percent of all eligible articles were randomly selected, and the titles and abstracts of these articles were independently screened by a second experienced investigator (EP). A 95% level of agreement was required between the two reviewers before moving to full phase one screening.

Data Management

Search results were managed using EndNote 20 (Clarivate Analytics), where duplicates were removed. Remaining references were uploaded to EPPI‐Reviewer V.4 (UCL Institute of Education) for screening, appraisal, data extraction, and maintaining an audit trail.

Data Extraction

The student lead (BM) extracted data for Objectives 1 and 2. For Objective 1, study characteristics were synthesized. For Objective 2, communication elements within deprescribing frameworks, guidelines, and tools were identified using theories such as Communication Accommodation Theory, Social Cognitive Theory, and Motivational Interviewing. These elements were grouped into five core strategies: shared decision‐making, tailored communication, active listening, clarification/correction, and encouraging patient participation.

Results

Study Selection

The search retrieved 5177 citations from the following databases: CINAHL, Ovid Medline, and Scopus (Figure 1). We removed 1704 duplicates and screened 3473 articles. Interrater agreement for phase 1 screening was 95% between BM and EP. A total of 343 full‐text articles were screened (phase 2). Of those, 18 articles were eligible for inclusion. Reasons for exclusion were ineligible population (n = 51), did not include a deprescribing framework, tool, or guidelines (n = 187), publication date (n = 1), study type (n = 58), and unable to locate (n = 25). Moreover, four studies did not specify an age range of the population (Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023; Phillips et al. 2023; Strassl et al. 2023; Thompson et al. 2016), therefore, authors were contacted for relevant information. Of these four studies, one author (Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023) responded and met the age eligibility and therefore was included in our synthesis; the other three were excluded (one did not respond, and the other two did not have an age restriction).

Study Characteristics

Study characteristics are presented in the Supporting Information (Appendix B Table S1: Characteristics of Included Studies).

Population

All studies focused on older adults aged 65 and older. The healthcare providers involved were mainly pharmacists (n = 15) (Armistead et al. 2022; Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Cossette et al. 2020; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Page et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; Scott et al. 2012; Scuderi et al. 2022), doctors/physicians (n = 14) (Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Cossette et al. 2020; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023; Mann et al. 2022; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Page et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; Scott et al. 2012), geriatricians (n = 4) (Armistead et al. 2022; Cossette et al. 2020; Page et al. 2016; Petersen et al. 2018), and nurses (n = 4) (Cossette et al. 2020; Forget et al. 2020; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018).

In the Canadian context, categories of nursing include RPNs, RNs, and NPs. NPs have an extended scope of prescribing, while RNs have limited prescribing authority. All categories of nursing play an important role in facilitating medication management and deprescribing (Canadian Nurses Association 2015).

Setting and Location

The settings in which these studies were conducted were in hospitals (n = 9) (Forget et al. 2020; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; Scott et al. 2012; Scuderi et al. 2022), outpatient clinics (n = 4) (Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023; Mann et al. 2022), long‐term care homes (n = 3) (Armistead et al. 2022; Cossette et al. 2020; Page et al. 2016), and patients' homes (n = 1) (Parkinson et al. 2021). Many of the frameworks, tools and guidelines included in this review were based in Canada (n = 9) (Cossette et al. 2020; Forget et al. 2020; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; Moryousef et al. 2022; Perri et al. 2022; Scuderi et al. 2022), with few conducted in the United States (n = 3) (Armistead et al. 2022; Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Petersen et al. 2018), Australia (n = 3) (Page et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Scott et al. 2012), the United Kingdom (n = 1) (McKean et al. 2016), and Germany (n = 1) (Mann et al. 2022).

Deprescribing Frameworks, Guidelines, and Tools

Our review identified three frameworks (Armistead et al. 2022; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023; Scott et al. 2012), two guidelines (Cossette et al. 2020; Mann et al. 2022), and seven tools (Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Forget et al. 2020; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Page et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; Scuderi et al. 2022) to assist healthcare professionals in the deprescribing process. Each is briefly summarized and discussed, comparing approaches to deprescribing, highlighting shared and unique elements (Refer to Appendix C, Table S2).

Frameworks

Three frameworks were identified: The A‐TAPER (Armistead et al. 2022), TAPER (Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023), and the 10‐ Step Conceptual framework (Scott et al. 2012).

The A‐TAPER framework provides a step‐by‐step deprescribing process that emphasizes shared decision‐making, assesses medication risks and benefits, identifies safer alternatives, and establishes monitoring and dose reduction plans to minimize the risks associated with PIMs (Armistead et al. 2022). The Talk, Alternatives, Plan, Engage, and Reduce (TAPER) approach, supported by the Team Approach to Polypharmacy Evaluation and Reduction—Medical Decision (TaperMD) tool, employs a team‐based strategy involving a pharmacist and family doctor to gather patient preferences, quality of life, and priorities, which are reviewed collaboratively to create a plan for tapering medication or monitoring PIMs with ongoing support and follow‐up (Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023).

Finally, the 10‐Step Conceptual Deprescribing Framework takes a comprehensive approach by reviewing all medications, considering patient life expectancy, functional capacity, and care goals, and guiding clinicians to identify high‐risk patients and evaluate medications for reduction or discontinuation based on long‐term risks and benefits.

All three frameworks focus on reducing PIMs by prioritizing patient‐centered care in deprescribing decisions. Each of these frameworks provides a structured approach to deprescribing by focusing on patient‐centered care and reducing medications no longer appropriate to the patient. They share common elements, including a systematic process involving patient input, ongoing monitoring, and prioritizing safe reduction of medications. Despite these similarities, each framework has a unique focus; for example, the TAPER Approach uses a team‐based structure supported by the TaperMD tool, A‐TAPER emphasizes a step‐by‐step tapering method with flexibility across medication types, and the 10‐Step Conceptual Framework offers a broad evaluation of each medication's benefits and risks in relation to the patient's overall health and long‐term goals.

Guidelines

This review uncovered two guidelines which are the Antipsychotic Deprescribing Guidelines (Cossette et al. 2020) and Care of Frail Older Adults (COFRAIL) Deprescribing Manual (Mann et al. 2022).

The Antipsychotics Deprescribing Guideline, developed under Québec's Optimizing Practices, Use, Care and Services—Antipsychotics (OPUS‐AP) program, supports deprescribing for long‐term care residents with dementia by improving quality of life through personalized care and reducing unnecessary antipsychotic medications. It outlines a structured process, starting with medication assessment, promoting nonpharmacological alternatives, and evaluating patients' eligibility based on health status. For eligible individuals, a gradual tapering plan is implemented and monitored, with a focus on team collaboration and thorough documentation to ensure safe and effective deprescribing (Cossette et al. 2020).

The COFRAIL Deprescribing Manual is a structured guide for general practitioners to address polypharmacy in frail older adults, offering steps to identify candidates, prioritize targets, and monitor adverse effects. It includes practical tools like algorithms and charts to streamline the process, emphasizing shared decision‐making and patient‐centered communication in primary care (Mann et al. 2022). We have categorized this manual as a guideline because it provides structured recommendations to assist physicians in identifying PIM, establishing a clear decision‐making process, facilitating patient‐centered communication, and using practical tools to support deprescribing in primary care.

The two guidelines have a patient‐centered approach, each structured to help healthcare providers in stopping, reducing, or tapering PIMs. They both emphasize risk assessment, a step‐by‐step deprescribing process, and open communication with patients/caregivers. However, they differ in focus; the Antipsychotic Deprescribing Guideline targets antipsychotics in dementia care, and the COFRAIL Manual addresses broader polypharmacy, guiding general practitioners in deprescribing multiple medication classes in frail older adults.

Tools

The review identified seven deprescribing tools aimed at reducing polypharmacy in various healthcare settings. These tools are the Good Palliative‐Geriatric Practice (GPGP) Tool, Vital, Important, Optional, Not indicated, or Every medication has an indication (VIONE), Medication Software Application for Deprescribing (MedSafer), TAPER MD/AusTAPER, Shed‐MEDS, Toronto Deprescribing Tool, and the Five‐Step Protocol (n = 7) (Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Forget et al. 2020; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Page et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; Scuderi et al. 2022).

The GPGP Tool simplifies deprescribing by providing a structured approach for physicians and pharmacists to assess each medication's appropriateness. Through independent assessment and a consensus process, they determine which medications should be deprescribed, supporting individualized, patient‐centered care by focusing on risk–benefit (Page et al. 2016).

VIONE categorizes medications as Vital, Important, Optional, Not indicated, or Every medication has an indication. Clinicians review medications based on this categorization, aiming to reduce PIMs and enhance patient safety while documenting deprescribing decisions in the electronic health record for easy reference (Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021).

MedSafer focuses on preoperative care for older adults by identifying PIMs based on risk categories (high, intermediate, low) and providing recommendations for each. It offers guidance on tapering and stopping medications, aiming to reduce ADEs and improve outcomes for patients undergoing surgery (Forget et al. 2020).

For adults transitioning to post‐acute care, the Shed‐Medications in De‐prescribing (Shed‐MEDS) is a hospital‐based deprescribing tool and involves a comprehensive medication history review, risk assessment, patient‐centered discussions, and communication with prescribers to reduce high‐risk medications, particularly those associated with geriatric syndromes, while supporting transitions in care (Petersen et al. 2018).

Australian TAPER (AusTAPER) uses the TAPERMD app, which involves a home medication review by pharmacists for older adults. It facilitates collaboration between pharmacists and general practitioners (GPs) to discuss deprescribing options and prioritize patient engagement, ultimately helping patients and GPs make shared decisions on medication adjustments (Parkinson et al. 2021).

The Toronto Deprescribing Tool Targets dialysis patients. This tool is pharmacist‐led and reviews specific medications (e.g., diuretics, PPIs). It involves collaboration with the nephrology team and follow‐up audits to ensure continued deprescribing, addressing medication appropriateness for patients with complex renal needs (Scuderi et al. 2022).

The Five‐Step Deprescribing Protocol on the other hand provides a standardized approach for inpatient deprescribing, assessing the appropriateness of medications through a structured five‐step process. It involves patient consultation, documentation of deprescribing decisions, and follow‐up to monitor for adverse effects, aiming to improve safety and reduce the medication burden (McKean et al. 2016).

All tools aim to reduce polypharmacy and enhance patient safety by providing structure to the medication review process. They generally emphasize collaboration among healthcare professionals and incorporate patient involvement in the deprescribing process, supporting adherence and tailored care. However, the tools also vary in process and structure, particularly in patient engagement and clinician collaboration. For instance, AusTAPER and GPGP focus on teamwork across healthcare roles, while MedSafer and Shed‐MEDS provide personalized recommendations. The VIONE and GPGP are integrated with the electronic systems enabling a streamlined documentation process and follow‐up with a large healthcare system.

Communication Strategies

Effective communication is a cornerstone of successful deprescribing, facilitating shared decision‐making, enhancing patient engagement, and ensuring the safe discontinuation of inappropriate medications (Peat et al. 2022). While pharmacological knowledge is essential to support the clinical judgment on what should be deprescribed and when to initiate deprescribing conversations, communication is the tool to support clinicians on how to effectively engage in these discussions and to solicit buy‐in from patients. However, many of the existing deprescribing frameworks, guidelines, and tools lack explicit instructions on communication strategies within the process. To address this gap and fulfill Objective 2 of this review, we systematically extracted communication elements from each resource, even though they were often embedded implicitly rather than clearly outlined. This process was challenging but essential, given the absence of dedicated communication frameworks for deprescribing and the need to inform the development of future tools. We utilized communication theories such as Communication Accommodation Theory, Speech Codes Theory, Social Cognitive Theory, and the Teach‐Back Method to guide the identification of key communication elements which we identified in the frameworks, guidelines and tools: (1) Communication Accommodation Theory focuses on how individuals adjust their communication styles to enhance understanding or express identity (Giles and Ogay 2013), (2) Speech Codes Theory highlights how cultural groups use distinct ways of speaking and interpreting messages (Hart 2017), (3) Social Cognitive Theory explains how people learn through observation and interaction within their environment (Luszczynska and Schwarzer 2015), (4). The Teach‐Back Method involves asking patients to repeat information in their own words to confirm understanding and promote clear communication (Yen and Leasure 2019). Using these theories, each framework, guideline, and tool were examined in detail to identify where communication characteristics were present (see Appendix D Table S3). These characteristics were then grouped to highlight recurring themes, which led to the development of five core communication strategies: encouraging patient participation, active listening, feedback, communication adaptation, and shared decision‐making (Figure 2). These themes emphasize key elements vital to effective deprescribing practices.

Communication cycle for deprescribing.

Encouraging Patients

Two Frameworks (Armistead et al. 2022; Scott et al. 2012) and two tools (Parkinson et al. 2021; Scuderi et al. 2022) focused on encouraging patients during the deprescribing process as its first core communication strategy. This strategy focused on motivating older adults to actively participate in the communication and creating a safe space for them to do that by validating their input and concerns and ensuring there is an open dialogue. Our review findings assessed the relationship between the different communication strategies and developed a communication cycle to illustrate their interconnected role in the deprescribing communication framework (Table 1).

Active Listening

Two Frameworks (Armistead et al. 2022; Scott et al. 2012), one guideline (Cossette et al. 2020), and three tools (Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; McKean et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021) focused on active listening, where HCPs prioritize listening attentively to patients' concerns and feedback (Armistead et al. 2022; Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Cossette et al. 2020; McKean et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Scott et al. 2012). Active listening fosters a deep understanding of the patient's issues that were linked with empathy and building rapport, which is important during the deprescribing process.

Feedback

Two frameworks (Armistead et al. 2022; Scott et al. 2012), one guideline (Mann et al. 2022), and two tools (Petersen et al. 2018; Scuderi et al. 2022) focused on feedback, where healthcare professionals provided clarity on any misunderstanding and confirmed understanding (Armistead et al. 2022; Mann et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; Scott et al. 2012; Scuderi et al. 2022). This communication strategy involves healthcare professionals proactively clarifying information by encouraging older adults to ask questions, provide clarity, and clear up any confusion they may have. This is important because in order for deprescribing to be successful and maintained, older adults need to be able to understand why deprescribing is important and how certain medications may pose a higher risk.

Communication Adaptation/Tailored Communication

Three frameworks (Armistead et al. 2022; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023), one guideline (Cossette et al. 2020), and nine tools (Forget et al. 2020; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Parkinson et al. 2021; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; Scuderi et al. 2022) highlighted communication adaptation. Communication adaptation refers to information that is delivered in a way that is relevant, accessible, and easily understandable to the patient. It involves tailoring the communication style to meet individual needs, whether by adjusting the approach to better connect with the patient or providing recommendations based on their health.

Shared‐Decision Making

Finally, two frameworks (Armistead et al. 2022; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023), two guidelines (Cossette et al. 2020; Mann et al. 2022), and five tools (Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Forget et al. 2020; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Page et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018) emphasized shared decision‐making, highlighting the importance of collaboration between HCPs and older adult patients in the deprescribing process (Armistead et al. 2022; Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Cossette et al. 2020; Forget et al. 2020; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mann et al. 2022; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Page et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018).

Discussion

Deprescribing manages polypharmacy in older adults by safely reducing or discontinuing unnecessary or harmful medications (Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023). To our knowledge this is the first rapid review to synthesize frameworks, guidelines, and tools supporting deprescribing in the older adult population and extracting communication elements. The healthcare professionals involved in the studies were predominately pharmacists (n = 15) (Armistead et al. 2022; Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Cossette et al. 2020; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Page et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; Scott et al. 2012; Scuderi et al. 2022), doctors/physicians (n = 14) (Constantino‐Corpuz and Alonso 2021; Cossette et al. 2020; Mangin, Lamarche, Agarwal, et al. 2023; Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023; Mann et al. 2022; McDonald et al. 2019, 2022; McKean et al. 2016; Moryousef et al. 2022; Page et al. 2016; Parkinson et al. 2021; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018; Scott et al. 2012), with few that included geriatricians (n = 4) (Armistead et al. 2022; Cossette et al. 2020; Page et al. 2016; Petersen et al. 2018), and nurses (Cossette et al. 2020; Forget et al. 2020; Perri et al. 2022; Petersen et al. 2018). The involvement of healthcare professionals varied across the studies. In the development phase of deprescribing frameworks, guidelines, and tools, only pharmacists, physicians, geriatricians, and researchers were involved. Notably, no studies included nurses in the development process, an absence that is both surprising and concerning given their critical role in caring for older adults, monitoring medication effects, and facilitating ongoing communication.

Moreover, there was limited detail on the clinical criteria or decision‐making frameworks used by nurses when identifying medications for deprescribing. Where such criteria were reported, nurses typically worked within interprofessional teams and contributed to deprescribing decisions using established resources such as the Beers Criteria, STOPP/START criteria, and drug‐specific deprescribing guidelines. In the studies, nurses did not independently determine medication appropriateness; rather, they facilitated patient engagement, gathered relevant clinical and medication history, and relayed concerns to prescribers as part of collaborative decision‐making processes.

Furthermore, the included studies provided limited information on whether nurses engaged in deprescribing interventions possessed formal pharmacotherapy training or participated within a collaborative practice model involving physicians and pharmacists, highlighting a gap in the literature regarding the scope and training of nurses in deprescribing activities. Nonetheless, four studies did incorporate nurses during the implementation phase. In the Shed‐MEDS study (Petersen et al. 2018), nurses were part of the interdisciplinary team applying the deprescribing framework in hospital discharge planning. In the MedSafer study (Perri et al. 2022), nurses supported implementation by gathering accurate medication histories and contributing to communication around deprescribing recommendations. The OPUS‐AP strategy (Cossette et al. 2020) included nurses in nonpharmacologic intervention delivery, care planning, and collaborative team discussions. Lastly, in the preoperative medication study by (Forget et al. 2020), nurses conducted medication reconciliation and supported the identification of PIMs prior to surgery. In the Canadian context, nurses typically contribute to deprescribing through collaborative practice with physicians and pharmacists. Their involvement focuses on patient assessments, communication, and supporting adherence, while decisions about which medications to deprescribe are generally made within interprofessional teams and guided by established clinical guidelines. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that while some studies acknowledge the role of nurses in deprescribing implementation, the overall evidence remains limited. Further research is needed to strengthen understanding of nurse‐led deprescribing and ensure their expertise is integrated into both the development and application of deprescribing frameworks.

Deprescribing occurs more predominantly in the hospital setting, where concerns about polypharmacy and PIMs are heightened due to their link to drug interactions and hospitalization. Hospitals offer controlled environments that allow healthcare professionals to re‐evaluate medications, identify PIMs, and implement deprescribing strategies with close patient monitoring, making them ideal for addressing these risks. This focus on hospitals aligns with the findings of our review. Thus, it was not surprising that the majority of the settings found in our review were in hospitals.

Studies were conducted across Canada (n = 9), the United States (n = 4), Australia (n = 3), the United Kingdom (n = 1), and Germany (n = 1), reflecting Canada's commitment in promoting safe deprescribing practices through initiatives like the Canadian Deprescribing Network and Choosing Wisely Canada. These programs raise awareness about deprescribing PIMs and safer medication practices among healthcare providers and patients. Key communication strategies identified in frameworks and tools include encouraging patient participation, active listening to understand concerns, providing feedback for clarity, adapting communication to patient needs, and shared decision‐making. Together, these strategies foster trust, collaboration, and confidence in deprescribing decisions, overcoming barriers to effective implementation.

Our review also showed that communication is often included in deprescribing tools and guidelines, but not in a clear or direct way. For example, MedSafer appears primarily focused on identifying PIMs and providing recommendations but subtly incorporates shared decision‐making by prompting healthcare professionals to consider patient preferences and health conditions, reflecting communication adaptation. Additionally, communication adaptation was reflected in the reports, which is emphasized through the patient's health condition, values, and other critical details to guide conversations. These implicit communication elements required a detailed understanding of each step in the tool to uncover the communication components. Similarly, the GPGP tool implicitly emphasizes communication by encouraging a focus on patient needs, comfort, and quality of life, supporting shared decision‐making and a patient‐centered deprescribing approach.

Several overlaps were identified in the communication strategies and characteristics. Core communication strategies that overlapped included shared decision‐making, feedback, clarification, and encouraging participation. In terms of communication characteristics, empathy and active listening were found.

Active Listening, a core strategy focused on listening attentively to understand the speaker's perspective, is essential in all the other core strategies such as Shared‐Decision Making, Feedback, Clarification, and Encouraging Participation in the Communication. In addition, we can also see Shared Decision‐Making as a strategy that overlaps with the other four core communication strategies. Shared Decision‐Making inherently involves Encouraging Participation, as patients are invited to actively engage in decisions about their care, ensuring their values and preferences are considered. This strategy also connects with Feedback, where healthcare providers provide and receive input, clarifying any uncertainties and ensuring mutual understanding throughout the decision‐making process. Furthermore, Communication Adaptation is integral to Shared Decision‐Making, as it requires tailoring the communication approach to align with the patient's needs, comprehension level, and comfort. Finally, Active Listening is essential in Shared Decision‐Making, as it enables healthcare providers to fully understand the patient's perspective and respond thoughtfully. This overlap is unsurprising, as communication is a dynamic process that often requires multiple strategies that work together to achieve effective interactions.

Moreover, other communication characteristics like “empathy” were difficult to categorize as it plays a fundamental role in all five core communication strategies. For example, it is needed for shared decision‐making to address patients' conditions, concerns, and needs, and for active listening to understand without bias. Empathy also supports encouraging patient participation, adapting communication to their understanding, and providing feedback to clarify confusion. Ultimately, empathy was categorized under shared decision‐making, as it fosters trust and collaboration, essential for patient‐centered care. Similarly, building rapport was placed in the category where it best aligned.

We also found that some core communication strategies received more emphasis than others, particularly shared decision‐making, which helps patients understand the rationale for deprescribing and improves adherence. Communication adaptation was also widely emphasized, involving tailoring communication to patients' needs, simplifying medical jargon, and ensuring information is accessible. This approach fosters a collaborative and supportive environment, empowering patients to participate in their care decisions.

While other communication strategies, like Feedback and Encouraging Patients to participate in the communication, were not emphasized as much. This could be because they are assumed to occur during the communication process or it could be that the framework, guideline, and tool may emphasize the clinical aspects of deprescribing, leaving communication strategies like feedback and patient encouragement out.

Communication plays an important role in facilitating deprescribing and in reducing barriers. It creates a supportive and collaborative environment. Studies have found that health care professionals who possess effective communication skills are more likely to have patients willing to have medication stoppers. While those that did not address the patients' concerns regarding stopping their medications had lower willingness to accept deprescribing. Strategies like shared‐decision making empower patients and increase the likelihood of engagement with deprescribing and also have the power to turn long‐term care centers into patient‐centered, supporting the deprescribing process. Furthermore, a tailored approach is a strong enabler for deprescribing (Mangin, Lamarche, Templeton, et al. 2023). Therefore, the core communication strategies, along with their respective characteristics, are essential in the deprescribing process and act as facilitators by fostering patient engagement, building trust, and addressing potential barriers.

Conclusion

This rapid review synthesized existing deprescribing frameworks, tools, and guidelines for older adults and is the first to examine their embedded communication elements. While these resources aid healthcare professionals in managing polypharmacy, communication strategies are often underrepresented. Effective communication is crucial for ensuring older adults understand and adhere to deprescribing recommendations. Nurses, with their close interactions with patients, are well‐positioned to support deprescribing but are rarely included in these processes. Future studies should focus on identifying key communication elements, particularly those impactful to nurses, to enhance the development of patient‐centered and effective deprescribing resources.
