Breast cancer and microbiome: a systematic review highlighting challenges for clinical translation
Authors: Chandrani Bose, Natalia Gontarczyk Uczkowski, Krishna Sukla, Nilixa Raval, Mohammed Monzoorul Haque, Yingting Zhang, Binuja Varma, Jaya M Satagopan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBreast cancer, the most common cancer in women worldwide, is linked to microbiome imbalances. This suggests the potential for microbiome-based breast cancer management. However, a comprehensive and reproducible understanding of the microbiome’s role in breast cancer is lacking. We conducted a systematic literature review to explore microbiome signatures and specific genera associated with breast cancer development, subtypes, and outcomes.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science using the terms “microbiome” and “breast cancer”, limiting to publications of human studies in English language between January 2011 and January 2025, and adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. We extracted the study characteristics and results and summarized our findings.ResultsFrom 2536 articles, we identified 48 eligible studies – 22 case-control, 13 cohorts without any intervention, and 13 cohorts with an intervention. These studies included 3735 women with and 2023 without a diagnosis of breast cancer. Most studies used fecal (29) or breast tissue (14) samples. The genera Clostridium, Lcatobacillus, Prevotella were most commonly associated with breast cancer. However, no genus was consistently linked to the same outcome across studies, rendering meta-analysis unfeasible. Overall, we observed heterogenous methods for data generation and computational analysis leading to challenges in comparative analysis.ConclusionsWhile several genera are linked to breast cancer, the findings lack consistency across studies. Future studies should employ standardized outcomes, data collection, and processing methods to establish reproducible microbial markers to develop effective microbiome-based breast cancer management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-03843-8.Keywords: Breast cancer, Genera, Microbial signatures, Microbiome, Systematic review

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer diagnosis and a leading cause of cancer-related death among women worldwide [1]. Numerous risk factors contribute to the development, progression, subtypes, treatment response, quality of life, and survival outcomes [2–5]. The human body’s microbial environment plays a critical role in maintaining health through its interactions with immune and metabolic pathways [6]. Imbalances in the microbial environment have been linked to the pathogenesis and progression of breast cancer and its subtypes [7–9]. Emerging research suggests that the microbial environment in the gut may affect estrogen metabolism and regulate immune system function, both of which are crucial in the development of breast cancer, especially estrogen receptor (ER)-positive cancers, and response to treatment [6, 8, 10–16]. In addition to the gut, breast tissue, mouth, urine, vagina, blood, and skin also have diverse and unique microbial communities that have been linked to breast cancer development and prognosis [6, 17–20]. These findings highlight the potential for microbiome-based approaches in diagnosis and therapeutic management of breast cancer [21].

A comprehensive understanding of the microbiome’s relationship to breast cancer is critical for realizing its clinical potential in effective disease management. Despite growing interest and research in this area, systematic evaluation of the findings and their reproducibility is lacking, which considerably hinders our ability to fully understand how the microbiome influences breast cancer. Consistent findings across studies are crucial to establish robust and reproducible understanding of the microbiome’s role in breast cancer biology and phenotypes and to unlock its potential for clinical applications [22].

While the role of the microbiome in breast cancer is increasingly recognized, significant gaps remain in the literature, particularly due to the lack of standardization in data collection and analysis [23]. Despite advancements in omics technologies that enable taxonomic and functional interpretation, studies vary widely in their choice of biospecimens (e.g., breast tissue, blood, stool, saliva), sequencing techniques (e.g., 16 S rRNA vs. metagenomic sequencing), and bioinformatics pipelines. These methodological differences contribute to inconsistencies in findings, hinder cross-study comparisons, and limit the reproducibility of results [24]. In this systematic review, we examine and summarize these variations across existing studies, highlighting their impact on research outcomes and underscoring the need for standardized approaches to improve comparability and clinical translation.

We systematically searched electronic databases and identified 48 published studies of microbiome in breast cancer, extracting details on participant demographics, biospecimen types, microbiome sequencing methods, study endpoints or outcomes, and microbial genera associated with the outcomes. Our findings revealed several genera corresponding to key metabolic pathways associated with various outcomes. However, there was considerable heterogeneity across studies, and no single genus was consistently associated with the same outcome across multiple studies. Further, several studies did not adjust their statistical analyses for multiple comparisons. Our results highlight the need for future studies with well-defined outcomes, comparable designs, standardized data collection and data processing, and robust statistical methods. Such improvements are essential to identify reproducible genera that could advance microbiome-based strategies for breast cancer management.

Methods

We performed a systematic literature search using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 Guidelines [25].

Search strategies

We constructed search strategies around two major concepts – microbiome and breast cancer – using medical subject headings (MeSH), keywords, and related terms. A pilot PubMed (U.S. National Library of Science) search, reviewed and validated by subject experts (BV and JMS), informed our comprehensive search across four databases – PubMed, Embase (Elsevier), Scopus (Elsevier), and Web of Science (Clarivate). The PRISMA-S extension checklist guided our process [26], with details of the search strategies provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

We considered studies published in English between 1 January 2011 and January 30, 2025. The inclusion criteria were: (1) the study was about the role of gut, oral, tissue-based, or vaginal microbiome in breast cancer risk or outcomes; (2) the study subjects were humans; (3) biospecimens in the study were from women with or without a diagnosis of breast cancer; and (4) the study was published in English language. We excluded in-vitro studies, animal trials, reviews, abstracts, and case reports. We registered our study protocol in the PROSPERO repository (registration number CRD42022346832) [27].

Screening

We used EndNote [28], a reference management tool, to collect and manage our search results from the four databases. We identified and eliminated duplicated references using the in-built feature of EndNote and through manual inspection. Two reviewers (CB, NR) screened the remaining references at the title and abstract level in Rayyan, a screening tool for systematic reviews [29]. The reviewers met to discuss concordance of their reviews and resolved their differences via discussion and with input from a third reviewer (MH). The differences were not significant disagreements between the reviewers; rather some uncertainty arose regarding the inclusion of certain studies. For example, one study included metabolites, that one reviewer was uncertain about including the study. The third reviewer facilitated discussions to reach a consensus by reviewing whether the study satisfied the eligibility criteria, even though it examined metabolites, thus ensuring a consistent and thorough approach in study selection. A library and information science expert (YZ) selected the final references emanating from this discussion and uploaded these in Rayyan for full-text evaluation by the same reviewers independently. Full-text articles that met the inclusion criteria were chosen to be in the final set for data extraction and analysis.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers (CB and NGU) rigorously reviewed the selected articles and extracted various study characteristics, including study title, author(s), year of publication, study design, sample size, study location, outcome, race/ethnicity of study participants, age, biospecimen type, sequencing type, statistical and computational methods used, key findings, and mechanistic or functional insights described in the study. The reviewers independently organized this information in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet [30], examined each other’s spreadsheet and finalized any differences over discussions and input from other reviewers (KS, MH, and JMS) to obtain a single final spreadsheet.

Quality assessment

Two independent reviewers (KS and CB) assessed the risk of bias and quality evidence of the selected studies using the ROBINS-I (risk of bias in non-randomized studies of intervention) tool for cohort studies [31] and the NOS (Newcastle-Ottawa Scale) tool for case-control studies [32]. For cohort studies, risk of bias was assessed across 7 domains: confounding, participant selection, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results. Case-control studies were evaluated in 3 domains: study selection, comparability, and ascertainment of exposure. The study team reviewed the bias classifications and resolved any discrepancies.

Data analysis

We calculated frequencies of articles according to study characteristics. In analyses of microbiome data, alpha diversity is a measure of microbial diversity within subgroups of samples, beta diversity is a measure of the differences in microbial communities between different sample groups, and relative abundance is a measure of the percentage of the microbiome that are made up of a specific organism. We calculated the frequencies of studies reporting these measures, summarized the results described in the articles, and extracted bacterial genus and species reported to be abundant in different subgroups of samples. We used the NCBI tree taxonomy browser to identify the phyla of the genera and species reported to have significantly different abundance between various subgroups [33, 34].

Ethics committee (Institutional Review Board) approval was not required for this systematic review.

Results

Study selection

The PRISMA diagram of Fig. 1 shows our study identification and selection process. We identified 2,338 studies through electronic searches across four databases, covering publications from January 2011 to August 2022. After removing 927 duplicate references, we screened 1,411 studies in Rayyan by titles and abstracts, which identified 66 studies for full-text retrieval and eligibility checking. Of these, 22 were conference abstracts and one was a protocol. We eliminated these, leaving 43 studies eligible for the review. An updated search was performed on January 30, 2025, covering publications from September 1, 2022 to January 30, 2025 with 198 references retrieved and screened independently in Rayyan by two reviewers. Five additional studies were selected from this updated search, making the total number of selected studies 48 for the final review and analysis. (Supplementary Table 1).

Study characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of these 48 studies from 15 countries,, involving 3,735 women with breast cancer and 2,023 without a diagnosis of breast cancer. The majority of studies were conducted in the United States (35%) and China (25%) (Fig. 2A), primarily focusing on Caucasian, African, and Han Chinese populations (Fig. 2B). A surge in microbiome research on breast cancer in more recent years is evident since 67% of the studies were published between January 2021 and January 2025 (Fig. 2C). Fecal samples (62.5%) and breast tissues (33%) were the most commonly used biospecimen, and 83% of the studies used 16 S ribosomal RNA sequencing.

The studies varied in designs, outcomes, and findings (Supplementary Table 2). Of the 48 studies, 26 (54%) used a cohort design, with 13 including an intervention investigating the microbiome’s role in treatment response [35–47]. The remaining 13 examined associations with disease subtype (5 studies), tissue types (3 studies), quality of life (2 studies), tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (1 study), disease recurrence (1 study), and diet (1 study) [14, 48–59]. Twenty two (46%) studies used a case-control design [16–19, 60–77], exploring the microbiome’s association with breast cancer (17 studies), premenopausal (1 study) and postmenopausal (1 study) breast cancer, disease subtypes and prognosis (1 study), tissue types (1 study), and bone metastasis (1 study). Some case-control studies included women with benign breast disease as controls or as a separate group.

Twenty three (48%) studies reported mean ages for breast cancer cases (37 to 63 years) and controls (35.5 to 61.6 years). Nine (19%) studies reported median ages, ranging from 47 to 62 years for cases and 30–62 years for controls. The remaining studies reported age ranges spanning 25–85 years for cases and 40–60 years for controls.

Quality assessment

Supplementary Table 3 presents the risk of bias analysis. Cohort studies showed low risk of bias based on ROBINS-I scales (Fig. 3A and B), and most case-control studies had low risk per the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (Fig. 3C). However, biases were noted mostly in comparability and confounder control (45%), exposure ascertainment (59%), and inconsistent ascertainment between cases and controls (50%). These biases were mainly due to failure to adjust for diet in analyses, insufficient clinical data details, and inadequate description of control selection.

Significant associations of alpha diversity

Alpha diversity was reported in 39 (81%) studies – 19 case-control and 20 cohort studies. The most commonly used metrics were the Shannon index (36 studies), followed by the Chao index (17 studies), Simpson index (10 studies), and phylogenetic diversity (8 studies).

Among the 19 case-control studies reporting alpha diversity, 8 (44%) found no significant microbial differences between breast cancer cases and controls. Of the 11 studies that identified significant differences, 8 reported reduced alpha diversity in cases, mainly in fecal or breast tissue samples. Two studies found increased alpha diversity in cases, while another associated alpha diversity to stromal fat percentage, both based on fecal samples.

Of the 20 cohort studies reporting alpha diversity, 12 (60%) found no significant difference between breast cancer and normal tissues or based on treatment or sleep disturbance. The remaining 8 (38%) observed reduced diversity in treated cases and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 positive (HER2+) tumors, while higher diversity was linked to effective neo-adjuvant therapy and increased fatigue. Notably, one study found lower diversity in tumor tissues of non-Hispanic Black women but higher diversity in non-Hispanic White women and in triple negative tumors, compared to adjacent normal tissues.

Significant associations of beta diversity

Beta diversity was reported in 37 (77%) studies – 16 case-control and 21 cohort studies. The commonly used measures were the Bray-Curtis metric, unweighted Unifrac, and weighted Unifrac, featured in 15, 21, and 22 studies, respectively. Some studies used multiple beta diversity measures.

Of the 16 case-control studies reporting beta diversity, 10 (63%) found differences in microbial communities between those with breast cancer or benign breast disease and healthy individuals. Similarly, among 21 cohort studies, 7 (33%) found differences between breast tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues, between tumor subtypes, between breast and skin tissues, and differences according to quality of life such as fatigue and sleep, and body mass index.

Microbial signatures

We identified 186 distinct genera having significantly different relative abundance with respect to disease status, subtypes, and outcomes across 30 (62.5%) studies (see Supplementary Table 4). Most genera belong to the phylum Firmicutes (37.6%), followed by a-Proteobacteria (9.7%) and Cytophaga / Fusobacterium / Bacteroides (CFB) Group Bacteria (8.6%). Further, 82 (44%) genera were less abundant in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues, and in fecal samples of breast cancer cases versus healthy controls. In contrast, 37 (20%) were more abundant in tumor tissues than normal tissues, 16 (8.6%) in cases with effective than ineffective chemotherapy, 14 (7.5%) in HER2 + than HER2- tumors, 11 (6%) in estrogen receptor positive (ER+) than estrogen receptor negative (ER-) tumors, and 10 (5.4%) in cases with low than high tumor infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) (Fig. 4A). Fewer than 5% of genera showed differences based on quality of life or post-chemotherapy changes.

Common results pertaining to statistically significant genera (A) and frequency of studies reporting a specific genus (B)

In these analyses, Clostridium, Lactobacillus, Prevotella, and Ruminococcus were the most frequently identified genera, each identified in at least 5 studies, followed by Acinetobacter, Faecalibacterium, Megasphaera, Roseburia, and Streptococcus documented in 4 studies (Fig. 4B). However, the results for each genus varied considerably across the studies, making meta-analysis unfeasible. For example, the diverse findings for Clostridium included a negative correlation with the percentage of fibrosis in breast cancer tissues and soluble dietary fiber, higher abundance in tumor tissues responsive to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and certain tumor subtypes, and higher abundance in benign conditions. Similarly, genus Lactobacillus demonstrated diverse findings, including association with post-chemotherapy increase, higher abundance in tumor tissues, neoadjuvant chemotherapy-responsive tumors, and fecal samples of breast cancer patients, but lower abundance in post-menopausal women. The genus Prevotella had lower abundance in fecal samples of breast cancer cases, breast cancer tissues receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and tissues of patients undergoing mammoplasty procedures. However, its abundance increased post-chemotherapy, and it was correlated with change in fatigue. The genus Acinetobacter had higher abundance in tumor and bone metastasis than normal samples, and in HER2 + than HER2- and progesterone receptor negative (PR-) than PR positive (PR+) samples. The genus Faecalibacterium had higher abundance in chemotherapy-responsive tumors, lower abundance in fecal samples of breast cancer cases, and a correlation with changes in fatigue before and after treatment. The abundance of genus Meghasphaera changed over time and this genus had higher abundance in tumor than normal, in ER + than ER-, and in HER2 + than HER2- samples.

Twelve genera, including 7 from the Firmicutes phylum, showed consistent patterns of relative abundance – either higher or lower abundance in tumor versus normal tissue – across at least two studies. However, meta-analysis was not feasible due to considerable heterogeneity in study design, populations, biospecimen types, outcomes, or caner subtypes (Supplementary Table 5).

Microbial signatures of breast cancer subtypes

Ten (21%) studies investigated the diversity and composition of the microbiome across different breast cancer subtypes, including ER+, HER2+, and triple negative (TN) tumors. These studies found that hormone receptor (HR) positive tumors (i.e., ER + or PR + tumors) generally have a more diverse microbiome than other subtypes. The subtype-specific genus-level microbial signatures are largely distinct across the studies. However, at the phylum level, HR positive tissues, especially ER+, had lower abundance of genera within the a-Proteobacteria phylum, including Methylobacterium, Caulobacter, and Bartonella. In contrast, bacterial genera within the Firmicutes, CGB Group Bacteria, and High G + C Gram-positive Bacteria phyla were more abundant in ER + tumors.

Fecal samples showed distinct microbial signature in the HR positive subtypes, with lower abundance of genera Enterococcus, Turicibacter, and Veillonella in ER + and lower abundance of Turicibacter and Clostridium in PR + patients. However, one study found higher abundance of Megasphaera, Roseburia, and Prevotella in ER + patients and higher abundance of Bacteroides and Hydrogenophillus in ER- patients. Further, Prevotellaceae and Tyzzerella were enriched in PR + patients, while Barnesiellaceae and Lactobacilliaceae (phylum Firmicutes) were enriched in PR- patients.

Additionally, triple-positive breast cancer (ER+, PR+, HER2+) had fewer unique microbial signatures, while TN tumors, known for aggressive behavior, had the least diverse microbiome, with non-overlapping taxa across studies.

Discussion

In this systematic review of 48 studies, we examined the microbiome’s role in breast cancer, identifying associations between microbial diversity, breast cancer, disease subtypes, and therapeutic interventions. The trend in diversity is consistent with pattens of microbial diversity observed in other diseases (such as colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular and neurological conditions), where loss of microbial diversity has been associated with disease etiology [78–83]. However, the studies in our review varied widely, with no two associating the same genus with identical outcomes using the same design or biospecimen types, precluding meta-analysis. This highlights the need for replication studies with comparable design and biospecimen and standardized outcomes to confirm findings and assess clinical relevance.

Despite this variability, several key microbial pathways emerged. Some studies inferred functional links between the microbiome and breast cancer using the PiCRUSt software package [38, 49, 51, 52, 58, 66–69, 72, 74, 84, 85], identifying enriched oncogenic pathways, lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis and steroid hormone biosynthesis, and reduced xenobiotic degradation, glutathione metabolism, and fatty acid biosynthesis in breast cancer patients [49, 68, 70, 72]. One study using shotgun sequencing, which maps microbial gene function more directly, confirmed similar findings [75]. Replication studies validating both microbial genera and their functions are essential for advancing clinical applications of microbiome research.

Some studies linked specific metabolites to microbial abundance, although few validated this through in vitro or in vivo experiments. For example, some Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus epidermidis strains, which are more common in breast cancer patients, were found to induce DNA damage in tissue cultures [17]. Future studies using in vitro and in vivo models could offer valuable insights into developing microbiome-based strategies for clinical management of breast cancer.

Recent research has extended beyond exploring the link between the microbiome and cancer etiology to investigate the microbiome’s functional interaction with cancer therapy. Studies involving patients undergoing chemotherapy have suggested enrichment of microbial pathways, including amino acid metabolism, short chain fatty acid (SCFA) biosynthesis and lipid biosynthesis [39, 41]. Interestingly, these pathways were also identified as down-regulated in treatment-naïve patients, highlighting potential parallels between microbiome alterations and chemotherapy and treatment-naïve states. Time-series studies of chemotherapy regimen could provide further insights into patient-specific microbiome alterations, which may inform personalized therapeutic strategies.

A few studies in our review analyzed microbial communities of oral, urine and vaginal samples from breast cancer patients. The oral microbiome of breast cancer patients showed lower alpha diversity and reduced relative abundances of Porphyromonas and Fusobacterium [18]. In the urinary microbiome, Lactobacillus was decreased and Corynebacterium, Staphylococcus, Actinomyces, and Propionibacteriaceae were increased in peri- or post-menopausal patients [19]. A study on the vaginal microbiome reported subtype-specific microbial profiles, including enriched anaerobic flora in luminal A and B subtypes, and higher abundance of facultative flora in Her2/Neu + subtypes [36]. These findings provide a basis for exploring microbiome changes in breast cancer through non-invasive sampling methods.

A number of studies also suggested associations between gut microbiota and quality of life outcomes such as sleep quality and postoperative pain, with specific bacteria associated with poor sleep and increased pain [14, 59]. Dietary fiber intake was correlated with gut bacteria linked to the β-glucuronidase activity, suggesting the critical need to consider quality of life outcomes, diet, and lifestyle in understanding the microbiome’s role in the pathophysiology of breast cancer.

Translating microbiome research into clinical practice presents several challenges, including the need for well-designed human studies with adequate sample size, standardized protocols, and robust statistical analyses. Our review reveals considerable heterogeneity in study design, sample types, research aims, outcomes, and findings, compounded by lack of adjustment for multiple comparisons in several studies. Differences in microbiome technologies such as 16 S and whole genome sequencing (WGS) also affect data quality and clinical viability. WGS is more robust but costly, while 16 S sequencing, though cost-effective, is sensitive to sample handling and storage methods. The computational methods used for analyzing microbiome data also varied in the studies reviewed, limiting reproducibility and translational potential.

Our review has several limitations. We did not conduct a meta-analysis due to inconsistencies in identifying the same genus in relation to comparable outcomes, study designs, biospecimen types, and samples across studies. Meta-analysis would have been feasible if more studies had provided summary data on relative abundance, effect estimates, standard deviation, and p-values; however, this information was often lacking in the main text or supplementary materials. Notably, over 60% of the studies did not make raw sequencing data publicly available, and even for those that did, heterogeneity in outcomes, participant demographics, biospecimens, sequencing methods, and 16 S gene regions amplified precluded meta-analysis. This issue of data sharing is widely acknowledged as a key obstacle in microbiome research, with the lack of standardization in data availability and methodology hindering reproducibility and meta-analysis efforts [84–88]. Despite these challenges, we identified the genera Fusobacterium and Bacteroides, which have been associated with cancer in other systematic reviews [22], although their associations in breast cancer were inconsistent (Supplementary Table 4). We did not assess age-related microbial variability due to demographic heterogeneity and limited menopausal data, underscoring the need for future microbiome research to consider these factors, along with controlling for diet in breast cancer patients [89].

In light of our review and identified challenges, we recommend that future microbiome studies prioritize larger sample sizes, standardized biospecimen types and collection protocols, clearly defined outcomes, and rigorous statistical methods. As emphasized in the literature, ensuring public availability of raw sequencing data is essential for enabling meta-analysis and strengthening the reliability of microbiome findings [90].

To mitigate confounding factors in future microbiome studies, it is essential to adopt rigorous standardization protocols across all stages of research. This includes harmonizing biospecimen collection procedures, ensuring that the choice of collection methods and storage conditions do not introduce biases [92]. Standardized DNA extraction methods are also crucial to improve comparability between studies and reduce variability in microbial profiles [93, 94]. Furthermore, controlling for key demographic variables such as age, sex, and menopausal status, and accounting for environmental factors like diet, can help mitigate these confounders, as highlighted in previous studies [89]. Implementing strict protocols for data analysis, including the adoption of uniform sequencing pipelines and bioinformatics tools, is essential for minimizing computational biases that may influence findings [86, 93]. These measures, when consistently applied, will improve the reproducibility of microbiome research and enhance its translational potential in clinical applications, including cancer management.

Conclusion

In summary, our review identified microbial features associated with breast cancer, but also highlighted significant heterogeneity across studies in participant demographics, biospecimen types, outcomes, and sequencing and analysis methods leading to inconsistent findings. This inconsistency underscores the need for future research to adopt standardized protocols for outcomes, study designs, biospecimens, and data analysis. Such rigorous efforts are essential to improve the reproducibility of microbiome-based strategies for clinical management of breast cancer. This challenge is not unique to breast cancer, but shared across microbiome research as a whole. To ensure robustness, a standardized approach across all aspects of microbiome research, including DNA extraction methods and sequencing pipelines, is crucial for minimizing biases and improving consistency in findings.
