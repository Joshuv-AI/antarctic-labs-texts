Field efficacy of insecticides for suppressing white mango scale insect (Aulacaspis tubercularis Newstead) (Hemiptera: Diaspididae) in southwest Ethiopia
Authors: Yassin Nurahmed Ebrahim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhite mango scale (WMS) Aulacaspis tubercularis Newstead (Hemiptera: Diaspididae) is a polyphagous armored scale insect which is considered one of the key pests of mango (Mangifera indica L.) around the world. Mango is widely grown in Ethiopia whereas its production is challenged by WMS in the last decade. Effective formulations that could help manage the scale as part of IPM practice were sought from field experiments at Seka mango farm, Ethiopia in 2019 and 2020 seasons. The study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of some formulations against WMS on mango trees. Randomized complete block designs with three replications were used for the experiments and each tree served as a plot. Allocation of each treatment within each replication was done randomly. The treatments were applied sequentially three times at 14 days interval using motorized Knapsack sprayer coinciding with peak period of natural infestation. Scale numbers before and after each spray were counted using a microscope with LCD. Sum of live crawler, female and male was registered as WMS count data. Results showed that dimethoate, diazinon, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin sprayed alone; dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifos-ethyl rotated with paraffin, and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin, caused total mortality of the scales. The results also showed that, chlorpyrifos-ethyl, deltamethrin, paraffin oil and λ-cyhalothrin sprayed alone caused percent reduction with range 83–95 % in both seasons. Hence, the study revealed that dimethoate, diazinon, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin applied individually, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifos-ethyl rotated with paraffin and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin fully protect mango trees from WMS and significantly superior to other treatments. Therefore, chemical control of A. tubercularis may consider the use of these materials as foliar application and can be used as components for integrated pest management plans for WMS. However, application in the form of rotation is preferred to the alone spray as the former could substantially reduce the likelihood of inducing pesticide resistance. Cost implications and effects of the products on the natural enemy and residual toxicity in fruits need to be studied.Keywords: Aulacaspis tubercularis, insecticide efficacy; Insecticide rotation; Foliar application; Mango pests

Introduction

Mango (Mangifera indica L.) is grown across the world [1]. Both domesticated and wild fruit species are grown in Sub-Saharan Africa farmers for its significance to health, ecological benefit and revenues [2]. In Ethiopia, mango is produced on about 8809 ha with a total annual production of about 6975.07 tons [3].

White Mango Scale (Aulacaspis tubercularis Newstead) insect is a serious threat to mango production in Ethiopia [4]. The insect became a key pest of mango since its introduction to Ethiopia in 2010 [5]. The insect has emerged to be the second most important mango pest after anthracnose disease [6]. It has spread to all mango producing areas quickly and become an economic pest in the country [7,8]. Threats by invasive pests result from loose quarantine system, globalization, human mobility, and climate change [9].

The white mango scale is known to lower productivity in mangos [10]. It has caused serious damage to mango production in many countries [11,12]. All the growth stages of mango crop from seedling to mature trees are attacked [4]. This insect causes severe damage to mango trees by sucking out plant sap as food resource leading to shriveling of leaves and branches, and impaired fruits; ultimately quality and quantity of yield are influenced [13]. It also releases phytotoxic substances while feeding [14]. If infestation occurs at the seedling stage, the whole plant can die; mature plants exhibit decreased fruit bearing, defoliation, poor blossoming and reduced juice content in fruit [12].

White mango scale pest spread so fast and is expected to be present throughout the year with high incidences at flowering and fruit maturation stages. Once present in a region, the pest spreads faster because of its small body size, which makes it easy to spread with fruits, seedlings, and even by the wind. This characteristic has made the pest to colonize most of the mango producing regions of Ethiopia. Indeed, most research studies in the country have focused around assessing the distribution and population dynamics [15,16] with some works around management practices [8].

Common control measures taken against the WMS include use of chemical insecticides, cultural practices and biological control using parasitoids and predators [8,12]. Efforts in the management of the pest has so far been limited to advising growers to prune and burn heavily infested branches, use of insecticides registered for the control of related armored scale and use of seedlings free from the pest for planting.

Being a new pest, there are no easily adaptable management practices prepared for farmers to use. Research on management strategies seemed to be few and slow despite the speed at which the pest is spreading and the potential losses it could cause to the already resource-poor farmers in Ethiopia. Once identified, the insecticides can serve as a short-term solution and any organic insecticides (mineral oil) that can be screened, in the long-term, can be integrated with other management options. Therefore, the present work dealt with the evaluation of insecticides in the form of alone or in rotation spray on mango trees to suppress white scale insects at field condition.

Materials and methods study area

The study was conducted at Seka Agro-processing private Ltd company mango orchard during 2019 and 2020. It is found at Guraferda district, southwest Ethiopia. Guraferda is located at 080 36″ N latitude and 400 19″ E longitudes with an altitude of around 1000 m above sea level, annual rainfall and mean temperature varies from 1200 to 2400 mm and 16.8oc to 29.1oc respectively.

Insecticides and their application rate.

The mango trees were 11 years old ‘Tommy Atkins’ variety and were uniform in shape and size. The experimental trees were planted at 8 m × 8m spacing. The height of mango trees on average was 4.3m and had manageable size to apply agricultural practices. Each tree served as a plot. The experiment was laid out in Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with three replications. Each plot was separated from the adjacent plot by a row of mango trees as a barrier. Allocation of each treatment within each replication was done randomly.

Before applying formulations, the volume of water required to fully wet the mango trees was calibrated. Twenty liters of water per tree was found to be sufficient for foliar application. Pesticides were dissolved in water to prepare insecticide solutions on volume by volume (v/v) basis. Then, the aqueous solution was thoroughly mixed to ensure homogeneity. Treatments were applied either alone or in rotation form for three consecutive rounds at fortnight intervals starting from March 9th/2019 and February 21st/2020, coinciding with the period of peak natural infestation, for season one and season two respectively. A motorized backpack sprayer of 20 L capacity was used to apply treatments. Spraying was effected with the spray man being supported by ladder for ease of accessing all parts of each mango tree leaves. The control plots were not sprayed.

Any change in color and texture of leaves due to probable phytotoxicity of the insecticides and plant mortality, due to the tested pesticides, were recorded. The visual scale of phytotoxicity proposed by Ref. [22] was used to rate the phytotoxic effects of insecticides, with 0: plants with normal leaves and no signs of burns; 1: plants with slightly damaged leaves and/or with small burned areas; 2: plants with medium damaged leaves, yellow with burnt edges and tips; 3: plants with heavily damaged leaves, showing severe defoliation.

Sampling procedure

Twelve leaves were randomly picked from each of the four cardinal directions from the upper, middle and lower canopy of the trees before each treatment application. The collected samples were placed in labeled polyethylene bags pierced for aeration and transported to Mizan plant health clinic laboratory for further examination. The number of live scales was counted and the average populations that served as pre-spray count for the first treatment application were recorded. The first spray was applied to all experimental units and post spray counts were made after two weeks. Then, the second and the third sprays followed at fortnight intervals. Live crawler; male and female WMS were counted, on the upper and lower leaf surfaces, by using a microscope with LCD screen.

Data analysis

Sum of live crawler, female and male A. tubercularis was registered as WMS count data. The first instar was recorded as crawler while female and immature male stages were also identified and recorded. In this study, it was impossible to count adult live male scales, as they fly out immediately upon achieving maturity. Accordingly, male mango scale armors were broken open by the use of dissecting needle to avoid counting empty scales. Furthermore, the needle was used to lift the armor of the adult female for ease of counting crawlers underneath.

In season 2019, after the second spraying, diazinon, dimethoate, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, diazinon rotated with azadirachtin and chlorpyrifos rotated with paraffin gave lowest WMS with range 0.0–23, whereas chlorpyryphos-ethyl and λ-cyhalothrin registered 53 and 98 WMS respectively. In the two successive experimental seasons, 2019 and 2020, post third spraying, naught was recorded on leaves treated with diazinon, dimethoate, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifos-ethyl with paraffin, and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin.

Therefore, in both trial seasons after the third spray diazinon, dimethoate, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifos-ethyl with paraffin, and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin were the most effective compounds in management of white mango scales. Moreover, Chlorpyrifos-ethyl and paraffin oil were highly promising materials to protect mango trees from the insect pest.

The results obtained in the study demonstrated a significant reduction in the number of WMS in treated trees. The percentages of the scales reduction on mango leaves varied among the tested materials in season 2019. The most obvious reduction was observed after the treatment with diazinon, dimethoate, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifosethyl rotated with paraffin and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin as they resulted in 100 % mortality followed by chlorpyrifos-ethyl, and λ-cyhalothrin above 90 % mortality post the third spray.

In the second season 2020, the same effective trend was noticed on mango leaves treated with diazinon, dimethoate, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifos-ethyl rotated with paraffin, and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin where they caused total mortality after the third treatment application followed by chlorpyrifos-ethyl and λ-cyhalothrin above 90 % mortality.

Therefore, diazinon, dimethoate, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin sprayed individually, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifos-ethyl rotated with paraffin, and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin were found to be most effective formulations to control WMS on mango trees. Furthermore, chlorpyrifos-ethyl and λ-cyhalothrin were also found superior over control and other treatments in both experimental seasons.

Discussion

Chemical control is necessary when crops are heavily infested with a pest, especially in the case of production of fruits for export [27]. Chemical option is important to offer instant solution to enable production of blemish-free fruits. The uses of insecticides (organophosphates, pyrethroids and neonicotinoids) have been shown to reduce A. tubercularis populations on mango trees [28,29]. Two large scale experiments were conducted in seasons (2019 and 2020) to evaluate the efficacy of some insecticides against A.tubercularis infested mango trees. The current study has shown that the use of insecticides as foliar application has been effective and promising to safeguard mango trees from WMS.

The results from the field experiment (Fig. 1, Fig. 2) demonstrated that diazinon, dimethoate, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifos-ethyl rotated with paraffin, and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin resulted in superb control effect on the scale pests. In addition, chlorpyrifos-ethyl, λ-cyhalothrin and paraffin oil were found promising to suppress the mango scale. According to Ref. [30] chlorpyrifos-ethyl was the most effective treatment against mango soft scale insect, Kilifia acuminate. This is in agreement with the present finding with respect to efficacy of chlorpyrifos-ethyl. Likewise, chlorpyrifos had highest toxic effect against white peach scale, Pseudaulacaspis pentagona. Furthermore [31], rated the toxicities of insecticides in following order chlorpyrifos > diazinon > mineral oil and are in support with the present findings with respect to the effectiveness of chlorpyrifos, diazinon and mineral oil.

In this study, the efficacy of paraffin oil against WMS was promising especially when rotated with chlorpyrifos. Medopaz (80 % paraffin oil) is the only closely related product to paraffin oil registered in Ethiopia for the control of Red, Orange, Purple, and Black scale on citrus [20]. As paraffin oil belongs to a category of mineral oils, there are no reported cases of resistance and that was associated with their mode of action, hypoxia [32], and/or their relatively low selection pressure against pests [33]. Paraffin oil, therefore, holds the potential to be used in rotation with chlorpyrifos-ethyl or other pesticides against WMS to safeguard mango trees. Field studies have shown the effectiveness of a range of mineral oils combinations. In addition [12], reported the effectiveness of mineral oils against A. tubercularis on mango trees by the following order: Diver > CAPL2® > super masrona. Similarly [34], reported that KZ-oil + Admiral and Super Misrona + Actilic were useful compounds to combat the red scale, Aonidiella aurantii, on mandarin trees. As well [35], reported up to 98 % mortality of white mango scale when treated with mineral oil. Mineral oils kill the scale insects by smothering them [36]. Thus, the reduction of the insect scale by mineral oils is recommended as a fundamental component for the integrated management of this pest [37]. In the present study imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin sprayed in the form of alone, and rotated with dimethoate gave excellent control of WMS. In this regard [29], reported that acephate and imidacloprid were effective for the management of the scale insects in mango trees. Dimethoate decimated A. tubercularis either sprayed alone or when rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin. In agreement with this result, scales were effectively controlled by the application of dimethoate [38,39]. Similarly, diazinon and Dimethoate were found to reduce A. tubercularis infestation and damage [15].

Azadirachtin after the third spray in both seasons gave 100 % mortality when rotated with diazinon but showed lesser efficacies when applied alone. In agreement with this result [40], reported that star oil (Mineral oil 95 % EC) in combination with azadirachtin gave highest percent reduction values against purple scale insect, Lepidosaphes bekii. Likewise [29], confirmed that applying neem oil alone was not effective for the management of scale insects including A. tubercularis. Similarly, this study indicated that rotating azadirachtin with diazinon at biweekly interval for three consecutive rounds could effectively control A. tubercularis. Disparity in efficacy between λ-cyhalothrin and imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin might be due to synergistic effects because a separate treatment with λ-cyhalothrin proved relatively less impact against the scale insect. The mango scales were effectively controlled with the spraying of dimethoate, chlorpyriphos and neem oil (azadirachtin) [38].

Hence, the current study has shown that trees treated with, diazinon, dimethoate, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifos-ethyl rotated with paraffin and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin, did not suffer from white mango scale infestation and damage. These materials are highly promising to protect mango trees from WMS and significantly superior to other treatments. Therefore, chemical control of A. tubercularis may consider the use of these field proven formulations as foliar application and can be used as components for integrated pest management plans for white mango scale. Increased dose and frequency of pesticide application might lead to the development of resistance. Due to this fact, it is recommended to rotate between active ingredients from different mode of action or chemical groups. This will reduce the likelihood of inducing pesticide resistance substantially [17]. For that reason, application in the form of rotation is preferred to the alone spray as the former could considerably reduce the possibility of pest resistance development.

Conclusion

Despite being distributed worldwide, the number of studies on A. tubercularis management is limited. The occurrence of this exotic species in Ethiopia since 2010 has significantly affected the mango crop, constituting a serious concern for farmers. Effective formulations that could help manage the scale pest as part of IPM practice were sought from field experiments. The studies aimed to evaluate the efficacy of some foliar applied formulations against WMS on established trees. The application of insecticides appears to offer protection to mango crop against the pest. From the present study, it was found that application, dimethoate, diazinon, imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, dimethoate rotated with imidacloprid & λ-cyhalothrin, chlorpyrifosethyl rotated with paraffin, and diazinon rotated with azadirachtin, were highly effective and considerably increased A. tubercularis mortality on mango trees. The results also suggested that trees treated with chlorpyrifos-ethyl, λ-cyhalothrin, and paraffin oil suffered less from white mango scale infestation and damage. Therefore, chemical control of WMS may consider the use of these field proven effective pesticides as foliar application. No phytotoxic effects were displayed by these pesticides. However, it is important to choose the rotational spray over the alone form as the former could substantially reduce pesticide resistance development by pests. Cost implications, effects of the insecticides on the natural enemy complex, residue in fruits, and integration with other management components are open for future study.
