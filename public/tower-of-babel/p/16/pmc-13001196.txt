Earlier fracture union and earlier weight-bearing: arthroscopy-assisted versus open reduction with percutaneous K-wire fixation for juvenile Tillaux fractures
Authors: Fengyong Mao, Lei Ni, Li Ju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare arthroscopy-assisted closed reduction with percutaneous K-wire fixation (AS) versus open reduction with percutaneous K-wire fixation (OR) for juvenile Tillaux fracturesMethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 22 adolescents with displaced Tillaux fractures treated at our institution between 2017 and 2021 (AS, n=8; OR, n=14). Operative time, time to radiographic union, time to full weight-bearing, and American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle–hindfoot scores at 3 and 6 months were compared.ResultsBaseline demographics and preoperative displacement were comparable between groups. Operative time was longer in the AS group. The AS group had shorter time to radiographic union and earlier progression to full weight-bearing, and higher AOFAS scores at 3 months; by 6 months, AOFAS scores were similarly excellent in both groups. No major complications were observed in either group.ConclusionIn this small retrospective cohort, arthroscopy-assisted reduction was associated with earlier radiographic union and earlier weight-bearing compared with open reduction using the same fixation method, with similarly excellent mid-term functional outcomes. Larger prospective studies with pediatric-validated outcome measures and longer follow-up are warranted.

Introduction

Juvenile Tillaux fractures are unique transitional injuries of the distal tibia that occur predominantly in adolescents during the period of asymmetrical physeal closure [1–3]. The fracture results from external rotation forces causing an avulsion of the anterolateral distal tibial epiphysis—the last portion of the physis to fuse [4]. Because the fracture involves a major weight-bearing articular surface, anatomic reduction is paramount. Residual articular step-off of greater than 2 mm is significantly associated with the development of premature post-traumatic osteoarthritis, chronic pain, and joint stiffness [5–8]. Therefore, surgical intervention is strictly indicated for displaced fractures to restore articular congruity.

Traditionally, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) has been the gold standard. While effective, the open approach requires extensive soft-tissue dissection and, paradoxically, may offer limited visualization of the posterior aspect of the fracture line [9, 10]. In recent years, arthroscopy-assisted reduction has emerged as a valuable alternative. This technique offers the dual advantages of minimizing soft-tissue trauma and providing direct, magnified visualization of the articular surface, allowing surgeons to identify and treat concomitant intra-articular pathologies—such as chondral loose bodies or entrapped periosteum—that might be missed during open surgery [11–13].

A distinct point of debate in treating these fractures concerns the fixation implant: cannulated screws versus Kirschner wires (K-wires). While screws are biomechanically strong, they often necessitate a second surgical procedure under general anesthesia for removal, or risk becoming buried by bone overgrowth in rapid-healing adolescents. In contrast, percutaneous K-wire fixation offers a practical advantage for this age group: it provides sufficient stability for the transitional epiphysis and can be removed in an outpatient setting without anesthesia, thereby eliminating the morbidity and costs associated with a second operation [14].

Currently, the literature regarding arthroscopic management of Tillaux fractures consists primarily of case reports. There is a paucity of comparative studies evaluating the clinical efficacy and safety of arthroscopy-assisted reduction versus traditional open reduction when the fixation method is controlled. Therefore, the purpose of this retrospective study was to compare the clinical and radiographic outcomes of arthroscopy-assisted closed reduction versus open reduction, both utilizing percutaneous K-wire fixation, for the treatment of adolescent Tillaux fractures. We hypothesized that the arthroscopic approach would achieve comparable union rates while reducing operative morbidity and improving early functional outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single pediatric medical center from January 2017 to December 2021. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Nanjing Children’s Hospital (Approval No. [202502007-1], approved on 26 January 2025). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We reviewed the medical records of pediatric patients aged 13 to 17 years who sustained a displaced Tillaux fracture. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Closed, isolated Tillaux fracture (Salter-Harris type III) confirmed by radiographs and computed tomography (CT); (2) Fracture displacement > 2 mm requiring surgical reduction; (3) Treatment via either arthroscopy-assisted reduction (AS group) or open reduction (OR group) with percutaneous Kirschner wire (K-wire) fixation; (4) Minimum follow-up of 12 months. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Open fractures or severe soft tissue injury; (2) Concomitant ipsilateral lower limb fractures (e.g., triplane fractures, tibial shaft fractures); (3) Pathological fractures or metabolic bone diseases; (4) Incomplete clinical or radiographic data.

Treatment allocation (AS vs. OR)

The choice between arthroscopy-assisted reduction (AS) and open reduction (OR) was not randomized. After confirming fracture displacement > 2 mm on CT, the operating surgeon discussed both approaches with the patient and guardians, covering expected benefits, limitations, and the specific requirements for arthroscopic intervention. The allocation was primarily influenced by the availability of arthroscopic equipment and trained ancillary staff rather than surgeon preference or patient characteristics. All procedures were performed by the same senior pediatric orthopaedic surgeon who had already completed formal arthroscopic training. The AS approach was selected when the arthroscopic tower, camera system, and trained scrub nurse were available. Open reduction was performed primarily during the earlier phase of the study when arthroscopic equipment was unavailable due to limited resources, or when the family preferred the open approach after informed discussion. All eligible patients were included consecutively, and baseline comparisons (Table 1) suggest no significant differences in injury severity between the groups.

Radiographic assessment

All patients underwent preoperative anteroposterior (AP), lateral, and mortise radiographs followed by a CT scan. Initial fracture displacement was measured on the axial CT slice demonstrating the widest fracture gap using digital calipers in the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Postoperative reduction quality was assessed on immediate postoperative Mortise and lateral radiographs. Routine postoperative CT was not performed to minimize radiation exposure in adolescents (ALARA principle); therefore, very small residual articular step-off may be underestimated on plain radiographs. Radiographic union was defined as disappearance/blurring of the fracture line with trabecular bridging across the fracture site on Mortise and lateral radiographs. Clinical union was defined as the absence of local tenderness and pain during ankle motion and progressive weight-bearing. To reduce assessment bias, radiographs used for study measurements were anonymized and independently reviewed by two investigators who were blinded to surgical approach; disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by the same senior pediatric orthopaedic surgeon specializing in arthroscopy. Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned supine with a pneumatic tourniquet applied to the thigh.

Group AS (Arthroscopy-Assisted Reduction): Standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals were established. A 3.5-mm arthroscope was introduced through the anteromedial portal for visualization. Step 1 (Debridement): The fracture site was inspected. Hematoma and, crucially, any entrapped periosteum or soft tissue within the fracture gap were meticulously debrided using an arthroscopic shaver or probe. This step is essential to remove mechanical blocks to reduction. Step 2 (Reduction): Reduction was achieved by internally rotating the foot combined with direct manipulation of the avulsed fragment using a probe introduced through the anterolateral portal. Anatomical reduction of the articular surface (step-off < 1 mm) was confirmed under direct arthroscopic visualization. Step 3 (Fixation): Two 1.5-mm or 2.0-mm smooth K-wires were inserted percutaneously under fluoroscopic guidance in a divergent (crossed) configuration to improve rotational stability. The first wire was advanced from the anterolateral fragment toward the posteromedial tibial metaphysis, and the second wire was directed more proximally with a different trajectory in the sagittal/coronal plane, achieving bicortical purchase without intra-articular penetration. Wire positions and fragment stability were confirmed on anteroposterior, lateral, and mortise fluoroscopic views. The wires were bent outside the skin to facilitate removal.

Group OR (Open Reduction): A 4–5 cm longitudinal incision was made over the anterolateral aspect of the distal tibia. The extensor retinaculum was incised, and the fracture site was exposed. The interposed periosteum was removed manually, and the fracture was reduced under direct vision using a reduction clamp. Fixation was performed using two divergent (crossed) smooth K-wires (1.5–2.0 mm) under fluoroscopic guidance, following the same principles described for the AS group.

Postoperative management

Postoperatively, the limb was immobilized in a short-leg non-weight-bearing cast. Patients were instructed to remain strictly non-weight-bearing. Follow-up radiographs were routinely obtained at 4 weeks (and repeated at 6 weeks if union was not yet confirmed). To minimize radiation exposure in adolescents (ALARA principle), additional radiographs at later follow-up visits were performed only when clinically indicated (e.g., persistent pain, swelling, or limited motion). Upon radiographic confirmation of callus formation (typically 4–6 weeks post-surgery), the cast and K-wires were removed in the outpatient clinic. Following hardware removal, patients were instructed to initiate partial weight-bearing with crutches/walker, progressing to full weight-bearing as tolerated over the subsequent two weeks.

Outcome evaluation

Primary outcome was time to radiographic union. Secondary outcomes were time to full weight-bearing, American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle–hindfoot scores at 3 and 6 months postoperatively, operative time, and complications (infection, neurovascular injury, malunion). Clinical outcomes were assessed using the AOFAS ankle–hindfoot score at 3 and 6 months postoperatively, as this score is routinely documented in our clinic records. Cosmetic outcome was evaluated descriptively by incision length/number of incisions and patient/parent satisfaction recorded during follow-up; a validated scar assessment scale (e.g., POSAS) was not applied in this retrospective series.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test; non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. In addition to P values, we calculated mean differences between groups with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for continuous outcomes. For binary outcomes, relative risks (RRs) with 95% CIs were planned; however, when no events occurred in both groups, RR estimation was not applicable. A post hoc power analysis was performed for the primary outcome (time to radiographic union) using the observed effect size (two-sided α = 0.05); the achieved power for detecting the observed difference in time to radiographic union was 0.999 (|Cohen’s d|=2.36). With the current sample sizes (n = 8 and n = 14), the minimum detectable standardized difference at 80% power was approximately Cohen’s d = 1.31. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics and baseline characteristics

A total of 22 adolescents with displaced Tillaux fractures met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The cohort consisted of 8 patients in the arthroscopy-assisted (AS) group and 14 patients in the open reduction (OR) group.

The demographic data and injury characteristics are summarized in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups regarding age (P = 0.446), sex distribution (P = 1.000), side of injury (P = 0.378), or initial fracture displacement (P = 0.572). The homogeneity of these baseline parameters confirms that the two cohorts were comparable prior to surgical intervention.

Perioperative and radiographic outcomes

All surgical procedures were successfully completed without conversion to open surgery in the AS group. Adequate reduction (no residual displacement > 2 mm on immediate postoperative radiographs) was achieved in all cases in both groups. Detailed perioperative data are presented in Table 2. The mean operative time was longer in the AS group (45.3 ± 4.7 min) than in the OR group (34.6 ± 2.3 min; P < 0.001), with a mean difference (AS–OR) of 10.7 min (95% CI 6.7–14.7).

Clinical functional outcomes

Functional recovery was assessed using the AOFAS ankle–hindfoot score (Table 2). At the 3-month follow-up, the AS group had higher AOFAS scores than the OR group (88.1 ± 2.7 vs. 78.4 ± 2.6; P < 0.001), with a mean difference (AS–OR) of 9.7 points (95% CI 7.2–12.2), indicating faster early functional recovery. However, this functional advantage plateaued over time. By the 6-month follow-up, both groups achieved excellent outcomes with no statistically significant difference in AOFAS scores (99.1 ± 1.1 vs. 99.2 ± 1.0; P = 0.854), with a mean difference (AS–OR) of − 0.1 points (95% CI − 1.1 to 0.9).

Complications and cosmesis

No major complications, such as deep infection, neurovascular injury, implant failure, or malunion, occurred in either group. Because no complications were observed in both groups, relative risk estimates were not applicable. No signs of premature physeal closure or angular deformity were observed during the follow-up period.

Regarding wound healing, the AS group required two small portal incisions (approximately 5 mm each) in addition to the K-wire pin sites, whereas the OR group required a 4–5 cm anterolateral incision. Because a validated scar assessment tool (e.g., POSAS) was not used, cosmetic outcomes are reported descriptively rather than as an objective comparison.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of surgically treated juvenile Tillaux fractures, both arthroscopy-assisted closed reduction (AS) and open reduction (OR)—each utilizing percutaneous K-wire fixation—achieved anatomic reduction and excellent mid-term functional outcomes. The most significant finding of our study was that the AS approach, despite requiring longer operative time, significantly accelerated the healing process, allowing for earlier radiographic union and faster progression to full weight-bearing. While the final functional outcomes at 6 months were equivalent, the early advantages in the AS group suggest a meaningful reduction in the burden of recovery for adolescent patients.

The AS group had longer operative times (45.3 min vs. 34.6 min), a finding consistent with existing literature and attributable to the additional setup and intraoperative positioning required for arthroscopy [9, 10]. However, this time investment appears justified by the biological benefits. Our finding of faster union in the AS group aligns with the concept that arthroscopic visualization allows for superior “biological reduction”. As noted by Leetun and Ireland and Panagopoulos et al [11, 13], Tillaux fractures frequently involve periosteum or soft-tissue interposition that hinders reduction. Direct, magnified arthroscopic views enable thorough debridement and evacuation of intra-articular hematoma—steps that are often incomplete in mini-open approaches. We postulate that this thorough “joint cleanup” combined with minimal soft-tissue stripping, creates an optimized intra-articular environment that accelerates trabecular bridging.

Regarding fixation, we utilized a divergent (crossed) K-wire configuration for both groups to maximize rotational stability. While valid concerns exist regarding the lower biomechanical strength of K-wires compared to screws [2], our study achieved a 100% union rate without secondary displacement. This supports the premise that in the rapid-healing adolescent population, divergent K-wire fixation provides sufficient stability. Crucially, K-wires offer a distinct advantage over screws: they eliminate the need for a second operation under general anesthesia for hardware removal [14]. Avoiding a second surgery not only reduces total healthcare costs but also alleviates the psychological burden on pediatric patients and their families, a factor that should be weighed heavily in treatment decision-making.

The clinical implication of earlier weight-bearing in the AS group is particularly relevant for the adolescent demographic. Earlier mobilization is known to reduce the risk of disuse atrophy and potentially facilitate a faster return to school and social activities, which are critical for patient quality of life [12]. Although our mid-term scores (6 months) equalized, the early clinical advantage in the first 3 months validates the arthroscopic approach as a method to minimize the disruption of the injury on the patient’s life.

Limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the design was retrospective and non-randomized, introducing potential selection bias, although baseline characteristics were comparable. Second, the sample size (n = 22) was small due to the rarity of this injury. However, a post-hoc power analysis of our primary outcome (time to union) revealed a power of > 0.99, driven by the large effect size (Cohen’s d = 2.36), suggesting that our findings regarding accelerated healing are statistically robust despite the limited cohort. Third, while AOFAS scores are standard, they are not pediatric-validated; future studies should incorporate instruments like the OxAFQ-C. Finally, routine CT was not used for postoperative assessment to adhere to ALARA principles, potentially missing subtle incongruities.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study, arthroscopy-assisted closed reduction demonstrated a shorter time to radiographic union and earlier progression to full weight-bearing compared with open reduction, despite utilizing the same percutaneous K-wire fixation. While both techniques achieved excellent mid-term functional outcomes without major complications, the accelerated early recovery observed in the arthroscopic group suggests it is a valuable surgical alternative for minimizing the burden of injury in adolescents. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to further evaluate long-term joint survival and cost-effectiveness.
