Joint effects of triglyceride glucose index and its obesity-related derivatives with estimated glucose disposal rate on cardiometabolic multimorbidity in middle-aged and older Chinese adults: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Xianlin Ruan, Yihang Ling, Jinming Chen, Yulong Xiang, Huangtao Ruan, Weipeng Zhang, Ling Jing, Xin Gao, Yibo He, Xiaozhao Lu, Tian Chang, Junyan Xu, Jiyan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-waist circumference (TyG-WC), TyG-waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR), and estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) serve as surrogate markers of insulin resistance (IR) and are associated with cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs). However, the joint effects of TyG-related indices and eGDR on cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) risk remains unclear. This study aims to assess both separate and combined effects of TyG-related indices and eGDR on CMM.MethodsThe data of this study derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). TyG-related indices and eGDR were dichotomized at their median levels for participant categorization. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression and restricted cubic splines (RCS) analyzed effects of TyG-related indices and eGDR on CMM, while receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) assessed their predictive performance. Meanwhile, the mutual mediating effects and interaction effects were further assessed.ResultsThis study included 5,854 participants (male: 47.5%, median age: 57.0 years). Compared to low TyG-related indices plus high eGDR, High TyG-related indices plus low eGDR had elevated CMM risks: TyG (HR 3.59, 95% CI 2.28–5.65), TyG-BMI (HR 3.40, 95% CI 2.30–5.02), TyG-WC (HR 3.85, 95% CI 2.58–5.75), and TyG-WHtR (HR 3.62, 95% CI 2.43–5.39). Furthermore, the addition of TyG-related indices combined with eGDR to the basic model significantly improved CMM risk prediction: TyG (AUC 0.713, NRI 0.363, IDI 0.008, all p < 0.05); TyG-BMI (AUC 0.729, NRI 0.479, IDI 0.011, all p < 0.05); TyG-WC (AUC 0.716, NRI 0.419, IDI 0.010, all p < 0.05); and TyG-WHtR (AUC 0.717, NRI 0.379, IDI 0.010, all p < 0.05). Moreover, the mediation analysis demonstrated that eGDR significantly mediated all TyG-related indices’ associations with CMM, with only obesity-related TyG indices mediating the association between eGDR and CMM. Notably, no significant additive or multiplicative interaction was observed between any TyG-related indices and eGDR for CMM risk.ConclusionsHigh TyG-related indices and low eGDR were independently and jointly associated with higher CMM risk. Introduction

Multimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions in an individual, represents a growing global health challenge that leads to reduced quality of life, increased healthcare burden, and premature mortality [1]. Particularly, cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), characterized by the co-occurrence of at least two cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) including heart disease, stroke, or diabetes, has become increasingly prevalent in aging societies. A study of over one million Chinese adults (2010–2016) revealed that CMM prevalence more than doubled from 2.4% to 5.9% [2]. Moreover, large cohort data demonstrated CMM carried substantially higher mortality than single CMDs, with double the risk for two conditions and nearly fourfold risk for three conditions [3]. However, current evidence predominantly focused on CMDs, neglecting the complex nature of CMM. Early prediction and intervention are thus critical to address its escalating health burden.

Insulin resistance (IR), featured as diminished insulin sensitivity in target tissues with consequent impaired glucose uptake and persistent hyperglycemia, is a key pathophysiological driver of cardiometabolic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity [4, 5]. While the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HEC) remains the gold standard for assessing IR [6], its clinical utility is limited by invasive procedures, technical complexity and substantial costs. To address this, surrogate markers such as the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) have emerged as practical alternatives. The TyG index, derived from fasting triglycerides and glucose, is a widely validated IR indicator [7]. Previous investigations have established significant associations between the TyG index and the development of ischemic heart disease [8], heart failure [9], stroke [10], and diabetes mellitus [11]. More recently, its obesity-related derivatives including TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-waist circumference (TyG-WC) and TyG-waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR), have been developed as more robust tools for cardiometabolic risk evaluation [12, 13]. Similarly, eGDR, calculated using waist circumference, hypertension status, and HbA1c, reflected insulin sensitivity and predicted cardiovascular outcomes [14–16]. Although IR indices were well-studied for CMD, their separate and combined role in CMM remains unclear.

Therefore, this study examined both the independent and combined associations of TyG-related indices and eGDR with CMM risk, along with their reciprocal mediating effects and interaction effects.

Method

Study design and populations

The study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort of adults aged 45 years and older in China. Launched in 2011, the project employed a multistage stratified probability-proportionate-to-size sampling method to recruit 17,708 baseline participants from 450 villages and urban communities across 28 provinces, with follow-up surveys conducted every two or three years. Data collection included standardized questionnaires, physical examinations, and biochemical analyses of blood samples. Ethical approval was obtained from Peking University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052–11015), with written informed consent provided by all participants.

The inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study were detailed in Fig. S1. First, participants lacking age data (n = 176) or younger than 45 years (n = 602) was excluded. Next, those with baseline disease or missing data for heart disease (n = 2,518), stroke (n = 392), and diabetes (n = 1,811) were removed. Finally, participants with incomplete measurements of fasting plasma glucose (FBG), triglyceride (TG), body mass index (BMI), glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), hypertension (n = 6,355) were eliminated. After applying all exclusion criteria, 5,854 participants were available for the final analysis.

Assessment of TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR and eGDR

Venous blood samples were almost collected after overnight fasting. The measurement of TG, FPG and HbA1c utilized enzymatic colorimetric assays, with the coefficient of variation less than 2%. Height and weight were measured using a vertical stadiometer and a weight scale, respectively. Waist circumference (WC) was measured horizontally around the umbilicus using a flexible measuring tape. The formulas for calculating TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR and eGDR were as follows:TyG = ln[TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2];TyG-BMI = TyG × BMI, and BMI = weight (kg)/height2 (m2);TyG-WC = TyG × WC (cm);TyG-WHtR = TyG × WHtR, and WHtR = WC (cm)/height (cm);eGDR = 21.158—(0.09 × WC (cm))—(3.407 × hypertension (yes = 1/no = 0))—(0.551 × HbA1c (%)).

Outcome

In this study, the outcome was CMM events, defined as the coexistence of two or more CMDs, including heart disease, stroke, or diabetes [17, 18]. Heart disease and stroke diagnosis relied on self-reported physician diagnoses using standardized questions: “Have you been diagnosed with any heart disease [myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems]/stroke by a doctor?”, along with medication use information. Diabetes diagnosis incorporated biochemical measures (FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%), current use of hypoglycemic medications, or self-reported physician diagnoses (“Have you been diagnosed with diabetes or high blood sugar by a doctor?”). The diagnosis of CMM was established when a second CMD was clinically confirmed [3, 19]. The CMM onset period was defined as the interval between the last interview and the one in which the CMM was first recorded [15, 20].

Covariates

In this study, the following covariates were collected. Sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex, education, and marital status. Lifestyle behaviors encompassed smoking status and drinking status. Physical measurements covered WC, BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Clinical histories incorporated hypertension, dyslipidemia, cancer, lung diseases, and liver diseases. Laboratory markers comprised FBG, TG, serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), C-reactive protein (CRP), glycated HbA1c, uric acid (UA), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Specially, education was categorized into three groups: below high school, high school, and college or above. Marital status was grouped as: married or partnered and other statuses. The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [21] was used to calculate eGFR. Hypertension criteria included: SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, self-reported diagnosis, or antihypertensive medication use. Other diseases were determined by self-report.

Statistical analyses

The extent of missing data was described in Table S2. To handle missing data, a multiple imputation approach using chained equations with 5 imputations was implemented. Continuous variables, all exhibiting non-normal distributions based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, were summarized as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for comparisons of two groups or the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons of three or more groups, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed with chi-square tests. Given the absence of established clinical thresholds for TyG-related indices and eGDR, we categorized participants using median values. Participants were then stratified into four groups based on combined levels: (1) low TyG-related indices and high eGDR, (2) high TyG-related indices and high eGDR, (3) low TyG-related indices and low eGDR, and (4) high TyG-related indices and low eGDR.

Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to assess the cumulative incidence of CMM, and the log-rank test was applied to compare differences across groups. The association of TyG-related indices and eGDR with CMM events were analyzed through univariate and multivariate Cox regression models with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. The proportional hazards assumption was confirmed by Schoenfeld residuals. Additionally, multicollinearity diagnostics (Tables S3–S11) revealed all generalized variance inflation factors (GVIFs) were below 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity among covariates. Four Cox models were applied. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 was further adjusted for education, marital status, smoking status, drinking status. Model 4 was additionally adjusted for SBP, BUN, Scr, LDL-C, CRP. To explore potential nonlinear relationships between TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, eGDR and CMM, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was employed.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess predictive performance of TyG-related indices and eGDR for CMM risk, both individually and in combination, quantified by area under the curve (AUC) values. To estimate the additional predictive power, the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated.

The causal mediation analysis was conducted using the “mediation” package with survival analysis via the “survreg” method to estimate both indirect and direct effects. Each TyG-related index (dichotomized at median) served as exposure variables (X), eGDR (dichotomized at median) as mediator (M), and CMM as outcome (Y). Reciprocally, the analysis evaluated eGDR as exposure (X) mediated through each TyG-related index (M) to predict CMM (Y).

Additive and multiplicative interactions between eGDR and TyG-related indices were evaluated by incorporating their product term in the model. Since preventive factors should not be used to calculate measures of interaction on an additive scale without recoding [22], high eGDR was set as the reference group to meet risk-factor assumptions. Multiplicative interaction was evaluated through the HR (95% CI) of the product term in regression models. For additive interaction assessment, three indices were calculated: relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (SI). Values of RERI > 0, AP > 0, and SI > 1 indicated synergistic effects, while RERI < 0, AP < 0, and SI < 1 suggested antagonistic effects. All three indices were computed with 95% CIs using the delta method.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the association between TyG-related indices with eGDR and CMM across age (< 60 and ≥ 60 years), sex, smoking status, and drinking status. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding all missing data (n = 5,708), recomputing eGDR based on the revised hypertension criteria (130/80 mmHg) (n = 5,854), and removing participants with less than 2 years follow-up (n = 5,692).

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1), with a two-sided P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

The baseline characteristics of study participants stratified by CMM status were displayed in Table 1. Correlation analysis among TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR and eGDR

The Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant associations (all p < 0.001) among TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR and eGDR (Fig. S2). The eGDR demonstrated moderate inverse correlations with TyG-WC (r = − 0.63), TyG-WHtR (r = − 0.62), and TyG-BMI (r = − 0.45), with the weakest association observed for TyG (r = − 0.23). Among TyG-related indices, TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR demonstrated exceptionally strong correlation (r = 0.95), while their correlations with TyG-BMI were moderately strong (TyG-WC: r = 0.70; TyG-WHtR: r = 0.69). Furthermore, TyG showed intermediate correlations with its modified indices (TyG-BMI: r = 0.57; TyG-WC: r = 0.57; TyG-WHtR: r = 0.56).

Associations of TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR and eGDR with incidence CMM

First, each TyG-related index and eGDR were analyzed independently for CMM risk associations. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed significantly higher cumulative incidence of CMM in participants with high TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, or low eGDR (all log-rank P < 0.001) (Fig. S3). These findings were corroborated by Cox regression models. Discussion

In this nationwide longitudinal study of 5,854 middle-aged and older Chinese adults, high TyG-related indices and low eGDR were independently and jointly associated with higher CMM risk. Furthermore, ROC analysis demonstrated that the addition of TyG-related indices to the basic model significantly improved CMM risk prediction, with further enhancement when combined with eGDR, as similarly evidenced by NRI and IDI metrics. Interestingly, mediation analysis revealed that eGDR universally mediated associations between TyG-related indices and CMM, whereas only obesity-related TyG indices significantly mediated the eGDR-CMM relationship. Notably, no significant additive or multiplicative effects were observed between any TyG-related indices and eGDR for CMM risk. Overall, these findings highlighted the clinical value of multiple insulin resistance markers. Joint application of TyG-related indices and eGDR could improve early identification and prevention of CMM.

The TyG index, a well-validated surrogate marker of insulin resistance, has been increasingly linked to various cardiometabolic disorders. Recent studies demonstrated its effectiveness in predicting adverse cardiovascular events across different high-risk populations [23, 24]. Furthermore, another study showed elevated TyG index was significantly associated with coronary heart disease (CHD) prevalence in hypertensive patients [25]. Similarly, higher TyG levels were independently associated with increased stroke risk in a dose-dependent manner [26]. Beyond cardiovascular outcomes, longitudinal community data further revealed its strong predictive value for T2DM development, with each unit increase conferring a 3.35-fold higher risk, particularly among younger adults and women [11]. More recently, researchers have developed composite indices integrating TyG with adiposity measures including BMI, WC, WHtR to provide more comprehensive cardiometabolic risk assessment. Emerging evidence has demonstrated significant associations of these combined indices with various cardiovascular outcomes, including hypertension [27], heart failure [28], and stroke [29]. However, few studies have specifically examined the relationship between TyG or its obesity-related derivatives and CMM. Our preliminary analyses focused on TyG-related indices and CMM risk. Notably, while TyG showed a linear association with CMM risk, its obesity-related derivatives exhibited a more pronounced, nonlinear J-shaped relationship, consistent with previous findings by Zhang et al. [30] and Xiao et al. [31] This J-shaped pattern may be explained by the association between extreme values of obesity-related indices and adverse cardiometabolic outcomes [32–35]. Elevated risk at high levels reflects the well-established pathologies of obesity. Conversely, the slightly increased risk at low levels may be attributed to poor underlying health, sarcopenia, or unintentional weight loss associated with subclinical or chronic diseases [32].

The eGDR, which incorporates WC, hypertension, and HbA1c, similarly functions as an alternative insulin resistance marker. Recent large-scale evidence from the UK Biobank study has established eGDR as a robust predictor of cardiovascular outcomes. Participants in the highest eGDR quartile showed 41% lower myocardial infarction risk (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.51–0.67), 34% lower heart failure risk (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.56–0.76), and 27% lower ischemic stroke risk (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.61–0.89) compared to the lowest quartile over a 13.8-year follow-up [14]. Notably, accumulating evidence connected eGDR levels to diabetes complications, including diabetic peripheral neuropathy [16], diabetic nephropathy [36], lower extremity arterial disease [37], and microvascular or macrovascular diseases [38]. Expanding on prior evidence, our preliminary analyses observed that eGDR demonstrated a linear inverse association with CMM risk, highlighting its clinical potential for early identification of high CMM-risk populations.

Insulin resistance, the core hallmark of metabolic syndrome, induces metabolic abnormalities, including hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and hypertension and subsequently drives cardiovascular disease development [39]. These pathological effects are further reflected at the cellular and molecular levels, such as endothelial cell and vascular smooth muscle dysfunction [40], oxidative stress [41], lipid metabolism dysregulation [42], and inflammatory pathway activation [43]. All these suggest that IR and CMM may share a possible association. Therefore, investigating surrogate insulin resistance indices, such as TyG-related indices and eGDR, and their relationship with CMM is crucial and worthwhile. A study of American adults from the NHANES revealed TyG index combined with eGDR significantly improved mortality prediction [44]. Meanwhile, a CHARLS study demonstrated TyG with hsCRP jointly enhanced CMM risk stratification [18]. However, no studies have comprehensively evaluated the joint effects of TyG and its obesity-related derivatives with eGDR on CMM. In this present study, high TyG-related indices were consistently associated with higher CMM risk compared to low indices, both in the general population and among individuals with either low or high eGDR. This association can be attributed to the fact that elevated TyG-related indices signify impaired glucose and lipid homeostasis, which promotes chronic inflammation and vascular damage, etc., ultimately leading to CMM [18]. Similarly, low eGDR was invariably linked to elevated CMM risk compared to high eGDR, both in the overall population and among individuals with either low or high TyG-related indices. The association between low eGDR and increased CMM risk may be explained by the fact that a low eGDR value explicitly represents elevated levels of its constituent components: HbA1c, blood pressure, and central adiposity. It is the presence of these three elevated risk factors that collectively drives the high risk of CMM [44]. In light of these consistent independent relationships, the high TyG-related indices plus low eGDR group showed the highest risk of CMM, with TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR demonstrating stronger combined effects than TyG-BMI or TyG. This likely reflects the superior metabolic specificity of central adiposity measures. While BMI cannot differentiate between body fat and muscle mass [45], changes in waist circumference primarily reflect abdominal fat accumulation [46]. This limitation of BMI is particularly relevant in aging populations where BMI changes may be attributable to alterations in skeletal muscle rather than body fat [47]. In contrast, WC and WHtR specifically capture visceral adiposity, which is more strongly associated with insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction [46]. Thus, when further integrated with eGDR, which captured distinct yet complementary aspects of metabolic dysfunction, TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR continued to exhibit stronger associations with CMM, suggesting a more pivotal pathophysiological role and enabling enhanced risk stratification. Additionally, combining TyG-related indices with eGDR significantly improved CMM prediction beyond conventional factors. Meanwhile, subgroup analyses by age, sex, smoking status, and drinking status demonstrated that the combination of high TyG-related indices and low eGDR was consistently associated with elevated CMM risk across all subgroups. Although no significant interactions were observed, the strength of the association tended to vary across certain subgroups. Specifically, the combined effect of high TyG and low eGDR appeared more pronounced among participants who were aged < 60 years, males, ever smokers, or never drinkers, which was partially supported by previous research reporting stronger associations of the TyG index with CMM in those aged < 60 years and males [30]. Similar trends were observed for TyG-BMI among those aged < 60 years, females, ever smokers, and never drinkers. For high TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR, the combined effect with low eGDR was stronger in participants aged ≥ 60 years, females, never smokers, and never drinkers, which aligned partially with existing evidence reporting stronger associations of these indices with CMM risk in females and never smokers [48]. Notably, some groups with high TyG-related indices plus high eGDR or low TyG-related indices plus low eGDR showed non-significant associations. These patterns may be attributable to limited subgroup sample sizes. Future studies with larger subgroup samples are needed to confirm these observations. Overall, these insights suggest that integrating TyG indices with eGDR may facilitate population-level screening for high-risk CMM subgroups, offering a practical approach to early identification and targeted intervention.

Previous epidemiological and clinical studies demonstrated that not only was hypertension more prevalent among individuals with elevated blood glucose [49] or triglycerides [50], but these metabolic abnormalities were also more pronounced in hypertensive patients compared to normotensive individuals [51]. Furthermore, waist circumference showed positive correlations with both blood glucose and triglyceride levels [52]. These clinical observations reflect the complementary biological pathways through which TyG-related indices and eGDR capture different aspects of metabolic dysregulation and suggest potential interconnected relationships between these markers. While TyG-related indices and eGDR both serve as surrogate markers of insulin resistance, the precise mechanistic links between them remain incompletely understood. Based on current evidence, the relationship between TyG-related indices and eGDR may be explained as follows. Elevated fasting blood glucose mainly reflects hepatic insulin resistance, which stimulates excessive hepatic glucose production and leads to fasting hyperglycemia [53]. Meanwhile, elevated fasting triglycerides predominantly indicate adipose tissue insulin resistance, promoting lipolysis and free fatty acid release that further impair glucose metabolism [44]. These disturbances collectively elevate HbA1c through chronic glycation and promote abdominal obesity [42]. Moreover, TyG has been conclusively associated with increased hypertension risk [54]. On the other hand, long-term poor glycemic control reflected by elevated HbA1c may conversely exacerbate hepatic insulin resistance [44] and impair β-cell function [53] through glucotoxicity. Furthermore, hepatic lipid accumulation induces hepatic insulin resistance by promoting excessive glucose production and impairing insulin degradation [55]. Given the potential biological links between TyG-related indices and eGDR, we further investigated their bidirectional mediating effects and interaction effects on CMM risk. The mediation analysis demonstrated that eGDR significantly mediated all TyG-related indices' associations with CMM. However, only obesity-related TyG indices mediated the association between eGDR and CMM. This observed asymmetry in mutual mediation can be interpreted as follows. The consistent mediation role of eGDR suggests that its core components, namely elevated HbA1c, hypertension, and central adiposity, constitute a significantly common pathway through which hepatic and adipose tissue insulin resistance, as captured by TyG-related indices, ultimately influences CMM risk [42, 54]. Conversely, the absence of a mediation effect for the TyG index in the eGDR-to-CMM pathway, unlike the other three obesity-related TyG indices, may reflect the critical role of adiposity measures in enhancing TyG's mediating capacity. The obesity-related TyG indices incorporate body composition information that complements TyG's metabolic signal, thereby creating a more complete pathway linking eGDR to CMM development. Notably, interaction analyses revealed no significant additive or multiplicative effects between any TyG-related indices and eGDR on CMM risk. These results provided new insights into the complex interplay between TyG-related indices and eGDR, highlighting the need for further research to elucidate their pathophysiological relationships in CMM development.

This study has several strengths. Initially, this study was the first to systematically examine both individual and joint associations of TyG-related indices and eGDR with CMM risk, providing novel insights into their combined predictive value for CMM development. Additionally, a substantial, nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults was utilized in this study, ensuring the reliability and validity of the observed associations. Moreover, the long follow-up period (median 7.25 years) in this study provided robust evidence for the sustained predictive value of TyG-related indices and eGDR. Furthermore, multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify the consistency of the results, confirming the robustness of our findings.

However, there are also several limitations in this study that should be acknowledged. First, the CMM diagnoses were mainly derived from self-reported data from participants, which may introduce misclassification bias due to underreporting or recall inaccuracies. Second, due to clinical thresholds for TyG-related indices and eGDR remained undefined, our median-based stratification approach, while methodologically sound, may not capture optimal discriminative values for disease risk assessment. Third, although we accounted for major confounding factors in our analysis, residual bias may remain due to unmeasured or uncontrolled variables. Finally, as the CHARLS database primarily comprises Chinese adults aged above 45 years, the generalizability of our findings to younger populations or other ethnic groups requires validation in future studies.

Conclusion

This study highlighted that high TyG-related indices (TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR) and low eGDR were separately and jointly associated with higher CMM risk and combining TyG-related indices with eGDR significantly improved CMM prediction. Furthermore, although no interaction effect between TyG-related indices and eGDR was observed, the mediation analysis demonstrated that eGDR significantly mediated all TyG-related indices’ associations with CMM, with only obesity-related TyG indices mediating the association between eGDR and CMM. These findings collectively suggested that the joint application of TyG-related indices and eGDR could improve early identification and prevention of CMM.
