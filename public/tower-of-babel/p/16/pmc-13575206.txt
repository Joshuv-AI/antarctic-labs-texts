Performance evaluation in micro-milling of Inconel 718 with coated tools through an integrated optimization framework
Authors: Ahmad Waqar Tehami, Muhammad Rizwan Ul Haq, Ahmad Sajjad, Bilal Akbar Chuddher, Shahid Ikramullah Butt, Abdulsalam Alqarni, Osamah Y. Moshebah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Miniaturized systems demand micro-components exhibiting high dimensional fidelity, superior surface quality, and complex geometries for aerospace, biomedical, and microelectronic applications. Micro-milling offers flexibility and precision, but consistent performance remains challenging by rapid tool degradation, burr generation, and size-effect-dominated cutting mechanism, particularly in high strength alloys. This study investigates machinability of Inconel 718 in low-speed micro-milling using uncoated and three coated micro-end mills, with feed rates defined relative to cutting-edge radius. Taguchi L16 orthogonal array methodically assesses the impact of cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut, and tool coating across four distinct levels each, on surface roughness, tool wear, and burr formation. The experimental outcomes were evaluated statistically using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), while Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) was applied to identify optimal machining conditions. Results reveal that TiAlN coatings improve surface finish, nACo suppresses burr formation, and uncoated tools enhance wear resistance. The optimal condition from GRA is 10.5 m/min cutting speed, 1.5 μm/tooth feed, and 120 μm depth of cut with uncoated tool. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) yielded average reductions of 23.81%, 11.49%, and 18.11% in surface roughness, tool wear, and burr formation, respectively. This integrated Taguchi-ANOVA-GRA-RSM Optimization (TANGRO) framework provides robust insights for optimal machining performance.

Introduction

Miniaturization has become a cardinal force behind the advancement of modern engineering technologies impelled by the demand for high-precision micro-components with improved system functionality while reducing component size, particularly in aerospace, biomedical, automotive, and electronic applications1,2. A range of micro-manufacturing techniques has been developed for producing high-precision micro-scale components, including laser-based processing, photolithography, ultrasonic machining, and ion beam machining3–8. Laser-based methods offer high flexibility for complex geometries but introduce thermal damage. Photolithography ensures excellent dimensional accuracy, although it is generally limited to planar structures. Ultrasonic machining is suitable for brittle materials due to reduced cutting forces, while ion beam machining enables ultra-precise material removal at the cost of low material removal rates. Recent developments have further improved the performance and applicability of these techniques. Nevertheless, mechanical micro-milling remains a versatile and economical approach for fabricating three-dimensional micro-features in high-strength alloys9–12. Unlike conventional machining, micro-milling is strongly influenced by size-effect-dominated phenomena because the cutting-edge radius becomes comparable to the undeformed chip thickness and microstructural features of the material13. Harsh mechanical load and high temperature, as well as corrosive medium components should be processed from modern super alloys14. Among nickel-based superalloys, Inconel 718 is extensively used in aerospace, energy, and propulsion systems due to its excellent high-temperature strength, corrosion resistance, and fatigue performance15–18. Despite their superior mechanical and thermal properties, nickel-based superalloys exhibit poor machinability due to excessive tool wear, burr formation, and surface degradation19. These difficulties become more pronounced under micro-scale cutting conditions where size-effect-dominated behavior strongly influences surface generation and tool performance. The low thermal conductivity of nickel alloys promotes work hardening, built-up edge formation, and strong tool–workpiece adhesion, significantly elevating cutting temperatures and reducing tool life20,21.

Many techniques have been explored for enhancing machinability of Inconel 718 such as advanced tool coatings, specific coolants, and optimization of machining parameters, laser assistance preheating method to lower the workpiece material strength during cutting19,22,23. One of very successful methods that addresses the machining problems is tool coatings. Aslantas et al. 24 demonstrated that in micro-milling of Ti6Al4V, tool coatings had a substantial effect on cutting forces, tool wear and machined surface quality. Gradual wear of the tool led to higher cutting forces and poorer machined surfaces, and both these factors deteriorated machining accuracy. Irfan et al. 25 studied the effects of different coatings on Inconel 718 during micromachining and discovered that TiAlN + WC and diamond-like coatings showed better resistance to wear behavior and less built-up edge (BUE) development. In the present work, uncoated tools were compared with three coated variants: TiSiN, TiAlN, and AlTiSi-based nanocomposite coating (nACo). These coatings were selected due to their distinct thermal and tribological properties and their widespread application in micro-milling of difficult-to-machine alloys26–28. TiAlN provides superior oxidation resistance, TiSiN offers enhanced hardness through nanocomposite strengthening, while nACo combines high hardness with improved oxidation resistance, enabling systematic comparative evaluation under low-speed micro-milling conditions. Due to the inherent nonlinearity in micro-milling, it is vital to gain a complete understanding of microchip generation mechanisms. All materials have a certain minimum chip thickness i.e., the value below which no effective chip separation takes place. Conventional milling chip thickness models are not directly applicable to micro-milling owing to the greatly reduced cutting edge radius, extremely low feed rates, and shallow depths of cut, resulting in a large negative rake angle. Abd Rahman et al.29 found that the feed and cutting depth are the leading considerations in controlling tool vibration, while the cutting speed depicted dominant impact on roughness.

Significant knowledge gaps still exist although many studies have examined the influence of machining parameters on important performance indicators like surface roughness, burr formation, and tool wear during the micromachining of nickel-based superalloys. Previous research on micro-milling of Inconel 718 has predominantly focused on high cutting speed regimes due to their advantages in reducing tool vibration and burr formation30–32. Consequently, systematic investigations addressing the integrity and quality of micro-machined features under low-speed conditions remain limited. This gap is significant, as machining of Inconel 718 involves high cutting forces, localized heat generation, rapid tool wear, and stringent surface integrity requirements, for which lower cutting speeds are essential to manage thermal and mechanical loads. The adopted low-speed strategy investigates a practically relevant regime governed by size-effect-dominated phenomena, where minimum chip thickness, ploughing–shearing transition, and coating-dependent tool–workpiece interactions strongly influence surface integrity. Prior studies have also demonstrated that acceptable surface finish and burr control can be achieved under optimized low-speed conditions. Moreover, low-speed machining setups are more accessible and economically feasible for industrial applications, while offering improved process stability and control27. Therefore, understanding coating-dependent machining behavior under low-speed micro-milling conditions is essential for developing reliable and cost-effective machining strategies for Inconel 718.

This study presents a comprehensive experimental framework to advance the understanding of micro-scale machinability of Inconel 718 under low speed micro-milling conditions, particularly in conjunction with advanced micro-tool coatings when the feed per tooth operates below, equal to, and above the cutting-edge radius. Such conditions are critically important where pronounced size effect phenomena, ploughing–shearing transitions, and coating-dependent tool workpiece interactions dominate the cutting mechanism. Within this context, the study systematically evaluates the influence of cutting speed, feed per tooth, depth of cut, and tool coating type on surface roughness, tool wear, and burr formation. A comparative assessment of uncoated and advanced variants of coated tools provides new insight into coating-driven performance trends under scale-dependent cutting conditions. For multi-response optimization, Grey Relational Analysis is employed to achieve multi-response optimization, followed by predictive and regression-based optimization using Response Surface Methodology. The overall machining performance is quantified through an integrated Taguchi–ANOVA–GRA–RSM Optimization (TANGRO) framework, with optimal parameter combinations validated through confirmatory experiments. The findings offer practical guidelines for improving low-speed micro-milling of difficult-to-machine superalloys and contribute to the development of cost-effective, high-precision micro-manufacturing strategies.

Materials and methods

An experimental methodology was created to study the machinability of Inconel 718 superalloy during low-speed micro-milling and investigate the combined impact of process parameters and tool coatings. The cutting conditions components: cutting speed, feed per tooth and depth of cut were assessed with the help of a Taguchi L-16 orthogonal array for satisfactory experimental coverage. Both uncoated and TiAlN, TiSiN, nACo coated tools were used to assess the effects of the tool coatings on critical performance responses. Multi-criteria optimization was adopted through GRA with the assistance of RSM to realize the optimum setting of parameters. The direct and interaction impacts of input variables were statistically assessed by ANOVA and regression analysis. Validation experiments were conducted at the optimized parameter settings to verify the predictive capability of the proposed optimization framework. The close agreement between the predicted and experimental results, together with the observed improvements under the optimized conditions, demonstrates the robustness, consistency, and repeatability of the proposed methodology. The integrated TANGRO framework adopted for experimental design, statistical analysis, multi-response optimization, and confirmatory validation is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Selection of workpiece material

Inconel 718, a nickel–chromium superalloy, exhibits exceptional strength at elevated temperatures and offers great fatigue, corrosion, and oxidation resistance over a wide temperature spectrum, allowing it to withstand harsh operating conditions with improved service life15–17. Owing to these characteristics, it is extensively used in high-performance sectors such as energy, and marine industries, particularly in critical components including turbine blades, combustion chambers, engine casings, exhaust systems, and structural elements in nuclear reactors, as well as in rocket and gas turbine systems operating under extreme thermal and mechanical conditions18,33. Consequently, the alloy has found broad applicability across aerospace, automotive, power generation, and defense industries34–36.

Specifications of cutting tools and coatings

Ezugwu et al.37 pointed out the successful machining of nickel-based superalloys requires tools with greater resistance to wear, high hot hardness, adequate toughness, and extreme mechanical strength38. Appropriate tool material selection now considers the intrinsic properties of the material, microscale cutting environment and tool mechanical stability in micro-milling Inconel 718. Due to its abrasive wear resistance at high temperature, Inconel 718 generates severe thermal and mechanical shock to cutting tools and the tool has a tendency for a rapid wear39. M. Takács et al.40 indicated that with the modern tool coating technologies, new materials were claimed to have had their machinability improved even though they belong to the difficult-to-cut group. New coatings with low friction coefficients, high surface hardness and good thermal stability prove capable to effectively damp tool wear mechanisms. These properties result in longer tool life, lower machining cost and better surface integrity of machined parts41. Similar improvements using PVD-coated tools have also been reported for difficult-to-machine alloys42,43.

To assist in efficient chip removal during micro-milling, it is also suggested in prior work that the use of tools with larger flute spacing reduce chip crowding and consequent cutting instabilities44,45. In accordance with the latter recommendation, a two-flute micro-milling cutter was chosen for this work. The smaller number of cutting edges reduces torque and power consumption, which is very good for low-speed micromachining. All the experiments were conducted employing 500 μm diameter tungsten carbide micro end mills featuring two flutes with a 30° helix angle, providing a balanced helical flute geometry that promotes gradual cutter engagement, smoother chip flow, and improved chip evacuation, which help to reduce force fluctuations, chip adhesion, and localized heat accumulation during precision micro-milling of nickel-based superalloys46,47. Although higher helix angles further reduce cutting forces under certain conditions, excessively large helix angles adversely affect burr formation and tool wear48. All cutting tools employed in this study were supplied by the same manufacturer “Changzhou North Carbide Tool Co., Ltd.” and possessed identical geometrical characteristics to ensure consistent cutting conditions across all experiments. By maintaining identical tool geometry and isolating coating characteristics as the only variable, the experimental design ensures that the observed differences in machining performance are attributed primarily to coating characteristics rather than geometric variation. Workpiece preparation procedure

For the micro-milling, a workpiece of Inconel 718 measuring 10 × 10 × 50 mm was rigidly clamped into a precision vice. Prior to slot milling, the surface of specimen subjected to micro-milling was face-milled with a 12 mm carbide square end mill to establish a flat and uniform reference plane. Slot micro-milling experiments were performed with a slot length of 10 mm and an invariable inter-slot distance of 3 mm, which was used to avoid interactions between adjacent cuts as it is depicted in Fig. 3 along with the scheme for micro-milling operations.

Parametric design of experiments

Poorly defined optimal values of micro-milling parameters lead to accelerated tool damage and shortening of tool life, which is several times shorter than during conventional machining. It is thus required to carry out the systematic optimization of key process variables to obtain stable and reproducible machining performance50. Genichi Taguchi’s robust design concept was used in this research to investigate the effect of machining parameters on process performance measures. The Taguchi method stresses not only the mean response but also variability, consequently improving robustness of processes exposed to varying noise factors and operational conditions51. The Taguchi approach was used because it reduces the need for experimentation and provides statistically substantiated results. Orthogonal arrays employed by the Taguchi method are generally known for an efficient return of definitive and reproducible results and were effectively utilized by many researchers to optimize machining processes52–54. The Taguchi method permits the optimization of factor levels without an actual provocative investigation being cost consuming and enhances process robustness to external interferences and inherent variability by using orthogonal arrays55.

Thus, an orthogonal array of L16 was developed with four factors i.e., cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut and coating for the proposed process. The Taguchi L16 orthogonal array was selected because it accommodates four factors at four levels while substantially reducing the number of experimental trials without compromising statistical balance and parameter representation, consistent with established robust design principles56–58. This experimental design is widely recognized as an efficient and structured methodology for systematically investigating the influence of key input parameters on the micro-milling performance. This is particularly important in micro-milling of Inconel 718, where experiments are associated with high tooling costs, rapid tool wear, and extensive post-machining characterization requirements. Similar Taguchi L16-based DOE approaches have also been widely employed in machining and micro-machining studies for process optimization and statistical analysis8,26,59,60. The factors with their levels under investigation are shown in Table 3. A full factorial design with 4 factors and 4 levels would require 256 experimental test runs; however, the Taguchi fractional factorial method reduces this to only 16. Confirmatory experiments were then performed to authenticate the optimized parameter combination values. To minimize potential confounding arising from time-dependent effects such as machine warm-up, environmental variation, and tool batch variability, the experimental runs were performed in randomized order. The objective of this research was to investigate the influence of four machining variables and effects of their interactions on the output responses. The input factors utilized in the study were cutting speed explored at four different levels, feed rate ranges below, at, and above the cutting-edge radius, four levels of cutting depth and four tool variants designated by coated and uncoated micro end mills. These response functions were determined to be important parameters when considering performance of micromachining, due to their relationship with surface quality, dimensional correctness and tool life in the low speed micro-milling processes. The statistical significance and relative influence of each factor in finding the main control parameters for micro-milling performance, was calculated using ANOVA.

The selected parameter combinations were designed to characterize the nonlinear response and transition behavior inherent to micro-milling of Inconel 718. The selected ranges: cutting speed (6–10.5 m/min), feed per tooth (0.5–3.5 μm/tooth), and depth of cut (60–150 μm) correspond to the micro-scale cutting mechanics, where minimum chip thickness effects, ploughing–shearing transition, tool–workpiece interaction, burr formation, and surface integrity are highly sensitive to parameter variation. Comparable parameter windows have been reported in micro-milling studies on Inconel 718 and similar high-strength alloys using tools of comparable scale27,30,61. Specifically, feed per tooth range are chosen to distinguish the three micro-milling regimes: ploughing, transition, and shearing. This design allows investigation of the coating and tool performance under both size-effect dominated and conventional cutting conditions, following the methodology adopted in prior micro-milling studies done by Aramcharoen & Mativenga62, de Oliveira et al.63, Anand-Krishnan & Mathew64 and Jaffery65. This four-level scheme provides adequate resolution to meaningful identification of nonlinear and transition trends while maintaining experimental feasibility and statistical reliability of the design66,67. The cutting speed range of 6–10.5 m/min is defined as a low-speed micro-milling regime based on established cutting speed classifications and is consistent with reported low-speed micro-milling of Inconel 718 in the range of 8–12 m/min27, whereas other studies used 25.1–75.4 m/min in high-speed domains1. According to Schulz and Moriwaki68, nickel-based alloys exhibit conventional machining at low cutting speeds (~ 10 m/min), followed by transition at higher tens of m/min and high-speed machining beyond ~ 50 m/min.

Response measurement and data acquisition

The quality of the machined slots was analyzed through micro-milling tests on critical response parameters. The geometries were characterized after machining with the aid of a calibrated DSX-1000 Olympus Digital Microscope having software version 1.1.5 along, as depicted in Fig. 6(a) and Fig. 6(b). The DSX-1000 is a high-definition digital microscope with a telecentric optical system that provides consistent reproduction results and accurate measurement. Key responses such as surface roughness, tool wear and formation of burrs were consistently measured and analyzed for a complete assessment of machining performance. Tool wear evaluation was performed after completion of a fixed machining length of 10 mm under all experimental conditions to maintain a standardized and consistent basis for comparative machining performance assessment. All measurements were conducted under controlled environmental conditions as prescribed by the manufacturer, and multiple readings were averaged where applicable to minimize random measurement error and improve measurement consistency. Surface roughness was quantified using the areal arithmetic mean height (Sa) obtained through the integrated surface analysis module. Measurements were performed by selecting a defined area of interest on the machined slot surface, as illustrated in Fig. 6(b), typically from the central region along the tool path to ensure consistency and to avoid edge effects such as burr accumulation. Areal parameters provide a more representative characterization of micro-milled surfaces by capturing height variations across the measured area rather than along a single profile, particularly under conditions where ploughing and shearing coexist and contribute to surface generation69,70. For each machining condition, three measurements were conducted at the start, middle, and end of the slot, and the average value was reported to ensure reliability and repeatability of the results.

Tool wear was evaluated by analyzing both flank wear and face wear using optical microscopy. Flank wear was assessed along the clearance face adjacent to the cutting edge, while face wear was examined on the rake face due to chip–tool interaction, in accordance with established tool wear characterization practices in micro-milling71–73. After each machining trial, the cutting tools were examined under consistent magnification. Since a new tool was used for each machining trial, tool wear was measured once per machining condition to ensure independent evaluation. For consistency, measurements were taken at the most affected region of the cutting edge, and the maximum wear value was recorded. Representative optical micrographs with annotated flank and face wear regions are provided in Fig. 7 to illustrate the measurement locations and improve reproducibility of the methodology.

Burr formation in micro-milling occur in several forms, including top, entrance, exit, and bottom burrs depending on cutting kinematics and tool engagement conditions74. In the present study, top burr formation was considered due to its direct influence on surface integrity and dimensional accuracy. Burrs were quantified by measuring burr width and burr height along the slot edges. Burr width was defined as the horizontal extension of the burr from the slot wall, and burr height as the vertical protrusion above the machined surface, following established measurement methodologies, as illustrated in Fig. 8(a) 75,76. Since burr formation was more pronounced on the down-milling side, consistent with reported trends in micro-milling, the maximum burr width and height along the down-milling edge of the entire slot were considered for analysis. Measurement approach and representative micrographs illustrating burr morphology are shown in Fig. 8 (b) and Fig. 8(c), respectively.

Results and discussions

All sixteen experimental conditions were run in triplicate with a fresh micro tool for each trial, to mitigate the effects of cumulative tool wear and provide consistent testing between trials. Similar repeated experimental strategies have also been reported in machining optimization studies77. The experimental results can be found in Table 4. Because of the intrinsic high sensitivity of micromachining processes, slight interferent or measurement errors result in significant discrepancies for the response values. The differences between repeats were mainly caused by unavoidable human operation error, practical tool’s geometrical deviation and surface deposition quality, damping equipment’s own vibrators and noise. The experimental results were statistically analyzed through ANOVA55, using the “lower-the-better” quality characteristic as a critical parameter to measure and determine the individual impact of each process parameter and their respective statistical significance8. Parameters exhibiting p-values below 0.05 were estimated statistically significant at 95% confidence level78. In addition, confirmatory experiments were performed for both best and worst machining conditions to validate the robustness of the ANOVA outcomes. Each confirmatory test was performed in triplicate, resulting in a total of 33 additional experimental runs, to ensure the repeatability and credibility of experimental framework. Effective micro-milling of Inconel 718 necessitates a comprehensive understanding of its mechanical behavior, judicious selection of cutting tool materials and coatings, and precise control of machining parameters to achieve stable and repeatable performance.

Surface roughness analysis

Surface roughness is one of the most critical quality indicators in micro-milling since it directly influences the fatigue strength, dimensional accuracy, and functional reliability of micro-scale components45. Maintaining superior surface integrity is particularly important in aerospace applications involving difficult-to-machine superalloys. D. Serje et al.9 demonstrated that tool geometry, machine tool repeatability, cooling and tool wear are the foremost candidates for influencing the roughness80. In the micro cutting processes, materials are often non-homogeneous at the local level and hardness varies as well which leads to tool’s vibration during cutting. These effects become more obvious at the lower spindle speeds and feeds, leading to unstable machining conditions and non-uniform surface30. Xiaohong Lu et al.81 further demonstrated that surface roughness in micromachining is strongly influenced by cutting variables, tool condition, machining induced vibrations, and the formation of built-up edges (BUE).

Surface finish in micro-milling is primarily influenced by cutting speed, feed rate and depth of cut49. ANOVA results specified that speed exhibited the strongest relative influence on surface roughness with about 32.66% to the total variance, as displayed in Table 5. The roughness generally decreased with the rising speed, indicating better cutting stability at higher cutting speeds. However, a localized increase in roughness was observed when the cutting speed increased from 7.5 to 9 m/min. This local departure is ascribed to increased thermal effects at the tool workpiece interface, where chip formation and surface generation mechanisms change, in accordance with Platt et al. observations82. U. Maheshwera et al.83 observed during machining of difficult-to-cut alloys like Inconel 718, higher values of feed and cutting depth lead to severe damage in surface quality. Conversely, employing high cutting speeds contributes to smoother surfaces. Improved surface finish is generally associated with stabilized cutting forces, reduced vibration, and suppression of built-up edge formation at higher cutting speeds. These results are consistent with the observed experimental trends and confirm that cutting speed is a key parameter for controlling surface quality in low-speed micro-milling.

The cutting depth is the second key parameter that significantly affected surface roughness and accounted for 30.40% of total variation, as shown in Table 5. Tool coating contributed 22.30% to the total variation, whereas feed rate showed the lowest influence of 8.09%. The influence of cutting depth on surface quality remains inconsistent in the literature due to variations in machining conditions and tool geometry84. In the current study with increasing axial depth of cut, surface roughness also increased gradually as indicated in Fig. 9. This is attributed to the intensified heat generation with increasing depth of cut that causes thermal stresses in the cutting zone and encourages microcracks on a machined surface85. Zhang and Addona similarly reported that increasing depth of cut amplifies cutting forces and induces chatter vibrations, both of which deteriorate surface integrity and elevate roughness values29,81. In addition, Z. In main effects results, the significant nonlinear relation between feed and surface roughness is observed in the plot of Fig. 9. A slight improvement in surface finish was observed when feed increased from 0.5 to 1.5 μm/tooth, as indicated by lower roughness values. However, at a higher level of feed rate beyond this transition point, from 1.5 to 3.5 μm/tooth, the surface quality deteriorates further with an increase in roughness values rather than linearly decreasing roughness. Lu et al.86 identified the cutting-edge radius as a critical factor governing surface roughness in micromachining operations. Experimental findings demonstrate a strong interdependence between feed and cutting-edge radius, indicating that optimal surface quality is achieved when the feed approaches the magnitude of the tool edge radius. This behavior is primarily attributed to the interaction between the undeformed chip thickness and the cutting-edge geometry. When the feed rate is set significantly below the cutting-edge radius, limited improvement is yielded in surface finish. Nevertheless, it has also been reported that employing a reduced feed rate in combination with a smaller cutting-edge radius contributes to a lower residual surface height and improved surface integrity9.

The main effects plots indicate that the lowest surface roughness was achieved using TiAlN-coated tools, whereas TiSiN-coated tools produced comparatively inferior surface quality under the investigated conditions. Uncoated and nACo-coated tools exhibited intermediate roughness values, highlighting the strong influence of coating chemistry and tribological behavior on surface generation mechanisms during micro-milling. Regardless of the machining parameters selection, tool wear remains one of the dominant factors influencing surface quality in micro-scale milling operations. Progressive tool wear changes the cutting geometry and induces vibration that deteriorates surface integrity. Thus, the suppression of tool wear is extensively considered an effective approach to increase surface quality, especially in high precision micromachining processes with demanding requirements on form accuracy and surface integrity87.

Tool wear analysis

Tool wear is a critical concern in micro-milling because thermal, mechanical, and chemical interactions at the tool–workpiece and tool–chip interfaces directly influence dimensional accuracy and surface integrity of micromachined features88. In difficult-to-machine superalloys such as Inconel 718, severe thermal loading and high-temperature strength retention accelerate wear progression during machining89,90. At the microscale, discontinuities in material properties across adjacent grains promote intermittent chip formation, built-up edge (BUE) formation, and localized tool damage during cutting. Furthermore, when the feed is less than the cutting-edge radius, ploughing dominates over shearing, resulting in increased frictional forces and accelerated wear91. Shallow depths of cut intensify frictional interaction between the cutting edge and the workpiece surface, promoting localized heat accumulation and accelerated wear progression that leads to an increase in the effective cutting-edge radius, which further deteriorates surface quality and accelerates tool failure92,93. Several studies have reported that advanced low-friction coatings improve wear resistance and surface integrity during micro-milling of Inconel 718 25,94–96. Consistent with these observations, K. Aslantas et al.24 investigated the influence of tool coatings on cutting forces and wear behavior during the micro-milling of Ti-6Al-4 V and concluded that progressive tool wear is the primary contributor to increased cutting forces, which ultimately degrades machining quality. Considering these findings, it is essential to systematically analyze the combined influence of machining parameters along with the tool and coating selection to fully understand their role in governing tool wear behavior during low-speed micro-milling of Inconel 718.

Understanding tool wear mechanisms is essential for improving machining productivity, tool life, and part quality. Of all wear modes during cutting, flank wear is considered the most harmful due to its direct relationship with cutting-edge stability, dimensional accuracy and surface quality of the workpiece. Flank wear mainly arises from high mechanical stress along with combination of high temperatures at cutting interface and thermochemical interactions during material removal. In micro-milling of Inconel 718, the size effect phenomenon strongly constrains chip–tool contact on the rake face thus restricting its potential to develop crater wear. As a result, flank wear is identified as the dominated tool-wear mode under micro-cutting conditions97,98. The low thermal conductivity and high-temperature strength of Inconel 718 promote localized heat accumulation and accelerated flank wear during micro-milling.

ANOVA results displayed in Tables 6 and 7, together with the main effect plots displaying the effect of machining parameters on tool flank and face wear in Fig. 10. Statistical analysis indicates that the cutting speed is the most dominant factor governing flank wear by contributing 32.17% to the total variation. This emphasizes that tool wear increases significantly with increased cutting zone temperature and thermomechanical loads encountered at higher speeds. Tool coating is identified as the second most governing factor with a contribution of 28.97%, emphasizing the significance of tool coating material in controlling wear progression during micro-milling of Inconel 718. Cutting depth follows with a contribution of 18.76%, implying a moderately significant effect on tool workpiece interaction, whereas feed rate exhibits the smallest contribution, accounting for only 8.98% of the overall variation within the investigated parameter range.

For face wear, cutting speed and tool coating are again identified as the dominant controlling factor, with contribution rates of 33.99% and 23.99%, respectively, whereas cutting depth represents 18.13% of the total variability. The dominant influence of cutting speed is associated with intensified chip-tool interaction, while coating properties influence friction behavior, thermal stability, and resistance to coating delamination during high precision micromachining. The comparatively higher face wear observed in the present study is attributed to severe chip–tool interaction along the rake face during micro-milling of Inconel 718. Continuous sliding contact between the hot flowing chip and rake face promotes localized heat accumulation, frictional stress, material adhesion, and coating delamination, thereby accelerating face wear progression99–101. This behavior becomes more significant in micro-milling because the feed per tooth is comparable to the cutting-edge radius. In contrast, flank wear is mainly associated with rubbing interaction along the clearance face and remained comparatively less severe under the selected machining conditions102,103. Similar observations regarding intensified face wear under severe thermo-mechanical conditions have also been reported in machining studies involving difficult-to-cut alloys104,105.

Figure 11 presents the response trends of all responses particularly of flank and face wear as functions of individual machining parameters. An initial increase of both flank and face wear takes place as speed rises. This phenomenon is primarily due to the increased thermal energy release in the cutting region leading to thermally softened tools and an intensified chemical interaction at tool workpiece interface. Conversely, Aramcharoen and Mativenga62 demonstrated that higher cutting speeds elevate cutting temperatures sufficiently to induce thermal softening to the material being processed, which subsequently lowers cutting forces and mitigates abrasive wear mechanisms. Similarly, Liu et al. 106 noted that increased cutting speeds reduce tool–workpiece contact time, limiting material adhesion along the cutting edge and suppressing adhesive wear. These competing thermal and mechanical effects explain the observed wear trends, as depicted in Fig. 11(a), where both flank and face wear attain their minimum values at the highest cutting speed of 10.5 m/min.

Figure 11(b) presents the nonlinear response of tool wear with varying feed rates, with this behavior being particularly evident in flank wear as the feed increases from 0.5 to 3.5 μm/tooth. A pronounced transition is observed near 2.5 μm/tooth, which closely corresponds to the cutting-edge radius of the micro cutting tool. At higher feed rates, the contact time between the tool and the workpiece is reduced, resulting in lower thermal and mechanical loads acting on the tool. Consequently, both flank and face wear tend to decrease, with this effect being more clearly reflected in the face wear trends. Residual effects are more pronounced when the feed is below the cutting-edge radius and under this condition, wear behavior becomes stochastic and less predictable due to increased sensitivity to local cutting variations. Instead, when cutting at excessive feed rates the predicable tool wear develops to that of conventional machining behavior due to more stable controlled shearing-dominated cutting65. N. Anand Krishnan and J. Mathew98 observed that when feed is less than the cutting edge radius, ploughing dominates the material removal, however a changeover to shearing takes place as soon as it crosses this value.

Figure 11(c) shows that the flank and face wear reduces with increasing depth of cut, although the extent of reduction varies, and this behavior is nonlinear for both wears. Such nonlinearity comes from the differential mechanical load and heat transfer between various cutting tool regions. In smaller depths of cut, the material-removal process is controlled not by a true shearing operation but by ploughing and rubbing modes. Consistent with this, Aramcharoen and Mativenga62 reported that with more depth of cut in micro-milling leads to lower specific cutting forces as well as lesser overall tool wear. Jin, K. et al. 107 further concluded that the high material removal rates of deep depths of cut help distribute generated heat, thus preventing thermally related wear mechanisms.

Figure 11(d) shows that the uncoated cutting tools have lower levels of both flank wear and face wear than their coated versions under the selected low-speed micro-milling conditions investigated in the present study. Among the coated tools, TiAlN coatings exhibited relatively better wear resistance, whereas TiSiN and nACo coatings were being observed with significant wear. This behavior is associated with the comparatively low thermal activation of coatings at low cutting speeds. The better wear resistance of uncoated carbide tools is attributed to their sharper cutting edges and finer surface morphology, whereas coating deposition introduces surface irregularities that degrade tool performance. From an economic perspective, uncoated tools are also advantageous due to their comparatively lower manufacturing cost. Typical coating failure modes such as edge chipping, cracking, and delamination indicate poor interface adhesion between the substrate and coating layers, thereby contributing to coating degradation108–110. Moreover, intense heat buildup and material adhesion during machining accelerate thermal softening, coating delamination, and wear propagation111,112.

Burr formation analysis

Inconel 718 is highly susceptible to burr formation during micro-milling because of its inherent plasticity, strong adhesion tendency, and poor chip-breakability. The burr size and shape are the result of an interplay between process parameters, cutting tool geometry, and the material mechanical properties as well as its thermal characteristics67,113. Burr formation deteriorates dimensional accuracy and surface integrity and require additional deburring operations. Unlike macro-machined parts, micro-components are more susceptible to post-processing damage because of their small feature size and limited structural support. Therefore, to reduce burr formation in the micro-milling operation, proper control of cutting conditions and process parameters is vital76. Improper deburring introduces surface defects and residual stresses, compromising the functional reliability of micro-scale components87.

Kovvuri114 found a critical minimum chip thickness, below which effective chip separation does not occur and material removal is dominated by elastic recovery and ploughing rather than stable shearing. Depending on cutting direction, burrs form through extrusion-dominated material flow or incomplete chip fracture when chip separation is unstable67. In the present study, burr formation was more pronounced on the down-milling side, where larger burr width and height were observed compared with the up-milling side. Similar directional trends in micro-milling burr formation have also been reported previously65. Therefore, burr evaluation was performed on the down-milling side, and the maximum burr width and height were considered to represent the worst-case condition for each trial. In micro-milling, no universal threshold value exists for burr width or burr height in micro-milling, since acceptable burr dimensions depend on feature size, dimensional tolerance, assembly requirements, and functional application of the component115. Existing literature consistently treats burr minimization itself as a critical quality objective rather than prescribing a fixed allowable burr size74. Furthermore, ISO 13715:2017 provides guidance for specifying permissible burr and edge conditions through dimensional indication, while the allowable limits are defined according to the specific functional and dimensional requirements of the component116. Excessive burr formation adversely affects dimensional accuracy, surface integrity, assembly capability, and post-processing in precision micromachining. Therefore, burr width and burr height were treated as critical quality indicators in the present study, and their minimization was considered essential for maintaining machining quality and dimensional consistency.

ANOVA results for burr width and burr height are presented in Tables 8 and 9, whereas Figs. 11 and 12 illustrate the corresponding response trends and main effects of machining parameters. Of all the factors studied, cutting speed was determined to be the most dominant factor influencing burr width and burr height contributing 32.16% and 32.02%, respectively. The overall trends of the main effects are that the higher cutting speed causes reduction in both burr width and height, though with a nonlinear relationship. This reduction is associated with reduced tool–chip contact time, improved chip separation, and lower adhesion at higher cutting speeds, which collectively restrict excessive plastic deformation and burr growth117–119.

The depth of cut was the second most significant parameter influencing burr formation, with 23.81% and 26.47% effect on the burr width and height, respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 12, a distinctly nonlinear response is observed, particularly for burr width; however, an overall increasing trend in burr dimensions is evident for both evaluated metrics as the depth of cut increases. Khan M. A. et al.120 reported similar observations, attributing the growth in burr width to the increase in uncut chip thickness associated with higher depths of cut. Larger penetration depths also intensify material engagement and plastic deformation, thereby increasing burr dimensions. Similar trends were found in a study by Baig A et al.30, where the size of burrs increased proportionately to the depth of cut with a strong correlation particularly for burr height. Giasin et al.27 40 similarly associated burr formation with unseparated chip material and reported that increased depth of cut affects both burr size and type.

The tool coatings were found to contribute 16.56% and 11.20% for burr width and height, respectively. Among the investigated tools, the nACo-coated cutters showed the best performance in reducing burr size, while uncoated tools generated slightly larger burrs. This phenomenon is associated with the higher hardness and tribological stability of the nACo coatings, which influence chip formation dynamics and plastic deformation during the process27. Feed rate showed the lowest influence on burr formation, contributing 14.94% and 10.76% to burr width and burr height, respectively. For both burr metrics shown in Fig. 12, there was also a nonmonotonic behavior with feed per tooth. Initially, burr width decreased as feed increased from 0.5 to 1.5 μm/tooth and then it increased when feed was increased from 2.5 to 3.5 μm/tooth. However, burr height continually grew with the elevation of feed between 0.5 μm/tooth and 2.5 μm/tooth, and decreased at a higher feed of 3.5 μm/tooth. At lower feed levels, ploughing-dominated material displacement promotes plastic side flow and burr formation, whereas higher feed levels encourage more stable shearing and chip separation, thereby reducing burr growth under selected conditions120,121.

Optimization of Individual performance metrics

Single criterion optimization seeks to enhance a single response using the “smaller-the-better” approach without accounting for compromise among different responses. The main effect plots have shown that the minimum value of a particular response corresponds to different combinations of machining input variables. Conformity tests were performed pairwise at the most promising and least appealing setting, to verify experimentally that the way in which the experiments are performed is robust and credible. These conformity tests have been reproduced three times each. The experimental results and corresponding input parameters are presented in Table 10. The data indicate that no single set of machining conditions allows all responses to be optimized at the same time. Optimum values of some performance characteristics result in worse performance of the others, indicating the conflict among responses and highlighting the restrictions of single-objective optimization. This dependence calls for a tradeoff between different objectives, that justifies the choice of multi-objective optimization strategies. These methods allow consideration of multiple responses at the same time and present a more realistic and efficient solution structure for complex micromachining problems122–124.

Integrated multi-objective optimization framework

Application of Multi Objective Optimization (MOO) is an important decision-supporting technique, when multiple performances have to be optimized simultaneously for which the objectives work in opposite directions. Traditional approaches using single-objective optimization are inadequate in these cases to bargain trade-offs between competing responses. The ability to apply MOO makes possible the methodical determination of best parameter sets by evaluating different solutions against several criteria at once. In the current work, ANOVA-based optimization of specific responses obtained through different combinations of input parameters were concluded as shown in Table 10, based on the trends depicted between main effects in Figs. 10 and 12. These findings show that the optimum conditions to achieve an optimal level of each response differ and justify the need of a MOO approach when comprehensive process assessment is required.

GRA, stemmed from Grey System Theory, is a vigorous analytical framework for solving complex optimization problems. It is especially applicable where data available is limited, incomplete, and uncertain such as occurs in engineering, manufacturing, and industrial processes with conflicting criteria. The GRA–RSM optimization workflow employed in this study is illustrated in Fig. 13. Based on Taguchi–GRA method, the initial MOO problem is converted to a single representative performance function. The procedural steps of GRA such as data standardization, calculation of grey relational coefficients, the judgment of grey relational grades and the ranking phase are rooted in Grey System Theory. It has been widely used in previous studies concerned with optimization of machining and multi-response decision-making125–130.

Computation of Grey Relational Grade (GRG)

The GRG value is considered as the optimal performance index for prioritization of all experimental alternatives. The mean value is calculated based on all GRCs corresponding with response variables. The GRG is the arithmetic mean of GRCs corresponding to each response parameter when they are equally weighted. In the present study, equal weighting was adopted because surface roughness, flank wear, face wear, burr width, and burr height represent distinct aspects of micro-milling performance. Since no response was prioritized over the others, each response was assigned an equal weight of 0.2 to ensure balanced multi-response optimization without introducing subjective preference, consistent with equal-weight GRG based optimization approaches widely reported in machining studies8,26,59,60. Flank wear and face wear were treated separately because they originate from different tribological interaction zones and are governed by different wear mechanisms during micro-milling, with face wear mainly associated with chip–tool interaction along the rake face and flank wear governed by rubbing interaction along the clearance face. The consolidated index indicates the general proximity of one alternative to the ideal solution mandated by the system, facilitating inter-comparison between trials. Response surface methodology (RSM)

RSM is an efficient statistical approach that is used to optimize processes with more than one factor, at interactive terms. It also allows process performance improvement in a systematic manner with empirical mathematical models and statistical analysis131. RSM is preferred over the Taguchi method since the RSM while incorporating interaction, nonlinear effects facilitate more accurate results and easier optimization.

Regression analysis is prevalent to create a correlation between one or more input parameters and their respective response variables. In a framework of multi-objective optimization, regression modeling is especially helpful to trade-off among conflicting performance requirements132,133. Regression model optimization

The validated regression models developed in Eq. (5) through Eq. (8) were subsequently optimized based on RSM to establish the optimal machining conditions. RSM provides a reliable statistical–mathematical framework to optimize processes affected by several interacting variables. The optimum parameters were determined using response surface optimizer at 95% confidence level according to maximum GRG to certain minimized surface roughness, tool wear and burr generation135. The optimal machining parameters obtained from the integrated Taguchi–GRA–RSM optimization analysis are presented in Fig. 15. The optimized conditions correspond to a cutting speed of 10.5 m/min, feed rate of 1.59 μm/tooth, depth of cut of 85.45 μm, and TiAlN-coated micro-tool. Although the GRG-based experimental ranking identified Test No. 14 using the uncoated micro-tool as the best-performing condition among the experimentally investigated parameter combinations with a maximum experimental GRG value of 0.850, the subsequent RSM-based optimization explored the continuous design space through regression modeling and predicted an improved optimal condition using the TiAlN-coated micro-tool with a predicted GRG value of 0.920, as summarized in Table 15. Thus, the GRA and RSM approaches are complementary rather than contradictory, where GRA identifies the best discrete experimental condition while RSM predicts the optimum condition within the investigated parameter domain. Further confirmatory experiments were conducted to validate the reliability and stability of the optimized conditions.

Experimental validation of optimized conditions

Through validation experiments, the efficacy of the optimized machining parameters was confirmed by comparing the outcomes with those from Test No. 14, the best-performing experimental run, as presented in Table 15. All the assessed response variables showed significant improvement under the optimized parameter combination. The most noticeable improvement among the responses was a 23.81% decrease in surface roughness. There was a substantial reduction in tool wear; flank wear and face wear decreased by 14.84% and 8.13%, respectively. Additionally, burr width and height decreased by 21.92% and 14.29%, respectively, indicating a significant reduction in burr formation. Together, these enhancements verify the suggested optimization framework’s resilience and effectiveness in raising overall micromachining performance.

Conclusions

This study investigated the combined influence of machining parameters and tool coatings on the low-speed micro-milling performance of Inconel 718 using an integrated Taguchi–ANOVA–GRA–RSM optimization (TANGRO) framework. The framework combined systematic experimental design, robust statistical analysis, interpretation of the experimental results, optimizing conflicting performance indicators, regression-based predictive modeling and determining optimal parameter combinations. The analysis focused on surface roughness, flank wear, face wear, burr width, and burr height under size-effect-dominated micro-milling conditions. The results contribute to a better understanding of the complex interrelationship between machining parameters and coating characteristics governing surface quality, tool life, and burr formation during low-speed micro-milling of Inconel 718. The key findings are summarized as follows:

Cutting speed was identified as the most influential factor affecting surface roughness with an SS Proportion of 32.66% to the total variation, followed by depth of cut (30.40%), tool coating (22.30%), and feed per tooth (8.09%).
