Obstacles to effective implementation of interprofessional education in Sudan
Authors: Eman Kheir, Qurashi Ali, Ahmed Fahal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The traditional approach of operating isolated medical and health professional schools has created a disconnect between the rapidly evolving challenges in healthcare and the static nature of educational systems. This disconnect has hindered the ability of educational institutions to adapt effectively to the changing health needs of populations. Interprofessional education (IPE), which fosters collaboration and interaction among various health professions, provides a promising framework for addressing these issues by promoting teamwork, shared understanding, and integrated practice. However, implementing the IPE model requires careful planning, substantial groundwork, and institutional commitment prior to its execution, and that is not always feasible. This study aimed to investigate the unique challenges encountered in integrating interprofessional education within Sudanese medical and health schools. Employing a descriptive, cross-sectional design, the study collected data through a structured questionnaire divided into two sections: participants’ demographic information and their perceptions of the potential challenges in applying IPE in the Sudanese context. The study population consisted of 101 candidates from various medical and health professions who responded to the survey, representing a response rate of 51.53%. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 28. Findings revealed that participants with higher academic ranks across different colleges recognised the need for additional time and resources to implement the IPE model successfully. They also identified the rigidity of traditional curricula as a significant barrier to progress. Most respondents emphasised the importance of strong leadership support, sufficient staffing, targeted training, and motivated students in facilitating IPE integration. Leadership support was highlighted as particularly critical, especially during the initial phases, as successful implementation depends on financial resources, policy adjustments, and modifications to academic calendars. These observations align with the existing literature, which emphasises the vital role of institutional leadership. Furthermore, adequate staffing and trained personnel were deemed essential, with respondents acknowledging that the complexity of implementing IPE necessitates coordinated efforts from multiple stakeholders operating simultaneously. Participants also expressed concerns about potential challenges such as insufficient funding, inadequate physical infrastructure, large student populations, and professional hierarchies or dominance, which could negatively impact the effective application of IPE. In conclusion, while IPE represents an innovative and promising approach to adult health education, its successful implementation in Sudan requires meticulous planning, effective resource allocation, and a strong institutional commitment to overcome the identified barriers. Introduction

In a rapidly evolving world marked by new challenges and risks driven by population growth and societal transformation, a significant gap has emerged between the changing health needs of populations and the outdated, static nature of traditional educational systems [1, 2]. These systems have struggled to keep pace with the daily shifts in health challenges, underscoring the urgent need for a more advanced, comprehensive, and adaptive health framework [3, 4]. Consequently, this necessitates the development of innovative educational models that can effectively address the growing complexities of modern healthcare [5, 6].

Interprofessional Education (IPE) is an innovative pedagogical approach that involves students from different health and social care professions learning collaboratively [7, 8]. Its primary goal is to foster teamwork, communication, and understanding across disciplines, ultimately improving patient safety and healthcare outcomes [9, 10]. As healthcare systems worldwide increasingly emphasise collaborative practice, understanding the benefits and potential challenges of IPE becomes crucial for educators, policymakers, and students alike [11, 12].

One of the most significant advantages of IPE is its potential to enhance patient care. When healthcare professionals collaborate effectively, errors are reduced, care is more coordinated, and patient satisfaction improves [13, 14]. Research indicates that interprofessional teams demonstrate better problem-solving skills and can adapt more readily to complex clinical situations, resulting in safer and higher-quality care [15].

IPE helps students develop essential skills such as effective communication, mutual respect, and teamwork, competencies that are critical in clinical practice [16, 17]. These skills facilitate smoother interprofessional interactions, reducing misunderstandings and conflicts that can compromise care [3, 18].

Traditional health education often isolates disciplines, fostering a culture of “us versus them.” IPE promotes shared understanding among professions, fostering respect for different roles and responsibilities [19, 20]. This cultural shift encourages a more holistic approach to patient care and nurtures mutual appreciation.

Healthcare delivery is inherently collaborative [21, 22]. Introducing students to interprofessional settings early in their education prepares them for actual clinical environments, where teamwork is essential [23]. This exposure enhances students’ confidence and readiness to work within multidisciplinary teams [24].

Collaborative learning environments stimulate critical thinking and creativity. When students from diverse backgrounds contribute different perspectives, innovative solutions to complex health problems often emerge [25]. By fostering teamwork, IPE can improve workforce efficiency, reduce duplication of efforts, and optimise resource utilisation [26]. It also supports retention by creating a more engaging and supportive learning environment [27].

Implementing IPE programmes requires significant coordination across faculties and disciplines. Differences in curricula, schedules, and institutional policies can hinder integration [28, 29]. Additionally, logistical issues such as space, funding, and faculty availability pose barriers [29, 30]. Faculty members and students accustomed to traditional discipline-specific education may resist adopting interprofessional approaches. Cultural barriers and entrenched professional identities can hinder acceptance [31, 32].

The effectiveness of IPE depends on well-designed curricula and skilled facilitators. Poorly planned or superficial programmes may fail to produce meaningful learning outcomes, leading to skepticism about their value [33, 34].

Interprofessional settings can sometimes exacerbate existing hierarchies and power imbalances among professions [35, 36]. For example, students or professionals from dominant disciplines may unintentionally overshadow others, hindering genuine collaboration [37, 38]. IPE often requires additional resources, including faculty training, simulation facilities, and curriculum development. These investments may be challenging for resource-limited institutions [39, 40].

Assessing the effectiveness of IPE is complex. Standardised metrics are lacking, and long-term impacts on clinical practice and patient care are difficult to quantify [41]. Interprofessional Education holds considerable promise for transforming healthcare training and practice [17]. Its advantages, such as improved patient safety, enhanced teamwork skills, and better preparation for clinical realities, are well-documented [42, 43]. However, the successful integration of IPE faces notable challenges, including logistical hurdles, cultural resistance, resource demands, and concerns regarding the quality of implementation [44, 45]. Addressing these issues requires strategic planning, institutional commitment, and ongoing evaluation to maximise the benefits of interprofessional learning and mitigate its drawbacks.

Study design and setting

This study employed a descriptive, cross-sectional design. It was conducted in Sudan, involving participants enrolled in the Master of Health Professions Education programme at the Sudan Medical Specialisation Board. The participants represented various fields within health and medical schools, from different teaching settings such as teaching hospitals, from both private and government universities and colleges across Sudan. The programme brought together learners and educators from diverse institutions in a unified setting. To achieve the study’s objectives, participants were selected based on their active involvement in the interprofessional education (IPE) model and their experiential exposure to it.

Inclusion Criteria include: all candidates registered and successfully enrolled in the programme, regardless of age, gender, or speciality. Those who completed the questionnaire were included in the analysis. Exclusion Criteria include candidates who withdrew after registration, failed to complete the questionnaire, or were unreachable (e.g., switched off phones). The data collection spanned from 2020 to 2023.

Methodology and data collection

The primary aim of this study was to identify institutional and other factors impeding the implementation of IPE in Sudan. Data were collected through an electronically administered questionnaire (Google Forms), specifically designed and adapted from previous studies [17, 26, 32]. Prior to distribution, the questionnaire was validated by content experts to ensure its relevance and appropriateness within the Sudanese context. The instrument consisted of a Likert scale with five options, ranging from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’, to capture participants’ attitudes and opinions, given the subjective nature of the topic.

Participants received the questionnaire link via email, SMS, and various social media platforms. The questionnaire was structured into sections aligned with the study objectives:

Section A: Collected demographic information, including participants’ institutional affiliation, academic rank, and years of teaching experience.Section B: Consisted of two subsections (B.1 and B.2), designed to explore participants’ perceptions of the anticipated challenges in implementing IPE in Sudan. The researcher developed this section based on two prior studies: one examining implementation difficulties in Iran [2] and another assessing challenges faced in both developed and developing countries [20, 28]. At the end of each subsection, open-ended questions provided participants the opportunity to identify additional obstacles they perceived, allowing for the emergence of unforeseen challenges.

Data management and analysis

Quantitative data were analysed descriptively using SPSS version 28. Inferential analysis involved the Chi-square test with cross-tabulations to explore associations between variables, with a significance threshold set at P ≤ 0.05. Qualitative data from open-ended responses were summarised narratively to highlight key themes and insights.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Sudan Medical Specialisation Board Ethical Committee, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Ref 2020/3, revised 2008) [46]. Participants received detailed information about the study, and written informed consent was secured from all. Participation was voluntary, and participants retained the right to withdraw at any point without penalty. The questionnaires were anonymous to protect confidentiality, and all collected data were securely stored and used solely for research purposes.

Results

A total of 101 respondents participated out of 196 invited, representing a response rate of approximately 51.5%. The majority of participants were from the medical faculties (55.4%), followed by dentistry (22.8%), medical laboratories (8.9%), pharmacy (7.9%), and physiotherapy (2%) Fig. 1. Regarding teaching experience, most respondents (35.6%) had between 5 and 10 years of experience, while 26.7% had less than 5 years, 21.8% had 10–15 years, and 15.8% had more than 15 years of teaching.

Regarding the traditional curriculum, 78.2% of participants felt it hindered the implementation of IPE, with 14.9% neutral and 6.9% disagreeing. The relationship between this perception and years of teaching was statistically significant (P = 0.017).

Most respondents (96%) agreed that learning approaches and styles need to be adapted to facilitate the smooth implementation of IPE, with only 3% neutral and 1% disagreeing. This response was significantly associated with academic rank (P = 0.001).

A substantial majority (60.4%) believed that additional time is essential to accommodate the IPE curriculum, while 26.7% disagreed and 12.9% were neutral. The association between this view and college affiliation, as well as academic rank, was statistically significant (P = 0.013 and P = 0.001, respectively).

Support from the institute’s administration and top management was deemed critical by 98% of participants for the successful implementation of IPE. The correlation between management support and variables such as college, years of experience, and academic rank showed significance only with academic rank (P = 0.005).

Regarding financial resources, 67.4% of participants indicated that a lack of adequate funding could negatively impact IPE implementation, while 18.7% disagreed, and 13.9% were uncertain. The significance of this response varied across colleges (P = 0.001).

Participants also emphasised the importance of dedicated premises for IPE, with 82.2% endorsing this need, 6% disagreeing, and 11.9% neutral. No significant relationship was found between this opinion and years of teaching, academic rank, or college speciality.

Many participants attributed challenges to the country’s political and economic instability, which contributes to “brain drain” and results in staff shortages rather than an increase in qualified teachers. They highlighted the necessity of staff and student training, emphasising that clear, shared objectives, roles, responsibilities, and values are fundamental to reducing conflicts and facilitating cooperation (Table 1).

Obstacles related to the learner

In Section B2, 70.3% of respondents believed that students with varying levels of knowledge and skills face different obstacles in applying IPE. Conversely, 19.8% disagreed, and 9.9% were uncertain. A significant relationship was observed between years of teaching experience and perceptions of this challenge (P = 0.020), while college affiliation and academic rank showed no significant associations (P = 0.252 and P = 0.132, respectively).

Participants expressed that early introduction of IPE could be beneficial, suggesting that clarifying the objectives and devising a clear action plan are crucial for successful implementation. Motivation and communication skills among learners were also identified as important facilitators of learning. Some emphasised that workplace and hospital-based training could help staff and students recognise the benefits of IPE for patient care.

Further, many participants believed that students should first develop problem-solving skills before engaging in IPE activities. Others highlighted the need for both students and staff to undergo training and orientation about IPE. A few suggested that aligning students’ academic levels, such as clinical, preclinical, and basic sciences, could prevent attitude-related difficulties and improve collaboration.

Discussion

This study offers valuable insights into the perceptions of faculty members from diverse health disciplines regarding the barriers and facilitators associated with implementing Interprofessional Education (IPE) within Sudanese universities and institutes. The response rate of 51.5% aligns with similar survey-based studies in academic settings, indicating a moderate level of engagement among faculty members, comparable to previous reports [47–49]. The distribution of respondents across disciplines, predominantly from medical faculties (55.4%), reflects the focus of health professional training in the region, yet al.so highlights the importance of engaging multiple disciplines for a comprehensive understanding of interprofessional challenges.

A significant proportion of respondents (61.4%) identified integrating IPE content into existing curricula as a primary obstacle. This perception is consistent with findings from similar studies conducted in regions with traditional, discipline-specific curricula [50]. It is reported from Qatar that rigid curricula and a lack of flexibility hindered the inclusion of interprofessional modules [51]. The entrenched discipline-based approach often leaves little room for shared learning experiences, which are critical for fostering teamwork and mutual understanding [45].

Moreover, the overwhelming majority (78.2%) perceived the traditional curriculum as a barrier, further emphasising the need for curricular reform. This challenge is not unique to Sudan; in many other countries, it has been identified that curriculum rigidity and compartmentalisation have been significant hurdles to implementing interprofessional training [52]. Such structural barriers are compounded by institutional inertia, where faculty and administrative resistance to change can slow or prevent the necessary curriculum modifications for IPE integration [52].

Most respondents (96%) emphasised the necessity of adapting current learning approaches to facilitate IPE. This echoes global trends, highlighting the shift from didactic teaching to active, learner-centred pedagogies, such as problem-based learning, case-based discussions, simulation exercises, and team-based projects [53, 54]. These methods promote interactivity, critical thinking, and collaboration which are core competencies for effective interprofessional practice. Implementing such pedagogies requires faculty development and institutional support, which are often overlooked or undervalued in resource-limited settings.

A striking 98% of participants underscored the importance of administrative and institutional support for the success of IPE. Leadership plays a crucial role in establishing policies, allocating resources, and fostering a culture receptive to interprofessional collaboration [55, 56]. In many low- and middle-income countries, including Sudan, leadership commitment is often challenged by competing priorities, political instability, and limited awareness of IPE’s benefits. Nonetheless, evidence suggests that institutional backing significantly correlates with the sustainability and effectiveness of interprofessional initiatives [57].

Funding constraints emerged as a prominent barrier, with 68% of respondents citing inadequate financial resources as a potential impediment. Similar findings have been reported in other resource-constrained settings, where establishing dedicated interprofessional training spaces, procuring simulation equipment, and remunerating faculty for additional workload pose significant challenges [29, 58]. The lack of dedicated premises for interprofessional activities further complicates implementation, although no significant relationship was found between infrastructure needs and demographic variables, indicating a universal challenge across institutions.

Participants attributed some challenges to broader systemic issues, including political and economic instability, which contribute to brain drain and staff shortages. This systemic instability hampers long-term planning and resource allocation for educational reforms. Similar challenges are documented globally, particularly in countries experiencing economic crises, where health workforce shortages and staff attrition undermine capacity-building efforts [59, 60].

A majority (70.3%) believed that differences in students’ knowledge, skills, and clinical exposure hinder effective participation in IPE. This aligns with prior research indicating that disparities in preparedness can create barriers to meaningful interprofessional learning [45, 54]. For example, students at different stages of their training may have divergent attitudes, maturity levels, and clinical experiences, which can impact team interactions and mutual respect.

Participants emphasised the potential benefits of early IPE exposure, advocating for its integration at preclinical or early clinical stages. Evidence demonstrated that early interprofessional experiences positively influence attitudes towards teamwork and reduce stereotypes [61]. Early exposure fosters mutual understanding, enhances communication skills, and promotes a collaborative mindset before professional identities become entrenched.

The importance of communication skills, motivation, and problem-solving abilities was recurrent in responses. These components are widely recognised as foundational for effective interprofessional collaboration. Reports identified interpersonal skills as core competencies necessary for team functioning [62]. Training in these areas enhances confidence and reduces conflicts within interprofessional teams, leading to better patient outcomes [45, 54].

Many respondents emphasised the need for formal training and orientation sessions on IPE concepts for both students and staff. This is consistent with WHO recommendations, which stress capacity building as a prerequisite for successful IPE implementation [41]. Structured workshops, faculty development programmes, and simulation exercises can facilitate understanding of interprofessional roles and foster a shared language among learners [63].

Some participants suggested that aligning students’ academic levels, such as pairing preclinical students with clinical students, could mitigate attitude-related difficulties. Literature indicates that mixed-level groupings can be beneficial if facilitated appropriately, fostering peer mentorship and collaborative skills [45, 54]. However, without proper facilitation, such groupings risk reinforcing hierarchical attitudes or creating frustration among less experienced students.

The findings reveal that institutional barriers, particularly curriculum rigidity, infrastructural limitations, and leadership support, are the predominant challenges to implementing IPE in Sudanese universities. Addressing these requires comprehensive strategies, including curriculum reform that embeds interprofessional modules, faculty development to shift pedagogical practices, and advocacy for institutional policies that prioritise interprofessional collaboration. Furthermore, resource allocation, infrastructural development, and policy support are essential to create an enabling environment.

Furthermore, early exposure to interprofessional concepts, targeted training, and fostering positive attitudes are critical. Developing a culture of mutual respect and understanding among students from different disciplines can be achieved through simulation-based exercises, joint clinical placements, and interprofessional workshops.

While this study provides valuable insights, it is limited by its cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported data, which may be subject to bias. Longitudinal studies examining the impact of specific interventions on attitudes, skills, and patient outcomes are warranted. Additionally, exploring students’ perspectives and involving policymakers could further enrich the understanding and facilitate the development of comprehensive strategies for IPE integration.
