Severe metabolic acidosis with renal tubular acidosis features attributed to topical brinzolamide in a patient with stage 3 chronic kidney disease: a case report
Authors: Ming-Yuan Victor Chao, Yi-chun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic acidosis is common in chronic kidney disease (CKD). However, when the severity of acidosis is disproportionate to renal dysfunction, exogenous causes must be excluded. Topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAIs) used for glaucoma are commonly perceived as locally acting; in patients with normal renal function their plasma free-drug concentrations are far below those required for systemic effects. In CKD, however, reduced clearance and reduced renal acid-excretory reserve can unmask systemic toxicity. Individual recognition of the syndrome remains uncommon at the bedside, particularly when it presents in the context of acute-on-chronic kidney injury, where its laboratory features can be obscured by acute kidney injury(AKI)-related acidosis.Case presentationA 73-year-old Asian male with stage 3 CKD and glaucoma presented with progressive dyspnea, anorexia, and a 3-kg weight loss one month after starting fixed-combination brinzolamide 1% / timolol 0.5% eye drops. Laboratory evaluation revealed severe metabolic acidosis (pH 7.29; serum bicarbonate 8.9 mmol/L, from a pre-exposure baseline of 20.3 mmol/L) and acute-on-chronic kidney injury (creatinine 3.9 mg/dL versus baseline ~ 2.0 mg/dL). The disturbance was mixed: a delta-delta ratio of 0.42 (calculated using a reference normal serum bicarbonate of 24 mmol/L) indicated a substantial non-anion-gap (hyperchloremic) component superimposed on a smaller high-anion-gap component, with hypokalemia (K+ 3.3 mmol/L), hyperchloremia (Cl- 107 mmol/L), positive urine anion gap (+ 33.4 mmol/L), and a fractional excretion of bicarbonate of 4.3% measured during established acidosis (without bicarbonate loading). Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for ophthalmic medications as hidden causes of mixed acid-base disorders in patients with CKD.

Background

Metabolic acidosis is a frequent and prognostically important complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD). However, it generally develops only when the glomerular filtration rate falls below 20–25 mL/min/1.73 m², and even at advanced stages the plasma bicarbonate is typically only mildly reduced — values below 14–15 mmol/L are unusual in CKD-related acidosis alone [1]. When the severity of acidosis is therefore disproportionate to the degree of renal dysfunction, an exogenous etiology must be actively excluded.

Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAIs) are well-established causes of hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis when administered orally, and the same pharmacological mechanism is exploited topically for glaucoma in fixed or single-agent ophthalmic formulations of brinzolamide and dorzolamide. Although these formulations are widely perceived as locally acting, instilled solutions drain through the nasolacrimal duct into the highly vascularized nasopharyngeal mucosa, entering the systemic circulation and bypassing hepatic first-pass metabolism [2]. The classic pharmacokinetic study by Maren et al. demonstrated that, on standard topical dorzolamide dosing, erythrocyte concentrations rise to ~ 20–25 µM and saturate carbonic anhydrase II in red cells, but that plasma free-drug concentrations remain at approximately 0.034 µM — about 1/200 of those required to produce systemic effects from oral acetazolamide [3]. The authors concluded that topical dorzolamide produces no systemic effects in subjects with normal renal function. Critically, however, the same study showed that the drug is renally cleared (renal clearance ~ 90 mL/min in healthy individuals), so reduced renal clearance in CKD predictably prolongs systemic exposure and may shift the balance toward clinically apparent toxicity [3]. Beyond metabolic acidosis, well-characterized systemic effects of CAIs include numbness and tingling of extremities, metallic taste, depression, fatigue, malaise, weight loss, decreased libido, gastrointestinal irritation, urolithiasis, and transient myopia, with rare reports of blood dyscrasias (including aplastic anemia) [4, 5].

Topical-CAI-induced acid-base disturbance has been described before. Menon and Vernon reported indirect evidence of metabolic acidosis (lethargy, dysgeusia, and oxidative blackening of a worn silver chain) in an adult started on topical brinzolamide; no formal blood gas was obtained in that case [6]. Hoffmanová and Sánchez documented overt normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis (HCO3- 17.3 mmol/L) accompanied by normocytic anemia and hyperkalemia in a 46-year-old man with diabetic nephropathy and moderately impaired renal function (creatinine clearance ~ 55 mL/min/1.73 m²) on topical dorzolamide, with full resolution after withdrawal [7]. At the population level, Wang et al. analyzed a nationwide cohort of 2,423 Taiwanese patients with advanced (pre-dialysis stage 5) CKD with glaucoma. Among the 257 patients receiving topical CAIs, the adjusted hazard ratio for hospital admission for metabolic acidosis was 1.89 (95% CI 1.07–3.36) and the adjusted hazard ratio for progression to long-term dialysis was 1.17 (95% CI 1.01–1.37) compared with non-users [8]. In daily nephrology practice, however, individual recognition remains uncommon, particularly when the patient presents with acute-on-chronic kidney injury (AKI on CKD): the laboratory features of CAI-induced renal tubular acidosis (RTA) can be obscured by, and may even be confused with, the metabolic acidosis intrinsic to AKI.

The aim of this case report is to illustrate a critical diagnostic blind spot in medication reconciliation. We present a case in which severe mixed metabolic acidosis with prominent non-anion-gap features developed one month after initiation of a topical brinzolamide-containing ophthalmic solution in a patient with stage 3 CKD and use this case to illustrate the diagnostic reasoning required to identify a CAI contribution against the background of AKI on CKD. This case report has been prepared in compliance with the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 73-year-old Asian man with a six-year history of stage 3 CKD (baseline serum creatinine ~ 2.0 mg/dL), with an estimated glomerular filtration rate ~ 30 mL/min/1.73 m²), chronic bronchiectasis, and bilateral primary open-angle glaucoma was referred to the nephrology outpatient clinic for evaluation of progressive dyspnea, anorexia, and weight loss of one month duration. He also reported mild diffuse abdominal discomfort and a 3-kg weight loss (from 38 kg to 35 kg) over the same period. He denied fever, cough, hemoptysis, diarrhea, vomiting, melena, or polyuria. He had no history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hepatic disease, autoimmune disease, ureteric or renal stones, or previous episodes of metabolic acidosis.

Medication and exposure history

His regular medications were unchanged from the preceding year and notably did not include diuretics, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin-receptor blockers, lithium, or antiretroviral agents. The single recent change was the initiation, one month before presentation, of a fixed-combination ophthalmic solution containing brinzolamide 1% and timolol 0.5% (AzargaⓇ), one drop in each eye twice daily, prescribed by the ophthalmology service for inadequate intraocular pressure control. There was no history of ammonium chloride, toluene, or laxative use, no recent gastrointestinal losses, and no acute infection. Adherence to the eye drops was confirmed by family caregiver report.

Clinical findings

On examination, the patient appeared to be alert and oriented. Blood pressure was 136/92 mmHg. Heart rate was 84 beats/min and regular, respiratory rate was slightly elevated 20/min and temperature 36.6°c. Chest auscultation revealed bilateral coarse breath sounds. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, and there was no costovertebral angle tenderness or peripheral edema. Neurological evaluation was unremarkable.

Diagnostic assessment

Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated severe metabolic acidosis (pH of 7.29; PaCO2 18.8 mmHg; HCO3- 8.9 mmol/L), with mild concurrent respiratory alkalosis by Winter’s formula. Serum chemistry showed creatinine at 3.9 mg/dL (versus a stable baseline of approximately 2.0 mg/dL), urea 46 mg/dL, sodium 131 mmol/L, potassium 3.3 mmol/L, chloride 107 mmol/L, and albumin 2.7 g/dL. The serum anion gap was 15.1 mmol/L; corrected for hypoalbuminemia (adding 2.5 mmol/L per 1 g/dL fall in albumin), the corrected anion gap was 18.3 mmol/L. The delta-delta ratio (Δ corrected anion gap / Δ HCO3-, calculated using reference values of 12 mmol/L for the corrected anion gap and 24 mmol/L for the serum bicarbonate) was 0.42, indicating that a non-anion-gap (hyperchloremic) component dominated and was superimposed on a smaller high-anion-gap component. The patient’s pre-exposure baseline serum bicarbonate, obtained as venous total CO2 on a routine basic metabolic panel at a stable nephrology follow-up visit two months before presentation (when serum creatinine was at his stable baseline of approximately 2.0 mg/dL), was 20.3 mmol/L, demonstrating that the value of 8.9 mmol/L on presentation represented an acute decrease of 11.4 mmol/L during the exposure.

Investigations directed at alternative causes of metabolic acidosis were unrevealing. Plasma lactate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone were normal; the serum osmolar gap was within normal limits, making toxic alcohol ingestion unlikely. Liver function tests, including alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, bilirubin, and ammonia, and thyroid function were normal. Autoimmune serology (anti-nuclear antibody, anti-dsDNA, anti-SSA, and anti-SSB, complement C3 and C4) was negative.

Electrocardiogram showed left ventricular hypertrophy. Chest and abdominal imaging reported bilateral hilar bronchiectasis but otherwise non-significant. Urine indices, obtained without prior bicarbonate or ammonium chloride loading, showed urine pH 5.5, urine sodium 72 mmol/L, urine potassium 15.4 mmol/L, urine chloride 54 mmol/L, urine anion gap + 33.4 mmol/L, and fractional excretion of bicarbonate (FeHCO3) 4.3%. The combination of hypokalemia, hyperchloremia, a positive urine anion gap, and a low FeHCO3 measured during established acidosis was interpreted as compatible with renal tubular acidosis with a distal-predominant pattern and possible partial proximal reabsorptive contribution. Differential diagnosis

The differential considered uremic acidosis, gastrointestinal bicarbonate loss, distal (Type 1) RTA from Sjögren’s syndrome or other autoimmune disease, proximal (Type 2) RTA, hypoaldosteronism (Type 4), toxic ingestions including ethylene glycol or methanol, and drug-induced acid-base disturbance. The pattern of the disturbance, the negative etiological work-up, the close temporal relationship to a newly initiated CA-inhibiting agent, and the rapid reversal after withdrawal made drug-induced renal tubular acidosis from systemically absorbed brinzolamide the most plausible explanation. Causality was formally assessed using the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale [9], yielding a total score of 7 (cut-offs: ≥9 definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, ≤ 0 doubtful), corresponding to a ‘probable’ adverse drug reaction (Table 2).

Therapeutic intervention

Brinzolamide/timolol eye drops were discontinued and replaced, in consultation with the ophthalmology team, with a prostaglandin-analogue eye drop that does not contain CAI. The patient received intravenous isotonic saline with sodium bicarbonate aimed at slow correction over 24 h, followed by oral sodium bicarbonate 1300 mg three times daily. Dietary advice emphasized increased fruit and vegetable intake as adjunctive alkali therapy [10]. Potassium repletion was administered. Renal-function-adjusted dosing of his other medications was reviewed.

Follow-up and outcomes

Within 24 h of admission, dyspnea improved and acidemia began to correct. By day 7, arterial blood gas had normalized (pH 7.41; HCO3- 25.7 mmol/L), serum potassium was 4.1 mmol/L, and serum creatinine had fallen to 1.8 mg/dL. Oral sodium bicarbonate was tapered over four weeks. At one-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with stable renal function (creatinine 2.0–2.2 mg/dL), normal serum bicarbonate (24 mmol/L), and adequate intraocular pressure control on the alternative ophthalmic regimen. No recurrence of acidosis occurred. Patient acceptability of the management was positive.

Discussion

This case highlights that topical ophthalmic agents can be a potent source of systemic toxicity in CKD patients, and that the resulting acid-base disturbance can be misinterpreted as the metabolic acidosis of acute kidney injury alone. Brinzolamide is a sulfonamide derivative that inhibits carbonic anhydrase (CA) isozyme II. Although intended for local effect, the solution drains through the nasolacrimal duct into the highly vascularized nasopharyngeal mucosa, allowing direct entry into systemic circulation and bypassing hepatic first-pass metabolism [2, 3]. In patients with normal renal function, plasma free-drug concentrations after topical dorzolamide are roughly 1/200 of those required for systemic effects, and Maren et al. concluded that no systemic effect is expected in such patients [3]. In CKD, however, the renal clearance pathway that normally constrains free-drug concentration is impaired, providing a plausible pharmacokinetic explanation for the susceptibility of CKD patients to systemic toxicity from topical CAIs [3, 8].

In the renal tubule, the cytosolic enzyme carbonic anhydrase II (CA-II) is essential for acid-base transport in multiple segments. In the proximal tubule, CA-II generates the intracellular protons required for apical secretion and the bicarbonate for basolateral reabsorption [11]. In the distal nephron, specifically within alpha-intercalated cells, CA-II provides the substrate protons for the apical H+-ATPase pump, a critical step for urinary acidification and ammonium trapping [11, 12]. Consequently, pharmacological inhibition of CA-II can mimic features of both proximal (Type 2) and distal (Type 1) RTA. In our case, the finding of a positive urine anion gap is consistent with a functional defect in distal hydrogen ion secretion (NH4+ excretion), supporting a mixed or distal-predominant RTA phenotype driven by this mechanism [13].

The diagnostic key in this case was the stepwise interpretation of the acid-base status against the patient’s own pre-exposure baseline. The pre-exposure serum bicarbonate of 20.3 mmol/L fell acutely to 8.9 mmol/L over the month of brinzolamide/timolol exposure. A cursory review might have attributed acidosis solely to uremia (High AG). However, the Delta-Delta ratio and the positive urine anion gap indicated that the non-anion-gap (hyperchloremic) component dominated. The temporal sequence between drug initiation and clinical presentation provided strong corroboration. Symptom onset, weight loss, and biochemical deterioration developed within one month of starting brinzolamide/timolol, with no other new exposures, no new acute illness, and no change in dietary or medication context. The most decisive piece of evidence, however, was the response to de-challenge: discontinuation of the agent together with alkali repletion produced complete normalization of bicarbonate within one week, with recovery of creatinine toward baseline.

A coherent pharmacological mechanism for the natriuretic component of the syndrome is provided by the recently published ADVOR trial [14]. In its pre-specified renal-function sub-analysis, intravenous acetazolamide added to loop diuretics in patients with acute decompensated heart failure significantly increased natriuresis and diuresis, with the magnitude of the diuretic effect larger in patients with low eGFR, while overall the incidence of worsening renal function during treatment was higher with acetazolamide than with placebo (40.5% vs. 18.9%). ADVOR studied intravenous acetazolamide in volume-overloaded heart failure, which is different from chronic topical brinzolamide use in our case, and a one-to-one analogy would therefore be inappropriate. Nonetheless, ADVOR provides direct pharmacological evidence that systemically active CAI exposure can produce a measurable natriuretic effect with attendant transient deterioration of renal function in susceptible patients, consistent with the gradual 3-kg weight loss and the creatinine rise observed during the month of brinzolamide/timolol exposure in our case.

This pathophysiological pattern also clarifies the mechanism of the AKI in our patient, which occurred in the absence of overt clinical signs of hypovolemia. We interpret the AKI as prerenal in origin, arising from the combination of CAI-induced natriuresis and diuresis on the one hand, and reduced oral intake during the preceding month of anorexia and the 3-kg weight loss (approximately 7.9% of body weight) on the other. Together these produced subclinical, compensated volume depletion on top of stage 3 CKD; the absence of resting orthostatic hypotension or tachycardia at presentation is consistent with compensated rather than overt hypovolemia in an elderly patient, and the rapid response of serum creatinine to intravenous isotonic saline (1.8 mg/dL by day 7) supports this prerenal interpretation.

Two important caveats must be acknowledged. First, a positive urine anion gap is not specific to RTA in the setting of AKI, as AKI can lower urinary NH4+ excretion and produce a positive UAG without an intrinsic distal defect. Second, partial recovery of bicarbonate paralleled recovery from AKI, so the relative contributions of AKI and of a discrete drug-induced RTA cannot be quantified from the clinical course alone. Definitive separation of these mechanisms would have required post-recovery provocative testing — ammonium chloride loading or measurement of the urine-blood PCO2 gradient under bicarbonate loading, the latter of which has high sensitivity and specificity for H+-ATPase-defect distal RTA [15] — but this was not undertaken because the abnormality fully resolved off the offending agent and provocative testing in a recovered, asymptomatic patient was not deemed ethically justifiable. We acknowledge that absolute proof of causality in a single-case report — which would require deliberate drug rechallenge — is not ethically feasible after a severe adverse event such as life-threatening metabolic acidosis. The accepted methodological standard for case-report-level causality assessment is therefore the application of structured probability scales, of which the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale is the most widely used. We therefore present drug-induced RTA as the most likely dominant contributor to a mixed acid-base disturbance, with AKI on CKD acting as a permissive and amplifying factor; this position is further supported by formal causality assessment using the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale, which yielded a score of 7, corresponding to a probable adverse drug reaction (Table 2) [9].

Interpretation of FeHCO3 in established acidosis

A FeHCO3 of ≥ 15–20% measured under physiological serum bicarbonate is the classical threshold for proximal RTA. In our case the value of 4.3% was measured during established acidemia (HCO3− 8.9 mmol/L), when the filtered load of bicarbonate is markedly reduced; under such conditions, even a substantial proximal re-absorptive defect can yield a low FeHCO3 because the absolute amount of bicarbonate filtered is small. Formal demonstration of a proximal defect would have required a bicarbonate-loading test, which was not performed. The 4.3% value is therefore best interpreted as compatible but not diagnostic of a partial proximal reabsorptive defect superimposed on a distal acidification defect, consistent with the dual-segment role of CA-II inhibition.

Vulnerability of CKD patients and the wider literature

While acid-base homeostasis is typically maintained in patients with normal renal function despite significant acid loads, patients with CKD are uniquely vulnerable. As detailed by Kraut and Madias, while residual nephrons in CKD adapt via increased per-nephron ammoniagenesis, the critical reduction in total nephron mass eventually limits the overall capacity [1]. Renal clearance of CAIs is also reduced, prolonging systemic exposure [3]. Consistent with this physiology, the nationwide population-based cohort by Wang et al. reported that pre-dialysis stage 5 CKD patients newly exposed to topical CAIs had an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.89 (95% CI 1.07–3.36) for hospital admission for metabolic acidosis and a significantly higher rate of progression to long-term dialysis (adjusted HR 1.17; 95% CI 1.01–1.37) compared with non-users [8]. Although the population-level signal is established, individual recognition at the bedside remains uncommon, and the present case is offered as a clinically instructive illustration of how the syndrome can present, be reasoned about, and be reversed. Interestingly, this case also highlights the utility of dietary interventions. Evidence suggests that increasing dietary alkali through fruits and vegetables can be as effective as oral sodium bicarbonate in correcting metabolic acidosis in CKD, with potential cardiovascular benefits [10].

Summary of notable key points

Strengths and limitations

The principal strengths of this report are the systematic exclusion of alternative causes of acidosis, the application of formal causality assessment using the Naranjo scale, the extended one-year follow-up that documents sustained reversal of the acid-base disturbance after withdrawal, and the explicit recognition of the diagnostic constraints imposed by concurrent AKI. Several limitations must, however, be acknowledged. As a single-patient observation, the report cannot prove individual causality beyond temporal and pharmacological plausibility, even though a population-level association is established. Definitive distinction of drug-induced RTA from the metabolic acidosis of AKI on CKD would require post-recovery provocative testing (NH4Cl loading or measurement of the urine-blood PCO2 gradient under NaHCO3 loading), neither of which was undertaken in this clinically recovered patient. The FeHCO3 was measured during established acidosis, which limits its interpretive value as a marker of proximal RTA, and serum or urinary brinzolamide concentrations were not measured. Finally, the Wang et al. cohort enrolled patients with advanced (pre-dialysis stage 5) CKD on erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, whereas our patient had stage 3 CKD; the population-level findings therefore support biological plausibility but do not directly establish the magnitude of risk for stage 3 disease.

Conclusion

Topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors can precipitate severe, potentially life-threatening metabolic acidosis in patients with reduced renal reserve, even when their plasma free-drug concentrations would be subclinical in patients with normal kidney function. Although the underlying pharmacology and the population-level risk are not novel, individual recognition of the syndrome at the bedside remains suboptimal — particularly in CKD patients who present with concurrent acute kidney injury, in whom the laboratory signature of a CAI-induced renal tubular acidosis can be partially masked by AKI-related acidosis. The case presented here illustrates that disproportionate severity of acidosis, a hyperchloremic-dominant disturbance pattern and an appropriate temporal relationship to drug initiation should prompt active consideration of an exogenous CAI source, even when the agent in question is topical ophthalmic preparation. We therefore propose that ophthalmic medications be routinely captured during nephrology medication reconciliation, that acid-base status be monitored within the first 1–2 months of initiating topical CAIs in CKD patients, and that formal causality assessment be undertaken whenever an adverse drug reaction is suspected.
