Letermovir use for cytomegalovirus prophylaxis following lung transplantation: A single-center review
Authors: Alyssa K Mezochow, Kevin D He, Kathryn Whitaker, Emily Blumberg, Maria M Crespo, Andrew Courtwright
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytomegalovirus (CMV) remains a major cause of infection following solid organ transplant and valganciclovir prophylaxis is the standard of care. However, valganciclovir-associated myelosuppression limits its tolerability and can require dose reduction in immunosuppression, particularly cell cycle inhibitors, or changes in antimicrobial prophylaxis. Letermovir, a viral terminase inhibitor, is approved by the FDA for use as CMV prophylaxis in kidney transplant recipients; data on its use in lung transplant recipients (LTRs) are limited.MethodsWe performed a single-center retrospective cohort study of LTRs prescribed letermovir for prophylaxis between January 1, 2018 and September 1, 2023.ResultsOver the study period, 88 LTRs were prescribed letermovir with most (93%) switching due to myelosuppression while on valganciclovir. Of those prescribed letermovir, 67 (76%) actually started letermovir, with the primary barrier being insufficient insurance coverage or high copay cost. Among LTRs who started letermovir due to myelosuppression, 90% had improvement in their leukopenia or neutropenia, with a significant increase in white blood cell count 1 month after switching therapy (p < 0.0001). Additionally, 46% of those who were not receiving a cell cycle inhibitor previously were able to restart this after switching to letermovir. CMV was detected in 14 LTRs following letermovir switch, with 2 discontinuing letermovir due to breakthrough CMV.ConclusionsOur findings support the use of letermovir for CMV prophylaxis in LTRs with possible benefits over valganciclovir, although a direct comparison is needed.KEYWORDS: cytomegalovirus, infection, letermovir, lung transplant, prophylaxis

Background

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) is a common opportunistic infection and remains a serious post-transplant complication with increased morbidity and mortality.1, 2 Solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients can develop symptomatic infection with CMV syndrome or end-organ disease. High-risk mismatch patients (donor CMV seropositive/recipient CMV seronegative [CMV D+/R−]) are at greatest risk.1 To prevent CMV infection in at-risk SOT recipients, many centers employ antiviral prophylaxis for patients during the period of highest risk.3 Ganciclovir (intravenous) and valganciclovir (oral) are indirect viral DNA polymerase inhibitors and the first-line therapy to prevent CMV disease.2 However, myelosuppression is a common adverse effect associated with these medications, often limiting their use or requiring alteration in other medications including immunosuppressants, particularly cell cycle inhibitors.2

Letermovir, a novel CMV antiviral that works as a viral terminase inhibitor, was approved by the FDA in 2017 for use in CMV seropositive allogenic hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients.4 In a double-blinded randomized trial of hematopoietic stem cell transplant patients, letermovir was found to be more effective than placebo in preventing clinically significant CMV infection without myelosuppression.5 In June 2023, the FDA expanded letermovir’s approved usage to include kidney transplant recipients with CMV D+/R− transplants based on the results of a phase 3 randomized trial showing noninferiority of letermovir as compared to valganciclovir for preventing CMV disease.6, 7 In addition to noninferiority, this trial demonstrated a significantly lower rate of leukopenia and neutropenia in individuals on letermovir as well as fewer discontinuations.7 This study did not explore whether the improved side effect profile of letermovir enabled recipients to remain on planned immunosuppression.

While letermovir is now approved for use in kidney transplant recipients, there is limited data regarding its use in thoracic organ transplants. Given issues with myelosuppression in patients receiving valganciclovir, letermovir use is particularly appealing for lung transplant recipients (LTRs) who are continued on CMV prophylaxis for longer periods of time post-transplant as compared to other SOT recipients and whose higher risk of rejection makes reduction in immunosuppression a greater concern.8 Multiple small case series of heart and LTRs have shown relative safety and efficacy of letermovir use for a mix of CMV prophylaxis and treatment, although these studies are limited due to their small size.9, 10, 11, 12 In a larger study of 41 thoracic organ recipients receiving letermovir for CMV prophylaxis by Saullo et al, myelosuppression was reported as the most common reason for switching to letermovir, although valganciclovir resistance was also frequent.13

In this study, we present a single transplant center’s real-world experience using letermovir for CMV prophylaxis in LTRs. Our objective was to describe the indications for letermovir prophylaxis in this cohort, potential barriers to prescribing therapy, and the impact of letermovir utilization on immunosuppression regimens, particularly cell cycle inhibitors.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of LTRs prescribed letermovir for prophylaxis at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania (HUP) between January 1, 2018 and September 1, 2023. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pennsylvania.

Patient population and CMV prophylaxis practices

All LTRs who were transplanted at HUP through May 1, 2023 and prescribed letermovir during the study period were eligible for inclusion in this study regardless of whether letermovir was started. Follow-up data were analyzed through September 1, 2023. All LTRs received immunosuppression in accordance with the standardized protocol used at HUP, which included methylprednisolone and basiliximab induction immunosuppression, and tacrolimus, prednisone, and mycophenolate mofetil 1,000 mg twice daily maintenance immunosuppression. Tacrolimus troughs were maintained between 8 and 12 micrograms/milliliter (mcg/ml) during the first year and 6 to 8 mcg/ml thereafter. Target troughs were adjusted based on other clinical factors, including renal insufficiency, skin malignancies, acute rejection episodes, and neurotoxicity such as tremors. Triazole aspergillus prophylaxis was only initiated for cause. Most commonly, this was in the setting of anastomotic ischemia or necrosis or the use of antithymocyte globulin or B cell-depleting therapy before or after transplant.

Initial CMV prophylaxis was prescribed for LTRs where the donor or recipient was CMV seropositive according to a standardized protocol. For high-risk mismatch individuals (CMV D+/R−), IV ganciclovir 5 mg/kg twice daily was initially used for 14 days post-transplant, followed by valganciclovir 900 mg orally daily for 12 months post-transplant. In individuals where the recipient was CMV seropositive with a seropositive or seronegative donor, LTRs received valganciclovir 900 mg orally daily for a minimum of 3 to 6 months post-transplant. The actual duration of CMV prophylaxis was left to the discretion of the patient’s transplant pulmonologist. In general, in the absence of a medication-related side effect or cost concerns, many patients continued CMV prophylaxis indefinitely. This was particularly true for recipients at increased risk for CMV reactivation, including those with significant pretransplant immunosuppression, telomere biology disorders, or augmented post-transplant immunosuppression for acute rejection. Standardized management of post-transplant myelosuppression included adjusting valganciclovir for renal function, reducing or holding cell cycle inhibitors, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) support for absolute neutrophil count <1,000 per microliter. Off-label use of letermovir was considered for LTRs with intolerance or resistance to the standard CMV prophylactic treatment as determined by the individual’s lung transplant provider. LTRs were excluded if letermovir was prescribed for an indication of CMV treatment as opposed to prophylaxis. Indication for letermovir prescription was determined by a transplant physician through chart review. Cases, where prior DNAemia or resistance was the reason for the switch, were adjudicated by 2 transplant physicians to determine whether the indication was prophylaxis vs treatment. In cases of disagreement, a third transplant physician acted as a tiebreaker. A cohort that included all LTRs occurring at HUP during a matched time period, but were not prescribed letermovir was included as part of the baseline comparison cohort.

Data collection

Data were abstracted from chart review and included recipient demographics, medication orders, donor and recipient CMV serostatus, laboratory values, and recipient outcomes. Tacrolimus levels, which were obtained within 5 days of starting letermovir, were considered to be supratherapeutic if the level was 1.5 times or above the highest therapeutic value based on our algorithm using time from transplant. Quantitative plasma CMV polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing was primarily performed at the HUP laboratory using the Roche cobas CMV Test for amplification and detection of CMV with a lower limit of 34.5 IU/ml quantification. CMV PCR testing for some individuals was conducted at laboratories outside of HUP based on patient location. All CMV PCR testing results were recorded in the electronic medical record and accessible for review. Per institutional protocol, all recipients undergo CMV PCR testing at least monthly in the first year following transplant, regardless of CMV prophylaxis agent, and every 3 months thereafter, if otherwise stable.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed to characterize LTRs who were prescribed and started letermovir. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort were compared to LTRs transplanted at HUP during that time period who were not prescribed letermovir. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were performed for categorical variables and Student’s t-tests were used for analyzing continuous variables. All analyses were performed using Stata v14-18 (College Station, TX).

Results

Cohort characteristics

There were 543 individuals who received a lung transplant HUP between May 1, 2016 and May 1, 2023 and were evaluated for this study. Five LTRs were excluded because they were prescribed letermovir for an indication of CMV treatment. All individuals receiving letermovir for prophylaxis had CMV viral loads <50 IU/ml at the time of switch to letermovir. There were 88 LTRs prescribed letermovir for CMV prophylaxis and 450 LTRs who were not prescribed letermovir. Baseline characteristics for these 2 groups were relatively similar and are described in Table 1. Exactly 21% of the nonletermovir group were at high risk for CMV (CMV D+/R−) as compared to 39% in the group prescribed letermovir. Additionally, in those prescribed letermovir, 19% were CMV D−/R+ and 35% were CMV D+/R+ while 25% of the nonletermovir group were CMV D−/R+ and 33% were D+/R+ (p < 0.001).

Prescribing letermovir

All LTRs started on letermovir had previously been on either ganciclovir or valganciclovir. Indications for switching included myelosuppression (most commonly leukopenia), challenges with dosing related to renal function impairment, and prior DNAemia (CMV >35 IU/ml) while on valganciclovir (Table 2). All LTRs who were prescribed letermovir for prior DNAemia while on valganciclovir did not have quantifiable DNAemia (i.e., serum viral load <lower limit of quantification) at the time of letermovir initiation. Sixty-seven (76%) of the LTRs prescribed letermovir started therapy. Of the 21 individuals who did not start letermovir, over half were because of lack of insurance coverage (33%) or high copay cost (29%). Six did not start as their indication for switching had resolved. Of those who did start letermovir, over two-thirds (69%) required prior authorization from their insurance and over one-third (37%) utilized a pharmaceutical payment assistance program to help cover the cost of the medication. The majority of individuals (75%) started on letermovir in the outpatient setting.

Letermovir treatment and outcomes

The median time for transitioning to letermovir following transplant was 245 days (interquartile range [IQR] 108-584). Of the LTRs who started letermovir (N = 67), 97% had myelosuppression as the primary indication: 90% for leukopenia and 36% for neutropenia. Of those with leukopenia, 12% (N = 7) were also noted to have thrombocytopenia as an indication for switching (no LTRs had thrombocytopenia alone as an indication). Nearly half (48%, N = 30) of the individuals with myelosuppression required G-CSF before starting letermovir. Of those requiring G-CSF before letermovir, 37% (N = 11/30) continued to require additional G-CSF at 1 month after starting letermovir. Among LTRs who were switched to letermovir due to leukopenia, 90% (N = 54/60) had improvement in their leukopenia and 88% (N = 21/24) had improved neutropenia. The majority of LTRs (57%) did not have a change in their thrombocytopenia. The median white blood cell count in this cohort before starting letermovir was 3,000/μl (IQR 2,400-5,100), which increased to 5,600/μl (IQR 4,500-6,900) 1 month after starting letermovir (p < 0.0001). For those who were switched for neutropenia, the median neutrophil count before starting letermovir was 1,200, which increased to 3,500 1 month after starting letermovir (p = 0.0017). The median lymphocyte count for LTRs in the cohort also significantly increased 1 month after switching to letermovir (p = 0.019). No patients in the letermovir group had clinically significant anemia requiring blood transfusion in the immediate period before or after letermovir initiation.

Before transitioning to letermovir, 70% (N = 46) of the cohort was not receiving a cell cycle inhibitor, which typically had been held due to myelosuppression. After switching to letermovir nearly half (46%) of that group was able to restart that medication (an additional 28% did not have their cell cycle inhibitor resumed for other reasons, most commonly infection concerns). Additionally, 5 LTRs in the cohort were able to restart trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis, which had been discontinued because of myelosuppression concerns. All LTRs in this cohort were treated with a calcineurin inhibitor as part of their immunosuppression regimen, with the vast majority (97%) receiving tacrolimus. One-third of the cohort developed supratherapeutic tacrolimus levels after switching to letermovir (N = 22), while 4 LTRs (6%) had subtherapeutic tacrolimus.

Two LTRs in the cohort switched to letermovir due to prior CMV DNAemia on valganciclovir (viral load <35 IU/ml at the time of switch). Both LTRs who started letermovir for prior DNAemia on valganciclovir did not have recurrent DNAemia on letermovir. There were 14 individuals (21%) who developed detectable CMV in blood while on letermovir and 8 who developed quantifiable DNAemia (>35 IU/ml). Of those who developed quantifiable DNAemia, only 1 individual had a peak viral load >400 IU/ml and half were continued on letermovir therapy with resolution of their DNAemia (Table 3). Two LTRs discontinued letermovir due to breakthrough CMV. No individuals who developed DNAemia in this study had resistance testing sent. Six other individuals stopped letermovir during the study period, including 1 for side effects (headaches), 3 for cost or insurance reasons, and 2 for other reasons. An additional 3 individuals required temporary cessation of letermovir due to costs or insurance coverage. Eight individuals in the cohort experienced acute cellular rejection and 10 died while receiving letermovir, although none of the deaths were directly attributed to CMV. At the conclusion of the study, LTRs in the cohort had been taking letermovir for a median of 497 days (IQR 199-800 days). Exactly 31% of LTRs receiving letermovir were still taking this at the conclusion of the study period.

Discussion

This study represents a large cohort of LTRs using letermovir as CMV prophylaxis to date and illustrates the real-world use of letermovir for CMV prophylaxis at an academic transplant center. Exactly 16% of the lung transplant population at HUP were prescribed letermovir over the study period, with only 12% of the population actually starting the medication. The baseline characteristics of the letermovir group were relatively similar to the nonletermovir LTRs transplanted at HUP over this time period, although LTRs prescribed letermovir were more likely to be high-risk CMV mismatches (CMV D+/R−).

The primary reason for switching to letermovir in this study was myelosuppression, which is consistent with prior studies.10, 13 There was a significant increase in white blood cell count 1 month after transitioning to letermovir. The majority of LTRs receiving letermovir were not actively prescribed a cell cycle inhibitor at the time of letermovir start, primarily due to myelosuppression. However, following the switch to letermovir, nearly half of this group was able to resume their cell cycle inhibitor. Additionally, 5 individuals in this study were also able to restart their trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis, which had been held for myelosuppression. This highlights the secondary benefits of letermovir for CMV prophylaxis in this group, allowing LTRs to resume optimal immunosuppression and infection prophylaxis. This is the first study to look at secondary effects of letermovir use on immunosuppression and other prophylaxis regimens in LTRs. Similar findings of improved leukopenia and resumption of immunosuppressants have been seen in studies of other SOT recipients.14 Although we did not collect data on glomerular filtrate rate (GFR), 13 patients were recommended to switch to letermovir from valganciclovir because of concerns regarding complex renal dosing. This suggests that the letermovir group was enriched for patients with either variable GFR or GFR on the borderline for dose adjustments in valganciclovir.

Relatively few LTRs were switched to letermovir for the indication of DNAemia or prior resistance on ganciclovir, with only 3% of those starting letermovir having this as their indication. This was a relatively smaller proportion of our cohort as compared to prior studies.11, 13 Notably, our study excluded any individuals who were not virologically suppressed at the time of initiation, which may have limited this population. Detectable CMV was seen in one-fifth of those who started letermovir, although only 1 LTR developed DNAemia >1,000 IU/ml and 2 LTRs discontinued letermovir for breakthrough CMV. No one in the study developed tissue-invasive CMV. This is similar to prior studies, which have noted some LTRs to develop low-level transient CVM DNAemia, although significantly elevated levels or development of disease were rare.10, 13

Letermovir was generally well tolerated in this study. Although a third of patients developed supratherapeutic tacrolimus levels following drug initiation, we did not have sufficient numbers to identify recipient characteristics that may predict calcineurin elevations. Centers that routinely use triazole aspergillus prophylaxis, which can increase the potential for supratherapeutic troughs, may consider empiric adjustments in tacrolimus dose with letermovir initiation. Few individuals stopped letermovir prematurely during the study period, with only 1 stopping due to side effects. LTRs were treated with letermovir for prolonged courses, with a median treatment time of longer than 16 months and nearly one-third of individuals were still on letermovir at the cessation of the study. Starting letermovir was noted to affect the levels of tacrolimus in over one-third of the cohort. The interaction of letermovir and tacrolimus has been previously reported and highlights the need for drug monitoring when starting this medication.14

Notably, nearly one-quarter of LTRs who were prescribed letermovir in our study did not actually start this medication. Over half of this group did not start due to the high costs of the medication or lack of insurance coverage. Of those individuals who did start, over one-third used a payment assistance program to help cover the cost of the medication. Cost was also the most common reason for stopping letermovir in this study. This highlights some of the practical difficulties of prescribing this medication at present, which impacts an individual’s ability to actually take letermovir. The need for prior authorizations, pharmacy assistance applications, and identification of funding sources also carry a significant administrative burden on transplant program staff and can delay the initiation of therapy.15

This study has several limitations. It is a retrospective study of individuals switched to letermovir by their transplant provider and so, as a result, there is likely variability in who was started on this medication based on patient and provider preferences. While the letermovir group appeared generally similar at baseline to other LTRs transplanted during this period, letermovir was being used as second-line prophylaxis for those unable to tolerate valganciclovir and so LTRs likely differ from those able to tolerate valganciclovir. Prospective studies are needed, which would be able to compare valganciclovir more directly with letermovir. Prospective studies could also enable the assessment of other outcomes relating to transplant morbidity and mortality, particularly if the ability to restart or continue optimal immunosuppression may affect outcomes. Additionally, because this was a single-center study, the immunosuppression and treatment practices may differ from those at other transplant centers, which may affect generalizability. In particular, centers that do not use 14 days of CMV treatment with intravenous ganciclovir in CMV D+/R− recipients or that use a less sensitive assay to identify CMV DNAemia may have a different experience with the transition from valganciclovir to letermovir.

This study represents the largest real-world descriptive study of LTRs receiving letermovir to date. Our study highlights the potential benefits of using letermovir in LTRs, particularly in those recipients experiencing myelosuppression on valganciclovir. Further prospective studies directly comparing letermovir and valganciclovir are needed in this population to better assess whether there are differences in outcomes as well as whether letermovir may provide benefits over valganciclovir in this population.
