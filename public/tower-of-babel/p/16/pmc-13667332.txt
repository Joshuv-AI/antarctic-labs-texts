Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory disease that causes synovial joint damage, disability, and reduced life expectancy [1]. Recognition of RA as early as possible is important because a considerable proportion of patients develop irreversible joint damage soon after disease onset [2]. The presence of bone erosions suggests highly active RA, and the progression of the erosive process after treatment necessitates therapeutic changes [3, 4]. Thus, assessment for bone erosions is important for both the early diagnosis and the management of RA patients. Plain radiography of the hand and wrists has traditionally been used for the clinical assessment of RA patients. According to the international recommendations of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) [5] and the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) [6], plain radiography remains the reference method for the diagnosis and follow‐up of RA patients. However, overlapping bony structures make some regions difficult to evaluate, and plain radiography has limited sensitivity for detecting bone erosions [7, 8].

MRI has been increasingly recognized as a useful method for diagnosis and monitoring of therapeutic response in RA patients since it enables clinicians to visually detect bone erosion and active synovitis in early RA before the changes are visible on plain radiographs [3]. On the other hand, several researchers have reported that conventional MRI has lower sensitivity to bone erosion than computed tomography (CT) [7, 9, 10, 11]. A previous study has reported that MRI has reduced sensitivity for detecting bone erosions in the carpal bones, particularly in the pisiform, trapezium, and hamate, when compared with CT [9]. Moreover, MRI has been shown to miss approximately 9% of erosions detected by CT at the metacarpal bases [10].

Recently, zero time‐to‐echo (ZTE) imaging has been developed to detect such low signals from tissues with extremely short T2* values. After gray‐scale inversion, this sequence can generate CT‐like images from MRI [12]. Although the use of ZTE is expected to overcome the weakness of MRI in bone evaluation, to our best knowledge, no studies have been conducted on the use of ZTE images for evaluating bone erosion in RA patients.

The purpose of this study is to prospectively evaluate the usefulness of adding ZTE imaging to conventional MRI for detecting hand and wrist bone erosions in RA patients, using CT as the reference standard.

Material and Methods

This study was approved by our institutional review board (number 22–090). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients

From December 2022 to October 2023, 15 consecutive patients with RA fulfilling the RA classification criteria by the American College of Rheumatology criteria/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) [13] were prospectively enrolled in this study. The patients met all of the following inclusion criteria: (a) presentation with polyarthralgia including wrist joint, (b) underwent plain radiography, CT examination, conventional MRI examinations, and ZTE sequences of the symptomatic hand and wrist within 3 weeks, and (c) fulfillment of the ACR diagnostic criteria as conclusively determined by certificated rheumatologists.

Image Data Acquisition

Plain Radiography

The digital radiographic examination (Flat panel detector, KONICA MINOLTA), including posteroanterior and oblique views of the bilateral hand and wrist, was obtained at a tube voltage of 50 kVp and a tube current of 200 mA, according to the standard protocol of our department. The mean total radiation dose was 0.04 mGy per patient.

CT Examination

Bilateral wrist and hand CT scan was obtained by using a 320‐row multi‐slice CT device (Aquilion ONE; Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan), and an 80‐row multi‐slice CT device (Aquilion PRIME; Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan). The patient was positioned prone with the dominant arm outstretched, palm facing downward. All exposures were standardized according to the standard protocol used in our department (120 kV on average, X‐ray tube rotation speed of 1.0 s, beamwidth of 320 × 0.5 mm, volume CT dose index of 16.4–18.4 mGy, CT‐Automatic Exposure Control [AEC] setting target SD of 9.5–10 Hounsfield units for a 1‐mm image of the hand standard kernel FC14). Axial sections 1 mm thick were obtained, and these were used to reconstruct sagittal, coronal, and axial images 1 mm thick. CT scan scoring was performed on 1‐mm sections. The mean dose‐length product was 7.15 mGy · cm (range, 6.5–8.1 mGy cm).

MRI Protocol

MRI was performed with a 3.0‐T MRI (SIGNA Premier, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) with GEM small flex coil (16‐M Array; NeoCoil, Pewaukee, Wisconsin, USA) in accordance with the outcome measures in rheumatology (OMERACT) scoring system recommendations [14], unenhanced T1‐weighted images, contrast‐enhanced fat‐suppressed T1‐weighted images (2D multipoint DIXON, repetition time/echo time (TR/TE), 683/13 ms; matrix size, 400 × 340; Field of View (FOV), 24 cm) that enable visualization in 2 planes, and fat‐suppressed fast spin‐echo T2‐weighted sequences (2D multipoint DIXON, TR/TE, 5377/81 ms; matrix size, 400 × 352; FOV, 24 cm) that enable visualization in two planes were included. Contrast‐enhanced images were obtained after bolus injection of gadoteridol, meglumine gadoterate, or gadobutrol into a vein in the contralateral arm. Contrast agents were used during MRI acquisition for clinical purposes, but the evaluation of bone erosions in this study was based exclusively on non–contrast‐enhanced sequences.

The 3D ZTE sequence was performed with the following parameters: flip angle, 1°; TR/TE, 529/0 ms; reconstruction matrix, 300 × 300; FOV, 22 cm; and pixel bandwidth, 326 Hz/pixel. The section thickness was 1.5 mm for coronal images and 2.0 mm for axial images, and the total acquisition time was 5 min 49 s, covering both bilateral hands and wrists. Patients were positioned laterally, with both hands held together in a prayer‐like position, and the hands were placed between two coils for signal reception.

Image Evaluation

The MR images and the radiographs were independently interpreted by three certified radiologists (Y.H., A.F., and J.T.) with 28, 13, and 7 years of experience in the imaging evaluation of arthritis, respectively. The three readers (Y.H., A.F., and J.T.) independently and consecutively read plain radiography, conventional MRI (axial and coronal T1‐weighted and fat‐suppressed T2‐weighted images), and ZTE images. The readers were blinded to clinical and imaging modalities (including those performed on the same day and prior images). Images obtained at plain radiography, conventional MRI, and ZTE were separated by imaging modality into three groups of patients, each randomly assigned. The three readers independently and consecutively read the radiographic, conventional MRI, and ZTE images, with a one‐week interval between each imaging modality group. Original ZTE images are primarily used for evaluation, but reformatted ZTE images are available for review if required. The CT scan, regarded as the reference standard, was analyzed by the experienced musculoskeletal radiologist (T.A.), who has 32 years of expertise. The readers were blinded to clinical and imaging modalities (including those performed on the same day and prior images). Bone erosion was considered to be a sharply delineated juxta‐articular lesion with a cortical defect. They analyzed the erosion by using a 4‐point scale of erosion (0, normal; 1, discrete erosion; 2, < 50% of the joint surface; 3, > 50% of the joint surface). Images were evaluated for bone erosions at 25 anatomical sites per finger and wrist, including the distal radius, distal ulna, eight carpal bones, first through fifth metacarpal bases, and the proximal and distal aspects of the first through fifth metacarpophalangeal joints [14]. For comparison of detection rates between modalities, a lesion was considered positive if at least one observer identified an erosion; the other observers then reviewed the site, and any discrepancies were resolved by interobserver agreement. For the CT images, erosions were assessed by using axial, coronal, and sagittal reformations with the same criteria.

Statistical Analysis

Comparison of bone erosion was analyzed statistically with the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test for multiple comparisons and Bonferroni correction. Diagnostic performance (sensitivity and specificity) using CT as the reference standard was compared using the McNemar test. Interobserver variability for bone erosions was calculated using Fleiss κ statistics, and 95% confidence intervals were obtained for kappa values. The strength of agreement was considered slight for kappa values of 0.400 or less, fair for kappa values of 0.40–0.60, good for kappa values of 0.60–0.80, and excellent for kappa values of 0.81 or greater. All calculations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (ver.31.0, IBM, Armonk, NY). p values less than 0.01 were considered significant in all analyses.

Results

Bone Erosion Detection Rate

Eleven female patients (mean age, 56.3 years; median, 61 years; range, 26–78 years), and 4 male patients (mean age, 77 years; median, 77 years; range, 70–84 years), with a mean disease duration of 79.9 months (±49.6 (SD)) participated in this prospective study (Table 1). The detection rate of bone erosion for plain radiography, conventional MRI, and ZTE images was 11.5%, 23.1%, 26.9%, respectively (Table 2). Significantly more bone erosions were revealed using ZTE than with plain radiography or conventional MRI (Figures 1, 2, 3).

Interobserver Agreement

Interobserver agreement for bone erosions is summarized in Table 4. Interobserver agreement on bone erosion assessment was excellent for ZTE imaging (κ = 0.822; 0.788–0.856) but was not excellent for plain radiography (κ = 0.427; 0.388–0.466) and conventional MRI (κ = 0.711; 0.678–0.745). By site evaluation, at the distal and the proximal MCP joints, interobserver agreement for bone erosion was excellent for ZTE imaging (κ = 0.883 and 0.858) but was not excellent for conventional MRI (κ = 0.649 and 0.793). ZTE also improved interobserver agreement for the metacarpal bases, the carpal bones, and the distal radius and ulna (κ = 0.785, 0.782, and 0.868) compared with conventional MRI (κ = 0.656, 0.649, and 0.804).

Discussion

This prospective study demonstrates the substantial benefits of incorporating ZTE imaging into conventional MRI, enhancing its diagnostic capability compared with plain radiography and conventional MRI alone. In addition, the results indicate that the diagnostic performance of ZTE for detecting bone erosion is comparable to that of CT. The improved sensitivity reduces the likelihood of missing bone erosions, which is particularly important in conditions such as RA where early detection can influence treatment outcomes. Furthermore, the high interobserver agreement observed with ZTE suggests that this technique may facilitate more consistent evaluations among radiologists, potentially leading to standardized diagnostic approaches in clinical practice. We believe that incorporating ZTE into conventional MRI improves its utility for assessing bone erosions in RA patients compared with conventional MRI alone.

Although interobserver agreement for bone erosions was fair with plain radiography and good for conventional MRI, it was excellent with ZTE imaging. According to previous reports, the sensitivity of MRI for detecting bone erosions in the carpal bones, as well as in the metacarpal bases, is lower than that of CT [7, 9]. ZTE imaging improved interobserver agreement even at these sites compared with conventional MRI. The boundaries of bone erosions are clearly delineated on CT‐like images produced by ZTE, which are not affected by osteitis/bone marrow edema, joint effusion, or synovial thickening that can complicate the interpretation of conventional MRI [15, 16]. Moreover, 3D volume data acquisition enables the generation of arbitrary cross‐sections at high resolution, allowing for detailed analysis of complexly shaped wrist joints [17, 18]. These advantages of ZTE may account for the improved interobserver agreement observed in this study.

When assessing bone erosions of the hand and wrist on ZTE images, the main challenge encountered in this study was differentiating true bone erosions from vascular foramina, particularly in anatomically complex regions. It should be recognized that vascular foramina may appear as areas of low signal intensity on ZTE images, mimicking bone erosions. Cross‐checking with other MRI sequences and identifying a linear low‐signal pattern of vessels extending from the extraosseous to the intraosseous regions may help overcome this pitfall.

The consensus among researchers is that CT is the optimal imaging modality for identifying bone erosions in RA patients because of its multiplanar capabilities and improved cortical definition [10, 19]. However, CT is not routinely used in RA patients due to several drawbacks, including cost, radiation exposure, and limited access compared with plain radiography [9, 10, 20]. Three‐dimensional ZTE can evaluate bone erosion at any cross‐section by adding less than 6 min of scanning time to conventional MRI, allowing complete coverage of bilateral hands and wrists. This technique can be readily incorporated into the clinical workflow and enables simultaneous assessment of other MR findings relevant to RA evaluation, such as synovitis and bone marrow edema, within a single examination. ZTE is therefore a promising technique for RA patients, offering a means to avoid unnecessary radiation exposure, reduce costs, and eliminate the need for additional CT examination.

Limitations

First, this study included a rather small number of cases from a single institution. A larger multicenter study would be expected to provide more comprehensive and generalizable results. Second, although RA is a systemic disease, we did not assess regions other than the hands and wrists because they are among the earliest and most frequently involved sites in RA patients. Third, although contrast material was administered as part of the MRI protocol, contrast‐enhanced images were not used for analysis. Postcontrast images can enhance the detection of synovitis and tenosynovitis in RA patients. However, according to the OMERACT recommendations, the recommended MRI sequences, which are primarily used to assess bone erosions, include non‐contrast‐enhanced T1‐weighted images, and contrast‐enhanced images are not mandatory [21, 22]. Because our study primarily focused on the efficacy of ZTE in detecting bone erosions, we consider the exclusion of postcontrast images to be appropriate. Finally, the prognostic implications of improved bone erosion detectability with ZTE remain unclear. Longitudinal studies investigating the prognostic value of ZTE findings in relation to treatment outcomes will be warranted for determining its clinical relevance.

Conclusion

The integration of ZTE with conventional MRI enhances the accuracy and reproducibility of bone erosion detection in RA, achieving diagnostic performance comparable to that of CT. The most important issue in the management of RA is the early intervention before bone erosion occurs. Therefore, clarifying bone erosion by ZTE can contribute to the appropriate management of RA patients.
