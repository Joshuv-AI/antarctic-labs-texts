‘I Put a Lot of Emotion in My Work, I Use It, It's My Tool’: An Ethnographic Account of Algorithmic Homecare Assistance in the Netherlands
Authors: Eliana Bergamin, Iris Wallenburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis paper explores how the introduction of AI‐powered remote technologies reconfigures emotional practices in homecare. Drawing on three months of ethnographic fieldwork and in‐depth interviews with a Dutch company that replaces in‐house nurses with remote monitoring systems and nonmedically trained ‘coaches’, the authors analyse the introduction of so‐called ‘empathy sessions’: 15‐minute per week (online) calls aimed at making up for the emotional void left by reduced or removed in‐person nursing. These sessions transform this nursing intervention into a new, technically mediated format that creates new professional roles, alters the training of both nurses and coaches and reshapes how homecare clients engage with care providers. We argue that the company's emphasis on efficiency as a guiding value plays a central role in shaping how these emotional practices are understood and enacted: empathy is condensed into time‐limited sessions, reassigned from nurses to newly created roles (coaches) and moved to technologically mediated exchanges. Beyond this reshaping, the study also shows the ambiguities involved and raises questions about how care is allocated, who gets to perform it and how its delivery is shaped by entrepreneurial frameworks of efficiency and market‐oriented healthcare delivery.Keywords: efficiency, emotions, empathy, homecare, telecare

Introduction

We always work for the nurse. So if we decide to implement a device, it needs to be for the profit [benefit] for the nurse. […] Most importantly, it needs to bring us to the goal to make care more efficient.

(CTO [Chief Technology Officer] of the company interviewed for our study)

The increasing implementation of artificial intelligence (AI) and algorithmic systems is shifting how healthcare is organised and delivered. One of the most prominent changes is the expansion of remote care, a broad category which encompasses telemedicine consultations, automated patient monitoring and digital communication platforms designed to facilitate care at a distance (Moore et al. 2024; Oudshoorn 2012; Pols 2012). Remote care is often presented as the panacea to longstanding and intensifying pressures on healthcare systems, including shortages of personnel, ageing population and increasing healthcare costs (Chen et al. 2023; Chernenko et al. 2020; Dorsey and Topol 2020, 202). It would make healthcare more professional‐centred and efficient, as the CTO (Chief Technology Officer) in the opening quote points out. Promoted through solutionist narratives, AI and algorithm‐supported remote care is presented as an efficient, scalable solution for maintaining oversight on patients whereas at the same time optimising scarce resources. This way, it is being framed not only as necessary, but as the inevitable future of healthcare.

Central to this technological narrative is the notion of efficiency (Asbath et al. 2025; Breslow 2007; Maines et al. 2024; Whitehead and Conley 2023). As Alexander (2009) has argued in her historical analysis of the concept, efficiency has long operated as more than a practical objective. It has become an ideology, shaping how technological innovations are conceived, implemented and evaluated. Technologies are not adopted merely because they solve problems, but because they align with the underlying belief that making processes faster and more streamlined is inherently valuable. Efficiency becomes then an end in itself rather than a means to achieve broader (care) goals. As sociologist Dowling (2021) has argued, these developments are deeply entangled with broader efforts to align care with commercial aims and as attempts to capitalise on and formalise affective care delivery. Strategies to routinise, standardise and rationalise care practices—continuously framed as means to enhance productivity, reduce costs and optimise time—can end up transforming the very possibility of providing relational care (Dowling 2021, 39). Here, algorithmic systems mediate not only data and processes, but also emotional encounters, shaping how care relationships unfold in practice.

Based on these premises, this paper will explore how the materiality of AI and algorithmic technologies in (remote) homecare practice come to shape new paradigms of care, where efficiency‐driven mechanisms are applied to nonquantifiable, embodied aspects of care, such as emotions. Against this background, this paper takes the promissory narratives through which (AI‐driven) algorithmic care is introduced and legitimised in the homecare sector and grounds it in an ethnographic study. Over the course of three months, one of the researchers immersed themselves in the operations of a private company situated in the Netherlands to explore how technology affects these relational and empathic care dimensions. The company offers a series of algorithm‐driven devices to care receivers in homecare settings, designed to substitute for nurses delivering in‐home care and defining themselves as a ‘nursing intervention’. These technological implementations, spanning from sleep to insulin trackers, from smart scales to pill‐cases with opening sensors, from fall detection devices to smart clocks and tablets, aim at reducing or completely substituting the presence of nurses in the homecare setting. Central to these technological implementations is the replacement of face‐to‐face empathy provision from nurses with 15‐minute online weekly coaching sessions, labelled as the ‘empathy of the week’, that home‐cared clients are supposed to receive. These sessions, delivered remotely by new (often not formally trained) care workers called ‘coaches’, represent a tangible reflection of the reconfiguration of healthcare practices due to algorithmic mediation. The study offers an empirical glimpse into how, through the introduction of algorithms, care practices are expanded and reshaped through the creation of new actors and the reconfiguration of empathic encounters.

Theoretical Background

Efficiency has long shaped the development of healthcare, but in the context of digitalisation it has become more than a practical imperative: it functions as a normative ideal that drives and justifies technological adoption in the field. Yet, as historian of technology Alexander (2009) has argued, the pursuit of efficiency is not a neutral or purely technical goal: it reflects broader historical and ideological commitments that influence how technologies are imagined and implemented within (healthcare) systems. From its pre‐industrial meaning, indicating both the comparison of inputs and outputs in machines and the power and goodness of God, the concept has then undergone a great modification during industrialisation (Alexander 2009). When humans started dealing with machines, it became clear that there was a set amount of resources that could lead to an expected result. Efficiency then came to indicate adequate or sufficient powers and nothing more (Alexander 2009, 10). It is however in the turn of the twentieth century that efficiency comes to indicate not only quantifiable, machine‐related outputs, but it starts to mingle with social, economic, governmental and personal concerns. As a response to a social crisis, in a world coming out of the Great Depression, efficiency became a value indicating how society should be organised and how it should function. Returning to the present, efficiency is now one of the core values in justifying the implementation of technologies in all aspects of society, specifically fitting in with the dominant neoliberal discourse of contemporary welfare states (Dowling 2021).

Although the drive for efficiency in healthcare was extensively analysed as a product of technocratic and neoliberal perspectives, its implications for the affective and emotional dimensions of care are no less profound. Emotional labour, broadly understood as the work of managing one's own and others' emotions in the course of professional interactions, has historically formed a central but often invisiblized component of healthcare practice (Erickson and Grove 2008; Hochschild 2012). As more recently Biss (2024) and Moretti and Pronzato (2024) have argued, this labour is not just incidental to care work, but constitutive of it. Rather than being opposed to rationality, emotional labour is a skilled and socially situated practice, which requires a nuanced response to different actors and institutional expectations. Yet, despite their complexity, emotions and emotional practices are often framed as ineffable or natural or expected from healthcare practitioners without explicit training (Moretti and Pronzato 2024).

At the same time, a lot of the literature on emotional labour was focused on questions of authenticity: whether caregivers truly feel attachment, whether they simply comply with rules and whether this type of compliance produces alienation. As Pugh (Pugh 2023, 2024) notes, care work debates have often moved between attachment and emotional labour, focussing on interior emotional states of workers as the aspect to analyse. Although these debates were important for feminist analyses of exploitation and inequality, they also tend to situate emotion within the subjectivity of caregivers. Pugh's concept of connective labour offers a useful addition in this definition. Instead of asking whether emotions are authentic or instrumental, connective labour treats them as relational practices through which recognition is produced and shared (Pugh 2023, 144). It draws attention to processes such as empathic listening, witnessing and acknowledgement, focussing on how meaning is co‐constructed in relational encounters between caregivers and care‐receivers instead of being cantered only around the worker. In this framing, emotions are not only states to be regulated, like in Horschild's perspective, but also relational tools (Pugh 2023, 147): they allow actors to interpret situations, reflect experiences back to others and create a shared understanding of what is taking place.

In digitally mediated and remote care environments, where telecare technologies reconfigure responsibilities, proximity and displace forms of care that are central to care relations, connective labour allows to create trust and connection between different actors in the broader spectrum of these newly emerging socio‐technical arrangements. It is this stabilisation of uncertain and new conditions that makes it possible for emotions to be reframed as a productive resource. Concurrently with the emergence and deployment of telecare and remote care technologies, emotions are undergoing a process of commodification and capitalisation, being strategically reframed as resources to be monitored and deployed (Biss 2024; Han 2017; Steinberg and Figart 1999). As Dowling (2021, 70) argues in her analysis of the contemporary care crisis, the underfunding and marketization of care are accompanied by the emergence of new ideologies that promote care as a form of individual responsibility and emotional resilience, undermining the idea of care as a collective responsibility.

This becomes especially pronounced in digitally mediated and remote care settings, where traditional, in‐person forms of emotional labour are replaced or supplemented by screen‐based interactions. Emotions are increasingly mediated through digital interfaces and through this, technologies reshape what counts as emotional labour, who performs it and under what conditions. As such, they play an active role in the commodification of emotions, contributing to a broader reconfiguration of care as both affective relation and economic activity. The work of Oudshoorn (2011) has been pioneering in the analysis of how these technologies redistribute responsibilities between patients, healthcare professionals and technologies themselves, introducing new actors and reframing new and existing responsibilities. The author highlights how telecare technologies have an explicit political agenda, namely increasing the efficiency of healthcare and making it more affordable (Oudshoorn 2011, 20). This justification presents technological artefacts in terms of tools that can facilitate the delivery of care. This claimed facilitation is, however, multifaceted. On the one hand, it reconfigures established practices, which can be emotional practices or care practices at large (Pols 2012). By doing so, technological implementations do, on the other hand, redistribute agencies and responsibilities, create new actors and carers and new scripts for interdependencies (Moore et al. 2024; Oudshoorn 2011). In this paper, we explore how efficiency claims drive technologically mediated (home)care provision and how emotions are (re)configured as part of this technology‐efficiency narrative. The guiding research question of this paper is: ‘How do algorithmic telecare technologies aimed at increasing efficiency reconfigure care practices, responsibilities and professional roles in emotionally charged healthcare service settings?’

Methods

The aim of this study was to examine how algorithmic telecare technologies aimed at increasing efficiency reconfigure emotional care practices and professional roles. To explore this, the researchers employed an ethnographic approach combining semi‐structured interviews with three months of ethnographic fieldwork at a private remote homecare company in the Netherlands. This allowed the authors to study both how coaches articulate their roles and enact them in practice. In the following, we will first provide some background to the case and the role of the coaches and then describe our research approach.

Employees and Organisational Structure of the Company

At the time when the fieldwork was conducted, the company employed approximately 15–20 coaches, on top of a CEO (Chief Executive Officer), a CTO (Chief Technology Officer), several staff members responsible for HR and technical aspects (3–4 employees), a small team responsible for ‘enthousiasming’ sessions (e.g., demonstrations of the company's technologies to in‐home nurses) and an expanding group of installers who would then implement technologies in clients' homes. All coaches were employed by the company. Their work tasks and arrangements varied from coach to coach: some worked fully remotely and did not physically go to the office, whereas others were present at the office to different degrees (from 4 days a week to one day a week). Their roles included different tasks, ranging from predominantly managerial and coordination tasks to hybrid roles that combined monitoring the technologies, conducting empathy sessions with clients and coordinating with in‐home nurses. The distribution of tasks among coaches was partly formalised but also internally self‐managed within the group. The category of coaches was composed entirely of women that were hired because of the emphatic skills (see below), also indicating how the technological restructuring of homecare practices redistributes emotional labour along existing gendered lines (Bolton 2001; Davies 2003).

Access to Data Field and Data Collection

Data was collected through a combination of semi‐structured interviews and in‐depth ethnographic observations. For three months, the first author, a female PhD‐candidate in her late‐twenties with a background in philosophy and theoretical humanities, conducted observations on the internal working of the company. Observations and interview transcripts were shared with the second author, a female full professor in care sociology with a background in nursing. The observations included shadowing coaches in the office as they monitored technologies, participated in meetings with nurses and interacted with clients in ‘empathy sessions’ through digital platforms or phone calls. The first author also attended two enthausiasmeringssessies (‘enthousiasming sessions’): promotional and educational sessions led by a company representative, where groups of nurses from contracted home care organisations were introduced to new technologies offered by the company. The sessions not only offered in‐depth insight into how the company framed its technological implementations, the kinds of expectations set for the nurses and how technologies were positioned in relation to pre‐existing professional routines and the work of the coaches, but also showcased the company's sales strategies. During the observations, notes were made—also from informal conversations—that were organised in detailed reports, usually within 24 hours. In total around 60 hours of observation were conducted. In addition, six semi‐structured interviews were conducted with participants in the organisation. These included interviews with the company's CEO and CTO and four interviews with coaches (Maren, Nienke, Julia, Sanne and Lotte, all fictional names, yet all coaches identified themselves as female). Coaches were recruited on a voluntary basis after one of them, already in contact with the researcher, shared a call for participation with her colleagues. Interviews lasted between 25 and 65 minute and were conducted via Microsoft Teams and transcribed verbatim.

All data was analysed using Atlas.ti, drawing on a combination of concept‐driven and inductive thematic coding. The initial coding structure was informed by core theoretical themes around the transformation of care, emotional labour and technological mediation. As the analysis progressed, further themes emerged from the data itself, including tensions between automation and empathy, glitches and malfunctionings in the applications of remote technologies and the centrality of the concept of efficiency. Coding was iterative and interpretive, allowing for constant comparison between interview transcripts and fieldnotes.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to interviews or observation. Depending on the situation, consent was provided either in written form (e.g., signed consent sheets) or verbally, especially during informal or spontaneous moments of fieldwork. Participants were informed of the purpose of the research and how the data would be used. All identifying information has been pseudonymised to protect the confidentiality of participants and care has been taken to avoid the inclusion of potentially identifying contextual details in the reporting of findings and results. Ethical approval for the research was obtained through the Ethics Review Committee of Erasmus University Rotterdam, affiliated with the researchers.

Findings

Contact With the Client—New Practices of Empathy

At 14:00, the coach logs into the online platform for the scheduled empathy call. When the interface loads, she can see that the client has already been there a while. She points to the notification and says, ‘I can see they’ve been waiting for fifteen minutes’.

While activating her camera, she opens the client’s file, which is shown under the video window, with some notes from previous conversations. When the video connection starts, she greets the client and says, ‘Sorry to keep you waiting!’.

During the conversation, her eyes look up and down between the camera and the notes on the screen. At one moment, she looks at the file and says, ‘Oh, I remember you mentioned that your daughter was going to visit this weekend. How did that go?’ Throughout the call, the screen is divided into sections, the video window on top and the client record and notes at the bottom and the coach navigates between them.

(Vignette based on ethnographic study)

This vignette shows an example of how empathy in telecare is organised through platform‐based interactions. The digital platform makes notes from past sessions easy to read on the screen during the chat. With this, it mediates the way through which the attentiveness—here translated to empathy—of the coach is enacted. The digital record on screen allows the coach to recall details from previous sessions very easily. When she asks about the daughter's visit, this continuity appears seamless from the side of the client thanks to the prompts on the page.

At the same time, the system makes delays and glitches explicit: the notification that the client has been waiting for 15 minutes is shown on the portal and it quantifies the gap that precedes the interaction. The same infrastructure that makes notes from previous sessions visible also measures and displays waiting time. Efficiency and delay are coexisting in the same socio‐technical arrangement. Instead of presenting telecare as simply a faster or more streamlined way of delivering care, the vignette shows how efficiency acts as a promise embedded in the platform. Immediate access to previous notes, visible time stamps and tightly scheduled sessions are a proof of the optimisation and coordination promised using this device and infrastructure. However, these features do not eliminate frictions, but make them explicit. In this way, technological mediation reshapes the temporal organisation of care: memories (in the form of notes) become instantly accessible and constantly displayed on the screen, sessions become strictly time‐limited and waiting becomes quantifiable and quantified. At the same time, the quantification is not explicitly picked up in the everyday work of coaches; the measurements of time do not automatically translate into performance evaluations, assessment of their work or of clients' wellbeing. It does, however, act as a background nudging in their everyday activities—at least those mediated by platforms. The inherent ambivalence of the role of quantification—on the one side not determining any tangible effects on their job, but on the other side acting as a veiled reminder of the standardisation of practices—suggests that the power of quantifying the client interaction lies precisely in its ambiguity. It is neither fully prescriptive nor fully absent, but it does at once help reshape the perception and enactment of these new labour practices.

Remote health technologies and telecare specifically, displace practices of care (Oudshoorn 2011; Pols 2012). In the previous vignette, the displacement and replacement happen mostly on a temporal level, with time being stretched out, thinned or commodified according to the needs and will of the company. The displacement of care, however, reconfigures practices also at a physical level: in telemedicine, mainly bodily expressions of empathy (e.g., sustained eye contact, touch, posture) are diminished or completely absent, reshaping how empathy is communicated. The screen and the internet connection mediate interactions, often disrupting natural eye contact, limiting the ability to read subtle facial expressions or gestures. Technogeographies of care (Oudshoorn 2011) are therefore reconfigured not only in physical spaces (the clinic, the home), but also in the geographies of the body and subsequently practices of emotions. One of the coaches, who is also in charge of training new coaches who are hired by the company, explains:

We have a specific training that teaches coaches how to convey or react to emotions, not a communications training or handle your emotions training, but more… What you [interviewer] are doing, the active listening, the ‘mmh‐mmh’ and the nodding. […] You don't want to create a status difference looking up or looking down. And when I actually want to convey something important or someone is telling me something very… yeah, that's very, very close to home, then I look into the camera directly to make to make them feel that I'm looking directly at them. Even though I'm not because I can't see your face right now, I'm looking at my camera. [laughs] So that's a that's a big difference in how you use video conferencing in showing emotions.

(Coach Lotte—interview)

Showing emotions—using empathy as one example—becomes then a technologically‐mediated performance: in a 15‐minute session, the coaches need to remember to look directly at the camera, to utter active listening sounds loud enough so that the microphone can pick them up. The emotional and connective labour that comes with the profession—and its physical and material exteriorisation—assumes a consequential, almost means‐to‐end characterisation. Another coach, when talking about emotions during coaching sessions, states:

Because I think you can be emotional without too much personal investing. It's something you have to do to reach to people. And you can make a connection. It's not something that is unprofessional. I'm like the person who would hug a person, you know? […] But this is distance, so you cannot hug them. But sometimes I can and let my emotions, so it has happened that I had a tear in my eye and the client could see, it's OK, it's allowed, why shouldn't they see it?

(Coach Julia—interview)

Here it becomes explicit how, through a narration that almost sounds like a script that can be followed, emotions are described as assets to be managed and used in order to reach a specific outcome: whether it's the bonding with the client, potential empathising, if ‘used right’ a certain display of emotions can create a certain emotional attunement with the client on the other side of the screen. It is interesting to note how the coach navigates their own emotional attunements: it can be the case that being ‘too emotional’ (for instance hugging, putting a hand on a shoulder) can pass as unprofessional. The coach, in this case, highlights that how they feel it is actually the opposite: emotional displays—such as the tear in the eye—are supposed to be shown in order to emotionally connect with the client. Hence, the value does not necessarily lie in the authenticity of feelings and gestures or in the connection that they can achieve between two or more actors, but more so in its effectiveness in reaching a certain result, as the notion of connective labour by Pugh (2023, 2024) points out.

In another observation, the ‘15 minutes of empathy’ takes an unexpected form in the session between client Marjolein and coach Nynke. Marjolein sits in her chair in a barely furnished room, following along as Nynke, alone in the office, leads a series of light physical exercises—rotating her arms, her neck, shifting her body from left to right—all visible through the video call. The coach marks the end of each exercise and the beginning of the next and Marjolein moves along without speaking. ‘This is how we do the sessions with Marjolein’, Nynke explains afterwards. The session became like this gradually: Marjolein wasn't particularly interested in talking to the coach in the beginning and physical (online) activity opened a space that conversation hadn't allowed for. What might appear at first as an absence of care becomes a subtle choreography of attunement, where empathy is not communicated through words but through mirroring an activity between two bodies. Afterwards, Nynke reflects that since incorporating these sessions, Marjolein had begun to open up more: bodily exercises, even when mediated by the screen, served here as a foundation to then build emotional connection. Empathy thus emerges here not as the goal of the interaction, but as a by‐product of a task‐oriented structure. In Pugh's (2023) terms, the emotional connection between coach and client functions as a sense‐making tool that opens up moments of recognition in scenarios not conventionally defined as care. What is new is not the activity itself, but the fact that it is provided as a packaged service: the company determines what counts as care and physical exercise becomes an activity that can potentially lead to emotional support. Anything the client needs (or asks for) is tried to be accommodated and what presents itself as tailored, personalised care turns out to fit neatly within the 15‐minute slots that allow the service to be profitable.

Instruments and Data—The Role of Tools and Technology

The empathy and emotional connection that are created between coaches and clients happen in technologically mediated environments. During one of the first observations, coach Lotte explains that the service offered by the company is that of ‘nursing intervention’: healthcare organisations approach the company and subsequently, after the nurses have received training from coaches and mentors, the nurses decide which type of service (technological tools, empathy sessions or both) each client will receive. During his interview, the CTO reminds us that every tool introduced among those offered by the company needs to be in line with the needs of nurses. Whether it's a fall detector sensor, a pill case with an opening sensor or a sleep tracker watch, the technologies should fulfil the purpose of reducing or completely eliminating in‐person caregiving from nurses. All of these tools, however, generate other types of workloads, displacing tasks rather than eliminating them. This is particularly evident in the case of the sleep‐tracking watch, which is designed to monitor clients during the night and flag irregularities through a simple interface: green, orange or red dots on the screen (in Dutch: bolletjes). The system relies heavily on the supervision of coaches, since they are scheduled into ‘monitoring shifts’ (bolletjes shifts) during which they are responsible for reviewing every orange and red signal—and, if deemed necessary, call the client. They interpret whether the signal points to an actual emergency or a technical malfunction, such as for instance when a client forgets to wear the device, leaving it to show 15 hours of (fake) inactivity on their bedside table. Interestingly, most alerts result from malfunctions, not actual concerns—introducing layers of ambiguity.

A similar pattern emerges in the case of the alarm station (alarmknop) that comes with the sleep‐tracking watch. Designed as a safety device that alerts others in case of an emergency, the alarm station also requires regular charging. When the battery is low, the signal is sent to the coaches. One coach, Sanne, has the task of responding to these alerts. One or two mornings a week, she dedicates part of her time to calling clients to inform them that their alarm station needs recharging. These days, a large part of Sanne's morning is spent repeating the same script over and over again: ‘Hello, it's coach Sanne, from the telecare company. The battery of your alarm station is low, could you please recharge it?’. This sentence might be spoken twenty or more times in just a few hours. Although some clients understand the message right away, many do not. Sometimes, they ask again who is calling or they don't know what the ‘alarm station’ is. In these cases, the coach repeats the script, sometimes several times in a single call. Clients occasionally tell her that family members will take care of it on their behalf. What appears as a mechanical task for the coach is in practice a highly repetitive and often uncertain process, where miscommunication, different levels of technical literacy and the need to involve informal caretakers come into play. Hence, rather than removing work from the system, the device introduces new layers of tasks that must be implemented into the coaches' workload. These new responsibilities are essential for keeping the technological infrastructure functioning smoothly. The appearance of technologies working smoothly and self‐sufficiency is in actuality maintained only through ongoing, low‐level intervention by coaches, who take on the constant repair and update tasks needed to keep the system running (Carboni et al. 2023). Like in the case of the monitoring shifts, the technology generates a new kind of work: less about direct care and more about supporting the conditions under which technologically mediated care can take place. From the perspective of coaches, this type of work is what allows them to then perform the most fulfiling part of their job, namely the empathy sessions. Rather than perceiving technical work as ‘cold’ and empathy sessions as ‘warm’ (Pols 2012), coaches view this part of their work as a necessary dimension of care work—even though it does not constitute what then shapes their professional identity or fulfilment. Interestingly, they do not question or resist the ‘technological fix’ narrative that drives the company and frames this distinction, allowing the division that is created to remain unproblematised in practice.

Neatly Packaged Emotions

It is not only digital technologies that are instrumentalized in this way. As coach Julia's reflection reveals, emotions themselves can also take on the character of tools:

We are all people person with big hearts, so we always give a little more than the most people usually do in life. So and then I have a lot of clients, which I would say the… they deserve more than this, even more so, yeah… You give what you get or… No, they get what we give. And I put a lot of emotion in my work, I use it, it's my tool.

(Coach Julia—interview)

Although Julia identifies with the idea of being someone with a ‘big heart’, committed to giving clients her full attention and care, her words reflect an interesting tension: emotions are both a genuine expression of care and a kind of instrument. In this way, the qualities that define her as a ‘people's person’ are also shaped by the constraints of the job, turning emotions into a resource to be managed and delivered, similarly to the technological tools the company offers.

Although emotions are framed as resources to be put to work, this instrumentalization generates tensions for coaches expected to embody empathy within tightly scheduled 15‐minute calls. Balancing the narrative of being ‘people's persons’ together with the demands of the company is not always easy and coaches are aware of it, as Julia reflects in her interview:

And I think [the CEO] thought of it like really this coffee meetings. That's how we started it. And of course this also has grown in the in the last two years and he just made a business model, you know? If a healthcare worker should go to a person to do his wound care. […] And I'm calling like the 10 minutes, like, ‘hey, how are you doing? What happens? Oh is it good/bad/what?’ So then it's a business model! If you can do it in like the 15 minutes, yes, then you can make you have money out of it. And because yeah, you also have to get the money for it. But it is tight, it's really tight and the time and it's really a challenge to always fit in, so that you have enough time for everything. So at the beginning when I saw it I was like no, this is way too little time to do this, but you find your way also in that.

(Coach Julia—interview)

There is a palpable tension between what Julia would like to achieve—the well‐being of her clients, the emotional connection—and the structural limitations imposed by the business model, by the 15 minutes and the physical distance mediated by the screen. Although the coach speaks about her 15‐minute sessions describing them as moments of connection and care, she also acknowledges the structural constraints, noting that ‘it's a business model’ and that she has to ‘find her way with that’. Her reflection highlights an inner tension in the role: although empathy and emotional presence are framed as spontaneous expressions of a ‘big heart’, they are also the very product being sold. Interestingly, in the observed practice, this difficulty rarely emerges as an explicit problem. Even when coaches stretch the 15‐minute slots (e.g., allocating more time to one client and less to another), their negotiations remain embedded within the company's narrative instead of challenging it. The wiggle room they allow themselves stretches only insofar as the preassigned boundaries allow. The ambivalence that emerges in retrospective reflections becomes a manageable tension absorbed within the corporate framework itself.

It's also the case of coach Maren who, during her interview, firstly tells me that she's really happy that thanks to her job she can ‘make time and get her time to talk to people’. Later in the interview, however, she says that she barely has any clients with whom she has empathy sessions and that the majority of her work is now dedicated to monitoring shifts, where she calls clients to check on their technological devices and medications. Again, like in the case of Julia, a quiet dissonance emerges between the ideal of relational care that coaches want to achieve and the commercial aim of the company.

This dissonance, however, seems to dissipate in the interview with the CEO of the company. Since a couple of coaches had talked about the 15 minute empathy sessions in terms of ‘coffee breaks’ with a friend, the first author brings it up in the interview, in order to further understand where this specific timeslot came from. The tension that emerged in the interviews with the coaches—about how to balance empathy with a client, the difficulty of accepting that a certain amount of time wouldn't be enough—here disappears:

But it it's, yeah, but it's not just 15 minutes. It's… it's mathematics [laughs]. And it's like, OK, we say that one coach can serve between 40 and 60 clients. I think then they are really connected to you, because everybody has their, regulars, so it's not that one, that one… So they have their regular clients. If you calculate it, it's a working month. So many clients divided by… Then you have an average of 15 minutes per client.

(CEO—interview)

Here, the commercial aim of the service is made explicit: emotions—delivered in the 15 minute calls—are no longer a byproduct or the consequence of being ‘people with big hearts’, but the very product being packaged and sold. Unlike in the coaches' accounts, there is no tension, only an explicit articulation of empathy as a strategic market offer. He describes them as a matter of simple ‘mathematics’: the number of clients divided by the number of available coaches. In this quote, the emotional and connective labour done by the coaches are reduced to a calculable resource to be distributed efficiently. His words become a reminder of the fact that, despite the compelling narratives surrounding care and connection, this work is ultimately part of a commercial service. Although for coaches empathy sessions become the central part of the way they define their professional identity—and for many the major reason why they chose to take up the job—in both the words of the CEO and the company strategy, the sessions are put in place simply to fill the emotional gap created by the introduction of digital technologies. They are one of the offers among many within the company's broader portfolio of technological tools, designed not primarily for emotional connection, but to function as a nursing intervention, ultimately aimed at alleviating the burden on professional nurses in healthcare. In this context, the coach becomes just one node in a larger techno‐commercial infrastructure, where both emotions and technological automation are deployed to make (health)care more efficient.

Happy Coaches—The Company's Story and the Commercial Aspect of Emotions

During the interviews and informal talks, most coaches speak with remarkable enthusiasm about their job. They describe their work as meaningful, often highlighting the small emotional connections they manage to create despite the distance and the boundaries forced by the 15‐minute sessions. Coach Julia starts crying during one of the interviews, stating that being there for people who are lonely, unhappy, feeling left out or like they don't matter is ‘amazing and incredible’. For many of the coaches, the role is not just a job: it is a source of fulfilment, purpose and a newly found relationship to work. This emotional investment can be understood not only as a reflection of the meaningful relationships they form with clients, but also as stemming from the personal circumstances that led them to this role. Some coaches arrived at this position after different types of impairments that made it impossible for them to continue in their former careers. For instance the case of Maren, who is younger than the other coaches and who explains that after completing her nursing studies she couldn't pursue her career as a nurse due to a physical impairment. The coaching job at the company allowed her to still perform a caring profession, whereas at the same time adapting to her physical limitations. Another case is the one of Coach Julia, who—during her interview—explains that in her previous career she had to deal with psychiatric patients and her job had become straining on her body as well. Being in her 40s and coming out of a burnout, she found in this new coaching career a way to re‐enter the care sector on her own terms. These stories are not isolated cases: they reflect a broader hiring attitude that values life experiences and emotional skills over formal healthcare training and credentials. As the CEO points out in his interview:

We said with coaches, there are two things most important: you have to be an empathic person, so it's really about personality. And in the beginning and now it's a little bit broader, I want people who have been or a teacher or worked in a bar or restaurant. Because then you are known to hospitality, if you work in in a bar restaurant, especially bars. You're used to listen to people, you are service driven. You know how to work hard, strange hours. So we really looked at what do you need in a person to help other persons as a… as a human being.

The thing is, I said, when we started: I don't want people with a background in healthcare. I don't want nurses. I don't want medical trained personnel. I don't want scientific people. Because they want to take care of somebody and control and hospitalise them.

(CEO—interview)

The CEO's words reflect the idea that the openness to emotional connection—which can be cultivated through service jobs open to the public, such as hospitality or teaching—is more important than formal healthcare training. The CEO explicitly mentions that, in the beginning, they avoided hiring people with a healthcare and academic background, since that would imply a ‘hospitalisation of the client’. His statement also carries an undertone against professional elites: by rejecting the need for formally trained healthcare staff, care is framed as something that should not be monopolised by those who possess the right credentials, but rather as a practice open to everyone, no matter the formal expertise. Formal healthcare training, in this view, almost comes at a clash with empathetic bonding, with ‘helping others as a human being’. The same idea is found in the interview with coach Julia, who says:

It touches me that I'm allowed to do what I want to do for real: be there for persons who are lonely, who are unhappy, who are feeling left out, who are feeling that they don't matter, you know? And all I have to do is just be there and just listen and then give the give somebody the feeling that they really matter. And it's possible through the computer, it’s incredible! So this, […] it works! You can a big fat emotional emotional connection, maybe more, because people are more open.

[…] People person is more important than to have a big fat degree here. You have to be social. You have to be feeling, you have to be a, yeah… A big heart.

(Coach Julia—interview)

This emphasis on being a ‘people's person’, on just having ‘a big heart’ rather than having formal training (‘a big fat degree’) is part of a broader narrative about what counts as professional in healthcare and about new professional paths that are emerging through and thanks to technological advancements and telecare. It reflects a shift in which new roles are emerging and different forms of expertise, such as empathy, intimacy and personal investment, are increasingly valued. Yet, Julia's genuine enthusiasm for her role—her idea that emotional connection is achievable and sufficient through the computer, that it requires just her personal investment—aligns closely with the narrative of the company. She finds deep fulfilment in identifying with it, with the possibility that meaningful care can emerge from her ‘big heart’. The tension, however, lies elsewhere, namely in the gap between this compelling story and the structural constraints that shape what their emotional work actually entails in practice.

Discussion

A red thread running through the empirical material is the efficiency narrative that shapes both the work practices of coaches and the ways in which emotions are managed within the company's telecare system. From calling clients to remind them to charge their alarm stations, to managing Wi‐Fi connection or more general technical failures during calls, to interpreting unclear alerts from malfunctioning sleep trackers, coaches are constantly mending both the technical infrastructure and the narrative of smooth, efficient care underneath it. Although the company promotes a vision of technology‐enabled support, these everyday glitches expose a practice where efficiency is not automated, but constantly performed through behind‐the‐scenes human labour. In this sense, the company wanting to pursue efficiency echoes what Alexander has shown in her historical analysis: efficiency has long depended not simply on technological progress, but on the reinterpretation of pre‐existing concepts and on the redistribution and reframing of labour (Alexander 2008, 2009). What appears as frictionless, technology‐driven care is in fact the outcome of ongoing, carefully managed human repair work. What is marketed as ‘more’ through telecare—more presence, more responsiveness, more coverage for more patients and clients—is only achievable because human workers absorb the glitches and breakings that automation cannot account for. Technologies that are supposed to save time or resources instead create new dependencies on human repair work, making care more fragmented and more demanding at an infrastructural level. This entrepreneurial discourse, however, reframes these dependencies as opportunities instead of drawbacks. The CEO, for example, stresses that the challenge is not a shortage of personnel as such, but that care is restricted to formally trained professionals. Although he does not literally describe them in these terms, his argument carries what can be interpreted as an anti‐elitist undertone: by rejecting the monopoly of trained professionals, he presents care as something that should be open to anyone who can ‘help other people as a human being’, provided that the right technological infrastructure is in place to help. In this way, the additional human labour generated by the system becomes part of the solution rather than the problem.

Yet, to frame these dynamics only in terms of failure or exploitation risks overlooking the complexities that characterise care in digitally mediated environments. As scholars such as Oudshoorn (2011) and Pols (2012) have shown, telecare does not simply replace traditional forms of care but reconfigures them: redistributing tasks, reshaping roles and transforming the spaces and geographies in which care takes place. It is not that efficiency is inherently problematic or that emotions are unambiguously good. The 15‐minute calls, for example, are not devoid of care. Instead, they represent what is realistically possible within the limitations of time, labour and available resources offered by the company to the coaches. Coaches find ways to be present, to listen and to convey warmth and closeness, even within these structured formats. Their emotional labour, as Moretti and Pronzato (2024) also illustrate in the context of digital platforms, involves balancing scripted routines with spontaneous expressions of empathy. As Pugh (2023, 142) highlights, the emotional practices of the coaches offer both closeness to clients, but at the same time provide a sense‐making tool for coaches to narrate their experience. Coach Julia's words, her necessity to also ‘find her way with that’ when talking about how short her meetings are with clients point to this ambivalence: although on the one hand, emotions are confined to fifteen‐minutes time slots, on the other hand they become a reflexive tool through which coaches make sense of the reconfiguration of care work and they can make sense of their professional identity. The same goes Coach Julia describes her use of emotions as a tool: if on one side this description can have a utilitarian undertone—reducing emotions to something to be used, another perspective can be found again in Pugh's work (2023). Emotions are a tool not as mere instruments, but as part of the relational infrastructure built around the entanglement created among coaches, clients and technologies (Pugh 2023, 157). These tensions—between emotional connection and time pressure, between being ‘people with big hearts’ and working at a distance within tightly scheduled slots—are not signs of dysfunction per se. They are, however, symptomatic of (health)care in transformation. Emotional and connective labour do not disappear in the context of telecare: they become rearticulated. As Pols (2012) argues, care at a distance is not less care, but different care. What emerges in this case is not just the adaptation of care to technology, but the emergence of new care workers, new practices and new frictions and glitches. The work of coaches does not simply fill the gaps left by automation: it becomes a central site where new care is performed, maintained and reshaped. As care is redistributed and displaced across digital infrastructures and new work categories, we witness the emergence of hybrid forms of caring work. These changes are not inherently negative or positive, but they do raise critical questions about how and what type of care is valued, measured and sustained in contexts of scarce resources, mediated by technologies.

It is however important to note that emotions remain a relevant and even central selling point for the company, an unusual case in the context of healthcare technologies, which are often positioned in opposition to emotional, human‐centred care (Pols 2012). The company actively markets empathy and relational connection as key features of their telecare services, highlighting the coaches' ‘big hearts’ as what distinguishes their offer. This emphasis on emotions shapes how coaches understand and perform their work. They sustain a narrative that centres their emotional and connective labour, even within highly constrained formats. This narrative functions as a kind of self‐fulfiling prophecy: although coaches recognise the structural limitations and pressures embedded in their work, they continually return to the importance of ‘doing enough with what is given’. This helps maintain motivation and professional identity, but at the same time diverts attention from larger systemic changes, reinforcing the boundaries of what is possible within the current model. What emerges from this analysis is ambivalence in how coaches navigate the emotional dimensions of their work, which differs from how care workers' tensions with technology are typically theorised in the literature (Carboni et al. 2025; Lebovitz et al. 2022; Petrakaki et al. 2025). Scholarship on care and technology, particularly Pols's (2012) distinction between ‘cold’ and ‘warm’ care, emphasises how workers often experience technological mediation as a constraint they consciously have to resist or negotiate. In this setting, however, the friction is largely absent from the practice itself. Although the coaches occasionally articulate tensions during interviews—for example Julia acknowledging that she must ‘find her way with’ the business model or moments when the 15 minutes are not enough for certain clients—these tensions rarely emerge as explicit problems. Instead, the coaches absorb this ambivalence within the existing corporate framework. They stretch time here, compress it there, but their solutions remain embedded within the preassigned narrative rather than challenging it. The ambivalence is palpable to the researcher and occasionally acknowledged by the coaches themselves, but it does not emerge as something to be problematised or resisted in practice. The coaches do not critique the technological‐corporate narrative, but they accept it coexist within it with a pragmatic acceptance of the given terms.

In this context, the pragmatic acceptance of this ambiguity becomes itself a mechanism through which marketisation deepens. These dynamics resonate with Dowling's (2021) analysis of the marketisation of the care crisis, where care is increasingly framed and delivered as a set of marketable services shaped by austerity and scarcity of resources. Emotions become a commodity to be managed within efficiency demands and narratives of individualised emotional commitment obscure broader structural pressures and changes. As Dowling (2021) shows, the marketisation of the care crisis works by reframing systemic scarcities and pressures as individualised responsibilities, so that they can be absorbed in everyday routines and not be framed as a responsibility for the whole community. The CEO's directive that coaches provide fifteen‐minute ‘empathy sessions’ is an example for this dynamic. Efficiency here is not simply about streamlining processes, but about calibrating emotional connection into marketable units, allowing the company to promise both compassion and productivity. When placed in a wider perspective, however, these commercial aims reveal a redistribution of responsibility: the imperative of efficiency is put onto coaches who must deliver care within very specific constraints. Building on Oudshoorn's (2011) perspective, it becomes clear that this displacement is not neutral. It redistributes care work and responsibilities across the healthcare system and infrastructure, reshaping what forms of work are made visible or invisible, which (non)expertise is privileged and what counts as valuable in telecare arrangements.

Conclusion

This paper has examined how algorithmic telecare technologies, introduced with the promise of increased efficiency, reconfigure (health)care practices, emotional responsibilities and professional roles in homecare settings. By returning to the research question—How do algorithmic telecare technologies aimed at increasing efficiency reconfigure care practices, responsibilities and professional roles in emotionally charged healthcare service settings?, the analysis shows that what is at stake is not just the presence or absence of empathy, but the transformation of its delivery, distribution and ultimately its meaning. Instead of removing emotions from care, telecare technologies contribute to its redistribution: across people (e.g., from nurses to ‘coaches’), across tasks (e.g., from emotional support to physical exercise) and across systems (e.g., from face‐to‐face interaction to video and phone calls). This contributes to a shift in how emotional practices are experienced and made visible or invisible. Empathy is no longer always enacted spontaneously through verbal interaction or physical presence, but is increasingly performed through structured routines, scripted performances or online co‐presence. These alternative forms of emotional care challenge dominant models of what empathy ‘should’ look like and raise sociological questions about which forms of emotional labour are recognised, rewarded or rendered invisible.

This reconfiguration also raises questions around conventional notions of professionalism. Coaches, who often lack formal healthcare training, are nonetheless required to perform diverse tasks that span from emotional to technical labour, adapting to the changing needs of clients whilst simultaneously working around technological breakdowns or limitations. Coaches are faced with very different tasks and demands—from providing support to managing emotions, to fixing technological breaks and glitches—and this reveals how their daily work is shaped by overlapping responsibilities that are often unacknowledged and difficult to articulate within existing care frameworks.

This research has not only shown how algorithmic telecare technologies automate care: it also reveals how the deployment of these technologies directly or indirectly transforms healthcare's emotional narrative. They reconfigure how empathic encounters are recognised, what their weight is, who is responsible for providing them and what counts as care in technologically mediated environments. It also revealed how the ideals of efficiency and automation, so central to design and promotion of algorithmic technologies in contemporary healthcare settings, are continually strained in practice. From a sociological perspective, these shifts highlight the need to pay more attention not only to what technologies do, but also to the values they privilege in the implementation of remote and homecare: values such as efficiency, professionalism and automation that shape which forms of labour are made visible or rendered invisible and which care workers are seen as important. This ultimately demands reflection on what should be the guiding principles of care and what kind of (health)care society we wish to prioritise.
