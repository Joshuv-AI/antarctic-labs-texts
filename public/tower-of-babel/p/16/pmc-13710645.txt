Dietary and supplemental calcium intake and bone changes during antiresorptive osteoporosis treatment in older women: a longitudinal observational study
Authors: Maria Papageorgiou, Yvan Gugler, Serge Ferrari, René Rizzoli, Philippe Zysset, Emmanuel Biver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCalcium supplements are often prescribed with osteoporosis medications without considering dietary adequacy. In calcium-replete older women, calcium supplements had little effect on the response to antiresorptives, whereas low protein intake blunted their benefits. Calcium supplementation may be unnecessary when intake is adequate, and nutrition should be optimized for osteoporosis treatment.PurposeOsteoporosis medications (OM) are commonly prescribed with calcium supplements (CaS) without considering dietary adequacy. We investigated whether calcium and protein intakes influence the effect of OM on total hip bone mineral density (BMD) and strength in calcium-replete women.MethodsData from 586 women (median age 67 years) from the Geneva Retirees Cohort were analyzed: 101 on menopausal hormone therapy (MHT), 67 on antiresorptives (AR) (bisphosphonates, denosumab or raloxifene), and 418 without OM. Annual changes in total hip BMD, strength, and structure were assessed over a median 3.5 years using 2D/3D-DXA and finite element analysis. Calcium and protein intakes were assessed by food frequency questionnaire; CaS use was recorded at baseline and follow-up.ResultsTotal calcium intake (diet + supplements) was 1503 mg/day; 71% women met recommendations (≥ 1200 mg/day), 51% used CaS, and 70% vitamin D. MHT or AR increased total hip BMD and strength versus no OM. Neither total calcium intake (above or below 1200 mg/day) nor CaS use significantly affected bone outcomes. However, in women with low calcium intake (< 800 mg/day), no difference in changes of BMD or bone strength was observed between women with and without OM in the absence of CaS. In women with protein intake < 0.8 g/kg/day, AR effects were blunted, with reduced improvements in trabecular BMD (P = 0.009) and total hip strength (P = 0.040).ConclusionIn calcium-replete older women, protein intake rather than CaS influences total hip bone changes with OM. These findings question routine CaS in women with adequate calcium intake and emphasize the importance of sufficient protein intake for optimal osteoporosis management.Clinical trial registrationGERICO http://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN11865958Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-08154-8.

Introduction

Current guidelines for the management of osteoporosis emphasize the importance of adequate calcium intake and sufficient vitamin D status as fundamental treatment strategies [1–4]. Given the critical roles of these nutrients in skeletal health by supporting bone mineralization and mitigating secondary hyperparathyroidism and to prevent hypocalcemia with antiresorptive osteoporosis medications, calcium and vitamin D supplements have been systematically included in the pivotal clinical trials of most osteoporotic drugs (bisphosphonates, denosumab, selective estrogen receptor modulators [SERMs], parathyroid hormone [PTH] analogs, or romosozumab) [5–11]. This common inclusion raises questions on whether such medications are effective in the absence of concomitant calcium and vitamin D supplementation. In addition, findings from Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) trials suggested a potential synergy between menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) and calcium/vitamin D supplementation for the prevention of fractures [12].

In routine clinical practice, osteoporosis medications, including MHT, are also commonly prescribed alongside calcium and vitamin D supplements to ensure repletion of these micronutrients, often without a prior/thorough dietary assessment. In Switzerland, adults over 66 years are recommended to consume 1000–1200 mg of calcium daily [13]. Dietary reference values for adults vary from 700 to 1300 mg per day depending on the reference guidelines [14]. Notably, excessive CaS, especially in the context of high total calcium intake, has been associated with adverse outcomes such as gastrointestinal symptoms, nephrolithiasis, and adverse cardiovascular events [15–17].

Beyond micronutrients, protein is the most extensively studied macronutrient in relation to bone health, with well-established anabolic effects, including stimulation of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), enhanced intestinal calcium absorption, and preservation of muscle mass [18–21]. For adults, the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) is 0.8 g of protein per kg of body weight per day with higher intakes reaching even 1.2–1.5 g/kg body weight/day for elderly with or at risk of malnutrition [19]. Conversely, low protein intake has been consistently linked to bone loss and fracture risk, particularly among older individuals [19, 22]. While the independent benefits of osteoporosis treatments and dietary protein on bone health are well documented, the extent to which dietary protein intake influences the skeletal responses to osteoporosis treatments remains poorly understood.

Total hip bone mineral density (BMD) is now recognized as a validated surrogate endpoint for fracture risk reduction in clinical trials of osteoporosis drugs [23]. In addition, 3D-DXA has been used in clinical studies to track trabecular and cortical bone parameter improvements with osteoporosis treatments and to derive femoral strength from a 2D DXA scan [24].

As such, the present longitudinal study aims to investigate whether calcium (diet ± supplements) and protein intakes modify the effects of osteoporosis therapies (with a focus on antiresorptive drugs and MHT) on changes in total hip BMD and strength in a population of older women with high calcium intake.

Methods

Study population

This is secondary analysis of the Geneva Retirees Cohort (GERICO; http://www.isrctn.com/, ISRCTN11865958). The objectives, design, and inclusion/exclusion criteria of the original study have been detailed in previous publications [25, 26]. Briefly, GERICO aimed to evaluate the associations of genetic, musculoskeletal, and lifestyle factors with bone microstructure and fracture risk in a recently retired population living in Geneva area (Switzerland). At baseline (2008–2011), community-based men and women aged 63–67 years old were approached at the time of their retirement by advertisements in the local press, Geneva University Hospitals, or various workplaces. Exclusion criteria included the presence of major medical conditions associated with altered bone health. After the baseline visit, individuals were asked to further participate in two follow-up visits, approximately 3 years apart, conducted during 2012–2014 and 2015–2018. During these visits, musculoskeletal health indicators, metabolic parameters, dietary and other lifestyle factors, and medication use were evaluated. Since musculoskeletal health was the primary outcome of this cohort, information of the use of osteoporosis medications and calcium/vitamin D supplements was collected including details on the type of drugs, date of initiation, and discontinuation. We considered MHT, raloxifene, oral and intravenous bisphosphonates, denosumab, and strontium ranelate. These drugs had to be taken at least 6 months between two DXA assessments (new and continuous users), at anti-osteoporosis doses. Very few patients used teriparatide, and romosozumab was not approved at the time of data collections; therefore, anabolic drugs were not assessed in the present study. Among the 837 women out of 1055 participants in the GERICO cohort, a total of 586 women had available hip BMD measurements at a minimum of two visits, with documented use or no-use of anti-osteoporotic therapy. Of these, 418 were not receiving any osteoporosis-specific medication (OM), 101 were receiving MHT and 67 were treated with other antiresorptive drugs (AR) (supplemental figure). These drugs included bisphosphonates (n = 45), denosumab (n = 11), raloxifene (n = 7), a bisphosphonate–denosumab sequence (n = 2), a raloxifene–bisphosphonate sequence (n = 1), and strontium ranelate (n = 1). The protocol of the GERICO has been approved by the Geneva University Hospitals’ Ethics Committee and participants provided a written consent before any study events. The study is registered as a clinical trial (http://www.isrctn.com/, no. ISRCTN11865958, approval: 21 July 2016).

Dietary assessment

A validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) containing 25 items and designed to assess calcium and protein intakes was used to assess habitual dietary intake over the preceding year at all study visits (adapted from [27]). The questionnaire was administered by trained research staff during a face-to-face interview with the participants. Individuals indicated the frequency they consumed specific food items, and serving sizes were estimated with the help of pictures used in the large multicenter survey (SU.VI.MAX) [28]. The various results were converted into mean daily consumption (grams per day) by multiplying the typical portion sizes (grams) by the consumption frequency for each food and making the appropriate division for the period assessed to obtain daily consumption. Estimates of calcium and protein intakes were based on the Centre d’Information sur la Qualité des Aliments (Information Center on Food Quality, CIQUAL) food composition tables (Ed. Tec&Doc, Lavoisier and INRA, Maison Alfort, France, 1995). The mean values of total calcium and protein intake were calculated using data from all available dietary assessments (baseline + follow-up visits) to minimize within-subject variation in the diet and capture long-term dietary habits over the follow-up period. At the last visit, participants were also asked to provide information about the dosage of the calcium/vitamin D supplements.

Assessment of aBMD, bone microstructure and bone strength

Areal bone mineral density (aBMD) at the lumbar spine and proximal femur (total hip and femoral neck) was measured at baseline and follow-up visits by DXA (Hologic QDR Discovery Instrument; Hologic, Inc.). The CV of repeated bone mineral density (BMD) measurements ranged from 1.0% to 1.6% [29]. 3D-DXA reconstructions of DXA images of the hip were generated using the 3D-Shaper software (version 2.9.0, 3D-Shaper Medical, Barcelona, Spain), to extract the following densitometric and structural parameters of the proximal femur (total hip): volumetric BMD (vBMD) for the total, trabecular and cortical bone compartments, cortical surface BMD (CtsBMD), and cortical thickness (CtTh). Hip bone strength was assessed by finite element analyses in fall configuration as previously described [24].

Assessment of other baseline characteristics

Height (measured using a Harpenden Stadiometer; Holtain Ltd.) and weight (measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using standard electronic scales) were used to calculate BMI (kg/m2). Physical activity was evaluated by a face-to-face questionnaire that uses an inventory of 45 activities to estimate the time spent on usual walking, cycling, stair climbing, organized sports, and recreational activity over the past 12 months. The collected data were converted to physical activity energy expenditure (kilocalories per day) using validated formulas developed in [30]. Established clinical risk factors of fractures were assessed as part of the evaluation of the 10-year probability of fracture using the Fracture Risk Assessment (FRAX) tool (www.shef.ac.uk/FRAX/) [31].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges or percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using nonparametric Mann-Whitney or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous data and χ2 tests for categorical variables. P values < 0.05 were considered to indicate significant statistical differences between groups. All analyses were performed using STATA software, version 18.0 (StataCorp LP).

Total calcium intake (diet + supplements) was 1503 (1079, 1866) mg/day. Four hundred and twelve (71%) of the participants were meeting calcium recommendations (≥ 1200 mg/day), and 82 (14%) of them were consuming < 800 mg Ca/day. Calcium and vitamin D supplements were used by 51% and 70% of women, respectively. Dietary protein intake was 1.06 (0.86, 1.25) g/kg body weight/day and 18% of the participants had intakes < 0.8 g/kg/kg body weight/day.

Women on AR had lower BMI, lower T-scores at both the spine and hip, and higher prevalence of osteoporosis compared to those without OM. Most of them were also taking calcium (81%) and vitamin D (93%) supplements. Women on MHT had lower BMI but higher T-scores at both the spine and hip compared to those without OM. Calcium and vitamin D supplements were taken in 49 and 65% of women with MHT, and 47 and 67% of women without OM, respectively. The distribution of calcium and vitamin D supplementation according to the use of OM is reported in the supplemental table.

Discussion

In this observational study of community-dwelling older women receiving antiresorptive therapy (bisphosphonates, denosumab, or raloxifene) or MHT, CaS was not generally associated with improved bone outcomes, likely because most participants had adequate dietary calcium intake. Modest benefits were observed only among women with low dietary calcium intake (< 800 mg/day) and, to a lesser extent, among those with calcium intake below the recommended level (< 1200 mg/day). These findings do not support the routine use of CaS in women with adequate calcium intake and highlight the importance of assessing dietary calcium intake before prescribing supplementation. In addition, changes in total hip BMD and bone strength associated with antiresorptive therapy were less pronounced in women with low compared with adequate protein intake (< 0.8 vs ≥ 0.8 g/kg body weight/day), with this association being largely driven by the trabecular compartment. These findings further emphasize the importance of adequate nutritional status in optimizing skeletal health in older women.

The vast majority of RCTs exploring the efficacy of osteoporotic drugs have incorporated routine calcium and vitamin D supplementation for all participants [5–11]. Thus, current guidelines for the management of osteoporosis emphasize the importance of achieving and maintaining adequate calcium intake and vitamin D status for treatment [1–4]. Nevertheless, given that excess calcium intake, especially from supplements, may carry risks such as gastrointestinal issues, kidney stones, or cardiovascular concerns, it is suggested that supplementation should not be applied universally, but rather be tailored to individual needs [15–17]. In this work, we demonstrated that CaS may not be necessary for all individuals for achieving the therapeutic benefits of common antiresorptive drugs. Notably, our findings apply to a population with overall adequate to high calcium dietary intake and do not question the benefits of calcium/vitamin D supplementation in malnourished older adults with low calcium intake or specific medical conditions such as malabsorption. In terms of vitamin D, this cohort was repleted, with 70% of women taking vitamin D supplements. A prior analysis of the GERICO cohort showed that 71% of women had vitamin D sufficiency (≥ 50 nmol/l) and only 17% had secondary hyperparathyroidism at baseline [32]. Vitamin D supplementation in women not taking CaS may have attenuated or obscured potential associations between CaS and changes in bone outcomes among women receiving OM, as vitamin D could have enhanced calcium absorption. Meanwhile, individuals with secondary hyperparathyroidism may be more likely to benefit from CaS, beyond women with dietary calcium intake < 800 mg/day. Importantly, the absence of CaS should not be interpreted as evidence of inadequate calcium status in this cohort since women not receiving CaS may nevertheless have adequate calcium absorption and calcium homeostasis. Consequently, our findings should not be generalized to populations with lower calcium intake, vitamin D deficiency, or a higher prevalence of secondary hyperparathyroidism.

In line with our results, some trials with bisphosphonates conducted without concurrent calcium and vitamin D supplementation have reported comparable improvements in BMD and fracture risk reductions to those seen in trials that included such supplements [33–36]. In contrast, Nakamura et al. suggested that compared to denosumab monotherapy, the co-administration of vitamin D and calcium attenuated denosumab-induced hypocalcemia, yielded a more marked reduction in bone turnover markers, and resulted in greater BMD increases, particularly at the level of the hip [37]. It is possible that such differences may be related to the different action mechanisms of bisphosphonates and denosumab or may be explained by differences in study populations (including dietary habits) and supplementation specifications.

While CaS may confer limited benefit for hip bone strength in women with adequate dietary calcium regardless of osteoporosis treatment, those with suboptimal intake appeared to benefit from supplementation either in the absence of antiresorptive drug therapy or while on MHT. MHT is commonly prescribed for alleviating menopausal symptoms [38–40], and it has also been shown to have protective skeletal effects including less pronounced BMD reductions and lower risk of vertebral, non-vertebral, and hip fractures, irrespective of age, falls risk, or baseline FRAX probability [41]. In accordance with our results on hip bone strength, data from the WHI trials showed that although calcium and vitamin D supplements were not statistically associated with benefits for fracture prevention, when co-administered with MHT, they augmented its protective effects against hip fractures [12].

We found that the efficacy of antiresorptive therapies on hip bone strength was influenced more by dietary protein intake, with attenuated changes associated with treatment seen among women with inadequate intakes. Mechanistically, dietary protein has well-documented anabolic effects on bone; these include the stimulation of IGF-1, the promotion of intestinal calcium absorption, and the maintenance of muscle mass [18–21]. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated positive associations between protein intake and BMD at the hip, lumbar spine, and total body, with protein intakes above the RDA explaining 2–4% of the variability in BMD [19]. Furthermore, in older adults, higher protein intakes have been associated with a lower risk of fragility fractures and in some studies, enhanced outcomes following such fractures, including better nutritional status, shorter hospital stays, and a more rapid return to independent living [42–44]. Here, we extend these findings to suggest an understudied nutrient-drug interaction with potential implications for optimizing the management of osteoporosis. To what extent this protein-treatment interaction may be even more pronounced with bone anabolic anti-osteoporotic therapies, such as PTH analogues or sclerostin inhibitors, remains to be investigated.

Our work is strengthened by the assessment of bone health using classical (2D DXA) but also additional techniques (3D-DXA and finite element analyses (FEA)) to assess hip bone strength. The annual loss of hip BMD observed among women not receiving OM (−0.7% per year) was consistent with the expected decline of approximately 0.5% to 1% per year in this age group [45]. Dietary assessments were performed at multiple timepoints over the follow-up period; thus, our estimates are more likely to reflect habitual diet, capture long-term dietary modifications, and reduce within-person variability. Conversely, our analysis is limited to a homogenous sample of women with high calcium intakes, restricting the generalizability of the observed associations to more diverse populations with lower calcium intakes. Women were not specifically recruited based on low BMD, diagnosis of osteoporosis, or history of fragility fractures. Women receiving MHT differed substantially from those receiving antiresorptive therapy and, in many respects, were more similar to women not receiving OM, since MHT was used for many years after menopause. The number of women receiving OM was relatively small, particularly after stratification according to calcium and protein intake, which may have limited statistical power and reduced the precision of some estimates. Our findings apply to antiresorptive drugs but not to anabolic osteoporosis drugs, which were not explored in this study. An analysis according to vitamin D supplements was not possible, as most participants on antiresorptive drugs were taking vitamin D supplements. Imperfect adherence both to supplements and to OM could have diluted observed effects. Although we used a validated FFQ to assess habitual dietary and protein intakes, this is a retrospective dietary assessment method prone to recall bias.

In conclusion, in this population of older women with overall adequate to high calcium intake, CaS was not consistently associated with more favorable bone outcomes among women receiving antiresorptive therapy. In contrast, some analyses suggested that adequate protein intake may be associated with more favorable bone parameters in women receiving antiresorptive treatment, although these findings were not consistent across all outcomes. Taken together, these results highlight the importance of considering nutritional status when evaluating osteoporosis treatment and support further investigation of the potential role of calcium and protein intake in modulating skeletal outcomes during antiresorptive therapy. These conclusions should not be extrapolated to populations with low calcium intake, malabsorption, institutionalized older adults, or those with clear nutritional deficiencies.
