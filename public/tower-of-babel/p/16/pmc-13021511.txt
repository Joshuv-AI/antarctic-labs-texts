A framework for estimating manure nitrogen balance and recycling potential for current and future conditions in the USA
Authors: Yanyu Wang, Xin Zhang, Sheri Spiegal, Eric A Davidson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractManure recycling can ameliorate pollution and fertilizer demand, but varying assumptions about recoverable manure nutrients and crop requirements complicate understanding of manure recycling potential. Using nitrogen (N) in the contiguous USA as a case study, we applied methods from six studies to compare manure N balance estimates (recoverable manure minus crop demand). We then developed a framework to assess both current and future potentials of manure recycling. The current balance in the USA is −13.3 ± 1.1 TgN yr−1, reflecting large crop demand currently met with synthetic fertilizer. Improved adoption of current manure management technologies could decrease this deficit by 5%, while future technologies could enable another 21% reduction. However, new manure N application should be reduced by 33–36% to avoid phosphorous (P) overapplication. Improved crop N efficiency could decrease the deficit by 27%. Applying this framework at county level demonstrates variable regional opportunities to improve manure recycling.Subject terms: Environmental impact, Sustainability, Element cycles

Main

Livestock production has intensified to meet the growing global demand for meat and dairy1. In the USA, current manure N production is estimated at 5–8 Tg yr−1(refs. 2–6) and only 15-31% is returned back to cropland2–6. Ineffective use of N in animal wastes contributes to air and water pollution, climate change and potential risk of diseases transmission7–9. Animal waste N is also a missed opportunity for food production: recycling manure back to cropland can help decarbonize the agriculture sector by substituting part of the fossil-fuel intensive synthetic fertilizer and also adding benefits for soil health10,11.

Despite potential benefits, manure recycling has become increasingly challenging. Crop farms and feeding operations have become specialized and, in some cases, located farther apart12,13. Substantial manure losses occur through volatilization, spillage, runoff and leaching during collection, storage, treatment and transfer with additional losses arising from in-field application practices14. Evaluating the recoverability of manure nutrients and how they match with the crop nutrient requirements within a defined spatial boundary is complex, but critical to inform agroecosystem management.

Calculations of ‘manure nutrient balance’, a core indicator, although variably named, have been proposed and widely used in literature3,15–19 (Table 1). All calculations of manure nutrient balance involve finding the difference between (1) recoverable nutrients from organic waste sources such as livestock manure, human excreta, food waste and sewage sludge and (2) the nutrient inputs required to achieve expected crop yield given current or improved management practices (hereafter called ‘crop nutrient demand’). A positive balance implies an oversupply of livestock manure nutrients that are prone to being lost to the air and water, whereas a negative balance implies that nutrients from livestock manure alone would not be sufficient to meet all crop nutrient demand19.

Assumptions and coefficients used for quantifying recoverable manure nutrient and crop nutrient demand vary among studies, partly reflecting differences in regional agricultural systems, technologies and environmental conditions. However, much of the variance arises from inconsistent definitions of manure nutrient recoverability and crop nutrient requirements. Such discrepancies, if not clearly communicated, can hinder meaningful comparisons across studies and may lead to discrepancies, confusion and potentially poorly informed policy decisions.

To address this gap, we first compare published manure N balance estimates by applying methods from six studies to the same dataset for the contiguous USA. We then propose a framework to calculate the manure N balance through clearly defined and adaptable metrics for recoverable manure N and crop N demand, incorporating both established and new methods. Since the imbalance between manure N and P relative to crop demand can cause excess P accumulation and water pollution risk, we extended the framework to account for manure P and crop P demand. Using the contiguous USA as a test case, we apply the framework at national and county scales and evaluate improvement potential under better manure and crop management to identify policy and management priorities.

Results

Divergent estimates in evaluating manure recycling

On the basis of the methods from six highly cited studies, estimates of manure N balance for the contiguous USA range from −21.5 ± 1.1 Tg yr−1 to −2.6 ± 1.4 Tg yr−1 (Table 1 and Fig. 1). The large discrepancies among estimates stem from differences in how each study defined and calculated their metrics of crop N demand and recoverable manure N (Tables 2 and 3).

Estimates of crop N demand metric ranged from 10.5 ± 1.3 Tg yr−1 to 23.0 ± 1.1 Tg yr−1 (Fig. 1), due to differing assumptions regarding crop nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) and regarding whether manure would probably replace some N inputs, such as those from biological N fixation (BNF), atmospheric deposition and irrigation water. The method of ref. 3 produced the highest estimate (23.0 ± 1.1 Tg yr−1), as it uses harvested crop N products and average crop NUE of 70%, but does not subtract N inputs from BNF, atmospheric deposition and irrigation water. The methods of two other studies17,18 used harvested crop N products as a proxy for estimating crop N demand with an underlying assumption of a 100% crop NUE, resulting in an estimate of 15.7 ± 1.4 TgN yr−1. In the remaining studies, crop N demand was calculated as required cropland N inputs minus those from BNF, atmospheric deposition and irrigation water. Specifically, one study16 applied a universal crop NUE of 50% to harvested N products. We updated this method by applying current crop-specific NUE in the USA from ref. 20, resulting in 14.1 ± 1.1 TgN yr−1 (Table 1 and Fig. 1). Approaches given in refs. 15,19 reflect crop N demand under improved management conditions. We applied these methods using target crop NUE of 80% (ref. 21), yielding the US crop demand at 10.5 ± 1.3 TgN yr−1 (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

Estimates of recoverable manure N in the USA ranged from 1.5 ± 0.2 TgN yr−1 to 7.9 ± 0.5 TgN yr−1 (Fig. 1). Three studies15,17,18 considered N in excreted manure from all livestock (on pasture and in confinement) as recoverable, yielding an estimate of 7.9 ± 0.5 TgN yr−1 (Table 1 and Fig. 1). By contrast, another study19 excluded ammonia (NH3) loss during manure storage from the estimate of recoverable manure N, resulting in an estimate of 6.0 ± 0.4 TgN yr−1 (Table 1 and Fig. 1). Others3,16, focused on recoverable manure N only from confined livestock considering challenges of collecting manure on pasture. The methods of these last two studies accounted for additional N losses from manure, both during handling beyond storage (for example, collection stage) and through non-NH3-volatilization pathways (for example, runoff and leaching from spillage), resulting in recoverable manure estimates at 1.5 ± 0.2 TgN yr−1 (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

A framework for assessing improvement potential of manure recycling

The large discrepancies found in manure N balance estimates from existing literature underscore the need for transparency and greater comparability to reliably assess manure recycling potential and to reveal priority areas for related management and policy efforts. Building on the rationale and metrics of the preceding studies, we present a framework (Fig. 2a,b) for calculating the manure N balance indicator at a given geographic scale.

The framework includes five key metrics, three of which relate to the manure side of the balance equation (Fig. 2a): (1) currently applied manure N (ref. 22), (2) potentially recoverable manure N given current N-efficient technologies23 and (3) total manure N excreted by confined livestock. The difference among these metrics reflects how manure recycling back to cropland may be enhanced: (1) increasing adoption of current manure handling and management technologies (M1N), such as those that reduce nutrient losses during collection, transfer, storage and treatment, thereby realizing the full potential of recoverable manure, as described by the existing practices23 and (2) developing and applying new technologies and policy efforts to recover and use nearly all the manure N excreted by confined livestock (M2N). Considering the risks of applying excess manure P, we defined M1N∗, which constrains recoverable manure N under current technologies (M1N) such that the accompanying manure P does not exceed the amount of P fertilizer currently in use in each county of the USA. A similar constraint is applied as M2N∗ (Fig. 2c).

The framework also includes two metrics for the crop N demand (Fig. 2b): (1) manure-replaceable crop N demand with current crop NUE; and (2) manure-replaceable crop N demand with target crop NUE (for example, 80%, as recommended by the European Nitrogen Expert Panel21, is used in this study; Fig. 2b). ‘Manure-replaceable’ refers to crop N inputs that can be met using recoverable manure, after subtracting contributions from BNF, atmospheric deposition and irrigation (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). The difference (iNUE) between the two metrics reflects the potential to reduce N inputs through more efficient N use in cropping systems (Fig. 2b,c).

Aligning increased manure recovery (M1N and M2N) with enhanced crop NUE (iNUE), demonstrates the combined potential of manure and crop management to reduce fertilizer demand and manure N losses (Fig. 2c).

Applying the framework to identify opportunities and priorities

Applying the framework to the contiguous USA, we find that the current status of manure N balance is at −13.3 ± 1.1 Tg yr−1 (Figs. 2c and 3a), marking the difference between manure currently applied on cropland (0.8 TgN yr−1) and current crop demand assuming current level of NUE (14.1 ± 1.1 TgN yr−1). This result falls near the midpoint of estimates based on previously published methods (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

With full diffusion of current recovery technologies, manure N recovery could increase by 0.7 ± 0.2 TgN yr−1 (corresponding to M1N, Fig. 3a), decreasing the deficit from −13.3 ± 1.1 TgN yr to −12.6 ± 1.1 TgN yr−1. Diffusion of future technologies and socioeconomic changes could further increase manure recovery by up to 2.8 ± 0.4 TgN yr−1 (M2N, Fig. 3a), reaching a manure N balance of −9.8 ± 1.2 Tg yr−1 (Figs. 2c and 3a). However, to avoid risks of overapplying P, 36% of M1N and 33% of M2N could not be applied on cropland or would require the removal and reuse of P from the manure elsewhere with emerging technologies for P removal24–26 (Fig. 3a). Improving current crop NUE to a target of 80% could lower crop N demand by 3.6 ± 1.2 TgN yr−1 (iNUE, Fig. 3a), further decreasing manure deficit to −6.2 ± 1.3 TgN yr−1.

Overall, increasing manure recycling (M1N and M2N) and improving crop NUE (iNUE) could collectively reduce synthetic N fertilizer inputs by more than 50%, from current 12.6 to 5.5 ± 1.4 TgN yr−1 (Fig. 3b). However, approximately 1.2 Tg of additional recycled manure N may risk P overapplication and require adopting technologies that enable P removal from manure (Fig. 3b).

Regional manure recycling priorities

Manure management faces localized challenges that scale to national concerns, necessitating a multiscalar understanding to enhance recycling15,16,27. Applying our framework at the county level across the USA, we assess current status and future recycling potential (Fig. 4a–d) and classify local priorities into five categories (Fig. 4e).

Only 51 of 3,107 counties show a positive manure N balance, where currently applied manure N exceeds the crop N demand of the county. We categorize these counties as having excess manure (Fig. 4e). Kershaw County in South Carolina has intensive turkey production, but limited crop cultivation (Supplementary Table 2). As a result, the amount of manure currently applied exceeds crop demand by 0.3 ± 0.03 GgN yr−1, contributing to risks for N losses to air and water (Fig. 5a). Interventions to improve manure N recovery (M1N, M2N) and crop NUE (iNUE) can further increase manure availability, but in this case where the manure N balance is already positive, this strategy needs to be accompanied with increasing the transport of excess manure N to counties that need additional manure N via ‘manureshed’ management16. On a related note, countries (for example, Denmark and the Netherlands) and municipalities with similar excess manure issues have considered capping livestock numbers on the basis of the surrounding cropland availability28,29.

Despite current manure N deficits, approximately 433 counties could raise their manure N balance from a current negative value to zero or above through improved manure recycling alone or in combination with enhanced crop NUE. We classify these counties as ‘potential to reach balance’, split among categories two to four (Fig. 4e). The second category is exemplified by Carroll County, Arkansas, where the agriculture of the county is dominated by poultry production with some hay cultivation. Closing the gap with existing technologies (M1N) would be sufficient to meet crop N demand and generating a surplus for export (Fig. 5c,d). However, M1N would be greatly reduced by 4.9 GgN yr−1 when considering the P excess risk due to the low N:P ratio of poultry manure (Fig. 5c,d). Maricopa County, Arizona (the third category example), hosts a large dairy industry, with sizeable crop N demand driven by animal feeds, including hay, corn silage and wheat. A positive manure balance would require manure recovery beyond what is thought to be feasible with existing technologies (Fig. 5e,f). In the case of this county, the high P fertilizer use and predominance of cattle-derived manure with a high N:P ratio (5.8), offer great potential for manure application. The fourth category is exemplified by Scott County, Kansas, where intensive beef cattle production coincides with massive crop production but low crop NUE (51%, Supplementary Table 5). Improving crop NUE to 80% could reduce crop demand by 6.8 ± 2.0 GgN yr−1, enabling local manure to meet crop N demand (Fig. 5g,h). Achieving balance between recoverable manure N and crop N demand requires joint efforts to enhance both manure and crop N management.

Finally, in the fifth category, 84% of all counties, mostly in the Northern Plains, Midwest and Prairie Gateway would continue to experience manure deficit (require synthetic fertilizers inputs) after improving manure N recovery (M1N and M2N) and crop NUE (iNUE) (Fig. 4e). This group and exemplified by Mountrail County, North Dakota, where wheat and sorghum are widely produced relative to limited animal production (Fig. 5i,j and Supplementary Table 6). Long-term solutions may include implementing policies to shift animal production towards crop-dominant areas15,30 and developing transportation technologies to enable manure importation from more distant regions with excess manure.

Discussion

Transforming manure from waste into valuable nutrient source is essential for promoting a more sustainable agricultural production. Motivated by the large divergence found in manure N balance estimates, we developed a framework, which advances previous approaches by systematically evaluating how varying assumptions about manure recoverability and crop NUE influence the estimated potential for manure recycling. It provides a flexible and informative assessment of opportunities to improve nutrient circularity under current and future conditions. However, there remain several opportunities to improve the data required by metrices used in this framework.

Our analysis for the USA reveals a data gap in quantifying currently applied manure metric. Most existing estimates reflect the amount of manure N that could be potentially recovered using existing manure management technology. In contrast, we estimate this metric using surveys of farmers22. Comparing these two values reveals that only half of the potentially recoverable manure N reaches croplands in the USA. This discrepancy could be attributed to site-specific practices with operational or technological constraints and limited farm survey samples22. For example, currently applied manure N estimate was derived by applying average manure application rates22 from the agricultural resource management survey (ARMS) sample to a broader manured acreage22, assuming those rates are nationally representative. However, this assumption may introduce bias, as ARMS places greater sampling emphasis on key agricultural states31 and may skew national averages if practices differ in under-represented regions. Overall, farm survey data show that only 19% of total excreted manure N is currently applied to cropland, which aligns with the previous work4,5 that report similarly low national average manure recycling rates in the USA. Bridging this gap requires more spatially and temporally robust surveys that are both transparent and accessible. Meanwhile, remote sensing techniques are useful to identify where manure is spread at the field level and to infer confined animal feed operations (CAFOs) impacts32, helping to fill critical data gaps on animal populations, facility locations and manure management practices.

The availability and quality of the data for key parameters need improvement. Many recoverable manure N estimates rely on parameters that were published decades ago and that do not account for crucial variations in space, time and management practices16,23. Manure characteristics, such as N content and moisture levels, can vary over time as a result of changes in production technologies, feed composition and regional climatic conditions. In addition, more spatially explicit soil testing data33 are needed to assess soil P levels indicative of either saturation or deficiency (Supplementary Information Section 4).

Despite some data limitations, applying the framework with existing data helps identify opportunities for improving manure recycling tailored to local conditions and stakeholders. This is especially important given the long-standing spatial mismatch of livestock and crop production in the USA, shaped by historical, climatic, economic, infrastructural and policy factors. Growing interest in manureshed-based strategies offers a promising path forward34–36. For example, poultry litter transport and brokering programmes in the Delmarva Peninsula34 demonstrate how coordinated efforts across stakeholders can help realign nutrient flows between areas of surplus and need.

Future studies could enhance the framework by disaggregating manure handling into collection, storage and transport processes to identify specific management bottlenecks as data availability and technological solutions improve. Accounting for site-specific soil, climate and management factors would further strengthen the framework and reduce unintended yield or NUE impacts of manure substitution (Supplementary Information Section 5). Additional refinements involve determining varying targets for improved crop NUE across different crop types. Expanding the framework to include alternative manure uses, such as energy production through anaerobic digestion37, would further broaden its scope and utility. While our study focuses on recovering nutrients from livestock manure, other organic nutrient sources, such as food waste, human waste and sewage sludge, also hold considerable potential to offset synthetic fertilizer use, albeit with additional concerns of contaminants such as heavy metals and per- and poly-fluoralkyl substances. In the USA, an estimated 4.5 million dry tons of treated sewage sludge were produced in 202138, yet over 40%, nearly 1.9 million tons, were landfilled rather than recycled38. Future research should also systematically assess contributions of alternative organic nutrient sources, supporting a more comprehensive understanding of nutrient recycling potential. Potential changes in dietary preferences, away from N-intensive commodities (for example, animal-derived foods) could also be included in scenarios of crop nutrients demand. By addressing these limitations and incorporating additional metrics, this framework has the potential to become a comprehensive tool for guiding sustainable manure management practices at local, regional and national scales, including global analyses39.

Improving manure recycling requires using consistent and transparent approaches, such as the one presented here, to evaluate manure nutrient recoverability, crop nutrient demand and manure nutrient balance. Looking forward, this framework can be adapted to other nutrients and geographies, while also incorporating additional factors that may influence manure recycling potential, such as odour, pathogens and broader environmental or social concerns. Future research should also examine the socioeconomic factors influencing the scalability of manure-based solutions. With the right data, policies and technologies, the food system can move towards reduced nutrient pollution and improved efficiency.

Methods

Data sources

We collected national-, state- and county-level animal inventories from the US Department of Agriculture-National Agricultural Statistics Service (USDA-NASS) for the year 2017 (https://quickstats.nass.usda.gov/)40 for dairy cows, beef cattle on feed, hogs, broilers, layers, pullets, turkeys and ducks. Aggregated county-level animal headcounts are lower than state-level totals as a result of redactions made by NASS to protect producer identities, which led to an underestimation of recoverable manure N at local level. To identify where discrepancies are most pronounced, we compared total excreted manure per animal type and M2N using aggregated county-level populations and obtained state population as aggregated state population is comparable to that of national data (Supplementary Figs. 7–9). The manure excretion factor, number of life cycles, animals per animal unit per confined animal type, proportion of manure that is recoverable and N and P retained in recoverable manure after losses during collection, transfer, storage and treatment were obtained from ref. 23. Manure currently applied on cropland, including corn, soybeans, wheat, cotton, barley, oats and peanuts and hay receiving manure and relative application rate were obtained from refs. 22,41 following protocols in the ManureMap Toolbox (https://acpf4watersheds.org/use-the-manuremap-toolbox/)42. Synthetic fertilizer (both N and P) consumption in 2017 at both national and county levels were obtained from the US Geological Survey report43. Crop production and acreages on national and county scales were obtained from USDA-NASS40, including 21 common US crops: alfalfa hay, apples, barley, dry beans, canola, corn for grain, corn for silage, cotton, other hay, oranges, peanuts, potatoes, rice, sorghum, soybeans, sugar beets, sugarcane, sunflower, sweet corn, tobacco and wheat. We used N content data for those 21 common US crops provided by NUGIS team4. P-content data were obtained from ref. 44. For the national-level analysis, current crop-specific NUE for the USA was derived from ref. 20 using the average estimates from 2010 to 2015 (Supplementary Table 12). For the county-level analysis, we applied state-specific average NUE values for corn and wheat (Supplementary Table 8) over the same period from ref. 45, while NUE values for the remaining crops were held constant using the national-level estimates as a result of a lack of spatially explicit data. The harvested area of crops and N deposition rate on cropland were derived from USDA-NASS40 and the National Atmospheric Deposition Program (NADP)46, respectively. Total volume of irrigation water used in agricultural production and its N content were obtained from AQUASTAT (https://data.apps.fao.org/aquastat/?lang=en) and literature47,48.

Divergent estimates of manure N balance among published studies

To compare manure N balance assessments and their implications across studies, we calculated recoverable manure N and crop N demand metrics using the contiguous USA as an example using their methods. Data inputs included national inventories of animal populations and crop production and acreages from USDA-NASS for 201740. While replicating each approach, we updated certain coefficients, such as animal excretion factors, manure recoverability rates and crop NUE as detailed in ‘Data Sources’, to better reflect the US context, while maintaining the original interpretive intent of each approach (Supplementary Information Section 3).

System boundary

The system boundary in this study defines how the proposed framework is applied to the US case (Supplementary Fig. 5). The system in this study includes manure N excreted by eight types of confined livestock and N demand from 21 US common crops that are harvested and removed from the field. Manure produced under confinement conditions is routinely collected and removed from built areas, making it suitable for evaluating recovery potential and substitution for synthetic fertilizers. Manure excreted by grazing livestock and demand from pasture or rangeland were excluded. Manure from grazing livestock deposits directly onto pasture, where it contributes to an internal nutrient cycle: animals consume forage, excrete manure onto the land and the nutrients are largely absorbed by the regrowing pasture biomass. Intervening in this internal nutrient cycle would not only be technologically challenging under current practices but could also undermine pasture productivity, potentially increasing demand for synthetic fertilizers. Although localized nutrient surpluses may occur in overgrazed areas, such imbalances are context-specific and are best addressed through targeted, site-level management strategies. Including grazing systems in this analysis would introduce considerable uncertainty due to limited data on grazing intensity, forage productivity and manure nutrient dynamics.

Methods for estimating constrained recoverable manure N considering P overapplication

Owing to the relatively low N:P ratio in manure, applying manure N to meet crop N needs may result in P overapplication. To account for this, we defined M1N∗ and M2N∗ as the constrained levels of recoverable manure N under current (M1N) and future (M2N) technologies that avoid excessive manure P application to cropland. The key criterion for considering P overapplication risks is that the P content in the recoverable manure cannot exceed the current P input from the synthetic fertilizers when used as a replacement. We calculated recoverable manure P using the same methodology as for N, according to refs. 22,23 (national level: total excreted manure P = 0.9 Tg yr−1; potentially recoverable manure P = 0.7 Tg yr−1; currently applied manure P = 0.2 Tg yr−1).

We first compare the additional recoverable manure phosphorus (P) under current technology in county MIP,ct with currently applied fertilizer P to cropland (fertilizer Pct) in each county (ct). The recoverable manure N under current technology (MIN,ct) remains unchanged when MIP,ct is less than fertilizer Pct; otherwise, it is revised down to match fertilizer Pct and then converted back to N as M1N,ct∗, using the N:P ratio in the recoverable manure under current technology M1N,ctM1P,ct.

As this framework is applied to the USA, we adopted a consistent approach to address potential P overapplication by constraining recoverable manure P so that it does not exceed current synthetic fertilizer P inputs. However, this approach may require adjustment in regions with P use efficiency (PUE) >1 (defined as the ratio of harvested crop P products to current P inputs including synthetic fertilizer and manure) and where P-deficient soils are being ‘mined’ of native soil P due to little or no P input. Such conditions are more common in low-income countries with limited access to P input, where the P demand by cropland should at least match the P in harvested crop products and could be increased further to help close estimated yield gaps. As more data on soil P availability become available, estimates of crop P demand and manure recovery potential can be further refined when applying this framework to such regions.

Downscaling currently applied manure N

While the farmer survey conducted by Lim et al.22 provides information on currently applied manure, it is not spatially explicit22. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a method to estimate the currently applied manure N metric using the survey data in combination with other available spatial datasets. To address this, we used crop-level information on the share of manure received from each animal source (Fig. 6 in ref. 22) to estimate the contribution of each livestock type to overall manure N application. By aggregating the manure shares across all crops, we estimated the proportion of total applied manure originating from each animal category (for example, dairy, beef, hogs and poultry). We then calculated the percentage of excreted manure N that is applied to cropland by livestock type, ranging from 11% to 31% (Supplementary Table 17). CAFO manure applied to hay was excluded in county-level estimates because of the absence of data on source-specific application rates and total manure applied to hay was estimated at only 0.08 TgN nationally41,42, representing a relatively small portion of overall manure application.

Evaluating priorities of counties in improving manure recycling

We applied the framework to the county level and calculated the county-level current manure N balance (the difference between currently applied manure N and current crop N demand). Next, we assessed the improved manure N balance by progressively adding M1N, M2N and iNUE to evaluate how counties could meet crop N needs using local manure N sources but with future potentials of recoverable manure N and crop N demand metrics. This stepwise approach helps to identify varying priorities of counties in improving manure recycling. These include counties with excess manure, where currently applied manure N already exceeds manure-replaceable crop N demand with current NUE; counties with the potential to reach balance, where the manure N balance can transition from negative to equal or greater than 0 with improved practices (M1N; M1N + M2N; M1N + M2N + iNUE); and counties with excess crop demand where the manure N balance remains negative after adopting M1N, M2N and iNUE.

Uncertainty analysis

We used a Monte Carlo approach to estimate uncertainty in recoverable manure N and crop N demand metrics. Uncertainty propagation was performed using 500 Monte Carlo iterations, in which key input parameters were randomly sampled from distributions defined by their means and standard deviations. Parameters include manure excretion factors, manure recoverability factors across animal types, harvested crop N products and crop-specific NUE. We calculated means and derived the standard deviations. For manure excretion factors and harvested crop N products, coefficients of variation were derived from several widely cited US-based studies23,49–54 and applied to values reported in ref. 23 and to harvested crop N estimated from USDA-NASS data40, respectively, to obtain standard deviations (Supplementary Tables 19 and 20). Manure recoverability factors were characterized using mean values and standard deviations from Table 9 in ref. 23, accounting for regional variation across animal types (Supplementary Table 20). Crop-specific NUE means and standard deviations were derived from data spanning 2010–2015 (Supplementary Table 12). The results we presented are shown as mean ± 1 s.d.
