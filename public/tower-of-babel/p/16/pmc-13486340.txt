Lauric acid engages an O-GlcNAc–sensitive BCKDH regulatory node to modulate branched-chain amino acid oxidation in skeletal myotubes
Authors: Koichiro Sumi, Miho Shioyama, Kinuyo Munakata, Satoshi Takasugi, Masashi Morifuji, Kentaro Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) catabolism is controlled by the phosphorylation state of the branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase (BCKDH) complex, which is regulated by the opposing actions of BCKDH kinase and the phosphatase PPM1K. Although fatty acids and amino acids both contribute to skeletal muscle energy metabolism, how fatty acid availability influences BCAA catabolic regulation remains incompletely understood. Here, we examined the effects of lauric acid (C12), a medium-chain fatty acid abundant in dietary lipids, on BCAA metabolism in differentiated skeletal myotubes. Lauric acid increased phosphorylation of the BCKDH E1α subunit at Ser293 during nutrient perturbation in both mouse and human skeletal myotubes. Stable isotope tracing with U-[13C6]-leucine revealed that C12 reduced incorporation of leucine-derived carbon into downstream tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle–associated metabolites, indicating suppression of BCAA oxidative flux, whereas incorporation of labeled leucine into protein was not significantly altered. Mechanistically, genetic and pharmacological perturbation experiments indicated that the C12 effect requires PPM1K and is sensitive to O-linked β-N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) cycling. Knockdown of O-GlcNAc transferase attenuated the C12-induced increase in BCKDH phosphorylation and reversed suppression of leucine-derived carbon flux. Dual-tracer experiments further showed that carbon derived from lauric acid and leucine converges in shared tricarboxylic acid cycle–associated metabolite pools, including glutamate and glutamine. Together, these findings identify a nutrient-sensitive regulatory node linking fatty acid availability, O-GlcNAc signaling, and BCKDH phosphorylation that modulates BCAA oxidation in skeletal myotubes.

Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, serve not only as essential substrates for protein synthesis but also as important metabolic fuels in skeletal muscle (1, 2). BCAA catabolism is initiated by reversible transamination catalyzed by branched-chain aminotransferases (BCAT1 in the cytosol and BCAT2 in mitochondria), which convert BCAAs to their corresponding branched-chain α-ketoacids (BCKAs). During this reaction, the amino group of BCAAs is transferred to α-ketoglutarate to generate glutamate, thereby linking BCAA metabolism to the glutamine–glutamate pool and central nitrogen metabolism (3, 4).

In mammals, BCAT2 is highly expressed in skeletal muscle (3), and skeletal muscle is therefore considered a major site for the initial step of BCAA catabolism (5, 6). The subsequent oxidative decarboxylation of BCKAs is catalyzed by the mitochondrial branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase (BCKDH) complex. Because this reaction is irreversible, it is generally regarded as the rate-limiting step of BCAA oxidation (7). Through this pathway, skeletal muscle contributes not only to the regulation of circulating BCAA levels but also to whole-body energy metabolism (4, 5).

BCKDH activity is tightly regulated through reversible phosphorylation of the E1α subunit (BCKDH-E1α). Phosphorylation of serine 293 residue on BCKDH-E1α inhibits catalytic activity and limits flux through the complex, whereas dephosphorylation restores activity (8). The phosphorylation state of BCKDH-E1α is determined by the opposing actions of the BCKDH kinase (BDK) (9) and the mitochondrial phosphatase PPM1K (10). Through this mechanism, the BCKDH complex functions as a central regulatory node controlling BCAA catabolic flux across multiple tissues (5, 11).

Dysregulation of BCAA metabolism has been associated with metabolic disorders including obesity, insulin resistance, and aging-related metabolic dysfunction (5, 11, 12, 13, 14). Accordingly, stimulation of BCAA catabolism has been explored as a potential therapeutic strategy. Pharmacological inhibition of BDK has been proposed as 1 approach to activate BCKDH and enhance BCAA oxidation, and several small-molecule BDK inhibitors have been reported to improve metabolic parameters in experimental models (15, 16). These findings highlight the importance of the BDK–PPM1K regulatory axis as a metabolic control point.

Another layer of metabolic regulation is provided by O-linked β-N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) modification, a nutrient-sensitive posttranslational modification catalyzed by O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) and removed by O-GlcNAcase (OGA). O-GlcNAcylation integrates carbon and nitrogen availability and regulates diverse metabolic enzymes and signaling pathways (17, 18). Recent work in hepatocellular carcinoma cells proposed an O-GlcNAc–PPM1K–BCKDH regulatory axis in which O-GlcNAcylation stabilizes PPM1K and promotes BCKDH dephosphorylation, thereby enhancing BCAA catabolism (19). Whether a similar regulatory mechanism operates in skeletal muscle and how nutrient signals influence this pathway remain largely unknown.

Fatty acids are major metabolic substrates for skeletal muscle and can strongly influence mitochondrial metabolism. Medium-chain fatty acids in particular are widely used in nutritional contexts because of their efficient mitochondrial utilization. However, individual medium-chain fatty acids may exhibit distinct metabolic and signaling properties beyond their general role as energy substrates. Octanoic acid (OA, C8) has been suggested to promote BCAA catabolism through inhibition of BDK (20, 21), whereas decanoic acid (DA, C10) has been reported to enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and function, particularly in neuronal cells (22). In contrast, long-chain fatty acids such as palmitic acid (PA, C16) are well known to induce insulin resistance (23, 24), whereas lauric acid (LA, C12) has been reported to attenuate insulin resistance in myotube models (25). These observations suggest that individual fatty acid species may exert distinct regulatory effects on cellular metabolism.

Despite these observations, it remains unclear whether medium-chain fatty acids directly influence the BCKDH phosphorylation gate and BCAA oxidative metabolism in skeletal muscle. In particular, it is unknown whether nutrient-responsive signaling pathways such as O-GlcNAcylation participate in integrating fatty acid availability with BCAA catabolic regulation. In the present study, we investigated whether LA, a major fatty acid derived from dietary medium-chain triglycerides, modulates BCAA catabolism in skeletal muscle cells. Using differentiated C2C12 myotubes, metabolic flux analysis, and genetic and pharmacological perturbation of the BDK–PPM1K regulatory axis, we show that LA suppresses leucine oxidative flux by maintaining BCKDH phosphorylation through an O-GlcNAc–sensitive regulatory mechanism. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized interaction among fatty acid availability, nutrient-dependent signaling, and BCAA catabolic regulation in skeletal muscle.

Lauric acid (C12) elevates BCKDH-E1α Ser293 phosphorylation across starvation–refeeding in mouse and human myotubes

In C2C12 myotubes, two-way ANOVA revealed a significant FA × BCAA interaction for pBCKDH-E1α(Ser293)/total, indicating that fatty acid effects depended on the BCAA condition (Fig. 1C). Under −BCAA conditions, C6, C8, C10, C12 (generally called medium-chain fatty acids; MCFAs), and C16 increased pBCKDH-E1α relative to vehicle, whereas under + BCAA conditions, all tested fatty acids significantly increased phosphorylation compared with vehicle, with the largest effect observed for C12 (Fig. 1C). These results establish lauric acid as the most potent inducer of BCKDH-E1α hyperphosphorylation among the fatty acids tested in this starvation–refeeding paradigm.

Because BCAA refeeding activates mTORC1 signaling, we assessed p70S6K phosphorylation in the same samples. BCAA refeeding robustly increased p70S6K phosphorylation across vehicle and fatty acid conditions, indicating that the refeeding response remained intact (Fig. 1D). Although the FA × BCAA interaction was significant, Dunnett’s tests did not identify individual fatty acids that significantly altered p70S6K phosphorylation relative to vehicle within either BCAA condition, with only a tendency toward a smaller decrease with C16. In additional signaling readouts, phosphorylation states of Akt, eEF2, and AMPKα showed some variation across fatty acid conditions, but apart from C16 no unusually large shifts were observed (Fig. S1), supporting that the dominant, consistent response to MCFAs was the shifting toward increase of the BCKDH phosphorylation in C2C12 myotube.

To assess whether the MCFAs effect is conserved in human muscle cells, we performed an analogous starvation–refeeding protocol in human skeletal myotubes differentiated under low-glucose conditions (Fig. 1E). In human myotubes, pBCKDH-E1α was again influenced by an FA × BCAA interaction (Fig. 1G). Notably, under −BCAA conditions, C8 decreased phosphorylation relative to vehicle, whereas C12 showed a trend toward increased phosphorylation. Upon BCAA refeeding, phosphorylation decreased across fatty acid conditions (#, secondary analysis), yet C12-treated cells retained significantly higher pBCKDH-E1α than vehicle (Fig. 1G). Thus, while the effect of C8 differed between mouse and human myotubes, C12 consistently maintained BCKDH toward higher Ser293 phosphorylation in both systems. Based on this shared directionality, subsequent mechanistic and tracer experiments focused on C12 as the primary fatty acid of interest.

Finally, because fatty acid oxidation assays commonly include exogenous L-carnitine, we tested whether carnitine availability is required for the C12-induced phosphorylation shift. The C12-associated increase in pBCKDH-E1α was not markedly altered by L-carnitine supplementation (Fig. S2), indicating that this effect is not strictly dependent on exogenous carnitine under our experimental conditions.

C12-induced BCKDH hyperphosphorylation is preserved under low-glucose conditions and is reversible without large transcriptional remodeling

Under these low-glucose conditions, BCAA refeeding decreased pBCKDH-E1α(Ser293)/total, whereas C12 maintained a higher phosphorylation level (Fig. 2B). To evaluate whether C12 acts by inducing major transcriptional changes in the BCKDH regulatory axis or upstream transamination, we measured mRNA abundance of Bdk, Ppm1k, and Bcat2 by qPCR. For this analysis, RNA was harvested either from cells continuously exposed to vehicle or C12 in DM or from cells exposed to 3 h serum/BCAA starvation in low-glucose medium (“Starve”) (Fig. 2, C–E). Bdk mRNA abundance increased in response to starvation (main effect of medium), whereas C12 did not alter Bdk mRNA abundance (Fig. 2C). In contrast, Ppm1k and Bcat2 showed main effects of C12, but the magnitude of change was small (Fig. 2, D and E). Notably, Bcat2 mRNA abundance increased under starvation conditions, whereas Bdk also increased, suggesting that this starvation paradigm modestly favors the upstream transamination module while simultaneously reinforcing the phosphorylation “brake” at the irreversible BCKDH step.

Given the limited transcriptional remodeling, we next tested whether the C12 effect reflects an acute and reversible input during the starvation/refeeding window. Using a dose–response design, myotubes were pretreated with increasing concentrations of C12 and then either switched to C12-free starvation medium (“washout”) or maintained in C12-containing starvation medium (“continuous”) before ± BCAA refeeding (Fig. 2F). Consistent with the two-way ANOVA, the main effect of C12 dose was not significant under washout conditions (Fig. 2G) but remained significant when C12 was present throughout starvation/refeeding (Fig. 2H). When C12 was washed out during the starvation/refeeding window, the dose-dependent elevation of pBCKDH was lost (Fig. 2G), whereas BCAA refeeding still decreased phosphorylation (secondary analysis, where shown). In contrast, under continuous C12 exposure, pBCKDH increased in a C12 dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2H). Together, these results indicate that C12 elevates BCKDH phosphorylation in a dose-dependent yet reversible manner and does so without requiring large changes in expression of key BCAA metabolic genes, supporting an acute regulatory mechanism.

Lauric acid reduces incorporation of leucine-derived carbon into downstream oxidative metabolites without altering protein synthesis

To determine whether the fatty acid–induced increase in BCKDH phosphorylation is accompanied by altered BCAA oxidative metabolism, we performed stable isotope tracing using U-[13C6]-leucine in differentiated C2C12 myotubes (Fig. 3A). After cellular uptake, leucine can either be incorporated into protein or metabolized in mitochondria. In the catabolic pathway, leucine is transaminated by BCAT2 to generate U-[13C6]-α-ketoisocaproate (KIC), which is subsequently decarboxylated by the BCKDH complex and further metabolized to yield 13C2-acetyl-CoA. Entry of this acetyl-CoA into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle results in labeling of downstream intermediates such as fumarate and malate. The enrichment of intracellular U-[13C6]-leucine and U-[13C6]-KIC was close to complete under tracer conditions and was not significantly affected by fatty acid treatment (Fig. 3, C and D), indicating comparable tracer availability and upstream transamination across conditions. In contrast, labeling of downstream 13C2-containing metabolites derived from leucine was significantly reduced by fatty acid treatment (Fig. 3, E–I). Compared with vehicle, both C8 and C12 decreased the 13C2 enrichment of acetyl-CoA and TCA cycle–related metabolites, with a greater reduction observed in C12-treated cells. This pattern parallels the increase in BCKDH phosphorylation observed in Figure 1, suggesting that fatty acid–induced changes in BCKDH phosphorylation are associated with reduced incorporation of leucine-derived carbon into oxidative metabolic pathways.

In contrast, the U-[13C6]-leucine enrichment detected in protein-bound leucine and the fractional synthesis rate (FSR) calculated from this enrichment were not significantly altered by fatty acid treatment (Fig. 3, J and K). We initially hypothesized that leucine spared from oxidative metabolism might be redirected toward protein synthesis; however, the present results do not support this possibility. Instead, the unchanged FSR is consistent with the observation that fatty acid treatment did not markedly alter p70S6K phosphorylation in Figure 1, suggesting that global protein synthesis signaling remained largely unaffected. The metabolic fate of leucine or KIC spared from oxidative metabolism therefore remains to be determined, although 1 possibility is that a portion may be retained in intracellular amino acid or ketoacid pools and/or released as KIC rather than being redirected to protein synthesis (5, 6).

BDK and PPM1K contribute to the C12-dependent phosphorylation response of BCKDH and differentially regulate leucine-derived oxidative labeling

To determine whether the C12-induced increase in BCKDH phosphorylation involves the canonical regulatory enzymes BDK and PPM1K, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments in differentiated C2C12 myotubes.

We first confirmed the efficiency of the knockdowns. siBDK markedly reduced Bdk mRNA abundance relative to scramble control (Scr) (Fig. 4A), and BDK protein levels were also reduced to approximately half of control levels (Fig. 4B). Similarly, siRNA targeting Ppm1k significantly reduced Ppm1k mRNA abundance (Fig. 4C), and PPM1K protein levels were modestly decreased (Fig. 4D). We then examined how knockdown of BDK or PPM1K influenced the phosphorylation response of BCKDH to C12 (Fig. 4E). Cells were transfected with siRNA one day before fatty-acid treatment, followed by incubation with vehicle or C12-containing medium. On the experimental day, cells were subjected to 3 h starvation in the presence of vehicle or C12 and then refed with BCAA-containing or BCAA-free medium for 45 min before harvest. Three-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of C12, siRNA treatment, and BCAA condition, as well as significant interactions among these factors.

Under vehicle conditions, siBDK produced only modest changes in basal BCKDH phosphorylation, although phosphorylation tended to be higher upon BCAA refeeding. Under C12 treatment, however, the phosphorylation increase induced by C12 was largely attenuated in siBDK-treated cells. In contrast, siPPM1K markedly increased BCKDH phosphorylation under vehicle conditions, particularly upon BCAA refeeding, consistent with reduced dephosphorylation of the complex. Under C12 treatment, the large phosphorylation increase observed in BCAA-free conditions was largely abolished in siPPM1K cells, resulting in phosphorylation levels similar to those observed in siPPM1K cells under vehicle conditions. These results support the idea that both BDK and PPM1K contribute to the phosphorylation response of BCKDH to C12.

To determine whether these knockdowns affected BCAA oxidative metabolism, we performed U-13C6-leucine tracing experiments (Fig. 4, F–J). Because the metabolic effects of siRNA perturbation were large, tracer incorporation was evaluated over time to capture early differences that were less apparent at later time points. In siBDK-treated cells, incorporation of leucine-derived carbon into downstream metabolites was increased relative to Scr controls, particularly at 3 h after tracer addition. Enrichment of 13C2-labeled acetyl-CoA, glutamate, glutamine, fumarate, and malate was elevated at this time point, consistent with enhanced BCAA catabolic flux when BDK activity is reduced.

In contrast, PPM1K knockdown showed the opposite trend. Across time points, enrichment of 13C2-labeled downstream metabolites tended to be lower than in Scr controls. Although acetyl-CoA labeling showed considerable variability, glutamine and malate labeling were consistently reduced, and fumarate labeling was significantly decreased at later time points. These observations indicate that reduced PPM1K activity is associated with decreased incorporation of leucine-derived carbon into oxidative metabolic pathways.

Interestingly, incorporation of U-[13C6]-leucine into cellular protein was reduced in both siBDK- and siPPM1K-treated cells (Fig. 4K). This finding suggests that disruption of the normal regulatory balance of BCAA catabolism may negatively influence protein synthesis capacity.

Medium composition modulates the effects of BT2 and C12 on BCKDH phosphorylation

Because BCKDH phosphorylation is controlled by the opposing actions of BDK and PPM1K, we next asked whether the nutrient environment determines how this regulatory node responds to pharmacological or fatty acid stimuli. Previous studies reported that the widely used BDK inhibitor BT2 shows limited ability to decrease BCKDH phosphorylation and stimulate BCAA catabolism in skeletal muscle compared with liver and heart (26), suggesting that regulation of the BCKDH node is context dependent in myotubes. In addition, studies in hepatocellular carcinoma cells have shown that glutamine deprivation stabilizes PPM1K through O-GlcNAcylation, thereby promoting BCAA catabolism (19). Based on these observations, we hypothesized that nutrient composition may modulate the functional state of the BDK–PPM1K regulatory node.

To test this possibility, we first examined whether the effect of BT2 depends on medium composition. Differentiated C2C12 myotubes were incubated for 24 h with BT2 (100 μm) or vehicle (DMSO) in standard DM, low-glucose DMEM (LG-DM), LG-DM lacking BCAA, or LG-DM lacking glutamine. Phosphorylation of p70S6K and BCKDH was then analyzed by capillary immunoassay.

Two-way ANOVA showed a significant main effect of medium composition but no significant interaction with BT2. In contrast, BCKDH phosphorylation was strongly affected by medium composition (Fig. 5B). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between medium composition and BT2 treatment. We next examined whether the phosphorylation response to C12 is similarly influenced by nutrient conditions. In these experiments, cells were subjected to the same 3-h starvation protocol used in previous C12 experiments, except that the starvation medium lacked serum together with neither leucine alone, glutamine alone, or both leucine and glutamine. In contrast, removal of glutamine markedly attenuated the phosphorylation-enhancing effect of C12 in both leucine/glutamine-free and glutamine-only-free media.

Interestingly, leucine refeeding normally reduced BCKDH phosphorylation under vehicle conditions, but this effect was lost when glutamine alone was removed. We therefore next examined how leucine- and laurate-derived carbon were incorporated into shared metabolic intermediates under these nutrient conditions.

β-oxidation of ω-13C1-lauric acid generates 13C1-acetyl-CoA, which can enter the TCA cycle and contribute to 13C1 labeling of downstream metabolites such as glutamate and glutamine. In addition, after 1 turn of the TCA cycle, symmetric intermediates such as succinate can redistribute the label, allowing 13C1-derived carbon to reappear as 13C2 enrichment in downstream metabolites during subsequent cycles. Because the current analysis was performed using single-stage high-resolution mass spectrometry, the positional origin of the 13C2 label could not be unambiguously assigned. In principle, 13C2 enrichment could arise from either U-[13C6]-leucine oxidation or from secondary labeling derived from ω-13C1-lauric acid metabolism.

The experimental design is shown in Figure 6B. Differentiated C2C12 myotubes were subjected to starvation in leucine-free DMEM supplemented with vehicle, unlabeled C12, or ω-13C1-C12. After 3 h of starvation, cells were refed with basal medium lacking leucine, medium containing natural leucine, or medium containing U-[13C6]-leucine. Cells were harvested 8 h after refeeding for LC–MS analysis. Thus, total fatty-acid exposure lasted 11 h, whereas leucine labeling was performed for 8 h.

We first examined 13C1 enrichment of acetyl-CoA, glutamate, and glutamine (Fig. 6, C–E). For acetyl-CoA and glutamate, two-way ANOVA revealed significant interactions between C12 treatment and leucine condition. Therefore, the primary analysis compared the effect of C12 within each leucine condition using Tukey’s post hoc tests. In all leucine conditions, addition of ω-13C1-C12 produced markedly higher 13C1 enrichment than vehicle or unlabeled C12. For glutamine, the interaction term was not significant, and the main effect of C12 was therefore evaluated. Similarly, ω-13C1-C12 significantly increased 13C1 enrichment compared with vehicle or unlabeled C12. Although smaller in magnitude, leucine availability also influenced 13C1 enrichment. Because pool sizes were not quantified in this experiment, it remains unclear whether these effects reflect altered laurate oxidation or dilution of the labeled pool by leucine-derived carbon. Nevertheless, these results demonstrate that carbon derived from C12 can enter the TCA cycle and contribute to glutamate and glutamine pools.

We next examined 13C2 enrichment in the same metabolites (Fig. 6, F–H). Overall patterns were similar across acetyl-CoA, glutamate, and glutamine. In all fatty-acid conditions, addition of U-[13C6]-leucine markedly increased 13C2 enrichment compared with leucine-free or natural-leucine conditions, consistent with the leucine-derived labeling pattern observed in Figure 3. Interestingly, ω-13C1-C12 treatment also increased 13C2 enrichment in all leucine conditions. For acetyl-CoA and glutamate, significant interactions between C12 treatment and leucine condition were detected, and Tukey comparisons within each leucine condition showed higher 13C2 enrichment with ω-13C1-C12 than with vehicle or unlabeled C12. For glutamine, where the interaction term was not significant, analysis of the main effect of C12 similarly indicated increased 13C2 enrichment with ω-13C1-C12.

Together, these results indicate that both leucine-derived and C12-derived carbon enter shared metabolic pathways through the TCA cycle and contribute to downstream glutamate and glutamine pools. Given that BCAA catabolism transfers amino groups to α-ketoglutarate, these findings suggest that the presence of C12 may influence BCAA catabolism and glutamine metabolism through shared metabolic intermediates. This interpretation is consistent with the nutrient-dependent regulation of BCKDH phosphorylation described in Figure 5 and raises the possibility that C12 affects BCAA metabolism through mechanisms linked to glutamine availability and posttranslational regulation of the BDK–PPM1K node.

Thus, the effect of OGT knockdown was not restricted to acetyl-CoA and amino acid pools but extended to downstream TCA cycle–associated metabolites.

Because siRNA-mediated knockdown can be influenced by sequence-specific off-target effects, we next tested an independent OGT-targeting siRNA, siOGT-B (Fig. S4). Among two additional OGT-targeting siRNAs examined, siOGT-B markedly reduced OGT protein abundance, whereas siOGT-C did not produce clear knockdown; therefore, subsequent validation experiments were performed using siOGT-B. siOGT-B reduced global O-GlcNAcylation in the starvation–refeeding experiment and shifted BCKDH phosphorylation in the direction opposite to the C12-associated increase, reproducing the effect observed with siOGT-A. In parallel U-[13C6]-leucine tracing experiments, C12 reduced 13C2 enrichment of acetyl-CoA, glutamate, and glutamine in scramble-treated cells, whereas siOGT-B increased 13C2 enrichment under vehicle conditions and partially attenuated the C12-associated reduction in labeling. Although the magnitude of the effect was smaller than that observed with siOGT-A, the overall direction of the phosphorylation and flux responses was reproduced with this independent siRNA.

Because 13C2 enrichment was elevated under OGT-knockdown conditions even in the absence of C12, these results suggest that reduced O-GlcNAcylation influences additional steps of BCAA metabolism beyond the specific site of C12 action. Taken together with the phosphorylation data and the independent siOGT-B validation, these findings support the idea that OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation is required for the C12-dependent suppression of BCAA catabolism. Although the precise molecular targets remain to be identified, these results are consistent with a model in which O-GlcNAc–dependent regulation of BDK and/or PPM1K contributes to the control of BCKDH activity.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrate that lauric acid (C12) suppresses BCAA oxidation in skeletal myotubes through a regulatory mechanism involving BCKDH phosphorylation. Our results identify an O-GlcNAc–sensitive regulatory node that modulates the balance between BDK and PPM1K, thereby controlling the phosphorylation state of the BCKDH complex. Metabolic tracing experiments further revealed that C12 alters the incorporation of leucine-derived carbon into TCA cycle–associated metabolite pools, indicating that fatty acid availability can directly influence BCAA catabolic flux (12).

BCAA catabolism is initiated by reversible transamination catalyzed by BCATs, which convert BCAAs into their corresponding BCKAs (7). During this reaction, the amino group of BCAAs is transferred to α-ketoglutarate to generate glutamate, thereby linking BCAA metabolism to the glutamate–glutamine pool. In mammals, BCAT2, the mitochondrial isoform, is highly expressed in skeletal muscle (3), and skeletal muscle is therefore considered a major site of the first step of BCAA catabolism (6).

The subsequent oxidative decarboxylation of BCKAs is catalyzed by the BCKDH complex. Because this reaction is irreversible, it is generally considered the rate-limiting step of BCAA oxidation (7). BCKDH activity is primarily regulated through reversible phosphorylation of the E1α subunit at the level of the enzyme complex. The phosphorylation state is determined by the opposing actions of the BDK (9) and the phosphatase PPM1K (10), which together control the activity of the BCKDH complex (7). This regulatory system has been established as a key metabolic gate governing BCAA oxidation in multiple tissues, including skeletal muscle. Our findings extend this framework by demonstrating that fatty acid availability, particularly lauric acid, can influence this regulatory node.

Dysregulation of BCAA metabolism has been associated with aging, obesity, and insulin resistance, and stimulation of BCAA catabolism has therefore been explored as a potential therapeutic strategy in metabolic disease (5, 11, 13). Consistent with this concept, inhibition of BDK has been proposed as a pharmacological approach to promote BCAA oxidation. The small-molecule BDK inhibitor BT2 has been reported to mimic fasting-like metabolic responses and improve systemic metabolic parameters in experimental models (16). However, more recent studies indicate that the metabolic effects of BT2 are not solely explained by BDK inhibition and may involve additional mechanisms, including mitochondrial depolarization (29, 30). Moreover, BT2 has relatively limited ability to decrease BCKDH phosphorylation in skeletal muscle compared with other tissues such as liver and heart (26), suggesting that responsiveness of the BCKDH regulatory node varies depending on cellular and metabolic context.

In contrast to many previous studies that aim to stimulate BCAA catabolism (15, 31, 32, 33), the present study demonstrates that lauric acid suppresses BCAA oxidative metabolism in skeletal muscle cells. Although this finding may appear counterintuitive in light of efforts to enhance BCAA oxidation for metabolic benefits, such regulation may have physiological relevance in skeletal muscle (34). Skeletal muscle functions not only as a major organ of locomotion but also as the largest reservoir of amino acids in the body (35). Preservation of muscle protein is therefore essential for maintaining physiological function, particularly during aging or disease, where loss of muscle mass is an independent predictor of poor clinical outcomes (36, 37, 38, 39, 40). Under resting conditions skeletal muscle relies primarily on carbohydrates for energy, whereas fatty acids and BCAAs contribute more substantially during prolonged exercise or metabolic stress (7, 41, 42). In such contexts, excessive BCAA oxidation could contribute to depletion of muscle amino acid pools (43). Our findings raise the possibility that lauric acid availability may help tune this balance by limiting BCAA oxidation when alternative fuels are available.

Multiple lines of evidence from the present study indicate that O-GlcNAc signaling contributes to regulation of the BCKDH phosphorylation node. Pharmacological manipulation of O-GlcNAc cycling altered BCKDH phosphorylation, and genetic suppression of OGT attenuated both the C12-dependent increase in BCKDH phosphorylation and the associated suppression of BCAA catabolism. The use of an independent OGT-targeting siRNA reproduced the overall direction of these phosphorylation and flux responses, supporting the interpretation that the effect was not attributable to a single siRNA reagent. These observations position O-GlcNAc signaling as a nutrient-responsive regulatory layer acting on the BCKDH control node.

Interestingly, previous studies in hepatocellular carcinoma cells reported that O-GlcNAcylation of PPM1K stabilizes the protein by preventing ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby promoting BCAA catabolism (19). Our findings suggest a distinct functional outcome in skeletal myotubes, where O-GlcNAc signaling appears to favor maintenance of BCKDH phosphorylation and suppression of BCAA oxidation. While the metabolic demands of proliferating cancer cells and differentiated muscle cells differ substantially, these observations raise the possibility that O-GlcNAcylation may regulate PPM1K through context-dependent mechanisms. One potential explanation is that O-GlcNAc modification interferes with efficient interaction of PPM1K with the BCKDH complex, thereby limiting dephosphorylation of BCKDH. Because both BDK and PPM1K function in close association with the BCKDH complex (4), steric effects on complex assembly or substrate access could provide a plausible mechanism for this regulation (Fig. 9). Our nutrient perturbation experiments further support the idea that responsiveness of the BCKDH regulatory system depends on the metabolic environment. In particular, glutamine availability strongly influenced both basal BCKDH phosphorylation and the response to C12. Dual-tracer experiments using labeled leucine and lauric acid demonstrated that carbon derived from both substrates converges in shared TCA cycle–associated metabolite pools, including glutamate and glutamine. Such convergence indicates that fatty acid-derived carbon and BCAA-derived carbon are metabolically integrated within central carbon metabolism.

Interestingly, although OGT knockdown attenuated the C12-dependent increase in BCKDH phosphorylation, leucine-derived labeling of downstream metabolites increased beyond the level expected from simple reversal of the C12 effect. This finding suggests that O-GlcNAc cycling may exert broader regulatory effects on BCAA metabolism beyond modulation of PPM1K alone. Consistent with this possibility, recent studies have identified BCAT2 as a potential O-GlcNAc target in yeast models (44), raising the possibility that additional enzymes within the BCAA metabolic pathway may be subject to nutrient-dependent O-GlcNAc regulation. Further studies will be required to determine how O-GlcNAc signaling coordinates regulation of multiple components within the BCAA metabolic network.

Based on these observations, we propose a model in which lauric acid engages a nutrient-sensitive regulatory node controlling the phosphorylation state of the BCKDH complex. In this model, O-GlcNAc–dependent regulation of the BDK–PPM1K balance favors maintenance of BCKDH phosphorylation, thereby limiting BCAA oxidative flux when alternative fuels such as fatty acids are available (Fig. 9). Such regulation may contribute to coordination of fuel utilization in skeletal muscle according to nutrient availability.

In skeletal muscle, substrate utilization typically follows a hierarchy in which carbohydrates and lipids serve as primary fuels, whereas BCAA oxidation increases mainly during prolonged energetic stress. Excessive BCAA oxidation can contribute to depletion of muscle amino acid pools and is often associated with muscle catabolism. Our findings therefore raise the possibility that lauric acid availability may help tune this balance by limiting BCAA oxidation when alternative fuels are present. Because lauric acid is present in certain dietary sources such as dairy products (45) and coconut-derived lipids (46), these findings raise the possibility of a previously unrecognized link between fatty acid availability and amino acid metabolism in skeletal muscle.

In addition to the C2C12 experiments, our pharmacological analysis in human skeletal myotubes showed that suppression of O-GlcNAcylation by OSMI-1 reduced global O-GlcNAcylation and produced a BCKDH phosphorylation pattern directionally consistent with that observed in C2C12 myotubes (Fig. S5). Although this experiment does not substitute for in vivo validation, it provides support that the O-GlcNAc-sensitive regulatory relationship between nutrient state and BCKDH phosphorylation may not be unique to the C2C12 cell line.

Several limitations of the present study should be considered. Although our data demonstrate that lauric acid–induced changes in BCKDH phosphorylation depend on PPM1K and are sensitive to O-GlcNAc cycling, the precise molecular target of O-GlcNAc modification within this regulatory system remains to be determined. It is therefore possible that additional regulatory proteins participate in this mechanism. In addition, the isotope tracing experiments were performed using high-resolution MS without positional isotope analysis, and thus the relative contribution of leucine-derived versus lauric acid-derived carbon to certain labeling patterns cannot be fully resolved. Finally, although the present study used differentiated skeletal myotubes and included additional pharmacological validation in human skeletal myotubes, these in vitro models do not fully recapitulate the complexity of intact skeletal muscle. Whether similar regulatory mechanisms operate in vivo therefore remains an important question for future investigation.

In summary, our results identify a regulatory mechanism through which lauric acid influences BCAA metabolism in skeletal myotubes. The data support a model in which lauric acid engages a nutrient-sensitive regulatory node involving O-GlcNAc signaling that modulates the balance between BDK and PPM1K, thereby controlling the phosphorylation state of the BCKDH complex (Fig. 9). Through this mechanism, fatty acid availability can influence BCAA oxidative flux without substantially altering protein synthesis. Together, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized interaction between fatty acid metabolism, nutrient-dependent signaling, and BCAA catabolic regulation in skeletal muscle.

Experimental procedures

Materials

General reagents, including free fatty acids, amino acids, and L-carnitine, were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Tokyo Chemical Industry, or Sigma-Aldrich Japan, unless otherwise noted. General media (e.g., DMEM and LG-DM without pyruvate) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Japan. Heat-inactivated horse serum (HS) was purchased from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and fetal bovine serum (non–heat-inactivated) was purchased from bioWEST (BioWest S.A.S.). Specialized media (e.g., high/LG-DM lacking BCAAs and/or Glutamine) were obtained via custom order from Funakoshi; the formulation was matched to the corresponding base DMEM in all components except for the absence of BCAAs. ThiametG and OSMI-1 were obtained from MedChemExpress. Stable isotope–labeled amino acids were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories. LC–MS-grade solvents and additives were used for metabolite analyses; formic acid was HPLC grade. Details for primary antibodies, PCR primers, siRNAs, and inhibitors are provided in Table S1.

Cell culture

A mouse myoblast cell line (C2C12) was obtained from ATCC. C2C12 myoblasts were obtained from ATCC, expanded as working stocks, and used within four passages after thawing. We used differentiated C2C12 myotubes as a reproducible in vitro model for skeletal muscle metabolism. Although myotubes do not fully recapitulate mature muscle fibers in vivo, C2C12 cells readily differentiate into multinucleated myotubes and are widely used for mechanistic studies of muscle metabolic regulation and signaling (47, 48). C2C12 myoblasts were maintained in DMEM (high glucose) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (growth medium). For differentiation, cells were seeded onto CellBIND six- or 12-well plates (Corning), grown to sub-confluence in growth medium, and then switched to DM, consisting of DMEM (high glucose) supplemented with 2% HS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Media were changed every 2 to 3 days unless otherwise noted. In our laboratory, multinucleated myotubes are typically evident by differentiation day 4 and appear mature by days 5 to 6; therefore, treatments were generally performed on differentiation days 5 to 6 (see individual figure legends for exact timelines and conditions).

Human skeletal myoblasts (HSkM; A12555, Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen) were differentiated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, myoblasts were plated on multiwell plates and cultured in DM formulated with LG-DM (1 g/L) containing 2% HS. Although the supplier protocol indicates myotube formation within ∼48 h, fatty acid treatment experiments were performed on days 5 to 6 to ensure robust myotube maturation.

Fatty acids preparation

Free fatty acids were complexed with fatty acid–free bovine serum albumin (BSA) prior to use. Briefly, each fatty acid was prepared as a 1000 × stock in ethanol (e.g., 200 mM for a 200 μm final concentration). The ethanol stock was added at 5% (v/v) to a medium containing 10% (w/v) fatty acid–free BSA and sonicated at 40 °C for 30 to 40 min to facilitate complex formation, yielding a fatty acid–BSA concentrate (e.g., 10 mM fatty acid) that contained approximately 5% (v/v) ethanol and ≈9.5% (w/v) BSA. This concentrate was then added to the final culture medium at 2% (v/v) to achieve the desired final fatty acid concentration. Under the standard 200 μm condition prepared as previously, the final concentrations of ethanol and BSA in the culture medium were 0.1% (v/v) and ≈0.19% (w/v), respectively. Vehicle controls were prepared identically except that ethanol without fatty acid was used. Across all experiments (including fatty acid dose–response studies), the final concentrations of ethanol and BSA were kept identical across groups. Unless otherwise noted, all media used in fatty acid treatment experiments (including pretreatment and starvation/refeeding phases) were supplemented with 1 mM L-carnitine.

Starvation and refeeding treatments (BCAA and leucine)

After pretreatment with vehicle or fatty acids, myotubes were switched to serum- and BCAA-free medium for 3 h (starvation). Refeeding was initiated using small-volume addition of concentrated media/stocks to maintain identical final nutrient concentrations while minimizing dilution of fatty acids and other medium components. For BCAA refeeding experiments, 1/10 volume of a sterile-filtered 10 × BCAA concentrate (prepared in the matched basal medium) was added to achieve final concentrations of 2 mM leucine, 1 mM isoleucine, and 1 mM valine; for the −BCAA control, 1/10 volume of the corresponding BCAA-free medium was added. For leucine-only refeeding and tracer experiments (including U-[13C6]-eucine flux assays), refeeding was initiated by adding 1/100 volume of a 100 × leucine stock (unlabeled leucine or U-[13C6]-leucine) to achieve the desired final leucine concentration, with 1/100 volume of matched leucine-free medium added for the −Leu control. Cells were incubated for the indicated times after refeeding (e.g., 45 min for phosphorylation assays; 1–3 h or up to 8.5 h for tracer flux assays) before harvest.

Gene silencing

For gene knockdown experiments, cells were transfected with Silencer Select predesigned siRNAs targeting the indicated genes or with a Silencer Select Negative Control siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In the figures, the negative control siRNA is referred to as Scramble (Scr). Knockdown efficiency was verified by quantitative PCR and/or capillary immunoassay (Simple Western). Details of the siRNAs and control RNA used in this study are provided in Table S1.

Capillary electrophoresis–based immunoassay (Simple Western)

Protein abundance and phosphorylation states were analyzed using an automated capillary electrophoresis immunoassay system (Wes or Jess; ProteinSimple), as described previously (49). To prepare whole-cell lysates, myotubes were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed directly in ice-cold Laemmli sample buffer (63 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 2% SDS, 50 mM dithiothreitol, 10% glycerol, 33 mM sodium fluoride, 16 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1.3 mM sodium orthovanadate, and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340; Sigma-Aldrich)). Lysates were homogenized by sonication using a BioRaptor 2 (Sonic Bio Co, Ltd, Japan) in an ice-cold water bath, and clarified by centrifugation (15,000g, 5 min, 4 °C). Protein concentration was determined using an SDS-tolerant Bradford reagent (Abcam), with lysates diluted 5–10-fold prior to measurement, and samples were adjusted to the indicated concentration in the same lysis buffer and denatured at 85 °C for 10 min prior to analysis.

For each antibody, we verified the presence of a detectable peak at the expected apparent molecular weight and confirmed antibody performance within the linear detection range under our assay conditions. Phosphorylation levels were quantified as the ratio of phosphorylated signal to the corresponding total-protein signal (phospho/total). To assess overall sample comparability, housekeeping proteins (e.g., β-tubulin or GAPDH) and/or total protein were measured as needed. Pseudo-blot images were aligned to a dedicated molecular weight ladder capillary, with additional per-capillary alignment based on internal molecular weight standards.

For analyses of global protein O-GlcNAcylation, cells were harvested in the same lysis buffer supplemented with inhibitors to preserve labile modifications during sample preparation. Specifically, lysates were supplemented with Thiamet-G (10 μM) to inhibit OGA and sodium butyrate (10 mM) as a deacetylase inhibitor at the time of cell lysis. Where Thiamet-G or other inhibitors were applied to intact cells as experimental treatments, the concentrations and timing are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Global O-GlcNAcylation and total protein signals were quantified by integrating the smear area between 12 and 220 kDa in the electropherograms, and global O-GlcNAcylation was expressed as the ratio of the O-GlcNAc smear area to the total protein smear area over the same molecular-weight range. Peak detection, quantification, and pseudo-blot imaging were performed using Compass software (ProteinSimple).

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the Maxwell RSC simplyRNA Kit and the Maxwell RSC automated extraction system (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including the recommended DNase I treatment step to minimize genomic DNA contamination. qPCR specificity was further assessed by melt curve analysis. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (DeNovix). RNA samples were adjusted to equal concentrations and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo). Quantitative PCR was performed using THUNDERBIRD Next SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo) on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Ct values were obtained, and relative mRNA expression levels were calculated by the standard curve method. Expression levels were normalized to the mean expression of Rpl7 and Rer1, which have been reported to be stably expressed in C2C12 cells (50). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Stable isotope tracing and LC–HRMS analysis

Flux analysis of U-[13C6]-leucine and ω-13C-lauric acid into acetyl-CoA, TCA cycle–related metabolites, glutamate, and glutamine was performed using an HPLC–high-resolution mass spectrometry platform established with reference to previous studies (51, 52, 53). Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Vanquish HPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mass spectrometer was operated with the following heated electrospray ionization settings: spray voltage, +3400 V in positive ion mode and −2300 V in negative ion mode; ion transfer tube temperature, 320 °C; vaporizer temperature, 400 °C; and resolution, 120,000. To ensure sufficient ion intensity and data points for tracer-derived species, full-scan selected ion monitoring (t-SIM) was performed over an m/z window of ±3 centered on each target analyte. Peak intensities of the relevant 13C isotopologues were quantified using TraceFinder version 5.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For each metabolite, 13C enrichment was calculated as follows:

13C1 enrichment = 13C1 peak intensity/(13C0 + 13C1 + 13C2 peak intensity).

13C2 enrichment = 13C2 peak intensity/(13C0 + 13C1 + 13C2 peak intensity).

approximate 13C6 enrichment = 13C6 peak intensity/(13C0 + 13C6 peak intensity).

Leucine, U-[13C6]-leucine, acetyl-CoA, glutamate, glutamine, and their isotopologues were analyzed using an ACQUITY Premier BEH Z-HILIC column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Waters) at 30 °C and a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min. Mobile phase A consisted of 15 mM ammonium bicarbonate in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 150 mM ammonium bicarbonate in water/acetonitrile (1:9, v/v). The gradient was 0 to 3 min, 90% B; 3 to 9.5 min, 90–65% B; 9.5 to 10.5 min, 65% B; and 10.5 to 15 min, 90% B.

Malate, fumarate, KIC, and their isotopologues were analyzed using an ACQUITY Premier BEH C8 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm; Waters) at 40 °C and a flow rate of 0.25 ml/min. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in methanol. The gradient was 0 to 3.6 min, 2% B; 3.6 to 7.2 min, 2 to 10% B; 7.2 to 12.5 min, 50% B; and 12.5 to 16 min, 2% B. The monitored ions for all analytes and isotopologues are listed in Table S2.

U-[13C6]-leucine incorporation into protein

Incorporation of U-[13C6]-leucine into cellular protein was measured by LC–MS/MS as previously described (49), with minor modifications. Following extraction of intracellular metabolites with 80% methanol, the residual protein pellets were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with water/methanol/isopropanol (1:1:1, v/v/v), and air-dried. Pellets were hydrolyzed in 6 N HCl for 1 h at 150 °C under reduced pressure. Hydrolysates were dried in a centrifugal evaporator (EZ-2 Plus; Genevac) to remove HCl, and the resulting residue was reconstituted in 80% acetonitrile. U-[13C6]-Leucine enrichment was analyzed using a Waters TQ-S micro mass spectrometer coupled to an ACQUITY Premier UPLC system (Waters) in positive electrospray ionization mode. Leucine and isoleucine were chromatographically separated on an Intrada Amino Acid column (Imtakt Corp.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. U-[13C6]-Leucine enrichment and FSR were calculated from the signal intensities of unlabeled leucine (M + 0; MRM 132.1 → 86.15) and U-[13C6]-leucine (M + 6; MRM 138.1 → 91.15).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.1). Post hoc multiple comparisons versus vehicle/control were conducted using Dunnett’s procedure implemented in the multcomp package. Figures were generated in R using ggplot2 (with associated extensions). For each experiment, the primary factor of interest (e.g., fatty acid treatment, siRNA condition, medium/nutrient context, or O-GlcNAc manipulation) was specified a priori. Data were analyzed using two- or three-way ANOVA within the linear model framework, and post hoc comparisons were conducted using multiplicity-adjusted procedures focused on the primary factor (typically Dunnett’s tests versus the vehicle/control group). When interactions were detected, planned contrasts were evaluated within the relevant strata (e.g., within −BCAA or + BCAA) without crosswise comparisons unless explicitly stated. Comparisons between −BCAA and +BCAA were treated as secondary analyses unless noted otherwise. Secondary analyses were predefined exploratory comparisons and were interpreted descriptively. Exact models, post hoc tests, and adjusted p values are provided in the corresponding figure legends. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± SD with individual biological replicates shown as dots. Biological replicates represent measurements from separate culture wells (typically from independent plates) prepared and treated in parallel unless otherwise noted.
