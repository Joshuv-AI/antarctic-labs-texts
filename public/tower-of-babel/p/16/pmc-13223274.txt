Differences in composition and diversity of the gut microbiota of post-menopausal breast cancer patients
Authors: Nadia Kabbej, John D. Sommerville, Raad Z. Gharaibeh, Khloe Dang, Erika Atencio, Erinn Rosenkrantz, Dominique Day, Frederick Ashby, Taqwa Naas, Josee Gauthier, Rachel Newsome, Christian Jobin, Amira Quevedo, Mansour MohamadZadeh, Coy D. Heldermon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota colonizes the intestinal tract with essential roles in immune function, gut epithelial integrity, and metabolic signaling. To investigate the relationship between microbiota composition and breast cancer development, stool samples were collected from 27 treatment naïve post-menopausal breast cancer patients of varying hormone receptor statuses and 25 post-menopausal participants without breast cancer who served as controls. Samples from breast cancer patients and controls were compared for significant differences (p<0.05) in alpha and beta diversity. Significant differences in alpha diversity were observed between all breast cancer types compared to controls (padj=0.042), and between ER+Her2- breast cancer compared to controls (padj=0.006). Using principal coordinate analyses (PCA), significant differences in beta diversity were identified between all breast cancer types compared to controls (padj=0.048), and ER+Her2- breast cancer compared to controls (padj=0.036). Further analyses identified key microbiota genera of microbiota related to breast cancer development and progression. Notably, Akkermansia, a genus associated with gut barrier dysfunction, was reduced in ER+Her2- patients. Overall, this study reports on alterations in microbiota composition in stool samples from untreated postmenopausal breast cancer patients compared to control subjects. The data obtained could provide important insights into the association between altered microbiota and breast cancer progression and guide future patient screening and prevention efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47558-8.

Introduction

Breast cancer has recently surpassed lung cancer as the most common cancer affecting women worldwide and is the second leading cause of female cancer deaths (10.3322/caac.21660). An estimated 319,750 U.S. women are expected to receive a breast cancer diagnosis in 2025, accounting for 32% of new cancer cases in women1. Current research on breast cancer focuses on discovering novel treatments to improve prognoses; however, much less is known about the factors that initially drive breast cancer progression. Alteration in gut microbiota, a community of organisms colonizing the intestinal tract, is one factor that has drawn particular interest in cancer research2. A healthy gut microbiome maintains the epithelial integrity of the gut, is essential for immune function, and produces metabolites involved in regulating cancer pathways3. When the gut microbiota environment is negatively altered, there can be a loss of one or more of these functions, increasing the probability of breast cancer development. For example, some genera of gut microbiota such as Bacteroides and Firmicutes produce B-glucuronidase, an enzyme that regulates the conjugation state of estrogen. When the gut microbial environment is disrupted, B-glucuronidase may excessively deconjugate estrogen, thus aberrantly increasing circulating estrogen levels in the body3,4. Elevated levels of circulating estrogen can readily bind to the Estrogen Receptor (ER) and promote the development of hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer, the most common breast cancer among women4. Other mechanisms proposed in the literature relate breast cancer development to altered or diminished microbial production of cancer-preventing metabolites such as cadaverine, lithocholic acid, enterolactone, and butyrate3,5,6.

In general, women who have been diagnosed with breast cancer have differences in their microbial diversity and composition when compared to healthy controls.7 However, because of the complexity and variation of gut microbiota amongst individuals, study findings have been inconsistent and difficult to apply to the general population. The variations in expression status of ER, Progesterone Receptor (PR), and Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (HER2) in a tumor introduces added complexity, as these reflect subtypes of breast cancer that vary greatly in the pathways and mechanisms that drive their progression. There is very limited current research, to our knowledge, evaluating microbiota of women with different breast cancer subtypes. Identifying global functions of microbiota that are lost or gained in breast cancer-related changes to the gut microbiota would elicit a concrete, generalizable framework for understanding gut microbiota mechanisms in early breast cancer among distinct types.

To identify and quantify species and strains of gut microbiota within individuals, current sequencing techniques employ short-read sequencing of ribosomal RNA (16 S rRNA), an advanced technique that offers insight into species, composition, and diversity of an individual’s microbiome8. In this study, fecal microbiota samples from post-menopausal women with newly diagnosed breast cancer were compared to age-matched, healthy controls using 16 S rRNA sequencing to determine differences in function, composition, and diversity that may be suggestive of a connection between changes in the gut microbiota and increased risk for breast cancer development.

Methods

Subjects

This study was approved by the University of Florida Institutional Review Board. This study was performed in accordance with all relevant guidelines and regulations. Post-menopausal women between the ages of 40 and 100 were recruited for this study. Patients in the experimental group were recruited based on a recent breast cancer diagnosis of any of the following: ER+, HER2+, ER+HER2+, or ER-HER2- breast cancer subtypes. Participants in the control group were recruited based on having a recent normal mammogram. Participants were excluded from the study for the following: ductal carcinoma in situ, recent antibiotic usage within one month, steroid usage, previous bowel resection, current gastrostomy tube, radiation exposure, Crohn’s Disease, Inflammatory Bowel Syndrome (IBS), active infection, prior transplant, immunosuppressive therapy, chemotherapy, and previous cancers (with the exception of non-melanoma skin cancer diagnosed within the last 5 years). Once baseline eligibility was reached and informed consent was obtained, participants were given a stool sample kit consisting of a box with a mailing address, a cooler, two ice packs, gloves, two collection tubes, one with Invitek DNA stabilizer and the other with anaerobic media (8.76 mM Sodium Thioglycollate, 8.1 mM Sodium Phosphate (Dibasic), 51.33 mM Sodium Chloride, 2.68 mM Potassium Chloride, 1.47 mM Potassium Phosphate (Monobasic), 0.41 mM Magnesium Sulfate, 8.25 mM L-Cysteine, 3.98 µM Resazurin, 15% v/v Glycerol in water), and a stool collection hat. Participants collected fecal samples at home and mailed them to the laboratory. Fecal samples from breast cancer patients were collected after diagnosis but before patients had received their first dose of chemotherapy or radiation. All samples were stored at -80 °F immediately upon receipt and until DNA extraction was performed.

In total, 60 samples were received by the laboratory. Demographics and tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) status for all samples can be located in Table 1. One sample was excluded because it did not have enough reads (< 5) during further analysis. Seven samples were excluded because they did not meet enrollment criteria or had no located consent form. Fifty-two samples were used in the final analysis. Of the fifty-two samples, twenty-seven were breast cancer samples. Specifically, two were ER+HER2+, five were ER-HER2- (triple negative), seventeen were ER+HER2-, and three were HER2 + only. The remaining twenty-five samples were from control group participants.

16 S rRNA sample preparation, sequencing, & bioinformatics

DNA was extracted from human fecal samples preserved in DNA stabilizer using the DNeasy 96 PowerSoil Pro QIAcube HT (QIAGEN). Briefly, each tube of preserved feces was completely homogenized and aliquoted. These homogenized aliquots were placed one at a time into a biosafety cabinet to slightly thaw to allow removal of 100–200 mg of frozen fecal slurry, which was directly placed into a 1.7-mL sterile Eppendorf tube. This tube was centrifuged at 10,000 RPM for 5 min. The DNA stabilizer supernatant was removed, and 50–70 mg of stool was promptly aliquoted into a 96 well PowerBead Pro Plate (QIAGEN). Samples were homogenized via bead beating in lysis buffer using the TissueLyser II (QIAGEN) before subsequent processing by the QIAcube HT as previously described9. Following fecal DNA extraction, the 16 S rRNA gene V3-V4 hypervariable region was amplified using barcoded primer pairs 341 F (5 =-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3 =) and 785R (5 =-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3 =) with universal Illumina paired-end adapter sequences. PCR products were purified, quantified, and pooled as described previously and sequenced with an Illumina MiSeq10in a single run of the Illumina MiSeq (2 × 300).

Demultiplexed reads were fed to the DADA2 pipeline11 for primer sequence removal, quality filtering, correction of Illumina amplicon sequencing errors and dereplication, pair merging followed by amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) generation and chimera removal. Taxonomic classification was then performed using DADA2 assignTaxonom and addSpecies functions utilizing SILVA reference dataset (v. 138.1). Except for “multithread parameter”, default DADA2 parameters were used: errorEstimationFunction = loessErrfun, selfConsist = FALSE, pool = FALSE, priors = character(0), verbose = TRUE. The parameter “multithread” was set to TRUE. Total ASV’s found were 4605; total ASV’s used in the analysis were 4602. We removed any ASV that were classified as non-bacterial (2) and all singleton ASVs (1).

Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) was generated using R phyloseq12 package from Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix used the log10 normalized counts as described previously13. Differences in sample clustering between the groups were tested using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) using adonis2 function from R’s vegan package14. Alpha diversity (Shannon diversity index) was calculated using phyloseq R package from the rarefied counts (set to 63,956 representing the minimum read count in all samples) and differences between groups were tested using R’s gls (Linear Model Using Generalized Least Squares). The Linear Model for Differential Abundance Analysis of High-dimensional Compositional Data (LinDA)15 was used for taxa analysis. We corrected the p-values for multiple testing using R’s p. adjust function with method set to BH (Benjamini and Hochberg)16.

Results

Gut microbial alpha and beta diversity differ in post-menopausal breast cancer patients compared to controls

In total, 52 samples were sequenced from post-menopausal breast cancer patients (n = 27) and post-menopausal healthy controls (n = 25). Post-menopausal breast cancer samples and post-menopausal controls accounted for age, BMI, and ethnicity (p > 0.05) in all analyses. A total of 5,810,814 reads were generated with an average of 111,746 for each sample. Following QC, 5,212,714 reads were retained with an average of 100,244 for each sample.

Alpha diversity, or within sample diversity, was calculated for all breast cancer types compared to healthy controls and ER+HER2- breast cancer compared to controls using the Generalized Least Squares Model (GLS) and the Shannon index. There was a significant difference in alpha diversity amongst all breast cancer types and healthy controls (padj=0.042) and ER+HER2- breast cancer and healthy controls (padj=0.006). Healthy controls exhibited a higher mean Shannon index than breast cancer patients, thus higher within sample diversity (Fig. 2). An additional metric of alpha diversity using Chao1 was performed with a similar trend to Shannon; these results can be found in Supplementary Fig. 1. Information regarding a breast cancer patient’s tumor size (T0-T4) and nodal status (N0-N3), which is used to determine staging, was known at the time of sample acquisition. To determine if there were differences in alpha diversity based on the tumor and nodal status of the patient, the same GLS and Shannon index analysis was performed. There were no significant differences in alpha diversity between tumor and nodal status of breast cancer patients.

Taxa analysis of post-menopausal breast cancer patients and healthy controls

Taxa analyses were conducted on all breast cancer types vs. healthy controls and ER+HER2- breast cancer vs. healthy controls. Taxa analysis was done using aggregated ASVs at the genus level. Age, BMI, and ethnicity were controlled for in the analysis (p>0.05) and any taxa that were detected to be significantly different due to age, BMI, or ethnicity were excluded.

When comparing all breast cancer patients to healthy controls, a total of 130 gut microbial genera were identified amongst the samples after sequencing. After adjusting for age, BMI, and ethnicity, 18 microbiota genera were found to be statistically significant (padj<0.05) between breast cancer types and healthy controls. 5/18 genera were found to be decreased, while 13/18 were increased. Discussion

Breast cancer is the second leading cause of cancer deaths in women, yet there is limited research into modifiable risk factors that may contribute to its development. The identification of these risk factors may not only decrease breast cancer risk but also help guide our approach to future screening. One proposed mechanism in the development of breast cancer is a dysregulated gut microbiome, which can potentiate chronic inflammation and disrupt gut integrity and function. Here, we used short read sequencing to determine that breast cancer patients, particularly ER+HER2- patients, exhibited significantly lower alpha and beta diversity than age-matched controls, and a significantly different composition of bacteria with functions potentially related to breast cancer development23,28,32,33.This suggests an altered microbiome in post-menopausal patients with breast cancer. Our results are similar to previous research by Goedert et al. and Bobin-Dubigeon et al., who also report decreased diversity in postmenopausal breast cancer patients7,17. Decreased alpha diversity in particular is predictive of worse gastrointestinal side effects during chemotherapeutic treatment and is a poor prognostic factor in triple-negative breast cancer18,19. Not only is lower diversity associated with the development of breast cancer, but it may also impact treatment response and side effect profiles20.

With regards to individual organisms with roles related to carcinogenesis, the genus Akkermansia was found to be significantly decreased (padj=0.00822) in ER+HER2- patients compared to controls. Akkermansia is a colonizer of the intestinal mucosa with protective functions against the development of IBD, obesity, and Type II diabetes21. Within the gut, it has anti-tumor effects through its ability to target PD-1/PD-L1; is immunoprotective against microbial pathogens; and produces short-chain fatty acids that interact with T cells to decrease systemic inflammation and promote homeostasis of the gut immune system22. Decreased levels of Akkermansia have been previously implicated in breast cancer microbial studies, where researchers found that lower abundance of Akkermansia in early breast cancer patients was associated with higher fat masses and decreased microbial alpha diversity23. It is known that obesity increases the risk for breast cancer development through increased aromatization of androstenediones in fat cells24. However, BMI was controlled for in our analysis and there was no association of BMI with the Akkermansia difference in our sample, thus the decreased Akkermansia we report here suggests the involvement of Akkermansia in a non-fat mass related carcinogenic mechanism.

In fecal samples of our triple negative breast cancer patients, the following butyrate-producing bacteria were found to be less abundant when compared to controls: Anaerostipes hadrus, Clostridium inocuum, Anaerobutyricum hallii and Eubacterium US25,26. Butyrate is a short chain fatty acid which promotes gut health by maintaining gut barrier integrity, inhibiting inflammatory molecules, balancing the composition of different microbial organisms in the gut, and acting as a molecule with anti-carcinogenic properties27–29. Recent research has postulated that butyrate is a protective factor against the development of gastric, colon, and breast cancer through a variety of different signaling mechanisms30–32. With regards to anti-carcinogenic properties, butyrate acts to inhibit histone deacetylation (HDACs), thus opening chromatin and allowing for the transcription of regulatory genes that control aberrant cell growth28. Decreased abundance of butyrate has been shown to lead to loss of inhibition of HDAC8, an important histone de-acetylator in the JAK2/STAT pathway28. This, in turn, leads to unregulated oncogenic pathway signaling and decreased apoptosis, conferring immortality to cells. The decreased abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria that we report in triple negative breast cancer samples may be one possible contributor to carcinogenesis. A recent study by Zhang et al. reported similarly decreased levels of butyrate in fecal samples of breast cancer patients32; we are the first to report decreased abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria in triple negative breast cancer patients specifically. The proposed mechanism for this is related to reduced expression of G-protein coupled receptors, FFAR2 and FFAR3, in TNBC cell lines.33 Butyrate binds to these receptors in breast tissue and has been shown to reduce actin polymerization and invasion of cells, thus potentially reducing metastases34. While we did not measure receptor expression, the decreased abundance we report may add contributing information into the mechanisms driving the early metastases and aggressive nature of TNBC.

There are several limitations to address in this study. First, the sample size of the study is relatively small, and the study itself was limited to post-menopausal women. While post-menopausal women were purposely chosen to better control for the effects of estrogen on the gut microbiome, our results are not generalizable to pre-menopausal women, precluding extrapolation to a large population which breast cancer affects. Additionally, the study design was cross-sectional, in that fecal samples were analyzed at one point in time. Though we attempted to control for known factors that affect the microbiome at the time of patient consent, we cannot rule out user error or environmental factors such as the time of sample collection to freezer which may have impacted the results. Because this study was conducted after a patient received their diagnosis but before chemotherapeutic treatment, there is no way to know if the microbial changes we report was a causative factor in breast cancer development or was alternatively a reactive change to chronic inflammation caused by breast cancer. While we discuss the effects of butyrate-producing bacteria in this study, we do not have functional analyses that would prove these bacteria are indeed effecting butyrate production differences. Thus, the discussion is theoretical based on mechanisms described previously in the literature. While this study was intended to guide screening and prevention measures for breast cancer patients, the lack of emergence of an organism with a clear mechanism of action linked to breast cancer makes this objective less likely. Much of gut microbial and cancer research has begun to focus on a patient’s gut microbial profile as a predictor of immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment response35. The results of this study may better be applied to guide future studies on this topic, namely how hormone receptor status and gut microbial profiles might impact what individual immunotherapy and/or chemotherapy regimen is chosen for a breast cancer patient.

Our study adds to the growing body of literature that gut microbial changes exist in breast cancer patients, specifically in treatment naïve post-menopausal patients of varying hormone receptor statuses. Here, we employed short-read sequencing to assess differences in the composition and diversity of the microbiota. To further characterize species of bacteria, fungi, and viruses that may be present in the fecal samples, we are currently in the process of employing long-read sequencing, a more comprehensive sequencing technique that will provide us with insight into the functions and virulence factors of these organisms.
