Folate receptor beta targeting of tumor‐associated macrophages improves lymphoma cell responses to CD19 CAR T cells
Authors: Kerttu Kalander, Katri Oksa, Selma Sorri, Hector Monzo, Suvi‐Katri Leivonen, Päivi M Ojala, Sirpa Leppä
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies have revolutionized the treatment of patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL). However, approximately half of the patients experience lymphoma progression after CD19 CAR T cell therapy. Immunosuppressive, M2‐like tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) contribute to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) and may facilitate resistance to CAR T cell therapy. Here, we identified folate receptor beta (FRβ) as a specific marker of M2‐like TAMs and a predictor of poor survival in multiple independent DLBCL cohorts. Co‐culture studies of lymphoma‐macrophage spheroids revealed reciprocal interactions between lymphoma cells and M2‐like macrophages. Lymphoma cell co‐cultures promoted the differentiation of monocytes into M2‐like macrophages, while M2‐like macrophages fostered lymphoma cell growth and interfered with CD19 CAR T cell‐mediated killing of lymphoma cells. We demonstrated that M2‑like macrophages drive CAR T cells toward an exhausted phenotype and validated this finding using data from patients treated with CD19 CAR T cell therapy. Lastly, we generated FRβ‐targeting CAR T cells and used them prior to CD19 CAR T cells to successfully improve lymphoma cell killing. Taken together, the results suggest active crosstalk between lymphoma cells and M2‐like macrophages, as well as TAM‐mediated resistance mechanisms to CD19 CAR T cells, which can be circumvented by FRβ CAR T cells targeting the M2‐like TAMs. These results support the use of macrophage‐targeting to improve CAR T cell therapy outcomes in DLBCL.

INTRODUCTION

Diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common aggressive lymphoid cancer in adults. While anthracycline‐based immunochemotherapy is curative for most patients, 30%–40% exhibit primary refractory disease or experience progression later during follow‐up. 1 Patients who develop relapsed or refractory (R/R) DLBCL can be considered for chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy, where autologous T cells are isolated and genetically engineered ex vivo to express a CAR that specifically recognizes a tumor‐associated antigen on the surface of malignant cells. 2 , 3 CD19, a ubiquitously expressed cell surface marker on B‐lymphocytes, is the most commonly targeted antigen for CAR T cell therapy in B‐cell lymphomas. Data from Phase II trials indicate durable responses to CD19‐targeted CAR T cells as third‐line or later therapy in approximately 40% of patients with R/R DLBCL. 2 , 4 , 5 Additionally, two Phase III studies have shown superiority of CD19 CAR T cell therapy over salvage immunochemotherapy followed by autologous stem cell transplantation. 6 , 7

Despite these compelling improvements in DLBCL patient care, approximately half of the patients treated with CD19 CAR T cells ultimately develop progressive disease. Among the factors contributing to resistance to therapy is the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).8, 9, 10 However, the crosstalk between malignant cells, TME, and CAR T cells and the mechanisms of therapeutic resistance remain poorly defined.

In primary DLBCL, various immune cell populations and markers within the TME are associated with patient outcomes.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 M2‐like, tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) appear to facilitate immunosuppression in the TME and correlate with poor survival.16, 17, 18, 19 TAMs represent a highly heterogeneous population of macrophages within the TME, exhibiting distinct phenotypic, transcriptional, functional, and spatial characteristics.16, 20, 21, 22 However, a simplified model categorizing TAMs into M1‐like and M2‐like subtypes, representing opposite ends of the dynamic TAM polarization spectrum, is confirmed and commonly used in preclinical models studying TAM functionality. 23

M1‐like and M2‐like TAMs exhibit distinct cell surface markers and functions, which can either promote tumor growth (M2‐like) or boost antitumor immunity (M1‐like). While TAMs in solid tumors predominantly facilitate tumor progression through metabolic, migratory, and immunosuppressive pathways, those in hematological malignancies, including lymphomas, tend to reinforce malignant cell survival and immune evasion. 19 Consequently, immunotherapeutic strategies that target these cells through depletion, re‐education, or blockade of recruitment offer promising avenues for overcoming treatment resistance and improving patient outcomes.

The contribution of M2‐like TAMs in the immunosuppressive TME sparked our interest in establishing clinically applicable strategies to counteract TAM‐mediated immunosuppression in DLBCL. One promising, yet less explored, approach for TAM inhibition is the use of CAR T cells, which have been previously investigated in solid tumors and acute myeloid leukemia.24, 25, 26 To determine if CD19 CAR T cell responses in DLBCL could be improved by reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME, we set out to identify M2‐like TAM‐specific biomarkers within DLBCL tumor tissue. We identified folate receptor beta (FRβ) as a cell surface marker specific to M2‐like TAMs and as a predictor of poor survival across multiple independent DLBCL datasets.27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 Co‐culture studies of differentiated macrophages and lymphoma spheroids revealed reciprocal interactions between lymphoma cells and M2‐like macrophages as well as between M2‐like TAMs and CAR‐T cells, which contributed to the resistance of CAR T cells. Finally, we generated FRβ CAR T cells and co‐administered them with CD19 CAR T cells, which improved lymphoma cell killing. These studies support the development of macrophage‐targeted CAR T cells as a way to potentiate endogenous anti‐lymphoma immunity.

METHODS

Study populations and CIBERSORTx

The Arthur et al. (EGAD00001003783, n = 301) 34 and Reddy et al. datasets (EGAS00001002606, n = 624) 27 were obtained from the European Genome–phenome Archive. The Schmitz et al. dataset was obtained from the National Institute of Health database of Genotypes and Phenotypes (phs001444.v2.p1, n = 523). 28 Dubois et al. (GSE87371, n = 223), 33 Lenz et al. (GSE10846, n = 414), 30 Sha et al. (GSE117556, n = 928), 31 Visco et al. (GSE31312, n = 498), 29 Lacy et al. (GSE98588, n = 1149), 32 and Locke et al. (GSE248835, n = 256) 10 were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus. Li et al. single‐cell atlas of large B‐cell lymphoma data was obtained from CellXGene. 35

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on a total of 117 primary DLBCL samples. Tissue microarrays (TMAs) were constructed from formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue blocks.

In vitro killing assays

CD19 CAR T cells

To assess the killing function of the CD19 CAR T cells, we performed killing assays against our lymphoma cell lines (OCI‐ly7, suDHL10, and suDHL5). A total of 5000 lymphoma cells per well were plated in RPMI into 96‐well plates (ViewPlate, 6005182, Revvity, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), and CAR T or control non‐transduced (NT) T cells were added at different effector to target (E:T) ratios (4, 2, and 1). After 72 h, the luciferase signal from lymphoma cells was measured using Steady Glo reagent (Promega, E2520, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) and subsequently measured with FLUOstar Omega (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany). Comparisons were made against untreated control cells.

For spheroid CD19 CAR T cell killing assays, lymphoma co‐culture spheroids were created with M1‐ or M2‐like macrophages, or monocytes at a 1:1 ratio, or with lymphoma cells alone. These spheroids were then cultured for 1–3 days to allow spheroid formation. The amount of CD19 CAR T cells was determined based on the killing efficacy observed in lymphoma monoculture killing assays. Consequently, CD19 CAR T cells were added at an E:T ratio of 1 (OCI‐ly7 and suDHL10) or 4 (suDHL5), considering the lymphoma cell proliferation after initiating the co‐culture. After 72 h, Steady Glo was used to measure the lymphoma cell‐derived luciferase signal, and signal comparisons were made with the respective co‐culture condition controls that had no added T cells.

For conditioned media (CM) CD19 CAR T cell killing assays, CM were collected from M2‐like macrophage cultures (72 h), from M2‐like macrophage and lymphoma co‐cultures (24/48 h), or the respective controls. Lymphoma cells were plated in a mixture of CM (75%) and fresh RPMI (25%), and lymphoma cell‐derived luciferase was quantified 72 h later as described above.

Combined targeting

To target both lymphoma cells and M2‐like macrophages, M1‐like/M2‐like/monocyte lymphoma co‐culture spheroids or lymphoma cells alone were targeted using either a pretreatment or simultaneous treatment model. In the pretreatment model, FRβ CAR T cells were added to OCI‐ly7 (2 500 cells of each type), suDHL10 (500 cells of each type) or suDHL5 (1250 cells of each type) spheroid co‐cultures at a 1:1 ratio with the macrophages/monocytes, and 3 (OCI‐ly7, suDHL5) or 2 (suDHL10) days later, CD19 CAR T cells were introduced at an E:T ratio of 1 (OCI‐ly7) or 2 (suDHL10, suDHL5) to the lymphoma cells. In the simultaneous treatment model, both CAR T types were added after a 3‐day (OCI‐ly7, suDHL5) or 2‐day (suDHL10) co‐culture period. Additional media was supplied in the wells at the 3‐day (OCI‐ly7, suDHL5) or 2‐day (suDHL10) time point. For each condition, corresponding NT and GFP CAR T cell controls were prepared by replacing FRβ, CD19, or both CAR T cells with control cells. Three/two days after the addition of CD19 CAR T cells, lymphoma cell viability was assessed using Steady Glo. The luminescence in each condition was initially compared with the corresponding NT cell‐treated control co‐cultures to determine the relative percentage of lymphoma killing. The difference between CD19 CAR T and NT treatment and CD19 CAR T and FRβ/GFP was then assessed.

To follow M2‐like macrophage targeting throughout the assay, the macrophages were stained with CellTracker RED CMTPX (C34552, Invitrogen) prior to co‐culture and imaged at Day 3 and Day 6. Images were taken using phase contrast and fluorescence modes with a Nikon Eclipse Ts2 microscope and a Nikon DS‐Fi3 12V camera (Japan) at 10× magnification.

RESULTS

Folate receptor beta is an M2‐like TAM‐specific marker associated with poor outcomes in patients with DLBCL

To investigate the clinical importance of M2‐like TAMs in primary and R/R DLBCL, we analyzed lymphoma gene expression profiles across eight independent cohorts of primary DLBCL patients27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 and in a cohort of R/R DLBCL patients treated with CD19 CAR T cells 10 (Figure S1A and Table S1). First, we performed CIBERSORTx deconvolution analysis on the transcriptomic data to estimate the proportions of distinct cell populations within the lymphoma TME. This analysis uncovered a heterogeneous TME, including highly variable M1‐ and M2‐like TAM proportions between patients and datasets (Figure 1A). A high proportion of M2‐like TAMs was associated with unfavorable overall survival (OS) in patients treated with immunochemotherapy across five of the eight study cohorts and with event‐free survival (EFS) in the R/R DLBCL cohort (Figure 1B,C), and further validated through multivariable analysis, adjusted for international prognostic index (IPI) (Table S2; IPI scores were not available in two datasets).

Next, we identified genes that were specifically expressed in the M2‐like TAMs in those study cohorts where M2‐like TAMs had a prognostic impact on survival. FRβ, encoded by FOLR2, has been previously found to be enriched in M2‐like TAMs within the solid tumor TME, and has also been targeted with CAR T cells, leading to improved responses in an ovarian cancer mouse model and acute myeloid leukemia. 24 , 25 , 36 , 37 We observed that FOLR2 expression was highly conserved in M2‐like TAMs across all six primary DLBCL cohorts (Figure 2A), even more so than the widely accepted M2 marker CD163 (Figure 2B). To reinforce the specificity of FOLR2 expression to M2‐like TAMs, we analyzed the single‑cell atlas of LBCL, 35 which provides comprehensive gene‑expression data on the LBCL TME. FOLR2 was most highly expressed in an M2 macrophage cell subset (M2 macrophages expressing MRC1) and showed virtually no expression in cell types outside the myeloid compartment (Figure 2C).

FOLR2 gene expression was also associated with poor OS in five of the six primary DLBCL cohorts (Figure 2D). While no correlation was found with lymphoma stage (data available from four datasets), FOLR2 expression was slightly higher in the ABC subtype in two of five patient cohorts with subtype data available (Figure S1B,C). In multivariable analysis adjusting for IPI, the association between high FOLR2 expression and poor survival remained significant in three of the four datasets (Table S3). The proportion of patients with high co‐expression of FOLR2 and CD163 was an even stronger predictor of poor OS than either marker alone in five of the six datasets (Figure 3A). The association of high FOLR2 and CD163 co‐expression with poor OS was confirmed in a multivariable analysis adjusted for IPI score in two cohorts (Table S4).

The FOLR2 gene was not included in the NanoString‐derived dataset from CD19 CAR T cell‐treated patients. 10 Therefore, we used MS4A6A as a surrogate marker for FOLR2, as it was identified among the top 15 genes that correlated with FOLR2 expression across all six study cohorts (Figure S1D), and showed a conserved expression pattern in M2‐like macrophages (Figure S1E). Accordingly, we observed that high MS4A6A expression alone was also associated with poor EFS (Figure S1F), and high co‐expression of MS4A6A and CD163 correlated with even worse EFS than MS4A6A alone (Figure S1G).

We next sought to investigate the expression and localization of the FRβ protein in DLBCL tissue. Immunofluorescence staining of a tissue microarray from 117 patients with primary DLBCL showed co‐localization of FRβ with CD163 and the pan‐macrophage marker CD68 (Table 1 and Figure 3B).

Most lymphomas positive for FRβ expression also tested positive for CD163 (91%, 82/90), further confirming FRβ as a reliable marker for M2‐like TAMs. FRβ expression alone was strongly associated with poor OS (Figure 3C), and co‐expression of FRβ and CD163 was associated with even worse OS than FRβ only (Figure 3D,E). The survival association of FRβ alone or when co‐expressed with CD163 was also validated in a multivariable model adjusted for the IPI score (Figure 3F,G). Together, these results suggest that FOLR2/FRβ is a biomarker for M2‐like TAMs in DLBCL.

To gain further functional insight into FRβ+ TAMs in DLBCL, we correlated FOLR2 expression with the abundance of immune cells in the TME. We found that in addition to M2‐like TAMs, FOLR2 expression positively correlated with monocytes, CD8+ cells, naïve B‐cells, and plasma cells (Figure 4A). Based on the pathways enriched for DEGs, DLBCLs with high FOLR2 expression showed high expression of genes related to immunosuppression, immune evasion, and drug resistance, including those involved in oxidative phosphorylation, complement activation, and STAT3 signaling (Figures 4B,C and S2A). Additionally, interferon gamma and alpha responses were also observed, which likely reflect the presence of CD8+ cells in TME. Finally, focusing on the Schmitz cohort, we identified DEGs between M2‐like TAM and FOLR2 high and low samples and generated a gene expression signature based on the DEGs with the highest and lowest t‐statistic values. This allowed clustering of the patients into two groups according to the signature, with the M2 and FOLR2 high cluster showing worse OS (Figure 4D,E).

In vitro differentiated M2‐like macrophages express FRβ and promote lymphoma cell proliferation

To assess the impact of M1‐ and M2‐like macrophages on lymphoma cell growth, we differentiated primary monocytes using polarizing cytokines, which produced morphologically distinct M1‐like macrophages with a “fried egg” appearance and M2‐like macrophages with a more elongated cell shape (Figures 5A and S3A). Successful differentiation into the two macrophage types was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels. M2‐like macrophages exhibited higher mRNA levels of FOLR2 and other common M2‐like TAM markers (CD163, IL‐10, and CD206) compared to M1‐like macrophages (Figure 5B). M2‐like macrophages were also positive for cell surface expression of FRβ, CD68, and CD163 (Figure 5C). In contrast, M1‐like macrophages demonstrated significantly lower expression of FOLR2, CD163, IL‐10, and CD206 genes than M2‐like macrophages, alongside higher expression of M1‐TAM markers CXCL10, TNFα, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 (Figures 5B and S3B). Additionally, M1‐like macrophages were positive for cell surface expression of CD68 but negative for FRβ and exhibited only low expression of CD163 (Figure 5C).

M2‐like macrophages have been associated with various tumor‐promoting traits. 13 , 22 , 38 To investigate the interaction between macrophages and lymphoma cells, we established co‐cultures of lymphoma cell lines OCI‐ly7, suDHL10, and suDHL5 with adherent M1‐ or M2‐like macrophages. Each lymphoma cell line displayed increased proliferation when co‐cultured with M2‐like macrophages (Figure 5D). In contrast, co‐culture with M1‐like macrophages led to decreased proliferation or cell death.

To evaluate whether lymphoma growth promotion depended on cell‐cell contacts or on macrophage secretome, we cultured lymphoma cells with and without macrophage‐CM. We observed a dose‐dependent increase in proliferation across all three lymphoma cell lines when using M2‐like macrophage‐derived CM (Figure 5E–G). We also analyzed the proliferation of lymphoma cells following transfer from direct co‐cultures into fresh media. The lymphoma cells derived from the M2‐like macrophage co‐cultures continued to proliferate at a more rapid rate (Figure S3C), indicating that the increased proliferation was sustained for at least 72 h.

Lymphoma cells and macrophages elicit reciprocal interactions in a three‐dimensional spheroid model

To facilitate a closer contact between the non‐adherent lymphoma cells and adherent macrophages, we employed a lymphoma spheroid model that leverages the inherent clustering growth pattern of the lymphoma cells OCI‐ly7, suDHL10, and suDHL5 (Figure 6A). Additionally, we used freshly isolated, undifferentiated monocytes to mimic the natural differentiation of monocytes into tissue‐associated macrophages.

The macrophages/monocytes persisted in the lymphoma spheroids for 7 days of culture (Figure 6B–D). Of the three lymphoma cell lines, spheroids derived from OCI‐ly7 appeared more compact when compared to suDHL10 and suDHL5 spheroids (Figures 6B,C and S4A). While quantification of the spheroid‐macrophage co‐cultures was not possible due to their fragility upon fibrin‐embedding and antibody staining procedures, macrophages were found to maintain their differentiation states in the spheroids, as indicated by CD163 expression in the M2‐like macrophages on Day 7. Moreover, the expression of CD163 and FRβ in macrophages within the spheroid was further confirmed with flow cytometry and staining of paraffin‐embedded co‐culture spheroids (Figures 6E and S4B,C). In flow cytometry analysis, we observed that freshly isolated monocytes also had detectable CD163 expression but negligible FRβ expression (Figure S4D). Interestingly, the incorporated monocytes had clearly sustained CD163 expression and had acquired a phenotype similar to that of M2‐like macrophages upon prolonged co‐culture with the OCI‐ly7 cells, indicating that they differentiated spontaneously into M2‐like macrophages upon co‐culture with lymphoma spheroids. The results suggest that interactions with the lymphoma cells promoted the differentiation of monocytes into M2‐like macrophages.

Moreover, the M1‐like macrophages in the spheroid co‐cultures were not completely negative for CD163 or FRβ, while the freshly differentiated, M1‐like macrophages from monotypic cultures showed only very low CD163 expression and no FRβ expression (Figures 5C and 6E). This might indicate a gradual re‐polarization of M1‐like macrophages toward M2‐like during co‐culture.

M2‐like macrophages interfere with CD19 CAR T cell killing efficacy

Given the immunosuppressive and tumor‐promoting roles of M2‐like TAMs, we investigated whether macrophages impair CD19 CAR T cell‐mediated killing of lymphoma cells. All three DLBCL cell lines were 100% positive for CD19 (Figure S5A), and CD19 CAR T cells effectively killed them in a dose‐dependent manner as monotypic cultures (Figures 7A and S5B,C). We observed some differences in killing efficacy between the cell lines, with suDHL5 cells being more resistant than the other two. In contrast, the NT cells did not affect the viability of lymphoma cells.

Next, we tested the killing efficacy of CD19 CAR T cells against macrophage‐lymphoma spheroid co‐cultures. We observed a higher luminescence signal from the M2‐like macrophage‐containing spheroids than from the control and M1‐like macrophage‐containing spheroids (Figure 7B), indicating reduced killing efficacy. The luminescence of each condition was always compared with its respective untreated control to eliminate discrepancies due to differences in cell proliferation across the co‐culture conditions. Thus, our results indicate that the presence of M2‐like macrophages reduces the efficacy of CD19 CAR T cell killing. The monocyte‐ and M1‐like spheroid co‐cultures exhibited a similar effect, albeit to a lesser extent, supporting the notion that lymphoma cells induce differentiation of monocytes and M1‐like macrophages toward tumor‐promoting, M2‐like macrophages. However, CM from M2‐like macrophage culture did not affect the lymphoma cell killing efficacy of CD19 CAR T cells (Figure S5D,E), indicating that the M2‐like macrophage‐mediated suppression is contact‐dependent.

M2‐like macrophages drive early CAR T cell exhaustion

We next investigated the M2‐like macrophage‐mediated mechanism of CD19 CAR T cell resistance. Like lymphoma cells, CAR T cells also showed increased proliferation both upon contact and in response to CM from M2‐like macrophages (Figure 7C,D). This was corroborated by higher expression of proliferation marker Ki67 (Figure 7E) in CD19 CAR T cells isolated from M2‐like macrophage‐containing co‐cultures. Intriguingly, markers of T cell activation (IFNγ, TNFα, GZMB, PRF1,and IL2RA) were higher when M2‐like macrophages and lymphoma cells were co‐cultured (Figures 7F and S5F). Moreover, CAR T cells from M2‐like macrophage‐containing co‐cultures presented with an increased exhaustion phenotype (LAG3, PDCD1, HAVCR2, CTLA4, and TIGIT) (Figures 7G and S5G). While all measured exhaustion markers showed a significant increase, PDCD1 expression was particularly elevated. This effect was contact‑dependent, as cultures exposed only to CM did not exhibit significant changes in exhaustion or activation marker expression (Figure S5H,I). Altogether, these findings indicate that M2‑like macrophages support CAR T cell proliferation and enhance CAR T cell activation when target cells are present. However, this initial activation is rapidly overridden by M2‑like macrophage‐induced, rapid‑onset T cell exhaustion, ultimately leading to reduced CAR T cell efficacy.

To further strengthen our findings, we analyzed single‐cell RNA sequencing data of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from CD19 CAR T cell‐treated patients 1 week post‐infusion. Consistent with our findings from co‐cultures, CAR T cells from 14 patients with a high baseline proportion of monocytes exhibited increased expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 (Figure 7H). While activation marker expression remained largely unchanged (Figure 7I), CD19 CAR T cells from the monocyte‐high group showed increased expression of exhaustion markers (Figure 7J). In this patient cohort, CAR T cells from monocyte‑high patients showed particularly elevated expression of LAG3 and CTLA4, and overall increases across all exhaustion markers, consistent with a more terminally exhausted phenotype (Figure 7K).

FRβ CAR T cells specifically target M2‐like macrophages

Next, we sought to eliminate the M2‐like macrophages by directly targeting them. We generated FRβ‐targeting CAR T cells with a high‐affinity FRβ‐recognizing scFv 26 (Figure S6A). The lentiviral transduction efficiency of the FRβ CAR T cells ranged between 60% and 80% (Figure 8A). The specificity of the FRβ CAR T cells was first tested against differentiated M1‐like and M2‐like macrophages. A clear dose‐dependent decrease in M2‐like macrophages was seen, whereas M1‐like macrophages were not similarly affected (Figures 8B and S6B).

Combined targeting of FRβ‐ and CD19‐expressing cells increases lymphoma cell killing

Next, we tested how targeting FRβ‐positive macrophages affects CD19 CAR T cell‐mediated killing of lymphoma cells. We administered both the CD19 and FRβ CAR T cells either simultaneously or sequentially, that is, first targeting the macrophages with FRβ CAR T cells, then adding the CD19 CAR T cells 2–3 days later. Additionally, we used GFP CAR T cells as a mock control to ensure specificity for the FRβ CAR T cells. Simultaneous co‐administration of FRβ and CD19 CAR T cells slightly improved lymphoma cell killing in the co‐cultures with M2‐like macrophages compared to targeting CD19 alone, though not significantly (Figures 8C and S6C). In contrast, in the M1‐like macrophage co‐cultures or monotypic control conditions (CTRL), there was no difference in killing efficacy compared to CD19 CAR T cell targeting alone in the OCI‐ly7 treatment (Figure 8C). When lymphoma spheroid co‐cultures were first treated with the FRβ CAR T cells before CD19 CAR T cells, we observed an even greater increase in lymphoma cell killing in the co‐cultures with M2‐like macrophages (Figures 8D and S6D). Interestingly, pretreatment with FRβ CAR T cells of lymphoma spheroid co‐cultures, both with M1‐like macrophages and monocytes, also led to enhanced killing compared to targeting CD19 alone. This effect was minor in the spheroid monotypic cultures, indicating that it was mediated by specific FRβ targeting. Thus, the data suggest that FRβ CAR T cell pretreatment results in more effective FRβ CAR activation and that even the low FRβ expression seen in the co‐cultured M1‐like macrophages (Figure 6E) is enough to activate the CAR T cells. However, during simultaneous CAR T cell exposure, significantly lower target expression in the M1‐like macrophages does not allow sufficient FRβ CAR T cell activation.

The increased efficacy of CD19 CAR T cells in the combined targeting approach was specific to M2‐like macrophages, as treatment with the mock GFP CAR T cells did not consistently improve lymphoma cell killing (Figures 8C,D and S6C,D).

To further elucidate the difference in CD19 CAR T efficacy between the pretreatment and simultaneous targeting assays, we stained the M2‐macrophages with a cell tracer prior to adding them into co‐culture to follow the consequences of FRβ CAR T targeting. Under pretreatment conditions, M2 macrophages were effectively cleared, whereas with simultaneous targeting, macrophages were still detected at Day 6 after 3 days of FRβ targeting (Figure 8E). The sequential addition of the two CAR T cell types enabled CD19 CAR T cells to enter a primed culture with virtually no remaining M2‑like macrophages, leading to more effective CAR T cell function.

DISCUSSION

M2‐like TAMs have been associated with worse survival in various cancers, including lymphomas. 16 , 18 , 39 , 40 In a recent study, crosstalk between follicular lymphoma and follicular dendritic cells, which mimics the germinal centers, was shown to promote tumor growth and, via the secretion of CCL2 and CSF‐1, facilitate monocyte recruitment, differentiation, and polarization toward an M2‐like protumoral phenotype. 41 However, little is known about the crosstalk between DLBCL cells and TAMs, as well as the mechanisms underlying therapeutic resistance. Here, we provide insights into the reciprocal interactions between lymphoma cells, M2‐like macrophages, and CAR T cells, with a particular focus on treatment outcomes in response to immunochemotherapy and CAR T cell therapy in patients with primary and R/R DLBCL.

Consistent with prior reports, 16 , 18 , 39 our analysis of various lymphoma cohorts revealed that a high proportion of M2‐like TAMs predicts poor outcomes in both primary and R/R DLBCL. Notably, we identified FOLR2/FRβ as a specific marker for M2‐like TAMs in DLBCL, and co‐expression of FRβ with CD163 revealed a subgroup of patients with particularly poor outcomes independent of IPI scores. FRβ has been identified as a transporter for folic acid, with its activity dependent on anti‐inflammatory conditions. 42 , 43 It is also recognized as a highly specific marker of immunosuppressive myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and TAMs in various solid cancers, where it exhibits diverse identities and even opposing functions—either tumor‐suppressing or tumor‐promoting—depending on the niches and stages of cancer development. 24 , 36 , 44 To our knowledge, FRβ has not been previously studied in lymphomas.

Our study builds on existing concepts of M2‐like and specifically FRβ+ TAMs, which prevent efficient responses to immunochemotherapy and CAR T cells within the DLBCL TME. After confirming that a subgroup of M2‐like FRβ+ TAMs is associated with poor outcomes, our functional studies revealed a reciprocal crosstalk between TAMs, DLBCL, and CD19 CAR T cells in a cell co‐culture model. While lymphoma cells promoted primary monocyte differentiation into FRβ+ M2‐like macrophages, these macrophages, in turn, supported lymphoma cell growth. The exact mechanism requires further research but is likely to involve secreted cytokines such as M‐CSF, IL‐4, IL‐13, IL‐10, and TGFβ. 23 , 45 , 46 Along with our data on DLBCL, mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) cells have been shown to polarize monocytes into macrophages through the secretion of IL‐10 and CSF1, which support MCL growth and survival. 47 Additionally, CSF1R blockade has been shown to transform the immunosuppressive TME into an immunosupportive environment and restore anti‐lymphoma immunity. 39 , 41 , 47 Interestingly, in addition to supporting lymphoma cell growth, we found that M2‑like macrophages promote T cell proliferation, an effect that occurs independently of direct cell–cell contact. However, this initial phase of enhanced T cell proliferation is eventually outweighed by M2‑like macrophage‐mediated T cell exhaustion. Notably, previous studies have reported reduced T cell proliferation in the presence of M2‑like macrophages, 48 but these effects were observed only at a later, 10‑day time point, in contrast to the 3‑day window examined in our study.

In lymphoma patients, the number/proportion of monocytes in circulation before CAR T cell therapy, as well as a higher TME contexture, have been associated with poor outcomes. However, little is known of the specific mechanism or related targets. 10 , 49 Overall, our findings demonstrate active reciprocal interplay between lymphoma cells and M2‐like macrophages, as well as TAM‐mediated exhaustion and suppression of CD19 CAR T cells.

The FRβ CAR T cells specifically targeted the M2‐like macrophages and facilitated circumventing the TAM‐mediated resistance to CD19 CAR T cells. Similar to findings by Rodriguez‐Garcia et al., 24 in their murine ovarian cancer model targeting macrophages with murine FRβ CAR T cells, preconditioning of the TME with FRβ CAR T cells followed by the CD19 CAR T cell treatment improved the killing efficacy more than the simultaneous addition of both cell types. Moreover, two recent studies targeting M2‑like macrophages in solid tumors using anti‐FOLR2‑ or anti‐TREM2 CAR T cells further support TAM targeting as a promising strategy to enhance CAR T cell efficacy. 50 , 51 Both anti‑FOLR2 and anti‑TREM2 CAR T cells demonstrated safe and durable activity across multiple murine solid tumor models, underscoring the therapeutic potential of TME reprogramming. Altogether, this suggests that priming the TME to be less immunosuppressive protects CD19 CAR T cells from suppression, thereby enabling more effective CAR T cell functions. In contrast to macrophage targeting or re‐education with small‐molecule inhibitors or checkpoint blockade, 39 , 48 , 52 , 53 our approach would not require continuous administration and is considered more specific. 24 , 38 , 50

In conclusion, we identified FRβ as a target specific to M2‐like TAMs in DLBCL. A high proportion of FRβ+ TAMs was associated with poor survival, and targeting M2‐like macrophages in an in vitro lymphoma‐macrophage co‐culture model with FRβ CAR‐T cells enhanced the efficacy of CD19 CAR‐T cells. We provide evidence for reciprocal crosstalk between M2‐like macrophages and CAR T cells, initially supporting T cell proliferation but ultimately leading to a T cell exhaustion phenotype. The strength of our study lies in combining multiple clinical datasets with functional analyses to uncover crosstalk between lymphoma cells and M2‐like macrophages, as well as TAM‐mediated mechanisms of resistance to CD19 CAR T cells. However, validation of these findings in animal models is warranted to establish their translational relevance. Together, the findings highlight the importance of identifying clinically relevant and selectively targetable TAM subsets and provide a basis for further research into targeting immunosuppressive M2‐like TAMs in the DLBCL TME.
