Foam Sclerotherapy as an alternate method for Periorbital Dermoid cases with Surgical Constraints: A Prospective study
Authors: Ankita Aishwarya, Adarsh K Singh, Rachna Agrawal, Vishwas Kapoor, Neha Nigam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:To analyze the efficacy and safety of foam sclerotherapy in medically unfit patients with periorbital dermoid cysts.Design:This was a prospective interventional single-center study conducted from February 2018 to January 2024.Participants:All patients who were diagnosed with periorbital cysts and were medically unfit for surgery were included in the study.Methods:All patients were treated with foam sclerotherapy using 3% sodium tetradecyl sulfate (STS). The cyst was aspirated using a wide-bore needle, followed by the injection of intralesional foam sclerotherapy in a ratio of 2:1 of the aspirate. The aspirated contents were sent for cytopathology. Outcome measures included regression of the lesion, side-effects, and recurrence.Result:Eleven patients were included in the study who were diagnosed with periorbital dermoid, and all were medically unfit for the surgery. Cysts were located in the medial angular region (four cases), lateral (three cases), intradiploic (two cases), and medial canthus (two cases). Comorbidities included poorly controlled diabetes with chronic kidney disease (five patients), severe coronary insufficiency (three patients), hypercoagulable states (two patients), and restrictive pulmonary disease with severe trismus (one patient). The mean aspirate was 3.5 mL, and a range of 1–3 injections was required. At a mean follow-up of 22.6 months, 82% showed complete remission. Two patients had partial resolution. There were no sclerotherapy-related complications or recurrence.Conclusion:Foam sclerotherapy with 3% STS is a safe and effective alternative for treating periorbital dermoid in medically unfit patients. It helps to achieve complete resolution in the majority of cases without significant complications.

Dermoid and epidermoid cysts account for 5% of all orbital lesions, 23–34% of head and neck cysts, with 61% of the latter occurring in the periorbital region, among the pediatric age group.[12345] Dermoid cysts occur mostly in the first decade of life but can be seen in the middle-aged group, while epidermoid cysts can occur from birth to the elderly.[67891011]

The term “dermoid” is generally used to refer to dermoid cysts, epidermoid cysts, and teratoid cysts.[691213] However, based on histopathology, they are categorized into: epidermoid cysts (lined with simple squamous epithelium), dermoid cysts (containing skin adnexa in the cyst wall), and teratoid cysts (featuring other tissues such as muscle, cartilage, and bone).[131415] Dermoids are usually congenital and primarily arise from embryonic ectodermal implantation during neural groove closure.[1617]

The treatment of choice for periorbital dermoid cysts is total surgical excision with intact capsule. Due to the high risk of rupture, incomplete removal, recurrence, and surgical scar, various methods have been introduced, like minimally invasive endoscopic techniques, percutaneous drainage and ablation, and foam sclerotherapy.[181920] However, none of the studies have documented the treatment outcomes among medically unfit patients. Our study evaluates the efficacy and safety of foam sclerotherapy in treating periorbital dermoids in patients who are not suitable candidates for surgery.

Methods

This was a prospective interventional study conducted from February 2018 to January 2024 at SGPGI, Lucknow, India. The study was approved by the Institute Ethics Committee SGPGI (IEC Code: 2018-98-IP-138). The study included all patients with clinico-radiological confirmed cases of congenital periorbital cystic lesions with bony fixity, patients who were considered unfit for surgery by anesthetists and physicians irrespective of age, and those who provided informed consent. The study excluded surgically fit patients, those who failed to give informed consent, patients with a history of spontaneous rupture or inflammation around the cyst, and radiological presence of calcifications and tooth or bone-like components within the cyst.

The diagnosis of periorbital dermoid was confirmed clinico-radiologically, supported by history and old photographs. Contrast Enhanced Computed Tomography (CECT)scans confirmed the presence of well-defined cystic lesions.

Prior to the procedure, all patients underwent skin sensitivity testing for 3% sodium tetradecyl sulfate (STS) using a 0.1 mL intradermal injection to rule out hypersensitivity reactions. All patients underwent a foam sclerotherapy procedure under local anesthesia [Fig. 1]. The perimeter and epicenter of the lesion were marked [Fig. 1a]. A 0.2 mL dose of 2% xylocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline was injected intradermally over the epicenter. An 18-gauge (G) needle mounted on a 10-cc syringe was then used to aspirate the pultaceous material from the cyst until it collapsed completely [Fig. 1b and c]. To prepare the foam, 1 mL of 30 mg/mL STS was froth-mixed with room air drawn through a filter using the modified Tessari tourbillon technique [Fig. 1d].[21] The sclerosant was injected in ratio of 2:1 of the aspirate [Fig. 1e].[20] The cyst aspirate was sent for cytopathological evaluation.

Technique of intralesional foam sclerotherapy using STS 3%. (a): Marked Perimeter and epicenter of the lesion; (b): Using 18G needle the cyst was punctured at epicenter; (c): Cyst was aspirated till it was completely collapsed, (d): 3 mL of pultaceous material was aspirated, (e): 1 mL of 3% STS and 1 mL of filtered air was used to create foam by using three way cannula and rapid swirling motion (f): 1.5 mL of the foam was injected. STS: Sodium tetradecyl sulfate

Primary outcome measures were clinical resolution of the lesion, improvement in vision, decrease in diplopia, and improvement in displacement. Clinical resolution of the lesion was defined as resolution of the clinically visible lesion with no palpable mass and resolution of associated symptoms. Partial clinical resolution was defined as ≤ 50% reduction of the visible lesion along with partial improvement in symptoms. Secondary outcome measures were side-effects, recurrence, and aesthetic blemish. Statistical package for social sciences, version 21, was used for data analysis.

Results

There were 11 patients with periorbital dermoids who were medically unfit for surgery, and were included in the study. The group exhibited various concurrent disorders: five patients had poorly controlled diabetes with chronic kidney disease, three had severe coronary insufficiency, two had hypercoagulable states, and one had restrictive pulmonary disease with severe trismus.

All patients had a unilateral lesion. Anatomically, the cysts were located as follows: four cases (36%) in the medial angular region [Fig. 2], three cases (27%) in the lateral region, two cases (18%) intradiploic [Fig. 3], and two cases (18%) at the medial canthus [Fig. 4]. The mean age of presentation was 32 years, ranging from 11 to 59 years. There was a slight male predominance, with six males (54%) and five females (45%). All cases were congenital, while one case presented with an increase in size following trauma.

A 30 year old male with hypercoagulable state presented with decreased vision (BCVA 6/24) in left eye, associated with diplopia in primary and upgaze since 14 days. (a): Inferior displacement of LE by 5 mm with mild ptosis, (b): CECT scan of orbit shows intradiploic cystic mass seen in superior orbital rim with globe indentation, (c) At 13 months following two sessions of foam sclerotherapy, BCVA in LE has improved to 6/9 with resolution of diplopia

A 29-year-old male with poor diabetes control and chronic kidney disease nearing his marriage presented with (a): a mass over medial canthus since birth and is gradually progressive since 2 weeks, (b): CECT scan orbit showed a well-ovoid cystic mass with enhancing wall and non-enhancing lumen. Irregular dense areas seen at the base presumably correspond to clumping of epithelial debris within the lumen of the cyst. (c): At 13 months, there was no residual lesion or aesthetic blemish

The most common presenting symptom was a painless, gradually progressive swelling observed in all cases (100%). Other associated symptoms included blurring of vision and diplopia in two cases (18%). The best corrected visual acuity ranged from 6/9 to 6/6 Snellen chart in nine cases (82%), while two patients (18%) had visual acuity ranging from 6/36 to 6/24. Inferior globe displacement was noted in two patients (18%), with measurements ranging from 3 mm to 5 mm. Foam sclerotherapy was performed due to a documented increase in lesion size in all patients, raising concerns about further progression, cosmetic disfigurement, and potential impact on visual function. Additionally, four patients had upcoming social events, specifically marriage, where cosmetic disfigurement was a primary concern.

The mean volume of cyst aspirate was 3.5 mL, ranging from 1 to 5 mL. The aspirate was typically pultaceous in nature. Cytopathology confirmed the presence of ectodermal contents consistent with dermoid cysts. Microscopically, the cytospin smears were paucicellular with a necrotic background. May-Grünwald Giemsa staining revealed abundant keratinous debris, sparse hair shafts admixed with inflammatory cells, including lymphocytes and polymorphs, suggesting a chronic inflammatory response. At higher magnification, numerous cystic macrophages were observed, some containing engulfed debris, further supporting the cystic nature of the lesion. These cytological findings, in conjunction with the clinical and radiological features, provided definitive confirmation of the diagnosis [Fig. 5]. Seven patients (64%) required only a single injection, while four patients (36%) required multiple injections (ranging from two to three injections), which were given at 3-weekly intervals.

None of the patients experienced postoperative pain or significant localized inflammation. At a mean follow-up period of 22.6 months (range 6 to 39 months), nine patients (82%) achieved complete clinical resolution with no palpable residual lesions. Best corrected visual acuity was in the range of 6/9 to 6/6 on the Snellen chart. None of the patients had diplopia post-treatment.

Two patients (18%) had partial resolution despite 2–3 injections. One of them had residual inferior displacement of 1 mm, which remained stable at 15 months of follow-up. The other had a dermoid present since childhood that had increased in size post-trauma, with a residual lesion that remained stable at 17 months of follow-up. One patient had developed hyperpigmentation at the site of injection, which resolved on its own in 3 weeks; however, no other sclerotherapy-related complications were noted. There were no recurrences or aesthetic blemishes throughout the follow-up period.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of foam sclerotherapy in surgically unfit patients with periorbital cysts, and the second one to explore its use in treating periorbital dermoids.[20] Although surgical excision with an intact capsule is the preferred treatment,[5] it has a higher risk of recurrence (50% to 100%) if incomplete removal occurs, mostly with sutural dermoids.[2223] The surgical approach also has a risk of intraoperative rupture,[1024] fistula formation,[23] and large scar formation,[25] which may have psychological implications.[26] To mitigate cosmetic concerns, endoscopic techniques have been introduced, but they carry a high risk of rupture and nerve injury.[13182527] Other minimal intervention procedures like percutaneous drainage and ablation have been explored,[1928] and foam sclerotherapy has been reported in a single study with a few cases.[20] In 2023, Pushker et al.[29] introduced the transconjunctival approach as a novel technique for treating external angular dermoid cysts in patients with mobile cysts confined to the eyelid, though it requires surgical expertise and offers limited surgical space. However, there is a lack of evidence specifically among surgically unfit patients.

The age of presentation in our study ranged from 11 to 59 years, differing from previously reported ages of 1 month to 45 years.[18] This discrepancy in presentation may be attributed to the lack of surgical fitness among these patients and slow growing nature of the dermoids. Notably, one patient was over 50 years old in our study, highlighting that orbital dermoids can progress even later in life.[630]

Most of the cases were congenital, which is consistent with the majority of the literature, with a slight male predominance.[20] The most common location was medial angular (36%), contrasting with other studies where lateral angular locations were more frequent.[101631] This variation might be due to differences in sample size, geographic location, and associated comorbidities.

One patient had developed hyperpigmentation at the injection site, which was reversible; this was also reported in a case series where hyperpigmentation was typically due to stab wound entry.[20] However, none of our patients required a stab wound or suturing, as it was a closed-chamber procedure, and patients with calcifications or tooth-like structures in the cyst were excluded in our study. The need for a pressure bandage, as used by Naik et al.,[20] was also not needed in our series.

Traditional statistical analysis indicated that age was the sole distinguishing factor between dermoids and epidermoids, though both can appear in younger patients. Radiological assessments were accurate 83% of the time but were not reliable for differentiating between the two types. The treatment approach remains consistent for both conditions.[31]

The primary limitation of this study is the small sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. However, it is rare to find periorbital dermoid and simultaneous systemic comorbidity that precludes surgery as a primary option. The second limitation was the absence of histopathological analysis, although clinical and radiological features were consistent with dermoid cysts. Additionally, while dermoid cysts are known to contain thick pultaceous material with numerous sticky hairs that can pose challenges during aspiration, all patients in our study required only a single aspiration, and no instances of needle blockage were encountered. However, the technique may be less effective in cases with denser or more complex cystic contents, highlighting a potential area for further research. The technique remains unsuitable for cysts with calcified structures. Repeated imaging was avoided to minimize radiation exposure; however, radiographic follow-up was selectively employed to confirm cyst status in partially resolved cases. Future prospective studies can explore the role of serial imaging using MRI or B-scan ultrasonography as safer, radiation-free alternatives in objectively documenting the pattern of cyst regression and recurrence following foam sclerotherapy.

Despite these limitations, foam sclerotherapy offers significant benefits. STS is a detergent sclerosant and an anionic surfactant which works by disrupting lipid membranes and denaturing proteins in epithelial cells lining the cyst wall.[20] As documented in various epithelial-lined cysts (like hepatic or thyroid cysts), the sclerosant induces cytotoxic injury to the squamous epithelium, triggering a local inflammatory response that leads to fibrosis and collapse of the cyst cavity.[3233] When used as a foam, it provides an advantage over the liquid by prolonging contact with the cyst wall, enhancing uniform sclerosis, and increasing the efficacy by reducing rapid dilution.

It is a minimally invasive, closed-chamber technique that reduces the risk of cyst rupture and spread. The procedure can be performed quickly under local anesthesia, with faster recovery and no scarring. It is especially beneficial for patients who are unfit for surgery and can be easily repeated if needed.

Conclusion

Despite the current limitations, our study highlights foam sclerotherapy with STS as a promising and minimally invasive treatment option for periorbital dermoid cysts, particularly in patients who are not ideal candidates for traditional surgery. However, larger studies with longer follow-up are needed to evaluate long-term outcomes and recurrence rates. Additionally, further research is needed to explore the factors contributing to treatment failure or incomplete remission following foam sclerotherapy, as well as the challenges associated with needle aspiration of thick cystic contents. Addressing these aspects could provide valuable insights into optimizing treatment strategies and improving procedural success rates.
