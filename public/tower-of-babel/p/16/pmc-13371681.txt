The adipomyokine paradox and sex-specific metabolic divergence in subclinical hypothyroidism: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sarang Barbind, Alaka Chandak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) increases CVD risk but the threshold for treatment remains controversial. Sex-based metabolic profiles, novel adipocytokines (Irisin/Apelin), and reversibility of vascular remodeling were examined.MethodsA PRISMA 2020-compliant systematic review of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science was conducted using the PROSPERO-aligned search window from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2023. Ten studies met the final eligibility criteria and were synthesized according to outcome comparability. Study quality was assessed using RoB 2 and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and certainty of evidence was considered using GRADE principles.ResultsSCH was associated with a pro-atherogenic phenotype, including higher Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) in observational evidence. Introduction

Subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH), which is characterized by high serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels with normal free thyroxine (fT4), constitutes a considerable public health problem with a worldwide prevalence ranging from 4% to 10% [1, 2]. Although it is considered a benign biochemical condition, longitudinal studies carried out within the Whickham Survey [2] and the Rotterdam Study [3] have conclusively demonstrated that SCH increases the likelihood of developing atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction. Notwithstanding the well-established relationships, the treatment of SCH is arguably one of the most controversial topics in contemporary endocrinology, partly owing to the age and gender diversity among patients with this disease [4, 5]. Current guidance generally supports stronger consideration of levothyroxine therapy when TSH is persistently ≥ 10 mIU/L, whereas treatment decisions for TSH 4.5–10 mIU/L remain individualized and should consider symptoms, age, thyroid autoimmunity, pregnancy intention, lipid profile, and cardiovascular risk [6, 7].

The primary mediator by which cardiovascular disease risk is mediated in SCH is through the induction of a pro-atherogenic lipid profile. SCH without treatment is marked by high total cholesterol concentrations and atherogenic LDL concentrations [8]. New evidence shows that the physical characteristics of lipoproteins are more important than their concentrations, with levels of TSH greater than 10 mIU/L associated with dense and atherogenic LDL sub particles [9]. As a marker for atherogenic potential, the Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP), calculated as a logarithmically scaled triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein ratio, is considered a better determinant of vascular damage in cases of thyroid disease [10, 11].

Apart from traditional lipids, new scientific papers have started addressing the “Adipomyokines Paradox,” whereby there is a disorder of tissue-specific messengers, such as Irisin and Apelin. Irisin, an exercise-induced myokine, is elevated in SCH individuals, perhaps as a sign of “compensatory metabolic stress” and possibly a defensive heat production system to counteract muscle oxidative damage [12]. On the other hand, the deficiency of Apelin, which serves to protect endothelial cells, indicates vascular impairment at an early stage [13]. This adipomyokines imbalance indicates a persistent “metabolic memory” effect of SCH on skeletal muscles and adipose tissues that might need a sustained euthyroid state to rectify, accounting for the failure of short L-T4 treatment regimens to regulate these markers [14].

These metabolic injuries occur structurally as thickening of the carotid intima-media layer (CIMT), which is an established biomarker for subclinical atherosclerosis. In studies where the effect of levothyroxine (L-T4) substitution therapy was analyzed, such as the seminal work conducted by [15], CIMT regression may be observed, although meta-analysis shows this is only when therapy lasts longer than six months and the TSH level is at least \ge 10 mIU/L [16, 17].

However, what constitutes the main gap in the current literature is the distinct difference in metabolic expression according to one’s sex. As cohort data show, the “architecture of metabolic syndrome” varies substantially depending on the phenotype involved: Younger males, specifically those under 50 years old, have a considerably greater incidence of metabolic syndrome, with the hazard ratio amounting to 1.87 due to visceral obesity [18]. On the other hand, pre-menopausal females have an atherogenic dyslipidemia and hypertension-related phenotype, and the mechanism may be attributed to estrogen deficiency coupled with inadequate thyroid hormones [19–22]. It should also be noted that numerous population-based studies have revealed that TSH affects different components of metabolic syndrome according to one’s gender and age group [23].

Although the number of independent investigations on these innovative biomarkers (Irisin/Apelin) is abundant, an overarching framework linking these with sex-dependent risk traits and their structural vascular consequences has not been developed. This study sought to analyze the link between SCH and cardiometabolic risk among various demographic groups by determining the key L-T4 treatment levels needed to correct subclinical vascular remodeling. Based on evidence gathered from inception until December 2023, We examined whether available evidence supports a phenotype-oriented interpretation of cardiometabolic risk in SCH while recognizing that current data are heterogeneous and mainly based on surrogate outcomes.

Despite growing evidence linking SCH with dyslipidemia, surrogate vascular remodeling, and emerging adipomyokine disturbances, the literature remains fragmented across study designs, sex-specific cohorts, biomarker platforms, and levothyroxine follow-up durations. In particular, few syntheses have integrated three clinically relevant dimensions: (i) qualitative lipid remodeling, including AIP and small dense LDL-related risk; (ii) sex- and age-specific metabolic phenotypes; and (iii) divergent adipomyokine responses after restoration of biochemical euthyroidism. This review, therefore, aimed to synthesize available evidence on cardiometabolic risk in SCH using a bifurcated approach: a quantitative synthesis for standardized lipid and CIMT outcomes and an SWiM-guided narrative synthesis for adipomyokines, where assay and population heterogeneity limited formal pooling. We specifically examined whether the available evidence supports a phenotype-oriented risk framework rather than a purely TSH-centric interpretation of SCH [24, 25].

Scientific rationale and conceptual framework

The adipomyokines paradox: a compensatory hypothesis

The physiological rationale for the current review is based on the concept of the “adipomyokines Paradox” in Subclinical Hypothyroidism (SCH). In contrast to overt hypothyroidism, which involves a complete metabolic shutdown, SCH may represent a state of mild thyroid-axis stress with downstream cardiometabolic consequences. Increased irisin, when observed, may reflect an adaptive or stress-associated adipomyokine response, but this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating [12].

The theory of metabolic imprinting

An important question that will be investigated in this review is the hypothesis of the “metabolic imprint.” It is hypothesized that the constant elevation of TSH leads to an epigenetic and metabolic “imprinting” of fat and muscle cells. We use the term “metabolic imprint” cautiously to describe possible delayed tissue-level recovery in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue after biochemical euthyroidism. This construct should be interpreted as exploratory and not as established epigenetic evidence. This is why L-T4 treatment for less than six months cannot result in a restoration of adipomyokines profile and carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT). The purpose of this review is to find evidence to support the hypothesis that a period of therapeutic lag of at least six months is needed [14, 15].

Sex-specific metabolic architectures

Moreover, this research is based on the theory of “Sexual Dimorphism in Metabolic Syndrome Risk.” According to the findings based on the incidence rates showing that there is a two times higher risk of developing metabolic syndrome among young men [18], we can formulate the following hypothesis:

Young men (< 50 years): The risk is related mainly to visceral obesity and insulin resistance, which results in the increased HOMA-IR and triglycerides.

Materials and methods

Study design and protocol registration

This review was reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1. The review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251011843). The intended search period for this trial according to the PROSPERO registry is from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2023, and all changes have been updated in line with the final manuscript. The overall goal of this study was the identification of the “architecture of metabolic syndrome” in subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH), particularly focusing on sex differences in metabolism and adipomyokine communication.

Data sources and search strategy

A search that was both systematic and comprehensive was done in the PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science databases from their origins up to December 2023. The search strategy included the use of Boolean logic with a mixture of MeSH terms and free-text keywords: “subclinical hypothyroidism,” “thyroid-stimulating hormone,” “carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT),” “atherogenic index of plasma (AIP),” “irisin,” “apelin,” and “metabolic syndrome.” Also, an additional “snowballing” search via manual checking of references from pertinent primary studies and review articles was conducted. The entire search strategy for each database searched, along with Boolean operators used, is detailed in Supplementary File 2.

Eligibility criteria (PICOS framework)

The studies were classified by their outcomes instead of just being categorized according to their study designs. The comparable results regarding lipids and CIMT in the intervention studies were incorporated into the quantitative synthesis while the adipomyokines, AIP, lipoprotein fractions, and phenotype-specific outcomes were synthesized qualitatively.

Pediatric populations (< 18 years), pregnant women, and patients with overt thyroid dysfunction were excluded to eliminate confounding metabolic influences.

Assessment of bias

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots for outcome measures with sufficient numbers of studies. Egger’s test, which can yield statistically invalid results due to low statistical power, was not performed for outcome measures with fewer than 10 studies.

After removal of duplicate records (n = 27), 581 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 426 records were excluded. A total of 155 reports were sought for retrieval, of which 85 were not retrieved or did not provide sufficient accessible full-text information for eligibility assessment. Discussion

For the current investigation, subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) is defined not only as a metabolic abnormality but rather as a heterogeneous cardiometabolic syndrome. The present meta-analysis reveals the association between SCH and a particular “metabolic architecture,” comprising qualitative alteration in lipid metabolism, structural changes in the vasculature, and complicated adipomyokines imbalance, based on a comprehensive assessment of 10 high-quality research articles (n > 1,400).

Sex-specific and phenotype-specific risk divergence

One of the key findings from this literature review is that there is sexual dimorphism associated with cardiometabolic risk factors. The two-fold increase in MetS risk associated with SCH among males HR 1.87 [18] implies a possible synergy between thyroid dysfunction and sex-specific pathways to visceral obesity and insulin resistance. The association between SCH and incident metabolic syndrome appears stronger among younger men in the available cohort evidence, but this finding remains insufficient for direct clinical threshold-setting without external validation.

On the other hand, the risk factors associated with perimenopausal females appear to be driven primarily by “atherogenic switching,” which involves hypertension and dyslipidemia, possibly aggravated by the lack of estrogen-related protection against lipid metabolism. While these phenotypic traits of “young male” and “peri-menopausal” subjects provide a more sophisticated model for personalized medicine, they still stand as empirical data that require validation through longitudinal studies.

Qualitative lipid remodeling and the AIP

Our results support the theory that SCH creates conditions favorable for premature atherosclerosis. Indeed, a statistically significant positive correlation between TSH levels and AIP (r = 0.72) proves the superiority of qualitative parameters over standard lipid profiles in identifying cardiovascular risk factors. Moreover, the increased presence of sdLDL (Pattern B) at TSH ≥ 10 mIU/L is of particular practical significance. Such lipoproteins demonstrate higher oxidative properties and easily pass into the inner layers of the arterial walls, leading to atherosclerosis development. Thus, the approach to evaluating the risks associated with SCH should change from quantitative lipid measurements to qualitative (AIP).

Vascular remodeling and reversibility

The available interventional evidence suggests that SCH may be associated with early vascular wall changes, as reflected by increased carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT). Levothyroxine therapy was associated with a modest reduction in CIMT after sustained treatment, particularly in studies in which biochemical euthyroidism was achieved and lipid parameters improved.

However, CIMT is a surrogate vascular marker, not a hard-cardiovascular endpoint. Therefore, the observed reduction of approximately 0.04–0.11 mm should be interpreted as possible vascular remodeling rather than proof of reduced myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular mortality, or major adverse cardiovascular events.

Overall, current evidence suggests that levothyroxine may improve selected lipid and CIMT outcomes in some SCH patients, especially those with higher baseline TSH or adverse lipid profiles; however, the clinical significance of this improvement remains to be confirmed in larger longitudinal studies.

The adipomyokine paradox: irisin, apelin, and delayed tissue-level recovery

In this review, there is a temporal difference between adipomyokine regulation in SCH, which can be tentatively termed as an “adipomyokine paradox.” There is sufficient data to postulate that even with biochemical euthyroidism restored by levothyroxine, the level of irisin does not decrease, while apelin responds better. This phenomenon does not imply the existence of a definite compensatory mechanism but rather indicates that the normalization of the TSH level and restoration of metabolism in target tissues might have different timelines.

The proposed reasoning involves the presence of changes in signaling in skeletal muscles and adipose tissue due to SCH. Irises is associated with metabolism in skeletal muscle cells, mitochondrial response to oxidative stress, insulin resistance, inflammation, and adipose tissue browning. Hence, increased serum irisin levels despite euthyroidism might result from oxidative and metabolic stress in muscles and adipose tissue rather than persistent biochemical hypothyroidism. Such a delay in the restoration of metabolic homeostasis might imply a certain type of “metabolic imprint,” which would signify a transient effect of previous thyroid hormonal deficiency on target tissues’ metabolism. Nonetheless, such a concept needs further exploration.

However, the results concerning apelin could be a new type of biological marker altogether. Given the involvement of apelin in endothelial biology, vasodilatation, and cardiometabolic homeostasis, the increase of this hormone observed following levothyroxine treatment suggests an improvement in protective endothelial biology to some extent. However, it must be stressed that studies involving apelin have been limited thus far to small sample sizes and short-term follow-up periods.

Therapeutic thresholds: severity, duration, and clinical individualization

The existing data indicate that responses of lipids and vascular surrogates to levothyroxine treatment would be more homogeneous in trials or subpopulations with high baseline TSH, especially TSH ≥ 10 mIU/L, and follow-up long enough to attain stable biochemical euthyroidism. Nevertheless, such results should not be regarded as proof of a definitive threshold for treating overt hypothyroidism with levothyroxine.

In individuals with TSH slightly above the upper limit of normal, especially between 4.5 and 10 mIU/L, the cardiovascular effect of levothyroxine treatment is still unclear. Thus, a personalized decision should be made according to recommendations, taking into account patient age, symptoms, autoimmune thyroid disease, pregnancy plans, lipid panel, cardiovascular risk score, frailty, and patient preferences [6, 7].

Thus, the existing data favor a prudent, phenotype-specific strategy, which implies that levothyroxine therapy would more effectively improve lipid parameters and CIMT in particular phenotypes of SCH. In contrast, there is no scientific basis for using levothyroxine for cardiovascular prevention in all individuals with SCH.

Limitations and future directions

There are various limitations to consider. Firstly, the included studies had a heterogenous study design, which involved both observational cohorts and case-control studies, together with some interventional studies. This makes causal inference impossible and limits the possibility of estimating the effects of interventions uniformly in outcomes. Secondly, there was a small number of studies that reported the levels of adipomyokines. The methodologies used in the assays, the units, the time period during which interventions were carried out, and adjustment for adiposity and/or insulin resistance varied greatly between the studies. As such, the results for adipomyokines were pooled narratively instead of being pooled as definitive effects.

Finally, conclusions based on sex differences have relied upon a few investigations, notably a large population-based cohort for incident metabolic syndrome and a smaller study group among perimenopausal women. There is no standardized confirmation of menopausal status based on hormonal markers, and ages differ in terms of classification across different studies. Next, CIMT and AIP are considered surrogate markers, which offer biologically sensible evidence but do not necessarily mean that there will be decreased rates of myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular mortality, and other adverse events until proven by longitudinal research. Lastly, because the number of studies included for the outcomes is less than 10, publication bias cannot be estimated, hence no Egger’s test was conducted due to insufficient statistical power.

Future research should be focused on adequately powered, multicenter, prospective cohort, and randomized trials involving both sexes, as well as stratified analyses according to age and baseline TSH, adiposity, and autoimmune thyroid disease. The need for uniform adipomyokine assays and serial post-treatment assessments should be considered to assess the applicability of the hypothesis of metabolic imprinting.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review shows that SCH is linked to diverse cardiometabolic risk profiles rather than to any common biochemical state. Current data imply that younger male subjects suffering from SCH are at risk of developing metabolic syndrome in the future, whereas women experiencing perimenopause might have an atherogenic profile dominated by lipids. The use of levothyroxine may be accompanied by favorable changes in specific lipids and vascular markers; nevertheless, these effects are limited to patients with elevated TSH at baseline levels and prolonged treatment duration, and they do not predict clinical events.

The adipomyokines represent a possible new mechanistic clue, although further research is warranted in this regard. Chronic alterations in irisin levels despite being biochemically euthyroid may be caused by incomplete recovery or metabolic imprinting, while apelin is more responsive to acute normalization of euthyroidism. Because the biomarker evidence is limited and heterogeneous, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Overall, the findings support a cautious shift away from a purely TSH-centric interpretation of SCH toward an individualized cardiometabolic risk assessment that incorporates age, sex, adiposity, lipid phenotype, and vascular risk markers. Larger longitudinal studies and randomized trials are required before phenotype-specific screening or treatment thresholds can be recommended for routine clinical practice.
