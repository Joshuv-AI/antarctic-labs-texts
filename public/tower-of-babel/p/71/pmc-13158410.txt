Coagulation protease-activated receptor-2 (PAR2) promotes dyslipidemia, obesity and MASLD through repression of the hepatic pioneer factor HNF4α
Authors: Xinru Chen, Nga Nguyen, Susan E. Turner, Albert K. Tai, Manal F. Abdelmalek, Lidija Covic, Athan Kuliopulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and its more severe version, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), are associated with obesity, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and atherothrombosis for reasons that are not well understood. Protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2), a receptor activated by coagulation proteases that are dysregulated in inflammatory and fibrotic diseases, is emerging as a new drug target for MASH.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of liver specimens from patients who were obese vs. non-obese (n = 105) with the diagnosis of MASLD or MASH to examine the relationship between hepatic PAR2 expression, MASLD/MASH, and obesity. To address the direct contribution of hepatocyte PAR2 to progression of MASLD and obesity, we fed hepatocyte-specific PAR2 knockout mice, whole-body PAR2-deficient mice, and control floxed wild-type mice high-fat diet and conducted metabolic, biochemical, and molecular analyses.ResultsWe discovered that expression of PAR2 is increased by five-to sevenfold (p = 0.002) in the livers of patients with obesity with MASLD and MASH. Hepatocyte-specific deletion of PAR2 caused reductions in body weight, liver fibrosis, and significantly lowered triglycerides and cholesterol levels in plasma and livers of mice fed high-fat diet. Global or hepatocyte-specific deletion of PAR2 resulted in significant improvements in fatty acid metabolic and reverse cholesterol transport pathways in liver. Mechanistic studies revealed that coagulation factor Xa activates a PAR2-cJun pathway that represses the HNF4α pioneer transcription factor which has global adverse effects on liver metabolism. OA-235i, a PAR2 inhibitor, enhanced HNF4α expression in livers and afforded a significant 15% reduction in weight gain (p <0.0001), and improvements in systemic and liver lipid and cholesterol levels in obese mouse models.ConclusionsThese data provide support that targeting hepatic PAR2 may potentially provide broad liver and cardiometabolic benefits to patients with MASLD and MASH.Impact and implicationsDyslipidemia and heightened coagulation are closely linked with poor cardiometabolic outcomes in patients with fatty liver disease, but the connection with progression of MASLD to steatohepatitis (MASH) is complex and not well understood. In this study, the authors now link the factor Xa coagulation protease receptor PAR2 with obesity and dyslipidemia and showed that hepatic PAR2 expression is significantly elevated in patients who are overweight or obese with MASLD and MASH compared with non-obese controls. A liver-homing PAR2 pepducin, OA-235i, conferred significantly lower body weight gain with major improvements in liver histology, fibrosis, and cholesterol and triglyceride levels in mice with MASLD. The translational studies presented here support additional investigation to determine whether a PAR2 inhibitor represents a new therapeutic strategy to treat patients with severe metabolic diseases such as MASLD and MASH.

Introduction

The global obesity epidemic has emerged as a major public health crisis, driving a surge in metabolic disorders, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and atherothrombotic disease. Super-caloric consumption eventually overwhelms the ability of the liver to properly regulate fat and sugar metabolism leading to hepatic insulin resistance, generation of reactive oxygen species and necro-inflammation. MASLD, characterized by excessive hepatic fat accumulation with concomitant cardiometabolic risk factors1,2 has become the most common chronic liver disease affecting nearly 40% of individuals worldwide.3,4 These metabolic disturbances not only promote further steatosis, but also increase the risk of progression to more severe liver pathologies, such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis, and cirrhosis.

Despite extensive efforts targeting specific pathways involved in MASH progression such as de novo lipogenesis, oxidative stress, and inflammation, these approaches have yet to yield any approved drugs.[5], [6], [7] Incretin therapies such as tirzepatide and semaglutide can confer weight loss and reduce liver fat mainly by curtailing appetite and improving insulin sensitivity but do not directly target liver tissue. Prolonged incretin treatments intended to confer improvements in MASH fibrosis may require administration for ∼2-year time periods before showing clinical benefit to patients who are pre-cirrhosis.2,8,9 The recent success and provisional approval of the thyroid hormone receptor β agonist resmetirom highlights the potential advantages of exerting pleiotropic effects on multiple pathways involved in MASH pathogenesis, including improving lipid beta-oxidation, glucose homeostasis, inflammation, and LDL cholesterol.10

Dyslipidemia is closely linked with poor cardiometabolic and liver-related outcomes,[11], [12], [13] but the mechanistic link to fibrotic progression in MASLD to MASH is complex and not well understood.2,14,15 In addition, the steatotic, inflamed liver affects the systemic vasculature through increased glucose production, dyslipidemia, and release of procoagulant factors.9 An emerging regulator of hepatic sugar and fat metabolism linked with coagulation is the G-protein-coupled, protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2).16 As a cell surface sensor of liver-produced coagulation and local inflammatory proteases, PAR2 enables cells to respond—or over-respond—to the dynamic proteolytic microenvironment associated with tissue remodeling and inflammation.[17], [18], [19] PAR2 activation occurs through cleavage of its N-terminal extracellular domain, generating a tethered ligand that transmits transmembrane signals to intracellular G proteins.20 PAR2 activation in macrophages has been implicated in promoting inflammation in visceral adipose tissue and liver in obese rodent models.[21], [22], [23] However, more recent work suggests that the role of PAR2 in the development of hepatic insulin resistance and worsening glycemic indices is mainly attributable to direct effects of hepatocyte PAR2 rather than immune cells on liver function.16 In addition, liver PAR2 was found to be associated with high cholesterol in mice and patients with MASLD and MASH, but the underlying mechanistic basis of this was not clear.24

To address these questions, we explored the actions of PAR2 in both human cellular and mouse diet-induced obesity models, focusing on lipid and cholesterol metabolism. The results presented here shed light on potential mechanisms underlying the metabolic benefits of genetic loss of PAR2 or pharmacologic blockade with pepducin[25], [26], [27] inhibitors. Additionally, we propose a mechanistic model for how PAR2 receptor activation by liver coagulation factor Xa directly influences hepatocytes to regulate overall metabolic homeostasis through suppression of a pioneer transcription factor.

As diabetes was a commonly occurring comorbidity in this MASLD group, we analyzed the liver biopsies in the subgroup of patients without diabetes to determine whether PAR2 expression increased independently of diabetes status. In the patients without diabetes (n = 54), the PAR2 staining of the liver biopsies was even more significantly increased, by ninefold (p = 0.0001) with obesity, indicating that diabetes was not the primary driver of the increased hepatic PAR2 expression in the patients who had obese MASLD (Fig. S1B).

Hepatocyte-specific PAR2-deficiency causes a reduction in body weight, steatosis, and liver fibrosis in obese mice fed HFD

To determine whether hepatocyte PAR2 itself had a direct effect on obesity and MASLD, we tested the effect of specific knockout (KO) of PAR2 in hepatocytes (PAR2ΔHep) after 13 weeks of HFD supplemented with refined sugars. As shown in Fig. 1B, there was a highly significant correlation (R = 0.74, p = 0.0002) between body weight and liver PAR2 expression in PAR2ΔHep mice vs. wild-type (WT; PAR2fl/fl). Similarly, liver weight was significantly correlated (R = 0.81, p <0.0001) with hepatic PAR2 expression in the PAR2ΔHep vs. WT mice fed HFD (Fig. 1C). Histopathology of liver sections indicated that hepatic deletion of PAR2 caused a large reduction in steatosis compared with WT mice (Fig. 1D). In addition, the obese WT mice fed HFD developed liver fibrosis (4 ± 2%) detectable by quantification of collagen staining, which was significantly reduced by >50% in the PAR2ΔHep mice (Fig. 1E and F).

Hepatocyte PAR2 expression promotes hypertriglyceridemia and hypercholesterolemia in mice

To determine whether PAR2 expression in hepatocytes plays a causal role in lipid homeostasis, we measured plasma and liver levels of triglycerides and total cholesterol in PAR2ΔHep vs. WT mice. After 13 weeks HFD, PAR2ΔHep mice had a mean 27% decrease (p <0.05) in liver triglycerides and a 40% decrease (p <0.05) in plasma triglycerides compared with WT mice (Fig. 1G and H). Likewise, the hepatocyte-PAR2 deficient mice had a significant 25% drop in mean liver cholesterol and a 17% drop in mean plasma cholesterol compared with WT mice (Fig. 1I and J). The lower cholesterol and triglyceride levels were not attributable to a reduction in food consumption which was not different between PAR2ΔHep and WT mice (Fig. S1C).

Hepatic PAR2 expression has global effects on gene expression pathways involved in metabolism and inflammation/extracellular matrix organization.

To determine the hepatocyte PAR2-specific signature, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNAseq) of livers from global PAR2-KO, PAR2ΔHep and WT mice after 13 weeks of HFD (GEO: GSE319296). Non-hierarchical clustering analysis of the RNAseq data revealed a potential major role for liver PAR2 in affecting metabolism and inflammation/extracellular matrix organization with 266 genes (q <0.01) up- or downregulated in a three-way analysis comparing PAR2ΔHep with whole-body PAR2-KO and WT. As shown in Fig. 2A, eight different gene clusters appeared which reflected close similarities of expression patterns between the global PAR2-KO livers and the PAR2ΔHep livers (clusters 1 – glucose/lipid metabolism; cluster 5 – extracellular matrix/inflammation) relative to WT. This is consistent with the theory that many of the observed transcriptional effects of global PAR2 deletion on metabolism and fibrosis were the result of the contribution of PAR2 expressed in hepatocytes. By comparison, there were different/opposite gene expression patterns between the global KO and the PAR2ΔHep livers in cluster 3 (anti-inflammatory signaling), cluster 4 (mitochondrial/energy), cluster 7 (translation), and cluster 8 (extracellular matrix/invasion) relative to WT in the three-way analysis. Cluster 2 (serine protease inhibitors) and cluster 6 (innate immune system/signaling) had a mixed pattern of differential gene expression with stronger effects seen in the whole-body PAR2 KO as compared with the PAR2ΔHep livers. Genes that were coordinately up- or downregulated together in the PAR2ΔHep and PAR2-KO vs. WT were identified by having significant -log10[q] cut-off values of >1.301 (q <0.05) using a two-way analysis. In total, there were 2,101 genes that were coordinately up- or downregulated in the PAR2-deficient livers relative to WT as depicted in the volcano plot of Fig. 2B. The genes most upregulated in the PAR2-deficient livers included genes involved in steroid metabolism (Hsd3b5 and Cyp7b1), cholesterol-lipoprotein transport (ApoM, ApoA1, ApoC4, ApoC1, and Lcat), lipid catabolism (Mup7, Mup1, Mup12, and Mup14), and trypsin-class protease inhibition (SerpinA9 and Serpin1e1). The genes most downregulated included genes involved in inflammation (Ly6d, Cidea, Cidec, Gprc5b, Trem2, Itgax, Cx3cr1, Ms4a4c, Ccr2, and Lgals3), fibrosis/extracellular matrix remodeling (Mmp13, Tnc, Timp1, Col3a1, and Col6a3), lipid metabolism (Scd1, Kbtbd11, ApoA4, Hmgcs1, Pnpla3, and Pparg), and cell cycling (Ccnb1 and Arhgap11a).

A gene set enrichment pathway analysis of the expression patterns from the combined PAR2-deficient livers vs. WT was performed using Reactome. The most upregulated pathways in the PAR2-deficient livers were those involved with protein translation and metabolism, mitochondrial activity and respiration, steroid metabolism and plasma lipoprotein clearance (Fig. 2C). The pathways most downregulated in the PAR2-deficient livers were those involved in extracellular matrix and collagen production, cell cycling, and inflammation.

Effect of liver PAR2 deficiency on transcription factor binding site enrichment scores

iRegulon was used to identify enriched transcription factor binding sites within 10 kb of the transcriptional start site of the coordinately expressed genes identified from the two-way analysis of the combined PAR2-deficient strains vs. WT. As shown in Fig. 2D, there were seven transcription factors with strong normalized enrichment scores (NES ≥3) for binding sites near the 2,101 genes that were significantly upregulated or downregulated (q <0.05) in the combined liver PAR2-deficient mice as compared with WT. Of these, four transcription factors (green: HNF4α, Gtf3c2, Irf3, and Brf1) were significantly upregulated themselves and three transcription factors (red: Ets2, Klf6, and Tcf4) were significantly downregulated. Of the upregulated transcription factors, the pioneer factor, HNF4α, had the highest increase in the NES with 605 putative gene targets out of a total of 2,101 genes (Fig. 2D). HNF4α-DNA binding was validated using chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-Atlas and Model-based Analysis of ChIP-Seq 2 (MACS2) of 75 mouse liver HNF4α ChIP-seq datasets mapped to the mouse reference genome (mm10) and filtered with a significance threshold MACS2 score of >50 (q <1 × 10-5) and a maximum score of 1,000 (q <1 × 10-100).28 Examining the 605 genes with high HNF4α NES scores, 554 genes (91.5%) were considered to have HNF4α protein binding located within 10 kb of the target gene transcription start site. Of these, 543 genes (89.8% of 605 genes) had HNF4α protein-binding peaks detected in at least two independent datasets with a median of 23 datasets per each HNF4α peak. HNF4α is of particular interest because it is a master regulator of hepatic lipid and cholesterol metabolism,29 both of which were strongly affected by hepatic deletion of PAR2. Therefore, we aimed to determine whether PAR2 was regulating expression of HNF4α which in turn could be responsible in part for the observed global effects of hepatic PAR2 deletion on metabolism, de novo lipogenesis, and reverse cholesterol transport as depicted in Fig. 2E.

Hepatocyte deletion of PAR2 and effects on HNF4α-regulated lipid and cholesterol genes

To confirm whether the bulk RNAseq data from the PAR2ΔHep mouse livers was accurately reflecting actual mRNA levels from a larger cohort of mice, we conducted quantitative PCR on a large array of genes involved in lipid and cholesterol metabolism from PAR2ΔHep and WT livers. As shown in Fig. 3A, we confirmed that mean hepatic Par2 expression was significantly reduced by 4.4-fold with a twofold increase in HNF4α mRNA and 1.3–2.7-fold decreases in the Ets2 and Klf6 transcription factors in the PAR2ΔHep mouse livers. There were large increases in a number of genes encoding proteins involved in reverse cholesterol and lipoprotein transport (Fxr, Srb1, Hsl, ApoA1, and ApoM), and bile steroid synthesis and secretion (Cyp7a1, Abcb11, and Abcg5) following hepatic deletion of PAR2. Conversely, the PAR2ΔHep livers had drops in expression of genes involved in de novo lipogenesis (Acaca, Fasn, and Scd1), an increase in the Acaa2 gene involved in fatty acid beta-oxidation, and a 7.4-fold drop in Pnpla3 involved in VLDL secretion. We confirmed that expression of HNF4α mRNA was inversely correlated with the increases in PAR2 mRNA (p = 0.007, R = 0.58) in WT and PAR2ΔHep livers (Fig. S2A). Likewise, expression of key HNF4α-regulated genes, Srb1, Cyp7a1, and Fxr were inversely correlated with the increases PAR2 mRNA in WT and PAR2ΔHep livers, and accordingly linearly increased with HNF4α mRNA levels (Fig. S2B–F).

PAR2 inhibits HNF4α expression through bifurcating Gi-Jnk1/2-cJun and Gq-Erk1/2-cEBPα pathways

To investigate which effectors and signaling nodes downstream of PAR2 might be involved in controlling HNF4α, PAR2 was activated with liver coagulation factor Xa in the presence of a large array of inhibitors of downstream signaling components (Fig. 3C). Treatment of Huh7 cells with Xa caused a significant lowering of HNF4α mRNA by 50% (p <0.0001). This inhibition by Xa was completely reversed by a PAR2 pepducin inhibitor, PZ-235.19 The Gq inhibitor YM254890 and Gi inhibitor, pertussis toxin, both partially reversed the Xa-mediated HNF4α suppression. Blockade of Gq effector PKC and downstream MAP kinases, RAF and MEK also partially reversed the Xa-mediated HNF4α suppression. However, the intracellular Ca2+ chelator BAPTA did not, thereby implying a calcium-independent mechanism. Inhibition of the PAR2-Gi-βγ effector β-Arrestin also had no effect on HNF4α. However, inhibition of downstream mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) mediators of Gi, namely MKK4/7 and Jnk1/2, showed partial reversal of PAR2 suppression of HNF4α mRNA. Notably, combined inhibition of MEK1/2 and MKK4/7 completely ablated the ability of Xa to cause suppression of HNF4α mRNA (Fig. 3C). This would indicate that the full PAR2 suppressive effects on HNF4α required activating both extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (Erk1/2) and cJun N-terminal kinases 1/2 (Jnk1/2) pathways.

These results in Huh7 cells were validated using a well differentiated, non-cancerous hepatocyte THLE2 cell line. As shown in Fig. S3A, Xa suppressed HNF4α expression by nearly 75% which was fully reversed by the PAR2 inhibitor, PZ-235, and partially reversed by blockade with MEK or Jnk1/2 inhibitors. The combination of the MEK+Jnk1/2 inhibitors caused a reversal of the Xa inhibition and increased HNF4α to threefold above vehicle baseline. As PAR2 is activated by multiple trypsin-like proteases aside from factor Xa, we compared the effects of trypsin and synthetic PAR2 agonist, SLIGRL, on suppression of HNF4α. As shown in Fig. S3B, trypsin and SLIGRL both caused significant (∼50%) suppression of HNF4α mRNA, similar to the effect of factor Xa.

Next, we delineated which G proteins were proximally coupled to PAR2 in the Huh7 hepatocyte cell line using the TRUPATH BRET (bioluminescence resonance energy transfer)30 system. The TRUPATH biosensor array of different Gαβγ subunits was made with each Gα having an inserted Rluc8 at variable positions to give an optimized resonance energy transfer signal to the Gγ subunits equipped with an optimally inserted GFP2 BRET acceptor. Upon adding different concentrations of PAR2 ligand (SLIGRL), we assessed the efficiency of PAR2-coupling to the various Gα subunits following dissociation of the heterotrimer complex. As shown in Fig. 3D, titration of Huh7 cells with increasing concentrations of PAR2 agonist gave dose-dependent activation of Gq, Gi2, Gi1, and G15, but not Gs, with EC50 values ranging from 1.1 to 3.5 μM. These data are consistent with PAR2 coupling with high affinity to both Gq and Gi family members in the hepatocyte cell line.

Treatment of Huh7 cells with coagulation factor Xa gave robust and prolonged activation of both pJnk1/2 and pErk1/2 signaling. As shown in Fig. 3E, factor Xa stimulated phosphorylation of Erk1/2 by 5 min which was followed by a pJnk1/2 signal after a delay of 25 min. As Jnk1/2 is the primary activator of its effector, cJun, we examined the effect of hepatocyte deletion of PAR2 on cJun expression in liver extracts. As shown in Fig. 3F, mean protein levels of cJun were significantly reduced by twofold in the livers from PAR2ΔHep relative to WT mice fed HFD for 13 weeks. Erk1/2 has been shown to suppress cEBPα, a transcription factor known to activate HNF4α transcription by binding to its enhancer.31 Mean protein levels of cEBPα were increased by 1.7-fold in livers from PAR2ΔHep mice relative to WT mice fed HFD (Fig. 3F). These data from mouse livers were reproduced in primary mouse hepatocytes. As shown in Fig. S3C, primary hepatocytes isolated from WT mice fed HFD had elevated cJun and lower cEBPα protein levels as compared with primary hepatocytes from PAR2ΔHep mice. All together, these data suggest that Xa-PAR2 can suppress HNF4α expression by two pathways: the first through a Gq-Erk1/2-cEBPα pathway and the second via a Gi-Jnk1/2-cJun pathway as represented schematically in Fig. 3G.

Coagulation factor Xa suppresses HNF4α expression through cJun sites on the HNF4α promoter

The footprint 2 region harbored a putative specificity protein 1 transcription factor (Sp1) site, and footprint 1 had two adjacent HNF1α binding sites. To directly show the requirement for HNF4α in controlling expression of the HDL receptor downstream of PAR2, we determined the effect of si-HNF4α in Huh7 hepatocytes. As shown in Fig. 5B, si-HNF4α transfection gave a highly significant (p <0.0001) suppression of HNF4α with a 40% knockdown of basal (Veh, p <0.01) SRB1 expression compared with si-Scr transfected cells. As expected, addition of PAR2 agonist Xa gave similar 40% suppression of SRB1 in control si-Scr cells (Veh, p <0.05). Consistent with HNF4α operating downstream of PAR2, Xa lost the ability to further suppress SRB1 in si-HNF4α-transfected cells (Fig. 5B).

Xa inhibited the uptake of HDL cholesterol (p <0.0001) into Huh7 cells by 60% (Fig. 5C). The PAR2 pepducin inhibitor PZ-23526 prevented the ability of Xa to inhibit HDL cholesterol uptake (Fig. 5C). To demonstrate that loss of HNF4α function should block any protective effects of the PAR2 inhibitor, we examined HDL cholesterol uptake in cells in Huh7 cells transfected with si-HNF4α vs. si-Scr control transfected cells. As shown in Fig. 5D and E, knockdown of HNF4α resulted in less HDL cholesterol uptake compared with si-Scr controls and prevented any protective effect by PAR2 antagonist PZ-235 or further inhibition by Xa agonist. By comparison, Xa still was able to significantly inhibit HDL cholesterol uptake in si-Scr control cells which was blocked by PZ-235 (Fig. 5D and E). Together, these data indicate that Xa-PAR2 is involved in inhibiting the process of reverse cholesterol transport in hepatocytes via HNF4α suppression.

We then determined whether deletion of hepatocyte PAR2 could affect the levels of bile acids (BAs) in the feces of mice. Mouse feces collected over a 12-week period during HFD feeding showed a significant threefold increase (p <0.05) in total mean BA content in the PAR2ΔHep mice compared with WT (Fig. S4A). A detailed analysis of individual fecal BAs was then conducted using mass spectrometry. A total of 26 different BAs were detected and quantified for PAR2ΔHep mice compared with WT. As shown in Fig. S4B and C, there were significant changes detected in 11 of 26 BAs, with increases in secondary BAs and a shift towards more hydrophobic BAs in the PAR2ΔHep mouse feces. The most notable changes included a fourfold drop in the major primary bile acid cholic acid (CA), with an attendant 730-fold increase in its microbial metabolite deoxycholic acid (DCA), and a 335-fold increase in lithocholic acid (LCA) in the PAR2ΔHep mice. Analysis of the gene transcriptional levels of the major cholesterol and BA synthetic enzymes and transporters indicated simultaneous upregulation of rate-limiting components of both classic (Cyp7A1) and alternative (Cyp27A1 and Cyp7B1) pathways of bile acid production and transport (Abcb11) in liver. However, the classic pathway sterol 12α-hydroxylase Cyp8B1 was suppressed, consistent with a shift from production of CA from the C4 intermediate (7α-hydroxy-4-cholestene-3-one) to the chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) pathway and its hydrophobic metabolite LCA as shown schematically in Fig. S4D.

Discussion

Individuals with MASLD are at high risk of progression into the fibrotic-inflammatory condition of MASH, which can evolve into severe cirrhosis in about 20% of cases.9 Hepatic steatosis itself is strongly associated with major cardiometabolic risk factors, including obesity, low-HDL cholesterol, hypertriglyceridemia, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation.13 As MASLD progresses, disruptions in coagulation, fibrinolysis, and activation or loss of platelets can occur, increasing the risk of both bleeding and thrombosis.33 These associations have led to a new focus on cardiometabolic risks in the diagnosis and management of hepatic steatosis,2 especially as the leading cause of death in these individuals is from atherothrombotic disease.34

In this study, we now link the coagulation protease receptor PAR2 with obesity, dyslipidemia, and fatty liver disease. We discovered that hepatic PAR2 expression is significantly elevated in patients who are obese with MASLD and MASH, displaying a sevenfold increase compared with non-obese controls. In mice fed an obesogenic diet, hepatocyte-specific deletion of PAR2 led to significant lowering in body weight, steatosis, and liver fibrosis. Notably, hepatic PAR2 deficiency improved lipid cardiometabolic indices with major reductions in liver and plasma triglyceride and cholesterol levels, with increased excretion of BAs into feces.

In-depth transcriptional analyses revealed that liver PAR2 had pleiotropic effects on lipid metabolism, extracellular matrix remodeling, and inflammatory gene pathways. HNF4α was identified as a key transcription factor that was negatively regulated by coagulation factor Xa-PAR2 signaling. HNF4α expression increased significantly in PAR2-deficient livers commensurate with heightened expression of a large array of genes involved in reverse cholesterol transport, bile acid secretion, plasma lipoprotein clearance, and reduction of lipogenesis. PAR2-deficient livers also had attenuated inflammation and fibrosis, with reduction of cell cycling and mitogenesis pathways.

As a major pioneer factor in hepatocytes, HNF4α plays a crucial role in liver development and metabolism and was previously shown to directly bind to 12% of genes in liver.35 HNF4α interacts with direct repeat (DR)-1 elements within the cis-regulatory regions of lipid metabolic genes, thereby controlling their expression. Similar to overexpression of PAR2 induced by obesogenic diets,16 mice with knockdown of liver HNF4α exhibit fatty liver disease with hypercholesterolemia.29,36 We found that 26% of the 2,101 differentially expressed genes identified from the livers of mice with hepatic deletion of PAR2 contained validated HNF4α DR-1 binding sites within 10 kb of their transcription start site. PAR2 inhibition of HNF4α broadly reduced expression of key genes involved in reverse cholesterol transport, affecting HDL uptake, BA production and secretion into the biliary tract, collectively linking PAR2 signaling to adverse cholesterol homeostasis in MASLD.

In-depth analysis of individual fecal bile acids from mice with hepatic PAR2 deficiency indicated a marked shift in the bile acid pool from production of the hydrophilic CA into the more hydrophobic BAs DCA and LCA. DCA and LCA are agonists of the FXR receptor, an important regulator of BA homeostasis in the liver and biliary system37 which was also upregulated in the PAR2-deficient livers. Consistent with activation of FXR in the PAR2ΔHep mice, Cyp8B1 expression was reduced and Abcb11 (BESP) increased, indicative of shifting production from CA into the CDCA pathway which ultimately is secreted and converted to LCA in the intestines. Remarkably, DCA which was nearly undetectable in the feces of WT mice fed HFD, became the most abundant BA in the PAR2ΔHep mice, comprising 45% of the total fecal BA. This large of amount of DCA circulating in the enterohepatic system could play a role in improving cardiometabolic indices in the PAR2ΔHep mice as previous studies have shown that diet supplementation with DCA enhanced glucose and fatty acid metabolism and had anti-obesogenic effects in both humans and mice.38

Three key components of hepatic reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), namely Srb1, Cyp7a1, and Fxr which play analogous roles in mice and humans were significantly upregulated in the PAR2ΔHep mouse. Both species use HDL particles as major mediators of cholesterol transport and rely on similar molecular mechanisms for hepatic uptake, classical bile acid synthesis and efflux via biliary/fecal excretion. Cholesterol in HDL is taken up by the liver via SR-B1, and cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (Cyp7a1) is the rate-limiting enzyme in classical BA synthesis in both species. Moreover, Cyp7a1 is under the control of FXR (Nr1h4) in the liver in both species in addition to being regulated by PAR2 and HNF4α in mice as shown here. Other mechanisms of mouse RCT can differ from human RCT, despite both having the main purpose of removing excess cholesterol from systemic tissues/blood by uptake into the liver and excretion into bile and feces. Species-specific differences in RCT include resistance to atherosclerosis, absence of CETP (cholesterol ester transfer protein), and less LDL in mice as compared with humans.

We found that PAR2 regulates a number of transcription factors in mouse liver aside from FXR and HNF4α, and PAR2 was previously shown to exert metabolic effects on de novo lipogenesis through SREBP1c, and its co-activators Pgc1α, Pgc1β, and Pparα.24 PAR2 can also suppress hepatic glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis through the FoxA3 transcription factor.16 Klf6, a transcription factor involved in adipogenesis,39 was identified as another downstream effector of PAR2 in the present study. Klf6 is known to be an important regulator of liver steatosis and fibrosis,40,41 however, whether it plays a role in MASLD or obesity specifically related to PAR2 requires further investigation.

The regulatory mechanisms governing HNF4α expression itself during MASLD pathogenesis remain largely unexplored. The c-Jun-N-terminal kinase 1/2 system has long been implicated in the hepatic response to lipotoxicity-induced ER stress and apoptosis during MASLD42,43 but was not known to affect global metabolism through regulation of HNF4α. In this study, we identified a novel mechanism by which coagulation factor Xa activates PAR2 to suppress HNF4α through bifurcating Gi-Jnk1/2-cJun and Gq-Erk1/2-cEBPα signaling pathways. We documented increased cJun binding to a specific site in the HNF4α P1 promoter following Xa activation of PAR2 that led to prolonged transcriptional suppression of HNF4α in human hepatocytes. Similarly, livers from PAR2ΔHep mice had a significant drop in cJun and basal cJun binding to the proximal promoter region of HNF4α as compared with WT mice. These data support the notion that PAR2 suppresses HNF4α expression by enhancing inhibitory cJun binding to the HNF4α P1 promoter in hepatocytes in vivo.

Interestingly, we also discovered that XBP-1, a transcription factor downstream of ER stress sensor inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α) and a key mediator of the crosstalk between ER stress and lipid metabolism,44,45 can enhance HNF4α transcription through its P1 promoter. These findings suggest that HNF4α might be involved in the ER stress response via XBP-1, linking metabolic stress to global transcriptional regulation in liver in a feedback mechanism. Efforts to target both HNF4α and XBP-1 genetically or pharmacologically to treat MASLD and obesity are ongoing but could have untoward consequences given their broad roles in normal liver physiology.45,46 Indeed, given the dramatic increase in incidence of MASLD (75%) and MASH (20%) in obese people in the US and worldwide, new drugs are urgently needed to safely impact both obesity and MASLD progression in addition to directly targeting fibrosis in patients with MASH.9

Lastly, we demonstrated that the lipidated OA-235i pepducin which targets PAR2, exerted a protective effect against fatty liver disease comparable to hepatocyte-specific deletion of PAR2 in mice. The OA-235i pepducin conferred significantly lower body weight gain compared with controls with significant improvements in liver histology. OA-235i treatment also led to lower plasma cholesterol and reductions in liver triglycerides and cholesterol with significantly increased HNF4α expression in liver and effects on genes involved in RCT and fatty acid beta-oxidation. Preclinical studies with prototype PAR2 pepducins showed multiple beneficial effects on liver fibrosis,19 reduction of ROS and improvements in glucose homeostasis16 which may offer certain advantages over current monotherapies, especially in people with diabetes and obesity with MASH. Given the multipronged beneficial effects of PAR2 pepducins in MASLD and fibrotic MASH models, OA-235i will be further explored for therapeutic purposes26 and is now in clinical development for the MASH indication.
