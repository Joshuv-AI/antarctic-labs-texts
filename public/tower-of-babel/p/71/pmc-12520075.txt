Cocaine-associated non-occlusive ischemic colitis mimicking appendicitis in a young woman: a case report
Authors: Haythem Elaskri, Sadri Ben Abid, Alaa Oueslati, Sondes Bizid, Sonia Ben Ali, Omar Beskri, Arine Aouadi, Issam Msakni, Hatem Ben Abdallah, Iheb Labbene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCocaine can trigger severe splanchnic vasoconstriction and non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia, occasionally mimicking common surgical emergencies. A previously healthy 27-year-old woman presented with 5 days of worsening right lower quadrant pain, nausea, and fever. CT suggested appendicitis. Laparoscopy revealed a normal appendix but ischemic ileum and cecal gangrene, requiring conversion to laparotomy and ileocecal resection with stoma. Histopathology showed transmural ischemic necrosis without vascular occlusion. Postoperatively, the patient disclosed recent cocaine use. Cocaine-related ischemia often affects young adults without vascular risk factors, may be radiologically subtle, and carries higher mortality when diagnosis is delayed. In young adults with unexplained acute abdomen, clinicians should consider cocaine-associated ischemia and obtain toxicology screening when the etiology is unclear, enabling timely management and improved outcomes.Keywords: Cocaine, ischemic colitis, non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia, acute abdomen, toxicology screening, young adult

Introduction

Cocaine abuse remains a significant public health issue, particularly among young adults. While its cardiovascular and neurological effects are well documented, cocaine-induced mesenteric ischemia is a rare but potentially fatal complication that is often overlooked. The vasoconstrictive properties of cocaine can lead to severe bowel ischemia, which may mimic common abdominal emergencies such as appendicitis or diverticulitis [1–3].

Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia (NOMI) induced by vasoconstrictors such as cocaine is increasingly recognized, associated with high rates of surgery and poor outcomes [4–7]. Mortality is significantly higher in cocaine-related ischemic colitis compared to non-cocaine ischemia (≈26% vs 7.7%) [8].

The present case emphasizes the importance of including cocaine use in the differential diagnosis of acute abdomen in young, otherwise healthy individuals, and reviews the pathophysiology, diagnostic challenges, and management strategies.

Case report

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication. Institutional review board exemption was granted.

A 27-year-old previously healthy woman presented with a five-day history of progressively worsening abdominal pain localized to the right lower quadrant, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and fever. She initially denied any history of illicit drug use or other risk factors for abdominal pathology.

On admission, vital signs showed tachycardia (120 bpm) and tachypnea (30 breaths/min) but no fever. Abdominal assessment revealed significant guarding and tenderness in the right lower quadrant. Rectal examination showed no evidence of bleeding.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated leukocytosis (14,000/mm³) and markedly elevated CRP (292 mg/L). CT abdomen with contrast was interpreted as acute appendicitis.

Exploratory laparoscopy revealed a normal appendix but dusky ileal ischemia and cecal gangrene. Conversion to open laparotomy was performed, followed by ileocecal resection with stoma formation (Figures 1 and 2).

Histopathology confirmed acute ischemic necrosis with cecal perforation, consistent with non-occlusive ischemia (Figure 3). No thrombus, embolus, or atherosclerosis was observed.

In the postoperative period, the patient disclosed recent intranasal cocaine use (6 days prior to symptom onset), establishing a temporal association. She was discharged in stable condition with stoma care instructions and referred for substance cessation counseling.

Discussion

Cocaine-induced mesenteric ischemia results from profound vasoconstriction through inhibition of norepinephrine reuptake and endothelial dysfunction [1,4,9]. This leads to non-occlusive ischemia that may not be evident on conventional imaging. Histology typically confirms ischemia without thromboembolic disease, as in our case. Levamisole-adulterated cocaine may also exacerbate vascular injury via vasculitis [10,11].

Establishing causality is challenging since direct rechallenge studies would be unethical, but several elements support cocaine as the etiology in this case. The temporal relationship is consistent, with the patient using cocaine six days before symptom onset which aligns with reported ischemic complications. Histopathology excluded thromboembolic disease, atherosclerosis and other common causes of mesenteric ischemia in young adults. The non-occlusive ischemia pattern observed histologically is typical of cocaine-induced vasospasm rather than embolic or thrombotic occlusion. Finally, the patient’s young age and absence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors make cocaine-induced ischemia a more plausible etiology than spontaneous mesenteric ischemia [2,3,5].

Several diagnostic challenges merit emphasis in such cases. The clinical presentation often mimics more common conditions like appendicitis or diverticulitis which can lead to misdiagnosis. In this case the initial CT interpretation and clinical presentation strongly suggested appendicitis showing how cocaine-induced ischemia can masquerade as common surgical emergencies. Patients may also initially withhold information regarding substance use which delays accurate diagnosis and this case illustrates that challenge as the patient only disclosed cocaine use postoperatively after surgical findings prompted more detailed questioning. Standard CT imaging may fail to detect early ischemic changes particularly in non-occlusive ischemia and the normal CT appearance in this case despite significant bowel ischemia found at surgery highlights the limitation of conventional imaging in detecting cocaine-induced ischemia [6,7,12,13].

Several aspects of this case warrant critical reflection. Earlier toxicology screening could have been considered given the atypical presentation in a young healthy patient which might have led to more targeted management and earlier recognition of the underlying etiology. More detailed and repeated history-taking about substance use particularly in young patients with atypical presentations might have revealed the cocaine use earlier in the clinical course. Recognition that normal CT imaging does not exclude mesenteric ischemia especially in non-occlusive forms could have prompted earlier surgical consultation.

Management strategies for cocaine-induced bowel ischemia involve several considerations. Surgical intervention is required when necrotic bowel is present to prevent life-threatening sepsis as shown in this case. In cases without perforation medical management with vasodilators such as papaverine may be considered although supporting evidence is limited [4,9]. Substance cessation counseling is a critical component of long-term management to prevent recurrence and should be initiated during hospitalization.

Review of existing literature reveals that cocaine-related bowel ischemia carries a disproportionately high mortality rate, primarily attributable to delayed diagnosis and subsequent sepsis. Recent studies have shown mortality rates approaching 50% in cases of cocaine-induced ischemic colitis, significantly higher than traditional ischemic colitis [4,5,8]. Affected patients typically present at a younger age compared to traditional ischemic colitis cases and frequently lack conventional vascular risk factors. The novelty of this case lies in its demonstration of the diagnostic complexity and the critical importance of maintaining clinical suspicion for drug-induced ischemia in young adults, even when initial imaging appears to suggest a common surgical condition like appendicitis.

Conclusion

This case highlights the crucial importance of considering cocaine use in the differential diagnosis of young adults presenting with acute abdominal pain, even in the absence of typical risk factors. The non-occlusive nature of cocaine-induced ischemia makes early diagnosis particularly challenging, often requiring surgical exploration for confirmation.

Early recognition through repeated history-taking, toxicology screening in atypical presentations, and appropriate surgical intervention can be life-saving. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when imaging and clinical findings are discordant, and prioritize toxicology testing in young patients with unexplained acute abdomen.
