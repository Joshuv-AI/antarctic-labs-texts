The time course of changes in bone mass and bone strength induced by the combination of voluntary running exercise and food restriction in young female rats
Authors: Yuki Aikawa, Yumi Kato, Kazuki Kioka, Yuki Iwama, Rikako Miyazawa, Hitomi Ogata, Ken Kiyono, Naomi Omi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose] This study aimed to determine the time course of changes in bone mass and bone strength induced by the combination of voluntary running exercise and food restriction in young female rats.[Methods] Female Sprague-Dawley rats (aged 7 weeks) were randomly divided into the following three experimental groups after a 1-week acclimatization period: sedentary and ad libitum feeding (SED), voluntary wheel running exercise and ad libitum feeding (EX), and voluntary wheel running and food restriction (EX-FR). Dissections were performed at 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks. The strength of the femoral mid-shaft was assessed using the three-point bending test. The bone mineral density (BMD) of the tibia was measured using a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry machine.[Results] At 2 weeks, the body weight, abdominal fat weight, and bone weight of the femur in the EX-FR group were significantly lower than those in the SED and EX groups. The BMD of the tibia in the EX-FR group was significantly lower than that in the EX group. At 4 weeks, the bone strength of the femur in the EX-FR group was significantly lower than that in the EX group. At 8 weeks, the bone strength of the femur in the EX-FR group was significantly lower than that in the SED group.[Conclusion] These findings demonstrate that bone mass is affected earlier than bone strength under energy-deficient exercise conditions, providing important insights into the temporal progression of bone impairment associated with relative energy deficiency

INTRODUCTION

A low bone mineral density (BMD) caused by inadequate food intake in female athletes remains unresolved despite having been recognized as a significant health problem for over 30 years. In 1992, the American College of Sports Medicine named the interrelated symptoms of eating disorders, amenorrhea, and osteoporosis frequently observed in lean female athletes the “female athlete triad (FAT),” raising awareness about this problem [1]. Subsequent studies have demonstrated that reduced energy availability, caused by insufficient dietary intake relative to the exercise demand, leads to a wide range of adverse physiologic consequences beyond disturbances in menstrual function and bone health. Reflecting this broader understanding, the International Olympic Committee has introduced the Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport concept in a 2014 consensus statement, expanding the framework beyond the FAT [2] and further updated this concept in a 2023 consensus statement, which explicitly includes bone-related outcomes [3].

Despite these repeated warnings, bone health impairment associated with energy deficiency remains prevalent among athletes. Recent studies published in the 2020s have continued to report low BMD in endurance athletes, particularly in females [4-6], underscoring the persistent and unresolved nature of this issue.

Animal models provide a valuable experimental platform for investigating bone responses under controlled conditions and within relatively short time frames. Previous studies by our group and others have utilized experimental animal models to demonstrate that the combination of running exercise and food or energy restriction at 30%–40% impairs multiple bone parameters [7-17]. However, although the intervention duration ranged from approximately 5–13 weeks in these studies, the temporal progression of bone impairment has not been systematically examined. Importantly, adverse effects of such interventions on bone mass and bone strength may not emerge simultaneously but may develop at different time points. Identifying the temporal relationship of these changes is essential for determining the optimal timing of preventive and therapeutic interventions.

In the present study, we aimed to determine the temporal changes in bone mass and bone strength induced by the combination of voluntary running exercise and food restriction in young female rats. Additionally, we evaluated the impact of this intervention at 2- and 4-week time points, shorter than the periods investigated in previous studies.

METHODS

Experimental design

The animal care and experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Experimental Committee of the University of Tsukuba (approval number: 19-346). All experimental protocols adhered to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Female Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 80, aged 7 weeks) purchased from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan) were randomly divided into the following three experimental groups after a 1-week acclimatization period: sedentary and ad libitum feeding (SED, n = 22), voluntary wheel running exercise and ad libitum feeding (EX, n = 28), and voluntary wheel running and food restriction (EX-FR, n = 30). Dissections were performed at 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks. The number of samples per group at each time point was as follows: at 2 weeks, SED = 5, EX = 7, and EX-FR = 7; at 4 weeks, SED = 5, EX = 7, and EX-FR = 8; at 8 weeks, SED = 5, EX = 7, and EX-FR = 7; and at 12 weeks, SED = 7, EX = 7, and EX-FR = 8.

The SED groups were housed individually in normal cages (15 × 25 × 19.5 cm), whereas the EX and EX-FR groups were housed individually in wheel cages to facilitate voluntary running exercise (circumference of the wheel, 1 m; cage dimensions, 27 × 35 × 35 cm). We used voluntary running to assess the changing daily running distance and to maintain momentum. Moreover, we assessed running distance (exercise volume) but not exercise intensity.

The rats were fed the standard diet containing 62.37% glucose monohydrate, 18.0% casein, 10.0% cottonseed oil, and other components (crude 0.6% Ca, 0.6% P) as described previously [12]. The EX-FR group was fed a 30% restricted diet to account for 70% of the SED group’s mean intake averaged over the last 3 days. As a result, the EX-FR group was fed a 40% lower amount of the diet provided to the EX group (Table 1). This food restriction level was based on previous studies demonstrating its ability to lower bone mass without posing a threat to life during experiments including 10–13 weeks of feeding [10-12,17]. The room was kept at a temperature of 24°C ± 1°C with a constant 12:12 h light-dark cycle (light period 8:00–20:00).

Daily data collection and specimen harvesting

Every day, the body weight, dietary intake, and running distance were measured. On the day before the dissections were performed, all rats were fasted for 12 h. Their energy intake was calculated by multiplying the diet intake (3.73 kcal/g). Exercise-induced energy expenditure due to daily wheel running exercise was calculated as 5.0 kcal/kg body weight times the number of kilometers run [18] using the following formula: exercise-induced energy expenditure = wheel-running distance × body weight × 5.0 kcal/kg body weight/km. Energy availability was determined by subtracting the energy intake from the exercise energy expenditure [7]. Although energy availability values are generally normalized to fat-free mass in humans [19], those in the present study were normalized to body weight because of the difficulty of analyzing fat-free mass in rats.

Whole blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta using syringes under pentobarbital anesthesia. The serum samples were separated by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The serum was frozen at −80°C for the bone metabolic markers and oxidative stress markers. After death, the abdominal fat, uterus, plantaris muscle, soleus muscle, femur, and tibia of each rat were collected. Both femurs were collected, the adhering connective tissues were removed, the wet femur weight and size were measured, and the bone strength was immediately measured. Subsequently, the femur was dried at 95°C for 24 h in an electric furnace, and its dry weight was measured. The dried femurs were then burned to ash at 600°C for 24 h, and the ash weight was measured. Both tibias were stored in 70% ethanol after being harvested and cleaned of soft tissue for bone mass measurement.

Measurement of bone strength using the three-point bending test

The strength of the femoral mid-shaft was assessed using the three-point bending test (DYN-1255, IIO DENKI, Tokyo, Japan) as previously described (distance between the fulcrums, 1 cm; plunger speed, 100 mm/min; full scale, 50 kg; chart speed, 120 cm/min) [20]. Breaking force was defined as the loading weight (gravitational acceleration) required for bone breaking. Breaking energy was defined as the workload that results in the breaking of the bone.

Measurement of bone mass using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry

The bone mineral content (BMC), bone area, and BMD of the tibia were measured using a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry machine (QDR-4500A, Hologic, Bedford, MA, USA). All scans were performed in small mode. The rat phantom BMD measurement had a coefficient of variation of 2.295%.

Bone metabolic markers in serum

Serum bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (Bone-ALP) activity, a marker of bone formation, and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) activity, a marker of bone resorption, were measured as previously reported [21]. With regard to the Bone-ALP activity measurement, the serum samples were either heated for 10 min at 56°C to selectively deactivate Bone-ALP or were used without heating. The release of p-nitrophenol from p-nitrophenyl phosphate (p-NPP) (10 mM, in 0.1 M Tris (2-amino-2-methyl-1-propanol)–HCl buffer (pH 10.0) containing 5 mM MgCl2·6H2O) at 37°C was measured. The reaction was stopped after 30 min by adding 1 M NaOH, and the absorbance of the solution at 420 nm was determined. The ALP activity was expressed in U, wherein 1 U hydrolyzed 1 μmol of p-NPP per minute. The Bone-ALP activity was calculated by subtracting the ALP activity value obtained for the heated serum from that of the intact serum. With regard to the TRAP activity measurement, the serum samples were preheated for 1 h at 37°C. Subsequently, equal volumes of a mixture of 0.1 M p-NPP, 0.2 M sodium citrate, 0.2 M NaCl, and 0.08 M L-(+) sodium tartrate were added to the serum samples, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min at 37°C. The reaction was stopped by adding 1 M NaOH, and the absorbance of the solution at 420 nm was measured. The TRAP activity was expressed in U, wherein 1 U represents the activity required to hydrolyze 1 μmol of p-NPP per minute.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In any analysis, if significant differences were observed, the variables were analyzed using the Tukey-Kramer post hoc comparison tests. The student’s t-test was used to analyze the running distance and exercise energy expenditure. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using the GraphPad Prism software (v. 9.4.0, Macintosh; Graph-Pad Software, La Jolla, CA).

RESULTS

Food intake, running distance, energy balance, final body weight, and tissue weights

Table 1 shows the results of food intake, running distance, and energy availability. At 4, 8, and 12 weeks, the total food intake, daily food intake, and energy intake of the EX group were significantly higher compared to the SED group. At 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks, these parameters were significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups. At 4, 8, and 12 weeks, the energy intake adjusted for body weight of the EX and EX-FR group were significantly higher compared to the SED group. At 12 weeks, the total running distance and daily running distance were significantly higher in the EX-FR group compared to the EX group. At any point, no significant difference in the exercise energy expenditure was observed between the EX-FR and EX groups. At 4 and 12 weeks, the exercise energy expenditure adjusted for body weight of the EX-FR group was significantly higher compared to the EX group. At 2 weeks, the energy availability was significantly lower in the EX group compared to the SED group. At 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks, this parameter was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups. At 2 weeks, the energy availability adjusted for body weight was significantly lower in the EX group compared to the SED group. At 2, and 8 weeks, this parameter was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups.

Table 2 shows the results of the body weight and tissue weights. At 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks, the body weight and abdominal fat weight were significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups. At 2 weeks, the abdominal fat weight was significantly lower in the EX group compared to the SED group. At 4, 8, and 12 weeks, the soleus muscle weight in the EX group was significantly higher than that in the SED and EX-FR groups. At 2, 4, and 12 weeks, the plantaris muscle weight was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups. At 8 weeks, this parameter was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the EX group. At 4 and 12 weeks, the uterus weight was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups.

Bone weight and size of the femur

Table 3 shows the results of the assessment of the bone weight and size of the femur. At 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks, the wet, dry, and ash weights of the femur were significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups. At 2 weeks, the femur length was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED group. At 4, 8, and 12 weeks, this parameter was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups. At 4 and 8 weeks, the major axis of the femur was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups, respectively. At 12 weeks, this parameter was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to both the SED and EX groups. At 4 and 12 weeks, the minor axis of the femur was significantly lower in the EX-FR group compared to the SED and EX groups. At 12 weeks, this parameter was significantly higher in the EX group compared to the SED group.

Bone strength of the femur

Figure 1 shows the results of the femur strength. At 4, 8, and 12 weeks, the breaking force and breaking energy of the femur in the EX-FR group were significantly lower than those in the EX group. At 8 and 12 weeks, the parameters in the EX-FR group were significantly lower than those in the SED and EX groups.

BMC, bone area, and BMD of the tibia

Figure 2 shows the results of the BMC, bone area, and BMD of the tibia. The BMC of the tibia at 4 weeks, the bone area of the tibia at 8 weeks, and the BMD of the tibia at 2 weeks in the EX group were significantly higher than those in the SED group. The BMC and bone area of the tibia at 4, 8, and 12 weeks and the BMD of the tibia at 2 and 12 weeks in the EX-FR group were significantly lower than those in the SED group. Moreover, the BMC and BMD of the tibia at 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks and the bone area of the tibia at 4, 8, and 12 weeks in the EX-FR group were significantly lower than those in the EX group.

Bone metabolic markers in serum

Table 4 shows the results of the bone metabolic markers in serum. At 4 weeks, the Bone-ALP activity in serum in the EX-FR group was significantly higher than that in the SED group. At 8 and 12 weeks, this parameter in the EX-FR group was significantly higher than that in the SED and EX groups. At 4 and 12 weeks, the TRAP activity in serum in the EX-FR group was significantly higher than that in the SED and EX groups. At 8 weeks, the parameter was significantly higher in the EX and EX-FR groups than in the SED group.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we investigated the time-dependent effects of the combination of voluntary running exercise and food restriction on bone mass and bone strength in young female rats by systematically varying the intervention duration (2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks). By defining the onset of a given adverse effect as the earliest time point at which a statistically significant difference emerged and persisted thereafter, we characterized the sequential progression of bone impairment under energy-deficient exercise conditions. Table 5 summarizes the study results.

Our main study finding was that the adverse effects of combined running exercise and food restriction on bone parameters occurred in a time-dependent and sequential manner. As early as 2 weeks after study initiation, body weight, abdominal fat mass, femoral bone weight, and tibial BMD were significantly lower in the EX-FR group than in the EX group, indicating the early suppression of bone mass accrual. In contrast, a significant decline in femoral bone strength in the EX-FR group became evident at 4 weeks compared with the EX group and at 8 weeks compared with the SED group. These results demonstrate that bone mass is impacted earlier than bone strength under conditions of insufficient energy availability during exercise.

The observed temporal dissociation between the changes in bone mass and bone strength is biologically plausible. Bone strength is determined by not only bone mineral density but also bone geometry, microarchitecture, and material properties. In our previous work, BMD reduction preceded measurable impairment in bone strength in the setting of energy restriction without exercise [22]. The present study findings extend this observation by demonstrating that energy deficiency first compromises bone mass, with deterioration of bone strength emerging later as cumulative structural and material alterations develop, even in the setting of mechanical loading induced by voluntary running.

Our finding that significant differences between the EXFR and EX groups emerged earlier than those between the EX-FR and SED groups underscores the protective effect of adequate energy intake during exercise. Voluntary running under sufficient energy intake has a positive impact on bone mass and strength in humans [23,24] and in animal models [12,25,26]. In the present study, the beneficial adaptations observed in the EX group likely accelerated the emergence of differences compared to the EX-FR group, highlighting the importance of selecting reference groups when interpreting the timing of adverse effects. From a translational perspective, this distinction is particularly relevant when considering preventive or therapeutic strategies for athletes versus nonathletes.

The evaluation of serum bone metabolic marker levels indicated an increase in bone turnover in the EX-FR group from 4 weeks onward. The delayed manifestation of these changes relative to the reduction in BMD was somewhat unexpected because alterations in bone metabolism are often assumed to precede or accompany changes in bone mass. This discrepancy may reflect the limited sensitivity of indirect serum markers compared with the direct assessment of bone tissue. It is possible that changes in bone formation and resorption occurred earlier but were not detectable through circulating marker levels at 2 weeks. Direct evaluation of bone tissue, such as histomorphometric and molecular analyses, may reveal earlier alterations consistent with the early decline in BMD.

We consider that growth suppression due to absolute low energy availability was the cause of the low bone mass and strength observed in the EX-FR group. Furthermore, although energy intake per body weight increased in the EXFR group compared to the SED group from week 4 onward, exercise energy expenditure per body weight also rose. Consequently, energy availability per unit of body weight did not improve, suggesting that the EX-FR group continuously expended energy to maintain vital functions commensurate with their body size, leaving no energy resources for bone and other tissue growth and consequently leading to the continuous widening of the difference in bone mass and body weight between the EX-FR group and the SED and EX groups.

The lower bone mass and strength resulting from the combination of running exercise and food restriction are presumably caused by abnormal bone metabolism driven by low energy availability rather than by the insufficient intake of specific nutrients, such as calcium or protein. The lower uterine weight supports decreased estrogen secretion [27], which suppresses bone resorption. Additionally, previous studies have confirmed reduced estrogen secretion under similar experimental conditions [7,9]. Furthermore, our previous study demonstrated that the combination of running exercise and energy-only restriction, without nutrient deficiencies other than carbohydrates, reduced bone mass and uterus weight with decreasing insulin-like growth factor 1 secretion, which promotes bone formation [17]. These findings support our consideration.

In the present study, the stage of the estrous cycle at the time of dissection was not standardized, which might have influenced the results on uterus weight, a commonly used indirect indicator of estrogen status [27]. However, uterus weight and estrogen secretion fluctuate markedly throughout the estrous cycle [28,29]. Therefore, the lack of estrous cycle synchronization may explain the transient disappearance of statistically significant differences at certain time points, such as 8 weeks, despite their reappearance at 12 weeks. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the reproductive-related outcomes of our study.

In the present study, we adopted voluntary wheel running to model endurance exercise because it can provide sufficient load to induce endurance adaptation [17] while also causing less stress than forced running [30]. Despite these advantages, food restriction stress can increase voluntary running distance during this exercise [7,11,13,17]; indeed, in the present study, the total running distance significantly increased over 12 weeks in the EX-FR group compared to the EX group. In a previous study [17], we prohibited access to the wheel once a week in the RUN-ER group to eliminate differences in running volume. However, running volume increased the day after the prohibited day and the differences in running volume could not be eliminated. Correcting differences in the running distance was challenging with the equipment used in the present study. In the EX-FR group, the long running distance resulted in greater energy expenditure, which might have led to further reductions in BMD and bone strength.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically delineate the temporal changes in bone impairment induced by the combination of voluntary running exercise and food restriction in young female rats. Our analyses demonstrate that energy-deficient exercise leads to an early reduction in bone mass, followed by a subsequent decline in bone strength. These results provide important insights into the temporal progression of bone deterioration associated with relative energy deficiency, informing the timing of preventive and therapeutic interventions aimed at preserving bone health under conditions of high physical activity and limited energy availability.
