Evaluation of the impact of unilateral palatally impacted maxillary canine on maxillary sinus volume: a split-mouth study
Authors: Filiz Aydoğan Akgün, Neslihan Ebru Şenışık
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpaction of maxillary canines may narrow the maxillary sinus volumetrically that may be a predisposed factor in difficulty of maxillary sinus functions. This study aimed to investigate whether the presence of unilateral palatally impacted canine teeth resulted in differences in maxillary sinus volume and dimensions between the impacted and erupted sides.MethodsTwenty-eight patients (female n = 23, male n = 5, and age range 13–36 years) were included in this study. Maxillary sinus volume, hemi-arch length, and hemi-arch width measurements were performed on the cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) records of the patients with a split-mouth design. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the numerical measurements in the two groups, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the measurements of impacted and nonimpacted sides.ResultsThe values for maxillary sinus volume did not differ between the impacted and nonimpacted sides of the maxillary canine tooth (p = 0.265). The hemi-arch length was significantly shorter on the impacted side compared with the nonimpacted side (p < 0.001). The transversal hemi-arch width measurement at 7 mm on the impacted canine side was narrower than that on the nonimpacted side (p = 0.009).ConclusionsThe volumes of the sinus were similar on the impacted canine and erupted canine sides of the maxilla. The presence of a unilaterally impacted maxillary canine was associated with a decrease in the hemi-arch length and the anterior transversal width of the hemi-arch on the impacted side compared with the erupted side.Keywords: Arch perimeter, Arch width, CBCT, Impacted canine, Sinus volume

Background

The incidence of palatal maxillary canine impaction in patients applying to a dental health institution/center has been reported to range between 1.74% and 8.4% [1, 2]. Palatal impactions are more common than buccal impactions in terms of localization and are more prevalent in women than men [3]. Various etiologic factors and theories have been proposed for impacted maxillary canines, including a long eruption path, a persistent deciduous canine root, crowding, trauma, the guidance theory, and genetic theory [3, 4].

The maxillary sinus, which is the first developed and the largest paranasal sinus, is located bilaterally and forms a large part of the body of the maxilla [5]. The maxillary sinus is typically pyramidal in shape, with its base adjacent to the lateral wall of the nasal cavity and its apex extending into the zygomatic process [5]. The maxillary sinus is an air-filled cavity whose dimensions and shapes vary by age, sex, and adjacent dentoalveolar structures [6].

The maxillary sinus performs many important functions summarized as follows: humidifying and warming the inhaled air, increasing the surface area of the olfactory mucosa, balancing the external and internal atmospheric pressure due to its air-filled interior, contributing to sound resonance, reducing the weight of the cranium, preventing brain trauma by absorbing shock blows to the head, contributing to facial growth and architecture, directing mucous and serous secretions to the ostium by mucociliary movement, and producing nitric oxide [7]. Nitric oxide has the task of preventing infections due to its bactericidal properties. The main source of nitric oxide in the nasal air is the paranasal sinuses [7].

The lower wall of the maxillary sinus is formed by the alveolar process of the maxilla and is usually adjacent to the roots of the premolars and molars. Sometimes, even the roots of the canine teeth may be in contact with the maxillary sinus [8], and the impacted canine teeth may be in closer contact with the maxillary sinuses in cases where the canine teeth are impacted [9].

Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) has become an indispensable routine diagnostic tool in dentistry in recent years. CBCT gives successful results in detailed imaging of hard tissues using a relatively low radiation dose in a short scanning duration [10].

Infections, cysts, and tumoral formations originating from the maxillary sinus or teeth can reciprocally affect each other due to the proximity of the teeth to the maxillary sinus, which has many important functions [11]. Furthermore, some dental pathologies include an increase in the thickness of the sinus membrane [12], a decrease in the volume of the maxillary sinus, and inflammation of the maxillary sinus due to a decline in the natural drainage of the maxillary sinus [13].

In studies evaluating impacted maxillary canine cases, various volumetric and millimetric measurements were made about these teeth and adjacent anatomical structures, and the etiology and consequences of impaction were investigated [14, 15]. Various studies have been performed on the anatomy of the maxillary sinus in two or three dimensions and the possible factors that may affect this anatomy [16–21]. However, studies evaluating the effect of impacted maxillary canine teeth on the sinus volume are limited [17, 21, 22]. The maxillary sinus has an anatomical relationship with the roots of the impacted maxillary canine teeth [9]. Due to its anatomical relationship, the effect of an impacted tooth adjacent to the maxillary sinus on the sinus volume has not yet been fully elucidated. A narrow maxillary sinus may cause sinusitis [23], or it may be a predisposing factor to mouth breathing. To the best of our knowledge, there is no study that determines whether the presence of impacted teeth is one of the factors that can narrow the volume of the maxillary sinus. The research conducted in this study concentrates on the influence of a palatally impacted maxillary canine on the volume of the maxillary sinus and dentoalveolar structures. This investigation employs CBCT imaging and utilizes a split-mouth design to examine the effects of the impacted canine. The study aims to determine whether the presence of a palatally impacted canine leads to variations in maxillary sinus volume and dentoalveolar structures. The null hypothesis of this study posited that the presence of a unilateral palatally impacted maxillary canine would exert no statistically significant effect on maxillary sinus volume, hemi-arch length, or transverse hemi-arch width when compared to the contralateral nonimpacted side.

Methods

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the xxxxx University (1.04.2021/Approval no: 167). This retrospective study was conducted on CBCT records of 28 patients with unilateral impacted maxillary canine teeth. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) full eruption of all permanent maxillary teeth except the wisdom teeth, (2) complete eruption of the contralateral canines, (3) dental age and the chronological age of individuals being older than or equal to 13 years, (4) individuals who had not undergone orthodontic treatment previously, (5) bilateral eruption or bilateral impaction or bilateral agenesis of maxillary wisdom teeth, and (6) absence of any resorption or/and pathology in the maxillary teeth and dentoalveolar structures. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the presence of crown/bridge restorations, and (2) a history of dental trauma or surgical procedures in the maxilla.

All CBCT records included in the study were previously obtained following a standard institutional imaging protocol using a Planmeca CBCT unit (Planmeca ProMax 3D Mid machine; Helsinki, Finland) with a standard field of view of 200 × 60 mm or 200 × 100 mm (90 kV, 10 mA) and a voxel size of 0.4 mm. The Romexis software program (Planmeca Romexis 3.2.0.R, Helsinki, Finland) and the 3D Doctor program (Able Software Corp., MA, USA) were used for dimensional/angular and volumetric measurements, respectively.

Calculation of sinus volume

To clarify the definition of sinus boundaries and ensure consistency, image contrast was standardized across all CBCT recordings by selecting the ‘Full Range’ optimization within the 3D Doctor software prior to initiating the segmentation process. Additionally, the voxel dimensions were uniformly adjusted to 0.4 mm along all three axes.

Manual segmentation of the maxillary sinus was achieved by tracing its radiographically delineable outer borders across all axial slices—from the floor to the roof—to determine the total sinus volume (Fig. 1a). To ensure a higher level of reproducibility in the ostium region, where a natural anatomical opening is found, we constructed an imaginary line elegantly coursing through the sinus walls to delineate the boundary. This process of careful planning allowed consistent closure of the sinus volume at the level of the ostium, in addition to improving segmentation accuracy. Moreover, we included the entire air-filled cavity, including incidental mucosal findings, in volume calculation as long as anatomical borders were clearly visible throughout the examination. Then, using the 3D Doctor program, a 3D image of the sinus was created, and the volume of the created sinus was calculated (Fig. 1b). Similarly, the contralateral sinus volume was calculated and recorded.

(a) Drawing of the outer borders of the sinus in axial sections. b 3D image of the maxillary sinus obtained after the completion of drawing the outer borders of the sinus in all axial sections. c Measurement of the hemi-arch length. d Measurement of the skeletal transversal hemi-arch widths at four levels

Measurement of arch perimeter

Two reference lines were used to standardize the measurements of the maxillary hemi-arch length (Fig. 1c). The first line was the projection of the intermaxillary suture and passed through the middle of the central teeth on the panoramic image obtained from the CBCT records. The second line was perpendicular to the first reference line, tangent to the apex of the alveolar crest in the region of the central incisors, and parallel to the alveolar crest.

The measurements of the hemi-arch length for both impacted and nonimpacted sides were carried out at the level of the second reference line. Measurements were performed on the center of the alveolar crest, from the mesial of the first molar tooth to the midpalatal suture for both impacted and nonimpacted sides (Fig. 1c) [24].

Measurement of the skeletal transversal widths

The main reference line was created parallel to the intermaxillary suture, passing through the contact point of the central teeth, for measuring the skeletal transversal width of the maxilla. The 7-, 14-, 21-, and 28-mm points were marked on this reference line, and the first (origin) was at the incisal point of the central teeth. The perpendicular distance to the 7-, 14-, 21-, and 28-mm points on the reference line at the first axial view in which the buccal cortical bone was measured and recorded separately for the impacted and nonimpacted sides (Fig. 1d).

To homogenise the skeletal characteristics of the individuals in the study, the 2D views of CBCT obtained from the patients were subjected to Wits assessment for sagittal direction analysis and GoGn-SN measurement for vertical direction assessment.

The power analysis of the study was carried out using G*Power software version 3.1.9.2. (Universitaet Kiel, Germany). The effect size was calculated using the sinus volume values on both impacted and erupted sides of the canine tooth in the pilot study. The sample size was calculated by choosing “t-test” as the test family and “Means: the difference between matched pairs” as the analysis type. The sample size was determined as 28, with an effect size of d = 0.936, a power value of 0.95, and a margin of error of 5%.

The statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS 20.0 program (IBM Inc, IL, USA). The conformity of continuous numerical measurements to normal distribution was analysed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The chi-square test of independence was applied to determine the relationships between categorical variables. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the numerical measurements in the two groups, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the measurements of impacted and nonimpacted sides. Method errors were performed on randomly selected patients by two independent observers for all measurements. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) method was used to calculate the agreement values. A p value of < 0.05 with test statistics (95% CI) and degrees of freedom indicated a statistically significant difference in all analyses.

No statistically significant difference was observed in the sinus volume values between the impacted and nonimpacted sides of the maxillary canine tooth (p = 0.265) (Table 6). The hemi-arch length was statistically significantly shorter on the impacted side than that on the nonimpacted side (p < 0.001). Similarly, the transversal dimension of the dental arch at 7 mm indicated a reduced width on the impacted side of the maxillary canine tooth in comparison to the nonimpacted side (p = 0.009). However, transversal hemi-arch width measurements at the 14-, 21-, and 28-mm points on the impacted and nonimpacted sides were found to be statistically similar (Table 6).

Discussion

The combined effect of impacted canines on the adjacent dentoalveolar structures and the volume of the maxillary sinus has been investigated in a limited number of studies. However, there are only a few studies that have investigated the effects of palatally impacted maxillary canines on these structures [21, 24, 25]. This study aimed to investigate the effects of palatally impacted maxillary canines on the above-mentioned surrounding structures.

The primary advantage of the split-mouth research design is its efficiency in terms of sample size. By employing this study design, not only are individual variations eliminated, but a comparison between the affected and erupted sides is also made possible, serving as a control. As the patients serve as their own controls, the study’s power is increased. The split-mouth design increases statistical power by eliminating inter-subject variability, thereby allowing for valid statistical conclusions even with a smaller sample size [26]. Given that palatally positioned impacted canines in close proximity to the sinus may exert a narrowing effect on the sinus volume, only individuals with palatally impacted canines were included in this study, rather than those with buccally positioned impacted canines [17, 21]. Furthermore, the higher prevalence of palatally impacted canines [3], expands the demographic that could potentially benefit from the findings of this study. However, due to the retrospective nature of the study and the limited sample size, the vertical and anteroposterior positions of the impacted canines were not evaluated as separate variables.

As sinus volume can vary due to age [6] and skeletal abnormalities [20], we restricted our study to participants between the ages of 13 and 36 years with ideal jaw relationship - sagittal and vertical - and complete dentition (Table 1). These inclusion criteria ensured that individuals within the normal ranges of both vertical and sagittal directions were considered, thereby eliminating any potential differences in sinus size due to skeletal variations [20, 21].

In this study, the study population consisted of participants who were 13 years or older. Since the maxillary canine is predicted to erupt in the mouth at the age of 12–13 years [27], and growth of the maxillary sinus also ceases at a similar age [28], cases aged 13 and above were recruited for this study. Most children undergo eruption of the maxillary canine by the age of 13, which could be erroneously assumed as non-eruption. In order to prevent such misinterpretations, this study included cases in which all permanent maxillary teeth had erupted, with the exception of the third molars. Furthermore, the criterion of complete eruption of the canine tooth on the contralateral side of the impacted maxillary canine was applied in the included cases. In addition, the potential eruption angle of the impacted canines was evaluated in an objective manner, and only cases exhibiting an insufficient eruption angle were included in the study.

The existing literature indicates that the size of the maxillary sinuses is typically more pronounced in males than in females [19]. This observation is attributed to the higher functional demands placed on males, which are a consequence of their larger body size and craniofacial skeleton. However, the current study found no significant gender differences in sinus volume. This finding was in accordance with the existing literature [18, 21]. Whereas, Wang et al. [29] showed that sinus volume varied depending on gender in cases with different vertical characteristics, including low, normo-, and high with normal sagittal relationship. This different result may suggest that the vertical feature affects the gender-related sinus volume difference. Nevertheless, in our study, the skeletal vertical feature was constrained by the inclusion of cases within the normal range. In order to ensure the reliability of the results, the study included both male and female subjects, who were selected to have a skeletally normal vertical orientation and sagittal relationship. On the other hand, the relatively small number of males in the current study may have contributed to the absence of any discernible gender differences.

The findings indicate that gender does not exert an influence on the hemi-arch length and width of the arch on either the impacted or non-impacted sides. And our finding is in agreement with Tadinada et al. [24].

The findings of this study demonstrate that the position of the impacted maxillary canine, whether on the right or left side, has no impact on the characteristics that were evaluated. The volume of the maxillary sinus on the impacted side was not affected by whether the impacted canine was positioned on the right or left side. These findings are supported by Belgin et al. [6]. However, according to the study of Öz et al., in the group in which the impacted maxillary canine was on the right side, the sinus volume of the impacted side was smaller than that of the erupted contralateral side. In the group in which the maxillary impacted canine was on the left side, the sinus volumes on the impacted and control side were found to be statistically similar [17]. This difference may be explained by the inclusion of individuals with both buccal and palatal impacted canine teeth in that study conducted by Oz et al.

According to our results, the right / left side position of the unilateral palatally impacted maxillary canine has no effect on hemi-arch length and hemi-arch widths on impacted and nonimpacted sides. Our findings are in accordance with Yassaei et al. [30] Consequently, the data from individuals with an impacted canine tooth on the right and left, and from female and male, were pooled to form a single group.

The analysis of the sinus volume as a right-left group and as a male-female group combined revealed that, although the mean sinus volume on the impacted canine side was numerically smaller than that of the contralateral non-impacted control side, no statistically significant difference was observed. Similar to our findings, Tassoker et al. [19] reported no statistically significant difference between the presence of impacted canine teeth and sinus volume. This finding is partially in accordance with the findings of Oz et al. [17], as they reported that when the canine was impacted on the left side, the sinus volume was similar between the impacted and non-impacted sides. Conversely, Alhaija et al. [21] and Faiq et al. [22] observed that when the canine was impacted on the palatal side, the volume of the maxillary sinus was significantly smaller on the impacted side compared to the non-impacted side. The discrepancy in outcomes may be attributed to the racial, numerical, and skeletal malocclusion characteristics of the sample groups, as well as the anatomical distance between the canine teeth and the sinus.

According to our study results, the hemi-arch length on the side of impaction was shorter than that on the non-impacted side. These outcomes were consistent with the findings of other research studies [24], with the exception of Dadgar et al. [31]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the mesial movement of the premolars and molars on the impacted canine side towards the site of the absent canine tooth, or it may be associated with eruption of the canine tooth, which widens the dental arch and increases its dimension. During the eruption of the maxillary teeth, the canine is the terminal tooth to erupt and develops the maxillary dental arch corner by erupting buccally, thereby providing the optimal arch shape. It has been proposed that the placement of the canines and intercanine width have a significant influence on the morphology of dental arch forms. [32] In the absence of maxillary canine, it is not possible to form the arch shape ideally. In this study, in order to determine the effect of impaction on dental hemi-arch width, hemi-arch width measurements were performed at 7-, 14-, 21- and 28-mm levels on the sutura palatina media from anterior to posterior in the sagittal direction, with the incisal edges of the central incisors as the starting point. In the present study, the hemi-arch width measurement on the reference line at the 7-mm point was found to be approximately at the level of the canine tooth. The results of this study indicate that the transverse hemi-arch width at the seventh millimetre reference line, where the maxillary canine tooth coincides, is statistically narrower on the side where the maxillary canine is impacted than on the contralateral erupted side. Moss’s functional matrix theory posits that the primary stimulus for bone growth and development is derived from functional matrices. The observation that the maxilla developed on the side where the canine tooth erupted, and the arch width increased, lends support to the theory proposed by Moss. [33] Arriola-Guillén et al. [25] reported a similar trend to our study, where they found a shorter transversal hemi-arch width on the impacted side compared to the contralateral erupted side in cases of unilaterally impacted canines, specifically from the region between the first premolar and the canine to the midpalatinal raphe.

The inclusion of a broad age range (13–36 years) may be considered a limitation, as maxillary sinus pneumatisation and alveolar bone morphology continue to develop into late adolescence [5] and typically stabilize after skeletal maturity. Although our inclusion criteria stipulated that the permanent maxillary dentition (excluding third molars) had fully erupted to provide an adequate functional dental arch, we acknowledge the developmental variability within our cohort. Additionally, the sample was predominantly female (23 females, 5 males). While this is consistent with the higher prevalence of canine impaction in women, it may limit the generalizability of our volumetric measurements to a broader population. Nonetheless, the split-mouth design inherently mitigated these developmental confounders; because each patient served as their own internal control, systemic age- and sex-related differences affected both the impacted and non-impacted sides equally.

Although the mid-palatal suture served as our midline reference point, assuming it as a perfect transverse symmetry landmark may be a limitation, given that single skeletal variations may exist even among clinically balanced subjects. An additional potential limitation is that certain reference lines are derived from dental landmarks, which may be influenced by tooth movements such as tipping or rotation. In our study, the potential for such biases was significantly mitigated by our split-mouth design [26] and the inclusion of skeletally Class I participants. Future research should consider utilizing more stable internal cranial or palatal landmarks to enhance measurement stability, especially in cases involving severe dental conditions and crowding.

There is a possible constraint in choosing the first axial slice that demonstrates buccal cortical bone as the vertical origin for measurements with an absolute reference to anatomy, which might not be standardized. Although this functional radiographic landmark elucidated a reproducible, measurable cortical margin, it may not be free of the effects of individual variability in vertical dentition positioning. In our split-mouth experimental setup, the horizontal reference was used symmetrically on both the impacted and non-impacted sides within the same subject. This approach effectively controlled for potential vertical position shifts that could influence comparisons of arch dimensions.

Regarding imaging, the 0.4 mm voxel size used for clinical consistency in this study may provide less precision compared to smaller voxels (≤ 0.3 mm), which could increase a potential measurement limitation.

Due to the retrospective nature of our CBCT evaluations, comprehensive sinonasal characterization, including the assessment of mucosal thickening and ostial patency, was not possible. Therefore, cases with evident sinus pathology were excluded, and three-dimensional volumetric segmentation was limited to the bony boundaries of the maxillary sinus. In addition, the vertical and anteroposterior positions of the impacted canines were not evaluated as separate variables. Variations in canine position may influence the anatomical relationship between the impacted canine and the maxillary sinus, potentially affecting sinus dimensions. Future prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes should incorporate detailed sinonasal assessments and canine positional analyses to further clarify the influence of these factors on maxillary sinus morphology.

Clinical implications

Although unilateral palatally impacted maxillary canines were not associated with a reduction in maxillary sinus volume, their close anatomical relationship with the maxillary sinus remains clinically relevant. This proximity should be carefully considered during diagnosis and treatment planning, as surgical exposure, extraction procedures, or infection-related complications involving impacted canines may potentially affect adjacent sinus structures. Furthermore, the observed reduction in hemi-arch length and anterior transverse arch width on the impacted side may have implications for orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. These findings may assist clinicians in the assessment of arch asymmetry, space management, and the development of individualized treatment strategies for patients with unilateral palatally impacted maxillary canines.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, the results suggest that maxillary sinus volume did not differ according to the presence of unilateral palatally impacted canine tooth. However, the impaction was reflected by a decrease in the transverse width of the dental arch at the level of the canine tooth and a reduction in the hemi-arch length compared to the erupted side.
