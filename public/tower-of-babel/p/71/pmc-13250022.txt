Lactobacillus salivarius li01 alleviates premature ovarian insufficiency via gut microbiota-mediated modulation of tryptophan metabolism
Authors: Lingnv Yao, Siyuan Xie, Shuobo Zhang, Furong Ba, Huijuan Jing, Bo Qiu, Lvwan Xu, Shengyi Han, Hang Xiao, Ming Zhao, Xiaowei Shi, Lanjuan Li, Mingfei Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremature ovarian insufficiency (POI) threatens women’s reproductive health. Emerging evidence implicates the gut-ovary axis in POI pathogenesis. This study explored the probiotic Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 in a Tripterygium wilfordii glycosides-induced POI mouse model. Li01 effectively mitigated POI progression by restoring hormone levels, increasing functional follicle numbers, and improving ovarian antioxidant function. These beneficial effects were primarily mediated through modulation of the gut microbiota and host tryptophan metabolism. Notably, a tryptophan-enriched diet alone did not improve POI. When combined with Li01, it synergistically amplified the kynurenine pathway. This shift promoted ovarian NAD⁺ production by upregulating the levels of NAMPT, while attenuating NAD⁺ depletion through downregulation of CD38 expression. The elevated NAD⁺ levels subsequently activated SIRT1 expression, stimulated the synthesis of SOD and other antioxidants, and inhibited the Caspase-1/IL‑1β inflammasome axis. These findings highlight Li01 as a potential POI therapy through microbiota-metabolic regulation, offering new mechanistic insights into gut-ovarian interactions. Subject terms: Diseases, Microbiology, Physiology

Introduction

Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) was formally defined by the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology in December 2015. POI is characterized by the loss of ovarian function before the age of 40, typically accompanied by elevated follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels. Globally, approximately 3% of women are affected by POI, with 2–3% of cases occurring in women under 40, and its prevalence has been observed to increase in recent years1. Beyond impaired fertility, POI has been associated with a heightened risk of various health issues, including depression, anxiety, cognitive decline, osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, and elevated mortality2. Although the pathogenesis of POI remains unclear, research has linked POI to multiple factors, such as genetic abnormalities (e.g., chromosomal defects and gene mutations), iatrogenic causes (e.g., surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy), immune dysfunction, and environmental influences3. Hormone replacement therapy is currently considered the standard treatment for managing symptoms and improving quality of life4. Experimental approaches, including immune modulation and gene editing, are being investigated5. Additionally, while in vitro follicular activation has resulted in clinical pregnancies in some instances, its efficacy is still limited, and additional research is required to develop more effective therapeutic strategies.

An accumulating body of research is establishing a strong link between ovarian aging and the gut microbiota. Alterations in the composition and functionality of the gut microbiota are suggested to significantly impact ovarian function via the gut-ovary axis, a bidirectional communication pathway. Women affected by POI frequently demonstrate dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, and this interaction may be reciprocal6. Emerging evidence suggests that hormonal concentrations can exert influence over the gut microbiota, and conversely, alterations in microbial composition may affect circulating hormone levels, as well as modulate the host’s immune response7–9.

Ovarian development and follicular maturation necessitate active energy metabolism to supply vital nutrients. Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, plays a critical role in ovarian function and reproductive health10. Previous research has highlighted the role of tryptophan metabolites (such as serotonin) in regulating ovarian activity and female fertility11. The metabolism of tryptophan can be modulated by changes in the gut microbiota. Clinical cohort studies have demonstrated that probiotics can regulate tryptophan metabolism by altering the composition of gut microbiota, thereby ameliorating mental health, metabolic disorders, and other diseases12,13. This evidence implies that probiotics supplementation may represent an accessible intervention to mitigate the onset of premature ovarian insufficiency.

Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 (Li01), a probiotic strain, was isolated from the feces of healthy individuals in our laboratory14. Previous studies from our group demonstrated that Li01 has the potential to alleviate intestinal inflammation through the modulation of both the gut microbiota and tryptophan metabolism15. Although tryptophan is essential for maintaining physiological homeostasis through the production of bioactive metabolites, excessive levels have been implicated in adverse health outcomes, including tumorigenesis and neuropsychiatric disorders such as anxiety and depression16–18. In this study, the therapeutic efficacy of Li01 was assessed in a POI mouse model. The objective was to determine whether Li01 could ameliorate the symptoms of POI in mice on a tryptophan-enriched diet by regulating tryptophan metabolism. The results of this investigation provide a novel perspective on the management of POI through targeted metabolic intervention.

Results

Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 alleviated Leigongteng-induced Premature Ovarian Insufficiency

Leigongteng (Tripterygium wilfordii), a traditional Chinese medicine used for managing autoimmune disorders due to its immunosuppressive properties5, was employed to establish a POI mouse model based on its reproductive toxicity. The schematic diagram of the animal experiment is shown in Fig. 1b. This study monitored the body weight and estrous cycle of mice in each group. The results showed that there was no significant difference in body weight between control group and POI group, while there is significant difference after Li01 intervention compared to that of POI group (Fig. 1c). Compared with controls, the POI group showed a significantly decreased ovarian coefficient, with ovarian atrophy and a severely disrupted estrous cycle (Fig. 1d, e), which indicated successful construction of the POI model. Interestingly, all the above pathological changes were effectively reversed in the POI+Li01 group.

Serum sex hormones are critical indicators for evaluating ovarian reserve function19. The indices, including AMH, E2, and FSH, were quantified to further assess ovarian function (Fig. 1f). In the POI group, a significant reduction in AMH level was accompanied by a pronounced increase in FSH levels compared to the C group. The relative hormones (AMH and FSH) were normalized in the POI+Li01 group. Meanwhile, it was observed that higher E2 with Li01 intervention compared to POI mice.

To further investigate the role of Li01 on POI, the ovarian reserve of mice was examined under a light microscope. The number of follicles in the POI group was significantly reduced compared to the C group, but supplementation with Li01 restored the follicle count in the ovaries, suggesting a potential protective effect. (Fig. 1g). Upon counting the number of follicles at each stage, although no significant difference was found between the C and POI groups, a downward trend in functional follicles and an uptrend of atretic follicles were observed in POI mice. Notably, the POI+Li01 group maintained the number of primary follicles and developing follicles.

Serum cytokine profiles, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-17a, and INF-γ, were also measured. A significantly higher level of IL-1β was detected in the serum of mice in the POI group, and this change was reversed by Li01 intervention. No significant differences were observed in the levels of TNF-α, IL-17A, and INF-γ among the experimental groups (Fig. 1h).

Given the serum levels of IL-1β in mice significantly reduced after Li01 treatment, to clarify its potential source, we examined IL-1β expression in ovarian tissue, as well as the key oxidative stress-related proteins in the ovaries, including SOD and GPX4. As shown by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 1i), Li01 reduced ovarian follicular IL-1β expression and elevated SOD and GPX4 levels, indicating improved local antioxidant and anti-inflammatory status.

Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 alters the gut microbiota and metabolism

To assess the gut microbiota dependency of Li01, we utilized a germ-free (GF) mouse model. In GF mice, Li01 failed to improve estrous cycle disorders (Supplementary Fig. 1c). It showed no effect on ovarian coefficient, hormone levels (E2, AMH, FSH), or most inflammatory markers compared to POI controls (Supplementary Fig. 1b, d–f). Notably, Li01 treatment in GF mice did not reduce but instead increased serum IL-1β (Supplementary Fig. 1f). Moreover, its protective effect, observed in SPF mice, was abolished in the GF setting, confirming that Li01’s efficacy requires an intact gut microbiota.

To investigate the effect of Li01 on the gut microbiota, we conducted a compositional analysis. The POI group was characterized by a reduced abundance of Desulfobacterota and Verrucomicrobiota, alongside an increased proportion of Firmicutes. In contrast, Li01 treatment reversed these shifts by enriching Bacteroidetes and Desulfobacterota and reducing Firmicutes, leading to a decreased Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio (Fig. 2a). At the genus level, the abundance of Parasutterella, Peptococcaceae_unclassified, and Parvibacter increased in POI mice, which was reversed after Li01 intervention (Fig. 2b, c). A dramatic increase of potentially beneficial bacteria like Muribaculaceae, Bacteroides, and Alistipes can be observed in the mice of the POI+Li01 group than the POI group, which shows no difference between the normal mice and the POI group. Non-targeted metabolomics and KEGG enrichment analysis further confirmed that Li01 caused significant alterations in gut microbiota metabolic profile, with the highest enrichment levels in the nicotinic acid and nicotinamide metabolism and tryptophan metabolism pathways (Fig. 2d, e). Moreover, it was found that Li01 intervention enhanced the NADH/NADPH and tryptophan metabolization-related pathways through PICRUSt2 functional predictive analysis (Fig. 2f).

Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 prevents colonic inflammation and may enhance antioxidant capacity of the ovaries through the kynurenine pathway

Given that Li01 reduces serum IL-1β—a cytokine linked to colonic inflammation20, affects microbial tryptophan metabolism, we further investigated the effects of Li01 on colonic inflammation and host tryptophan metabolism, we therefore investigated effects of Li01 on colonic inflammation and host tryptophan metabolism. Histological examination revealed that the colonic structures of each group were intact (Fig. 3a). Our results revealed that, compared with the POI group, Li01 intervention significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of Caspase-1 and NF-κB in colonic tissue (Fig. 3b).

Enzymes, including Kyat 1, Kyun, and IDO, are key enzymes involved in tryptophan metabolism16. In this study, we found that Li01 specifically upregulated colonic gene expression of kynurinase (KYUN) rather than the other two enzymes, indicating that it may activate the tryptophan kynurenine (KP) metabolic pathway of the host (Fig. 3c).

In ovarian tissue, IL-1β was predominantly enriched within the follicles. Immunofluorescence analysis of IL-1β pathway proteins (caspase-1, NF-κB, NLRP3) revealed elevated levels of caspase-1 and NF-κB in POI mice compared to controls. Prophylactic treatment with Li01 significantly reduced caspase-1 expression. This suppression aligns with the observed decrease in serum IL-1β levels, supporting the conclusion that Li01 alleviates ovarian damage by inhibiting the caspase-1/IL-1β inflammasome axis.

The mitigation of premature ovarian insufficiency by tryptophan is dependent on Li01

It has been reported that dietary tryptophan supplementation may have anti-aging effects21. In this study, we found that the tryptophan metabolic pathway is crucial for Li01 to alleviate POI. This prompted us to investigate whether Li01 in combination with a tryptophan-enriched diet could more effectively alleviate POI.

The results showed that compared with the C group, both the POI group and the POI+Trp group mice experienced ovarian atrophy (Fig. 4c) and estrous cycle disorders (Fig. 4d). Body weight did not differ significantly among the four experimental groups (Fig. 4b). Tryptophan supplementation alone failed to reverse ovarian atrophy or hormonal disorders. However, after the intervention of Li01 (POI+Trp+Li01 group), we observed significant improvements: the ovarian coefficient of mice was restored (Fig. 4c), the level of serum E2 and AMH significantly increased, and FSH showed a downward trend (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, Li01 intervention decreased the levels of IL-1β in POI mice. Histological analysis confirmed that only the POI+Trp+Li01 group significantly restored the number of primary follicles in the ovaries (Fig. 4g). The immunofluorescence staining results of the ovaries of mice in each group also showed that the levels of SOD and GPX-4 was reversed after Trp+Li01 combination intervention in POI mice (Fig. 4h). These data indicate that Li01 is necessary in preventing the occurrence of POI.

Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 regulates gut microbiota and metabolic profile of mice on a tryptophan-enriched diet

The gut microbiota compositions were analyzed across four experimental groups to examine the effects of a tryptophan-enriched diet on the gut microbial community. Analysis revealed significant differences in the β-diversity of gut microbiota among them (Fig. 5a). Notably, only the phylum Actinobacteria exhibited significant differences between the C and POI groups. Consistent with previous findings, the Li01 intervention was found to reduce the F/B ratio in the intestine. Furthermore, compared to the POI+Trp group, the Li01 intervention significantly enhanced the abundance of Verrucomicrobiota at the phylum level (Fig. 5b, c). A total of 29 distinct bacterial genera were identified in the gut microbiota using LEfSe analysis (Fig. 5d). NM07_P_09, Amulumruptor, and other genera were predominant in the intestines of normal mice. Conversely, Adlercreutzia, Sporofaciens, Velocimicrobium, and Evtepia displayed a significant increase in abundance in mice on a tryptophan diet. Additionally, Akkermansia, Phacaeicola A, and Ligilactobacillus were the genera that were most significantly increased in mice that received Li01 intervention (Fig.5e).

Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 facilitates the metabolism of tryptophan through the kynurenine pathway to prevent POI

Targeted metabolomics reveals that the metabolic fate of tryptophan depends on the presence of Li01. As shown in Fig. 6a, there were significant differences in the composition of tryptophan metabolites among the various experimental groups. No significant difference in tryptophan metabolism was observed between the C and POI groups. The levels of intermediate products such as indole-3-acrylic acid, tryptophol, 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid, and quinolinic acid were significantly higher in the POI+Trp group (Fig. 6b). On the contrary, the POI+Trp+Li01 group showed that the kynurenine pathway was significantly activated, and the levels of its downstream metabolites such as niacin, kynurenic acid and xanthuric acid were significantly higher than those of the POI group and the POI+Trp group (Fig. 6b). This proves that Li01 is the key factor driving the conversion of tryptophan to the kynurenine pathway, which has ovarian protective potential.

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Significant differences are denoted by (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001).

Tryptophan metabolism in the gastrointestinal tract is primarily mediated through three main pathways: the kynurenine (KP) pathway, the 5-hydroxytryptamine(serotonin) pathway, and the indole pathway. Mice in the POI+Trp+Li01 group exhibited significant alterations in metabolites related to the KP metabolic pathway compared to the other two groups, whereas mice in the POI+Trp group mainly affected the tryptophan-related indole pathway (Fig. 6c), and specifically upregulated the tryptophan metabolism enzyme Kyat1 (Fig. 6d). The findings suggest that tryptophan-enriched diets can markedly increase the levels of tryptophan-related metabolites in mice, while Li01 intervention predominantly enhances the kynurenine pathway of tryptophan metabolism. Association analysis of 29 bacterial genera with niacin and nicotinamide revealed a significantly positive association between niacinamide and the genera Sporofaciens and NM07-P-09, while niacin was negatively associated with most of these genera (Fig. 6e). Therefore, Li01 was identified to selectively enhance the ability of gut microbiota to metabolize tryptophan into kynurenine.

Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 Restores NAD+ Levels in the Ovary to Exert Ovarian Protective Effects

Interestingly, compared to the POI group, both intervention groups showed increased serum nicotinamide but decreased niacin (Fig. 7a). However, this was not mirrored in the ovaries, where a significant rise in NAD+ occurred in the POI+Trp+Li01 group compared with the POI group (Fig. 7b). NAD⁺ is typically synthesized from nicotinamide via the action of the enzymes NAMPT and NMNAT. Notably, Li01 treatment significantly upregulated ovarian NAMPT expression in POI mice (Fig. 7c), which consequently restored NAD⁺ levels to those of the C group.

CD38 is a major nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-degrading enzyme, whose upregulation (common in aging and inflammation) accelerates NAD+ decline. When evaluating ovarian expression of CD38, no significant difference was observed between the C group, POI group, and POI+Trp. However, it was found that the Li01 intervention significantly downregulated the expression of CD38. SIRT1 is an evolutionarily conserved NAD-dependent deacetylase, and no significant difference in the level of Sirt1 was found among these groups (Fig. 7d). At the protein level, CD38 expression was markedly elevated in the POI group versus the C group. Tryptophan supplementation alone or combined with Li01 reduced CD38 levels compared to the POI group, while Sirt1 upregulation was observed exclusively in the POI+Trp+Li01 co-treatment group (Fig. 7e).

In our experiments, it was detected significantly lower IL-1β levels were in the peripheral blood of both the POI+Trp and POI+Trp+Li01 groups compared to controls. Immunofluorescence analysis of ovarian tissues further revealed a reduction in IL-1β protein expression, but only in the POI+Trp+Li01 group. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we assessed the protein expression of key inflammatory mediators, including Caspase-1, NF-κB, and NLRP3. Consistent with prior findings, the POI group exhibited marked upregulation of Caspase-1 and NF-κB in ovarian tissues relative to the C group, while treatment with POI+Trp+Li01 significantly attenuated the POI-induced increase in Caspase-1 expression (Fig. 7f). Tryptophan supplementation alone did not affect colonic structure (Supplementary Fig. 2a) or colonic expression of Caspase-1, NF-κB, and NLRP3 in POI mice. While tryptophan alone reduced Caspase-1 mRNA, only the combined intervention (POI+Trp+Li01) significantly downregulated key pro-inflammatory genes Caspase-1 and NF-κB (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

Collectively, the above data reveal that by modulating the gut microbiota and shifting tryptophan metabolism to the kynurenine pathway, Li01 upregulates ovarian NAMPT while suppressing CD38, thereby elevating NAD⁺ levels and exerting ovarian protection. Without Li01, tryptophan supplementation alone cannot activate this metabolic axis.

Discussion

Ovarian dysfunction is one of the major causes of the significant decline in female fertility. Factors such as increased DNA damage, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction contribute to ovarian aging22,23. Many studies have confirmed that alterations in gut microbiota structure can alleviate premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), suggesting that gut microbiota may play an important role in the development of POI disease24. Probiotics have been shown to ameliorate various diseases through the regulation of the gut microbiota25. Leigongteng is a traditional Chinese medicine that may induce severe reproductive toxicity through oxidative stress in the ovary26,27. In this study, a POI animal model was established through oral gavage with Leigongteng, according to a previously reported method28. Subsequently, the mice were fed with Li01 (POI+Li01) to assess its impact on POI.

POI is often characterized by a deficiency of sex hormones, including follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), estradiol (E2), and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH)29. AMH, a glycoprotein hormone secreted by small ovarian follicles, is a direct biomarker of the ovarian reserve (follicular pool) and is commonly used clinically to evaluate diminished ovarian reserve30. FSH is crucial for reproduction, regulating the menstrual cycle, and stimulates egg growth in the ovaries. Abnormal levels of FSH can lead to infertility. Serum E2 concentration is used to assess follicle maturation, with higher levels typically indicating more growing follicles. In line with previous reports28, our Leigongteng-treated mice exhibited hallmark POI phenotypes - reduced body mass and ovarian coefficient, profound AMH depletion, elevated FSH, and persistent estrous disruptions - underscoring the reproducibility of the Leigongteng-induced POI model. Interestingly, Li01 intervention significantly restored estrous cyclicity, increased the ovarian coefficient, and reversed the abnormal hormone profile characterized by decreased AMH and elevated FSH in POI mice. Histological analysis further revealed that Li01 increased the number of primary and developing follicles, indicating protection of ovarian reserve.

These improvements were closely associated with reduced levels of the inflammatory cytokine IL-1β in ovarian tissue. Previous research has highlighted that the inflammatory cytokine IL-1β is significantly elevated in mice with POI31. High expression of IL-1 has also been observed in granulosa cells from patients with ovarian insufficiency32. Consequently, IL-1β is also a critical indicator in the development of POI. Caspase-1, also referred to as interleukin-1β converting enzyme (ICE), is a critical member of the caspase family. It plays a pivotal role in mediating inflammatory responses. Upon pathogen infection or cellular damage, Caspase-1 becomes activated, leading to the cleavage and subsequent activation of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β. This process initiates a potent inflammatory cascade33. In this study, it was found that the intervention of Li01 reversed the caspase-1 levels in the ovaries, suggesting that Li01 may mitigate ovarian damage by suppressing the Caspase-1/IL-1β pathway.

The gut-ovary axis provides a plausible framework for these effects. Sex steroids influence gut microbial ecology, while microbiota shape host endocrine and immune tone through microbial-associated metabolites. Sex hormones like E2 also participate in communication between gut microbiota and their hosts, playing crucial roles in reproduction, differentiation, cell proliferation, apoptosis, inflammation, metabolism, and homeostasis34. Additionally, the gut microbiota affects various stages and levels of female reproduction, including follicle and oocyte maturation, fertilization, embryo migration, implantation, and even the entire pregnancy and delivery process35. Evidence suggests that gut microbiota dysbiosis may induce POI22,24. Consistent with prior observations in aging models6, our POI mice exhibited an increased Firmicutes/Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio. Li01 reversed these compositional shifts, increasing beneficial commensals, such as Muribaculum, Lachnospiaceae, Bacteroides, and Lactobacillus, which are considered protective bacterial genera in the aging process36–38. The complete failure of Li01 in germ-free mice demonstrates that its protective effect on POI is strictly contingent upon its capacity to remodel the gut microbiota ecosystem.

To further explore the gut-ovary axis, we conducted serum metabolomics. Pathway analysis highlighted disturbances in tryptophan and nicotinamide metabolism. Since metabolites from these pathways (e.g., kynurenine, NAD⁺) are strongly linked to ovarian health39–41, our findings point to the modulation of host tryptophan metabolism as a key mechanism through which Li01 prevents POI.

Tryptophan metabolism serves as a critical link between the gut microbiota and host health, with reported anti-aging effects21. As our prior data showed that Li01 significantly alters the host’s tryptophan metabolic profile, we hypothesized that Li01 protects the ovaries by modulating this pathway. To test this and define Li01’s role, we employed a tryptophan-fortified diet to compare simple tryptophan supplementation versus its combination with Li01 in POI prevention.

The metabolism of tryptophan in the body primarily involves three pathways: the kynurenine pathway, the 5-hydroxytryptamine (serotonin) pathway, and the indole pathway16. Existing research suggests that metabolites from the serotonin pathway are involved in regulating oocyte maturation and embryonic development, while indole metabolites (such as indole-3-acetic acid and indole-3-aldehyde) exert a certain protective effect on the ovaries42–44. However, in this study, intervention with Li01 did not lead to significant changes in metabolites related to the serotonin or indole pathways, with their levels showing no notable differences compared to the POI group.

In this study, tryptophan supplementation alone failed to improve POI. In contrast, Li01 intervention shifted tryptophan metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway, significantly elevating downstream metabolites such as niacin and kynurenic acid. The core finding was that only the Li01 combination group (POI+Trp+Li01), not tryptophan alone, significantly increased local ovarian levels of nicotinamide and NAD⁺. This key distinction establishes Li01 as the essential driver that channels tryptophan metabolism into an ovarian-protective state.

NAD⁺ is a central molecule in cellular energy metabolism and stress response, whose depletion is a hallmark of aging45. In tissues, NAD+ is primarily replenished from nicotinamide through the salvage pathway, the process largely governed by the rate-limiting enzymes NAMPT and NMNAT46–48. Here, the results revealed that Li01 significantly increased NAD⁺ levels in ovarian tissue through a dual regulatory mechanism: first, by enhancing the expression of NAMPT, a rate-limiting enzyme in the NAD⁺ salvage pathway; and second, by downregulating the NAD⁺-consuming enzyme CD38, which is often activated during inflammation and aging49,50. Additionally, Li01 increased the protein level of SIRT1, an NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase, further strengthening the cellular homeostasis network51,52. As an NAD + -dependent deacetylase, SIRT1 can deacetylate and activate transcription factors such as FOXO3 and PGC‑1α, thereby promoting the expression of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD53. As a crucial intracellular antioxidant enzyme, SOD alleviates oxidative stress and cellular damage by catalyzing the conversion of superoxide radicals54,55. In the present study, Li01 intervention not only elevated Sirt1 protein levels but also significantly upregulated SOD expression in ovarian tissues. This coordinated upregulation reduces oxidative damage, which in turn suppresses pro-inflammatory signaling by inhibiting Caspase‑1 and IL‑1β activation to maintain normal follicular development and ovarian function. Given that inflammation is a known inducer of CD38 expression56,57, the reduction in ovarian IL‑1β likely contributes to the downregulation of CD38 observed in our study. This interaction forms a beneficial feed‑forward loop that ultimately favors ovarian homeostasis.

This study demonstrates that Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 effectively prevents primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) in mice by restoring follicular development, normalizing hormone profiles, and rescuing estrous cyclicity. The protective effect is strictly dependent on an intact gut microbiota. Mechanistically, Li01 reshapes the gut microbial community and directs host tryptophan metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway. This metabolic reprogramming elevates ovarian NAD+ levels while suppressing the NAD⁺-degrading enzyme CD38, thereby boosting local NAD⁺ bioavailability, alleviating inflammation, and preserving ovarian reserve. Our work establishes a novel link between microbial modulation, tryptophan metabolic reprogramming, and ovarian NAD⁺ homeostasis. A key finding is the critical diet‑microbe interaction: Li01 converts the potential pro‑inflammatory effect of a tryptophan‑enriched diet into a therapeutic outcome by orchestrating its metabolic fate.

The leigongteng-induced POI model, while recapitulating certain aspects of POI, may not fully capture the complex pathophysiology of spontaneous human cases, particularly those with genetic or immune-mediated etiologies. The clinical correlation of our current findings is limited. We will focus on enhancing the translational aspect of this work in subsequent studies.

Methods

Bacterial propagation and growth conditions

Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 (CGMCC 7045) was isolated from healthy individuals. The stock culture was maintained in MRS broth (Oxoid, Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK) supplemented with 25% glycerol at −80 °C. For propagation, Li01 was anaerobically cultured in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h using a COY vinyl anaerobic chamber (Grass Lake, USA) with an airlock system (95% N₂ and 5% H₂). After incubation, the bacterial pellet was washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then resuspended in PBS to achieve a Li01 solution concentration of approximately 10⁹ CFU/mL for further use.

Animal experiment design

The tryptophan diet was prepared by supplementing standard chow with 0.4% tryptophan (SYSE BIO, Changzhou, China)58. The POI mouse model was induced by daily oral administration of Tripterygium Glycosides (TG, Yuanda Feiyun Pharmacy, Chongqing, China, Batch No.: 20221002) at a dose of 50 mg/Kg28. Prior to administration, TG tablets were ground into powder, dissolved in PBS, and thoroughly mixed before use.

Experiment 1

Twenty-four female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly assigned into three groups (n = 8 per group): Control (C) group, POI group, and POI+Li01 group (Fig. 1a). The POI+Li01 group was given 200 µL of Li01 (~10⁹ CFU/mL) via oral gavage every day. The Control and POI groups were given 200 µL of PBS instead of bacteria. All POI groups were given TG every day from day 22 for two consecutive weeks. Vaginal exfoliated cells were collected daily from mice between 9:00 am and 11:00 am. Mice were weighed and recorded every 2 days15. On day 35, serum, colonic tissues, colonic contents, bilateral ovaries, and vaginal secretions were collected for subsequent analysis.

Experiment 2

Ten female ICR mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomized into two groups (n = 5 per group): POI group, POI+Li01 group (Fig. 3e). To ensure successful colonization, the POI+Li01 group was given 200 µL of Li01 (~106 CFU/mL) via oral gavage every day for three continuous days. Mice in the POI group were given 200 µL of PBS instead of bacteria. Mice were given TG from day 4 every day for two consecutive weeks. Vaginal exfoliated cells were collected daily from mice between 9:00 am and 11:00 am. On day 17, serum and bilateral ovaries were collected for subsequent analysis.

Experiment 3

Thirty-two female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomized into four groups (n = 8 per group): C group, POI group, POI+Try group, and POI+Try+Li01 group (Fig. 3a). The POI+Try and POI+Try+Li01 groups were fed with tryptophan-enriched diets, while the POI group was provided with standard chow. The POI+Try+Li01 group was given 200 µL of Li01 (~10⁹ CFU/mL) via oral gavage every day. C, POI, and POI+Trp groups were given 200 µL of PBS instead of bacteria. Mice were weighed and recorded every 2 days. All POI groups were given TG every day from day 22 for two consecutive weeks. Vaginal exfoliated cells were collected daily from mice between 9:00 am and 11:00 am. Mice were weighed and recorded every 2 days15. On day 35, serum, colonic tissues, colonic contents, bilateral ovaries, and vaginal secretions were collected for subsequent analysis.

All mice involved in the experiment will be anesthetized in cages containing cotton soaked with a 5% isoflurane solution (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China). Once immobilized, blood samples will be collected via ocular extraction, followed by euthanasia through decapitation. All animal procedures complied with ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Animal Experimental Ethical Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, College of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Permit No. 2024-0023), in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Estrous cycle monitoring

Vaginal exfoliated cells were fixed on slides, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and observed under a microscope to determine estrous cycle stages (Fig. 1a).

Quantification of serum hormones and cytokines

Blood samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to isolate serum. Hormone levels of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), estradiol (E2), and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) were measured using ELISA kits (Reed Biotech, Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum cytokines were also measured by ELISA kits (BIO-RAD, Shanghai, China).

Histopathology examination

The collected ovary and colon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 µm thickness. Sections were stained with H&E and scanned using a 3DHISTECH Pannoramic 250 scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

16S rRNA sequencing and gut microbiota analysis

Fecal DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, USA). The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers: Forward: ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA; Reverse: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform by Shanghai Paisano Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Microbiota composition was analyzed using the GenesCloud platform (www.genescloud.cn), as previously described27.

Serum metabolomics analysis

Serum samples from each group were used for metabolomics analysis. The frozen samples were thawed at 4 °C and vortexed for 1 min to ensure homogeneity. Afterwards, an aliquot of 400 μL methanol was added to each serum sample (2 mL centrifuge tube) and vortexed for 1 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube, concentrated, and lyophilized. The dried extracts were then reconstituted in 150 μL of 2-chloro-l-phenylalanine solution (4 ppm in 80% methanol/water, v/v), filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and subjected to LC-MS analysis.

Determination of fecal tryptophan and its metabolites

Colonic content (50 mg) was extracted with 80% methanol overnight for protein precipitation. After homogenization and centrifugation, the supernatant was freeze-dried, reconstituted in 100 µL of a 50% acetonitrile: water solution, and analyzed via LC–MS. A 5 µL aliquot was injected into an Agilent 1290 UPLC system coupled to a 6495 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was conducted using an Agilent Eclipse Plus C18 column (3.0 × 150 mm, 1.8 µm). The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.5 mM ammonium fluoride with 0.1% acetic acid and (B) acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient program was:0–0.5 min: 98% A; 0.5–8 min: 20%A; 8–8.5 min:0%A; 8,5–8.6 min:98%A; 8.6–11.5 min: 98%A. Flow rate: 0.3 mL/min.

LC–MS analysis of tryptophan metabolites in ovaries

Ovarian tissues were homogenized in 500 µL of 80% aqueous methanol on ice, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C and 3000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was diluted with LC-MS-grade water and centrifuged again. The final supernatant was analyzed using LC–MS. Equal volumes of all samples were pooled for quality control. Standards included nicotinamide, L-kynurenine, kynurenic acid (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and NAD+ (Aladdin, Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence staining

After dewaxing, hydration, and antigen retrieval (EDTA, pH 9.0; high-temperature/pressure), sections were subjected to sequential quadruple immunofluorescence using TSA. The procedure was: serum blocking, followed by sequential incubation with primary antibody, HRP-secondary antibody, and tyramide-fluorophore for each target: GPX4 (abcam, ab125066), IL-1β (proteintech 26048-1-AP), superoxide dismutase (SOD) 2 (Starter S0B6056), Caspase-1 ((proteintech 22915-1-AP), NF-κB (abcam, ab32536), NLRP3 (Huabio, ET1610-93), CD38 (STARTER, S0B2020), Sirt1 (Huabio, ET1603-3), NAMPT (proteintech,11776-1-AP), and NMNAT (Abcam, ab56980). An antibody elution step followed each round. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and sections were mounted for fluorescence microscopy.

Quantitative real-time PCR analyses

Total RNA of the samples was extracted according to the instructions of AFTSpin Tissue/Cell Fast RNA Extraction Kit for Animal (Abclonal, RK30120). The RNA concentration and purity of RNA were detected by NanoDrop 2000. The prime script RT Master Mix (Abclonal, RK20402) was applied to reverse 1 μg total RNA into cDNA. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, real-time qPCR was carried out on the QuantStudio5 (Applied Biosystems) using the SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (Abclonal, RK21203). The normalization control for relative quantification of the data was GAPDH. Primers were listed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Graphs were generated using DataGraph (Visual Data Tools, Inc.). Statistical significance was evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test.
