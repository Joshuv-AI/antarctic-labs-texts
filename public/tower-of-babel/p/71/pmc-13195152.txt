Study of sand particle motion characteristics and distribution patterns in a helical-blade multiphase pump
Authors: Xuhui Zhao, Guangtai Shi, Zonghui Cui, Yanping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo reveal the dynamic characteristics and distribution patterns of sand particles in multiphase pumps used in closed oilfield gathering and transportation systems, this study focuses on a helical-blade multiphase pump. Through a systematic investigation of the effects of sand particle parameters (such as diameter, concentration, and shape) and pump operating parameters (including flow rate, rotational speed, and oil content) on the solid-liquid two-phase flow within the pump, the study aims to elucidate how these factors influence the internal motion behavior and spatial distribution of sand particles. The results indicate that sand particle diameter significantly influences two-phase flow trajectories, vortex structures, and particle motion, whereas the effects of concentration and shape are relatively weak. Higher flow rates and rotational speeds, combined with lower oil content, increase the kinetic energy of the fluid, thereby intensifying radial momentum fluctuations in the impeller. Axial momentum increases initially and then decreases with increasing particle size, rises with higher particle concentration, and remains unaffected by particle shape. Larger particles reduce transport efficiency, while higher concentrations increase the mass loading in key regions of the pump. High flow rates reduce the internal sand concentration, thereby facilitating transport; low rotational speeds increase sand concentration in critical locations. Increased oil content enhances fluid viscosity and drag forces, facilitating sand discharge. These findings provide theoretical insights into the coupled mechanisms of particle motion and energy dissipation in sand-laden multiphase flows, and offer practical guidance for anti-wear design and operational optimization of helical-blade multiphase pumps.Keywords: Multiphase pump, Sand particle motion, Momentum analysis, Distribution characteristicsSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Petroleum resources serve as a cornerstone of the global energy system, permeating virtually every facet of modern society-from aerospace and transportation to chemical manufacturing and agricultural security. Global oil demand continues on an upward trajectory and is projected to peak at approximately 4.8 to 5.2 billion metric tons by 20301. However, the gradual depletion of conventional oil and gas resources means that the reserves of thin oil and shallow reservoirs can no longer meet demand2, thus compelling the industry to advance into resource-rich unconventional regions such as deserts, arid zones, and ultra-deep formations3–7. Nevertheless, this shift has led to a significant increase in the sand content of the oil-gas mixed transportation medium, resulting in severe wear of the pump’s flow-passing components8–10 and a substantial reduction in their service life.

To address these challenges, scholars have conducted a series of studies through both experimental investigations and numerical simulations. In terms of experimental research, Fang et al.11 modified the solid-liquid two-phase flow model, thereby improving prediction accuracy. Rajkumar and Othayo et al.12,13 employed a method combining flow visualization and CFD simulation to observe particle motion, clarifying the behavior of solid particles under different operating conditions. Espinoza et al.14 successfully determined particle velocity and impact angle from turbulent slurry jets onto moving target surfaces using ultrasonic velocimetry. To further analyze the spatiotemporal distribution of particles in flow channels, Shi et al.15 applied an improved PIV technique to measure solid-liquid two-phase flow fields, examining particle distribution and its influence on the performance of different impellers; Kang et al.16 employed a Phase Doppler Particle Analyzer (PDPA) to obtain real velocity data of solid particles in actual flows. Wang et al.17 used high-speed photography to capture particle trajectories, providing an intuitive and accurate data foundation for analysis. Furthermore, Wang and Chen et al.18,19 revealed fluid flow paths and velocity distributions inside pumps by analyzing particle trajectory concentration distributions; Hu et al.20 visually assessed the extent of accumulation inside the pump under different particle sizes based on the residual mass of particles, and Calderon et al.21 found that particle concentration and mass have a direct impact on wear rate. These studies demonstrate that particle concentration distribution and mass distribution are also key focuses in experimental research.

However, designing and conducting complex solid-liquid two-phase flow experiments entail high costs and significant technical challenges. Therefore, many researchers have turned to numerical simulation methods to investigate flow characteristics within fluid machinery. In terms of numerical simulation, Wydrych and Bukreev et al.22,23 employed the Euler-Euler method by treating particles as a pseudo-continuous medium and validated the model’s applicability to complex flow problems. Similarly, based on the Euler-Euler framework, Tarodiya and Peng et al.24,25 simulated the influence of solid particles on centrifugal pump performance and the characteristics of solid-liquid two-phase flow and wear inside pumps, respectively. Nevertheless, the applicability of this method has certain limitations. For instance, Wang et al.26 found substantial deviations between the predictions of the Euler-Euler method and experimental observations in the numerical simulation of gas-solid flow in a rectangular fluidized bed; Geng et al.27 in their study of dust motion in simplified coal ducts and mixed ventilation systems, also demonstrated that this method struggles to accurately capture particle behavior at the microscopic scale. These studies reveal that the Euler-Euler method may still suffer from insufficient simulation accuracy and limited ability to represent microscopic mechanisms under certain operating conditions. Due to its advantages of low cost, short cycle time, and the ability to reveal complex flow and mechanical mechanisms, numerical simulation has been widely applied in the fields of oil and gas drilling, production, and gathering and transportation. In drilling engineering, Yang et al.28 revealed the intrinsic mechanical mechanisms underlying the improvement of mechanical drilling rate in hot dry rock by simulating the coupling effect of impact load and confining pressure release. In the field of reservoir stimulation, Tahir et al.29 conducted numerical simulations to investigate the influence of hard-core imperfections in natural hydrogen reservoirs on CO₂ fracture propagation behavior, clarifying the controlling effect of defect morphology on fracture pressure. In terms of gathering medium modification, Xu et al.30 employed numerical methods to evaluate the improvement effect of nanomaterials on the apparent viscosity of heavy oil and verified its engineering applicability through reservoir environmental assessment. These studies collectively demonstrate the significant role of numerical simulation in analyzing complex engineering problems.

In contrast, the Euler-Lagrange method based on a discrete framework exhibits distinct advantages in characterizing particle-scale behavior. For example, Pandey et al.31 adopted the Euler-Lagrange approach to track the trajectory of individual particles, achieving high accuracy. Building on this method, further studies have elucidated key factors influencing particle motion. Li et al.32, investigating particle transport and deposition characteristics inside turbine blade cooling passages, identified particle velocity as a primary factor affecting particle behavior. Ren et al.33 by analyzing the interactions between fluid and particles as well as particle-particle/wall interactions in vertical pipes, found that these forces significantly influence particle separation and mixing behavior. Hong et al.34 explored the effects of particle parameters (such as size, concentration, and density) and operating conditions (such as flow rate and velocity) on the pressure and particle velocity distribution in mining pumps, showing that particle size and shape play key roles in flow characteristics and particle trajectories within the pump. Song et al.35 conducted numerical simulations of particle motion and erosion characteristics inside pumps under different flow rates and particle concentrations, revealing that vortex structures and secondary flows in the flow field can significantly alter particle trajectories.

In recent years, researchers have conducted extensive work on the internal flow and energy dissipation mechanisms of pumps. Chang et al.36 investigated the energy dissipation mechanism and unsteady flow characteristics of a cooling pump based on the sweeping jet inflow method, revealing the influence of jet excitation on flow structure and energy loss. Chang et al.37 systematically explored the effects of wall roughness on energy dissipation and vortex dynamics in a self-priming centrifugal pump using entropy generation analysis and the Liutex vortex identification method, finding that rough walls significantly alter vortex structures and exacerbate energy loss. Chang et al.38 combined the energy balance equation with the non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm to achieve multi-objective efficiency optimization of an energy storage centrifugal pump, providing theoretical guidance for the efficient conversion of energy within the pump.

Although significant progress has been achieved in the field of solid-liquid two-phase flow, particularly regarding internal flow characteristics and energy loss mechanisms, most studies on multiphase pumps still focus on gas-liquid two-phase flow, clear water conditions, or specific operating parameters. Consequently, attention to sand-related issues in crude oil transportation and the coupling mechanism between particle motion and energy dissipation in sand-laden multiphase media remains relatively limited. The complex interactions between high-viscosity oil-water mixtures and sand particles within helical-blade multiphase pumps have not yet been systematically elucidated. Moreover, recent research on particle motion under sand-containing conditions has primarily focused on centrifugal or slurry pumps, whose blade geometries and medium properties differ significantly from those of helical-blade multiphase pumps, limiting their applicability to sand-laden crude oil scenarios. Additionally, most existing studies employ simplified particle models or neglect the effects of fluid viscosity variations, failing to fully account for complex mechanisms such as drag enhancement, vortex structure evolution, and altered particle followability in high-viscosity media.

Therefore, this study focuses on a helical-blade multiphase pump to systematically investigate the effects of sand particle parameters and operating conditions on solid-liquid two-phase flow behavior and particle distribution patterns. The primary objective is to reveal the interaction mechanism between energy dissipation and particle motion under sand-laden conditions. This work not only provides a theoretical basis for the anti-wear design and operational optimization of multiphase pumps but also holds significant theoretical importance and engineering value for elucidating the internal flow mechanisms of helical-blade multiphase pumps and guiding their reliable operation in sand-containing crude oil environments.

Numerical simulation method

Physical model

The object of study is a multistage helical-blade multiphase pump independently developed by the research team and already deployed in an actual oilfield. Since all stages of the pump have identical structure and similar flow characteristics, a single stage is adopted as the research object to reduce computational cost. The particle motion in a single stage can represent the overall behavior in a multistage pump, and the multistage effect is mainly reflected in the cumulative increase of head, with little influence on the particle distribution trend, which has been verified by previous studies6,34. The performance parameters of the pump are detailed in Table 1. The inlet and outlet sections of the pump were extended to more than three and six times the length of the pressure-increasing unit, respectively, using UG software, to ensure fully developed flow fields. The geometric models of the impeller domain and the diffuser domain were constructed with BladeGen software. The numerical model of the multiphase pump is shown in Fig. 1.

Meshing of the computational domain

The computational domain for the simulation primarily consists of four parts: the inlet extension, the impeller, the diffuser, and the outlet extension. Structured meshes for the inlet and outlet sections were generated using ICEM, while TurboGrid was employed to create the structured meshes for the impeller and diffuser domains. The boundary layer in the near-wall regions was refined through grid clustering. Thirty grid layers were arranged longitudinally within the tip clearance, and an O-grid topology was applied around the blades. Figure 2 shows the local mesh refinement in the regions of the blade leading edge, trailing edge, and tip clearance. In the numerical simulation, the wall y⁺ values are controlled below 5 (0–2.0), meeting the computational requirements of the SST k-ω turbulence model without the need for wall functions.

Grid independence verification

Mesh quality is a critical factor governing the accuracy and computational efficiency of Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations. In this study, the Euler-Lagrange approach was adopted to numerically simulate the solid-liquid two-phase flow within the target multiphase pump. While this method accurately tracks particle trajectories in a Lagrangian framework, it is computationally intensive for large particle populations. Therefore, to balance precision and efficiency for subsequent multi-condition simulations, a systematic grid independence study was performed, using the pump head under clear water conditions as the primary validation metric.

As presented in Table 2, the calculated pump head values for different grid schemes fluctuated during the initial 1,000 iterations but gradually stabilized to an order of magnitude of 10⁻⁴. Convergence was achieved when the total grid number reached 4.19 million, with the head deviation from finer grids being less than 0.3%. Furthermore, the non-dimensional wall distance (y⁺) was maintained below 5, which is essential for the validity of the SST k-ω turbulence model and ensures accurate capture of flow features in critical regions such as the near-wall boundary layer and tip clearance.

Boundary conditions setup

The finite volume method was employed to discretize the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations for numerical simulation of the multiphase pump’s flow under various operating conditions. The specific settings were as follows: the SST k-ω model was applied to handle liquid-phase turbulence. The inlet boundary was set as a velocity inlet, and the outlet as a pressure outlet. The SIMPLE algorithm was used for coupled pressure-velocity solving. The interface between rotating and stationary domains was treated using the frozen rotor model. A two-way coupled Discrete Phase Model (DPM) was adopted for the particle phase.

Turbulent Inlet Conditions: The turbulent intensity and turbulent length scale were determined based on the hydraulic diameter and mean flow velocity using standard empirical correlations for pipe flow. The turbulent kinetic energy and specific dissipation rate were derived accordingly. A uniform velocity profile corresponding to the design flow rate was applied at the inlet. To ensure fully developed flow before entering the impeller, an inlet extension with a length of three times the impeller axial length was employed, which is consistent with common practice in turbomachinery simulation.

Discrete Phase Model Settings: Sand particles (density 2650 kg/m³) were uniformly injected normal to the inlet boundary with a velocity equal to the fluid velocity. The particle injection rate was calculated based on the inlet sand concentration and the mixture volumetric flow rate (see Table 3). Based on field data, the Rosin-Rammler function was used to describe the particle size distribution, with a size range of 0.1–5.0 mm. Various forces acting on the particles (as described in Eqs. (1)-(12)) were considered. For non-spherical particles, the drag coefficient was corrected using the Haider-Levenspiel model. To ensure statistical representativeness, a total of 35,000 particle parcels were tracked, with the time-averaged results sampled over the last five impeller rotation periods after flow convergence. Particle-wall interactions were modeled using the reflect boundary condition with normal and tangential restitution coefficients of 0.8 and 0.9. The walls were assumed smooth with no adhesion. The inlet and outlet boundaries were set to “escape”, allowing particles to leave the domain freely, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

It should be noted that the first-order upwind scheme may introduce numerical diffusion, which can potentially affect the prediction accuracy of vortex structures and particle trajectories. To evaluate this effect, a comprehensive analysis was conducted in conjunction with the grid independence study. The total number of grids reached 4.19 million, with sufficient refinement in critical areas such as the tip clearance, leading edge, and trailing edge of the blades, enabling effective capture of the main vortex structures in the flow field. With this fine grid resolution, the influence of numerical diffusion from the first-order scheme was significantly suppressed. Furthermore, previous studies33,34 that adopted the first-order scheme at similar grid densities showed good agreement with experimental results, confirming its reliability in engineering applications.

Analysis of results

Trajectories of solid-liquid two-phase flow in the multiphase pump

Based on the solid-liquid two-phase flow trajectories presented in Fig. 6, the flow characteristics under different sand particle parameters are systematically analyzed. As shown in Fig. 6, the fluid velocity inside the flow passage of the multiphase pump gradually decreases along the flow direction, with a portion of low-velocity leakage flow occurring at the impeller rim, while vortices are formed in the middle of the guide vane passage due to a certain level of circulation. Under different sand particle parameters, the trajectories of most sand particles are generally consistent with those of the fluid, yet distinct behaviors are observed owing to the differences in particle properties. With the increase of particle diameter from 0.1 mm to 5.0 mm, the deviation between particle trajectories and fluid trajectories is significantly enhanced, and obvious low-velocity regions of trajectory accumulation appear at the impeller outlet and on the suction side of the guide vanes under the large particle diameter of 5.0 mm, indicating an increase in low-velocity sand particles near the impeller rim and blade surfaces. As the particle volume concentration rises from 2% to 10%, the particle trajectories near the guide vane inlet and blade suction side become notably denser, and the trajectory lines are condensed and partially overlapped at the high concentration of 10%, suggesting a substantial growth in the number of particles passing through the tip clearance and blade surfaces. When the shape factor φ decreases from 1.0 (spherical) to 0.6, the trajectory deflection of particles at the guide vane inlet is gradually intensified, and non-spherical particles deviate more obviously from the main flow direction and penetrate into the low-velocity recirculation zones on both sides of the guide vanes. In contrast, spherical particles (φ = 1.0) exhibit smooth and continuous trajectories, and they can still maintain their motion driven by the fluid even after colliding with flow passage components, thus demonstrating superior conveying performance.

Two-phase flow trajectories under different sand particle parameters.

The solid-liquid two-phase flow trajectories under different flow rate conditions are shown in Fig. 7. Specifically, Fig. 7a presents the liquid phase trajectories, while the three columns in Fig. 7b show the corresponding sand particle trajectories at flow rates of 0.9, 1.0, and 1.1 times the design flow rate, respectively. It can be observed from the figures that as the operating flow rate of the multiphase pump increases from 0.9 to 1.1 times the design flow rate, the fluid velocity inside the flow passage increases significantly, the intensity of fluid vortices in the guide vanes is enhanced accordingly, and the vortices are concentrated in some guide vane passages. Under off-design conditions (0.9Q and 1.1Q), part of the fluid at the inlet of the multiphase pump deviates from the main flow direction, forming local jet regions that hinder fluid flow. The sand particle trajectories under different flow rate conditions reveal several key phenomena. As the flow rate increases, the overall particle velocity rises, reaching a maximum at 1.1Q. However, the enhanced vortices in the guide vanes under high flow rate entrain more sand particles, creating dense trajectory regions within the guide vane passages where particle velocity drops significantly and particles become trapped. At the low flow rate of 0.9Q, particle trajectory accumulation is observed on the blade surfaces and impeller rim, suggesting that fluid energy is insufficient to continue driving the particles after wall collisions. Furthermore, under both off-design conditions (0.9Q and 1.1Q), the influence of the inlet jet leads to relatively dense particle trajectories in the impeller inlet region, implying prolonged particle residence time in this area.

As the rotational speed decreases, the fluid velocity inside the pump significantly reduces, and the inlet flow of the multiphase pump becomes more stable. The solid-liquid two-phase flow trajectories under different oil content conditions shown in Fig. 9, the flow characteristics of the liquid phase and particle phase are first analyzed, where Fig. 9a denotes the liquid phase trajectories and Fig. 9b presents the sand particle trajectories under the corresponding conditions with oil contents set to 10%, 30%, and 50%, respectively. As the oil content in the transported liquid rises, part of the fluid at the impeller inlet moves away from the suction surface. Meanwhile, when the oil content increases from 10% to 50%, the viscosity of the mixed medium grows, leading to a significant decrease in the intensity of vortices in the guide vanes and a gradual weakening of the vortex structures. The increase in mixed viscosity enhances the drag force acting on the sand particles. Consequently, the particle trajectories become more consistent with the liquid motion trajectories as the oil content rises. This alignment is evident under the high oil content condition (50%), where particle trajectories closely follow the distribution pattern of the liquid phase. In contrast, a significant discrepancy between the two is observed at the low oil content (10%). These trajectory characteristics indicate that the oil content significantly affects the motion followability of sand particles in the flow passage by changing the viscosity and drag force of the mixed medium.

Conclusions

Effects of particle parameters

Particle diameter (0.1 mm to 5.0 mm) intensifies trajectory deviation, enhances diffuser vortices, and shifts accumulation from outlet (small) to inlet (large). Radial momentum peaks at 2.5 mm; axial momentum first increases then decreases with diameter.

Particle shape (1.0 to 0.6) causes local deflection at diffuser inlet, but outlet distribution similar to spherical. Non-sphericity reduces average particle size inside flow passages.

Effects of operating parameters

Flow Rate Variation (0.9Q to 1.1Q): Under off-design flow conditions, particles tend to be retained within the flow passage. When the flow rate exceeds the design value, the particle concentration (ranging from 2% to 10%) increases the density of particle trajectories at the diffuser inlet. This elevates both the radial and axial momentum, thereby exacerbating particle accumulation, particularly in the diffuser region. Concurrently, the internal sand concentration decreases, which facilitates particle transport. Off-design conditions also simultaneously increase the positive axial momentum in the impeller and the negative axial momentum in the diffuser.

Rotational Speed Variation (2980 rpm to 2080 rpm): A reduction in rotational speed improves the followability of particles. At a medium speed of 2480 rpm, spiral trajectories are induced within the diffuser. The decrease in speed causes the axial momentum at the impeller inlet to shift from positive to negative, increasing the risk of clogging. Furthermore, this speed reduction leads to a higher concentration of particles in critical regions.

Increase in Oil Content (10% to 50%): As the oil content increases, the higher viscosity of the medium causes particle trajectories to align more closely with the liquid streamlines. This suppresses vortex formation, reduces particle concentration within the flow passages, and increases the concentration at the outlet. Additionally, the average particle size decreases.

Momentum response

Radial momentum fluctuates strongly in impeller, near zero in diffuser. Peaks at 2.5 mm; increases with concentration; unaffected by shape. Higher flow rate, higher speed, and lower oil content intensify impeller radial fluctuations.

Axial momentum in impeller shows two peaks; in diffuser, negative in first three-quarters, positive near outlet. Increases then decreases with diameter (peak at 2.5 mm); rises with concentration. Off-design flow increases both impeller positive and diffuser negative momentum. Lower speed turns impeller inlet momentum negative; higher oil content increases rotor‑stator interface momentum and reduces diffuser negative momentum.

Distribution and concentration response

Mass concentration: Small particles (0.1 mm) concentrate at outlet; large particles (5.0 mm) accumulate at inlet. Higher inlet concentration aggravates accumulation, especially in diffuser. Spherical particles have lower passage concentration than non‑spherical.

Average particle size: Higher inlet concentration increases size in front impeller and rear diffuser. Non‑sphericity reduces passage size. Off-design flow enlarges size in most sections. Lower speed reduces impeller size but increases near diffuser outlet. Higher oil content reduces size throughout, especially at impeller inlet and front diffuser.

Model limitations and engineering implications

Model scope: Assumes spherical or near‑spherical particles (shape factor φ ≥ 0.6), and neglects particle breakage, agglomeration, and electrostatic effects. Grid independence (4.19 million cells, head deviation < 0.3%) and experimental validation (head MAPE 3.03%, efficiency MAPE 7.65%, torque MAPE 2.98%) support the reliability of the results. The findings are applicable within the investigated parameter ranges: particle size 0.1–5.0 mm, concentration 2–10%, and oil content 10–50%.

Engineering guidance: (1) Operate near design flow to reduce deposition; (2) increase oil content to enhance sand transport; (3) strengthen impeller anti‑wear design at high speeds; (4) optimize diffuser inlet or add flushing at low speeds; (5) apply wear‑resistant coatings on diffuser suction side when φ < 0.8.
