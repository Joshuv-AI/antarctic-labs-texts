A dual-function dry electrode for electromyography recording and transcutaneous electrical stimulation
Authors: Maedeh Mohammadiazni, Yue Zhou, Ana Luisa Trejos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectromyography and transcutaneous electrical stimulation are widely used for neuromuscular assessment and therapy, respectively. However, when the target muscle group is small, such as in post-stroke rehabilitation, there is limited space to place separate electrodes for sensing and stimulation. Developing an integrated electrode capable of both functions would address this limitation by enabling precise monitoring and targeted stimulation within the same localized region, while also simplifying system design and improving functional outcomes. Additionally, most current electrodes are wet and require gel, which can be inconvenient for users. This study explored the feasibility of using a dual-function dry electrode to both record electromyography signals and deliver transcutaneous electrical stimulation. To evaluate the practical effectiveness of the electrode for functional applications, a hand gesture recognition algorithm was developed to assess the quality and reliability of the electromyography signals recorded by the electrode. Forty participants wore an armband equipped with these electrodes and performed six different hand gestures. The algorithm achieved a recognition accuracy of 80.5%, which was comparable to the accuracy of the commercial Myo armband-based hand gesture recognition algorithm. The performance of the dual-function electrode in delivering electrical stimulation was assessed by measuring stimulation intensity at three threshold levels across all 40 participants and by analyzing the induced torque data due to stimulation. Results indicated no significant difference in stimulation intensity and the induced torque measurements when compared to those obtained with a commercial electrode (). These findings show potential in integrating transcutaneous electrical stimulation and electromyography functionalities into a single dry electrode, offering a path toward enhanced user comfort, reduced costs, and improved therapeutic interventions.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Engineering, Health care, Medical research, Neuroscience

Introduction

The combination of electromyography (EMG) and transcutaneous electrical stimulation (TES) marks an innovative fusion in evaluating neuromuscular function and administering therapeutic treatments1–3. EMG functions as a diagnostic tool4. It records the electrical signals produced by skeletal muscles, providing insights into their function and aiding in the diagnosis of neuromuscular disorders5–7. On the other hand, TES uses electrical impulses applied externally to the skin to regulate nerve and muscle function8. It is used in a variety of fields such as pain alleviation, aiding in rehabilitation, and addressing neurological conditions9–11. Together, these modalities offer a comprehensive approach to understanding and treating neuromuscular conditions, with EMG providing internal insights12, while TES delivers stimulation externally to modulate muscular function13. In rehabilitation, therapists often rely on EMG signals to assess muscle activity and identify impaired regions within muscles. Once these areas are pinpointed, TES is applied to stimulate the muscles for rehabilitation purposes. A challenge arises when both functions, EMG measurement and TES delivery, need to be co-located (or at least placed very close to each other). This requirement becomes particularly critical in applications where precise targeting of the same muscle region is essential, such as in post-stroke rehabilitation or when working with small intrinsic muscle groups. In such cases, therapists typically have to switch between EMG and TES electrodes, which complicates the procedure, may disrupt continuity, and can potentially lead to inaccuracies in identifying and targeting the correct muscle locations. Therefore, a dual-function electrode that can both measure EMG and deliver TES would be highly advantageous, as it would eliminate the need for electrode switching.

In terms of design, electrodes fall into two categories: wet and dry. Wet electrodes, which rely on conductive gels, offer low electrode–skin impedance in the short term as long as the gel remains; however, the gel dries out over time and requires frequent reapplication, which reduces their suitability for prolonged monitoring, particularly in wearable applications. Dry electrodes, by contrast, eliminate the need for gels and enhance comfort during long-term use in wearable applications. While dry electrodes may exhibit higher impedance compared to wet electrodes, several factors can help reduce this impedance. Using highly conductive materials, such as silver, copper, or conductive polymers14, can lower the electrode–skin impedance. Another approach is the application of surface treatments, such as gold-plating15 or conductive coatings, such as PEDOT:PSS16, which enhance surface conductivity. Additionally, proper skin preparation, such as cleaning with alcohol wipes can improve contact quality and further decrease impedance. Gold plating has been shown to be effective in lowering electrode–skin impedance for dry electrodes. Kusche et al.17 demonstrated that dry electrodes fabricated with gold as the contact material can, under optimized conditions such as appropriate pressure and electrode geometry, achieve electrode–skin impedance values within or even below the range of conventional hydrogel electrodes. The actual electrode–skin impedances were determined under several conditions in a frequency range from 24 kHz to 391 kHz, corresponding to the range typically used in bioimpedance setups. Kaveh et al.15 reported that electroless gold plating on 3D-printed electrodes produced average electrode–skin impedance of 66.7 k at 50 Hz, which is comparable to wet electrodes without requiring hydrogels. In addition, they found that the plating process yielded a stable and well-adhered conductive surface that maintained performance after repeated skin contact and environmental exposure. While non-polarizable materials are typically preferred for EMG detection to minimize polarization effects, and electron-rich materials are favored for stimulation to facilitate charge transfer, the gold-plated copper configuration can provide a practical balance that supports both recording and stimulation functions. Taken together, these findings offer promising evidence that gold plating may allow dry electrodes to combine low impedance with robustness for physiological sensing.

Beyond electrode type, the evaluation of electrode performance requires appropriate metrics to quantify recording and stimulation effectiveness. One of the most common metrics used to evaluate EMG electrodes is the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), which quantifies the ratio of wanted EMG signal to unwanted noise or interference, typically ranging from 10 to 50 dB in ideal conditions18–22. Although SNR can assess signal quality, it provides limited insight into how well an electrode performs under realistic conditions involving voluntary muscle activation and signal interpretation. Since the ultimate application of EMG–TES systems is to decode motor intent and deliver stimulation in response to muscle activity, it is essential to verify that the signals recorded by the electrode are of sufficient quality to enable accurate functional interpretation. To this end, implementing a hand gesture recognition (HGR) algorithm serves as a practical method to evaluate electrode performance in a task-relevant context. HGR algorithms are widely used in EMG-based control systems for prosthetics, rehabilitation devices, and human–computer interfaces. Their classification accuracy directly depends on the quality and consistency of the acquired EMG signals, including signal amplitude, spectral content, and temporal stability. Therefore, the classification performance achieved using EMG data collected with the proposed electrode serves as an indirect but comprehensive indicator of its recording capability. In contrast to SNR measurements alone, HGR-based evaluation captures the electrode’s ability to record discriminative and repeatable muscle activity patterns relevant to real-world applications. Besides EMG measurements, evaluating electrode effectiveness in TES delivery typically involves measuring physiological responses, such as sensory and motor thresholds23,24. The sensory threshold refers to the lowest current intensity that induces a tingling feeling, whereas the motor threshold denotes the higher current required to trigger a muscle contraction. Evaluating the electrode through both hand gesture recognition and stimulation delivery provides a comprehensive assessment. This approach is particularly important for intention-driven applications, such as post-stroke rehabilitation, where accurate detection of the user’s intended movements and timely stimulation of weakened muscle groups are essential for effective therapy.

Based on the considerations discussed above, this study developed a dual-function dry electrode with a gold-plated copper interface, designed to enable both EMG recording and TES delivery. The electrode’s performance was evaluated using both SNR and HGR accuracy, with direct comparisons against established commercial single-function electrodes to ensure assessment relative to current standards. While several studies have proposed either wet or dry electrodes, these have typically been optimized for TES or EMG recording individually, with limited focus on dry electrodes offering dual functionality. By addressing the gap in existing technologies, specifically the lack of dry electrodes capable of supporting both EMG-based hand gesture recognition and effective TES delivery, the key contribution of this study is the demonstration of a dual-function electrode that can reliably record EMG signals for gesture recognition while delivering targeted stimulation, which, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, has not been previously demonstrated. This approach can support the future development of neuromuscular interfaces and enhance usability in wearable applications.

Methods

This study involved the design, fabrication, and experimental evaluation of a dual-function dry electrode capable of both EMG recording and TES delivery. The overall methodology consisted of three main stages: (1) electrode design and fabrication using a gold-plated copper interface, (2) experimental validation of EMG recording performance through SNR analysis and HGR, and (3) evaluation of TES delivery effectiveness through stimulation threshold and induced torque measurements. Comparative analyses were conducted against commercial single-function electrodes to benchmark performance and assess the feasibility of the proposed integrated approach.

Electrode design

Multipurpose 110 copper sheets, 0.508 mm thick, were cut into 10 mm10 mm square electrodes, each with one copper pin welded onto it, enabling efficient connection to devices for data acquisition. The electrodes underwent gold plating using the Plug N’ Plate Gold solution (Caswell Inc, Palmerston, ON, Canada). A 4.5-volt power supply was employed for the process. The cathode was connected to the electrode that was immersed in the gold solution, while the anode was immersed directly in the same solution. After 20 seconds of bubbling, indicating the deposition of gold, the electrode was removed from the solution. This process was repeated for all electrodes to ensure uniform gold plating. Once the electrodes were crafted, six of them were mounted on each 3D-printed electrode base. These bases were then linked using 3D-printed flexible straps, as illustrated in Fig. 1, #1, to create an armband. The armband was designed, inspired by the commercial Myo hand gesture control armband27, to securely hold multiple electrodes around the forearm and enable stable signal acquisition for implementing the HGR algorithm.

EMG electrode efficacy assessment

To evaluate the DFDE, forty healthy individuals were recruited, encompassing a diverse demographic to ensure the generalizability of the findings. Ethics approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Board at Western University (Project ID: 117923). Prior to participation, all individuals provided informed consent and were briefed on the study’s procedures and goals. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations approved by the Human Research Ethics Board. Participants were instructed to wear the DFDE armband (as shown in Fig. 1) and received instructions to perform six different hand gestures, illustrated in Fig. 2, with a moderate, consistent force level, without any imposed restrictions on exerted force. Prior to armband placement, the skin was prepared using an alcohol wipe to minimize factors such as sweating that could potentially affect signal transmission. The DFDE armband consists of eight sections, each containing six electrodes. In this research, a data acquisition device, Intronix 2024F Isolated Amplifier (Intronix Technologies Corporation, Bolton, ON, Canada, Fig. 1, #2), with a sampling frequency of 4 kHz consisting of 16 channels was used. Therefore, due to the device’s channel limitations, only two electrodes from each section of the DFDE armband were used: the outer electrodes on one side of each section (highlighted in Fig. 1, #1). Ten consecutive repetitions of each gesture were completed, with each repetition lasting five seconds and a three-second rest interval between repetitions. This protocol was carried out with participants’ elbows flexed to a 90-degree angle. Following the completion of the six gestures, the DFDE armband was substituted with a Myo Armband (Fig. 1, #3), and participants were required to repeat the six gestures. To maintain consistency and minimize variability in the EMG readings during the data collection phase, the fourth sensor of the Myo Armband and the fourth section of the DFDE armband were positioned above the Extensor Carpi Ulnaris muscle, guided by palpation techniques.

Predictions from these binary classifiers on the training data are stacked to create additional feature vectors. A secondary learner then processes the training data through a series of convolutional and pooling layers to extract high-level features, which are subsequently flattened into a one-dimensional vector. The CNN-derived feature vector and the additional feature vector are concatenated to form a unified representation, which is then input into fully connected layers for final classification. Targeted hand gesture classes are wrist flexion (WF), wrist extension (WE), wrist abduction (WAb), wrist adduction (WAd), hand fist (HF), and hand opening (HO). The figure on the bottom shows a sample schematic illustration of a fundamental CNN. First, the raw EMG signals underwent processing, their image shapes were merged, and finally fed into the CNN network. The CNN layers are categorized into two components: feature extraction and classification. First, a convolutional layer uses several learning kernels and shared weights to convolve the input image. The pooling layer then minimizes the image size while preserving the information it contains. The retrieved features are then combined in the fully connected layers. Finally, the output layer provides one output neuron for each class of objects.

A HGR algorithm was developed using the Stacked One-vs-One (SOvO) method, as proposed in28. SOvO converts a multi-class classification problem into multiple binary classification tasks, akin to the One-vs-One (OvO) strategy. Unlike OvO, which relies on majority voting of predictions to decide the final class, SOvO stacks the predictions from the binary classifiers on the training data into new feature vectors. These feature vectors are subsequently employed in training a secondary classifier to generate the final prediction. As depicted in Fig. 2, the data were initially divided into two groups: training and testing, comprising 80% and 20% of the total data, respectively. Fifteen binary convolutional neural network (CNN) classifiers were considered as base learners and trained on different pairs of the dataset; with N classes, there are a total of different combinations for binary classification. Afterward, the predictions from all 15 binary classifiers were stacked and considered as new feature vectors for a secondary learner, which was a CNN classifier. The following subsections detail the steps involved in implementing the HGR algorithm.

Identifying the onset of muscle activity is crucial for hand gesture intention detection, as it allows for the precise identification of the beginning of a specific movement30–32. Consequently, training the classifier with a window of EMG signal following the muscle activity onset is typically more effective. More specifically, training on multiple windows that might include portions of the signal without the initiation can cause the classifier to learn irrelevant or confusing patterns, thereby decreasing the accuracy of hand gesture intention detection algorithms. Therefore, selecting an appropriate signal window following muscle activity onset is vital. The following paragraphs detail the steps involved in processing EMG signals for this purpose.

Initially, the DFDE raw EMG signals were downsampled from 4 kHz to 1 kHz to reduce data size and computational load, while preserving the essential EMG frequency content. This downsampling is justified based on the Nyquist theorem, as the dominant EMG signal components are typically below 500 Hz. The signals were then filtered using a bandpass filter to remove low and high frequency noise. Specifically, a high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 10 Hz and a low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 400 Hz were employed. To prevent the filtered EMG signals from averaging to zero, rectification was applied, converting negative oscillations into positive values. To detect onsets, or sudden increases in muscle activation levels, the rectified EMG signals were smoothed by calculating the RMS value of the signal within a sliding window of 100 samples. The onset point in the energy signal obtained with Teager-Kaiser energy operator (TKEO)33 was identified as the point where the most significant increase in the RMS of the signal occurred. Spectrum generation

To effectively input the processed EMG signals into the CNN classifier, the signals were transformed into images. This transformation leverages the CNN’s strength in image recognition and tasks that involve pixel data processing. The one-dimensional arrays of processed EMG signals were reshaped into 2-dimensional arrays with dimensions of 654. The images from the eight channels were then concatenated to create a single image with dimensions of 4854. These images represented a time-domain view of the EMG signal, where each element in the 2-dimensional arrays corresponded to the color of a square in the image. Since the array values ranged from 0 to 1, the resulting images were in grayscale.

Secondary learner

The predictions from each trained binary CNN classifier on the training set were obtained. Following the procedure outlined in the SOvO method28, these predictions were stacked and used as new feature vectors for the secondary learner. The hyperparameters considered for the secondary model were the same as those mentioned in Table 2, with the following differences: the output layer had six units with a Softmax activation function, and the loss function was categorical cross-entropy, both of which are suitable for a multi-class problem. Along with these hyperparameters, the secondary model also included extra feature vectors, as outlined in the SOvO method procedure. These vectors were derived from the stacked predictions of 15 binary base learners. Each vector is a one-dimensional array of size 30, representing the probability values generated by 15 base learners. Training the secondary model begins with using the training data to extract high-level features, which are then flattened into one-dimensional vectors. These CNN-derived feature vectors are concatenated with the additional feature vectors derived from the stacked predictions of base learners to form a unified representation. This combined representation is then fed into fully connected layers for final classification. To evaluate the performance of the secondary learner, the training data underwent five-fold cross-validation. In this process, the data were divided into five equal subsets. For each fold, four subsets were used for training, and the remaining subset served as the validation set. This procedure was repeated five times, ensuring that each subset was used as the validation set once. The performance of the CNN classifier was assessed on each validation set, and the model with the highest average performance across all folds was selected. The accuracy on the test set was then calculated using the Eq. (3).

Electrode efficiency in TES delivery

The sensory and motor thresholds were measured in this study to ensure that the TES electrode administers stimuli that are both effective and within a safe and comfortable range for the user. Participants sat on a chair positioned in front of a height-adjustable table, with their arm resting on an arm support, as depicted in Fig. 3. Two types of electrodes were used on two distinct muscles: two sections of the DFDE band (#2) and a pair of commercially available wet electrodes (Kendall H590P, #8). These electrodes were placed on the muscles responsible for wrist and ring finger flexion.

As shown in Fig. 3, first, two sections of the DFDE band were placed just below the elbow joint (#2). The first section was positioned approximately 8 cm distal to the medial epicondyle, and the second section was placed 1 cm distal to the first section along the longitudinal direction of the flexor digitorum superficialis. These two sections were connected to a transcutaneous electrical stimulator (TENS 210, Mettler Electronics Corp., #1). Before the stimulation procedures, a torque sensor (TRS605-FSH02056, FUTEK, #5) with a sampling frequency of 1 kHz was attached to the participant’s ring finger to measure the induced torque. A consistent series of electrical pulses were delivered to the electrodes. The pulse width was set at 200 s, and the pulse frequency was 50 Hz. The stimulation current increased from 0 mA to the participant’s sensory threshold (ST), motor threshold (MT), and MT+30%, with each value being recorded. The torque signal was recorded onto a PC through a data acquisition card (USB-6002, NI, #6).

The same procedures were done for the wrist flexor muscle. One section of the DFDE armband was positioned approximately 5 cm distal to the medial epicondyle, and the second section was placed 1 cm distal to the first section along the longitudinal direction of the flexor carpi radialis. The torque sensor was attached to the participant’s hand to measure the generated torque. Afterwards, the commercial wet electrodes were tested with the same procedures done for the DFDE for both wrist and ring finger flexion. The order of testing between dry and wet electrodes was randomized to avoid sequence effects. The following section outlines the method used to analyze the collected data.

Data analysis

First, the offset was removed from the torque data, followed by low-pass filtering using a 2nd-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 20 Hz to eliminate high-frequency noise. Subsequently, the data were enveloped within a window of 20 samples, wherein the peak value was extracted for analysis. The ST, MT, and MT+30% data, along with the processed torque data for both DFDE and wet electrodes were analyzed statistically to evaluate pairwise differences between dry and wet electrodes for each target area and stimulation level. Normality of the data distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated deviations from normality; therefore, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was chosen. This test is especially suited for comparing two independent samples when the conditions necessary for parametric tests are not fulfilled35.

EMG signal quality evaluation

SNR analysis

The SNR values of the EMG measurements were calculated using Eq. (1). The comparison between initial and subsequent SNR values was conducted to evaluate any degradation in signal quality and electrode performance over time. For accurate SNR calculation, it is important to analyze EMG measurements from channels that offer a strong signal, minimal baseline noise, and few artifacts. Accordingly, Channels #3 and #7 of the DFDE band were selected as they satisfied the aforementioned criteria. These channels were positioned over the flexor digitorum profundus and extensor digitorum muscles respectively, where placement on muscle tissue rather than bone supports better signal quality. The SNR values of those channels for all participants were calculated for a randomly chosen hand gesture. Figure 4 illustrates the distribution of SNR values recorded from Channel #7 and Channel #3 across a 21-month period. The SNR values for Channel #7 ranged from 6.06 dB to 29.29 dB, while Channel #3 values ranged from 10.42 dB to 29.29 dB. There is no observable downward trend in the SNR values over time, indicating that the signal quality has not degraded, even after extended use. Although a relatively low SNR was recorded for Channel #7 on 2023-11-15, the following day (2023-11-16)—using the same electrode but on a new participant–the SNR value increased substantially. This suggests that the variation is more likely attributable to participant-specific or setup-related factors rather than deterioration of the electrode’s performance. Overall, the consistent SNR range and absence of degradation support the conclusion that the fabricated DFDEs are stable and reliable for long-term EMG acquisition, aligning with the study’s objective of evaluating their durability in repeated applications.

TES delivery

The ST, MT, and MT+30% data, along with the processed torque data for both dry and wet electrodes, are represented visually through box plots (Fig. 11). The figure illustrates the median, interquartile range, and potential outliers for each dataset, providing a clear comparison of the distribution of data for dry and wet electrodes. Outliers indicate instances where the response to electrical stimulation or the resulting induced torque significantly differed from that of the majority of participants. These outliers may result from factors such as challenges with precisely placing the electrodes on the targeted muscle or variations in skin conductance among individuals. All measured data were subjected to statistical analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test. The p value greater than 0.05 from this analysis indicates no significant difference between the dry and wet electrodes across all measured parameters. It should be noted that, while the constant-current stimulator (TENS210) used in this study is designed to maintain the same set current regardless of electrode type, this is achievable only if the electrode–skin impedance remains within the device’s operational voltage range. Based on the results presented in Fig. 11, where no significant differences in current intensity were observed, it can be concluded that, although the dry electrodes likely had higher impedance than the wet electrodes, this impedance was not high enough to exceed the stimulator’s voltage limit. In cases of very high impedance, the stimulator would be unable to maintain the set current, potentially reducing stimulation effectiveness; however, no such issues occurred across the 40 participants. These results indicate that both dry and wet electrodes provided comparable performance in delivering safe and effective stimulation.

Discussion

Previous efforts to integrate TES and sEMG functionalities within a single electrode have been limited and have primarily relied on wet electrodes requiring conductive gel. However, the reliance on gel introduces practical drawbacks, including user discomfort, messy application, drying over time, and inconsistent signal quality. Most existing dry electrodes are optimized for either signal acquisition or stimulation individually, and their evaluation typically relies on signal quality metrics such as impedance or SNR rather than task-driven, closed-loop rehabilitation scenarios. In contrast, the present study focuses on a dual-function dry electrode intended to support both EMG-based hand gesture recognition and effective TES delivery at the same anatomical location. By validating performance using both functional classification accuracy and physiological stimulation thresholds, and by directly comparing against commercial EMG and TES electrodes, this work extends prior dry electrode studies toward practical neuromuscular interface applications where sensing and stimulation must operate together within a wearable system. This study evaluates a dual-function dry electrode with a gold-plated copper interface, demonstrating its capability to provide reliable EMG signal acquisition and TES delivery without the need for conductive gel. The proposed electrode was validated with forty participants, showing no degradation in SNR, achieving gesture recognition accuracy comparable to the commercial Myo armband, and producing stimulation outcomes equivalent to those obtained with commercial TES electrodes. These findings highlight that dry electrodes can overcome the usability limitations of wet electrodes while preserving clinical effectiveness, thereby offering a more practical and scalable solution for integrated rehabilitation applications.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. In this study, EMG measurement and TES delivery were performed one at a time manually by the experimenter, which helped avoid interference between the signals. When EMG and TES are applied simultaneously at the same site, stimulation artifacts can interfere with EMG signals, complicating accurate measurement. Mitigating stimulation artifacts typically can be done with hardware or software solutions. Hardware techniques include the use of differential amplifiers with high common-mode rejection ratios and careful electrode placement to separate stimulation and recording sites36–39. Software-based approaches include blanking intervals40, in which sEMG signals are ignored for a brief period following each stimulation pulse, as well as artifact suppression techniques such as template subtraction41,42, adaptive filtering43, and interpolation44. Another approach is sequential (phased) operation, in which EMG recording and TES delivery alternate in brief time intervals, such as through time-division multiplexing (TDM)45, to prevent interference while maintaining functional responsiveness. As future work, the system will be modified, as illustrated in Fig. 12, to enable sequential sensing and stimulation without requiring manual intervention and to minimize stimulation artifacts. In this configuration, EMG acquisition would be temporarily disabled during each stimulation pulse (i.e., through automatic blanking intervals) so that EMG signals are ignored while TES is active. Such a system could be applied in post-stroke hand rehabilitation, where the armband detects the user’s intended hand gestures and delivers TES to assist the corresponding muscles in executing the movement. By providing real-time stimulation based on detected motor intent, the device could facilitate motor relearning and improve functional hand use, while continuously monitoring EMG activity to adjust stimulation parameters automatically.

Schematic representation of the proposed armband system, designed as a future extension for sequential (phased) sensing and stimulation while reducing stimulation-related artifacts. Two DFDE armbands are positioned next to each other. Electrodes marked with blue dots are connected to the EMG acquisition device, while those with red dots are connected to the TES stimulator. During TES delivery, EMG acquisition would be disabled, and during EMG acquisition, stimulation would be paused, enabling alternating sensing and stimulation modes for intention-driven neuromuscular rehabilitation. Unconnected electrodes are included to allow flexibility in targeting different muscle groups as needed.

Another factor that can influence EMG signal quality, particularly for dry electrodes, is susceptibility to 50–60 Hz power-line interference. This noise arises from ambient electromagnetic fields and can couple more strongly when electrode–skin impedance is high. In the present study, the electrodes were mounted on an armband that ensured stable fixation, and a ground electrode placed near the elbow further minimized ambient electrical interference. Future work could explore additional hardware and software filtering strategies to suppress residual noise under more variable recording conditions.

In addition, the evaluation focused on a limited range of hand gestures and controlled laboratory conditions. Real-world applications may involve greater movement variability, external noise, or skin conditions that could affect performance. Future work should include clinical validation across a broader range of users and movement tasks.

Moreover, while SNR measurements over 21 months suggest that the electrodes are durable and maintain consistent signal quality, these results were influenced by inter-subject variability. Future work should include longitudinal monitoring of SNR in the same participants to isolate electrode performance more effectively.

For electrode systems intended for both sensing and stimulation, key electrical and electrochemical properties at the electrode–skin interface, such as electrode–skin impedance, polarization behavior, and charge delivery capacity, play critical roles in determining performance and safety. Low impedance is important for reducing thermal and motion-related noise in EMG recordings, while sufficient charge transfer capability is required to deliver stimulation currents without excessive polarization or voltage saturation at the interface. In the present study, these properties were not measured directly; instead, electrode performance was evaluated using functional metrics, including EMG SNR, gesture recognition accuracy, and stimulation thresholds. The comparable EMG classification performance relative to commercial electrodes indicates that the effective electrode–skin impedance and noise characteristics were sufficient for reliable signal acquisition, while the absence of significant differences in stimulation current and induced torque indicates that charge delivery was not limited by interface impedance within the operating range of the stimulator. Nevertheless, future work should include direct electrochemical characterization, such as impedance and charge injection capacity measurements, to fully quantify interface behavior and to support optimization of electrode geometry and surface treatments for long-term applications.

Furthermore, one potential limitation of using either dry or wet electrodes is their susceptibility to compromising signal reliability during arm movements. In this study, all EMG measurements were collected during a static arm posture, whereas in real-time applications, dynamic arm movements are prevalent. To be more specific, changes in arm posture can alter the shape and position of underlying muscles, potentially causing electrodes to shift with the skin. To tackle this challenge, future work will involve integrating a previously established approach46 designed to reduce the impact of arm posture on EMG pattern recognition.

Another limitation is the sensitivity of dry electrodes to skin moisture, and the DFDE is similarly affected by this limitation. In this study, the skin was cleaned with alcohol before electrode placement. However, if alcohol is not used, factors such as sweating can potentially affect signal transmission. Future work could focus on exploring more robust electrode materials or designs that are less sensitive to skin moisture, such as incorporating materials with better moisture resistance. Furthermore, improving real-time calibration methods to account for moisture-related variations during data collection could be a key focus.

In addition, the DFDE armband uses flexible straps to secure the device around the arm. However, this method may not be sufficient as the straps might not fully maintain electrode contact with the skin, leading to potential displacement during movement. Future research will focus on developing more robust fixation methods, such as innovative textile designs that provide both flexibility and secure attachment to the skin.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the feasibility of a dual-function dry electrode capable of both EMG signal acquisition and TES. The proposed electrode achieved reliable EMG recordings, enabling hand gesture recognition accuracy comparable to a commercial Myo armband, while delivering TES with outcomes equivalent to standard electrodes. These results highlight the potential of integrating sensing and stimulation within a single dry electrode, offering practical advantages such as enhanced user comfort, simplified system design, and reduced cost. In the current study, EMG recording and TES were applied separately to avoid interference; future work will enable simultaneous sensing and stimulation using automated blanking intervals, supporting real-time, intention-driven neuromuscular rehabilitation. Additionally, further work is needed to validate performance under dynamic movements, varying skin conditions, and extended clinical scenarios. Overall, the findings support the potential of dual-function dry electrodes as a scalable and clinically relevant solution for integrated neuromuscular monitoring and therapy, paving the way for more effective and user-friendly rehabilitation technologies.
