Population-based estimates of healthy working life expectancy in China
Authors: Guogui Huang, Yao Pan, Yanan Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite growing interest in population aging and extended working lives, little is known in developing countries about people’s quality of working life and their capacity to work for longer years when approaching retirement age. This study therefore aimed to estimate the healthy working life expectancy for the middle-aged and older population in China.MethodsWe used the data of 7294 adults aged 60 years and older from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) 2011–2018. Healthy working life expectancy was estimated for the Chinese population aged 60 years and older overall and analyzed by four dimensions: sex, educational attainment, occupational type, and geographic location, using the population-based multistate life table method.ResultsAdults in China can expect 21.60 years (95% CI 21.38–21.82) of remaining lifetime at age 60, of which 7.64 years (95% CI 7.52–7.76) were in healthy working status and 10.04 years (95% CI 9.86–10.21) were in healthy but not working status. Females had a shorter healthy working life expectancy (6.40 years [95% CI 6.26–6.53]) than males (8.90 years [95% CI 8.72–9.05]) at age 60, despite their longer total life expectancy at that age. Geographically, the highest healthy working life expectancy was observed in the Southwest Zone (7.95 years [95% CI 7.72–8.18]) and the lowest in the Northeast Zone (5.15 years [95% CI 4.87–5.42]).ConclusionThe healthy working life expectancy at 60 years old in China exceeds the targeted retirement age of 65. Salient socioeconomic and geographic variations of healthy working life expectancy exist in China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06262-4.Keywords: Healthy working life expectancy, China, Later life

Introduction

Populations are aging rapidly worldwide, positing profound socioeconomic challenges from shrinking workforce, growing demand for healthcare services, and decreasing financial sustainability of pension system. Many countries have designed and implemented a broad range of policy reforms in work, healthcare, and retirement policies to tackle the challenges arising from population aging. A key component of these policy responses is to increase the statutory retirement age [1]. For instance, the pension reform in the United Kingdom has increased the statutory retirement age of women from 60 to 66 years old since 2010 [2]. Similar policy interventions are implemented in the United States, Canada, and many European countries [3].

The most recent national census data from China indicates that the working-age population stands at 880 million, which the working-age group faces considerable health challenges. Between 2007 and 2016, the proportion of all-cause mortality and chronic non-communicable diseases within the labor force has risen each year, with an average annual increase of 0.8% (95% CI 0.7%-0.9%)[4]. Furthermore, research reveals that health behaviors and health status among the labor force vary based on different subgroups. For instance, as educational attainment increases, health behaviors among the working-age population improve, and the age-standardized mortality rates for major chronic diseases (such as COPD, malignant tumors, cerebrovascular diseases, and cardiovascular diseases) are higher in male workers compared to their female counterparts [4, 5].

The successful policy implementation of postponing retirement age requires a substantial proportion of population with working capacity and productivity [6]. Good health is one of the main drivers to maintain, and a key dimension to reflect, people’s working capacity and productivity [7, 8]. Poor health conditions would lead to labor capacity reduction and a lowered willingness to enter the labor market, resulting in absenteeism, early retirement, and inadequate employment [9]. For example, a previous study indicated that, in China, workers lose up to 9 days of work per year due to illness, with 53% of workers experiencing reduced work efficiency due to health issues [10]. Without improvement in the health and wellbeing of the general population, it is infeasible and impractical to motivate people to stay in workforce after retirement.

To measure the experience and the quality of working life, a series of indicators have been applied in the previous literature, such as employment rate, working life expectancy, healthy life expectancy, and years of working life loss due to specific events/diseases [11–14]. However, these indicators generally gauge individuals’ health conditions and work experience separately, failing to consider the two dimensions holistically [15]. An emerging indicator, healthy working life expectancy, has hence been used in recent studies to compensate for the drawbacks of prior measures [16–19]. This indicator is defined as the number of years that a person would spend in both healthy and working status in remaining life, combining information of mortality, morbidity, and labor participation [20].

A systematic review indicated that healthy working life expectancy better captures the length and quality of working life than previous indicators and has the potential to be a suitable measure for monitoring life expectancy in health and work [20]. Healthy working life expectancy takes into account not just the number of years a person is expected to live, but also the quality of those years in terms of physical and mental health. By focusing on the health aspect of working life expectancy, it provides a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of an individual's productive years and overall well-being. Moreover, healthy working life expectancy can serve as a more holistic measure of workforce sustainability and productivity. It considers factors such as workplace safety, job satisfaction, work-life balance, and access to healthcare, which are crucial for maintaining a healthy and productive workforce. By prioritizing health in the context of work, policies and interventions can be tailored to promote a healthier and more sustainable work environment, leading to benefits for both individuals and society as a whole. Therefore, emphasizing healthy working life expectancy as a key indicator can help guide policymakers, employers, and individuals towards strategies that not only extend the length of working life but also enhance its quality, ultimately contributing to a more resilient and thriving workforce.

A handful of studies have estimated healthy working life expectancy mainly in Europe, demonstrating that healthy working life expectancy varies across subpopulations, generally higher for males, better-educated individuals, and those taking non-manual occupations or living in economically advanced regions [18]. Additionally, countries with a higher employment level of older adults generally witness a longer healthy working life expectancy [16]. However, existing research on healthy working life expectancy is mainly Europe-based so far [16–19].

China is experiencing an unprecedented process of population aging, with the number of its residents aged 65 years old and above projected to reach a stunning 400 million by 2050 [21, 22]. To address the challenges accompanied with population aging, particularly on its immature healthcare and pension systems, China is also seeking to increase its statutory retirement age, from age 60 to age 65 in the coming years. However, little is known about people’s working capacity in later working life in China. By using data from a nationally representative dataset, this study fills this gap by estimating the healthy working life expectancy for the population aged 60 years and older in China.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data in this study were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) in 2011–2018. Using the multistage probability sampling method, CHALRS surveyed more than 17,000 participants aged 45 years and older from 450 villages/resident committees, 150 counties/districts, and 28 provinces in China, providing a broad range of information at both individual level (e.g., demographic characteristics, family status, health level, and health care utilization) and community level (e.g., population size and economic level of community). All information was collected by training staff through face-to-face computer-aided personal interviews. CHARLS was conducted every 2 years with four waves in 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018, respectively. The details of CHARLS can be found at http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en.

A longitudinal dataset based on the four waves of CHALRS was used in this study. Specifically, a total of 17,708 individuals were interviewed at baseline (Wave 1, 2011), and 15,186 individuals were reinterviewed in Wave 2 (in 2013). During Wave 3 (in 2015), 13,565 individuals were followed up, and 11,988 respondents were reinterviewed in Wave 4 (in 2018). Although some participants failed to be followed up in certain waves, they were included in the analysis given that missing information of status can be imputed by the interpolated Markov Chain (IMaCh) software used in this study. This also helps maintain sample representativeness, keeping the data attrition rate to less than 2%. After excluding participants aged under at baseline 60 (n = 10,241), without complete information on health status at baseline (n = 97), demographic characteristics (n = 46), and mortality (n = 30), a total of 7,307 individuals were included in the final sample.

Measures

Health status

We used disability status to measure health. In CHALRS, disability was measured by the basic activities of daily living (ADL) scale [23], including six survey items to measure participants’ ability of eating, dressing, bathing, using the toilet, getting in or out of bed, and controlling urination and defecation. The questions were based on four answers: ‘do not have any difficulty,’ ‘have difficulty but can still do it,’ ‘have difficulty and need help,’ and ‘cannot do it.’ Participants requiring no assistance in performing all ADL activities were considered ‘healthy’ otherwise ‘unhealthy’.

Disability is used to measure the status of health in this study, given that compared with other health indicators, such as non-communicable diseases, disability status can better assess the condition of a person regarding work-related limitations and labor participation restrictions. Disability can also reflect whether a person is of inability to fully participate in social activity or interact with their living/working environments, which is vital for individuals’ overall wellbeing and is closely related to working capacity [24, 25].

Work states

The status of work was measured by the self-reported working status in each wave. Being in work was defined as individuals who participated in paid work or self-employment during the year prior to the interview.

Mortality

The information of mortality was obtained by the gathering information through follow-up surveys with surviving family members and official death records.

Identifications of population subgroups

This study included subgroups by sex, educational attainment, occupational type, and geographic location. Sex was a dummy (male/female), while educational attainment was divided into three groups considering complete or uncomplete levels on each category (illiteracy, primary school, junior high school and above). Type of occupation included manual, non-manual and self-employed jobs. Two classification methods were used to examine healthy working life expectancy by geographic location. The first classification method classified Chinese provinces into eight economic zones (i.e., Northeast Zone, Northern Coastal Areas, South Coastal Areas, East Coastal Areas, Middle Reaches of the Yellow River, Middle Reaches of the Yangtze River, Southwest Zone, and Northwest Zone) based on the standards of the Development Research Center of the State Council in China. Due to the similar economic development levels of Eastern Coastal Area and Southern Coastal Area, we combined these two areas into Southeast Coastal Areas. The second classification method was to divide geographic locations into four categories: the first/second-tier cities, the first/second-tier city suburbs, the third/lower tier cities, and the third/lower tier city suburbs according to the degree of urbanicity. This was conducted based on the hierarchical classification standard released by the China Broadcast Network, which assesses the commercial attraction of 338 Chinese cities primarily based on the concentration of commercial resources, pivot ability, and citizen vitality.

This method considers the transition from the initial status to other statuses between two waves of a longitudinal survey, a setting proven to be more realistic than those without considering such status transition [26]. Ethical approval

Ethics approval for CHARLS was issued by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015) and University of Newcastle Human Research Ethics Committee (H-2015-0290). Each respondent signed the informed consent form before the data collection.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically assess healthy working life of Chinese older population using nationally representative population-based data. We found that adults in China are expected to spend 7.64 years in healthy working status from 60 years old. The identified healthy working life expectancy beyond age 60 exceeds China’s targeted retirement age of 65, suggesting potential for later retirement. This should encourage policymakers to better align health and employment policies to support extended working lives.

We also observed 10.04 years of life expectancy staying healthy but not working for the Chinese population at age 60. Specifically, this is 12.09 years for females and 8.10 years for males. This implies somewhat inadequate utilization of human capital of China’s workforce. This may be due to the lack of suitable job opportunities for middle-aged and older adults and an age-unfriendly workplace environment in China that discourages labor participation in old age. In addition, the policy of early retirement for employees of government sections and state-run enterprises, which allows people to depart from the labor market at an age prior to the official retirement age, might also play a role. In China, the current retirement policy states that women working as laborers retire at 50, while men in the same occupation retire at 60. Women in non-labor occupations retire at 55, while men retire at 60. Additionally, individuals may retire before the legally mandated retirement age due to illness or other special reasons. With 7.64 years of healthy non-working life expectancy observed in our study, it suggests that, on average, the 60-year-old individuals in our research could potentially delay their retirement age. Our findings also partially support the benefits of raising the retirement age to address the socio-economic issues stemming from population aging.

Our research has highlighted the crucial role of health in the expected working life. Health and factors related to health, such as poor working conditions, can impact an individual's healthy working life expectancy. A person's health directly determines their ability to continue working. If someone suffers from serious or chronic illnesses, they may be unable to work or experience a significant decrease in work efficiency. Additionally, other factors like working environment conditions also affect healthy working life expectancy through their influence on health. Poor working conditions may result in a harsh work environment with a lack of necessary safety measures and protections, increasing the risk of work-related injuries and illnesses. Prolonged exposure to such unfavorable working conditions can have a detrimental effect on health, shortening healthy working life expectancy.

This study also demonstrated that Chinese females had a significantly shorter healthy working life expectancy than males, despite their longer life expectancy. This aligns to those observed mainly in European countries [16, 18]. Three reasons might explain this salient sex difference in healthy working life expectancy in China. First, unlike Western societies generally with similar statutory retirement ages for both genders, the statutory retirement age in China was higher for males than for females, which means males are required to stay longer in the workforce, and hence, have an overall longer working life expectancy. Second, female labor participation rate is constrained due to gender stereotyping in the family role in China [29, 30]. And the traditional values and norms negatively affect women’s income, educational opportunities, and nutrition during their life. Under the traditional intrahousehold labor division, Chinese females need to allocate significantly more time on housework and childcare responsibilities than males [31]. For older females, they are also expected to take the responsibility to care for their grandchildren given Chinese traditional cultural requirement and also limited availability of public childcaring services in China. This might further reduce female intention to stay in workforce when approaching retirement age. Third, due to lower female mortality, unhealthy females are more likely to survive in old age and experience unhealthy conditions during aging process [32, 33]. In China, female health disadvantage in middle and later life might be further exacerbated by the traditional male preference, which sees females in disadvantaged status in terms of income distribution, educational opportunity, and nutrition provision, which jeopardizes female health and wellbeing [34].

Our study also indicated prominent socioeconomic inequalities of healthy working life expectancy in China, with individuals having higher educational attainment and non-manual occupation experiencing a relatively lower healthy working life expectancy. This finding is opposite to the evidence reported in the United Kingdom, which reported higher healthy working life outcomes for individuals with high education and non-manual occupations [27]. Such discrepancies may be due to the unique profile of employment policy and social security systems in China. Specifically, in China, higher-educated individuals are more likely to be employed in non-manual jobs, particularly in state-owned enterprises and government sectors, which ensures them to access pension support after retirement. They might therefore lack incentives to re-enter the labor market after retirement, even in good health status. This may result in their relatively short healthy working life expectancy but relatively longer life expectancy in good health but not in work. In contrast, individuals with low educational level in China are more likely to take up manual occupations, which are less likely to be covered by current China’s pension system and have a greater likelihood of working longer for additional income after retirement age. This might result in both longer healthy working life expectancy and life expectancy in unhealthy but working status for this subpopulation.

Furthermore, the results reflected apparent geographic disparities in healthy working life expectancy, which may be due to China’s regional inequalities in economic development level and industrial structure. The Northeast Zone in China is a heavy manufacturing industrial region but has suffered from a severe economic downturn since the 1990s, featured by a long-term low income level and employment rate, which might prevent people in the Northeast Zone from obtaining suitable jobs and achieving adequate employment, and hence, lead to low healthy working life expectancy in this region [35]. In contrast, southeastern coastal areas and northern coastal areas are the economic engines of China, with greater economic prosperity in the past few decades and relatively advanced healthcare and social welfare systems compared with other regions. Therefore, people in coastal regions are likely to sufficiently participate in the labor market and are less likely to re-enter the job market after retirement age given their pension support. This may lead to their relatively longer life expectancy in health and not in work compared with other regions [36]. Moreover, the Middle Reaches of the Yangtze River, with the highest healthy working life expectancy in China, has also experienced a rapid economic growth since the new millennium, which contributes to a viable labor market in this region. Favorable climatic conditions and abundant water resources have enabled the development of a thriving agricultural system in this area have [37]; therefore, many rural residents of this area may choose to continue their agricultural work in old age given the yet-well-established pension system in China. The same reason might also explain the difference in healthy working life expectancy by urbanicity with the longer healthy working life for residents living in suburban areas, which is possibly caused by the limited coverage of the social welfare system for this subpopulation.

Limitations

Although this is the first study to estimate healthy working life expectancy of older population in China, it still has several limitations. First, only community-dwelling older adults were included in our analysis, while the population living in institutions was excluded. Consequently, total life expectancy and healthy working life expectancy may be overestimated in this study because of the generally worse health status for the institutionalized older adults. However, this bias should be minor given the extremely low proportion of Chinese older adults receiving institutional care. Second, this study used ADL as a health measure when estimating healthy working life expectancy. Although the ADL-based health definition captures the inability of activity limitations and participation restrictions, it neglects other health dimensions, such as psychological wellbeing, which also critically affects working performance. Future work should consider more health dimensions when estimating healthy working life expectancy. Third, as some studies may use the Global Activity Limitation Index (GALI) to measure functional ability, while our study uses ADL, this may have a certain impact on the comparability of the research results. It should be further verified in the future by selecting indicators such as GALI to measure health and disability for the accuracy of our study. Fourth, the estimation of healthy working life expectancy in this study is based on a longitudinal dataset collected every two years. Therefore, the status transition in our estimation only reflects the changes in health conditions in a two-year period but is unable to capture the status transitions within this two-year period (e.g., monthly changes). This might compromise the estimation accuracy and needs to be addressed with available data in the future. Fifth, while CHARLS utilizes a stringent methodology to determine mortality by gathering information through follow-up surveys with surviving family members and official death records, thereby enhancing the reliability and accuracy of mortality status capture, it is still unable to entirely eliminate certain estimation biases resulting from self-reports.

Conclusions

In China, healthy working life expectancy at 60 years old is above the targeted retirement age of 65. Pronounced socioeconomic and geographic inequalities of healthy working life expectancy exist. With an aging population, targeted measures are needed to empower people’s capacity and enhance their willingness to work for longer in China and elsewhere. These measures might include promoting healthy aging, providing tailed jobs to seek assistance for middle-aged or older adults, and cultivating age-friendly employment environments. These policy interventions are suggestive to focus on females and residents of economically disadvantaged regions, and would be more efficient if supported by efforts to strengthen social security safety and to narrow regional/urban–rural development inequality. Furthermore, to increase healthy working life expectancy, policy recommendations can be implemented by improving poor working conditions. These policies may include strengthening the enforcement of labor protection regulations to ensure workplaces provide good safety facilities and health conditions. Additionally, promoting the implementation of health promotion programs in companies to encourage employee participation in activities promoting healthy lifestyles, thereby reducing the incidence of chronic diseases.
