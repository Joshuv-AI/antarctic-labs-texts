Obesity and Osteoporotic Fractures: Solving the Paradox
Authors: Khushboo Agarwal, Shinjan Patra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a global health issue linked to conditions like metabolic syndrome and heart disease, but its relationship with fracture risk is complex. While higher body mass index (BMI) is associated with increased bone mineral density (BMD) due to mechanical loading, it does not uniformly protect against fractures. Obese individuals face higher risks of proximal humerus and ankle fractures, along with propensity for increased falls and sarcopenic obesity. Higher fat mass, particularly visceral adiposity, correlates with lower bone quality and increased fracture risk, independent of BMD. Obesity triggers inflammatory pathways (e.g., TNF-α, Monocyte Chemotactic Protein-1 [MCP-1]) and adipokine imbalances (e.g., adiponectin, leptin), impairing bone formation and resorption. Vitamin D deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism further exacerbate cortical bone loss. Diagnostic challenges arise as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and FRAX tools may underestimate fracture risk in obesity. Preventive strategies emphasize lifestyle interventions, including moderate weight loss targeting subcutaneous fat, resistance training, and optimal dairy intake to enhance BMD.

INTRODUCTION

Obesity represents a major global health challenge due to its links with conditions such as metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease. Body weight is widely recognized as a key determinant of bone mass in both men and women. Numerous large-scale studies have shown that higher body weight or body mass index (BMI) is associated with higher bone mineral density (BMD) and a lower likelihood of fragility fractures. In contrast, low BMI (below 18.5 kg/m²) is a well-known risk factor for reduced bone density and increased fracture risk.[1] The common understanding of this connection is that increased body mass places greater mechanical stress on bones, leading to enhanced BMD to support the additional load.[2]

Obesity’s relationship with fracture risk is complex and varies by skeletal site.[3] Obese individuals, both male and female, exhibit a higher likelihood of falls and diminished physical capabilities. People with obesity who sustain fragility fractures often have concurrent sarcopenic obesity that is associated with multiple health conditions, such as asthma, emphysema, and type 2 diabetes.[4] Although increased soft tissue may cushion bones during a fall, this advantage may be offset by weaker protective reflexes and greater impact forces due to elevated body weight. Additionally, reduced muscle strength and impaired mobility in obese individuals may further elevate the risk of falling.[5] The review explores the intriguing association of fragility fractures in people with obesity.

METHODOLOGY

We tried to summarize our findings by including all original articles that have investigated the fracture risks among obese patients. A detailed electronic database search of Medline (via PubMed), Embase (via Ovid SP), the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Google Scholar was conducted using a Boolean search strategy. We retrieved the following results by using the keywords ‘Fragility fractures’, ‘Obesity’, ‘Osteoporosis’ and ‘Anti-Osteoporotic therapy’.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

In a large retrospective analysis of the database from Spain of 832,775 women found that women with obesity who sustained a hip or spine fracture are younger than women with normal/underweight BMI with similar fractures.[6] They were also at higher risk for proximal humerus fracture (RR 1.28 [95% CI: 1.04–1.58], P = 0.018). The Global Longitudinal Study of Osteoporosis in Women Study with 44. Five hundred and thirty four women reported an increased risk of ankle and upper leg fractures in women with obesity, along with a higher risk of early menopause and more falls.[4] In a meta-analysis of 398,610 women, there was an increased risk of osteoporotic fracture at BMI >35 kg/m2 (HR, 1.16; 95% CI: 1.09–1.23) when adjusted for BMD.[7] Premaor et al.[8] showed that their Fracture Liaison Service had reported 27.7% fractures occurring in women with a BMI >30 kg/m2, with a higher proportion of hip fractures occurring in women with obesity. Using the data from the Women’s Health Initiative, it was shown that women with higher BMI had more fractures, falls, and less physical activity, with hip fractures trending upwards with BMI.[9] In a community-based study from China involving 7,137 men; 4,585 premenopausal women; and 2,248 postmenopausal women; self-reported fractures; osteoporosis; and low bone mass were higher among subjects with higher body fat percentage after adjusting for age, body weight, gender, menopause, and physical activity.[10] In a prospective study with 54,934 participants, it was found that women with higher waist circumference had a higher risk of vertebral fracture (HR 1.76 95% CI: 1.06–2.92; P = 0.01) which, when adjusted for BMI increased to 2.49 (95% CI: 1.44–4.33; P < 0.001).[11] A similar trend was seen in hip fractures with a meta-analysis involving 180,600 women, which showed increased risk of hip fracture with increasing waist-hip ratio [RR 1.58 (95% CI: 1.20–2.08) P < 0.001]. In the CartaGENE cohort, BMI was associated with the risk of distal lower limb fractures (P = 0.018), and the risk of fracture increased by 11% in people with a BMI >30 kg/m2 compared to <28 kg/m2.[12]

In a cross-sectional study, post-menopausal women having a BMI >35 kg/m2 had a lower femoral BMD (0.723 versus 0.762 g/cm2, P-value = 0.002) and TBS (1.244 vs 1.228, P < 0.013) compared to age-matched controls with normal BMI.[13] In a cross-sectional study from Italy, higher BMI was associated with the number of vertebral fractures (OR = 1.045; P = 0.016; 95% CI: 1.008–1.084)] after correcting for confounding factors like smoking, early menopause and positive family history.[14] Similar trends have also been shown for men. In the Osteoporotic Fractures in Men Study (MrOS), there was an increased risk for non-spine fractures in men with obesity class 2 [HR 1.94 (95% CI: 1.25–3.02)], which were additionally influenced by mobility limitations and walking pace.[15] In elderly men, a study using quantitative CT found that greater psoas muscle volume reduced the risk of vertebral fractures by 28%. In contrast, higher intramuscular fat in abdominal muscles increased fracture risk, independent of BMD.[16] Table 1. summarizes the findings.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

The principle of Wolff’s law has been described in various epidemiological papers related to the risks of fragility fractures and obesity. Obesity can trigger a cascade of transduction signals that affect the osteoblastic activity and the Wnt/catenin signalling pathway.[17] It is said to increase the bone quantity only and compromise bone quality. It is also postulated that the bone’s ability to adapt to mechanical overload is not maintained beyond an inevitable weight gain.[18]

Patients with obesity tend to fall backward or sideways that would explain the lower risk of wrist and the higher risk of humerus fracture shown in various studies.[17] A greater amount of subcutaneous adipose tissue in the hips in gynoid-type obesities helps prevent falls and sustaining fractures. Obesity can be associated with increased sex steroid production from adipocytes that are known for their anabolic and anti-resorptive actions. Estrogen induces osteoclast apoptosis and inhibits osteoclast formation. Androgens are converted to estrogens by aromatization in subcutaneous adipose tissue, which is found in postmenopausal obese women, and is expected to exert a positive effect on bone mass and mineralization.[19] This aromatization is primarily observed in subcutaneous adipose tissue, rather than in visceral adipose tissue.

Obesity is correlated with vitamin D deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism that can contribute to increased risk of osteoporosis and fractures. Secondary hyperparathyroidism typically affects cortical bone more than trabecular bone. Interestingly, obese patients were found to harbour fragility fractures in the humerus and ankle.[20] Increased pro-inflammatory environment is the hallmark of obesity, which could also increase the risk of osteoporosis and fractures by alteration of bone formation and resorption mechanisms.[21] This pro-inflammatory state increases the risk of metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes, which in turn can worsen bone quality.[22] TNF-α promotes RANKL expression in bone marrow stromal cells and exerts osteoclastogenic effects.[23] Increased expression of Monocyte Chemotactic Protein-1 (MCP-1) is seen in the visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue of obese patients when compared to non-obese persons. It interacts with the CCR2 receptor present in monocytes and macrophages, and stimulates osteoclastogenesis through the JAK/STAT and Ras/MAPK pathways.[24] A couple of TNF superfamily inflammatory mediators, TRAIL (TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand) and TNFSF14 (also known as LIGHT, a type II transmembrane protein), possess pro-osteoclastogenic effects in patients with obesity[18] [Figure 1].

Multiprong pathogenic mechanisms that lead to increased fragility features in patients with obesity

Adiponectin, an adipokine, stimulates the RANKL receptor, thereby inhibiting osteoprotegerin production in osteoblasts and indirectly increasing osteoclastogenesis. An inverse relationship between BMD and adiponectin levels has been observed in various studies, after adjusting for total body fat mass.[25] However, this relationship was inconsistent among premenopausal women. The levels of sex hormones could be a confounding factor that warrants further study.[26] Leptin, a prominent adipokine, has been found to directly stimulate osteoblast cell differentiation and inhibit osteoclast cell differentiation. This could be mediated by its inhibition of the activated NF-κB ligand receptor, leading to a consequent increase in osteoprotegerin expression.[27] Leptin activates the hypothalamic sympathetic nervous system, which may contribute to impaired bone formation. The relationship between leptin levels and BMD is inconsistent, and its exact role in bone metabolism is yet to be determined.[28]

Limitations in the diagnosis of Osteoporosis and prediction of fracture risk in patients with obesity:

BMD assessment by dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) may yield inaccurate values if not interpreted in relation to body weight. Generally, it shows falsely high and low values in patients with high and low BMI, respectively. Greater cortical and trabecular BMD and a greater number of trabeculae in the distal radius and distal tibia are observed in obese patients on HRpQCT.[29] Premaor et al. compared fracture risk assessment tool (FRAX) scores between obese and non-obese postmenopausal women in 2013. They observed that the 10-year fracture probability calculated by FRAX was significantly lower in the obese group (7.1 vs. 10.9% for hip fracture and 18.2 vs. 23.3% for major osteoporotic fracture, respectively). This difference remains significant even after excluding BMI from the calculation.[30] Deriving trabecular bone score (TBS) from DXA images necessitates adjustment for soft tissue thickness anterior and posterior to the lumbar spine. TBS values can be underestimated due to X-ray attenuation and associated noise interference that is highest in high adiposity. TBS software version 4.0 [currently TBS v4.0 (TBS Core Module v19.4.0)] accommodates variability in soft tissue thickness among individuals.[31]

Prevention of fragility fractures in subjects with obesity:

Special emphasis should be placed on lifestyle measures to prevent fragility fractures in elderly subjects with obesity. Cessation of smoking and alcohol intake should be advised, and physical exercise and a healthy diet should be encouraged. The weight loss in obesity patients must be advised to promote losing subcutaneous obesity and not to lose muscle mass that can further jeopardise BMD. Weight loss has been associated with mild bone mass in the hip and trabecular bone, especially in older subjects, particularly in cases of sarcopenia.[3233] To reduce the loss of BMD and muscle mass associated with exercise, physical activity is advised; both aerobic and resistance exercises have been shown to increase BMD and prevent fragility fractures.[34] Patients must ensure adequate calcium and protein intake. The ISBMR (Indian Society of Bone Mineral Research) position statement recommends 1000–2000 IU of vitamin D and 1000 mg/day of calcium, particularly in women >50 years.[35] Additionally, a minimum of 1–1.2 g/kg protein must be ensured for the prevention and treatment of sarcopenia.[36]

Relationship between Anti-obesity medications and risk of fragility fractures:

GLP-1RAs are among the most used drugs for the treatment of T2DM in subjects with cardiovascular disease and for chronic weight management. Zheer Kejlberg Al-Mashhadi et al. found that the risk of major osteoporotic fractures was slightly lower, albeit not significantly, in those treated with GLP-1 RA compared with those treated with DPP-4 inhibitors as add-on therapies to metformin. The difference in fracture rates was between 10 and 15%.[37] Most observational and meta-analyses on GLP-1 RA use and osteoporotic fracture rates have found neutral effects.[2238]

Anti-osteoporotic therapy in obesity:

Most of the fracture prevention trials are focused on post-menopausal women with low BMD with or without obesity. It is tricky to extrapolate those findings to the obese population. Previous bone turnover markers study confirmed the low bone turnover state in the obese population, and that’s why anti-resorptive therapy has questionable efficacy in obese patients at risk of osteoporotic fractures. In one of the randomized controlled trials, alendronate was found to be efficacious against non-obese persons with osteoporosis but not with obese patients.[39] A significant reduction in non-vertebral fractures was observed only among those without obesity in one of the sub-analyses of the FREEDOM study cohort.[40] Annual intravenous zoledronic acid treatment decreased the risk of vertebral fracture in patients with BMI >25 kg/m2, but not in non-vertebral fractures in the HORIZON study.[41] An increase in the anti-resorptive was also proposed to reduce vertebral fractures among obese patients. The GLOW study highlighted underreporting of osteoporotic fragility fractures among the obese population, where only 27% obese women with fractures were treated compared to 41% of non-obese women.[4]

CONCLUSION

Obesity and osteoporotic fractures have a complex relationship where an increase in the risk of fractures cannot be attributed to a decrease in BMD in most cases. The deterioration of the bony microarchitecture, along with increased risk of falls, makes this group of patients susceptible to a unique risk profile. The most important predictors for fragility fractures are higher subcutaneous or visceral fat and lower muscle mass (sarcopenic obesity). Professionals need to be aware of the increased risk of osteoporosis and fracture in patients with obesity, as they are currently considered low risk and tend to be underdiagnosed and undertreated.
