Dendrochronology reveals the response of a riparian forest to water management in an arid river basin
Authors: Noelle K Patterson, Xiaoli Dong, Belize A Lane, Adam Csank, Stewart B Rood, Samuel Sandoval‐Solis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRiparian corridors in arid climates sustain life in otherwise inhospitable environments, creating zones of ecological and cultural importance. However, rivers in arid climates are often managed to provide water for human populations at the expense of a river's freshwater biodiversity. In this study, ecosystem response to river flow management is assessed using mature cottonwood tree‐ring growth and carbon isotope composition as bio‐indicator proxies for river ecosystem health. We examine the ecological impacts of flow management on the Lower Truckee River in Nevada, USA, which runs through an arid‐climate basin that has been subject to decades of heavy flow diversion and management. Particular attention is given to the effects of major lawsuits in 1973 and 1982 that restored spring and summer flows to the river following progressive dewatering since 1905. Most mature trees (>30 years old) downstream of diversions responded strongly to restored flows, with average annual tree‐ring growth increases of 160%. Among tested streamflow metrics, average annual flow had the strongest positive influence on cottonwood growth, and aspects of the spring snowmelt recession were also influential. Precipitation was also linked with cottonwood growth, primarily during the period of management before 1973 when dry season flows were severely limited. Not all floodplain trees responded similarly to changes in flow metrics, suggesting that individual tree attributes and heterogeneity in floodplain soils are highly important to tree growth. Results offer promising evidence that flow restoration can lead to measurable improvement in riparian forest productivity, although site‐specific considerations including channel form and location on the floodplain are important in determining response to changes in flow patterns.Keywords: dendrochronology, functional flows, Nevada, riparian ecology, water resources management

INTRODUCTION

Rivers worldwide and particularly in arid environments have experienced profound levels of human alteration that affect flow patterns, channel form, and species composition (Grill et al., 2019; Kondolf et al., 2013; Vorosmarty et al., 2010). River alterations resulting from dams, such as disruption of natural flow, sediment, and chemical regimes, threaten freshwater biodiversity on continental and global scales (Maavara et al., 2020; Magilligan & Nislow, 2005; Poff et al., 2007). River ecosystem health depends on numerous interacting physical and biogeochemical processes involving flow, sediment and temperature regimes, water quality, channel form and floodplain connection, and vegetation composition (Poff & Ward, 1989; Wohl et al., 2015). Native species with sensitive habitat requirements, known as bio‐indicators, are therefore often used to gauge the health of altered river ecosystems, because these species respond to a wide range of environmental influences that determine their abundance (Huang et al., 2013; Norris & Thoms, 1999). Pairing bio‐indicator metrics with physical habitat indicators such as streamflow metrics creates a more robust measurement of river ecosystem health and effects of alteration.

Trees are particularly useful bio‐indicators; through annual growth rings, trees record their response to environmental conditions across their lifetime, which can range from decades to centuries. In arid environments where water availability is a limiting growth factor, tree‐ring widths have been correlated with water availability, recording a tree's response to drought conditions (Meko et al., 2015; Philipsen et al., 2018; Schook et al., 2022; Stromberg & Patten, 1990). Cottonwoods (Populus spp.) dominate floodplain habitats in many arid regions of the Northern Hemisphere (Friedman et al., 2005), and their extent covers most of the United States and Canada (Cooke & Rood, 2008). Cottonwoods are also a keystone species of floodplains across North America, providing habitat, food sources, and temperature regulation for riparian and instream ecosystems (Beechie et al., 2010; Cooke & Rood, 2008; Hillman et al., 2016). Cottonwoods can be an excellent bio‐indicator species for ecohydrological analysis because their life history and growth are closely tied to flow patterns (Rood et al., 2013), providing an ecological record of past hydrologic conditions on the floodplain.

Cottonwoods in arid regions tend to be phreatophytes, meaning they are deep‐rooted and rely on groundwater for survival (Rood et al., 2011). Cottonwoods cannot survive long without continuous access to root water, and mortality has been documented on numerous occasions where cottonwoods lost access to a stable groundwater supply (Foster et al., 2018; Rood, Braatne, & Hughes, 2003; Schook et al., 2020; Scott et al., 1999; Stella et al., 2013). In addition to river‐supported groundwater, cottonwoods have been found to source water from shallow soil water delivered through precipitation or overbank flooding (Phelan et al., 2022). Plasticity of rooting structures within cottonwood species indicates an ability to adapt to local water availability (Rood et al., 2011).

In arid regions, cottonwood growth rings often correlate with streamflow availability (Foster et al., 2018; Philipsen et al., 2018; Schook et al., 2016; Schook et al., 2022; Stromberg & Patten, 1990), although this relationship is not conclusive in some studies (Scott et al., 1999; St. George, 2014). Other cottonwood bio‐indicator metrics such as the ratio of 13C to 12C in tree‐ring cellulose can be related to water stress in arid environments because the preferential discrimination of plants against heavier 13C isotopes decreases as drought stress leads to stomatal closure (McCarroll & Loader, 2004). 13C/12C ratios have therefore been used alongside ring widths to improve understanding of biological response to water availability (Schook et al., 2020). Studying cottonwood–flow interactions can help identify river conditions most suitable for riparian ecosystem productivity, which is especially instructive in managed river systems.

Streamflow metrics can be paired with bio‐indicators to better understand how particular aspects of streamflow affect river ecosystems. Selecting appropriate streamflow metrics to assess river ecological status and set restoration targets has been the focus of many studies (Carlisle et al., 2017; Olden & Poff, 2003; Poff et al., 1997; Yarnell et al., 2020). Functional flows theory has emerged in recent years as an approach to assess river ecosystem status by linking specific components of flow regimes to ecology‐supporting functions (Escobar‐Arias & Pasternack, 2010; Yarnell et al., 2020). Functional flows refer to seasonal flows (e.g., spring recession flows) understood to support critical ecosystem processes such as habitat formation and sediment transport, maintenance of chemical and temperature regimes, and life history cues such as migration and spawning (Yarnell et al., 2020). In California, functional flow metrics describing flow magnitude, timing, duration, frequency, or rate of change have been applied to distinguish natural flow regimes (Patterson et al., 2020) and to predict natural functional flows for ungauged streams (Grantham et al., 2022).

An important application of functional flows assessment is designing or evaluating environmental flow programs. Environmental flows are prescribed streamflow patterns on regulated rivers meant to sustain river ecosystems while balancing human water needs (Arthington et al., 1992; Glenn et al., 2017). Restoration of riparian corridors and fisheries has been attempted on dammed rivers using environmental flow programs, though results are often mixed (Glenn et al., 2017; Konrad et al., 2011; Olden et al., 2014). In some cases, insufficient monitoring of river ecosystem health takes place following environmental flow implementation, which limits the ability to adapt and learn from these experimental flows (Glenn et al., 2017).

The objective of this study is to assess ecosystem response to environmental flows using mature cottonwood tree‐ring growth and carbon isotope composition as bio‐indicators representing river ecosystem health. We use the Lower Truckee River in Nevada, USA, as a case study river that has been subject to heavy irrigation diversions since the early 1900s, and an environmental flow regime starting in the 1970s. We examine the ecological impacts of river diversions through both the period of extreme flow diversions and the subsequent period marked by the return of streamflow through an environmental flows program.

We hypothesize that increases in streamflow following decades of heavy diversions caused sustained shifts in functional flows, leading to increases in forest health indicated by an increase in mature cottonwood growth rate. We expect that metrics describing the spring snowmelt recession will have a significant influence on cottonwood growth, since these flows could most directly affect spring and summer water availability during the growing season of Northern hemisphere trees (St. George, 2014).

Our functional flows approach to assessing streamflow, paired with annual tree‐ring data, allows for a seasonal‐scale analysis of flow and ecological effect in a level of detail not before possible. A Bayesian model is used to assess the potential influence of streamflow and climate on annual cottonwood growth. The additional measure of carbon isotope concentrations in tree‐ring wood reveals tree responses not apparent from growth alone. While many studies have assessed the ecological effects of stream dewatering (Andersen, 2016; Rood & Mahoney, 2023; Schook et al., 2020; Williams & Cooper, 2005), this study system offers the unique opportunity to investigate the effects of both dewatering and the reintroduction of streamflow through environmental flow policies.

METHODS

Study area

The Truckee River is an interstate river crossing through California and Nevada, USA, flowing from the northern Sierra Nevada mountains surrounding Lake Tahoe to Pyramid Lake in the Great Basin Desert (Figure 1). The river supports riparian ecosystems along its entire 195‐km length, but the environmental conditions shift dramatically as the river passes east of the city of Reno into the rain shadow of the Sierra Nevada. Mean temperature in the study area ranges from 0°C in January to 23°C in July (Figure 2). Mean annual precipitation varies from 130 cm/year in the basin's Sierra Nevada headwaters to 18 cm/year in the desert east of Reno, NV.

Annual temperature, average daily streamflow, and annual precipitation patterns for the Lower Truckee River since 1950. Streamflow is plotted for gages above and below Derby Dam, the river's primary diversion point.

On the 95‐km river segment downstream of the city of Reno, known as the Lower Truckee River, the river is low gradient, alluvial, and meandering, although portions of the channel have been straightened and entrenched as part of flood control efforts (US Army Corps of Engineers, 1995). Average annual river flow at the Reno, NV USGS gage (number 10348000) is about 20 cms (cubic meter per second, or m3/s), and peak flows (in the top 5th percentile) reach above 70 cms. These highest magnitude floods are primarily driven by heavy rainfall or rain‐on‐snow events (Adams, 2012). Fremont cottonwoods (Populus fremontii) and willows (including sandbar willow, Salix exigua) populate the riparian corridor on the Lower Truckee River, although riparian coverage is sparse and patchy. Cottonwoods serve as the only native tree and the foundational species of the lower river's floodplain habitat (Rood, Gourley, et al., 2003).

The Truckee River has been subject to more than a century of heavy flow modifications (Gourley, 1998; Kramer, 1988; US Army Corps of Engineers, 1995). Several dams regulate flow on major tributaries of the Truckee River and together have a significant effect on mainstem flow. The largest flow alteration on the lower river is the Newlands Irrigation Project, constructed in 1905, which diverts flow at Derby Dam to agricultural areas in the Carson Valley to the south. Diversions for agriculture continued uncontested for several decades following the completion of the dam, causing the Lower Truckee River to run dry annually (Kramer, 1988).

By the 1970s, increased attention to the river's ecological damage, including federal listing of the endangered cui‐ui sucker, led to policy changes to promote restoration (Gourley, 1998; Rood, Gourley, et al., 2003). In 1973, environmental litigation introduced by the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe required significant flow increases along the final 60 km of the river and mostly ended the seasonal dewatering of the river in this corridor. In 1982, an additional lawsuit from the Tribe led to the implementation of an environmental flow regime operated from Stampede Reservoir (Figure 1). This policy introduced flow ramping to achieve a water stage decline of 2.5–1 cm/day during the spring snowmelt period to support both endangered fish spawning and vegetation recruitment (Rood, Gourley, et al., 2003). Both 1973 and 1982 marked historic changes in the Lower Truckee River's regulated flow regime. These dates are therefore used as a focus in streamflow and dendrochronological analyses in this study.

Experimental questions and tests.

Site selection

Study sites were selected at four separate cottonwood groves along the Lower Truckee River to compare ecological response across varying hydrologic influences (Figure 1). Two sites are above Derby Dam, the river's primary diversion point, and two sites are below. All four sites are downstream of Stampede Reservoir. Of the two upstream sites (US), site US‐1 is on the Nature Conservancy property that has undergone channel and floodplain restoration, while site US‐2 is near a restoration area but has not undergone any physical channel restoration (Peternel‐staggs et al., 2008). The two downstream sites (DS), DS‐1 and DS‐2, are both on Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe land.

Hydrology and climate data

Daily average streamflow data for the Lower Truckee River were available from long‐term USGS streamflow gages above and below the Derby Dam diversion point (Figure 1). Streamflow representing conditions at the upstream sites is primarily drawn from the Truckee River gage at Vista (number 10350000), with some gaps in the record (a few days or less) filled with the nearby Reno gage (number 10348000) using a linear regression (r 2 = 0.975). Flow representing the downstream sites came from the Truckee River gage below Derby Dam (number 10351600). After filling gaps, both gages had periods of record from 1918 to 2019 to align with collected tree‐ring data.

Daily flow data were processed using the Seasonal Flows Detection Algorithm using parameters adjusted for the low volume snowmelt and rain natural flow class to calculate 16 annual functional flow metrics representing seasonal to annual aspects of streamflow (Patterson et al., 2020). These metrics describe the magnitude, timing, duration, frequency, and rate of change of five functional flows identified for Mediterranean climate regions: (1) fall pulse, (2) wet season baseflow, (3) peak flows, (4) spring recession, and (5) dry season baseflow, that each serve ecological purposes as described in Yarnell et al. (2020). Average annual flow and coefficient of variation metrics are additionally included to represent annual streamflow conditions. The functional flows and associated functional flow metrics used in this study are visualized in Figure 3.

Five functional flow components (boxes) for a mixed snowmelt‐rain runoff regime typical of the mid‐elevation Sierra Nevada region capture aspects of the hydrograph most relevant for riverine ecosystems. Functional flow characteristics corresponding to each component are shaded, and metrics used in this study are marked with an X. Figure adapted from Yarnell et al. (2020).

Annual precipitation, temperature (annual average), and vapor pressure deficit (VPD) (annual maximum) were obtained from Parameter‐elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (Daly et al., 2008) across a set of 144 km grids, averaged to cover a continuous reach of the Lower Truckee River including all study sites. Climate data were used as predictors in the Bayesian model, described below. All annual streamflow and climate metrics were calculated based on water years (October 1–September 30, where the year refers to January 1 of the included range).

Dendrochronology

Increment cores were collected from trees at the four sites along the Lower Truckee River between June and September 2020. Cores were collected from 20‐30 mature trees at each site, for 100 total individuals. Upon visual inspection, 18 cores were found to have fuzzy or extremely faint ring boundaries, preventing accurate ring counting or measuring. These cores were removed, lowering the final total to 82 individual trees. Removal of juvenile rings (the first 25 years of growth) eliminated some trees from analysis and further reduced the total to 64 individual trees. In the field, study trees were selected to include a full range of distances from the channel extending from the channel banks to the outer edge of the riparian forest. We prioritized the selection of straight trees without apparent signs of damage that would most likely yield clear and consistent tree‐rings. Selection was otherwise haphazard, with an effort to include trees of all sizes. Information collected for each tree included GPS coordinates, recorded with GAIA GPS software on a smartphone (v2022.7, GAIAGPS.com), diameter at breast height, and a categorical assessment of crown fullness (sparse, moderate, or full). Trees were revisited in the spring of 2021 when flowers were visible to identify the sex of each tree, although many trees did not have visible flowers and sex could not be determined.

We cored trees with a 5.15‐mm diameter Haglof increment borer at the lowest level on the trunk permitting borer handle rotation, to obtain the longest record. At each site, one core per tree was taken from ~75% of trees, and duplicate cores were taken from ~25% of trees. This allowed for some additional quality control on a subset of tree cores, while prioritizing collection from as many individuals as possible. Collected cores were glued on wooden mounts and sanded with progressively finer grits down to 30 μm. Prepared cores were then visually cross‐dated using skeleton plots (Stokes & Smiley, 1968), and measured to a precision of 0.001 mm using a dissecting microscope and Velmex sliding scale (Bloomfield, NY) with MeasureJ2X software. Ring width results were quality controlled using the cross‐dating quality analysis computer program COFECHA, achieving series intercorrelation values of 0.3–0.6 for each site (Holmes, 1983).

Next, radial increment measurements were converted to basal area increment (BAI). Following the slower growing juvenile phase, BAI is relatively constant, while radial increments decrease over time as tree diameter increases (Willms et al., 2006). BAI was calculated using measured trunk diameter at breast height (dbh) and radial increments in the “dplyr” package in R (Bunn, 2010). Bark width, which does not represent annual growth, was removed from dbh measurements using a regression of bark width and dbh created from a sample of trees measured across the study area. Establishment date was estimated for each tree using methods described by Altman et al. (2006) that factor in differences in both missing core length and BAI. The estimated first 25 years of growth were removed from each tree before analysis to account for potential juvenile growth effects.

Stable carbon isotope discrimination

Subsets of tree cores were tested for stable carbon isotope ratios in individual growth rings (n = 402). Trees from US‐1, US‐2, and DS‐2 were used for testing because these sites had the best series intercorrelation as determined from COFECHA. The four best‐correlated, longest‐lived trees at each of the three sites were selected for sampling. A pooled site measurement of Δ13C was developed by combining samples from the four trees in each year of their shared growth period (54–90 years), resulting in a single time series of Δ13C representing the site. Between four and six trees have been considered a reliable sample size for Δ13C measurements (Leavitt, 2010).

To prepare samples for testing, purified α‐cellulose was extracted from wood samples to remove potential age effects that can occur when testing raw wood samples (Friedman et al., 2019). ɑ‐cellulose extraction was performed using an adaptation of methods from Leavitt and Danzer (1993) and Rinne et al. (2005) that use an acidified sodium chlorite solution to obtain cellulose followed by processing with sodium hydroxide to purify to α‐cellulose. Samples were packaged in 4 × 6 mm tin capsules and analyzed at the University of California, Davis Stable Isotope Facility using continuous flow ratio mass spectrometry with a PDZ Europa ANCA‐GSL elemental analyzer interfaced to a PDZ Europa 20–20 mass spectrometer. Analyses

Q1: Flow regulation effects on hydrology

To quantify changes to streamflow following flow management policies on the Truckee River, we performed a step change detection analysis on each functional flow metric from upstream and downstream gage records from 1918 to 2019. Each metric's record (102 years total) was inspected along a 60‐year sliding window (split into two intervals of 30 years each), and the window containing the greatest difference in paired interval conditions was identified as the record's step change point. Percentage change across the metric record from before and after the step change was quantified, and we used Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) statistical test to test for significance between the paired intervals at a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Q2: Flow regulation effects on tree growth

We used Tukey's HSD test to assess BAI from each tree and Δ13C from selected trees for significant changes caused by river regulation, with significance set as p < 0.05. For each tree, the two comparison populations were split by separating measurements before and after 1973 for sites below Derby Dam, and before and after 1982 for upstream sites. We required a minimum of 15 years for each testing pool to mitigate potential trends associated with short‐term climate fluctuations. This limited the analysis to trees with records beginning at or before 1967 or 1958, for upstream or downstream trees, respectively. In total, 5 trees from the upstream sites and 10 trees from the downstream sites met these conditions for analysis.

Bayesian hierarchical model

We expect that the effects of climatic conditions on growth might vary with the regulation period (r); hence, the slope of each climatic predictor (P, T, and VPD) is indexed by r (r = 1, 2). Regulation periods are defined differently for sites upstream and downstream of Derby Dam, based on important policy dates that reflect detectable changes in annual hydrologic metrics. For upstream sites, regulation periods are split before and after 1982, and for downstream sites, regulation periods are defined before and after 1973.

We specified noninformative priors for all the parameters. Models were implemented in RJAGS, an open‐access software package designed for Bayesian data analysis in the R statistical programming language (Plummer, 2003). The identifiability problem (Raue et al., 2013) arising from having an intercept term and multiple random‐effect terms in the mean model is solved by postsweeping of random effects (Ogle & Barber, 2020). JAGS uses Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to sample model parameter values from their joint posterior distribution. The model was run with a single MCMC chain, and convergence of parameters was confirmed visually through traceplots. Models were specified with 5000 adaptive iterations, 2000 burn‐in iterations, and a thinning interval of 20 to reduce autocorrelation to achieve a total of 1000 effective samples. These effective samples were used to construct the posterior distribution for each parameter.

Q3/4: Streamflow, climate, and regulation influence on cottonwood growth

We determined significance for each model parameter as whether or not the 95% credible interval of the posterior estimates overlapped with zero, where a credible interval containing zero was deemed not significant. Marginal significance, that is, significance with a lower credible interval such as 90% or 80%, is in some cases considered and discussed. To examine how flow regulation potentially modified the effect of climate conditions on cottonwood growth, we indexed the slope of climate parameters by regulation period and compared the posterior distributions separately in plots and analysis. The posterior means and 95% credible intervals of random‐effects parameters at the site and tree levels were also plotted as part of the analyses.

RESULTS

Q1: Flow regulation effects on hydrology

Distinct differences in streamflow patterns exist above and below the Derby Dam diversion point. Annual flow patterns varied between historical and modern periods of flow management, and between wet and dry years (Figure 4). In the historical management era, streamflow was nearly fully diverted during dry years, such as 1959. Even in a wet year, such as 1943, flows during the summer growing season were nearly fully diverted at Derby Dam. In dry years, diversions become more apparent as a larger fraction of annual flow, in both historical and modern management. Modern management practices, however, avoid full dewatering of the lower river, unlike historical practices.

Comparisons of average daily streamflow from gages above and below Derby Dam across modern and historical management periods, in select wet and dry water years. Differences in flow between the two gages are predominantly from diversions at Derby Dam.

The step change detection analysis revealed large shifts in dry season flow magnitude metrics downstream of Derby Dam (Figure 5). The years 1972 and 1974 emerged as step change years for the 50th and 90th percentile dry season flow magnitudes. Both metrics more than quadrupled following a step change, increasing in magnitude by 727% and 318%, respectively. These results align strongly with the environmental flows introduced for the Truckee River in 1973. Other large metric changes around this step change period included an increase in average annual flow of 77%, a 45% reduction in the coefficient of variation (a measurement of stream flashiness, with lower values representing less flashiness), a 31% increase in wet season magnitude, and a 30% increase in wet season duration.

Select annual flow metrics show change through time at flow gages above and below Derby Dam. Step changes are marked with black lines to illustrate where a transition in average flow metric values occurred, and the percent shift in metric value is specified for each flow metric. Metrics marked with an asterisk exhibit statistically significant change. The dates of substantial flow management changes in 1973 and 1982 are marked with dashed gray lines.

Upstream of Derby Dam, functional flow metrics did not change as much. Several metrics changed by more than 10%, with most step changes occurring between 1985 and 1989. The spring recession rate‐of‐change lowered by 24% with a statistically significant step change identified in 1961, though inspection of the plotted records suggests that a gradual lowering of the spring recession rate‐of‐change continued until the mid‐1990s (Figure 5). This aligns with flow policies enacted in the mid‐1990s to match spring flow recession with the cottonwood recruitment box model described by Rood, Gourley, et al. (2003). Step change results for the rest of the functional flow metrics at both streamflow gages are tabulated in Appendix S1: Table S1.

Q2: Flow regulation effects on tree growth and stress

Upstream and downstream trees exhibited similar growth patterns on a decadal scale, although annual‐scale growth patterns deviate in some years (Figure 6). Growth distinctly rose between 1950 and 1970 at the downstream sites, followed by generally greater growth after 1970. Growth at the upstream sites shows a slight increase among fluctuations over time, occasionally aligning with rises and drops in growth also experienced at the downstream sites. Certain years with notable weather patterns stood out across both groups, such as a drop in growth in the exceptionally dry year of 1977 and a sharp increase in growth in the exceptionally wet year of 2017.

Annual basal area increments across trees upstream and downstream of Derby Dam, revealing an increased growth trend in downstream trees from 1950 to 1970.

Outcomes from Tukey's HSD test indicated a clear increase in tree growth at downstream sites after 1973 across the 10 trees tested. Eight out of 10 trees (80%) exhibited a significant increase in growth (p < 0.05), and only one tree experienced a significant decrease. The average growth increase among these trees was 160%. Results for upstream trees were more mixed, with three out of five trees (60%) experiencing a significant increase in growth after 1982. Although Δ13C measurements available for Tukey's HSD test were limited, some outcomes aligned with the BAI results. Δ13C increased across pooled samples in DS‐2 and US‐2, suggesting lowered stress over time for the represented trees. Δ13C significantly decreased across pooled samples at US‐1. Tukey's HSD test outcomes for all trees with sufficient records are available in Appendix S1: Table S2, with average and percent differences reported.

Although Δ13C measurements were performed on a small subset of annual tree‐rings, patterns of Δ13C revealed responses to streamflow conditions that are less apparent from aggregate BAI analysis. Figure 7 illustrates growth and carbon isotope discrimination of four long‐lived trees at site DS‐2 which exhibited distinct patterns aligning with the 1973 flow management changes. Increases in downstream Δ13C after 1973 were statistically significant, as confirmed by the Tukey's HSD test, indicating lowered water stress. Since 1930, Δ13C revealed a gradual onset of water stress that began to reverse in the 1970s. Most years after 1973 show less water stress than the historical era. BAI patterns in these trees demonstrate a similar response; very low levels of growth occurred before the 1970s, followed by much greater growth after 1973. Although the trees respond to very dry years such as 2008, growth rebounds afterward, demonstrating resiliency to drought conditions.

Q3: Streamflow and climate influences on tree growth

The Bayesian model explained 57% of the variance in BAI (r 2 = 0.57), with 15% due to explanatory variables and 42% due to random effects. While the overall fit of the model is satisfying, indicated by the low divergence of most BAI values from the 1:1 line, it underpredicts growth for small BAI values and slightly overpredicts growth for large BAI values (Appendix S1: Figure S9).

Of all climate and flow variables examined, annual precipitation has the highest influence on annual growth and is statistically significant (i.e., the 95% credible interval does not overlap with zero) (Figure 8). The influence of precipitation, however, is limited to the historical period of regulation (before reoperation of nearby major dams). The spring recession flow has the greatest influence on tree growth of the functional flow components, with a statistically significant influence from both spring recession rate‐of‐change and spring recession start timing. Recession rate‐of‐change has a negative influence; a higher spring recession rate‐of‐change corresponds to lower tree growth. Spring recession start timing has a positive influence; a later spring recession start date is associated with increased tree growth. Wet season flow magnitude has a marginally significant negative influence on tree growth (at a 90% credible interval). This result is opposite to the relationship of average annual flow, which has a positive influence on tree growth. The negative influence of wet season magnitude on growth may be due to the potentially destructive effects of high‐magnitude winter floods on riparian trees (Rood et al., 2009). Dry season start timing had a negative significant influence on tree growth; a later dry season start timing (and an associated prolonged spring recession period) corresponds to greater tree growth.

DISCUSSION

Functional flow effects on cottonwood growth

The increase in flow, and especially summer flow, below Derby Dam was enabled by the additional storage provided by Stampede Reservoir, which was completed in 1970. It followed the endangered species listing of the endemic cui‐ui sucker, which prompted litigation by the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe in 1973 that enabled increased Truckee River flows and subsequent improvements in tree growth witnessed in the following decades. Our findings build directly on those of Rood, Gourley, et al. (2003), who found that environmental flows established in the 1970s and 1980s improved conditions for cottonwood recruitment. We extend this conclusion with evidence that environmental flows also improved growing conditions for mature cottonwoods downstream of Derby Dam, some of which were declining in growth rate up until the implementation of environmental flows in the 1970s.

The functional flow metric step change detection analysis confirmed large shifts in some flow metrics following the establishment of environmental flow regulations, especially increases in dry season magnitude downstream of Derby Dam (Figure 5). Cottonwoods require access to water year‐round, and the link between cottonwood growth and groundwater access during dry periods is well established (Foster et al., 2018; Schook et al., 2022; Stromberg & Patten, 1990). Other flow metric shifts, such as increase in average annual flow and decrease in the coefficient of variation, were likely also beneficial to cottonwoods, indicated by their statistically significant (or marginally significant) effect on cottonwood growth in the Bayesian model (Figure 8). Annual coefficient of variation was used to measure fluctuations in flow within a water year, with flashy or irregular spikes in flow increasing the metric value. Irregular flow spikes in other systems have been found to correspond with loss in cottonwood cover over time (Rood & Mahoney, 2023).

Spring recession rate‐of‐change has declined by 24% over the past several decades to become more gradual, due to strategies implemented by agencies operating dams and diversions along the river to provide suitable conditions for fish and cottonwood seedling recruitment (Rood, Gourley, et al., 2003) (Figure 5). The significant effect of spring recession rate‐of‐change on mature cottonwood growth in the Bayesian model was unexpected, since the importance of this flow metric has more commonly been associated with cottonwood seedling root growth in the shallow capillary fringe (Amlin & Rood, 2002; Foster et al., 2018; Willms et al., 2006). However, mature cottonwoods have been found to access shallow soil water, especially where groundwater is deep, so it is possible that trees on the Lower Truckee River strategically source from shallow soil water as high flows recede in the spring (Snyder & Williams, 2000; Williams & Cooper, 2005).

Modeling of hydrologic and climatic influences on tree growth revealed the importance of average annual flow, precipitation, coefficient of variation, the spring recession flow, and certain aspects of other functional flows (Figure 8). Spring recession start timing had a significant effect on growth, with later timing corresponding to greater growth. The importance of spring recession timing is less established than the importance of spring recession magnitude on growth and may relate to the alignment of sustained flows and higher temperatures later in the spring. Some evidence supports increased growth of cottonwoods in warmer temperatures (Kalischuk et al., 2001; Rood et al., 2007). Wet season flow magnitude, unlike average annual flow, had a strong negative influence on tree growth. This result, although counterintuitive, may reflect the potentially destructive effects wet season floods have on riparian vegetation. Although cottonwoods are considered tolerant to periodic flooding, floods can cause short‐term damage, reducing photosynthetic rate and subsequent growth (Rood et al., 2009; Stromberg, 1997).

Dry season flow magnitude did not have a significant effect on cottonwood growth based on modeling (Figure 8). This was a surprising outcome compared to other water‐limited river corridors in which lowered dry season streamflow has been linked to lowered growth or other stress responses in cottonwoods (Foster et al., 2018; Schook et al., 2022; Shafroth et al., 2000). Metrics such as spring recession timing and rate of change that had a significant influence may be more closely tied to tree growth. Tree stress response to low dry season flows may present more directly as branch dieback, which is a well‐documented response of cottonwoods to drought stress (Andersen, 2016; Schook et al., 2016; Scott et al., 1996). When dry season flows were greatly increased on the Truckee River in 1973, it is also possible that mature cottonwood roots regained perennial connection with the groundwater table, leading to a weakened correspondence with dry season flow magnitude.

Hydrologic and environmental influences on cottonwood growth

Changes in streamflow driven by changes in river regulation in the 1970s and 1980s have likely contributed to improvements in growth rate in mature cottonwoods, based on significant increases in growth and decreases in stress confirmed by Tukey's test (Appendix S1: Table S2). However, modeling points to other environmental factors influencing tree growth. Annual precipitation has a significant effect on tree growth, although this influence is primarily limited to the historical flow management period when streamflow was severely reduced (Figure 8). Environmental flows in the modern management period may have restored water availability enough to act as a buffer against the most severe water stress periods in which trees would need to opportunistically uptake shallow soil water from precipitation. This may suggest the success of modern flow regulations for stable tree growth. The importance of precipitation on cottonwood growth is notable given the extremely arid conditions on the Lower Truckee River (less than 200 mm rainfall annually). Other studies have found an importance in summertime precipitation (Cooper et al., 1999) or shallow soil water for cottonwood water uptake during dry periods. Cottonwood tree rooting systems include a collar of shallow roots adapted to uptake shallow soil water originating from precipitation or overbank flows, which provide a different pathway for water availability in addition to groundwater supported by river flows (Hultine et al., 2010; Phelan et al., 2022; Williams & Cooper, 2005).

Variation in growth remained among individual trees after accounting for streamflow and climatic effects (Figure 9). Water demand of individual trees may vary due to factors affecting evaporative demand such as light exposure, or stress‐inducing factors such as competition or browsing (Rood et al., 2013). Groundwater dynamics on the floodplain likely have an important influence on individual tree growth. Williams et al. (2022) for instance found cottonwood growth and Δ13C more correlated to groundwater levels than hydroclimatic variables in a dryland riparian woodland. The dynamics of this relationship at each site depend on complex factors affecting tree groundwater availability, including substrate composition, topography of the channel and floodplain, and vegetation composition (Stella et al., 2013). The texture of sediments can also influence tree water availability, as fine‐textured sediments are more capable of storing plant‐available water than sands (Cooper et al., 1999). Although the Bayesian model achieved satisfactory predictive potential when comparing predicted and observed BAI, the model underpredicted BAI for low values of BAI (Appendix S1: Figure S9). This discrepancy could indicate a growth influence that is not explicitly included in the model, such as groundwater levels. It is possible that while streamflow declines during dry years, a stable groundwater table may support actual tree growth in a way that the Bayesian model could not predict.

Site‐level random effects from Bayesian modeling revealed a partially significant negative effect at the sites downstream of Derby Dam (Figure 9). Random effects capture residual influence beyond the explanatory power of the predictor variables, namely streamflow and the tested climate variables. Dams have a wide range of primarily harmful effects on downstream river ecosystems caused by disruption of sediment, chemical, and temperature regimes, and physical obstruction to passage (Cambray, 1991; Merritt & Cooper, 2002; Yarnell et al., 2015). These factors likely contributed to the modeled reduction in tree growth based on downstream site location. Other studies of cottonwood growth have found similarly significant growth influence due to site, such as unexplained site random effects on the South Platte River (Christensen et al., 2023) or faster growth due to site‐specific nutrient input from livestock on the Little Bow River, Alberta (Bigelow et al., 2020). Random effects at the individual tree level were significant in 64% of individuals. While individual factors among trees are highly influential on their growth, there was no correspondence found based on site, or proximity to the river within a site. Highly individual characteristics such as sunlight availability, browsing, and damage from pests and disease may influence the importance of random effects seen within the model, and deserve further exploration.

The Δ13C analysis revealed a notable increase in Δ13C, indicating lower water stress in downstream trees after 1973 (Figure 7). However, unlike other studies, Δ13C did not exhibit a more rapid response to increased flows than growth (Schook et al., 2016). It is possible that trees on the Lower Truckee River floodplain have a more stable floodplain water table than the smaller system studied in Schook et al. (2020), and changes in Δ13C would not appear as abruptly.

Streamflow management in arid systems

Environmental flows established on the Truckee River have been considered a success based on recorded recovery of several native riparian and aquatic species (Rood, Gourley, et al., 2003). Environmental flows have been implemented in other arid watersheds of the western United States to restore riparian vegetation, with mixed success (Glenn et al., 2017; Shafroth et al., 1998; Williams & Cooper, 2005). On the Colorado River, the experimental “Minute 319 Pulse Flow” negotiated in 2014 had less success than expected in its goal of recruiting new cottonwood and willow seedlings, with most of the satellite‐measured revegetation diminishing a year after the pulse flow event (Glenn et al., 2017). On the Bill Williams River in Arizona, releases from Alamo Dam since the 1990s have been successful in controlling invasive tamarisk populations while allowing cottonwoods and willows to establish, although their geographic extent is limited to valley floors because of dam‐regulated flow reductions (Glenn et al., 2017; Shafroth et al., 1998). In Alberta, Canada, an environmental flow strategy similar to that on the Truckee River reported successful cottonwood establishment and mature tree growth by raising dry season flows and gradually ramping spring recession flows (Foster et al., 2018). Across these examples, a permanent environmental flows program providing sufficient instream flows during dry periods has benefitted mature cottonwoods more than experimental flood pulses negotiated on an individual basis. The permanence of environmental flows on the Truckee River has likely contributed to the river's ecological improvement, which has benefitted fish and riparian birds alongside riparian vegetation (Rood, Gourley, et al., 2003).

Our study provides strong evidence that degraded riparian corridors can respond positively to environmental management programs that balance ecosystem needs with human needs. Mature cottonwood forests are important to preserve for their cultural and ecological value, and as providers of habitat and food for riparian and riverine communities (Sabo et al., 2008). Mature cottonwoods are also the seed source for future cottonwood regeneration, which promotes a genetically diverse and healthy population (Reed & Frankham, 2003). In regions where cottonwood riparian forests have been depleted, manual replanting of seeds or plant stakes requires a substantial effort with many mortality risks (Alpert et al., 1999; Rood et al., 2016). Preserving existing cottonwood forests through environmental flow management is therefore a more effective strategy for sustaining riparian and riverine ecosystems. Differences in growth and Δ13C patterns across and within sites, along with random‐effects model significance across most individuals suggest that factors affecting individual trees play an important role in determining cottonwood growth and stress. Site‐specific considerations including channel form and individual tree physiology are important in determining response to changes in flow patterns, along with the proven effectiveness of environmental flows implementation.
