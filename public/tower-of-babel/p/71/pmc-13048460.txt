My journey to find fulfillment in life and work
Authors: Gabriel Bosslet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis manuscript explores the author’s journey of balancing life and work through mindful reflection and intentional changes, prompted by the loss of a close friend and mentor. The author shares 6 key changes he made to align actions with his life priorities: completing clinical work at work, removing work email from his phone, avoiding work outside of work hours, disconnecting during vacations, seeking help when needed, and cultivating fulfilling hobbies. The manuscript emphasizes the importance of regular reflection on priorities and creating a balanced life that values personal well-being alongside professional responsibilities. By sharing personal experiences and practical strategies, the author suggests ways others can assess their priorities and make changes that foster fulfillment and balance.Keywords: balance, priorities, reflection

My wake-up call

While sitting on a bench at the Denver Botanic Garden watching my 2-year-old climb the monkey bars, I’m jolted out of my sunny coffee bliss by the ringing of my phone. It is a friend telling me through tears that Alex has died. The words don’t really register, and my head swims in colors and sounds as I try to understand.

Alex is gone. He took his own life.

This moment was pivotal in ways I couldn’t understand at the time. The death of a friend is always tragic, but Alex’s suicide in 2016 was especially impactful. He was 10 years my senior and a fellow program director. Gregarious and well-liked, he was nationally known for his work as a medical educator. He was who I wanted to be when I “grew up.”

Viewing my life and career from a wider lens

The loss of a close friend and mentor to suicide made me closely examine my own life and habits. By all external measures, I was successful. I was working as a Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine Program Director at a major academic institution, served on several national committees, and was happy with my career trajectory.

However, Alex’s death made me evaluate my life beyond work. I realized I was out of shape and constantly connected to email. My kids were growing up quickly and I felt like I was missing it. This examination led me to understand that my default life setting was work, and I occasionally opted into the rest of my life.

This revelation—that work had cannibalized most of my life—is a common struggle among many in medicine, especially those in academic and leadership positions. When work can take place outside of the exoskeleton of a traditional workplace—an intensive care unit (ICU), a clinic, a laboratory, or an academic office—that work risks becoming fluid, filling all the time and space to which it is given access.1

Alex’s death made me think philosophically about my time on earth, my values, and whether I would have regrets at the end of my life. In Spiritual Exercises, published in 1548, Ignatius of Loyola came to essentially the same conclusion I did regarding how to approach an assessment of his life’s endeavors:

I will consider, as if I were at the point of death, what procedure and norm I will at that time wish I had used in the manner of making the present election. Then, guiding myself by that norm, I should make my decision on the whole matter.2

After a year of reflection with this “deathbed” lens, I realized I was productive but not happy when viewed from the perspective of life’s finitude. This led me to make significant changes in how I approached life, work, and time (Table 1) and to explore resources on productivity and time management (Table 2). I began regularly reflecting on whether the way I spent my time aligned with my life priorities.

I am going to share some of these changes below, but I don’t pretend that my priorities are everyone’s priorities, or that these changes are right for everyone. If there is a lesson from my experience that I want to share, it is to stop every so often to assess your priorities, how you want to spend your time, and how you want to be remembered. I consider this a type of meditative mindfulness—a time where I try to leave the fray of the everyday and view my life from above, from the lens of an 80- or 100-year epoch. This can be done alone or with a partner (often a spouse). The important thing is that it is done with some regularity.

Caveats to the way I operationalized needed change

There are several caveats about the changes this experience brought about for me. First, while they are relatively simple, they are not easy. They require thought and work and persistence.

These changes reflect my priorities, which may differ for others. Having spoken to colleagues about this over the years, I suspect at least a few will ring true for many.

This approach to my career does not imply that my job is not an important part of my life. I love my job and derive a lot of ­happiness from my work as a physician. However, I found that ­allowing work to encroach on all hours was unhealthy for me, and these changes aimed to improve that.

I follow the 80% rule: If I can achieve these changes 80% of the time, I consider it a success. Flexibility is important as responsibilities ebb and flow, but returning to the default state is crucial. If I deviate more than 20% of the time, it requires thought and reflection as to why, and an assessment of whether the circumstances fit within my life philosophy.

I acknowledge that as a White, cisgender, heterosexual male, I benefit from a demographic privilege that is historically and institutionally valued in my environment. This privilege undoubtedly makes it easier for me to discuss and challenge the productivity imperative compared to others who may not share this advantage. While I cannot immediately eliminate this privilege differential, I want to name it and emphasize that my experiences may not reflect the challenges faced by others. Nonetheless, I believe it is important to address these issues as we strive toward a more equitable environment for everyone.

My 6 simple (but not easy) changes

With that, here are the specific changes I have made over the last several years that I have found most helpful.

I structure my clinical work as much as I can to be able to finish it while at work

Early in my career, I often brought clinical work home, including clinic, ICU, and consult notes. This meant that I would come home, make dinner, and hang out with my family. Then, when the kids were in bed, I was tied to my computer for an hour or two finishing daily work. My mind tended to mull over these notes over dinner and family time, and I was less present than I wanted to be as a dad and husband. Work me never really stopped.

To address this, I stopped having clinic half-days when in the ICU, allowing me to be more present for critical care patients and to complete notes before leaving the hospital. I also changed my clinic template from 40-minute new patients and 20-minute returns to a 60/30 template. This reduced the number of patients I could see in a day but ensured I completed both the visits and all documentation during work hours. These changes were possible because my institution values physician wellness, and I accepted a small pay cut on our productivity model to prioritize not working at home. Alex’s death made me realize I would rather have the time than the money.

I don’t have my work email on my phone

I remember the night I committed to this change. I was cooking spaghetti with my 8-year-old daughter and having a wonderful conversation with her when I picked up my phone and checked my email. I found a petty issue that needed urgent attention the next day, which ruined my night. What was a pleasant experience with my daughter turned into me ruminating over something that I needn’t have known about until tomorrow.

So, I took my work email off my phone. While a simple task (a click in settings), it was not easy—it took about a year of fits and starts until it stuck. Now, 7 years later, you couldn’t pay me enough to put email back on my phone. I am more efficient with my email, as I must get it done when in front of a computer. This step has helped me disconnect from work in ways I couldn’t when email was immediately available on the computer in my pocket.

When I tell others about this, many say, “Oh, I could never do that,” and give reasons why they must have their email on their phone. I usually just smile because those are the same things I told myself before I did it.

As a rule, I don’t do work after work hours or on weekends when I don’t have clinical duties

I can remember early in my career saying to myself, “I will get to that [random work task] this weekend.” At the time, I thought it was just how things were done. I found that my weekends were busier than my weekdays between parenting, chores, and work. My reevaluation made me rethink my approach. Now I very rarely do work on weekends.

While I still have clinical responsibilities on some weekends, those without clinical work are dedicated to my chore list, sporting events, spending time with my family, cooking meals for the week, and taking my dog for trail runs.

I also don’t check email on weekends, as opening my email puts me in work mode, which I no longer consider appropriate for weekends. Removing email from my phone has been instrumental in this change. For me, most weekends and evenings are now reserved for life, not work.

I take all my vacation days, and I don’t do work or email when on vacation

I once spoke with a colleague who took a month off yearly and didn’t check her work email at all. This idea gnawed at me for years, especially as I noticed how intrusive work and email were during vacations. I once took my computer to the beach and spent an entire afternoon while my kids were boogieboarding editing a manuscript that “had to be done before I got back” (it didn’t) while trying desperately not to get sand in my keyboard. My default mode, even on vacation, was work; I would opt in to family time when I felt like things at work were under control.

So, I stopped checking email on vacation. This change has dramatically improved my ability to recharge and engage, and I haven’t missed anything important in 7 years. To accomplish this, I do several things:

I have realized that there are times I need life help, and I ask for it when needed

In my last year of fellowship, I started waking up at 3 am for no reason. I can remember specifically one time waking and ruminating on whether I checked a hemoglobin on a patient who had died several weeks before. I was struggling with my decision about what job to take and what my career would look like. I was sad more days than I was happy. It took me a long time to admit that I was depressed, and I have taken an antidepressant on and off since then. It helps me greatly when I need it, and I know from experience that there are a lot of us in medicine in this boat and we don’t talk about it because we think we are above that. But we aren’t.

And then during the pandemic, I began drinking way too much. A habit that bothered me but that I was able to control just sort of slowly grew and became a problem. My youngest son pointed out that I smelled like beer one night when I was reading him his bedtime story, which prompted me to seek help. I have written about my experience with “gray-area drinking” (but alcoholism nonetheless), and admitting I needed help at this time has been an important step for me.3 I attended Alcoholics Anonymous, and asking for help there was crucial in my journey.

There is a myth that physicians can handle the slings and arrows of life better than others, and that is false. Being honest with myself meant being vulnerable, which is both uncomfortable and imperative for growth. And I’ve grown a lot over the last 10 years.

I cultivate hobbies that fill my cup

Currently my hobbies include running, reading, cooking, and growing small trees. These hobbies for me have varied as I have gotten older (I used to make wine and coach cross-country). Many of us neglect hobbies as we go through medical training because of the long hours. At the same time, life never really seems to get easier and time pressures remain after we are done with residency, fellowship, that first grant, this block of time in the ICU, etc.

Assuming we will “have time for life” later is a version of the ­arrival fallacy: “I’ll be happy when I achieve X.” The problem is that X never arrives: X becomes Y becomes Z, and we risk spending a life waiting rather than living.

Harnessing efficiency to support change

For me these efforts have changed things dramatically. First, it means that I must be more efficient at work. Since I am prioritizing and blocking home time for recharging and life, it means I have less time for work. I have found several resources that have been helpful in improving my efficiency (Table 2), and these have been instrumental in helping to structure my work time for efficiency. However, I also find that allowing myself time to recharge at home means that I am more tuned for efficiency at work. It is easier for me to be productive when I know this production must happen in a fixed period (a manifestation of Parkinson’s law; see Table 2). Since taking work home and completing it later is no longer an option, I tend to be more productive.

The benefits of these changes for me

I am much more present at home. I now do all the meal planning, grocery shopping, prep cooking, and cleanup. I was a cross-country coach for my kids’ cross-country team. I make it to nearly all of their evening and weekend events. While hard to quantify, the benefits for my roles as a father and spouse are immense. My wife and I have started taking occasional random days off just to spend being “unproductive” together—going to a coffee shop, the art museum, on a hike. I find these days of leisure together to be some of my most fulfilling, and this is something I would not have considered doing before when “unproductive” time was verboten. (I use quotes around “unproductive” because this time is incredibly productive in fostering a healthy relationship.)

I’m often asked whether this intentional restructuring of time and priorities could have worked earlier in my career. I believe it could have, at least in part. Early career is a time of exploration, and I think it’s important to say “yes” to new roles and responsibilities, especially when they seem to align with your broader career narrative. I liken this phase to the early growth of a bonsai tree: Branches grow in many directions, and that’s not only expected; it is essential. But eventually, to shape the tree into something intentional and sustainable, you must prune. Looking back, I could have been more deliberate about which branches to keep. I said yes to many things without always reflecting on whether they meaningfully served me or the institution. Alex’s death showed me the importance of pruning and selective growth. The earlier one begins this process of thoughtful reflection and selective commitment, the more likely one is to cultivate a career that is both fulfilling and aligned with one’s values.

Reflecting on Alex’s death and the lessons it imparted has been transformative for me. The changes I made in my life and work have helped me find a balance that I lacked before. While I am not perfect at carrying them out, occasionally reflecting on how I spend my time based on my priorities has been invaluable. I share these experiences because I believe that there are a lot of us who struggle with similar challenges. For me the key change has been periodically reflecting on my priorities and ensuring that my time is spent in alignment with what these priorities suggest truly matters. By making intentional changes based upon my priorities, I have restructured the way I spend my time, allowing me to be more present and engaged while not at work. This restructuring has enabled me to enjoy a more balanced and rewarding life.
