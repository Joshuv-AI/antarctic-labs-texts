Effectiveness of CPP-ACP and fluoridated toothpastes in preserving enamel microhardness after erosion and abrasion challenges at different time intervals
Authors: Laila Al Dehailan, Shahad T. Alameer, Fatimah A. Alhassan, Razan Fahad Alghamdi, Deena Abdullah Alghamdi, Sukinah Bakr Alabdulmuhsin, Anwar Nabeel Almulhim, Maria Salem Ibrahim, Abdulrahman A. Balhaddad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo investigate the impact of fluoridated (1450 ppm F⁻) and CPP-ACP (MI Paste) toothpastes applied after different time points following erosive-abrasive challenge on the perseverance of enamel hardness. Materials and Methods Enamel specimens were subjected to an erosive-abrasive model using erosive beverage and toothbrushing simulation. The erosive challenge was achieved by soaking the specimens in Coca-Cola for 2 min. Distilled water was used as a control. Then, the specimens were subjected to brushing using fluoridated and CPP-ACP dentifrices applied via a brushing simulation device. The waiting time between the erosive and abrasive challenges was investigated at four levels (0, 15, 30, 60 min). The Vickers’ microhardness values of enamel at baseline and after the erosive-abrasive challenge were analyzed. Paired t-test and ANOVA analyzed the data.ResultsFollowing brushing with distilled water and fluoridated toothpaste, a significant reduction in enamel microhardness was observed at all time points (before vs. after) (P < 0.01). Brushing with CPP-ACP toothpaste achieved significant enamel microhardness reduction at 0 min (P < 0.001) and 30 min (P = 0.007). The erosive challenge and the toothpaste type were significant factors (P < 0.001) in modulating the enamel microhardness but not the waiting time (P = 0.149). There were significant interactions between the erosive challenge and the waiting time (P = 0.005), the toothpaste time and the waiting time (P < 0.001), and there was a significant interaction between the three variables (P = 0.005). CPP-ACP and fluoridated toothpastes resulted in less microhardness reduction compared to brushing using distilled water (P < 0.05), with no difference between CPP-ACP and fluoridated toothpastes.ConclusionThis study found that both fluoridated and CPP-ACP toothpastes are equally effective in protecting enamel, and delaying toothbrushing offers no protective benefits after such exposure. Delaying toothbrushing did not provide any protective benefits for enamel following exposure to the erosive-abrasive model, as fluoridated and CPP-ACP toothpastes were effective in protecting the enamel hardness.

Introduction

Dental erosion of dental hard tissues is a significant concern [1]. It involves the dissolution of the tooth surface by non-bacterial acids that soften the enamel and dentin surface, allowing the tooth’s inorganic phase to be demineralized [1]. The onset of erosion can lead to several complications by decreasing the hardness of teeth and making them more susceptible to dissolution [2]. Dental erosion could result from external sources such as acidic diet and medication or internally from gastric acid when reflux occurs [3]. Most investigations have concentrated on the biochemical components of erosive phenomena, and it has been determined that various parameters, including salivary pH, salivary flow, and the concentrations of calcium, phosphate, and fluoride affect the erosive susceptibility of acidic beverages [4].

Mechanical factors, such as abrasion and attrition, may cause further loss of tooth tissues, making the erosion process more challenging when they occur together [5]. It has been stated that early toothbrushing after consumption of erosive products may induce erosive enamel wear [6], as saliva-induced remineralization occurs on the surface of the lesions at slow rate [7, 8]. However, The efficiency of waiting periods between an erosive attack and tooth brushing remains a topic of debate, as brushing can exacerbate the wear of enamel and dentine that has been softened by demineralization [9]. Currently, it is uncertain how long time should pass after acid exposure to brush the teeth and reduce tooth loss caused by toothbrush chemical abrasion.

One study found no differences in enamel loss when specimens were brushed immediately or after 240 min following an erosion challenge [10]. Same results were observed in another investigation where enamel microhardness was not affected by the time between the erosion challenge and the onset of abrasion via a toothbrush [11]. A recent scoping review summarizing the results of seventeen studies concluded that brushing with fluoridated toothpaste immediately following erosive challenge had no impact on enamel wear [12]. Also, when fluoride-containing toothpastes were utilized, there was a significant difference in enamel loss between the enamel brushed with fluoridated and non-fluoridated toothpastes [13–15]. The fluoride in toothpaste may partially counteract some of its abrasiveness, resulting in minor mineral precipitation removal instead of major removal [13–15].

MI Paste is a toothpaste that contains casein phosphopeptides-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP), and it is designed to aid in the remineralization of enamel and reduce the risk of dental caries [16]. It contains a special formula that includes the complex of CPP-ACP, which besides fluoride, can improve the remineralization properties of the toothpaste [17]. Limited investigations were conducted regarding the effect of this toothpaste after the erosive challenge. Therefore, investigating its preventive effect on the microhardness of enamel surfaces, compared to fluoridated toothpaste, can provide insight into its preventive effect against acidic erosion. Therefore, this study aimed to compare fluoridated toothpaste and CPP-ACP toothpaste applied after different erosive challenges at different time points in the perseverance of enamel hardness. The null hypothesis is that using CPP-ACP toothpaste would not provide better protection for enamel than fluoridated toothpaste.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This article adheres to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Imam Abdulrahman bin Faisal University Institutional Review Board (IRB-2023-02-413). The teeth were not extracted for research purposes, and their use without consent was waived by the IRB (IRB-2023-02-413) and national regulations.

Study design, and sample size calculation

In this in vitro study, we investigated the combinatory effect of different erosive and abrasive challenges at different time points on the enamel hardness. The erosive challenge was the first exposure challenge against prepared enamel specimens, and it was investigated at two levels, Coca-Cola and distilled water as a control. After the erosive challenge, the enamel specimens were subjected to an abrasive challenge using different kinds of toothpaste: fluoridated, CPP-ACP toothpastes, and distilled water as control. The waiting time between the erosive and abrasive challenges was investigated at four levels (0, 15, 30, and 60 min). Therefore, the total number of groups was twenty-four. This calculation determined the required sample size for a three-way ANOVA test comparing the mean scores of the groups at a significance level of p < 0.05. Teeth selection and Preparation

The inclusion criteria for the teeth selection were sound teeth with no cracks or carious lesions. On the other hand, the exclusion criteria were teeth with abrasion, erosion, caries, cracks, or any defect. Samples consisted of 96 human wisdom teeth extracted during dental treatment. The 96 extracted teeth were longitudinally split into lingual-palatal and buccal halves using an IsoMet 4000 water-cooled precision saw (Buehler, Lake Bluff, IL, USA) to prepare 192 enamel specimens. Each specimen was inserted in an acrylic resin block, and the exposed enamel surface of each specimen was ground flat using 600-, 1200-, and 2000-grit silicon carbide paper (Wirtz-Buehler, Du ¨sseldorf, Germany) and 1-mm diamond suspension (Wirtz- Buehler) on a polishing cloth, to create nearly 4 × 4 mm flattened window of enamel [21].

The Erosive-Abrasive challenge

Enamel samples with microhardness values that ranged approximately ± 10% around the mean of the samples were included in the study, and randomly divided into 24 groups, as shown in Fig. 1, and exposed for 2 min to Coca-Cola and distilled water (control). After the exposures and after different time points of storage in artificial saliva (0, 15, 30 and 60 min), the specimens were brushed with different types of toothpaste: fluoridated toothpaste, CPP-ACP toothpaste, and distilled water as a control. All the brushing simulations were performed for 90 cycles, which is equivalent to one week of clinical service [21]. This erosion-abrasion model was adopted from previous studies with slight modifications [10, 22]. For the brushing simulation protocol, this study adhered to the methods of previous research, as no International Organization for Standardization (ISO) protocol exists for brushing enamel. The specimens were placed in a custom-made V-8 toothbrushing machine with the long axes of the specimens perpendicular to the long axis of the soft toothbrushes with filament diameter of 0.09 mm (Tara Toothbrush Company LLC, Dammam, Saudi Arabia). Each specimen contained a volume of 60 mL of toothpaste mixed with distilled water at a ratio of 1:3 [23]. Custom-made silicone trays were used to protect the reference areas. The brushing simulation was achieved at 200 g brushing load, simulating typical brushing pressure [23].

Before the erosive-abrasive cycle, the Vickers microhardness of the enamel surfaces was collected. After finishing the cycle, the specimens were thoroughly rinsed in deionized water and immersed in artificial saliva overnight to mimic the remineralization effect of human saliva. On the following day, the Vickers microhardness was measured again. The artificial saliva solution was prepared with 1 L of distilled water and 0.1029 g of CaCl2.2H2O, 0.04066 g of MgCl2, 0.544 g of KH2PO4, 4.766 g of Hepes, and 2.2365 g of KCl. The pH of artificial saliva was adjusted to 7 using KOH [24].

Evaluating vickers’ microhardness

Before and after the erosive-abrasive cycle, the Vickers tester was used to measure the surface hardness of the enamel samples (Wilson Hardness, ITW Test & Measurement GmbH, Shanghai, China). After applying a diamond indenter (25-gf force) perpendicular to the enamel surface for 15 s and at the center of the sample, the Vickers microhardness values for each specimen were digitally recorded. Calculations were made based on an average of three indentations per specimen. After the erosive-abrasive cycle, a second set of three indentations was made in the same manner as described earlier but positioned approximately 200 μm to the right of the baseline indentations.

Statistical analysis

Sigma Plot recorded and analyzed the data. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentages) were used to summarize the information. Three-way ANOVA followed by Post-hoc Tukey test were used to compare the outcomes and to investigate the interaction between the time point and the type of toothpaste. In addition, paired t-test was used to compare the microhardness before vs. after in each group. One-way ANOVA was conducted as well to compare the groups despite the main three variables. A P-value of < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

No differences were found between CPP-ACP and fluoridated toothpastes. In addition, no significant differences were observed when the different waiting times before brushing were compared to each other (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Consumption of acidic drinks followed by toothbrushing can affect the integrity of enamel, reducing its microhardness and increasing susceptibility to surface loss. In this study, we observed no differences between fluoridated and CPP-ACP toothpastes in protecting the enamel microhardness following erosive-abrasive challenge. Therefore, the null hypothesis has been accepted. The results of this study also highlight that the time of brushing following the erosive challenge is not a determinant factor in modulating the enamel microhardness. As a result, it might not be significant to ask people to wait for a certain time or use a specific toothpaste after consuming acidic beverages.

Several studies have investigated the optimal time interval for brushing after exposure to erosive drinks. Ganss et al. found that delaying brushing for 60min after a Coca-Cola challenge resulted in significantly higher enamel microhardness compared to brushing immediately after [25]. Similarly, Aykut-Yetkiner et al. reported that waiting 30min before brushing led to greater enamel protection than brushing right away [26]. In contrast, Lussi et al. recommended brushing immediately after an erosive challenge, as delayed brushing (60 min) did not provide additional benefits [27],, which is comparable to this study’s results. These conflicting findings suggest that the ideal time interval may depend on factors such as the specific drink, brushing technique, and individual variations in enamel composition and response to erosion. Subsequently, it might be crucial for clinical recommendations to be individualized. Dentists may consider a patient’s specific caries risk profile when advising on the optimal time interval for brushing after exposure to erosive drinks. Modifying these recommendations can enhance enamel protection and improve overall oral health outcomes.

In situ studies demonstrated that 1 and 2-hour exposure to saliva prior to toothbrushing can protect the tooth from abrasion and reharden the enamel surface [28–30]. However, it was suggested in another investigation that using fluoridated toothpaste and mouthwash can achieve enamel protection even if they were administered immediately following the erosion challenge [31]. This may imply that using remineralizing toothpaste can substitute the impact of the waiting period and saliva. Fluoride has the potential to interact with dental hard tissues in various ways, influenced by factors such as its concentration, the specific agent utilized, the duration of application, and the pH level of the surrounding environment [32, 33]. It can substitute hydroxyl ions in hydroxyapatite through an ion exchange process, leading to the formation of less soluble mineral variants like fluorapatite or fluorohydroxyapatite [34, 35]. Alternatively, fluoride can adhere to the surface of hydroxyapatite, creating a non-specific adsorbed fluoride layer. This layer is thought to enhance the availability of fluoride ions, thereby decreasing the rate of hydroxyapatite demineralization [36]. Besides, in vitro investigations found that fluoride can interfere with Streptococcus mutans capabilities to produce acid and synthesize glucan [37].

Another remineralizing agent that gains attention is CPP-ACP. Casein phosphopeptide (CPP) forms nanoclusters with amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP), creating a reservoir of calcium and phosphate that helps maintain the supersaturation of saliva [38, 39]. Because CPP–ACP can stabilize calcium and phosphate in solution, it also contributes to buffering the pH levels in dental plaque, leading to an increase in calcium and phosphate concentrations within the plaque itself. Consequently, this elevated concentration of calcium and phosphate in subsurface lesions is sustained, promoting the process of remineralization [38, 39]. In addition, calcium and phosphate ions were found to except antibacterial properties against oral pathogens [40, 41], which can contribute in the prevention of biofilm-triggered diseases. As a result, The use of remineralizing agents containing CPP-ACP, like MI Paste, has been investigated to protect the enamel from erosive challenges.

One study investigated the effects of varying time intervals for applying CPP-ACP toothpaste following exposure to erosive beverages without including a comparison group of fluoridated toothpaste. Poggio et al. found that applying CPP-ACP toothpaste immediately after a Coca-Cola challenge helped maintain enamel microhardness, whereas delaying the application by 60min offered less protection [42]. This was the opposite of our results, as more enamel softening was observed following immediate brushing with CPP-ACP toothpaste. In other times, the application of CPP-ACP toothpaste demonstrated superior protection for the enamel surface, suggesting that CPP-ACP toothpaste is effective in mitigating enamel erosion under most conditions. These mixed results suggest that the optimal timing for applying remineralizing agents like CPP-ACP toothpaste may depend on the specific erosive challenge, as well as individual variations in enamel composition and susceptibility to erosion.

Although no statistically significant differences were found between fluoridated and CPP-ACP toothpastes, it is important to note that, in general, CPP-ACP toothpaste offers superior protection compared to its fluoridated equivalent. When before and after microhardness values were compared, the microhardness was significantly reduced in all the time points following the erosive-abrasive challenge, while this happened at only two time points when CPP-ACP toothpaste was applied. It is evident that both fluoride and CPP-ACP have different mechanisms in remineralizing hard dental tissues [43]. In addition to their remineralization capabilities, CPP-ACP may provide supplementary benefits via increasing the absorption of fluoride when available from other sources [34]. However, it is too early to conclude if one of these agents is superior to the others, as more randomized clinical trials are needed for further investigation.

The primary biological factor in preventing erosion is saliva [44, 45]. It plays a crucial role by forming an acquired pellicle on the surfaces of teeth. Saliva possesses buffering abilities, allowing it to dilute, wash away, and neutralize acids. Additionally, it supplies essential minerals like calcium and phosphate, which help decrease the rate of demineralization and promote the process of remineralization [44, 45]. However, it is important to recognize that the protective effect of saliva is limited, as the erosive challenge can easily remove the salivary pellicle [10]. As a result, it has been suggested that the use of fluoridated and remineralizing products can provide better protection than saliva [31]. Consequently, it is advisable not to delay toothbrushing after consuming acidic beverages. This recommendation is supported by a recent scoping review, which indicates that brushing immediately after an erosive challenge is linked to a lower risk of enamel softening [6]. While the review primarily focused on fluoridated toothpaste, the findings of this study suggest that the same advice could apply to the use of CPP-ACP toothpaste.

It is essential to recognize that, in addition to the toothpaste itself and the abrasiveness of the bristles, factors such as salivary pH, and toothpastes’ ingredients may play critical roles in this process. Saliva neutralizes acids produced by harmful bacteria and dietary sources, with a higher saliva pH helping to buffer these acids and facilitate the remineralization of enamel using minerals such as calcium and phosphate [46]. Salivary pH should be considered in future in vitro or in situ studies that investigate the erosion and abrasion of hard dental tissues. Another crucial aspect is the ingredients in toothpaste. Ideally, studies should test toothpastes with identical formulations that differ only in their active ingredients, as this would allow for a more precise comparison between fluoride and CPP-ACP regarding their effectiveness in protecting enamel against erosion and abrasion.

To summarize, this study yields several important conclusions. While both erosive challenges and toothpaste usage are key factors in enamel softening, delayed brushing appears to have no effect on enamel integrity. Furthermore, no significant difference was found between fluoridated and CPP-ACP toothpastes, with CPP-ACP toothpaste providing better protection except after immediate brushing. It is essential to note that this study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a laboratory setting using extracted teeth. Although in vitro studies are valuable for standardizing various parameters, they do not allow for direct comparisons to clinical environments. As such, the findings require validation through randomized clinical trials. Second, the study examined only a limited range of erosive beverages and toothpastes. It is likely that different erosive agents and a wider variety of toothpaste formulations could yield varying results. Third, this study focused on toothpastes with low abrasivity, which are recommended for dental patients. However, the abrasivity of different toothpastes may lead to varying results, as more abrasive formulas can compromise enamel integrity, resulting in greater surface loss. Finally, the ingredients and pH levels of the toothpastes could affect enamel hardness. Additionally, using human saliva instead of artificial saliva will resemble the clinical settings, especially that sodium monofluorophosphate necessitates enzymatic hydrolysis by salivary enzymes to release fluoride [47].

Conclusion

This study revealed that both fluoridated and CPP-ACP toothpastes are effective in protecting erosive-abrasive enamel softening, showing no significant differences in their efficacy. Additionally, delaying toothbrushing did not provide any protective benefits for enamel following exposure to the erosive-abrasive model. These findings should be confirmed through randomized clinical trials for further validation.
