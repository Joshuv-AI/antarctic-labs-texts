Genetic differentiation of the supralittoral gastropod Tectarius striatus (North Atlantic Archipelagos) and development of new microsatellite resources
Authors: Frederik Feldmann, Manuel Curto, Livia Sinigaglia, Cátia Alves, Claudia Sacchetti, António Múrias dos Santos, Patrícia Madeira, Sérgio P Ávila, Harald Meimberg, Lara Baptista
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrosatellite markers are invaluable tools for assessing genetic diversity and elucidating population structure across any species. This study reports the development and application of ten novel polymorphic microsatellite loci for Tectarius striatus, a littorinid species native to the shores of Macaronesia, a geographical region that includes the archipelagos of the Azores, Madeira, Selvagens, Canary Islands, and Cabo Verde. These markers together with a portion of the COI gene were used to genotype 65 individuals, using Illumina amplicon -sequencing across five geographically distinct populations. Our analysis shows moderate to high levels of allelic diversity across all populations. Furthermore, microsatellite markers supported genetic structure between a distant population of Cabo Verde Archipelago and the northern Macaronesian archipelagos. Conversely variation of the COI showed high levels of homogeneity across the sampled populations. While the presence of null alleles and moderate levels of missing data at several loci represent challenges to this study, the overall consistency of our results with earlier research underscores the reliability of microsatellite markers for population genetic inference in this marine gastropod. Nevertheless, our findings highlight the need for cautious interpretation of diversity estimates and population structure metrics, particularly when null alleles are frequent, and underscore the value of expanding the panel of available microsatellite markers for Tectarius striatus and related taxa to improve resolution and accuracy for future studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12862-026-02545-5.Keywords: Microsatellite markers, Tectarius striatus, Macaronesian archipelagos, Diversity estimates, Population structure metrics

Introduction

Tectarius striatus [1], formerly known as Littorina striata, is a periwinkle of the family Littorinidae endemic to the supralittoral of rocky shorelines of the NE Atlantic archipelagos, which include the Azores (AZO), Madeira (MAD), Selvagens (SEL), Canary Islands (CAN), and Cabo Verde (CAB). This species was also reported from São Tomé Island (São Tomé and Príncipe Archipelago) by Lamy [2] but, according to Reid [3], it was misidentified as Echinolittorina granosa [4].

As a species with planktotrophic larval development, free-swimming feeding larvae of T. striatus remain in the pelagic zone until undergoing metamorphosis and settling onto benthic substrate, a process that can take several weeks but has only been documented in other littorinid species such as Littorina littorea [5, 6]. During this period, they are subjected to marine current regimes that act as primary drivers of their dispersal, affecting their population dynamics [7–9]. This also allows for long-distance dispersal (Avila et al. 2009 [10]). Motility during the larval stage differs across phyla and life stages and can catalyse the dispersal range of some species more than that of others, as reported for several littorinid species by Montgomery et al. [11].

The water circulation system in the North Atlantic, with a variety of major and minor currents is also a key factor for the movement of planktonic larvae across the North Atlantic Archipelagos [12]. However, ecological constraints arising from the differences between mean surface temperatures across different climate zones may prevent successful episodes of dispersal and colonization. These archipelagos span a broad geographic range (14°N to 39°N, and 32°W to 13°W), separated by approximately 2,500 between the Azores (AZO) and Cabo Verde (CAB) [13]. The North Atlantic Drift is a major facilitator of connectivity between populations of planktotrophic species, as it transports warmer waters from the Caribbean region northwards via the Gulf Stream, reaching the Azores in the central Atlantic and southwards to other NE Atlantic regions including the Canary Islands, Selvagens or Madeira, generally in a clockwise direction [14]. In contrast, the Canary Current, a cold-water current flowing southward along the coast of Africa before reaching the Canary Islands, also plays a crucial role in the dispersal of marine fauna [15].

This dispersibility of organisms with planktotrophic larvae enables colonization across long distances, facilitating gene-flow between populations of different islands and even archipelagos, often preventing genetic differentiation (as shown by [6]). This is also facilitated by the dynamic nature of the oceanographic conditions surrounding the NE Atlantic Archipelagos, as localized eddies and gyres change the direction of main currents, directly influencing larval transport [16]. Oceanic circulation patterns play a key role in species distribution and therefore in shaping marine biodiversity, affecting levels of genetic connectivity and contributing to different patterns of population structures (cf. [17–21]). As a result, oceanic circulation not only supports species survival in times of global change, but also helps maintain the ecological resilience of marine ecosystems [22].

Tectarius striatus is reported from the fossil record of last interglacial deposits from all Macaronesian archipelagos (thus including the AZO, MAD, SEL, CAN, and CAB [23]). Nonetheless, a phylogenetic tree of Littorinidae, calibrated from fossil and geological evidence, indicated that the diversification of this species took root from 44 to 32 Ma, coinciding with the Eocene-Oligocene [24]. De Wolf et al. [25] proposed that CAB served as the centre from which T. striatus colonized other NE Atlantic archipelagos. Assuming that the planktonic stage of T. striatus is too short to permit long-distance dispersal along the prevailing clockwise currents without intermediate stepping-stone populations [26], the combined effects of current directionality and geographic distance likely limit larval exchange, thereby reinforcing an oceanographic barrier to gene flow between Cabo Verde and the northern Macaronesian archipelagos [27, 28].

Tectarius striatus species shows morphological indications of differentiation across its geographic distribution. This littorinid species displays considerable phenotypic variations in shell sculpture, shape and size, likely related to environmental and geographical factors. For example, shells found near wave exposed shores are generally larger in size, more globose, have larger apertures and lack external sculpture, whereas those from sheltered sites are smaller, less globose, have smaller apertures and are more nodulous [29]. Compared with other Macaronesian Archipelagos, shells from CAB are larger, heavier, have large apertures and exhibit greater morphological variance [30]. Considering these morphological variations, we hypothesize that the variations should be visible on a molecular level and a higher genetic diversity in CAB is expected. Genetic structure might correlate with morphological differences between population, as suggested by the first population genetic studies done on T. striatus from MAD and CAN using allozyme and RAPD [31]. However, following population genetic studies focused on AZO and CAB did not find populational structure patterns matching to the above-mentioned shell variation [6]. Instead, populations from CAB displayed a higher number of private alleles, more electrophoretic esterase bands, and greater heterozygosity, suggesting weak genetic differentiation and slightly higher diversity compared to other regions [25, 31]. Detailed genetic differentiation patterns among may T. striatus populations have been overlooked by these previous works due to the limited capacity of the molecular approaches used to capture variation at the population level.

The use of highly polymorphic microsatellite markers allows genetic differentiation patterns among T. striatus populations to be re-evaluated at higher resolution. Microsatellites, as codominant nuclear markers, are particularly suitable for assessing population structure, due to their high polymorphism and ability to unambiguously distinguish between heterozygous and homozygous genotypes [32]. This enables precise estimation of allele frequencies, genetic diversity and contemporary gene flow, as well as detection of subtle population subdivision. In contrast to commonly used mitochondrial markers, which are maternally inherited and often reflect historical demographic processes, nuclear microsatellites provide a biparentally inherited and higher-resolution view of recent connectivity among populations. Still, we included population genetic analysis using the COI gene for better comparison, potentially highlighting the differences between both marker types. Previous attempts to apply microsatellite genotyping in marine gastropods have encountered technical challenges, especially during s DNA extraction and amplification, leading to high sample dropout rates, likely due to low DNA quality and the presence of PCR inhibitors [33]. Nevertheless, microsatellites markers have been developed for littorinid species, such as Littoraria sinensis [4, 34], but this approach has not yet been attempted with Tectarius striatus.

Genotype-by-Amplicon-Sequencing (GBAS) approach the use of amplicon sequencing data to produce genotype information can be combined with microsatellites in what is known as SSR-GBAS [35]. By using high throughput sequencing platforms, like Illumina sequencing, SSR-GBAS can cost-effectively simultaneously genotype of a large number of loci across many individuals [35]. Contrary to traditional microsatellite genotyping, GBAS defines variants based on all variation captured by an amplicon. This reduces the impact of length homoplasy, characteristic to microsatellites, while increasing the number of alleles, thereby enhancing information content and therefore resolution of population genetic analyses [35, 36]. It is also a highly automated approach which minimizes human error and biases in the identification of alleles [36, 37]. Therefore, the SSR-GBAS (Microsatellite Genotype by Amplicon Sequencing) has the potential to reveal overlook genetic patterns for populations of T. striatus.

This study aims to contribute to the improvement of the understanding of population connectivity in a planktotrophic marine species across the NE Atlantic archipelagos by assessing genetic differentiation among insular populations of T. striatus. Given the large geographic distance and the potential influence of ocean currents on dispersal, the use of polymorphic, codominant nuclear markers is particularly advantageous for detecting fine-scale population structure and evaluating contemporary connectivity patterns. In this context, we aim to develop a set of microsatellite markers compatible with the GBAS approach. While doing this we explore some of the challenges related to marker development and consequently implementation given the difficulty in extracting high quality DNA for littorinid gastropod species.

Methods

Sample collection and DNA isolation

A total of 65 specimens from 5 locations at 4 different archipelagos were used for this study, directly collected from the rocky intertidal areas of the islands of Santa Maria (AZO), Porto Santo (MAD), Fuerteventura (CAN), and Sal (CAB). To ensure no degradation of the gDNA, most of the individuals were stored in salt after a few hours of being collected from the sampling sites and brought to the laboratory alive. In addition, samples collected in 2019 on Flores Island (AZO) by the Department of Biology of the University of Azores (DBUA), stored in 96% ethanol, were also included in the study. A full list of specimens is available in Table S1.

DNA from live individuals was extracted 1–3 weeks after being frozen using a protocol incorporating an CTAB-based buffer with proteinase K (Supplementary Material 1). For samples preserved in ethanol, the lysis step was complemented by chloroform + isoamylalcohol (24:1) extraction using 400 μl on 1048 μl lysate to remove excess of antimicrobial proteins and mucopolysaccharides. This approach improved DNA yield from ethanol-preserved samples. To evaluate the integrity of the gDNA extracted, an electrophoretic run was performed at 100 V for 20 minutes in 1.5% agarose gel. Extracts with visible DNA in the agarose gel were used for genotyping.

Microsatellite marker discovery

A genomic library was prepared using a sample from São Jorge Island of the Azores Archipelago and shot-gun sequenced with low coverage with Illumina paired-end (600 cycles) MiSeq run. This was conducted as a service at the Genomics Service Unit, Ludwig-Maximilians Universität (LMU), Munich, Germany.

To identify sequences containing SSR-GBAS, the protocol developed by Curto et al. [35] was used. The programmes FastQC v0.11.9 [38], Trimmomatic v0.39 [39], Usearch v11 [40] and the SSR_pipeline’s script SSR_search.py [41] S, were used for evaluating read quality, trimming and filtering low-quality regions and adapters, merging paired reads, and identifying SSR-containing sequences, respectively. All sequences selected contained at least 6, 5 and 4 repeats for tri-, tetra- and pentanucleotides motifs, respectively. A minimum of 30 bp flanking regions on each side of the motif was considered for primer design. Sequences containing multiple microsatellite motifs, incomplete motifs or mononucleotide stretches longer than 6 bp were not further considered. Primers were designed using Primer3 tool [42] as implemented in Geneious v2022.2.2 [43] due to this program user friendly interface and ability to automate primer design for large volumes of sequence data. Primer design was done with the following settings: Amplicon length = 400–500 bp; Primer size = 18–20; Tm = 52–57 °C; %GC = 20–80. A total of 39 primers pairs were designed (Supplementary Table S2) and tested on a sample from Santa Maria Island, Azores, in PCR reactions of 10 μl containing 5 μl of QIAGEN Multiplex PCR Master Mix (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany), 2 μl of the Primer Mix (1 μl of each primer in concentration of 1 nM, diluted 1:100) and 0.5 μl of gDNA diluted in proportions of 1:10. PCR was conducted using the following temperature: a long initial denaturation of 15 minutes at 95 °C given the hotstart nature of the polymerase used, 30 cycles of 30 seconds at 95 °C for denaturation, 1 minute at 52–56 °C for annealing, 1 minute at 72 °C for extension and 10 minutes at 72 °C for the final extension. The annealing temperature was adjusted between 52 °C and 56°C°C for primers that didn’t reach enough amplification at 55 °C.

Simple sequence repeat genotype by amplicon sequencing (SSR-GBAS)

Successfully generated amplicons were used for the SSR-GBAS protocol, and individual primer-pairs were tested on three samples from Sal Island (Cabo Verde Archipelago). Primer pairs producing clear and visible bands were subsequently used in single or multiplexed PCR reactions, depending on their quality (cf. Supplementary Table S1). PCR conditions for both multiplex and single reactions followed those described above for samples from Santa Maria Island, (Azores). Subsequent library preparation included PCR cleanup using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Inc., Bree, CA, USA) and indexing following Curto et al. [35]. Libraries were sequenced on a Illumina MiSeq (PE 300bp) at the Genomic Service Unit of LMU.

FastQ files were generated by the MiSeq system based on index combinations. Data was analysed using the GBAS GUI program (https://github.com/sonnenbe-dot/GBAS-GUI) that includes read quality control and merging, marker demultiplexing and genotype calling described by Curto et al. [35]. The genotyping process entails two steps that progressively reduce data complexity until the final variation is resolved. The first step follows a strategy similar to traditional microsatellite genotyping, in which amplicon length distributions are used to identify the most likely fragment sizes. In the second step, variation is characterized within each selected amplicon length category. Potential polymorphic sites are identified and used to define haplotypes. The most abundant haplotypes are considered true variants, taking ploidy constraints into account. When two amplicon length categories are identified in the first step, the most abundant haplotype within each category is retained. When only a single length category is present, up to two haplotypes may be retained. Finally, each haplotype is assigned an allele number, and the resulting information is recorded in a codominant matrix used for the following population genetics analysis as described in the next paragraph and sections. This method also allows to use the haplotype sequences resolved from the genotyping approach to sequence-based analyses. The effect of missing data in different parameters was explored using GenAlex v.6.5 [44]. For each locus, the percentage of missing data was plotted against measures of genetic diversity, including the number of alleles (Na), Shannon index (Sh), observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), and fixation index (Fi). Linear regressions were performed to evaluate the strength and significance of determination (R2) and associated p-values were calculated.

Impacts of missing data and prevalence of null alleles

Missing data per marker were compared with several diversity parameters, including the number of alleles, Shannon index, and expected and observed heterozygosity, as well as the inbreeding coefficient. Negative correlations are expected when missing data are associated with a high prevalence of null alleles, leading to an underestimation of genetic diversity. Conversely, a positive correlation is expected with the inbreeding coefficient. Genetic diversity parameters and the inbreeding coefficient per marker were calculated using GenAlEx v6.5 [44], while correlation coefficients and their significance were computed in R v4.5.2. The proportion of null alleles per population was estimated using FreeNa [45] and the R package “PopGenReport” utilizing a method by Brookfield [46].

Population genetics analysis

Samples and markers with more than 55% missing data were removed from the analysis. This is above the usual cutoff but necessary for gastropod species, since a high number of missing data has often been an issue for testing the reliability of the designed primers [47]. GenAlex v6.5 [44] was used to calculate genetic diversity parameters per population, such as number of alleles (Na), expected (He) and observed heterozygosity (Ho), plus Shannon’s mutual information index (I). The programme also allowed for measurements of deviations from Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) and inbreeding coefficient (F). Genepop v4.8.5 [48] wasused to estimate population divergence through pairwise FST.

Population structure patterns were primarily assessed through a Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) using codominant genotypic distances between the individuals as implemented in GenAlex v6.5. A Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) was also using the R package adegenet [49] on the five sampled locations across the four NE Atlantic archipelagos studied free from assumptions of HWE and linkage equilibrium. Such patterns were further evaluated using the software STRUCTURE v.2.3.4 [50], which estimates the probability of assigning each individual to a hypothetical population with assumptions of HWE and linkage equilibrium. The number of clusters (K) tested varied between 1 and 6, running for 10 different replicates for 100,000 generations after a burn-in period of 100,000 following the default settings of the programme. Following the methods of Evanno et al. [51], the results from STRUCTURE were graphically displayed using Microsoft Excel (version 365, Microsoft Cooperation). The optimum K-value was determined using StructureSelector [52].

Assessing mitochondrial variation

Variation at the mitochondrial level was also assessed by sequencing a 389 bp fragment of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I gene (COI). The primers LCO1490/HCO2198 [53] were adapted for amplicon sequencing by using primers elongated with part of the sequence of Illumina adapters as described in [35] and an annealing temperature of 55 °C. Illumina sequencing and initial processing followed the SSR-GBAS protocol, as described above. In this case we took the sequence of most abundant haplotype given the haploid inheritance of the mitochondrial genome. The resulting 76 sequences (Table S1) were combined with additional 75 sequences obtained from NCBI were added (Table S3) and aligned using Clustal W [54] as integrated in the programme BioEdit [55]. The alignment of 151 sequences, together with a trait table displaying the different geographical locations per archipelago, were used to generate a TCS haplotype network in PopART [56]. Since information about the islands were not always available, the analysis was carried out on an archipelagic level. To test for demographic changes complementary to the Haplotype Network, a Tajima’s D and a Fu FS test was conducted in DNASp v6.12.03 [57].

Results

Microsatellite marker set development

The marker discovery run resulted in 1,272,632 raw reads, of which 339 contained microsatellite motifs and were used to design 39 primer pairs. Amplification was tested in 5 samples with the ID codes “TSSC4B/6/33/5/32”: 9 markers worked for TSSC4B, 9 for TSSC6, 8 for TSSC33, 5 for TSSC5 and 3 for TSSC32. A total of 13 primer pairs successfully generated amplicons and were used for the SSR-GBAS protocol. The SSR-GBAS run produced 3,913,616 raw reads, of which 2,576,719 were used for genotyping. Of the 13 markers, 10 had a proportion of missing data below 55% and were used for further analysis (Table 1). From these, most (8) had a proportion of missing data of below 30%. A total of 65 individuals had genotype data for most markers and were used for population genetic analysis (Supplementary Table S1).

All markers showed overall high number of alleles (29 to 63), with seven out of 10 having more than 50 alleles (Table 1). Considering the total of 65 individuals genotyped this constitutes nearly one unique allele detected per individual for most markers. Accordingly, expected heterozygosity values were also elevated (0.78 to 0.97), with only one marker showing values below 0.9 (Ts34). Observed heterozygosity was much lower (<0.3 for most markers), which is also reflected by the high F values (>0.6) for most markers. The only exceptions correspond to the markers Ts10 and Ts15 that showed comparable He and Ho values. Interestingly, these were also the markers with the highest number of alleles (>60).

Cabo Verde showed the highest genetic diversity values across all estimated indices, except for F, which was the highest at Porto Santo (MAD; cf. Table 2). Conversely, Flores Island (AZO) displayed the lowest values, except for Ho which was the lowest in Porto Santo. Consistently, He was much higher than Ho, which is also reflected by positive F values indicating deviations from HWE that were further statistically confirmed (Table 3). Most loci showed significant deviations in populations MAD, AZO and CAB (p < 0.05), characterized by a deficit of heterozygotes. This result is congruent with the high portion of null alleles (>0.2) detected for most loci and populations (Table 3). However, the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium tests also revealed that most loci in CAN did not deviate significantly from expectations (p > 0.05).

Effects of missing data in genetic diversity inferences

Diversity measures decline as the amount of missing data increases (Fig. 1), which is displayed Na with R2 = 0.85, p < 0.001, Sh with R2 = 0.94, p < 0.001, Ho, R2 = 0.61, p = 0.007, and He with R2 = 0.91, p < 0.001. Conversely, Fi showed he opposite pattern (R2 = 0.61, p = 0.007).

Scatter plots of missing data (x-axis) against diversity indices (y-axis): number of alleles (Na), Shannon Index (Sh), observed heterozygosity (Ho), fixation Index (Fi), and expected heterozygosity (He)

Observed heterozygosity declined sharply from 0.800 to 0.300 with a 0.1 increase in missing data, indicating a downward bias in genetic diversity estimates. Interestingly, this pattern is less pronounced for expected heterozygosity. This shows that loci with higher missing data exhibit a much lower Ho and He, which is consistent with the expectations for loci affected by null alleles and confirmed by the high portion of null alleles estimated (Table 3).

Genetic structure of Tectarius striatus across North Atlantic Archipelagos

Pairwise FST values were low (FST < 0.1; cf. Table 4), with Cabo Verde presenting the highest levels of differentiation relative to the other populations. Discussion

Impediment during marker development

A total of 13 microsatellites genotyped by amplicon sequencing were developed, providing further insights into previous studies on population structure in T. striatus utilizing mitochondrial markers across the Macaronesian archipelagos (e.g. [6]). While several biological and technical factors, as discussed below, added complexity to our population analysis, they offered valuable insights.

Marker development was limited due to the stringent filtering criteria. From the 39 newly designed primer sets only 13 showed amplification products in the first test. These were reduced to 10 by filtering based on the threshold o 55% missing data. Although standard practices of excluding primers above 30% of missing data are usually followed claiming a higher bias in estimating population differentiation with higher frequencies of null alleles [45], we increased the threshold, since in the face of poor genotyping efficiency, rigid filtering rules can result in non-random distribution of missing data and or null alleles [58].

The heterogeneity in DNA quality may have impacted genotyping results. As shown in previous studies, it is vital to use fresh samples or samples stored in liquid nitrogen to extract DNA in pristine quality. This is especially important when working with gastropods, as they exhibit mucopolysaccharides in their epidermis that co-precipitate with gDNA, increasing the sample viscosity during extraction [59]. This viscosity usually hinders enzyme activity and impedes the proper homogenization of mixtures [60, 61], exacerbating missing data and allele dropout.

The inclusion of full amplicon sequence variation in SSR-GBAS increases the number of detectable alleles, resulting in highly polymorphic loci. While this may complicate the identification of allele-sharing patterns among individuals and populations in certain analytical frameworks, it can also provide increased resolution for detecting genetic structure when appropriately implemented [62]. The SSR-GBAS approach calls alleles based on all variation captured by an amplicon thus increasing the number of alleles retrieved per marker [35]. While this may enhance the detection of diversity in less polymorphic loci, it may conversely reduce resolution in hypervariable regions.

The high number of alleles might result from a combination of technical issues, such as designing primers amplifying high-copy regions that were overseen due to the low quality of the shotgun sequencing data from low-quality DNA isolate. Conversely, the hypervariable nature of microsatellite loci has been described in multiple groups of molluscs (e.g., the cephalopods, Sepia officinalis in Pérez-Losada et al. [63], and Loligo pealeii in Maxwell et al. [64]; and the bivalve Perna viridis in Lukehurst et al. [65]) and thus it may also have a biological origin as well. Large population sizes, overlapping populations, and long-term large stable population sizes that allow for accumulation of mutations might lie at the base of what has been called hypervariability for these systems. On a smaller scale, populations of damselfish across 1,000 km within the Caribbean have also shown high gene flow among populations, but singular populations display self-recruitment patterns indicating temporal and spatial sweepstakes events [66]. Another explanation would be the establishment of highly diver populations as a response to low larval survival rate in marine species [67] carrying unique allele combinations.

Excess of null alleles

We detected a high prevalence of null alleles across all markers. Furthermore, we show that missing data are associated with an underestimation of genetic diversity and likely deviations HWE. We hypothesize that this underestimation of diversity is caused by the high prevalence of null alleles, which can lead to heterozygotes being misclassified as homozygotes. However, we could not directly test this relationship, as it would require comparing genetic diversity estimates between datasets with and without markers exhibiting high proportions of null alleles. In the present dataset, all markers showed substantial levels of null alleles, preventing such a comparison.

It has also been shown that high frequencies of null alleles at microsatellite loci are common among bivalve species and do not appear to have a significant effect on the population genetic parameters commonly assessed [68]. Since gastropods are in the same phylum as bivalves, this might also be the case for Tectarius striatus.

The high occurrence of null alleles can also be an aspect of the hypervariability because the result from genetic variations among individuals due to mutations in primer-binding sites are hindering primer annealing during PCR and therefore leading to non-amplification of those alleles [69]. High allelic variability may increase the occurrence of null alleles, potentially due to mutations in primer binding regions, thereby leading to higher levels of missing data [58]. This was also an issue during marker optimization, since many developed markers were excluded because of a presumed poor amplification success. In follow up work, a higher number of initial samples to test amplification should be used to accommodate high dropout rates.

Low information content due to high levels of diversity and low sampling sizes

Variation of COI showed high levels of haplotype sharing with no apparent population structure in our study. This is congruent to a study by Van den Broeck et al. [6] describing high values of nucleotide and haplotype diversity. Considering the high population density, dispersal ability and the low survival rate of marine larvae, the observed variability may simply indicate that the majority of the analysed individuals came from a single panmictic population. Our results also correlate with a former study of mitochondrial DNA in another littorinid – Melarhaphe neritoides [70] – displaying high rates of diversity [71]. However, further interpretation must be taken carefully, since these high levels of diversity may be linked to a high mutation rate of the CoI for this group.

This species high levels of diversity may be obscuring underlying genetic patterns detectable by highly polymorphic markers like microsatellites. For many markers, we observed an extraordinarily high number of alleles, often approaching one unique allele per individual. This high allelic diversity resulted in minimal allele sharing among individuals and, consequently, limited the ability to detect population genetic structure.

This effect is further compounded by the relatively small sample size, which constrains accurate allele frequency estimation and limits the statistical power to resolve subtle genetic differentiation. Consequently, the absence of clear population structure should be interpreted cautiously, and the possibility that existing population structure remains undetected cannot be excluded. However, despite this hypervariability and limited sampling size, we still managed to detect a differentiation for CAB in comparison to the northern archipelagos, suggesting that major population structure is detectable.

Despite the high variability of microsatellites relative to mtDNA [58], both markers show limited differentiation among northern archipelagos but consistent divergence of Cabo Verde. The congruence between both marker systems with different rates of evolution,impacted by hypervariability in different degrees, indicates the possibility that there might behigh geneflow among the studied populations. The combination of markers impacted by different biological processes, as done for T. striatus in this study, is important since it provides complementarity. Specifically, microsatellites due to their co-dominant nature can distinguish between homozygous and heterozygous genotypes, while able to differentiate among individuals and populations. Mitochondria markers due to their lower mutation rate can provide a more historical evolutionary perspective.

Populational structure and marine biogeography

The addition of a microsatellite dataset allowed us to disentangle some population structure for T. striatus, with the population of CAB showing some degree of differentiation towards the others. We do not have much information about T. striatus on a molecular level, since it has not been the subject of other genetic studies. Morphological differences in the width and height of their whorls, as well as shell lengths of individuals of different populations, have been spotted between the archipelagos, but may be dismissed as phenotypic plasticity, as they were not reflected in the molecular analysis.

Notably, and despite comparable geographic distances (~1,200 km), substantial gene flow occurs between CAN, MAD and the AZO archipelagos, but not as much between CAN and CAB. The biogeographical isolation of CAB in relation to the remaining Macaronesian archipelagos is related to the Canaries Current and the Cabo Verde Front, which nowadays act as an effective biogeographic barrier/filter for the dispersal of marine species [72, 73]. This barrier/filter is hypothesized to diminish during “windows of opportunity” that take place in the ending of glacial terminations/beginning of interglacials [74–81], thus allowing for the northward range expansion of Cabo Verdean species to higher latitudes. This short-time, intermittent communication, with long periods of no gene flow between CAB and the remaining Macaronesian archipelagos helps explain the populational structure patterns that we detect and that are visible in Figs. 2B and 3. This isolation of CAB was precisely one of the grounds to restructure Macaronesia region, with the resulting AZO ecoregion, the Webbnesia ecoregion (with MAD, SEL and CAN archipelagos), and the CAB biogeographic subprovince (cf. [12]).

Cabo Verde also showed the highest number of unique haplotypes, which was already reported by Van den Broeck et al. [6], further supporting a lower degree of gene flow involving this archipelago. Considering the wide geographic distances between CAB and the remaining Macaronesian archipelagos [13], this can be expected. Therefore, our results support the role of the Cabo Verde Front as a biogeographical barrier for the dispersal of marine organisms [12].

Since T. striatus is confined to the NE Atlantic islands, no outgroup population on the continental shores could be used to verify the current population structure. The low FST values, clustering overlap in PCA and DAPC analyses, and cluster sharing among most populations could indicate high levels of gene flow across archipelagos. However, it is more likely that it is a product of the low number of genetically analyzed specimens. Considering the predominantly southward circulation and the distances between the archipelagos, especially CAB and AZO, it is unlikely that dispersal occurs within one generation during the larval stage, especially with an estimated travel distance of 15 km/day [16]. High connectivity, indicated through the low FST values, across the NE Atlantic is congruent with the findings of Freitas et al. [12], suggesting the role of island chains as stepping-stones in facilitating gene flow among populations of species with planktotrophic larvae across the NE Atlantic. It suggests long distance dispersal via archipelago and seamount chains, despite short larval life-stage and southwards currents. Seamounts are hypothesized to have played a crucial role in facilitating transoceanic dispersal during different geological periods, either in a previous island topology or emerging above sea level during glacial periods [82, 83]. Ávila et al. [84] provided evidence for the range expansion of 22 cold-temperate marine molluscs (12 gastropods and 10 bivalves species) towards CAB in glacial times, with a probable origin from CAN. This southward dispersal took advantage on several seamounts situated between CAN and CAB which, during glacial times, could be (at least some of them) islands [85]. In fact, some of the obtained results mirror a recent expansion. The star-shaped outline of the mtDNA haplotype network (Fig. 5) could indicate a recent and rapid expansion of T. striatus from a bottlenecked population and further supported by highly negative Tajima’s D and Fu’s FS values for COI. Similar observations were made for black-lip oysters by Li et al. [86].

Cabo Verde exhibits the most genetic diversity and could be the source for the populations distributed through the NE Atlantic, as hypothesized by Van den Broeck et al. [6]. This could lead us to a possible explanation that T. striatus could have originated in one archipelago and recently spread across the remaining archipelagos. However, such interpretations should be taken with caution. The fossil record cannot help on this matter, as T. striatus is reported from the late Pleistocene Marine Isotopic Substage 5e (MIS 5e, i.e., 128–116 ka) from all five Macaronesian archipelagos [79]. Moreover, the Canary Islands were under-sampled compared to the remaining archipelagos, which may have resulted in the underestimation of genetic diversity. Furthermore, little differences in genetic diversity were found across the NE Atlantic archipelagos, indicating a lack of founder effect that is usually associated with an expansion process. The high dispersal capacity of the larvae, which can remain in that stage for more than 15 days [87], could have ensured that genetic diversity is spread across the NE Atlantic.

Conclusions

Despite the limitations of the approach, this study gives valuable insights into the challenges of working with microsatellites, highlighting areas for future improvements and incorporating them in the prospect of revising standard methods of excluding primers based on proportion of missing data. It underlines the technical difficulties of DNA isolation in gastropods, since the antimicrobial proteins and mucopolysaccharides often interfere with proper DNA extraction and the lack of data on null alleles in SSR-GBAS datasets. Nonetheless, this study still provides valuable insights into the genetic diversity of T. striatus across the NE Atlantic archipelagos with microsatellites and COI markers suggesting high diversity, low differentiation between northern archipelagos and consistent differentiation of CAB, accordant with oceanic barriers and previous studies of mtDNA. This is similar to trends of previous studies [6, 12] and raises the question if the high amount of null alleles being found displays high variability between the individuals studied, or if it is also a product of the low larval survival rates. Continued development of SSR markers, combined with increased sampling and contemporary modelling approaches would improve the resolution of the genetic diversity of T. striatus. Increased sample size and coverage are particularly important for reliably inferring the species’ evolutionary and biogeographic history in Macaronesia during the Quaternary .
