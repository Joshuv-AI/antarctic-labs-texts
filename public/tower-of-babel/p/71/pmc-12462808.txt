Cingulate and frontopolar cortical projections to the cerebellar vermis support prolonged reaction time in identifying negative emotional scenes in women
Authors: Hak Kei Wong, Shefali Chaudhary, Yu Chen, Jaime S Ide, Sheng Zhang, Chiang-Shan R Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe previously observed sex differences in the association of individual anxiety and reaction time (RT) during identification of negative emotional scenes in a Hariri task. Prolonged RT, an attention marker, in identifying negative (vs. neutral) images correlated with anxiety level in women but not in men. However, the neural circuit that supports this behavioral observation remains unclear. Here, with a larger sample (64 men and 62 women), we employed whole-brain regression on individual differences in RT during matching negative vs. neutral images or RT (negative – neutral) and evaluated the results at a corrected threshold. Women but not men showed a significant correlation between individual anxiety and RT (negative – neutral), with a slope test confirming the sex difference. In women alone the cerebellar vermis showed activity in positive correlation with RT (negative – neutral). Further, Granger causality mapping (GCM) showed multiple brain regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex/frontopolar cortex (ACC/FPC), that provide inputs to the cerebellar vermis in women. Amongst these regions, only the ACC/FPC cluster showed activity (β) in significant correlation with both STAI State score and RT (negative – neutral) in women. GCM also identified a small cluster in the pons, suggesting that the cortical pontine cerebellar circuit may support prolonged RT during identification of negative emotions. Path analyses further characterized the inter-relationships amongst the neural markers, RT, and anxiety. These findings highlight a behavioral and circuit marker of anxiety state in neurotypical women. Studies with different behavioral paradigms are needed to characterize the behavioral and neural mechanisms of male anxiety.Keywords: Negative emotion; Sex difference; Hariri task, reaction time, cortico-pontine-cerebellar loop

Introduction

Anxiety and negative emotion processing

Dysfunctional negative emotion processing is a hallmark of anxiety state and anxiety disorders, affecting approximately 4 % of the global population (Javaid et al., 2023). Many studies have combined behavioral paradigms and functional brain imaging to identify these neural processes. For instance, a recent meta-analysis of over 300 patients with anxiety disorders and 300 controls from 20 studies demonstrated elevated activation in core cortical regions of default mode and frontal-parietal networks during negative emotion processing as well as sex and age differences in these regional activities (Wang et al., 2024). The authors highlighted the importance of functional disturbances in self-reference and cognitive control as a neural mechanism of anxiety disorders. This along with other meta-analyses and reviews of the literature of fear learning, anticipation of aversive stimuli, and the processing and regulation of emotion (Akiki et al., 2025; Servaas et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2019) elucidates the neural markers of anxiety and suggests potential targets of behavioral treatment for people with anxiety disorders. A more recent work employed a hidden Markov model to identify brain activation states during video watching in adolescents (Camacho et al., 2024). With the videos annotated for emotion-specific and nonspecific information and the moment-to-moment probability of being in each brain state, authors showed higher probability of staying in the ventral attention and default mode activation state and lower probability in cingulo-opercular network activation state in individuals with higher levels of generalized anxiety (Camacho et al., 2024). Together, these imaging studies implicate attention, executive control, and default mode circuits in emotion processing and the pathophysiology of anxiety.

Behavioral correlates of negative emotion processing – a marker of individual anxiety?

Emotions influence an individual’s task performance by modulating the allocation of attention (Pedale et al., 2019; Pessoa, 2010). In tasks where emotional stimuli are presented as distractors, RT tend to be prolonged in response to negative relative to neutral emotions (Lu et al., 2021). An earlier study too suggested that low-arousal, negatively vs. positively valenced distractors led to slower responses (Lu et al., 2017). In another study using an emotional stop-signal task, both go and stop-signal RT were prolonged during negative compared to neutral trials (Kalanthroff et al., 2013). These findings suggest that negative stimuli capture attention more strongly, interfering with task performance. Further, negative images draw attention for a longer duration than neutral images in individuals with high anxiety and bias in total viewing time towards negative images increased with higher levels of trait anxiety (Veerapa et al., 2020). Another study found that individuals with higher anxiety showed not only greater initial attention to, but also greater difficulty disengaging from negative information (Rudaizky et al., 2014). Collectively, the findings suggest that heightened attention to negative emotional stimuli and reduced disengagement may plausibly influence performance on tasks involving negative emotion processing in relation to individual anxiety.

Notably, while many studies of cognitive-motor tasks with emotional stimuli as distractors have utilized RT as a behavioral measure of attentional modulation, those investigating emotion processing rather than emotion as a distractor have overlooked RT as a key behavioral index. For instance, in one study using the Hariri face-matching task, in which the top picture is to be matched with one of two pictures at the bottom, participants responded faster to happy than to angry or fearful faces (Padula et al., 2011). However, RT was not considered an outcome of interest in evaluating brain activity during the task (Padula et al., 2011). Another study investigated emotion processing and regulation by superimposing angry/fear/neutral faces on non-face images (e.g., houses) and requiring participants to focus on face or non-face images (Ma et al., 2017). Emotion processing was assessed by RT in distinguishing whether the emotional faces represented a male or female. However, RT was not considered as an outcome measure in evaluating regional brain activations during the task (Ma et al., 2017). Likewise, a study using threatening and neutral images in the Hariri task reported longer RT during threat vs. neutral conditions but did not examine the neural correlates of RT (Tupak et al., 2014). A few studies that examined correlations between RT and brain activity relied on region-of-interest (ROI) analyses (Lima Portugal et al., 2020; Marxen et al., 2021; Riedel et al., 2016). For instance, Marxen et al. studied attention capture by emotional stimuli in healthy adults and noted that insula but not amygdala activation was positively correlated with the RT difference between negative and neutral trials (Marxen et al., 2021). In another study of young adults, threat and neutral cues were presented either directed toward or away from the participants (Lima Portugal et al., 2020). Participants were instructed to respond as soon as the picture appeared on the screen. Middle cingulate cortex (MCC) activity was greater during threat versus neutral conditions, regardless of cue direction, and the difference in MCC activity and in RT correlated negatively in the toward but positively in the away condition (Lima Portugal et al., 2020). These ROI studies helped in confirming or falsifying specific hypotheses. On the other hand, a broader view of the brain circuits in support of RT regulation during emotional exposure requires whole-brain analyses. Importantly, despite literature associating the behavioral effects of negative emotions to anxiety state, none of these studies have specifically examined the behavioral or neural correlates of individual anxiety.

Sex differences in anxiety and negative emotion processing

It is well known that clinical manifestations of anxiety vary between men and women, with men often showing externalizing symptoms like anger, aggression or irritability and women internalizing symptoms such as heightened worry and somatic complaints (Altemus et al., 2014; Farhane-Medina et al., 2022). Furthermore, the rates of lifetime anxiety disorders and subclinical anxiety are approximately twice as high in women as in men (Altemus et al., 2014). Other studies suggest critical sex differences in negative emotion processing (Chaudhary et al., 2023a, 2024; Fu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2020a; Stevens and Hamann, 2012; Wu et al., 2024) and other psychological processes of importance to emotional disorders, such as reward, punishment sensitivity and self-control (Dhingra et al., 2021; Kann et al., 2016; Li et al., 2006, 2020b). Likewise, many studies have reported sex differences in the neural markers of anxiety and depressive disorders (Borst et al., 2024; Doucet et al., 2024; Hejazi et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; see also Bangasser and Cuarenta, 2021 for a review). For instance, when matching identical facial versus shape stimuli, men as compared with women engaged the occipital-temporal visual cortex and both anterior and posterior cingulate cortex to a greater extent, whereas women relative to men showed higher activation of bilateral middle frontal cortex (Li et al., 2020a). These sex differences in regional activities varied widely across behavioral paradigms, so did the neural correlates of anxiety and/or depression as revealed with these behavioral tasks. Although it is not entirely clear what other factors may have accounted for the sex differences, it is clearly important to consider sex as a biological variable in investigating the neural markers of individual anxiety.

Men and women may also differ in their RTs to emotional stimuli. For instance, a recent work reported sex-specific effects in reaction time, but not in accuracy, during a sustained attention task in which participants were required to respond to a target letter on 70 % of trials under various emotion induction conditions (Shen et al., 2024). Although a direct contrast for sex differences was not reported, males responded faster under high-arousal negative than high-arousal positive emotion induction, while females responded faster under high-arousal than low-arousal positive emotion conditions. This suggests emotional arousal and valence as well as their interaction may modulate response speed differently in males and females. Additionally, females showed similar reactivity to high and low arousal negative emotions, suggesting that women may be more generally sensitive to negative emotional induction, whereas men may be more specifically sensitive to high arousing negatives (Shen et al., 2024). However, a review highlighted the absence of a consistent pattern of sex differences in RT across various emotion processing tasks (Kret and De Gelder, 2012). In accord, in our previous study, we did not observe differences between men and women in RT of negative emotion processing in Hariri task (Chaudhary et al., 2024). On the other hand, higher anxiety correlated with slower RT in identifying images of negative emotional relative to neutral pictures in women but not in men. Thus, individual anxiety may bias the attention mechanism in women in processing negative emotional stimuli in a way that delays the identification of target images (Prasad et al., 2024). However, the neural circuits underlying this behavioral marker of anxiety remain unclear. This gap highlights the need to investigate how variations in RT during emotion processing may reflect individual differences in anxiety, whether these relationships vary between sexes, and, if so, what neural mechanisms underlie the link and sex difference.

The present study

To address this question in the current study, we first identified whole-brain regional responses correlated with RT during identification of negative vs. neutral emotional images [RT (Neg – Neu)] in a sample of neurotypical men and women. Examining anxiety in neurotypical individuals may offer valuable insights into early or subclinical variations in anxiety-related neural processing, prior to the onset of clinical symptoms. Participants completed the Hariri picture matching task (Hariri et al., 2002), using negative and neutral pictures from International Affective Picture System (IAPS, a database of pictures for studying emotion). This task is widely used to probe brain responses to negative emotional stimuli and reliably engages corticolimbic structures (Foland-Ross et al., 2010; Hariri et al., 2002; Preckel et al., 2019).

Using the identified brain region as a seed (cerebellar vermis; see results), we performed Granger causality mapping to examine brain regions with significant projections to and/or from the seed and evaluate whether these regional activities were associated with RT (Neg – Neu) and individual anxiety. Finally, we performed mediation and path analyses to characterize the inter-relationship amongst the regional activities, RT (Neg – Neu), and individual anxiety. The goal of the study is to characterize the neural mechanisms of prolonged RT in identifying negative emotional images as a behavioral marker of anxiety.

Accurate and efficient performance on the Hariri task requires participants to shift attention away from their emotional responses and instead focus on executing the correct motor action. Therefore, faster RTs are interpreted as reflecting more effective emotion regulation and reduced emotional reactivity (Chaudhary et al., 2023b). Drawing on previous studies, including our own prior findings, we hypothesized that individuals with higher anxiety levels would be more vulnerable to interference from negative emotional stimuli, leading to slower RTs during negative vs. neutral trials. As many emotion studies did not examine correlations between RT and brain activity (Lima Portugal et al., 2020; Marxen et al., 2021; Riedel et al., 2016), in the present study, we explored whole-brain neural correlates of RT without relying on a priori region-based assumptions. Given prior evidence suggesting that women may be more sensitive to negative emotional stimuli, we further hypothesized that these effects would be more pronounced in women than in men.

Methods

Participants and assessments

Neurotypical adults volunteered to participate in the study. We screened ~170 volunteers from the greater New Haven, Connecticut area. The inclusion/exclusion criteria included 1) all participants needed to be physically healthy, with no major medical conditions and score ≥ 27 in Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE); 2) those with current use of prescription medications or with a history of head injury or neurological illness were excluded; 3) those who met criteria of current or history of Axis I disorders according to the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (First et al., 1996) or reported current use of illicit substances or tested positive for cocaine, methamphetamine, opioids, marijuana, barbiturates, or benzodiazepines were excluded. Post-screening, we invited 143 (71M/66F) volunteers for study participation. Among those, 2 were excluded because of MMSE score <27 (1M/1F), suggesting cognitive impairment (Salis et al., 2023), and 4 (2M/2F) could not complete the MR scan. Thus, we analyzed data from 140 healthy adults (73M/67F) 19 to 85 years of age. All participants were assessed with the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI; Spielberger, 1989). The Human Investigation Committee at Yale School of Medicine approved the study procedures. All participants signed an informed consent prior to the study.

We considered the statistical power of our sample size based on prior fMRI studies that examined brain-behavior correlations at the whole-brain level. Such studies remain relatively rare, particularly those assessing the relationship between RT and brain activation during emotion tasks. One study investigating attentional capture by emotional stimuli found that anterior insula activation during negative versus neutral conditions, identified via ROI and confirmed in whole-brain analysis, significantly correlated with RT differences, yielding an estimated power of 0.74 (n = 38; age range: 18 to 42 years; t = 2.59, p = 0.013) (Marxen et al., 2021). Riedel and colleagues examined RT differences during threat versus safe conditions using an ROI-based approach and found that right amygdala activation correlated with RT under both conditions, with >90 % power (n = 36, age= 19–39 years; multiple comparison–corrected p = 0.048, corresponding to t > 7.0). (Riedel et al., 2016). Considering these findings, we estimated that a sample size of approximately 60 participants in each group (men and women) would achieve at least 80 % power for detecting moderate correlations between RT and brain activation. Note that none of the cited studies used tasks identical to ours, and, therefore, these estimates were tentative.

Behavioral task

Participants performed the Hariri task (Hariri et al., 2000) during functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). This task involved 24 International Affective Picture System (IAPS database) images (Lang et al., 2008), divided equally between negative scenes with a normative valence of 2.31 ± 0.67 (all), 2.66 ± 0.66 (men), 2.19 ± 0.88, p = 0.159 (men vs. women) and normative arousal: 6.30 ± 0.76 (all), 5.82 ± 0.72 (men), 6.41 ± 0.85 (women), p = 0.085 (men vs. women) and neutral scenes with a normative valence of 5.98 ± 0.99 (all), 6.02 ± 1.03 (men), 6.20 ± 1.11 (women), p = 0.680 (men vs. women) and normative arousal: 3.87 ± 1.28 (all), 4.09 ± 1.25 (men), 4.03 ± 1.33 (women), p = 0.904 (men vs. women)). Negative and neutral images were matched in luminance (mean pixel intensity, p = 0.158), contrast (standard deviation of pixel intensity, p = 0.110), and complexity (indexed by edge density, p = 0.261), and presented in a block design. Each trial displayed a target scene at the top, accompanied by two scenes at the bottom, one matching and the other not matching the target. Participants were instructed to match one of the two bottom scenes with the target by pressing either the left or right button using their dominant hand (Figure S1). The session began with 10 s of dummy scans, followed by a 2-second instruction phase displaying “choose one to match the picture at the top.” This was followed by four blocks: two blocks of negative scenes interspersed with two blocks of neutral scenes, presented in the order: neutral, negative × 2. Each block commenced with a 2-second fixation period, followed by six consecutive stimuli, each displayed for 6 s without inter-stimulus gaps. The scanning session lasted approximately 152 s (~2.5 min). During the task, participants responded via button presses, allowing for the measurement of accuracy and RT. Subjects were told that the stimuli would be presented long enough for them to make an accurate match but were not explicitly instructed to respond as fast as possible. This allowed us to assess the natural preferences in emotion processing across subjects (Fakra et al., 2008). Please note that this task is a component of a larger task, and we focused on the negative and neutral picture matching blocks in the current manuscript.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Brain images were collected using multiband imaging with a 3-Tesla MR scanner (Siemens Trio, Erlangen, Germany). Conventional T1-weighted spin echo sagittal anatomical images were acquired for slice localization. Anatomical 3D magnetization prepared-rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) image were next obtained with spin echo imaging in the axial plane parallel to the AC–PC line with TR = 1900 ms, TE = 2.52 ms, bandwidth = 170 Hz/pixel, field of view = 250 × 250 mm, matrix = 256 × 256, 176 slices with slice thickness = 1 mm and no gap. Next, a T1-Fast low angle shot (FLASH) reference image was acquired to verify full brain coverage. Functional, blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals were acquired with a single-shot gradient echoplanar imaging (EPI) sequence. Fifty-one axial slices parallel to the AC–PC line covering the whole brain were acquired with TR = 1000 ms, TE = 30 ms, bandwidth = 2290 Hz/pixel, flip angle = 62°, field of view = 210 × 210 mm, matrix = 84 × 84, 51 slices with slice thickness = 2.5 mm and no gap, 392 vol, and multiband acceleration factor = 3. EPI in-plane FOV in the x/y dimensions was manually adjusted when necessary to accommodate individual head sizes and ensure optimal spatial resolution, particularly in participants with larger heads. The number of slices and slice thickness were kept constant across all participants, providing consistent superior-inferior (z-axis) coverage. Images from the first ten TRs at the beginning of each scan were discarded to ensure that only BOLD signals in steady-state equilibrium between RF pulsing and relaxation were included in data analyses.

Imaging data analyses

First, we visually inspected EPI images to ensure uniform coverage with no signal drop across the whole brain during. We excluded 6 participants data (5 M/1F) due to potential signal loss in frontopolar and cerebellar regions. Next, data were analyzed with Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, Wellcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, University College London, U.K.), following our published routines (Li et al., 2020a). Briefly, images of each individual subject were first realigned (motion corrected) and corrected for slice timing. A mean functional image volume was constructed for each subject per run from the realigned image volumes. These mean images were co-registered with the high-resolution structural image and segmented for normalization with affine registration followed by nonlinear transformation. The normalization parameters determined for the structure volume were then applied to the corresponding functional image volumes for each subject. The resampled voxel size is 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm3. Finally, the images were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm at Full Width at Half Maximum.

Runs with excessive head motion, defined as greater than 2 mm translation, 3° rotation (Rosenberg et al., 2016), or a mean frame-to-frame displacement exceeding 0.5 mm (Anderson et al., 2021; Siegel et al., 2014; Song et al., 2023) during the BOLD run were excluded from the analysis. We excluded 5 participants (2 men and 3 women) due to excessive motion.

To ensure optimal normalization, we performed a visual inspection of each participant’s normalized EPI image to identify any clear misregistration or distortion. Additionally, we generated a mean normalized EPI image across all participants and computed voxel-wise absolute difference maps between this group mean and each subject’s normalized EPI image. We then extracted the mean difference value for each subject and flagged potential normalization outliers as those exceeding two standard deviations above the group mean. We identified and excluded three subjects with substantial deviation from the group template.

To assess whether the normalization issues were associated with age, we plotted each subject’s mean difference value against age. No age-related trend or clustering was observed, suggesting that spatial normalization quality did not systematically vary with age (Figure S2). We presented movement parameters (Figure S3), as well as brain coverage based on the mean normalized EPI across the whole brain (Figure S4) and cerebellum (Figure S5) for the subjects included in the group analysis. For cerebellar coverage, the SUIT cerebellar mask was applied to each subject’s normalized mean EPI image, followed by subject-level thresholding and binarization. A whole-brain coverage is presented as mean normalized EPI image across subjects.

Next, a statistical analytical block design was constructed for each individual subject using a general linear model (GLM) by convolving the canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF) with the boxcar function in SPM, separately for negative and neutral scenes. Realignment parameters in all six dimensions were also entered into the model as covariates of no interest. The GLM estimated the component of variance that could be explained by each of the regressors. In the first-level analysis, we constructed for each individual subject a contrast of “negative – neutral scene” (Neg-Neu) block to evaluate regions that responded differently to matching these images. The contrast images of the first-level analysis were used for group statistics. In random effects analyses, we performed a one-sample t-test to evaluate regional responses to negative vs. neutral images in all participants as well as in men and women separately and a two-sample t-test with age as a covariate to evaluate sex differences. We also performed a linear regression of the contrast images against RT (Neg – Neu) across all subjects, with age as a covariate, to evaluate neural correlates of the differences in RT for the whole brain. Following current reporting standards and to ensure a balance between false positive control and adequate sensitivity (statistical power) (Kessler et al., 2017; Woo et al., 2014), we employed a two-tiered thresholding approach. We used a voxel-wise uncorrected threshold of p < 0.001 to identify potential activation clusters, followed by a family-wise error (FWE) corrected cluster-level threshold of p < 0.05 to control for multiple comparisons across the whole brain. The voxel-level threshold serves to reduce the likelihood of noise-driven false positives by minimizing the number of voxels eligible for cluster formation. The cluster-level correction, implemented using gaussian random field theory in SPM, accounts for spatial smoothness and dependencies among neighboring voxels to estimate the probability of observing clusters of a given size under the null hypothesis (Friston et al., 1994; Hayasaka and Nichols, 2003; Worsley et al., 1992). This combined approach has become standard in neuroimaging research and is recommended for achieving a reliable trade-off between sensitivity and specificity.

Granger causality mapping (GCM)

The methods of GCM was described in detail in our earlier work (Ide and Li, 2011). With the brain region (cerebellar vermis, see Results) identified from whole-brain regression analyses of the correlates of RT (Neg – Neu) as a seed, we performed GCM (Goebel et al., 2003) to show its regional inputs and outputs. Granger causal mapping in MRI is a statistical method used to identify directed connectivity between brain regions. It determines whether the activity (BOLD signals) in one region “causes” changes in another by examining the temporal precedence of signals, based on the concept of Granger causality. Essentially, it assesses whether the activity in one region can predict the activity in another over time (Goebel et al., 2003; Granger et al., 2001).

Briefly, BOLD time series were entered into a multivariate autoregressive model (Harrison et al., 2003), where the model order indicates how many previous time points are included in the regression. In GCM, we had two time-series x and y, each representing average BOLD series from the seed and the target regions. To test whether variable x Granger causes y, we computed the autoregressive model of y with and without variable x, the unrestricted and restricted models, respectively. The influence from x→y can be measured by the fractional F-value (Hamilton, 1994) given by the difference between the residual sum of squares (RSS) of the restricted and unrestricted models. If the difference is significantly different from zero, it means that x significantly Granger causes y (significant connection). We used model order 1 for Granger causality analysis, as suggested by previous simulation studies (Roebroeck et al., 2005), to focus on the hemodynamic responses around 1 s and to avoid model overfitting. We called F-out the GC influence (connectivity strength) of the seed on an output region and F-in the influence of an input region on the seed. For each subject, the possible seed-region connections were evaluated correcting for false discovery rate (FDR) in multiple comparisons (Genovese et al., 2002). Group results of input and output were generated by counting the number of subjects with significant connections, with a binomial test to determine group-level significance at p < 0.05. This two-level approach provides a control for multiple comparisons by first minimizing subject-level false positives and then testing for consistent, non-random effects across subjects (Ide and Li, 2011).

We used MarsBaR (http://marsbar.sourceforge.net/) to extract, for each individual subject, the parameter estimates (β’s) of significant clusters (brain regions) identified from imaging analyses, and assessed their correlations with behavioral data. For instance, we examined correlations between regional activity β (Neg – Neu), identified via t-tests and regression analyses, and behavioral measures such as STAI State and Trait scores and RT (Neg – Neu). Similarly, for GCM-derived input and output regions, the corresponding F-values (GC strength) were correlated with these behavioral measures. For bidirectional correlations, we ran mediation analyses, and to understand regional interrelations in behavioral modulations, we did path analyses (Supplementary methods).

Behavioral correlates of anxiety

Men showed a STAI State (mean ± SD: 30.2 ± 10.2) and Trait (31.9 ± 10.1) score and women showed a STAI State (33.9 ± 10.2) and Trait (35.9 ± 9.5) score. A two-sample t-test with age as a covariate showed no differences in the anxiety scores between men and women (Table 1). Both men and women were highly accurate in matching both negative (men: 98.7 ± 5.2 %; women: 99.6 ± 1.8 %) and neutral (men: 98.8 ± 4.2 %; 99.5 ± 2.1 %) images and showed no differences in Accuracy (Neg-Neu) (men: −0.1 ± 5.0 %; women: 0.1 ± 2.3 %; t = 0.4, p = 0.725; two-sample t-test with age as a covariate). Men showed an RT of 1608 ± 458 and 1598 ± 434 ms and women showed an RT of 1445 ± 360 and 1442 ± 341 ms in matching negative and neutral images, respectively. Both men and women showed quite a bit of variation in individual differences in RT during matching of negative vs. neutral emotional images or RT (Neg – Neu). Men showed a RT (Neg – Neu) of 10 ± 235 (mean ± SD) and women 0 ± 201 ms. A two-sample t-test with age as a covariate showed no sex difference (t = −0.46, p = 0.643).

We performed linear regressions of RT (Neg – Neu) on both STAI state and trait scores, with age as a covariate. Across all subjects, RT (Neg – Neu) was not significantly correlated with STAI state (r = 0.131, p = 0.144) or trait score (r = 0.111, p = 0.217). In men alone, RT (Neg – Neu) was not significantly correlated with STAI state (r = −0.050, p = 0.699) or trait (r = −0.097, p = 0.451) score. However, in women, RT (Neg – Neu) was significantly and positively correlated with both STAI state (r = 0.394, p < 0.002) and trait (r = 0.398, p < 0.002) score. A slope test confirmed the sex differences (state: z = −2.53, p = 0.011; trait: z = −2.87, p = 0.0041). Fig. 1 shows the scatter plots and regression lines. Thus, in women but not in men, higher levels of anxiety were associated with prolonged RT in identifying negative vs. neutral images.

Neural correlates of prolonged RT during negative emotion processing

We employed RT (Neg – Neu) as a regressor in a whole brain regression of the contrast “negative – neutral” with age as a covariate. No clusters met the threshold across all subjects or in men alone. We computed the β (Neg – Neu) of the cerebellar vermis cluster for all subjects and examined the correlation of the β’s with RT (Neg – Neu) across all subjects and in women and men separately, with age as a covariate. As expected, RT (Neg – Neu) was significantly correlated with the β (Neg – Neu) in women (r = 0.47, p < 0.001). It also showed a significant correlation for all subjects (r = 0.27, p = 0.0024) but not for men alone (r = 0.104, p = 0.418). Further, a slope test confirms the sex difference (z=−2.24, p = 0.025), suggesting that cerebellar vermis response as a neural correlate of prolonged RT during matching of negative vs. neutral images was sex specific. Note that this analysis did not represent “double-dipping.” As the voxels and clusters were identified with a threshold and those that were identified in women could have just missed the threshold in men, and vice versa. Thus, a post-hoc slope test was needed to confirm sex differences.

We also examined the correlation of the β of the cerebellar vermis with STAI State and Trait scores for all subjects and for men and women separately, with age as a covariate. The results showed that the correlation was significant across all subjects (State: r = 0.22, p = 0.013; Trait: r = 0.27, p = 0.0019) and for women (State: r = 0.37, p = 0.0035; Trait: r = 0.35, p = 0.0056) but not for men (State: r = 0.052, p = 0.69; Trait: r = 0.20, p = 0.11). However, a slope test showed only a trend-level difference between men and women for the correlation with STAI State score (State: z = −1.84, p = 0.066; Trait: z = −0.89, p = 0.37).

Thus, for women alone, the β’s of the cerebellar vermis were significantly correlated with both RT (Neg – Neu) and with STAI state and trait scores. In a mediation analysis, we observed that cerebellar vermis β significantly mediated the correlation between both STAI State/Trait scores and RT (Neg – Neu) in women (Fig. 4).

Mediation analyses of the inter-relationship between cerebellar vermis (Vermis) β’s, STAI State (A, B) or Trait (C, D) score, and RT (Neg – Neu) in women.

GCM of cerebellar vermis connectivity during identification of negative emotion in women

We performed Granger causality mapping (GCM) with the cerebellar vermis as the seed region in women. Evaluated with a p < 0.05, binomial test (see Methods) and a cluster size of 10 voxels, the results showed a multitude of brain regions with significant inputs to the cerebellar vermis (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S3). Evaluated at the same threshold, no clusters were identified as outputs of the cerebellar vermis.

These input regions comprise the saliency circuit and include the anterior cingulate cortex/frontopolar cortex (ACC/FPC), bilateral middle frontal gyri, angular gyri, temporo-parietal junction, and insula. We extracted β (Neg – Neu) from 22 clusters and averaged them into 8 anatomically defined regions, for correlation with RT (Neg – Neu) and with STAI State and Trait scores in women and, for comparison, in men. A p-value of 0.006 (p = 0.05/8) was considered significant. Of these correlations, only the ACC/FPC cluster showed a significant correlation with STAI State (r = −0.34, p < 0.008), but not Trait (r = −0.18, p = 0.16) score, and a modest negative association with RT (Neg – Neu) (r = −0.28, p = 0.027), with age as a covariate, in women. The same regressions were not significant in men (all p’s > 0.49), and slope tests confirmed sex differences in the correlation with STAI State (z = 2.43, p = 0.015). Thus, the ACC/FPC shows regional response to negative vs. neural images in association with individual anxiety and RT (Neg – Neu) and provides inputs to the cerebellar vermis where the response to the same was also significantly associated with both RT (Neg – Neu) and individual anxiety in women.

Path analyses of regional activities, RT (Neg – Neu), and anxiety

The preceding analyses identified cerebellar vermis and ACC/FPC with activities (β’s) in correlation with both individual STAI State score and RT (Neg – Neu). We performed a path analysis to evaluate the interrelationships amongst the β’s of the cerebellar vermis and ACC/FPC, STAI State score, and RT (Neg – Neu), with age as a covariate, in women. A good model fit in path analysis is typically assessed with indices including χ2 at p > 0.05, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA)≤0.08, standardized root mean squared residual (SRMR)≤0.08, and comparative fit index (CFI)≥0.90 (Chaudhary et al., 2022).

Building on the mediation results and GCM finding of cerebellar vermis receiving inputs from ACC/FPC, the analysis demonstrated optimal fit for two models, where ACC/FPC β’s had an indirect effect on RT (Neg – Neu) via cerebellar vermis β’s and STAI State score had a direct effect or, via ACC/FPC and cerebellar vermis β’s or cerebellar vermis β’s alone, an indirect effect on RT (Neg – Neu), as shown in Fig. 5. The models showed comparable model fit parameters including χ2= 0.062, p > χ2= 0.803, RMSEA= 0.00, CFI= 1.00, SRMR= 0.06. Thus, the optimal models suggest that, during a state of anxiety, the vermis may receive reduced input from the ACC/FPC and, as a result, higher activation of the cerebellar circuit to support prolonged RT during the processing of negative versus neutral stimuli.

Optimal-fit path models illustrating the interrelationships among the β’s of the cerebellar vermis and ACC/FPC, STAI State score, and RT (Neg – Neu), with age as a covariate, in women. Positive and negative coefficients are represented by paths in ‘red’ and ‘blue,’ respectively. Path statistics are presented as coefficients (β) and p-values.

The results of other path models were shown in Supplementary Figure S6 and all path statistics are shown in Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

We aimed to characterize the neural circuits that support individual anxiety and prolonged RT in identifying negative vs. neutral images [RT (Neg – Neu)] in women. With whole-brain regression on RT (Neg – Neu), we identified a cluster in the cerebellar vermis with β (Neg – Neu) in positive correlation with both RT (Neg -Neu) and individual anxiety. Further, Granger causality mapping (GCM) demonstrated a cluster in the anterior cingulate and frontopolar cortex (ACC/FPC) with β (Neg – Neu) in negative correlation with both RT (Neg -Neu) and individual anxiety. GCM also showed a small cluster in the pontine nuclei, suggesting that the circuit ACC → pontine nuclei → cerebellar vermis (and potentially → deep cerebellar nuclei → spinal cord) may support prolonged RT in identifying negative emotional images as a behavioral marker of individual anxiety in women. We discuss the main findings below.

Cingulate/frontopolar cortical cerebellar projection and emotional processing

Prefrontal cortex projects to the pontine nuclei, which relay signals to the cerebellum via the middle cerebellar peduncle. Through the superior cerebellar peduncle, the cerebellar cortex projects to the deep cerebellar nuclei, which then project to the thalamus and in turn a wide swath of prefrontal cortical regions. This topographically organized cortico-pontine-cerebellar-thalamic-cortical circuit supports a multitude of cognitive and emotional motor processes (Middleton and Strick, 2001), including motor planning (Zhu et al., 2023). In particular, beyond cerebellum’s well-known function in motor control, cerebellar connections with the limbic circuits regulate the experience and expression of emotion as well as physiological responses associated with the emotional experiences (Blatt et al., 2013).

Amongst these cortical regions, the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and frontopolar cortex (FPC) have been widely investigated for their roles in emotion processing, including goal-directed behavior in affect-laden contexts (Etkin et al., 2011; Rolls, 2019; Ziaei et al., 2019). For instance, the ACC is involved in motor inhibition during a valenced emotional state in a go/nogo task (Albert et al., 2012). The FPC is involved in update of emotional memory (Sakaki et al., 2011), and failure in recruiting the FPC to control emotional behavior may account for individual anxiety (Bramson et al., 2023). The FPC is involved in representing the congruency of emotion-action goals – faster go RT and higher no-go accuracy each during an appetitive and aversive emotional state – suggesting a role of the FPC in integrating emotional states and goal representations for motor action (Lapate et al., 2022). In clinical studies, ACC dysfunction is widely implicated in pathophysiology of dysregulated emotional states, including anxiety and depression (García-Cabezas and Barbas, 2017; Xiao and Zhang, 2018). Greater activation of the FPC during reappraisal of negative emotions during the course of psychotherapy was associated with symptom improvement in people with anxiety disorders (Fonzo et al., 2017). Thus, the current findings of lower ACC/FPC response to matching of negative vs. neutral emotional images in association with higher levels of individual anxiety is consistent with this literature. The current findings extend this literature by characterizing a cortical-cerebellar circuit mechanism of prolonged RT in matching negative vs. neutral emotional images, a behavioral marker of individual anxiety.

Sex differences in the circuit mechanism of prolonged RT in matching negative vs. neutral emotions

Notably, we identified this circuit mechanism that supports prolonged RT in matching negative vs. neutral emotional images as a behavioral marker of individual anxiety only in women. The “null” finding in men seems unlikely to reflect an issue of statistical power, as an approximately equal number of men (n = 64) and women (n = 62) participated in the study. Further, normative valence and arousal ratings of the IAPS images did not show sex differences, suggesting that men and women found these images similarly negative and arousing. Additional support comes from our finding that both men and women showed comparable activation in corticolimbic brain regions during negative versus neutral image processing, with no statistically significant group differences (see Results, Section 3.2). Finally, men and women also showed similar extent of individual variation in RT (Neg - Neu) to allow regression analysis of the sample. This “lack of finding” in men has also been reported in earlier studies of emotion tasks. For instance, in an MR imaging study of emotion regulation with cognitive reappraisal as a strategy, females showed different levels of self-rated emotional intensity and amygdala activity for negative versus positive emotions, while males did not. Further, females showed greater overall prefrontal cortical but similar levels of amygdala activity compared to males (Min et al., 2023). Conversely, other studies reported null findings in women. For instance, men but not women showed a significant, positive correlation between ventral striatal loss reactivity in a guessing task and externalizing traits, with the sex difference confirmed by a slope test (Li et al., 2023). A majority of studies reported significant, sex-specific imaging findings in both men and women, including, for instance, studies of facial emotion processing and correlates of anger and fear traits (Li et al., 2020a) as well as reward response in the monetary incentive delay task and correlates of reward and punishment sensitivity (Dhingra et al., 2021). With these considerations, it would seem most appropriate to conclude that RT (Neg – Neu) in the Hariri task or likely other behavioral paradigms that simply involve exposure to negative emotions may not be ideal for elucidation of the mechanisms of emotion processing or the identification of a behavioral or neural marker of individual anxiety in men.

Men and women showed differences in physiological responses to emotional exposure while watching videos, with women reporting higher levels of arousal and stronger avoidance motivation than men when exposed to negative emotions (Deng et al., 2016). Consistently, in an event-related potential (ERP) study, women showed greater ERP to unpleasant as compared with pleasant slides whereas men had greater ERP to pleasant vs. neutral slides (Bianchin and Angrilli, 2012). Further, women relative to men perceived all slides as less pleasant and reported greater arousal to unpleasant condition. In an MR imaging study that required participants to reappraise negative emotions, men relative to women showed less elevation in prefrontal cortical activities that are associated with reappraisal but greater decrement in amygdala activities, which are associated with emotional responding (McRae et al., 2008). The latter findings suggest that men may expend less effort in employing cognitive regulation but somehow were able to mitigate their emotional responses. The sex differences in subjective, physiological, perceptual responses to negative emotions were also reported in many other studies (Gard and Kring, 2007; Gohier et al., 2013; Poláčková Solcová and Lačev, 2017). However, the exact behavioral and neurobiological underpinnings of the sex differences remain unclear. Candidate mechanisms for this male “advantage” include expressive suppression that can perhaps be automatized (Cai et al., 2016). To reveal how men process negative emotions would likely require behavioral paradigms that involve active processing beyond automatized suppression of emotion, such as those of emotional working memory and complex stimulus-response association learning. Studies are needed to test this hypothesis.

Limitations of the study

We consider several limitations in view of the current findings. First, in claiming sex differences we did not consider the potential impact of female hormones on the findings. For instance, females in the late luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, characterized by high progesterone and moderate estradiol levels, exhibited enhanced skin conductance responses to negative stimuli compared to those in the mid-cycle phase and those using oral contraceptives (Gamsakhurdashvili et al., 2021a). This as well as other studies as reviewed by Gamsakhurdashvili and colleagues suggest important roles of female hormones in emotion processing (Gamsakhurdashvili et al., 2021b) and, along with genetics and environmental stressors, in contributing to changes in brain structure and function that may underlie sex differences in anxiety (Kundakovic and Rocks, 2022). Taken together, hormonal status in women may confound their responses to negative emotional stimuli. Future research examining sex differences in emotion processing should include assays and analyses of hormonal status. Likewise, we did not consider potential differences in neurodevelopment or the impact of social environmental factors on individual anxiety. Finally, as discussed earlier, while men and women did not differ in corticolimbic activation or RT, anxiety levels were marginally higher in women. Although this did not translate into RT differences, heightened anxiety in women may have influenced cognitive-motor processses underlying the response time. More studies are needed with a greater number of male participants and higher anxiety levels. These issues would need to be addressed in future work to understand the sources of sex differences in the findings. Second, our sample comprised neurotypical individuals and candidates with clinical anxiety or depression were not recruited for the study. Thus, the current findings should be considered as specific to this population. Moreover, we did not specifically quantify the psychological components of anxiety, including rumination, which may have an outsized influence on task performance. While the STAI questionnaire includes items that reflect ruminative tendencies (Ugalde-Araya et al., 2020), the present study could not disentangle the effects of worry and rumination within the broader anxiety construct. Third, our sample ranged widely in age. Although we considered age as a covariate in all analyses and one could argue that the findings obtained of the sample may generalize to this age range, how age impacts the current findings remains to be clarified. Despite quality control checks, the heterogeneity in brain structure and function across the lifespan may not be fully accounted for and thus remains a limitation. Non-linear age effects and age as a potential moderator of the anxiety-RT relationship warrant further investigation. Finally, as discussed earlier, the “null” findings in men should be interpreted as specific to the behavioral paradigm. More studies that require active engagement in negative emotions are needed to investigate the behavioral and neural markers of anxiety in men.

Conclusions

To conclude, with a direct regression on the difference in RT in matching negative vs. neutral emotional images or RT (Neg – Neu), we identified cerebellar vermis response as a neural correlate of both the RT marker and individual anxiety. As revealed by Granger causality mapping, the anterior cingulate/frontopolar cortex also shows response in correlation with both RT (Neg – Neu) and anxiety state. Together, the findings support a cortico-cerebellar circuit in supporting prolong RT as a behavioral marker of individual anxiety in women.
