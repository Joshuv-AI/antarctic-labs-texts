Acute Plaque Rupture and an ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction in a Young Competitive Athlete
Authors: Heather DiGregorio, Neeya Patel, Parker Grow, Wissam A. Jaber, Bryan J. Wells, Jonathan H. Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute myocardial infarction in young competitive athletes is uncommon but requires consideration of a broad underlying differential diagnosis.Case SummaryA 22-year-old male collegiate swimmer with no significant past medical or family history presented to the hospital with an ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. After a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, the athlete was deemed to have acute plaque rupture secondary to underlying coronary artery disease. Appropriate treatments were implemented, and return-to-play was considered. In accordance with guideline-based risk stratification and shared decision making, the athlete returned to competitive swimming.DiscussionAcute myocardial infarction as a consequence of coronary artery disease is uncommon among young competitive athletes. Athletes who present with acute myocardial infarction require an understanding of potential etiologies, appropriate diagnostic evaluations, and clinical management strategies.Take-Home MessageAcute coronary syndromes are rare in young competitive athletes and require a focused history and physical examination and a comprehensive diagnostic work-up.

History of Presentation

A 22-year-old collegiate male swimmer (400-meter individual medley) presented to the emergency department after experiencing chest heaviness associated with nausea while in the pool during practice. He continued to feel poorly and brought himself to the hospital approximately 1 hour after symptom initiation. Two weeks prior, he experienced a similar, but less severe episode during a competitive swim meet. Those symptoms subsided, however, and he felt well without recurrence until the day of initial presentation.Take-Home Messages•Acute coronary syndromes are less common in young competitive athletes compared with older masters athletes.•Clinical evaluation of competitive athletes who present with chest pain, abnormal ECG, and elevated troponin requires a focused and targeted clinical history and physical examination and a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation based on the potential etiologies present in this population.

Past Medical History

The athlete had an unremarkable past medical history. He reported an orthopedic history with past patellar dislocation but no other orthopedic injuries, ectopia lentis, pneumothorax, or scoliosis. His younger brother is also a collegiate swimmer and has kyphosis. There was no family history of sudden cardiac death (SCD), inherited cardiomyopathies or genetic arrhythmia syndromes, aortic aneurysms, aortic or other arterial dissections, or hypercoagulable conditions. His maternal grandfather had a coronary stent placed at age 49. He reported no stimulant, performance-enhancing drug, tobacco, alcohol, or illicit drug use.

Differential Diagnosis and Investigations

On presentation, the patient was mildly hypertensive and experiencing ongoing mild chest discomfort. Laboratory test results were notable for a high-sensitivity troponin of 24 ng/L. The initial 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) (Figure 1) was notable for hyperacute T waves and anterior ST-segment elevations in the anterior and lateral leads. Because of the clinical uncertainty of the observed ST-segment elevations, an emergent bedside echocardiogram was obtained (images not saved). Per report, there was concern for mildly depressed left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <50% and anteroseptal wall hypokinesis. The coronary angiogram showed a filling defect in the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) with more distal 100% occlusion in the mid-LAD (Figure 2). Other coronary arteries were patent. The LVEF was estimated at 50% with anteroseptal and apical wall motion abnormalities. One drug-eluting stent was placed in the proximal LAD along with percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty to the distal occluded LAD segment. Post-revascularization, high-sensitivity troponin peaked at 92,034 ng/L. A complete transthoracic echocardiogram confirmed an LVEF of 50% to 55% with hypokinesis in the anterior wall and apical segments (Videos 1 and 2). A lipid panel revealed a total cholesterol of 118 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein of 46 mg/dL, triglycerides of 77 mg/dL, and low-density lipoprotein of 56 mg/dL. Later, lipoprotein(a) was tested and was <6 mg/dL. He was started on aspirin, prasugrel, and atorvastatin medical therapy.Figure 2Emergent Coronary AngiographyCoronary angiographic left anterior oblique cranial view demonstrating proximal left anterior descending artery filling defect and mid-left anterior descending artery occlusion (arrows)

After discharge, the athlete presented to our sports cardiology center for ongoing clinical management and evaluation of the underlying mechanism of his ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Angiographic images, including intravascular ultrasound, demonstrated an acute thrombus and not an intramural hematoma (Figure 3, Videos 3 to 6), which would have been consistent with spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD). In addition, a mid-LAD myocardial bridge was appreciated in the location of the acute thrombus and downstream from the proximal LAD atherosclerotic lesion. We proceeded with a comprehensive diagnostic work-up addressing the potential for underlying connective tissue disease, fibromuscular dysplasia, and hypercoagulable disorders. Genetic tests; computed tomography angiography of the head/neck, chest, and abdomen/pelvis; and hypercoagulable panel all were normal. Management

The athlete was maintained on medical therapy, referred to cardiac rehabilitation, and advised to abstain from training for an initial 3-month period. Additionally, he inquired about a return to competitive swimming. We emphasized that competitive sports after acute coronary syndrome can be considered in a shared decision making (SDM) paradigm. In accordance with current guidelines,1 certain parameters, including left ventricular function, clinical status, and results from exercise testing, would be factored into a risk stratification assessment to be completed after cardiac rehabilitation and this 3-month time frame. During this period of training abstinence, we counseled the athlete and his family to consider the qualitative risks inherent to competitive sports training and to determine his desire to return to sport.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After 3 months, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was obtained, which affirmed normal LVEF (54%), although anterior wall hypokinesis with apical akinesis and transmural scar involving the distal LAD distribution were appreciated. In considering the mid-LAD bridge, exercise ECG stress testing also revealed no evidence of ischemic ST-segment changes or induced ventricular arrhythmias. Because of the presence of transmural scar associated with regional wall motion abnormalities, although it was determined that there was some qualitative degree of increased risk associated with return-to-play, this risk was not prohibitive in our estimation. With SDM and support from the entire medical team, family, and school, the athlete sought full return-to-play. In conjunction with an emphasis on emergency action planning, the athlete has successfully returned to competitive swimming.

Discussion

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the most common cause of sudden cardiac arrest among Masters athletes;2 however, in the absence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, CAD is uncommon among young competitive athletes. Moreover, sudden cardiac arrest and/or SCD as a consequence of CAD is rare among young competitive athletes based on recent 20-year outcomes data from the National Collegiate Athletic Association, with 6% of SCD cases attributed to underlying CAD.3 Although our case was an extremely rare presentation of acute coronary syndrome with type 1 myocardial infarction (MI)4 in a young athlete without traditional risk factors, it affords the opportunity to review the differential diagnosis, diagnostic work-up, and considerations for return-to-play for young competitive athletes with a diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction.

Competitive athletes who present with acute myocardial infarction require assessment for key historical clues and consideration of a broad differential diagnosis. Acute viral myocarditis may be the most common condition associated with chest pain and elevated troponin in an athlete, warranting inquiry about any recent viral prodrome or illness. Important demographic features, as discussed later, may raise suspicion for SCAD. Possible CAD requires review of a family history of premature CAD and presence of traditional risk factors (eg, familial hyperlipidemia). Embolic etiologies should be considered with assessment and testing for hypercoagulable conditions. Etiologies, such as coronary vasospasm, that can lead to type 2 MI should also be considered, particularly coronary artery anomalies.4 Finally, it is essential to assess for use of exogenous substances, such as stimulants, cocaine, or performance-enhancing drugs.

Because atherosclerotic CAD is rare among young athletes, SCAD as the cause of type 1 MI requires careful consideration. SCAD is characterized by the spontaneous development of a false lumen within the coronary arterial wall, typically from a tear or spontaneous hemorrhage, and the development of an intramural hematoma, which can occlude blood flow leading to ST-segment elevation MI. Indeed, it has been reported that almost half of SCAD cases manifest as ST-segment elevation MI.5 The demographics of SCAD are a critical determinant, with the diagnosis more common in women compared with men.5,6 Family history is relevant for SCAD with concerns for underlying connective tissue disease. Finally, SCAD is associated with a high prevalence of extracoronary vascular abnormalities, such as fibromuscular dysplasia.7 In our case, genetic testing for connective tissue diseases was normal, and no noncoronary vascular abnormalities were identified. In addition, our athlete was male, and intravascular ultrasound did not demonstrate an intramural hematoma. Finally, despite the absence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, we identified a mid-LAD myocardial bridge, which can be associated with upstream, earlier development of atherosclerosis.

The diagnostic evaluation of symptomatic young athletes who present with elevated troponin and ECG abnormalities must include assessment for underlying coronary pathology. Even with a high clinical suspicion for acute viral myocarditis, cases may still require coronary imaging more emergently. Without a clinical history consistent with viral myocarditis, emergent coronary angiography, either invasive or computed tomography angiography, is the first step in diagnosis. With SCAD, comprehensive computed tomography imaging of the extracoronary vasculature from the head and neck through the abdomen and pelvis is recommended.

Recently, the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association updated clinical considerations for competitive sports participation for athletes with cardiac conditions, which included return-to-play recommendations after acute coronary syndromes.1 Although these recommendations were purposed for Masters athletes, it is reasonable to generalize them to younger athletes. For competitive athletes who have completed cardiac rehabilitation and are adherent to guideline-based medical therapy, resumption of competitive sports is reasonable post-ischemic event after 3 to 6 months and with risk stratification demonstrating normal left ventricular systolic function, normal wall motion, no ischemic ECG changes or exertional symptoms during stress testing, and no exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias. In our case, resting wall motion abnormalities and presence of ischemic scar were observed. Although these factors may qualitatively increase his cardiac risk during intensive training, we did not believe these risks were prohibitive or outweighed the benefits of competitive sport for the athlete. Although myocardial bridging was present in this athlete, risk stratification with exercise stress testing was normal. Leveraging SDM with involvement of his family and school officials, the athlete was supported for full return-to-play.

Conclusions

In young competitive athletes with chest pain associated with ECG abnormalities and elevated troponin, acute coronary syndrome may be an underlying cause. Although rare compared with Masters athletes, CAD and type 1 MI may still occur in young athletes, even without traditional risk factors as demonstrated in this case. It is critical that sports cardiologists understand the appropriate differential diagnosis, assess for key historical clues in the evaluation, and proceed with proper diagnostic evaluations. Similar to Masters athletes, return-to-play after acute coronary syndrome is possible with an SDM approach.
