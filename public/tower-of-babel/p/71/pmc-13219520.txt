An AI-based mental health guardrail and dataset for identifying psychiatric crises in text-based conversations
Authors: Benjamin W Nelson, Celeste Wong, Matthew T Silvestrini, Sooyoon Shin, Alanna Robinson, Jessica Lee, Eric Yang, John Torous, Andrew Trister
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge language models often mishandle psychiatric emergencies, offering harmful or inappropriate advice. This study evaluated the Verily Mental Health Guardrail (VMHG) on two clinician-labeled datasets: the Verily Mental Health Crisis Dataset v1.0, containing 1800 simulated messages and the NVIDIA Aegis AI Content Safety Dataset subsetted to 794 mental health-related messages. Performance was benchmarked against OpenAI Omni Moderation Latest and NVIDIA NeMo Guardrails. The VMHG demonstrated high sensitivity (0.990) and specificity (0.992) on the Verily dataset, with an F1-score of 0.939 and high category-level sensitivity (0.917–0.992) and specificity (≥0.978). On the NVIDIA dataset, it maintained strong sensitivity (0.982) and accuracy (0.921) with reduced specificity (0.859). Compared with NVIDIA and OpenAI guardrails, the VMHG achieved significantly higher sensitivity (all p < 0.001) and comparable specificity (NVIDIA p < 0.001, OpenAI p = 0.094). Overall, the VMHG demonstrated robust, generalizable, and clinically oriented safety performance that prioritizes sensitivity to minimize missed mental health crises.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Psychiatric emergencies are prevalent, multidimensional, and rising1,2. Detecting crises is particularly challenging, as clinicians perform only slightly better than chance3,4 and individuals often communicate distress indirectly5–7. People experiencing psychiatric challenges are increasingly turning to chatbots leveraging large language models (LLMs) and generative artificial intelligence (AI) for information and emotional support8,9.

LLMs have been implicated in high-profile clinical failures, including missed warning signs, unsafe advice, or even harm facilitation. These failures include enabling suicide10–12, promoting violence13, and providing dangerous guidance on self-harm14, drug use, and eating disorders15,16. These incidents have drawn national attention, prompting American Psychological Association testimony before the US Senate Judiciary Committee17, a bipartisan letter by 54 state attorneys general on AI and exploitation of children18 and a special FDA panel on mental health and AI in November 202519,20.

Accurate detection of psychiatric crises is critical for ensuring safety, yet significant challenges remain. Indicators of risk are often nuanced, subtle, and multidimensional, spanning suicide to abuse, and require clinicians to recognize subtle and nuanced signals5–7. While developers have implemented general safety filters21–27, current solutions are designed for generic risks (e.g., sexual content, fraud, hate, and general violence)22,24 and are not fit-for-purpose for detecting psychiatric crises.

Several critical gaps remain before LLMs can reliably detect and respond to psychiatric crises. Common frameworks and evaluation datasets are needed to define crises and evaluate model performance28. Existing benchmark datasets lack mental health messages, and/or clinical annotation, resulting in low-quality datasets for developing behavioral health safety filters and guardrails29–31. Consequently, there are two key challenges for developing such filters: general-purpose safety filters may lack clinical nuance for psychiatric crisis detection, and available datasets are rarely clinically validated or specific to psychiatric risk.

To address these challenges, we (1) identified eight dimensions of mental health crises that represent the most urgent and high-risk presentations in collaboration with clinical experts: abuse, neglect, eating-disorder behaviors, psychosis, self-harm, suicide, substance misuse, violence towards others, and mixed presentations corresponding to risk factors that clinicians are trained to monitor closely and, in many cases, are legally mandated to report; (2) developed the Verily Mental Health Guardrail (VMHG) to identify these crises in user messages using a simulated dataset of 1800 crisis and non-crisis messages reflecting real-world texting behaviors that were labeled and reviewed by two licensed clinicians; (3) evaluated the performance of VMHG in the Verily Mental Health Crisis Dataset v1.0 (internal evaluation) and in a subset of mental health messages from the NVIDIA Aegis AI Content Safety Dataset 2.0 (external evaluation); and (4) compared the VMHG performance to two state-of-the-art general-purpose guardrails.

Per-category sensitivity ranged from 0.917 to 0.992, and specificity was at or above 0.978 for all crisis types (Table 3). Among the 898 messages flagged as “Crisis”, 88 messages (9.8%) contained at least one category misclassification, resulting in 125 individual category (false positive or false negative) errors (see Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Comparative evaluation

In the comparative evaluation on the Verily dataset (n = 1800), the VMHG demonstrated the highest overall performance, with a sensitivity of 0.990 (95% CI: 0.981-0.995) and a specificity of 0.992 (95% CI: 0.984–0.996) (see Fig. 1 and Table 2). OpenAI guardrail achieved the highest specificity of 0.999 (95% CI: 0.994–1.000) with a sensitivity of 0.419 (95% CI: 0.387–0.451), while NVIDIA’s model had sensitivity of 0.759 (95% CI: 0.730–0.786) and a specificity of 0.756 (95% CI: 0.726–0.783). A Cochran’s Q test revealed a significant difference among the models for both sensitivity (Q(2) = 751.12, p < 0.001) and specificity (Q(2) = 418.68, p < 0.001). Pairwise McNemar’s tests with Bonferroni correction revealed the VMHG’s sensitivity was significantly higher than the NVIDIA and OpenAI guardrails (p < 0.001). The VMHG’s specificity was significantly higher than the NVIDIA (p < 0.001), but was not significantly different from the OpenAI guardrail (p = 0.094).

To assess generalizability, all models were evaluated on the NVIDIA dataset. The VMHG showed the highest sensitivity (0.982, 95% CI: 0.964–0.991), which was significantly higher than both OpenAI (0.882, p < 0.001) and NVIDIA (0.907, p < 0.001) guardrails. No significant differences in specificity were observed among the guardrails (Q(2) = 4.28, p = 0.118). Table 2 shows a comprehensive summary of all performance metrics for each model across datasets.

In the comparative evaluation of specific mental health crisis detection, VMHG sensitivity ranged from 0.88 to 0.992 and accuracy ranged from 0.987 to 0.995. The OpenAI sensitivity ranged from 0.097 to 0.916 and accuracy ranged from 0.739 to 0.845. NVIDIA sensitivity ranged from 0.503 to 1000 and accuracy ranged from 0.500 to 0.565. Detailed performance metrics for all models across all categories are presented in Table 2.

To assess operational utility, PPV was projected across a range of plausible low-prevalence crisis rates for both datasets. On the Verily dataset with an assumed 2% prevalence rate, the PPV was 0.895 (95% CI: 0.717–0.989) for OpenAI, 0.716 (95% CI: 0.576–0.822) for VMHG, and 0.060 (95% CI: 0.041–0.085) for NVIDIA (see Supplementary Tables 1 and 2; Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrated the feasibility of building and evaluating a fit-for-purpose guardrail for text-based LLM applications. The VMHG exhibited strong performance in accurately detecting mental health crises and classifying crises into eight specific categories linked to adverse outcomes. Evaluation was conducted rigorously using both internal and external datasets, each appropriately labeled by clinicians.

This work showed one of the first efforts to create stronger content filtering, specifically for mental and behavioral health risks, by utilizing clinician-guided prompt design with explicit risk criteria and few-shot examples. The two-stage architectural design, which decouples risk detection from categorization and uses “conservative” prompt instructions that treat any plausible risk signal as actionable, allowed the VMHG to achieve a critical balance for clinical safety systems: minimizing missed crisis messages (false negatives) while maintaining a manageable false-positive rate. When evaluated against the external NVIDIA dataset, the VMHG still maintained high sensitivity, suggesting that the safety-critical prioritization of sensitivity generalizes to novel samples. The VMHG model’s reduced specificity on an out-of-domain, external dataset raises the possibility of increased clinical or moderation burden. However, this trade-off was judged acceptable in a safety-critical setting because prioritizing the detection of true crisis signals and routing them through layered moderation steps was considered more important than eliminating all false positives.

Evaluation against two general-purpose safety guardrail models (OpenAI and NVIDIA) for mental health crisis detection contextualized these findings. While the OpenAI guardrail prioritized specificity at the expense of sensitivity, and the NVIDIA guardrail showed inconsistent detection across categories, the VMHG consistently prioritized the capture of true crisis signals. This variability in performance of general-purpose guadrails across some categories was expected, as the general-purpose guardrails did not cover all of the same risk categories as the VMHG. For example, the VMHG includes specific clinical categories like neglect and psychosis, which are typically absent from general-purpose safety taxonomies. Therefore, the lower sensitivity observed in these areas reflects a difference in scope rather than a failure of function. Although the specific designs of the OpenAI and NVIDIA guardrails are not available, we hypothesize that their broad, general scope, spanning domains from suicide to terrorism, enabled wide safety-risk detection but offered less specificity for mental health crises. For health-focused AI-based applications, the variability in category-level results points to broader implications. First, guardrails should be purpose-built for the specific risks for a given population and intended/expected use of the application. Second, benchmark datasets must be subjected to expert review to ensure a high-quality meaningful assessment. Third, label quality alone is insufficient, and quality must also be understood in terms of how well a dataset reflects the distribution of real-world language from patients and users, particularly if performance on benchmarks is expected to generalize to real-world deployment. Research needs to determine how well benchmark datasets, including simulated datasets, reflect real-world language patterns and usage, as the relationship between simulated data distributions and those encountered in the real-world remain unknown.

The analysis of projected PPV in a low-prevalence environment highlights a crucial consideration for deployment: even the best-performing models will inevitably produce a substantial number of false alarms in real-world settings where crises are rare. This precludes fully autonomous use without unnecessarily burdening users who are not in crisis. Rather than replacing human judgment, the VMHG’s high sensitivity makes it most valuable as a screening tool, with human-in-the-loop oversight essential to adjudicate alerts, filter false positives to prevent alert fatigue, and ensure timely and appropriate crisis resources for those at risk.

The ability to screen user messages in real-time and at scale with high sensitivity and specificity would be a critical advancement over established clinical methodologies. Conventional risk factor assessments, often relying on clinician-rated scales, have performed only marginally better than chance over the past 50 years32–34 For example, in a large sample of almost 90,000 patients, clinician judgment during emergency department encounters had an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of only 0.60 (95% CI, 0.55–0.64) for predicting 90-day suicide risk4. In contrast, moment-to-moment linguistic signals within LLM user messages can provide direct insight into a user’s mental state, making them especially well-suited for detecting acute crises as they emerge and enabling delivery of just-in-time resources. This is critical given that human clinicians cannot operate at the continuous, high-volume scale required in modern virtual environments26,35.

With its performance characteristics, the VMHG may be an appropriate tool for virtual care applications where the risk of missing crises is high (i.e., given its high sensitivity), and the burden of alarms can impact the usability of the product. We also recognize that further validation is needed before deployment and widespread adoption.

Despite strengths like a diverse, clinician-labeled dataset of mental health messages and generalizing performance to an NVIDIA dataset, the study has limitations. Messages were English-only, which may restrict applicability across languages, though LLMs often transfer well across languages36,37. Future studies should simulate multi-language data. All messages were single-turn, and safety guardrails are known to fail more frequently in multi-turn conversations, suggesting performance may drop8. The NVIDIA dataset30 included messages used to train their guardrail, so the reported performance may not generalize and should be interpreted with caution as a possible source of methodological bias. Another key limitation of this study arises from the constraints of the comparative analysis. Because the VMHG’s crisis subcategories did not overlap with those used by the two benchmark guardrails, we were required to evaluate our full hierarchical system as a single “flat” classifier across the full 1800 message dataset. This methodology was essential to ensure the identical denominators required for the power and validity of the Cochran’s Q test and McNemar’s paired tests. While this approach created a structured environment for benchmarking, it necessarily diverged from our model’s intended operational flow (where Stage 2 only processes the N = 898 messages that were flagged as crisis in Stage 1). Nevertheless, we validated that this approach did not bias the results: the 95% CIs for all category-specific metrics fully overlapped between the full dataset comparative evaluation (Table 3) and the filtered real-world operational evaluation (Table 4). This confirmed that our methodological choice did not introduce a statistically distinguishable difference in the assessment of our model’s true performance. The drop in VMHG specificity during external validation from 0.992 on the Verily dataset to 0.859 on the NVIDIA dataset likely suggests either potential overfitting to specific language patterns or a broader mismatch between dataset and deployment contexts. Whereas the Verily dataset was designed to simulate short, conversational patient messages centered on individual concerns, the NVIDIA dataset consists of long-form, open-web posts, blogs, and forum discussions whose narrative style differs substantially from the setting of intended use. This discrepancy suggests that external validation using open-web text may inflate false positives and increase clinical or moderation burden. Nevertheless, this limitation was considered acceptable in a safety-critical setting where capturing true crisis signals is prioritized. Future work should red-team VMHG performance on real patient messages in the setting of intended use. Another limitation is that the simulated dataset was heavily skewed toward direct, explicit expressions of crisis (91.1%). Although a prior psychological autopsy study reported that the majority of individuals who died by suicide used direct language prior to an attempt38, the low number of ambiguous and masked examples in the dataset means the study was not powered to rigorously compare model performance across different levels of risk explicitness. Future work should oversample ambiguous and masked language to stress-test the guardrails on implicit risk signals. Additionally, although the VMHG demonstrated strong accuracy, LLMs with advanced reasoning capabilities can face latency and cost constraints that may limit their scalability for real-time, high-volume mental health contexts, raising practical considerations about deployment feasibility. We also acknowledge the potential for implicit data leakage, since the same research team developed the prompt and the internal evaluation dataset. To rigorously confirm generalizability, future work should assess performance on datasets entirely unseen by the research team. Finally, all messages were simulated, not real user messages.

This initial work establishes a framework for identifying psychiatric crises in user prompts. The industry urgently needs a standardized assessment framework for safety concerns, coupled with representative and clinician-annotated datasets with real-world multi-turn conversations and be updated to capture evolving slang and coded language used in crises. Standardized protocols for red-teaming, adversarial testing, and post-deployment monitoring are needed to ensure safety as technology and contexts evolve. These steps will move the field towards a shared infrastructure for improving mental health safety guardrails, ensuring LLMs are reliable, clinically informed, and protective of users in crisis.

This work presents an advancement in digital mental health safety. We successfully developed the VMHG, a fit-for-purpose mental health safety guardrail that detects and categorizes subtle crisis signals. VMHG showed high, generalized performance on rigorously clinically annotated, internal and external datasets. Its balanced sensitivity and specificity indicate potential suitability for healthcare settings that must manage both crisis detection and alert fatigue, though confirmation through future red-teaming, adversarial testing, and validation on patient data will be essential before deployment in healthcare contexts, such as clinical triage, integrated behavioral health platforms, or large-scale digital health conversational agents.

Methods

This research was conducted as a fully simulated study using computer-generated data; no human participants were enrolled, and no identifiable personal or clinical information was collected. As a result, Consent to Participate is not applicable, and no Institutional Review Board (IRB) or ethics committee approval was required. Although human subjects were not involved, the study followed general principles of responsible research conduct and relevant institutional guidelines; therefore, Human Ethics and Consent to Participate declarations are not applicable. Likewise, this work does not constitute a clinical trial. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Verily mental health crisis dataset v1.0

We developed a simulated dataset of 1800 messages designed to reflect realistic digital communication patterns across mental health crisis and non-crisis messages, herein referred to as the Verily dataset. We used pre-trained language models to synthesize the dataset to mitigate the unavailability of highly sensitive mental health crisis messages, as has been done with other datasets22,23. The crisis messages (n = 900) included 100 messages for each of the following categories: abuse, neglect, eating-disorder behaviors, psychosis, self-harm, suicide, substance misuse, violence toward others, and mixed crisis presentation categories. These categories reflect clinical understanding of risks for psychiatric emergencies and adverse outcomes39–42, often serious enough to require mandated reporting. The non-crisis messages (n = 900) consisted of health-related conversational messages including, appointment logistics, behavior change, follow-up care, follow-up clarification, health education, health monitoring, insurance and billing, lab and wearable data interpretation, lifestyle modification, locating providers, medical record request, preventative care, referral request, test scheduling, symptom tracking, medication pickup logistics, chronic condition management, and diet and nutrition.

Crisis messages consisted of two categories, with 91.10% being classified as direct explicit expression of risk (e.g.,“I want to kill myself”) and 8.90% classified as ambiguous statements of harm (e.g., “I feel like I’m always walking on eggshells when he’s around n it scares me”) 8.6% of messages included slang or masked language (e.g., “13’ing” for suicide or “relief lines” for self-harm scars). These proportions reflect the composition of the simulated dataset and were not intended to model real-world epidemiological prevalence. Direct and ambiguous expressions of risk were mutually exclusive, but either category could overlap with masked language, as in “dude teh urge to unalive myself is getting stronger”, where “unalive” serves as both a clear indicator of harm and slang for suicide. To ensure coverage of diverse real-world writing styles, the crisis and non-crisis messages were constructed to reflect a broad spectrum of real-world language mechanics and texting behaviors from the literature43–46 and online communities47–49. Across the full dataset, crisis and non-crisis messages contained similar rates of the following non-mutually exclusive language characteristics:

Language mechanics errors (Overall 55.90%, Crisis 56.60%, Non-crisis 55.30%): spelling, grammar, punctuation, capitalization, or spacing issues (e.g., “definately”, “recieve”, and “seperate”).

Textese (Overall 45.80%, Crisis 42.90%, Non-crisis 48.70%): deliberate shorthand or abbreviations (e.g., “u” and “bc”).

Emojis and symbolic markers (Overall 13.50%, Crisis 15.70%, Non-crisis 11.30%): symbols conveying affect (e.g., and ).

Two licensed clinicians (BWN, AR) independently reviewed and annotated 1800 messages as crisis or non-crisis with high inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s κ = 0.99). Discrepancies were resolved through adjudication and consensus.

NVIDIA Aegis AI content safety dataset 2.0

We utilized a subset of the NVIDIA Aegis AI Content Safety Dataset 2.030 (cc-by-4.0), specifically 794 messages (397 crisis, 397 non-crisis) that were human data, focusing on “Suicide and Self Harm,” excluding samples from the Suicide Detection dataset and duplicate rows. Two licensed clinicians (BWN, JT) independently reviewed messages, reclassifying 6.927% of the total (26 “safe” to “unsafe,” 29 “unsafe” to “safe”) to ensure label accuracy.

Safety filters, content moderation, and guardrail models

The Verily Mental Health Guardrail (VMHG) operates as a two-stage classifier and was implemented using prompt-engineering on an LLM. In the first step, the model determines whether a message contains any crisis-related content. If a crisis is detected, a second step classifies the specific crisis type(s). The crisis-detection and multi-label classification are generated directly from a structured prompt that encodes the label schema and clinical reasoning principles. The underlying model parameters and architecture remain unchanged, and all outputs are formatted as JSON to support consistent downstream integration.

We selected two open-source content moderation guardrails, with specific categories that included safety and mental health. The first, OpenAI’s omni-moderation-latest model22,25, built on GPT-4o, automatically flags text and images for policy violations across 13 predefined categories and returns a binary flag and JSON output. The second, nvidia/llama-3.1-nemoguard-8b-content-safety21,24 is an open-source model that classifies content across 23 risk categories and outputs a JSON.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.9.6). The analyses were structured to evaluate the internal performance of the VMHG, followed by evaluation with the NVIDIA dataset, and against external guardrails.

The internal evaluation assessed the VMHG on the Verily dataset. This included the performance of the Stage 1 classifier for overall crisis detection, and the performance of the Stage 2 multi-label classifier on messages flagged as “Crisis” by the Stage 1 model. The Stage 2 evaluation used a one-vs-rest framework, with outcomes including per-category metrics, a confusion matrix to analyze misclassification patterns, and macro-averaged metrics to assess balanced performance across categories. On the NVIDIA dataset, only the performance of the Stage 1 classifier for overall crisis detection was assessed due to the availability of clinician-reviewed labels (i.e., only crisis vs. non-crisis labels were generated by clinicians as part of this study).

For the comparative analysis, the VMHG was compared against benchmark models on simulated (n = 1800) and external (n = 794) datasets. For overall crisis detection, differences in the primary outcomes of sensitivity and specificity were assessed using an omnibus Cochran’s Q test, followed by pairwise McNemar’s tests with a Bonferroni correction (α = 0.017) for multiple comparisons. Operational utility was evaluated by projecting the positive predictive value (PPV) across a range of real-world prevalence rates.

For the comparative per-category analysis on the Verily simulated dataset, our model’s hierarchical predictions were evaluated as a “flat” system on the full dataset (n = 1800) to ensure identical denominators across all models, a requirement for the validity of the paired statistical tests (Cochran’s Q and McNemar’s). Differences in metrics were assessed with the same statistical approach. Due to incompatible crisis categories across models, the benchmark models’ overall crisis flag was evaluated against each specific ground truth. This comparison was designed to quantify the effectiveness of generic safety flags as implicit detectors for specific clinical risks, thereby assessing whether broad detection is sufficient for clinical safety guardrail needs.
