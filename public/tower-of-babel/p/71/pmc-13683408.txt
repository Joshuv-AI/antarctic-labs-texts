Cathepsin G and neutrophil elastase control Leishmania infantum parasitism through TLRs-dependent induction of IFN-α and TNF-α
Authors: Amy Goundry, Aislan C Vivarini, Nathalia S Rodrigues, Ulisses G Lopes, Jeremy C Mottram, Ana Paula C A Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeishmania infantum and Leishmania donovani are protozoan parasites that cause visceral leishmaniasis, a potentially fatal disease targeting the liver, spleen and bone marrow. To survive, these parasites manipulate host macrophage microbicidal responses and metabolic pathways. Here, we demonstrate that the host serine proteases neutrophil elastase (NE) and cathepsin G (CG), acting in concert with Toll-like receptors TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4, induce the inflammatory cytokines IFN-α and TNF-α to control L. infantum infection in macrophages. Conversely, in vivo experiments revealed that while NE and CG are necessary for inflammatory responses, including IL-12, nitric oxide, and CCL2 production, they unexpectedly promote splenic parasitism. CG-deficient mice failed to recruit Ly6Chi myeloid cells to the spleen, providing a potential mechanism for their reduced parasite burden. Finally, our data suggest that unlike L. donovani, L. infantum cannot hijack cellular host serine peptidases and TLR signalling for survival, yet it remains dependent on these host enzymes to establish systemic spleen infection.Subject terms: Parasite host response, Mechanisms of disease, Infection

Introduction

The clinical outcome of infection with Leishmania spp. results from a combination of parasite and host factors, including the parasite species or strain, the host genetic background, and the immune response. In human visceral leishmaniasis (HVL), symptomatic individuals develop liver granulomas and significant alterations in the spleen architecture, leading to organ dysfunction that is potentially deadly. There are significant differences in the pathogenesis of HVL in India and East Africa, caused by L. donovani, and in Brazil, caused by L. infantum, which can also be observed in experimental murine models1. In HVL, severe bleeding and coagulopathy have been reported in Brazil, which enhances chance of death by 3.5-fold, greater than all other risk factors2. These conditions have been attributed to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and the severity of the disease in patients from Brazil has been associated with a higher concentration of serum inflammatory cytokines2. At the cellular level, mechanisms that enable or restrain L. donovani growth in macrophages were well explored, while less is known for L. infantum. Of relevance to this study, type I interferons (IFNs; IFN-α and IFN-β) have been reported as major regulators of macrophage parasitism by different Leishmania species. For example, while type I IFNs help to control experimental infection by L. major3, IFN-β was shown to favor infection by L. amazonensis4 and by L. donovani5,6, downstream of Toll-like (TLR) receptor stimulation. In experimental mice infections with L. donovani, type I IFNs were further identified as negative regulators of CD4+ T cells, and mice knockout for the common type I IFN receptor (ifnar-/-) have significantly lower parasite burdens7.

At the cellular level, we have previously uncovered that the host cell serine peptidase of the S1A family, neutrophil elastase (NE), is one of the upstream players in the pathway leading to IFN-β production in Leishmania-infected primary macrophages5,6,8,9. NE and cathepsin G (CG) are abundant not only in neutrophil granules but also in monocytes10, and perform proteolytic modification of cytokines and chemokines, receptor activation, and cleavage of coagulation factors11, which might be of relevance during leishmaniasis. We have previously shown that NE activity, TLR4, TLR2, and double-stranded RNA-activated protein kinase (PKR) take part in a molecular pathway that results in the production of IFN-β and in the expression of additional genes with relevance for macrophage parasitism4,6,9. The activation of the NE-TLR4 pathway, and the resulting IFN-β, has opposite consequences for parasite fate, depending on the Leishmania species. While NE and IFN-β are detrimental to L. major8,9, L. donovani requires NE activity and the resulting IFN-β for efficient growth in macrophages, and to develop high systemic parasite loads in mice5,6. In addition to NE-TLR4, TLR2 also contributes to the induction of IFN-β in macrophages infected with either L. donovani6 or L. amazonensis4, and is positively correlated with parasitism. Considering that the visceralizing species, L. donovani and L. infantum, can provoke varying pathologies in HVL, and might have evolved to trigger different molecular mechanisms at the cellular level to enable efficient parasitism, we were prompted to investigate which are the mechanistic requirements involving S1A peptidases for parasitism by L. infantum at the cellular level.

Using chemical inhibition and genetic approaches, we investigated how NE and CG affect the uptake, survival, and intracellular growth of L. infantum in macrophages and mapped the underlying molecular pathways associated with the infection in vitro. Furthermore, we assessed whether SPs affect the in vivo infection by using transgenic mice. We found that NE and CG, and TLRs 2, 3, and 4, act in conjunction in a molecular pathway induced in macrophages upon L. infantum infection, resulting in the production of TNFα and IFNα, which limits L. infantum development at the cellular level, while relying of these SPs to establish splenic infection in mice.

Results

We reported that L. donovani-induced IFN-β expression in macrophages is crucial for parasite growth/development in the infected cell, and this is conveyed upstream through the activation of a pathway that requires NE activity and TLR45. In contrast, the activation of the NE-TLR4 pathway by L. major, leading to IFN-β and TNF-α expression, results in parasite killing8. To investigate how this molecular mechanism applies to macrophage infection by L. infantum, and to dissect the underlying pathways, we first sought to compare the pattern of expression of inflammation-related genes by peritoneal macrophages from C57BL/6 mice infected in vitro with two visceralizing Leishmania species. For this, we used an L. infantum reference strain from Brazil and a Sudanese L. donovani strain, the latter of which had been the focus of previous studies from our group5,6. We first verified that the L. infantum strain was able to infect primary macrophages obtained from C57BL/6 mice and cause in vivo infection in the same mouse strain (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig 1). Next, we performed parallel macrophage infections with the two species to determine the pattern of expression of type 1 IFNs and of additional inflammatory genes. To enable comparisons to be made with data of our previous studies with L. donovani5,6, we opted to determine gene expression at six hours after parasite uptake. We promptly detected mRNA for type I interferons, with IFN-α induced at similar levels from macrophages infected with either species (Fig. 2A), while IFN-β was induced exclusively by L. donovani (Fig. 2B). Higher TNF-α mRNA (Fig. 2C) was detected in L. infantum-infected macrophages than in uninfected controls. Exposure of macrophages to inert beads had no effect in the expression of those cytokines, indicating that the induction of mRNA was a specific response to infection. The expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (Fig. 2D) was significantly induced in macrophages infected with L. infantum, as compared to uninfected controls. To check if counter-inflammatory responses were being triggered, we evaluated superoxide dismutase (SOD1) (Fig. 2E), related to the mechanisms of immune evasion by L. amazonensis12, and IL-10 (Fig. 2F), associated with the control of L. donovani parasitism and pathology13. L. infantum also induced SOD1 and IL-10 in infected macrophages. These observations indicated that macrophages infected with L. infantum seem prone to an inflammatory response (i.e., higher levels of TNF-α, IFN-α, and iNOS), while not producing IFN-β, which is opposite for that observed in L. donovani infection.

Considering that there is a correlation between NE and IFN-β for sustained macrophage infection by L. donovani5,6 and that we observed opposite macrophage response in infections with both species, we asked whether NE plays a role in L. infantum infection (Fig. 3). Infection in macrophages from C57BL/6 mice showed that parasites did not grow within 72 h after parasite uptake (Fig. 3A). However, a much higher parasite burden at 72 hours occurred in the presence of an irreversible chloro-methyl ketone peptidyl inhibitor of NE, MeOSuc-AAPV-CMK, suggesting that enzyme inhibition favored parasite survival and/or proliferation. This was further addressed by analyzing the timecourse of infection using macrophages derived from NE-knockout mice (ela2-/-), in comparison to those from the background C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 3B). There was no difference in the uptake of parasites by the macrophages from the two mouse lines after 3 hours, and a proportion of the intracellular parasites were killed by 24 h, presumably of the internalized promastigote forms. At 48 hours, an increase in the number of intracellular parasites occurred, denoting amastigote proliferation. However, while a 25% reduction in the parasite burden was observed in macrophages from C57BL/6 mice at 72 h, the burden remained unchanged in macrophages from ela2-/- mice, confirming that NE contributes to the control of L. infantum inside macrophages. Although the levels of parasite internalization differed between assays, the pattern of infection within the macrophages over 72 h was reproducible. Such variation in the baseline phagocytosis of parasites is expected, since the activation levels of primary macrophages recruited with thioglycolate might differ between experiments. Independent experiments showing the kinetics of parasite infection/survival in macrophages of C57BL/6 mice are depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1.

To address if NE took part in the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines upon L. infantum infection, we determined the mRNA levels for type I IFNs and TNF-α (Fig. 4). We found that NE contributes to the induction of inflammatory IFN-α (Fig. 4A, D) and TNF-α (Fig. 4B, E) by L. infantum, which might contribute to parasite killing and/or prevention of growth observed at 72 h. Again, we did not detect induction of IFN-β by L. infantum-infected macrophages (Fig. 4C, F). While induced in macrophages from C57BL/6 mice, iNOS, SOD1, and IL-10 were not induced in ela2-/- macrophages upon infection (Fig. 4G–I). Even though fold-increase of iNOS mRNA in infected macrophages in relation to uninfected controls were lower than in Fig. 2, the observation that the parasite induces iNOS in C57BL/6 macrophages was consistent in both experiments.

The assumption that the pro-inflammatory cytokines induced in the macrophages from C57BL/6 mice affected the intracellular survival of L. infantum was directly tested by the addition of exogenous cytokines immediately after parasite uptake (Fig. 5). L. infantum was sensitive to IFN-α, whose induction depends on NE, as exogenous IFN-α reverted the sustained growth in ela2-/- macrophages (Fig. 5A). A similar pattern was observed for TNF-α (Fig. 5B), which provoked a drastic reduction in the number of intracellular parasites at 72 h in both C57BL/6 and ela2-/- macrophages. In contrast, there was no effect on parasite burdens upon addition of IFN-β (Fig. 5C), supporting the notion that, while this type I IFN is crucial for infection by L. donovani and L. amazonensis, it is dispensable for the infection by L. infantum, at least so in the levels tested here. A time-course infection of macrophages from mice deficient in the IFN-α/β receptor alpha chain (ifnar-/-), one of the two chains of the receptor for type I IFNs, further showed that at the 72 h timepoint, type I IFN signaling, presumably through IFN-α, is required to control L. infantum burden in macrophages (Fig. 5D).

Since the molecular components that were previously shown to be beneficial for L. donovani infection of macrophages5,6 did not seem to be beneficial for the L. infantum-macrophage infection, we asked how this relates to the requirement of different TLRs for infection. In agreement with a mechanistic link between TLR2 and NE, macrophages derived from TLR2-knockout mice (tlr2-/-) displayed a significant increase in the parasite burden after 72 h, as compared to macrophages from background C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, parasites were unable to grow in macrophages from TLR3-knockout mice (tlr3-/-), indicating that this receptor contributes to infection (Fig. 6B). The role of TLR4 was verified upon addition of a selective inhibitor of TLR4 signaling, Resatorvid14, which increased parasite burdens by 100%, as compared to untreated macrophages (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. 2). Although NE is primarily an enzyme present in neutrophil granules, it is knowingly present in mononuclear cells10, and we have previously detected it at the surface of macrophages, colocalized with TLR4 and L. donovani, enabling the NE-TLR4 platform to be triggered upon parasite infection5. Next, we sought to address the role of TLR4 in the induction of the expression of the leishmanicidal cytokines described in the above results. We observed that inhibition of TLR4 signaling with resatorvid prevented the induction of IFN-α (Fig. 6D), TNF-α (Fig. 6E) and iNOS (Fig. 6F). These results show that both NE and TLR4 are required for the induction of IFN-α and TNF-α, and to control parasite growth in macrophages. The findings that the absence of NE (Fig. 3) or the blockade of TLR4 function alone (Fig. 6C), and affect the NE-TLR4 axis, allowing L. infantum to reach higher numbers in macrophages, support the notion that NE and TLR4 act in conjunction to promote the anti-parasitic effect. We next analyzed if/how additional TLRs contributed to parasite-induced gene expression. As seen in the ela2-/- macrophages, the expression of IFN-α, TNF-α, and iNOS was not induced in the tlr2-/- macrophages upon infection as compared to the C57BL/6 macrophages (Fig. 7A–C). A similar pattern was observed for the contribution of TLR3. IFN-α and TNF-α did not seem to be induced in tlr3-/- macrophages, while significantly induced in infected C57BL/6 macrophages (Fig. 7D, E). Again, IFN-β mRNA was not detected upon infection and this remained unchanged in the absence of TLR3 (Fig. 7F), indicating that this TLR also plays a role in the induction of those genes by L. infantum.

The cytoplasmic kinase PKR, associated with responses to double-stranded RNA viruses, was identified as the main component of the signaling pathway leading to type I IFNs in macrophage infection by L. amazonensis15. We showed that the NE-TLR4 pathway is also conveyed through PKR, and the resulting pattern of gene expression varies according to the Leishmania species, i.e., in a model of transgenic L. major it leads to IFN-β, TNF-α, and parasite killing9, while in L. donovani infections it results in IFN-β, IL-10, SOD1, OASL2, and parasite growth6. We next verified if PKR was associated with the control of L. infantum in macrophages. At 48 h, the parasite burden in macrophages of PKR-knockout mice (pkr-/-) was higher than that of macrophages of the background 129/Sv mice (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Fig. 3), linking this kinase to the anti-parasite activity. At 72 h, burdens were not sustained in 129/Sv macrophages, which agrees with a high leishmanicidal activity for macrophages from this genetic background. We tested whether NE activity played a role in the leishmanicidal phenotype of those macrophages by adding the NE inhibitor. This treatment increased parasite burdens in 129/Sv macrophages at 48 h, which remained elevated at 72 h, as compared to untreated controls, supporting a role for NE in controlling parasitism. In pkr-/- macrophages, we found higher parasitism at 48 h as compared to 129/Sv macrophages, confirming that PKR contributes to the responses leading to parasite control. In contrast, the NE inhibitor did not further enhance parasite burden at 48 h in those macrophages, supporting the notion that the leishmanicidal effect of NE is conveyed through PKR, and in its absence, enzyme inhibition does not provide further advantage to sustain infection.

Besides NE, cathepsin G (CG) is a S1A serine peptidase that is present in monocytes10, and we asked if it plays a role in infection by L. infantum. We observed that macrophages derived from CG-knockout mice (ctsg-/-) were more permissive to the proliferation of L. infantum at 48–72 hours as compared to macrophages from background mice (Fig. 9A). To test whether NE and CG acted through a common pathway, we used a CG inhibitor in infections of macrophages from ela2-/- and respective C57BL/6 background mice. While the CG inhibitor had a mild effect in increasing parasite burdens at 72 hours in C57BL/6 macrophages, it did not further increase parasitism in ela2-/- macrophages (Fig. 9B). This suggests that in C57BL/6 macrophages, although both S1A peptidases are required to control infection by L. infantum they do not seem to have additive effects. This was further tested in C57BL/6 macrophages by checking if both NEI and CGI would display an additive effect in sustaining parasite burden at 72 h (Fig. 9C). The addition of each inhibitor separately or in conjunction exerted a similar effect in sustaining parasite burden at 72 h, favoring the conclusion that both enzymes do not act additively but rather in common pathways in C57BL/6 macrophages. We further verified the effect of the NE inhibitor in macrophages of CG knockout mice. Inhibition of NE increased parasite burdens at 72 h in 129/Sv (Fig. 9D). In ctsg-/- macrophages, results were variable, and there was no clear increase in the burden at 72 h. This supports the notion that NE and CG do not have additive roles to enable parasite killing. Furthermore, we observed increased parasite burdens at 72 h in ifnar-/- macrophages (Fig. 9D), confirming that L. infantum infection is negatively controlled by type-I IFN. 129/Sv macrophages likely kill L. infantum through IFN-α, whose levels are induced upon infection, are sufficient to prevent growth at 72 h, and this requires CG. Given that L. infantum was unable to colonize macrophages from TLR3-knockout mice, we asked whether this was a consequence of NE- or CG-mediated pathways. Inhibition of either peptidase promptly enhanced parasitism of C57BL/6 macrophages (Fig. 9E) but, conversely, was ineffective in restoring parasite burdens in tlr3-/- macrophages (Fig. 9F). This suggests that although TLR3 contributes to induction of IFN-α and TNF-α via NE and CG, that promote killing, this alone cannot account for the lack of parasite growth in 72 h in the absence of TLR3, and likely additional molecules and/or pathways downstream of TLR3 are necessary to support L. infantum infection. The contribution of CG for the induction of gene expression was then addressed by qPCR. We found that the induction of IFN-α, TNF-α, and iNOS (Fig. 9G-I) in infected macrophages was dependent on CG. Even though IFN-α and TNF-α were induced in macrophages deficient in CG (Fig. 9G-H), the levels were like those provoked by inert beads, suggesting that, at least to some extent, IFN−α induction is dependent on CG, while it was completely abolished in the absence of NE. This indicates that both NE and CG contribute directly to the control of L. infantum in macrophages through the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and presumably NOS, while NE is a major contributor to the induction of IFN-α.

The contribution of host SPs for the initial establishment of parasite infection in the target organs was subsequently verified in the experimental mouse model infected with L. infantum promastigotes. In the absence of NE, at day 7, parasite burdens in the spleen were significantly reduced as compared to those of the background C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 10A; P = 0.0016), while they were similar in the infected livers (Fig. 10B; P = 0.5460). These observations were discrepant with the leishmanicidal effect of NE for macrophages in vitro. However, the gene expressions of IFN-α and TNF-α (Fig. 10C, D) were reduced in the spleens of infected ela2-/- mice, as compared to those of infected C57BL/6 mice, mirroring the findings in infected macrophages in vitro. The analysis of main inflammatory cytokines at the protein level revealed that the microenvironment in the spleens of infected ela2-/- mice differed from that of C57BL/6 mice in a variety of ways. In the absence of NE, there were significant levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α (Fig. 10E, F) in the spleen, which were significantly increased when compared to infected C57Bl/6 mice. Even though the baseline levels in uninfected knockout mice are generally higher than in C57Bl/6 mice, no statistical significance was found between those groups. In contrast, IL-12 and NO levels (Fig. 10G, H) were elevated in infected C57Bl/6 mice as compared to uninfected controls. In contrast, in mice deficient in NE, infection does not induce IL12 or NO, suggesting a role for this peptidase in the accumulation/induction of those inflammatory molecules during early stages of infection by L. infantum. Furthermore, there was no statistical significance for the levels of IL-10 among the groups (Fig. 10I). The pattern of chemokines was also verified to aid in the prediction of cellular subtypes that could be recruited to the spleen at the initial stages of infection. The protein levels of all tested chemokines in the spleens differed between infected C57BL/6 and ela2-/- mice, as compared to their respective uninfected controls. While infection induced high levels of CCL2 and CCL5 (also known as RANTES) in C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 10J, K), those remained unchanged in ela2-/- mice upon infection, revealing that NE is required to induce those chemokines in L. infantum-infected mice. CCL2, but not CCL5, is involved in the early recruitment of monocytes16, which could increase the delivery of potential host cells for Leishmania to the spleen. In vivo infection did not induce changes in CXCL2 (also known as MIP-2) nor CX3CL1 in the spleen (Fig. 10L, M), as compared to uninfected controls. However, ela2-/- mice contained higher background levels of those chemokines compared to C57BL/6 mice, which could affect the baseline composition of the cellular subpopulations in the spleens of the knockout mice.

Taken together, our results show that the same molecular machinery utilized by L. donovani and L. infantum can have opposite consequences to parasite fate at the cellular level. While both NE and CG exert a prominent role in controlling parasite burdens in macrophages via IFN-α and TNF-α, these enzymes contribute positively to the initial survival of L. infantum in the spleens of mice, likely due to changes in the balance of a network of infiltrating cellular subsets and cytokines, which could favor the recruitment of cells that contribute to initial parasitism. The conclusions are summarized in the schematic representation (Fig. 12).

Modulation of L. infantum infection by neutrophil elastase and cathepsin G.

During the infection of inflammatory primary murine macrophages by L. infantum, neutrophil elastase (NE) acts in conjunction with TLR4 to induce the expression of pro-inflammatory IFN-α, TNFα, iNOS, and of anti-inflammatory IL-10 and SOD-1, but not of IFN-β, within 6 h. IFN-α and TNF-α contribute to control intracellular parasitism at day 3 post-infection. TLR2 also contributes to control cellular parasitism and to provoke expression of TNFα and iNOS, but contributes to a less extent to the induction of IFN-α. TLR3 contributes to the expression of IFN-α and TNFα. Nevertheless, TLR3 and protein kinase R (PKR) are required for sustained macrophage parasitism by L. infantum. Cathepsin G is also required to promote the expression of IFN-α, TNF-α, and iNOS in infected macrophages, and acts in a common pathway with NE. In mice of the C57Bl/6 background, at the initial stage of infection (day 7), NE contributes to elevated mRNA of IFN-α, TNFα, and to the production of CCL-2, IL-12, and nitric oxide (NO) in the spleen. Despite the less inflammatory spleen environment in the absence of NE, the enzyme contributes to promote parasite burden in vivo. In mice of the 129/Sv background, cathepsin G did not contribute to the levels of TNF-α, CCL-2, and NO, but was required for the production of IL-12 and CCL-2, as well as for the recruitment of myeloid cells, especially that of Ly6+ monocytes.

Discussion

The diverse species of Leishmania are finely adapted to survive and proliferate in macrophages, and have evolved different strategies to modulate the host cell response. We have previously described a mechanistic pathway dependent on the proteolytic activity of NE that acts distinctively to promote or control infection, depending on the Leishmania species5,6,8,9,18. In common, NE present in macrophages requires TLR4 to induce responses upon infection, but the outcome varies with the Leishmania species. While in L. major infection, NE activity induces TNF-α and both type I IFNs, IFN-α and IFN-β, leading to parasite killing, in L. donovani-infected macrophages, high levels of NE-induced IFN-β are crucial for parasite growth. This is in line with other reports showing that in L. amazonensis infections, TLR2-induced IFN-β is likewise essential for parasite proliferation and sustained intracellular burdens4,15. In common, the induction of type I IFNs is a central response in infected macrophages, and Leishmania has adapted to either avoid or allow it to take place. L. major inhibits NE activity by its endogenous inhibitor ISP2, while L. donovani does not express ISP2 as an adaptation to engage the TLR-NE pathway required for growth. Here, we found that the responses induced in macrophages by L. infantum are remarkably different from those observed with L. donovani, suggesting that the two visceralizing species have evolved alternative ways to survive in macrophages. Even though we have previously shown that in chronic infection in BALB/c mice with either L. donovani (LV9 strain) or L. infantum, the host immunity-related genes induced at the mRNA level are similar, we did not dissect how specific pathways could directly contribute to infection of the host cell19. Remarkably, L. infantum was less adapted than L. donovani to modulate antiparasitic responses of macrophages at the cellular level. L. infantum induced high expression of iNOS and was less capable than L. donovani to induce SOD1, suggesting that L. infantum is less efficient in controlling oxidative stress, at least so in thioglycolate-primed macrophages. IL-10 is a main cytokine to act on macrophages to render them permissive of Leishmania20, and mice lacking IL-10 are highly resistant to L. donovani infection21. The fact that high levels of IL-10 mRNA (nearly 20-fold increase) were induced in macrophages by L. infantum argues against variations in IL-10 being the main reason for the increased signs of oxidative stress in L. infantum-infected macrophages. It remains to be determined if this premise also applies to Kupffer cells, which are the host cells for visceral Leishmania in active liver infection.

In common with L. major, we found that engagement of the NE-TLR axis by L. infantum induces pro-inflammatory cytokines that are detrimental for parasite growth. More specifically, type I IFNs are able to kill intracellular parasites, and the levels induced by L. infantum were sufficient to control parasitism, as shown by increased parasite loads in macrophages from IFNAR-knockout mice. Of note, we found that IFN-β was irrelevant to L. infantum capability to colonize macrophages, i.e., besides not being induced, exogenous IFN-β did not affect parasite burdens in host cells, denoting a main difference between the adaptations of L. donovani and L. infantum. Considering that both IFN-α and IFN-β act through IFNAR, one could expect that both cytokines would exert an effect in intracellular parasitism. However, differences in receptor binding affinity for the different type I IFNs and the number of surface receptor molecules available for binding determine different activities of the individual IFNs22. In addition, controlled receptor endocytosis is also a major player in the regulation of the signal transduction of IFNAR and of the downstream gene expression profile23. In that sense, exogenously added IFNs may have induced different signaling outcomes with consequences to parasitism, resulting in effective parasite control via IFN-α, but not via IFN-β. It is intriguing that while L. donovani induced a detectable increase in the expression of both IFN-α and IFN-β6, we only observed increases in IFN-α in L. infantum-infected cells. This pattern was repeatedly observed in infected macrophages, which could otherwise induce IFN-β upon stimulation with poly I:C, showing that the macrophages used in our infection model did not have an impairment in the expression of IFN-β. While there is one IFN-β gene, there are multiple IFN-α genes that can be grouped in clusters and vary in number according to the species, there being 14 IFN-α genes in the mouse24. Since we used degenerate primers to detect all IFN-α subtypes, we cannot discriminate if a specific IFN-α gene, or which subtypes, are being transcribed in response to L. infantum. While expression of IFN-β can be achieved in isolation, the expression of IFN-α genes is usually preceded by and enhanced by IFN-β25, which was not the case in L. infantum-infected macrophages. Alternatively, a subtle amount of IFN-β that is below detection in our methods could be induced, helping to increase expression of IFN-α via an autocrine loop. IFN-β expression is regulated by transcription factors that bind to four regulatory promoter domains, the main factors being IRF3 and IRF7. Although the promoter domains for the IFN-β gene and for the different IFN-α genes share 95% identity, there are several insertions and three short deletions in the promoters of the different IFN-α genes, as compared to that of IFN-β, that alter their binding to IRFs. Such differences could help to explain why L. infantum induced IFN-α expression, but not IFN-β, at least at the timepoints used in this study. The current model proposes differential regulation of IFN-α genes by activation of either IRF3 or IRF7, or co-activation of both, depending on their protein levels22,26. We have shown that L. donovani induces the translocation of IRF3 to the nucleus of the infected macrophage6. In a different infection model using RAW cells and L. donovani 8G83, Saha et al. reported reduced phosphorylation of IRF3 at early infection (15-60 min), as compared to cells stimulated with LPS, even though IRF3 phosphorylation still took place at 120 min post-infection27. However, the degree of IRF3 translocation to the nucleus was not addressed. Infected RAW cells also exhibited elevated levels of heme oxygenase, whose heme degradation byproducts correlated with reduced TNF-α, NFkB, and IRF3 phosphorylation. Although not addressed here, it is possible that differences in the balance of IRF3 and/or IRF7 levels in the L. infantum-infected cell also contribute to the selective increase in mRNA for IFN-α.

Previous work showed that L. infantum induces IFN-β mRNA in bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (DCs) via TLR4, and that this receptor was central to regulate the transcriptional profile of infected DCs28. Although we have not addressed the pattern of gene expression of DCs in our work, we did not observe IFN-β induction in primary macrophages, suggesting that engagement of TLR4 by L. infantum can have different consequences for innate and adaptive immunity, depending on the cellular subtype. Optimal IFN-β induction by L. donovani required the additive roles of TLR2, presumably via lipophosphoglycan (LPG), and of TLR4 via NE6. Although the exact mechanisms through which IFN-β promotes macrophage colonization by L. donovani are unknown, an elegant study has connected the requirement of type I IFNs with mitochondrial biogenesis and parasite burden in macrophages29. In line with our findings, the authors found that both TLR2 and TLR4 were required for long-term parasitism in macrophages, denoting a positive role for those TLRs, and for downstream IFN-β, in the ability of L. donovani to grow in macrophages29. In line with opposite adaptations of the two visceralizing species, we found that both TLR2 and TLR4 contribute to control macrophage parasitism by L. infantum. While it is expected that TLR2 is promptly activated by L. infantum via LPG, by analogy to other Leishmania species, it is intriguing at first that the parasite engages the NE-TLR4 pathway, which drastically reduces long-term macrophage colonization. Although L. infantum could grow in inflammatory macrophages in vitro, this took place at an apparent suboptimal condition because parasite burdens were significantly improved by the inhibition or absence of NE or CG.

TNF-α and IFN-α showed high leishmanicidal activity against intracellular L. infantum, which lies well with the notion that TNF is leishmanicidal30. The expression of these cytokines was induced by NE and was fully dependent on TLR2 and TLR3, which was also the case for the induction of IFN-β by L. donovani6. This indicates that a common upstream signaling pathway is shared by both species, and suggests that the direction of the response diverges downstream of TLR3 and PKR. Downstream TLR3 signaling is TRIF-dependent, leading to a complex cascade that involves NF-κB, AP1, and IRF3 for the induction of multiple genes30. Since ubiquitination and phosphorylation of many signaling molecules of the TLR3 pathway are essential to tune the response31, it is feasible that L. infantum and L. donovani might differ in how they provoke alterations in the network of downstream players. While NE acts through TLR4, LPG is expected to be the main activator of TLR232 and the crosstalk between both receptors could result from a converged single signaling platform at the cell surface, formed at the region of contact with the parasite. Indeed, we had shown that L. major induces the colocalization of NE and CD11b at the surface of macrophages8, while NE and TLR4 colocalize with L. donovani5. We reported that TLR3 activation by L. amazonensis involves a nucleic acid-independent pathway by direct proteolytic cleavage of the receptor33. Lack of TLR3 resulted in lower IFN-β and IL-10 and increased IL-12 in macrophages, which became less permissive to L. amazonensis growth33. Here, L. infantum did not induce IFN-β and was refractory to it, so its dependence on TLR3 might rely on other mechanisms. Considering that the induction of SOD1 was dependent on TLR3, it is possible that the absence of this enzyme to counteract oxidative stress in a condition that is already suboptimal for parasite growth renders macrophages fully resistant to infection.

We have also shown that cathepsin G contributes directly to control infection, and we had indication that it does not play an additive role to that of NE in macrophages in the C57BL6 background. Since the increase in IFN-α mRNA in ctsg-/- macrophages was still greater than that in uninfected controls, whereas it was completely abolished in ela2-/- macrophages, it is plausible to reason that while IFN-α induction depends mainly on the crosstalk between NE-TLR4 and LPG-TLR2, additional mechanisms could also contribute to CG-dependent leishmanicidal activity. CG was described as a pivotal enzyme determining pyroptosis in monocytes and neutrophils, and its inhibition increases cellular survival at steady-state levels34. In macrophages, CG was responsible for the cleavage of Gasdermin-D and increased release of inflammatory cytokines upon lipopolysaccharide challenge34. Noteworthy, Gasdermin-D was found as the activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome during Leishmania infection, being connected to host resistance35. In such a model, although the inflammasome was activated within 2 h of infection with L. amazonensis, the parasite prevented subsequent macrophage pyroptosis, and Gasdermin-D cleavage resulted in a noncanonical 25 kDa fragment. It remains to be tested whether L. infantum is also capable of downmodulating host cell death. Although we have not tested inflammasome activation, an attractive hypothesis is that the leishmanicidal role of CG involves Gasdermin-D-dependent inflammasome activation.

Finally, our in vivo experimental infections revealed that both NE and CG are fundamental for establishing early splenic infection, despite their paradoxically leishmanicidal role at the cellular level. We determined that NE is crucial for dampening early IFN-γ and TNF-α. This suppression may be critical for allowing infection to take hold while the adaptive immune response is still mounting, as these cytokines are potent activators of microbicidal M1-type macrophage responses. Conversely, mice lacking NE or CG failed to induce splenic IL-12 upon infection compared to their genetic background controls, suggesting that these proteases may participate in driving the early Th1 response. Notably, NE exhibited a strong pro-inflammatory profile in vivo, also mediating increases in NO and RANTES, and in the mRNA levels for IFN-α.

While the opposing roles of NE and CG in parasite survival in vitro and in vivo initially appear discrepant, our cellular recruitment data offer a plausible explanation for why L. infantum requires these proteases to establish parasitism by day 7. At this early time point, infection is likely still restricted to the initial wave of host cells, given that the full intracellular replication cycle of the parasite exceeds 5 days. We found that NE is crucial for the production of monocyte chemoattractant CCL2, while CG is required for the recruitment of Ly6+ myeloid cells. In line with this, Romano et al. demonstrated in a murine L. major infection model that inflammatory monocytes serve as the primary mononuclear hosts for initial parasite replication36. Therefore, by driving CCL2 production and subsequent myeloid cell recruitment, NE and CG inadvertently provide L. infantum with a continuous supply of highly susceptible host cells to sustain early splenic infection.

Here, the most noticeable differences in vivo were the lack of CCL2 in the infected spleens in the absence of NE. We confirmed that macrophages infected in vitro induced CCL2 mRNA, which was fully dependent on NE, even though in vivo, a broad range of cells can be the source of CCL2. Besides being the main chemoattractant of myeloid cells, CCL2 has a bigger role in leukocyte biology, impacting its behavior, polarization, autophagy, and survival16. In addition to recruiting T-cells, CCL2 can direct their maturation to the Th2 phenotype37, contributing to the modulation of infected tissue architecture. Noteworthy some of those other functions of CCL2 can depend on additional stimuli, and it is feasible to hypothesize that in the context of L. infantum infection, this cytokine plays a central role to enable parasitism, enabling recruitment of myeloid cells. Thus, preserving NE function in vivo could contribute to the production of adequate levels of CCL2, which in turn, adds to the recruitment of monocytes. Indeed, in a model of L. major infection, infiltrating permissive CCR2+ monocytes in the skin were pivotal in the establishment and the expansion of a replication niche for parasite38. A similar scenario could dictate initial infection in the spleen, where monocytes could be a primary source of permissive cells for L. infantum. This fits well with the increase in the Ly6Chi population in the spleens of infected mice, which was absent from the spleens of infected ctsg-/- mice. Notably, it has been reported that CG promotes monocyte chemotaxis directly via proteolytic activation of PAR-139. Combined with CLL2, the proteolytic action of CG, resulting in chemotaxis of myeloid cells, could be crucial to maintain Ly6+ cellular infiltrates in the spleen. If so, this could help to explain why L. infantum has adapted to allow activation of macrophages by host SPs that, although resulting in cytokines that restrict macrophage colonization, at the same time, are crucial for the production of CCL2 and the recruitment of monocytes to the spleen. Together with other molecules, this could help to shape myeloid cell activation state and permissiveness to parasitism before adaptive immunity kicks in. In conclusion, we have identified SPs as the main pathway by which macrophages respond to infection by L. infantum and have established differences in the downstream mechanisms, as compared to the responses to L. donovani.

Parasites

Wild-type promastigotes of the L. donovani MW8975 and L. infantum PP75 (MHOM/BR/1974/PP75) strains were grown in hemoflagellate modified Eagle’s medium (designated HOMEM medium) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum (FCS) (Gibco) at 27°C.

Mice

The mouse strains used in this study are given in Table 1. The study received ethical approval from Comissão de Ética no Uso de Animais (CEUA) at the Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro. All mice were handled according to protocols approved by the ethics committee [Comissão de Ética no Uso de Animais (CEUA) 173/19-UFRJ]. The mice were kept in ventilated cages, in 12 h light/dark cycles, and provided with food and water ad libidum. For the in vivo experiments, male and female mice, aged between 8 and 14 weeks at the time of infection, were used (Table 1). For each independent experiment, the groups of different mouse strains were age- and sex-matched.

Macrophage infections

Peritoneal macrophages of mice were elicited by thioglycolate injection and collected as described previously6. For infection and survival assays, macrophages plated in 24-well culture plates containing coverslips (4 × 105 cells/well) were infected with late-stage promastigotes (culture density of 2 × 107 mL-1) at a 5:1 parasite:cell ratio in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma) supplemented with 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After 3 h, cells were washed with Hank’s buffered saline solution (HBSS) to remove extracellular parasites. Macrophages were then either fixed with 70% methanol and Giemsa stained or cultured in RPMI-10% FCS for 24, 48, or 72 h and then fixed and stained. The number of intracellular parasites was determined by counting at least 100 cells/replicate under light microscopy at a 100× oil immersion objective. Where indicated in the figures, the TLR4 inhibitor (Resatorvid, synonym: TAK-242; 1 µM; MedChemExpress, catalog # Hy-11109) was added 15 minutes before the parasites. The irreversible NE inhibitor III, N-Methoxysuccinyl-Ala-Ala-Pro-Val-Chloro-methyl-ketone (MeMeOSuc-AAPV-CMK) referred to as NEI; 10 µM; Calbiochem, catalog #324245), or the reversible cathepsin G inhibitor I (10 µM; Calbiochem, catalog #219372) were added 5 min before the parasites, while IFN-α (200 ng mL−1; eBioscience, catalog # 4312049), IFN-β (200 ng mL−1; BioLegend, catalog # 581302), and TNF-α (200 ng mL−1; BioLegend, catalog # 575204) were added after the removal of extracellular parasites at 3 h and cultivated for 48 or 72 h in RPMI-10% FCS.

For RNA extraction, macrophages in 6-well plates (3 × 106 cells/well) were infected with promastigotes at a 10:1 ratio in RPMI-0.1% BSA at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After 2 h, extracellular parasites were removed by washing with HBSS. Where indicated, polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid sodium salt (25 µg mL−1; poly I:C, Merck-Sigma, catalog # 31852-29-6) was added to macrophages alone, or macrophages were pre-treated with NEI (10 µM; Calbiochem) for 5 min prior to the addition of parasites. After removal of the parasites, cultures were incubated for a further 4 h in RPMI-0.1% BSA. Total RNA was extracted with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) using a 1 mL syringe to initially lyse the cells.

Quantitative PCR assays

The GoScript Reverse Transcriptase kit (Promega) was used to synthesize cDNA from total RNA. Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays of first-strand cDNA were performed using the GoTaq qPCR system (Promega), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the primers given in Table 2. Primers were synthesized by IDT (Integrated DNA Technology Inc.) and were used in prior work6. The qPCR reactions were performed at one cycle of 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 15 seconds at 95 °C and 1 minute at 60 °C. The melting condition was 1 min at 60 °C at 0.1 °C per second up to 95 °C. Expression ratios were computed via the ΔΔCt method.

In vivo infections

Stationary-phase promastigotes, 107 in 100 µl PBS, were injected in the retro-orbital cavity of mice. After 1 or 5 wk, mice were euthanized, and the spleens and livers were collected. A sample of each organ (5 mg) was preserved in RNAlater™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) until processing; samples were processed using a T 10 basic ULTRA-TURRAX® (IKA), and RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). The spleens were ruptured to homogeneity with the back of a syringe through a nylon membrane or cell strainer in RPMI medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FCS as described6, aliquots of the homogenates were submitted to 2-fold serial dilutions in 96-well plates, which were cultivated for 7 d at 27°C for parasite burden analysis; the highest dilution well was used to estimate the number of parasites multiplied by the dilution factors. The remaining homogenate volumes were centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min and the supernatants were collected and kept at -20°C for cytokine analysis. T n-sample calculation for a two-tailed t-test for independent samples was performed on the website powerandsampesize.com (http://powerandsamplesize.com/Calculators/Compare-2-means/2-Sample-Equality). The following parameters were applied: Statistical power 90%; Statistical significance threshold p < 0.05; Estimated difference between groups 20%; expected standard deviation 15%; Sampling ratio: 1 (considering comparison of groups with the same n).

ELISAs

TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-10, IL-12p40, CCL2, CX3CL1, CXCL2 (also known as MIP-2), and CCL5 (also known as RANTES) concentrations in the spleen supernatants were evaluated using R&D Systems DuoSet® kits. The nitric oxide concentrations were evaluated by the Griess reaction assay as described previously9. Plates were read on an Emax® Plus microplate reader (Molecular Devices).

Flow cytometry

ACK-treated single-cell suspensions from the spleen (107 cells) were washed in PBS at 300 × g for 5 min, incubated with an anti-Fc-γ III/II (CD16/32) receptor antibody (BioLegend) for 10 min, then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for 30 min: BV421-CD11b (BioLegend, catalog # 101236), BV605-Ly6C clone HK1.4 (BioLegend, catalog # 128031), FITC-CD4 clone GK1.5 (eBioscience, catalog # 11-0041-85), PE-Cy5-CD8 clone 53-6.7 (eBioscience, catalog # 15-0081-83), and APC-CD19 clone MB19-1 (eBioscience, catalog # 17-0191-82). Cells were washed in PBS, fixed in 100 µl IC Fixation Buffer (eBioscience) for 30 min, then washed and resuspended in PBS. One hundred thousand events were acquired on a FACSCanto™ II (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed on FlowJo v10. Gating strategies are described in Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software). The data were analyzed as described in the figure legends, and the number of technical or biological replicates used to generate the graphs and the number of independent assays or experiments performed yielding similar trends between groups are also given in the figure legends. As variation in absolute numbers occurred between independent assays involving peritoneal thioglycolate-recruited macrophages, possibly owing to different activation states at each collection, statistical analysis was performed using technical replicates for each group in each independent assay.
