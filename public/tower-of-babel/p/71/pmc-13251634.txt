Exploiting the Luminous Properties of Blue-Emitting Silicon Nanoparticles (Si-NPs) Derived from Raw Diatomite in Fungal Biosystems
Authors: Icaro Ferreira de Abreu, Jeferson Rosário, Verônica Bastos Maciel, Regina Geris, Antonio Ferreira da Silva, Jéssica G.S. Ramalho, Tiago Franca Paes, Claudilene R. Chaves, Leonis L. da Luz, Severino Alves Júnior, Marcos Malta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Raw diatomite is converted into luminescent Si-NPs via magnesiothermy.•Stable Si-NP suspensions enable in situ formation of fungus/Si-NP biohybrids.•Fungi build a ∼1 μm mineral shell while preserving hyphal morphology.•Smaller Si crystallites (∼11–13 nm) in biohybrids suggest size-selective uptake.•Internalized Si-NPs localize in vacuoles and act as intrinsic fluorescent probes.

Introduction

Cell functionalization involves integrating abiotic nanomaterials into living cells (eukaryotic or prokaryotic) to confer them with functions or properties that are not naturally present [1]. This incorporation alters the physicochemical and biological properties of microorganisms, expanding their potential for catalysis, sensing, and biotechnology [[2], [3], [4]].

Filamentous fungi are eukaryotic organisms ubiquitous in various ecological compartments on Earth. They consist of microscopic tubular cells that form filaments called hyphae; these elongated, branched filaments make up a network called mycelium. In the life cycle of these microorganisms, spores (in both asexual and sexual phases) and hyphal fragments (in the asexual phase) are formed, which can lead to fungal reproduction [5]. In the search for nutrients, the mycelial network extends through tip elongation, increasing the area/volume ratio and increasing the efficiency of nutrient absorption [6]. Fungi are of significant economic importance as they produce many compounds used in the pharmaceutical and food industries [5,7]. Moreover, recent advances have revealed their ability to assimilate diverse nanomaterials into their structure, thus opening up biotechnological applications [8,9].

The cultivation of filamentous fungi in liquid media containing dispersed nanoparticles promotes the association of abiotic nanomaterials with the fungal architecture [[10], [11], [12]]. In general, as the microorganisms grow and the mycelial biomass increases, nanoparticles of the medium progressively become immobilized onto the fungal cell wall and, in some cases, are internalized into the cytoplasm, leading to the formation of biohybrid systems [13,14]. Fungal biohybrids exhibit biological and physicochemical properties that differ markedly from those of their native counterparts due to interfacial interactions between the inorganic phase and the biological scaffold. For example, Han and co-workers developed Aspergillus sp./Fe3O4 biohybrids for the adsorption of radioactive uranyl ions (UO2)2+ from nuclear power plant wastewater [15]. In addition to acquiring magnetic responsiveness under an external magnetic field, which is absent in the native fungus, the resulting biomaterial displayed a significantly enhanced (UO2)2+ adsorption capacity (171 mg g⁻¹) compared with pure Aspergillus sp. (63 mg g⁻¹) and isolated Fe3O4 nanoparticles (110 mg g⁻¹), evidencing a synergistic adsorption mechanism [15].

In another study, our research group investigated the integration of small Pd nanoparticles (around 8 nm in diameter) into different species of filamentous fungi [16]. Ultrastructural analyses showed that Pd nanoparticles may accumulate at the cell wall interface or, depending on the fungal species and surface chemistry, be internalized into the cytosol. This spatial confinement of Pd-NPs within cells directly influences nanoparticle dispersion and aggregation state, as the fungal matrix can act as a three-dimensional scaffold that stabilizes isolated nanoparticles. In addition to physical constraints, the chemical environment surrounding the nanoparticles differs substantially from that of free Pd-NPs in solution. The fungal matrix is enriched in polysaccharides, proteins, and other biomolecules containing hydroxyl, amine/amide, carboxyl, phosphate, and carbonyl groups [[17], [18], [19]]. These moieties are widely involved in metal binding by microbial/fungal biomass and can also participate in capping and stabilizing metal nanoparticles, thus promoting interfacial interactions with the metallic surface [10,12]. We found that palladized fungal biohybrids exhibit catalytic activity in hydrogenation reactions under mild aqueous conditions, demonstrating that the biological scaffold can serve as a three-dimensional heterogeneous catalytic support. Taken together, these examples highlight that fungal biohybrids generate synergistic properties arising from bio-nano interfaces, something that isolated native cells cannot achieve.

In this study, we report our findings on integrating luminescent silicon nanoparticles (Si-NPs) into the structures of the fungal species Trichoderma sp. and Aspergillus niger (as detailed in ESI, Fig. S1). Si-NPs are an exceptional material due to their low toxicity, high surface area, and significant photoluminescence arising from quantum confinement and defects at the Si-SiO2 interface [[20], [21], [22]]. From an economic perspective, it is relevant to employ advanced materials that are abundant, inexpensive, and non-toxic for the functionalization of living organisms. Our work uses commercial raw diatomite without any chemical treatment to synthesize Si-NPs directly. To our knowledge, this is the first time that biosystems based on fungal/Si-NP materials have been studied using a combination of microscopy and spectroscopy techniques. Interestingly, in addition to integrating Si-NPs into the fungal cell wall, we also observed their internalization into fungal organelles, which could be attractive to researchers interested in models for delivering drugs and luminescent probes into fungi.

Experimental

Chemicals

Samples were prepared using deionized water (Millipore Milli-Q Water System). Diatomite (CIEMIL CA/223) was generously supplied by Ciemil (Comércio, Industria e Exportação de Minérios Ltda, Vitória da Conquista, BA, Brazil). According to the supplier, the raw diatomite was initially calcined in a rotary kiln at 800 °C to remove organic impurities, then pulverized before packaging. Absolute ethanol (99.5%, QHEMIS), magnesium powder (Mg 99.9%, EXODUS), hydrochloric acid (HCl 37%, QHEMIS), and hydrofluoric acid (HF 40%, VETEC) were used as purchased, without further purification.

Synthesis of Si-NPsnps from diatomite

Si-NPs were synthesized from commercial diatomite using a slightly modified magnesiothermic reduction procedure adapted from the literature [23]. In a typical synthesis, 6.0 g of diatomite was thoroughly mixed with 6.0 g of metallic magnesium powder, yielding a 1:1 mass ratio. The mixture was loaded into reaction boats and heated in a tubular furnace at 650 °C for 7 h at a rate of 10 °C min⁻¹ under an argon flow of 50 mL min⁻¹. After thermal treatment, the powder was washed with 2.0 mol L⁻¹ HCl at 60 °C for 5 h to dissolve magnesium oxide and other magnesium-containing byproducts. The HCl-treated material was then treated with a diluted hydrofluoric acid solution prepared at a 1:10 HF:H₂O volume ratio to remove residual silica/oxide species from the silicon surface. The final HF-treated product was thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and used in all subsequent experiments as Si-NPs.

Caution: Hydrofluoric acid is extremely hazardous and can cause severe injury to living tissues. Therefore, all procedures involving HF must be performed in a certified chemical fume hood, with appropriate personal protective equipment and in strict compliance with safety protocols.

Preparation of Si-NP suspension

Silicon particles were subjected to high-intensity ultrasound irradiation using a Vibracell VC 130 ultrasonic processor (Sonics Instruments) to homogenize the dispersion and reduce particle aggregation. Briefly, 0.30 g of Si particles were dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water, yielding a final Si concentration of 0.006 g mL⁻¹. The suspension was sonicated for 1 h with a titanium tip (0.6 cm diameter) immersed approximately 2.0 cm below the liquid surface. This Si-NP concentration was selected based on preliminary tests, in which higher Si contents led to visible sedimentation of the inorganic material during fungal cultivation. Therefore, 0.006 g mL⁻¹ was used to maintain a feasible nanoparticle suspension while minimizing excessive precipitation in the culture medium. The resulting Si-NP suspension was transferred to 125 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, autoclaved for 30 min at 120 °C, and subsequently used for fungal cultivation.

Fungi cultivation

Microorganism/Si-NP biohybrids were obtained by inoculating selected fungal spores into a sterilized medium containing the sonicated Si-NPs suspension and potato-dextrose broth (PDB) [24]. For this purpose, 5.0 mL of PDB was added to an Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of Si-NP dispersion at 0.006 g mL⁻¹. Then, 100 µL of spore suspension from each fungal species was added to the medium and incubated at 28 °C for 6 days in a rotary shaker at 140 rpm. Fungal pellets decorated with Si-NPs were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and split into different sets for viability assessment and physicochemical characterization. The qualitative viability/recolonization assay is described in Section 2.5. The biohybrids used for XRD, SEM, Raman, and photoluminescence measurements were dried using critical-point drying, as previously described in the literature [10]. Fluorescence microscopy was performed on small mycelial fragments stored in deionized water without further treatment.

Qualitative viability/recolonization assay

To assess whether Si-NPs exposure compromised the biological activity of the fungal biomass, a qualitative viability/recolonization assay was performed using fungal/Si-NP biohybrids obtained after 6 days of cultivation. Briefly, after biohybrid formation, the fungal pellets decorated with Si-NPs were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove loosely bound nanoparticles and residual culture medium. One washed fungal/Si-NP pellet was then transferred to a Petri dish containing fresh potato-dextrose agar and incubated at 28 °C for 5 days. Fungal recolonization of the Petri dish was visually monitored as a qualitative indicator of preserved biological activity following Si-NPs association.

Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a Jeol JSM-5400 (at LAMUME, UFBA) and a Quanta 200 SEM (at CETENE, Recife-PE). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses were done in a Jeol EM 1213 (at Instituto Goncalo Muniz, Fiocruz, Salvador-BA) or in a Tecnai G2-12- SpiritBiotwin FEI - 120 kV (at UFMG Electronic Microscopy Center, Belo Horizonte-MG). Fungal biohybrid samples for TEM analysis were processed according to the fixation protocol detailed in our previous publication [10]. X-Ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis was performed on a Shimadzu XRF-1800 spectrometer. The Raman spectrum was acquired with a Jasco NRS-5100 using 532 nm monochromatic radiation as the excitation source. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was conducted using Shimadzu IRAffinity-1 equipment. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were performed using a Shimadzu LAB X XRD-6000 equipment with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5416 Å) at 40 kV. The Scherrer equation was utilized to determine the apparent crystallite size of nanoparticles, which is given by D = (kλ/β cos θ), where D represents the size of the particle, K is referred to as Scherrer's constant (K = 0.94), λ stands for the X-ray wavelength (1.5416 Å), β represents the full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peak, and θ represents the diffraction angle.

Photoluminescence measurements were performed on a Horiba-Jobin Yvon Fluorolog-3 spectrofluorimeter equipped with a 450 W continuous xenon lamp and UV xenon flash tube for excitation. All emission spectra were corrected for the spectral response of the monochromators using a silicon photodiode reference detector to monitor and compensate for variations in the xenon lamp output, using typical correction spectra provided by the manufacturer.

For fluorescence microscopy, Si-NPs or fungal mycelial fragments were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and mounted between a coverslip and a microscope slide. Subsequently, these specimens were analyzed using an Olympus BX51 microscope and a Retiga 2000R monochromatic CCD camera (located at Instituto Goncalo Muniz, Fiocruz, Bahia). Illumination was provided by a 100 W Hg vapor source, with a BP380-385 excitation filter and a BA420 emission filter to detect blue luminescence. Likewise, a BP450-480 excitation filter and a BA515 emission filter were used to detect green emissions, and a BP530-550 excitation filter and a BA575-625 emission filter were used to detect red emissions. The acquired images were then processed using Leica LAS X software.

Results

Silicon nanoparticles were obtained from diatomite through magnesiothermic reduction followed by acid purification. After thermal treatment and acid etching, marked changes were observed in the XRD profile (Fig. 1c). The original diatomite pattern showed a broad amorphous silica contribution in the 20–30° range, together with crystalline reflections assigned to mineral phases present in the raw material. In contrast, the diffractogram of the final Si-NPs showed reflections indexed to polycrystalline silicon, with no detectable reflections of MgO or other crystalline oxide impurities. The apparent crystallite size of the synthesized Si-NPs, calculated using the Scherrer equation, was 38.5 ± 5.96 nm.

The morphological transformation resulting from magnesiothermic reduction and acid treatment was also observed by electron microscopy. The original porous, organized frustule-like morphology of diatomite was destroyed, yielding a granular material composed of randomly distributed particles (Fig. 1d). TEM analysis of different sample regions revealed that ultrasonic treatment produces a spongy structure composed of polydisperse NPs with crystals ranging from 5 to 30 nm (Fig. 1e). As shown in Fig. S2 at ESI, high-resolution TEM confirms the formation of crystalline Si-NPs. The Raman spectrum of the final material showed an intense band at approximately 510 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the Si–Si vibrational mode, corroborating the formation of crystalline Si-NPs (Fig. 1f).

The optical properties of the Si-NPs were evaluated by fluorescence microscopy and photoluminescence spectroscopy. The particles displayed visible emission under fluorescence microscopy, as exemplified in the bright-field and fluorescence images shown in Fig. 1g, h. Photoluminescence spectra were recorded at different excitation wavelengths. When excited at 335 and 385 nm, the Si-NPs exhibited a blue emission band, with maximum intensity near 420 nm. Upon excitation at 430 nm, the emission shifted to the green region, with a broad band centered at approximately 500–550 nm (Fig. 1i). These results confirmed that the diatomite-derived Si-NPs display photoluminescent behavior before fungal integration.

The formation of microorganism/Si-NP biohybrids was evaluated by cultivating filamentous fungi in a liquid medium containing a sonicated Si-NP suspension. Native A. niger grown in the absence of Si-NPs exhibited hyphae with a smooth, uniform micromorphology (Fig. 2a). In contrast, when A. niger (Fig. 2b) and Trichoderma sp. (Fig. 2c) were cultivated in the presence of Si-NPs, their hyphae became extensively covered with inorganic material. XRD analysis (Fig. 2d) confirmed the presence of crystalline silicon in the fungal biohybrids. Both Trichoderma sp./Si-NPs and A. niger/Si-NPs exhibited diffraction peaks characteristic of crystalline silicon. The apparent crystallite sizes, calculated from the XRD patterns, were 10.80 ± 0.48 nm for Trichoderma sp./Si-NPs and 12.94 ± 0.35 nm for A. niger/Si-NPs. These values were lower than those calculated for the Si-NPs before fungal integration.

FTIR analysis was performed to evaluate the chemical features of the Si-NPs and the corresponding fungal biohybrids (Fig. 2e). Before integration into biological systems, the Si-NPs exhibited bands associated with Si–OH (3431 and 1634 cm−1), Si–O–Si (1010 cm−1), and Si–Si (447 cm−1) vibrational modes. After fungal cultivation in the presence of Si-NPs, the FTIR spectra of the biohybrids remained dominated by silicon-related bands. Signals commonly associated with fungal biomass, including bands related to carboxyl, amide, and polysaccharide groups, were weakly distinguishable or overlapped with the intense Si–O–Si contribution. These data indicate that a large amount of Si-NPs was associated with the fungal biomass. TEM analysis was used to further examine the distribution of Si-NPs within the fungal structure. Cross-sectional images of A. niger/Si-NPs showed that the inorganic material surrounded the fungal cell, forming a dense external layer with a thickness on the order of one micrometer (Fig. 2f). Based on SEM, XRD, and TEM measurements, a schematic representation of the integration of Si-NPs on the fungal cells is presented in Fig. 2g.

As previously emphasized, a crucial aspect of integrating nanomaterials into filamentous fungi is to confer new physicochemical characteristics or properties that the microorganism naturally lacks. In this study, our main objective was to determine whether Si-NPs induce “artificial” photoluminescence in fungal cells. Consequently, we evaluated the fluorescence emission of fungal cells after their interaction with silicon NPs. We focused our investigation on photoluminescence in the blue spectrum, driven by the remarkable concentration of Si-NPs assimilated by microorganisms. Trichoderma sp./Si-NPs and A. niger/Si-NPs were visualized under bright-field, fluorescence, and composite imaging modes (Fig. 3a–f). The fungal biohybrids displayed blue emission under the selected fluorescence microscopy conditions. In A. niger/Si-NPs (Fig. 3d–f), the luminescent signal allowed the visualization of spherical and tubular intracellular structures (probably fungal vacuoles) distributed along the hyphae. The biological activity of the fungal/Si-NP biohybrids was evaluated using a qualitative viability/recolonization assay. After six days of cultivation in the presence of Si-NPs, fungal pellets were thoroughly washed with deionized water and transferred to fresh potato-dextrose agar medium. After five days of incubation, the fungal material recolonized the Petri dish (Fig. 3g, f). This result indicates that the fungal biomass remained biologically active after Si-NP association under the tested conditions.

Discussion

The results demonstrate that commercial raw diatomite can be converted into photoluminescent Si-NPs via magnesiothermic reduction followed by acid purification. Diatomite is an attractive silica-rich precursor because it is abundant, inexpensive, and structurally organized, composed mainly of amorphous silica with minor inorganic oxides [25,26]. Because SiO2 is a major component of diatomite, this mineral is among the best choices for producing nanostructured silicon. Silicon was obtained via magnesiothermic reduction according to the following reaction:2Mg(g)+SiO2(s)→2MgO(s)+Si(s)

In this synthesis, magnesium is vaporized, thereby reducing SiO2 to Si. The intermediate product is a mixture of co-continuous interpenetrating phases of MgO and Si. Complete dissolution of MgO (and possibly other oxide impurities detected by XRF) can be easily obtained by attack with 2.0 mol. L−1 HCl solution, followed by treatment with diluted HF solution. The conversion of this material into Si-NPs is consistent with previous reports showing that diatomaceous silica can be transformed into silicon-based nanostructures by reduction routes [23,27]. In the present work, the disappearance of the original frustule-like morphology, the emergence of crystalline silicon reflections in the XRD pattern, and the Raman band associated with Si–Si vibrations support the successful formation of crystalline Si-NPs.

Although bulk crystalline silicon is an inefficient light emitter due to its indirect bandgap, Si-NPs may exhibit visible photoluminescence due to nanoscale and surface-related effects [28–30]. In Si nanostructures, quantum confinement can modify the electronic structure of nanosized Si domains and increase the probability of radiative recombination [29,31]. In addition, surface and interface states associated with Si/SiO₂ interfaces, silicon suboxides, oxygen-related defects, and localized surface sites may introduce emissive levels within the band structure [30–32]. Therefore, the photoluminescence observed in Si-NPs should not be interpreted as a bulk-like optical response of crystalline silicon. Rather, it is more likely associated with the combined contributions of confined Si domains, defects, and interfaces mediated recombination pathways. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies on photoluminescent silicon nanocrystals and surface-oxidized Si-NPs [28,30,32].

The use of a magnesiothermic route was also appropriate for the objective of this work. Compared with physical methods such as laser ablation, the reduction of diatomite enables the preparation of gram-scale quantities of particulate Si-based material, which is advantageous for fungal cultivation, extensive washing, replication, and multiple characterization experiments. Moreover, the particulate nature of the product allows its dispersion by sonication and direct incorporation into the fungal growth medium. This is particularly important for biohybrid formation, since the nanoparticles must remain available in the liquid medium long enough to interact with the growing fungal biomass.

The formation of fungal/Si-NP biohybrids is favored by interactions between the inorganic particles and the fungal cell wall. Because nanoparticles are assimilated only during cell growth, the key parameters for producing fungal biohybrids are the stability of the nanomaterials in solution (to prevent precipitation during fungal growth) and the presence of a carbohydrate source [24]. Filamentous fungi have a chemically rich wall and extracellular matrix composed of polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and other biomolecules containing hydroxyl, amine/amide, carboxyl, phosphate, and carbonyl groups [18,19,33]. These functional moieties can interact with inorganic surfaces through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, coordination, and surface complexation. In the present system, FTIR data indicate that the Si-NPs contain oxygenated surface species, including Si–OH and Si–O–Si groups. This surface chemistry may favor interfacial interactions between the Si-NPs and fungal wall components, thereby contributing to nanoparticle immobilization on the fungal architecture.

The SEM, XRD, FTIR, and TEM data indicate that the fungi assimilated a large amount of Si-NPs during growth. The dense inorganic layer observed around the hyphae suggests that the fungal cell wall acts as a three-dimensional biological scaffold for nanoparticle immobilization. Similar behavior has been observed in other fungal biohybrid systems, where growing hyphae promote the assembly or stabilization of inorganic nanoparticles along their surface [8,9,[11], [12], [13],34,35]. The biological scaffold can therefore act not only as a passive support, but also as a chemically active interface that modulates nanoparticle distribution, aggregation state, and retention. The TEM observations and the proposed schematic model further support this interfacial interpretation (Fig. 2f, g). Cross-sectional TEM images show that the fungal hyphae are surrounded by a continuous inorganic layer, indicating that Si-NPs are not randomly distributed in the medium after cultivation, but become spatially organized around the fungal cell envelope. In the proposed model, the cell wall serves as a three-dimensional biological scaffold in which Si-NPs are immobilized via multiple weak interfacial interactions rather than a single binding event. The oxygenated surface of the Si-NPs, containing Si–OH and Si–O–Si groups, may interact with hydroxyl, amine/amide, carboxylate, phosphate, and carbonyl groups present in polysaccharides, proteins, chitin, glucans, and extracellular polymeric substances of the fungal wall. These interactions may include hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, surface complexation, and steric entrapment within the wall matrix. Therefore, the mineral-like shell observed by TEM can be interpreted as resulting from progressive nanoparticle capture and stabilization during hyphal growth, in which the fungal wall provides a chemically active interface for Si-NP assembly.

An interesting result is that the apparent crystallite size of Si-NPs in the fungal biohybrids was smaller than that of the synthesized Si-NPs before biological integration. Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, this behavior can be rationalized by a combination of colloidal and biological interfacial effects. During fungal cultivation, larger Si-NP aggregates are expected to sediment more readily, reducing their availability for interaction with the growing mycelium. Conversely, smaller particles or aggregates may remain dispersed longer and thus have a higher probability of contacting the fungal surface. In addition, smaller Si-NPs have a higher specific surface area and may interact more efficiently with functional groups on the fungal cell wall and extracellular matrix. The fungal wall can also act as a porous and chemically active scaffold, favoring the trapping or embedding of smaller particles, whereas larger aggregates may remain more weakly attached to the outer surface and be more readily removed during washing. Therefore, the smaller crystallite size observed in the biohybrids likely reflects colloidal fractionation during cultivation, enhanced interfacial interactions, and preferential retention of smaller Si-NPs within the fungal matrix, rather than an active biological selection process in a strict metabolic sense.

Fungal/Si-NPs biohybrids were examined under a fluorescent microscope, and Fig. 3a–f shows representative blue emissions for A. niger/Si-NPs and Trichoderma sp./Si-NPs, respectively. In contrast to the findings of Losic et al. [27], who reported photoluminescence at 682 nm in silicon diatoms when excited at 458 nm under confocal microscopy, our material exhibits negligible light emission in the red spectral region after acid etching (see Fig. 3S in the ESI). Consequently, we investigated the blue luminescent properties of the material after its integration into filamentous fungi. By selecting arbitrary regions throughout the fungal biohybrid mycelia, the incorporation of Si-NPs enhanced visualization of spherical and tubular organelles within cells, as illustrated in Fig. 3e. These observations support the role of Si-NPs as photoluminescent probes, predominantly localized in the fungal vacuoles of Aspergillus niger biohybrids. In fungi, vacuoles are large organelles involved in storage, detoxification, ion homeostasis, and sequestration of exogenous materials [36]. In previous studies conducted in our group, we demonstrated the internalization and accumulation of lanthanide metal organic frameworks (Ln-MOFs) within fungal vacuoles, enabling direct visualization of these organelles via fluorescence microscopy [13]. Furthermore, TEM analyses of another biosystem, the Penicillium sp./Pd-NPs biohybrids, confirmed the presence of nanoparticles inside the fungal cell [16]. Presumably, nanoparticle uptake is governed by incidental processes regulated by a size-exclusion limit imposed by the fungal cell wall. Specifically, small nanoparticles are incidentally transported into fungal cells during the acquisition, transport, and assimilation of nutrients from the environment, as long as their size falls below a critical threshold [14].

We also checked the photoluminescence emission of fungus/Si-NPs biohybrids, such as A. niger/Si-NPs. As shown in Fig. S4 (ESI), the characteristic autofluorescence spectra exhibited by filamentous fungi under ultraviolet light are attributed to the presence of several biomolecules in the cytoplasm and cell wall. To ensure that only Si-NPs in the biohybrids would be excited by light, we selected an excitation wavelength of 290 nm. Fig. S4 (b) displays the PL spectra of A. niger/Si-NPs excited at this wavelength. According to Yang and colleagues [32], a possible mechanism for the blue emission in Si-NPs is that excitons first formed inside quantum-sized Si-NPs undergo direct transitions at the Γ or Χ point, with some of them being captured by nonradiative Pb centers. However, the blue emission band in surface-oxidized Si-NPs is assigned to defect formation, in which the photoexcitation and emission processes do not necessarily require the participation of the Si(0) core, and the surface fulfills the photoluminescence process [37]. Although the Si core could also display blue photoluminescence, depending on the core size, the most probable core emission of the synthesized nanoparticle is in the green range, as presented in Fig. 3c.

The qualitative recolonization assay provides an important biological indication that the fungal biomass remained active after Si-NP exposure. After cultivation in the presence of Si-NPs, the washed fungal/Si-NP pellet recolonized fresh potato-dextrose agar medium (Fig. 3g–j). This result suggests that the association of Si-NPs did not stop fungal growth capacity under the tested conditions. However, this assay should be interpreted as a qualitative biological assessment rather than a quantitative cytotoxicity test. Further studies involving growth kinetics, biomass quantification, colony-forming unit analysis, metabolic activity assays, live/dead staining, and longer exposure periods will be necessary to fully determine the cytotoxicity and biocompatibility profile of Si-NPs in fungal systems. The photoluminescence stability of the fungal/Si-NP biohybrids over time, under different storage conditions, and upon repeated illumination was not systematically evaluated in the present work. Future studies should monitor emission intensity, spectral profile, and possible photodegradation and repeated excitation cycles. These experiments will be important to evaluate the practical use of fungal/Si-NP biohybrids as luminescent biological platforms.

Overall, the results support the formation of microorganism/Si-NP biohybrids in which the biological scaffold provides a three-dimensional architecture for Si-NP immobilization and, in some cases, intracellular localization. The resulting biohybrids combine the growth capacity and structural complexity of filamentous fungi with the photoluminescent properties of Si-NPs. This association creates a hybrid biosystem with optical properties that are not present in native fungal cells and may be useful for future studies involving fungal imaging, biological tracking, environmental interfaces, and biohybrid luminescent materials.

Conclusions

This study presents a novel biosystem composed of filamentous fungi and luminescent silicon nanoparticles (Si-NPs) derived from a low-cost, readily available diatomite source. After cultivation in a nutrient solution containing Si-NPs, the fungi developed a dense layer of abiotic material, which was characterized by microscopy and spectroscopy. We also observed internalization of luminescent Si-NPs within fungal organelles and their incorporation into the fungal cell wall. Notably, the apparent crystallite size of the Si-NPs, calculated using the Scherrer equation, was smaller within the fungal biohybrids than in the synthesized counterparts, measuring 10.80 nm (±0.48) and 12.94 nm (±0.35) for Trichoderma sp./Si-NPs and A. niger/Si-NPs, respectively. This finding suggests that, under the cultivation and preparation conditions, microorganisms assimilate smaller Si-NPs more readily than larger ones. Therefore, nanoparticles smaller than 10 nm can incidentally permeate the cell wall and be concentrated in fungal vacuoles, as observed by fluorescence microscopy.
