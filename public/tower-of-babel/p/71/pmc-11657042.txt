Elucidating siRNA Cellular Delivery Mechanism Mediated by Quaternized Starch Nanoparticles
Authors: Eliz Amar‐Lewis, Limor Cohen, Ramesh Chintakunta, Chen Benafsha, Yael Lavi, Riki Goldbart, Tamar Traitel, Levi A Gheber, Joseph Kost
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStarch‐based nanoparticles are highly utilized in the realm of drug delivery taking advantage of their biocompatibility and biodegradability. Studies have utilized Quaternized starch (Q‐starch) for small interfering RNA (siRNA) delivery, in which quaternary amines enable interaction with negatively charged siRNA, resulting in self‐assembly complexation. Although reports present numerous applications, the demonstrated efficacy is nonetheless limited due to undiscovered cellular mechanistic delivery. In this study, a deep dive into Q‐starch/siRNA complexes’ cellular mechanism and kinetics at the cellular level is revealed using single‐particle tracking and cell population level using imaging flow cytometry. Uptake studies depict the efficient cellular internalization via endocytosis while a significant fraction of complexes’ intracellular fate is lysosome. Utilizing single‐particle tracking, it is found that an average of 15% of cellular detected complexes escape the endosome which holds the potential for the integration in the cytoplasmatic gene silencing mechanism. Additional experimental manipulations (overcoming endosomal escape) demonstrate that the complex's disassembly is the rate‐limiting step, correlating Q‐starch's structure‐function properties as siRNA carrier. Structure‐function properties accentuating the high affinity of the interaction between Q‐starch's quaternary groups and siRNA's phosphate groups that results in low release efficiency. However, low‐frequency ultrasound (20 kHz) application may have induced siRNA release resulting in faster gene silencing kinetics.Keywords: delivery mechanism, endocytosis, particle tracking, polysaccharide, siRNA, starch

Introduction

RNA interference (RNAi) is a gene therapy platform, that selectively downregulates overexpressed disease‐causing genes.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] This pathway employs small interfering RNA (siRNA) molecules that initiate the gene‐silencing mechanism in the cell cytoplasm. Due to its large size (more than 21 base pairs) and negative charge, siRNA transport through the impermeable and negatively charged cellular membrane is hampered resulting in low siRNA uptake into cells that induces minimal therapeutic efficiency. Moreover, in vivo systemic delivery results in rapid degradation by RNases therefore hindering delivery to the target organ.[ 4 , 5 ] In order to resolve these barriers, different approaches have been developed to deliver siRNA into cells among which, some are based on entrapping siRNA in nanoparticles. The design and development of the nanoparticles are based on screening their physicochemical properties that will allow the delivery and the release of siRNA in the cell cytoplasm, the target site of action.[ 6 , 7 , 8 ] The most studied delivery modalities presented in the current literature include lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)[ 9 , 10 , 11 ] cationic polymers,[ 12 , 13 , 14 ] and peptides[ 15 , 16 , 17 ] presenting efficiency as well as toxicity, indicating that the application is still limited.

Polysaccharides serve as attractive candidates for drug delivery since they are considered biocompatible, biodegradable, easy and chip to manufacture, can be easily chemically modified in order to serve the delivery requirements, and most importantly without any severe toxicity outcome upon systemic administration.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ] In a previous study, we have presented that positively charged starch polysaccharide is a promising platform for siRNA delivery both in vitro[ 22 ] and in vivo.[ 23 ] Starch is composed of 2 polymer components amylose, the linear α(1–4) linked glucose; and amylopectin, α(1–4) linked glucose with α(1–6) branch points.[ 24 ] We modified starch with quaternary ammonium groups along the polymer backbone to form positively charged starch (Q‐starch). In the presence of siRNA, a nanoparticle complex is assembled as a result of electrostatic interactions. We have demonstrated that Q‐starch/siRNA complexes are nanosized (ranging between 10–100 nm) with a positive zeta potential (+25 mV), properties which were required for successful internalization into cells. Moreover, Q‐starch was able to protect siRNA from nuclease degradation in the human serum environment which is highly important for successful in vivo systemic administration. Finally, we have presented gene knockdown efficacy in vitro in the human ovarian adenocarcinoma cell model and successfully downregulated the overexpressed protein P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), while maintaining low cellular toxicity.[ 22 ] We utilized this model due to overexpression of the P‐gp gene which contributes to multidrug resistance in adenocarcinoma cells, keeping cytotoxic drug cellular load below a cell‐killing dose. Therefore, this model represents the potential to target the downregulation of a human gene in a human cell line. Our previous in vivo study[ 23 ] revealed that Q‐starch induces the accumulation of siRNA at the tumor site with P‐gp gene silencing at the tumor cells. Interestingly, the Q‐starch/siRNA complexes biodistribution study revealed favorable accumulation in the lung tissue. Altogether, we found that this delivery system has a high potential to deliver siRNA to cells and tumors.

In addition to our studies, a few additional studies have also employed starch in siRNA delivery for different therapeutic targets. Engelberth et al.[ 25 ] have studied quaternized dendritic starch that formulates into nanoparticles with siRNA. They were characterized for their physicochemical properties and have demonstrated efficient gene silencing in vitro for up to 6 days. Li et al.[ 26 , 27 ] have developed a folate‐biotin‐quaternized starch nanoparticles for targeted co‐delivery of siRNA and doxorubicin. In addition, our previous study, by Zimon et al.,[ 28 ] has developed Q‐starch for transdermal delivery of miRNA targeting the treatment of psoriasis. These studies along with ongoing research and increasing utility of Q‐starch for gene and siRNA delivery have highlighted the need for further profound insight into this delivery platform.

Our previous in vitro study[ 22 ] has presented an interesting phenomenon in which Q‐starch/siRNA complexes’ cellular uptake was highly efficient with 100% of cells presenting uptake after only 4 h of incubation while the gene silencing effect wasn't detected 24 h following incubation, against expected outcome. Only after 72 h of incubation, 52% of gene silencing was accomplished. Altogether, this data along with the increasing interest in Q‐starch‐based delivery platforms have raised numerous questions that presented additional incentives for further deep mechanistic study. Such questions consider that if the complexes efficiently enter the cells, might an intracellular barrier have hindered the kinetics of gene silencing? And while studying the complex's intracellular path, will take a step back into the internalization mechanism to point out the subsequent intracellular path? Most importantly, along the cellular delivery path, what is the rate‐limiting step and to what extent do the complexes lose their capability to induce gene silencing as a result of delivery barriers? Answers to these questions will not only highlight our knowledge of the delivery mechanism of Q‐starch/siRNA complexes, which is currently not clear based on current literature reports but also allow us to further design this carrier and generate solutions for the presented barriers. Therefore, our driving motivation is that elucidating the Q‐starch/siRNA complexes delivery mechanism will reveal a delivery barrier that was unknown previously to this study. Our hypothesis is that the barrier is most likely an intracellular barrier (i.e., endosomal escape or siRNA release) therefore this study will mainly focus on studying the intracellular barriers.

We utilized a couple of approaches to study the delivery mechanism. Live cell fluorescent microscopy and single particle tracking were employed in order to reveal the complex's intracellular path in a detailed manner at the cellular level.[ 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 ] In addition, we used imaging flow cytometry as a tool to perform quantitative analysis with the great advantage of performing analysis on a large cell population using high throughput image analysis.[ 33 , 34 , 35 ] These 2 methods are complementary with the first providing information at the cell level and the second about the whole population.

Results and Discussion

Q‐starch/siRNA Cellular Uptake; Kinetics and Mechanism

In part of the study to elucidate Q‐starch/siRNA delivery barriers, cellular uptake was examined as the first stage for siRNA delivery. Qualitative observation of internalization efficiency was conducted 24 h post‐treatment, confirming the necessity of Q‐starch for siRNA delivery. Labeled complexes administered to NAR cells exhibited significant uptake, whereas free siRNA showed negligible uptake (Figure 1a). siRNA under cell growth conditions is highly prone to degradation by RNases and repelled from the negatively charged cellular membrane. siRNA encapsulation by Q‐starch improved siRNA potential to internalize NAR cells and finally integrate into the RNAi mechanism.

To delve deeper into the delivery mechanism, a quantitative method was optimized using imaging flow cytometry. To determine the extent of uptake efficiency, experiments were performed under cell growth conditions with Cy5‐labeled siRNA. Figure 1b demonstrates cell images from flow cytometry presenting complexes uptake during the 48 h study period and the percentage of cell population presenting uptake (the percentage was determined according to Figure S5, Supporting Information) after complexes application. Flow cytometry data confirms confocal results, demonstrating that free siRNA doesn't internalize into cells while following 1 h of incubation, 67% of the cells presented complexes uptake (Figure S5, Supporting Information). The uptake was further scrutinized over 48 h, analyzing both the percentage of cells showing uptake (Figure 1c) and the mean fluorescence intensity of the cell population (Figure 1d). Observation under cell growth conditions reveals a gradual internalization of complexes, with 100% of cells exhibiting uptake after 4 h (Figure 1b,D). Figure 1d (with serum, + Serum) indicates that the majority of internalization occurs within the first 12 h, followed by a decrease in cellular signal at 24 and 48 h. Due to the potential probability that the dilution can be caused by either cellular proliferation, nanoparticle exocytosis,[ 36 ] or both, we examined the cell uptake profile under cell starvation conditions (without serum, – Serum). Figure 1d (‐ Serum) demonstrates that the main pulse of uptake occurs during the first 12 h of the study (the same as for the case of “+ Serum”) and complexes accumulation reaches a plateau after 24 h. The resulting plateau demonstrates that cell proliferation is responsible for the uptake results under growth conditions, which is also confirmed by the NAR cell proliferation study (Figure S6, Supporting Information). These findings suggest that during cell division, complexes inside cells are distributed between daughter cells. Additionally, the steady‐state observed may result from a balance between internalization and exocytosis, indicating that exocytosis does not outweigh endocytosis during the uptake of Q‐starch/siRNA complexes. Moreover, this study demonstrates that the Q‐starch‐based delivery platform retains complexes within cells to some extent, potentially facilitating siRNA release into the cytoplasm, the target site. Furthermore, it can be inferred that the membrane barrier does not hinder the transfection process observed within the first 24 h of complex treatment at N/P 2, suggesting that the rate‐limiting step for transfection is intracellular.

Additional important data we have come to realize from this study is the effect of serum components on Q‐starch/siRNA complexes' cellular uptake. Once complexes are supplemented in the presence of serum proteins, we can identify that cell uptake is hindered to some extent in comparison to supplementing in the absence of serum proteins, particularly during the first few hours of incubation (more than 2‐fold difference). Serum proteins interact with the surface potential of Q‐starch complexes and potentially form a protein corona that later on affects the nature of complexes' interaction with the cellular membrane. Most importantly, for a therapeutic application, this nature of interaction can't be avoided upon complex administration since the tissue biological environment is surrounded by protein components (whether we refer to systemic administration, or local administration). We[ 23 ] and others[ 37 ] have shown before that for permanently positively charged nanoparticles, such as in the case of Q‐starch complexes, protein corona affects cellular interaction and organ tropism specifically in the lungs.

Given the uncertainty surrounding the uptake of Q‐starch/siRNA complexes and their physicochemical properties, we have confirmed endocytosis as the required pathway for siRNA delivery by Q‐starch. Initially, we ruled out uptake via passive diffusion by preventing complex uptake at 4 °C (Figure S7, Supporting Information). At this temperature, several active processes, including the uptake of nanoparticles reliant on protein activity,[ 38 , 39 ] are thermally inhibited. This implies that Q‐starch/siRNA complexes internalize via an endocytic‐dependent mechanism.

Subsequently, we confirmed uptake through endocytosis using an inhibition study, where NAR cells were pre‐treated with Dynasore,[ 40 , 41 ] a small molecule inhibitor of Dynamine involved in most (but not all) endocytic entry mechanisms and the formation of intracellular vesicles from cell organelles.[ 42 , 43 ] Pre‐incubation with the inhibitor was strictly limited to 4 h to mitigate its effect on cell viability and reduce non‐specificity.[ 44 ] The uptake efficiency into cells was tracked and analyzed using fluorescently labeled complexes. Flow cytometry results indicate that Dynasore completely blocked the complexes' uptake (Figure 1e). Uptake inhibition was also examined with Q‐starch‐labeled complexes, confirming that the internalization of both components depends on Dynamin activity (Figure S8, Supporting Information) concluding that the entry mechanism of Q‐starch/siRNA complexes indeed involves endocytosis. Considering intracellular barriers as significant obstacles to siRNA delivery, we aimed to further explore the complexes' cellular pathways in endocytic vesicles.

Intracellular Trafficking of Q‐starch/siRNA Complexes in Intracellular Organelles Reveals Accumulation in Lysosomes

Recognizing the endocytic pathway as the route for internalizing complexes intracellularly, it is reasonable to anticipate that internalized complexes would progress through early endosomes maturing into late endosomes. However, in the absence of endosomal escape, vesicle fusion with endolysosomes may lead to complex degradation, presenting a significant barrier for siRNA delivery via Q‐starch. To explore the intracellular barriers, we initially tracked the co‐localization of Q‐starch/siRNA complexes with early and late endosomes using live cell fluorescent microscopy imaging and single particle tracking. Early and late endosomes were distinguished using Rab5a‐RFP for early and Rab7a‐RFP for late endosomes. Cells were incubated with the complexes for the indicated time and imaged (Figure 2 ). Indication for early kinetics shows that following 4 h of incubation, complexes were co‐localized with early endosomes by only a few events. However, incubation in the presence of Bafilomycin (a H+ ATPase inhibitor), which inhibits endosome acidification and maturation to the late endosome demonstrates increased colocalization events with early endosomes, suggesting that complexes are endocytosed and transported in this compartment (Figure 2a). Notably, complex uptake decreased under Bafilomycin's influence, likely due to halted endosomal maturation.

The graph presents means BDS change along the experimental kinetic, (n = 3, mean ± STDEV) *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.

We then examined co‐localization with late endosomes/lysosomes after longer transfection durations using Lysotracker‐green labeling. To prevent cell overload with complexes and facilitate the tracking of single particles and their co‐localization with stained organelles, we conducted a “pulse and chase” experiment. Cells were initially incubated with complexes for 4 h, then washed and further incubated until imaging.

Fluorescent images (Figure 2c) and quantitative image analysis using ImageJ (Figure S9, Supporting Information) revealed significant co‐localization of the complexes with late endosomes and lysosomes after 24 h of incubation, compared to early endosomes. This indicates that intracellular complexes were trafficked from early endosomes to late endosomes/lysosomes.

To complement live cell microscopy, we conducted a quantitative analysis of complex co‐localization with late endosomes/lysosomes using imaging flow cytometry. This method allowed us to track and analyze thousands of cells, providing results consistent with single‐cell analysis from live imaging. The co‐localization between the complexes and Lysotracker was quantified using the bright detail similarity (BDS) feature by ImagestreamX (gating strategy is detailed in Figures S1 and S2, Supporting Information). The plotted BDS histogram indicates numerically the extent of co‐localization and its change along the experimental kinetics. BDS1h is considered low with a mean BDS = 0.63, and by comparing to BDS24h, a shift is observed to a mean BDS = 1.00 (Figure S10, Supporting Information). Conceptually, since complexes are being uptaken into the cells during the first hours of incubation it would be accurate to compare changes in BDS once uptake has reached a plateau, which was found to be 8 h under the “pulse and chase” set‐up (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Therefore, based on comparison to BDS8 along 48 h, an increase in BDS is identified which was found significant according to p values and by representative images from the cells population (Figure 2d,e). It's important to note that cells showing co‐localization (increase in BDS) do not necessarily mean that all complexes within these cells are in lysosomes, but rather a portion of the entire complexes' population. Live cell imaging after 72 h of incubation supported these findings, indicating that some complexes remain in lysosomes (Figure S12, Supporting Information).

Evidently realizing that complexes are accumulating in lysosomes indicates a first barrier to complexes’ delivery (endosomal release). The persistence of complexes in lysosomes up to 72 h post‐incubation suggests a strong interaction between Q‐starch and siRNA, retaining the siRNA from degradation as indicated by its fluorescent signal. Considering not all complexes show co‐localization with the lysosome, raises questions about the presence of complexes in the cytoplasm and siRNA release. Therefore, we directed the study toward further investigation of the complexes' intracellular path using single particle tracking and live cell imaging for detailed analysis.

Complexes Dynamic Transport Reveals Subpopulations Based on Complex Localization

Cellular uptake and intracellular transport are dynamic processes, thus live cell fluorescent microscopy and single particle tracking can provide insight into the dynamics of the complexes in live cells and elucidate the mechanism of siRNA delivery by Q‐starch. Using single‐particle tracking, complexes’ movements can be tracked and analyzed by means of rates and diffusivity at the single‐particle resolution. This approach provides a nuanced perspective compared to conventional flow data, as it furnishes additional information at the single‐cell level, shedding light on the heterogeneous nature of complex behaviors within the cell population. Analysis of such data can be particularly informative in understanding intracellular delivery processes, as the rates of movement, diffusivity, and trajectories of complexes can hint at their intracellular localization and potential barriers to delivery.

To investigate the dynamics of siRNA delivery by Q‐starch, NAR cells were treated with Q‐starch5‐DTAF/siRNA complexes for varying durations (15 min, 30 min, and 4 h), followed by live cell imaging. Images were captured at 3‐s intervals over a total duration of 120 s. Figure 3a shows images of single cells, with bright spots representing the complexes (top row), and the superimposed trajectories of the complexes (bottom row) as tracked along the imaging time. Notably, within 15 min of incubation (Figure 3a, 15 min), complexes were observed to attach to the cellular membrane, consistent with previous findings suggesting interactions between positively charged complexes and negatively charged cell surface proteoglycans, leading to membrane association and subsequent endocytic uptake.[ 45 , 46 ] The specific mechanism and association with the cellular membrane have been argued in different studies for the case of Polyethyleneimine nanoparticles[ 47 ] and therefore in this case additional future studies on Q‐starch nanoparticles would be insightful. As the incubation time increased, a greater number of complexes were detected within the cytoplasm, exhibiting longer trajectories compared to their initial attachment to the membrane (Figure 3a, 30 min). After 4 h, a significant portion of complexes had penetrated the cells, displaying diverse movement patterns, akin to observations with other nanoparticle systems.[ 29 , 30 , 48 ] The trajectories of the complexes were classified by the analytical fit of the Mean Square Displacement (MSD) versus time to a power‐law function of the type <r2 > ∼ tα revealing 3 distinct types of motion according to the value of α: normal diffusion (α = 1), super‐diffusion (α > 1), and sub‐diffusion (α < 1).

Q‐starch/siRNA complexes tracking analysis. NAR cells were incubated with 5‐DTAF‐labeled (green) complexes (siRNA concentration was 50 nM) for the indicated time and imaged over time using a live cell fluorescent microscope. a) Representative images taken from live cell imaging and particle cellular tracks following 15 min, 30 min, and 4 h of incubation with the complexes. b) Tracking analysis and mean square displacement (MSD) of Q‐starch/siRNA complexes using the Fiji Trackmate plugin. MSD of particle population following 4 h of incubation demonstrating 3 distinct types of motion (flow, free diffusion, and obstructed motion). Each line represents an average particle population with the same type of motion. c) Cellular particle tracking analysis at varying times of incubation with complexes demonstrating the fraction of particles presenting each type of motion.

Figure 3b depicts representative plots, where each curve represents the average behavior of multiple particles within a specific class. This analytical approach has been extensively documented.[ 29 , 30 , 31 ] Super‐diffusion (α > 1.15) is typically associated with diffusion accompanied by flow or active transport (Flowmotion in Figure 3b). A plausible explanation for this motion is that after internalization via endocytosis, the complexes are engulfed inside endosomes that undergo motor protein‐driven movement along microtubules, as they mature along the endolysosomal pathway (early endosome, late endosome, and lysosome), in agreement with co‐localization data. Sub‐diffusion (α<0.85) is attributed to confinement, obstructed diffusion. A possible source of such behavior is particles that move along the endolysosomal path that eventually fuse with the lysosome in which complexes are in a confined space (Figure 3b, Obstructed motion). Finally, normal diffusion (0.85<α<1, Figure 3b, Diffusion) may indicate free diffusion in the cell cytoplasm following endosome escape.

Given the importance and implications of each pathway and its final fate on the gene silencing efficiency of complexes, we measured and plotted the fraction of particles exhibiting each type of motion at various incubation times with the complexes, divided into early (0–4 h) and late (4–24 h) kinetics (Figure 3c). The classification was based on the α value calculated from the MSD fit curve. The results clarify that during early incubation, most particles exhibit localized motion due to membrane localization of the complexes, with almost no flow motion observed. As incubation time increases, the majority (69%) of particles display obstructed motion, 16% exhibit active motion, and 15% show free diffusion. To compare our results with previously reported data, we calculated the apparent diffusion coefficients (Dapp ) for each type of motion. The values obtained were Dapp = 2.4 × 10−2 µm2 s−1 for complexes exhibiting active motion, Dapp = 3.7 × 10−3 µm2 s−1 for those diffusing in the cell cytoplasm, and Dapp = 5.6 × 10−6 µm2 s−1 for complexes experiencing confined motion. These values align with previously reported data for polyplexes.[ 31 ] Although the majority of particles are likely trapped in endosomes, the presence of freely diffusing particles indicates that some complexes escape the endosome into the cytoplasm, where they have the potential to induce efficient gene silencing. The gene silencing mechanism is highly robust, with only a few siRNA molecules needed to induce efficient silencing due to the recyclable nature of the RNA‐induced silencing complex (RISC), which can degrade multiple complementary mRNA molecules. Therefore, the presence of Q‐starch/siRNA complexes in the cytoplasm suggests that efficient gene silencing can be expected 24 h post‐transfection. Based on these findings, we propose that a significant fraction of complexes lose functionality due to entrapment in lysosomes, resulting from inadequate endosomal escape and inefficient siRNA decomplexation among those complexes that do manage to escape into the cytoplasm.

Exploring the Endosomal Escape in Order to Increase Gene Silencing Efficiency

To elucidate the role of endosomal escape as a barrier in the transport of Q‐starch/siRNA complexes, we grafted Q‐starch with imidazole. Previous studies have shown that grafting imidazole onto siRNA carrier backbones can enhance gene silencing efficiency due to its buffering capacity in the acidic conditions of endolysosomal vesicles, thereby promoting endosomal escape through the “proton sponge” hypothesis.[ 49 , 50 , 51 ] To test if endosomal escape is the primary delivery barrier, we synthesized Q‐starch‐Imidazole (Q‐starch‐Im) as depicted in Figure 4a. The synthesis was confirmed by FT‐IR and 1H‐NMR data (Figures S13 and S14, respectively, Supporting Information).

Effect of Imidazole grafting on Q‐starch complexes gene silencing efficiency. a) Synthesis and chemical structure of Q‐starch‐Imidazole (Q‐starch‐Im) using 4‐Imidazolecarboxylic acid. b) In vitro gene silencing transfecting NAR cells with Q‐starch‐Im −1siP‐gp complexes at N/P 4–8 with and without chloroquine (Chlorq.). Noncoding siRNA (NC control) was utilized as off‐targeting control at N/P = 8. Gene silencing was also examined for non‐grafted Q‐starch/siRNA complexes in the presence of chloroquine. Gene silencing was quantified at the mRNA level using RT‐PCR and the results were normalized to the untreated group (n = 3, mean ± STDEV).

Complexation of siRNA with Q‐starch‐Im was confirmed at varying N/P ratios by gel electrophoresis and the physical properties of the complexes were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential (Figure S15, Supporting Information). Complete complexation between siRNA and Q‐starch‐Im was detected starting at an N/P ratio of 1 and above, with sizes ranging from 40–50 nm, independent of the N/P ratio. The zeta potential transitioned from negative values at N/P ratios of 0.5–2 to positive values at N/P 4. Compared to Q‐starch, the N/P ratio required to achieve positively charged complexes was higher for Q‐starch‐Im. This is due to the pKa values of the imidazole amines (6.9 and 13.4), which are not protonated at the pH of the water during complex assembly. Therefore, the groups contributing to siRNA complexation are the quaternary amine groups, constituting only a third of the total nitrogen content.

Gene silencing efficacy of Q‐starch‐Im −1siRNA complexes was examined in NAR cells. Cells were treated with complexes at N/P ratios of 4, 6, and 8, which were found suitable based on their physical properties for siRNA delivery. P‐gp gene silencing was assessed 24 h postexposure and quantified using quantitative RT‐PCR. As shown in Figure 4b, the complexes did not induce P‐gp gene silencing at the tested N/P ratios. Treatment with Q‐starch‐Im−1siP‐gp complexes resulted in mRNA P‐gp expression levels similar to those observed in the non‐coding (NC) complexes and untreated groups. This indicates that promoting endosomal escape did not enhance gene silencing efficiency. Previous studies have employed imidazole modification on polysaccharide backbone. For instance, Dhanya et al.[ 52 ] developed AuNPs using starch‐PEI particles, which presented efficient cellular uptake with low transfection efficiency unless modified with histidine (containing imidazole ring) or arginine due to aiding in the endosomal escape barrier. Similarly, other studies have utilized imidazole modification on liposomes to deliver mRNA with improved delivery upon modification.[ 53 , 54 ] However, seems that in the current study utilizing imidazole modification on Q‐starch backbone didn't enhance gene silencing. To further validate this finding, NAR cells were treated with chloroquine (100 µM) in the presence of either Q‐starch/siP‐gp complexes (Figure 4b and Q‐starch/siRNA, N/P = 2 + Chlorq.) or Imidazole‐grafted complexes (Figure 4b and Q‐starch‐Im −1siRNA). Chloroquine, a weak base that accumulates in acidic compartments, induces endosomal escape of nanoparticles. However, according to the current data, gene silencing in the presence of chloroquine also did not improve efficiency, suggesting that decomplexation, the subsequent step to endosomal escape, is likely the rate‐limiting step in siRNA delivery using Q‐starch. This hypothesis is supported by results showing fluorescent‐labeled complexes in lysosomes 72 h post‐treatment (Figure S12, Supporting Information), highlighting the stability of the complexes in the lysosomal environment.

Explore the Application of Low Frequency (20 kHz) Ultrasound by a Mean to Study Q‐Starch Intracellular Delivery Barriers

To investigate the decomplexation barrier during Q‐starch/siRNA cellular transport, we employed low‐frequency (20 kHz) ultrasound (LFUS). Previous studies have demonstrated that LFUS can induce the decomplexation of polymeric nanoparticles and stimulate drug release, suggesting its potential to promote Q‐starch/siRNA decomplexation[ 55 , 56 , 57 ] therefore has potential to promote Q‐starch/siRNA decomplexation. We utilized an LFUS apparatus[ 58 ] in which the cell culture plate is positioned in a plate horn transducer and ultrasound is applied at 2 W cm−2 for 10 and 20 s. As a means to induce decomplexation inside the cells, ultrasound was applied for 1 h following NAR cell exposure to Q‐starch/siP‐gp complexes at N/P = 2. During this time point complex attachment to the membrane and uptake into the cells was achieved; therefore, mechanistically the LFUS has the potential to demonstrate both increased cellular uptake and decomplexation. NAR cells were treated with complexes following which LFUS was applied for different durations. Following 24 h gene expression was identified at the protein level. In addition, NAR cellular viability was also measured to ensure that application of LFUS will not induce cell death and can bias gene silencing data. Indeed, the application of LFUS did not affect cell viability (Figure S16, Supporting Information) along with had a significant effect on gene silencing efficiency following 24 h of transfection (Figure 5 ).

Effect of LFUS on Q‐starch/siRNA delivery barriers and gene silencing efficiency. a) Gene silencing efficiency of NAR cells treated by Q‐starch/siPgp complexes for 1 h followed by application of LFUS (2 W cm−2, 10 and 20 s). Gene silencing was quantified at the protein level 24 h post‐treatment (siRNA concentration 50 nM, n = 3, mean ± STDEV). b) Effect of LFUS on Q‐starch/siRNACy5 complexes uptake into NAR cells visualized by confocal microscopy, images present representative cells from 3 biological replicates, n = 3 (Cell membrane labeled in green, Complexes labeled in red).

Cells treated with complexes and subjected to LFUS showed ≈50%–60% gene knockdown. Notably, the duration of LFUS exposure did not influence the efficiency of this setup. It appears that LFUS exposure affected the kinetics of the transfection pathway, likely aiding in overcoming delivery barriers. To determine whether LFUS influenced the uptake of the complexes or addressed intracellular barriers, we examined cellular uptake efficiency. NAR cells were treated with fluorescently labeled complexes (siRNACy5), and LFUS was applied 1 h post‐treatment for 10 and 20 s. Cellular uptake was visualized using confocal microscopy, with cell membranes and cytoplasm stained green. The results indicated that Q‐starch/siRNA complexes could efficiently internalize NAR cells independently of LFUS. The intracellular uptake levels were unaffected by ultrasound application, suggesting that the enhanced gene silencing was due to overcoming subsequent intracellular barriers, endosomal escape, and decomplexation.

Conclusion

This study delves deeply into the mechanism of siRNA delivery by quaternary starch. Due to the potential to deliver genes in vitro and in vivo, elucidating the delivery mechanism and potential barriers is of high importance. Our study points out that Q‐starch/siRNA complexes internalize the cells efficiently via dynamin‐dependent endocytosis, reaching various destinations within the cell, such as the cytoplasm or cellular compartments like lysosomes. Localization in the cell cytoplasm is crucial due to the localization of the RNAi mechanism and indeed cytoplasmic localized complexes were identified and released the siRNA. Additionally, our findings suggest that the disassembly of the complexes could be the major delivery barrier, nonetheless employing LFUS could overcome this barrier, resulting in faster and more effective gene silencing. Future investigations should focus on studying LFUS's impact on facilitating endosomal escape, enabling the release of more complexes into the cell cytoplasm, thereby potentially enhancing the efficiency of this delivery system.

Experimental Section

Materials

Starch Quaternization

Q‐starch was synthesized according to a previously published and detailed procedure.[ 20 , 22 , 28 ] Shortly, soluble potato starch (500 mg, 0.0030 mol anhydroglucose units) reacted with CHMAC quaternization reagent (7.8 mL, 9 g, 0.0478 mol) for 24 h at room temperature (RT) under basic conditions (0.0475 mol sodium hydroxide). The product was isolated using precipitation with acidified (1 vol % HCl) mixture of ethanol and acetone (1:3 vol). The precipitate was washed (80% ethanol) and subsequently dialyzed against DDW for 48 h. The dialyzed product was then dried by lyophilization for 72 h.

Q‐Starch Fluorescent Labeling

In order to elucidate the delivery mechanism, 2 approaches, fluorescent microscopy and Imaging flow cytometry were utilized. Each approach relies on fluorescent probe tracking therefore Q‐starch was modified with fluorophores. The fluorophores were chosen according to the cellular organelle labeling (early endosome, late endosome, and Lysosome) excitation and emission spectrum in order to avoid overlap with the excitation and emission of the complexes and the cellular organelle. The chosen fluorophores were 5‐(4,6‐dichlorotriazinyl) amino fluorescein (5‐DTAF), and cyanine‐3.5 (Cy3.5).

Q‐Starch‐5‐(4,6‐Dichlorotriazinyl) Amino Fluorescein (5‐DTAF, Ex. 492 nm, Em. 516 nm)

Q‐starch (100 mg) was dissolved in DDW (3.0 mL) and adjusted to pH 11–12 with 1 M NaOH. Following, 5‐DTAF dissolved in DMSO (7.5 mg in 0.3 mL) was added to Q‐starch solution and stirred for 24 h at room temperature in the dark. The reaction mixture was neutralized with 0.2 M HCl and the product, Q‐starch5‐DTAF, was separated from free 5‐DTAF by dialysis against PBS solution for 72 h and then against DDW for 48 h. The dialyzed product was then freeze‐dried and lyophilized for 72 h to obtain the purified Q‐starch5‐DTAF.

Q‐Starch Cyanine‐3.5 (Cy3.5, Ex. 591 nm, Em. 604 nm)

Cyanine‐3.5‐carboxylic acid in DMSO (2.7 mg in 0.2 mL) was added to Q‐starch dissolved in DMSO (50 mg in 7.0 mL). 30 mg of 1‐ethyl‐3‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) and 10 mg of 4‐dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) were added to the above mixture and the reaction mixture was stirred for 48 h at RT in the dark. The labeled Q‐starchCy3.5 was separated from free cyanine‐3.5‐carboxylic acid by dialysis against PBS for 72 h and then against DDW for 48 h. The dialyzed product was then freeze‐dried and lyophilized for 72 h to obtain the purified Q‐starchCy3.5.

Q‐Starch Modification by 4‐Imidazole

100 mg of Q‐starch and 86 mg of 4‐Imidazole‐COOH were dissolved in 12 mL of DMSO solution while stirring for 5 min at RT. 146 mg of EDC.HCl and 43 mg of DMAP were added to the above reaction mixture and mixed for 48 h at RT. Following, the solvent and unreacted substances were removed by dialysis against DDW for 72 h. The dialyzed product was then freeze‐dried and lyophilized for 72 h to obtain the purified Q‐starch‐4‐ Imidazole (Q‐starch‐Im).

Preparation of Q‐Starch/siRNA Complexes for In Vitro Study

Q‐starch/siRNA complexes were prepared as previously published[ 22 ] at the desired N/P molar ratio (molar ratio between positive nitrogen groups on Q‐starch and negative phosphate groups on siRNA backbone). 15 µL of Q‐starch, dissolved in DDW, was added into siRNA solution (185 µL, 0.27 µM, in RNase‐free water). Following gentle vortexing, the sample was incubated at RT for 40 min before use to allow self‐assembly formation of the complexes. The concentration of Q‐starch solution to be added was determined according to the N/P ratio and %N, measured by elemental analysis following each synthesis. For each experiment, the complexes were freshly prepared. The complexes were examined and characterized using dynamic light scattering (DLS), and ζ potential. Methods are supplemented in the supporting information file.

Cell Culture

In this study, the human ovarian adenocarcinoma cell line NCI‐ADR/Res (NAR) as the cancer cell model was utilized. This type of cancer was highly resistant to chemotherapeutic drugs and expresses a prominent level of the transmembrane protein p‐glycoprotein (P‐gp, encoded by ABCB1 gene) extrusion pump as part of its drug resistance mechanism.

NAR cells were used for all experiments and were cultured in RPMI growth media containing 1% L‐glutamine, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in a sterile incubator at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. In addition, the culture was continuously evaluated for mycoplasma (using an EZ‐PCR Mycoplasma test kit according to the manufacturer's instructions) and was found mycoplasma‐free.

P‐gp Gene Knockdown In Vitro

Gene knockdown studies were done using NAR cells and the targeted gene to downregulate was ABCB1, which was responsible for P‐gp overexpression in this cell line. This model represents different types of cancer that were resistant to chemotherapy and lack an efficient therapeutic platform.[ 59 , 60 ]

NAR cells, seeded in a 6‐well plate at a density of 105 cells/well, were supplemented with 800 µL of serum‐ and antibiotic‐free media. Q‐starch/siRNA complexes, encapsulating non‐coding siRNA (siNC5) or with siRNA targeting P‐gp (siP‐gp), were prepared at N/P 1 or 2 as described above to reach 50 nM siRNA concentration in each well. The complexes solution (200 µL) was supplemented to the cells and the cell plate was incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Untreated cells were supplemented with 200 µL of serum‐ and antibiotic‐free media. Following 4 h, 500 µL of RPMI compensation medium (30% serum without antibiotics), was added and further incubation was continued for 24, 48, and 72 h. Gene silencing efficiency was quantified according to P‐gp mRNA level using quantitative real‐time (RT) PCR (method described in the supporting information file).

NAR Cell Proliferation

NAR cell proliferation rate was determined at the same conditions as for transfection. NAR cells were seeded in a 6‐well plate in RPMI growth media at a density of 105 cells/well. Following 24 h, which was considered as day 0, and in each day thereafter, the cells were trypsinized and counted using trypan blue assay. Two conditions were examined, cell growth (medium containing 10% serum) and cell starvation (serum‐free medium).

Quantification of Q‐Starch/siRNA Complexes’ Cellular Uptake Efficiency

NAR cells, grown in a 6‐well plate at a density of 105 cells/well, were supplied with 800 µL of fresh RPMI growth medium, and incubated with fluorescently labeled Q‐starch/siRNACy5 complexes at N/P 2 (250 nM, 200 µL). Following incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, at indicated times, the media was aspirated, and the cells were washed with PBS and harvested using 300 µL of trypsin. The cells were resuspended in 1 mL of RPMI medium which was then replaced by precipitating the cells (260 g, 8 min, and 4 °C) and resuspending the pellet in FACS buffer (PBS 99% vol. and FBS 1% vol.). The cells were kept on ice until data acquisition was performed. Cellular uptake experiments at cell starvation conditions were repeated under the same protocol except for the supplied media which was RPMI starvation media (serum‐free medium).

Data acquisition was done by ImagestreamX (ISX) mark II imaging flow cytometer and InspireTM software (Amnis). Camera magnification was x40 at a slow cell flow rate. Cellular uptake experiments were done with Q‐starch/siRNACy5 complexes. A 642 nm excitation laser was used to excite the Cy5 fluorophore, and its emission was observed with a 640–745 nm filter (cell images were observed accordingly in channel 05, ch05). During all experiments, laser intensity was set to 50 mW, which was adjusted by calibrating the system according to the highest and lowest intensity samples in order to set up the dynamic range of the fluorescence readout along the kinetics and to avoid saturation of the emitted signal at the ISX setup. At least 10 000 events (cells) were recorded in each sample and each event was imaged. Data analysis and cell gating into subpopulations were done using IDEAS software (Amnis) (The gating strategy is presented in the supporting information file, Figure S3, Supporting Information). Cells in focus (using gradient RMS plot of the bright field channel) and single cells (using the bivariate plot of aspect ratio versus cell's area of the bright field channel) were first gated in order to avoid analysis of out‐of‐focus cell images and of cell clusters respectively. Following, using a histogram plot presenting the distribution of the cells according to their fluorescent intensity in the Cy5 channel, untreated cells (cell autofluorescence) and cells presenting complexes uptake were identified and gated. The percentage of cells presenting uptake and quantitative analysis of complexes' cellular uptake was then analyzed and plotted as a function of time.

Co‐Localization with Lysosome/Late Endosome Using Imagestream Flow Cytometry

Q‐starch/siRNA complexes’ co‐localization with lysosome study was done in a “pulse and chase” mode. The “pulse and chase” mode was used in order to avoid over‐loading of cells with complexes and in order to allow accurate analysis of co‐localization with the Lysotracker. Using this approach, a specific population of complexes that were taken up by the cells and followed their delivery path as a function of time could be tracked. Cell seeding and complex delivery conditions were the same as in the uptake study, however, complexes incubation with the cells was limited to 4 h after which the complexes were washed and replaced by RPMI growth medium. The 4 h pulse of complexes was followed by continuous monitoring (“chase”) of both cellular uptake and co‐localization as a function of time. At the indicated time, before cell harvesting, the cell lysosome/late endosome was fluorescently labeled using Lysotracker Green DND‐26. The stock dye (1 mM) was diluted x10000 using RPMI growth medium and the cells were incubated for 5 min with 1 mL/well dye solution. Following, the cells were harvested, and data acquisition and analysis were done using ISX flow cytometry. Data collection was performed at the same illumination as was used to calibrate for continuous cellular uptake experiment. 488 nm excitation laser (25 mW) was used to excite the Lysotracker fluorophore (green) and its emission was observed with a 505–560 nm filter (cell images were observed accordingly in channel 02, ch02). Co‐localization of Q‐starch/siRNACy5 complexes with the lysosome/late endosome was quantified using the ISX Bright Detail Similarity (BDS) feature. This method allows to analysis of at least 5000 events, therefore significant changes in BDS during time indicate the intracellular localization with the Lysosome/late endosome. Co‐localization gating strategy is presented in the supporting information file (Figure S4, Supporting Information).

Live Cell Imaging and Co‐Localization

NAR cells grown on 13 mm cover slides in 6‐well plates were incubated with 5 nM of fluorescently labeled Q‐starch/siRNA complexes (Q‐starch5‐DTAF‐labeled or Q‐starchCy3.5‐labeled) for the indicated times. In case the experiments were performed in a pulse‐chase approach, cells were incubated with the complexes for 4 h followed by the removal of the complexes and tracking them for the next 4 to 24 h.

Co‐localization with early endosomes or late endosomes was done by labeling the organelle. Cells were infected with early and late endosome fluorescent markers 18 h before incubation with complexes. Colocalization with the lysosome was done by labeling the lysosome with LysoTracker green (Invitrogen). Cells were first incubated with complexes for the indicated times, then stained by Lysotracker as mentioned above.

The imaging system consisted of an Axioplan2 upright light microscope (Zeiss) equipped with laser‐based illumination. Imaging was performed with an IXON3 (ANDOR, Oxford), which was controlled by micro‐manager software (µManager, Vale Lab, UCSF 2006–2011). DTAF‐labeled particles and the lysotracker‐labeled cells were imaged with the 473 nm laser, and the RFP‐labeled early and late endosome‐stained cells were imaged using the 561 nm laser, and a double emission filter set (FITC, TRITC) and FERT set in the optosplit for G1/2 and G4. Co‐localization was performed using the coloc2 plugin on Fiji software. Manders’ overlap coefficients M1 and M2 were used to evaluate the degree of co‐localization between 2 fluorescent labels. Background intensity was subtracted from each image individually. M1 was defined as the proportion of pixels with non‐zero intensities from the green image (Q‐starch5‐DTAF‐labeled, or Lysotracker green), for which the intensity in the red channel was also above zero. Conversely, M2 was defined as the proportion of pixels with nonzero intensities from the red image (Q‐starchCy3.5, Early endosome Rab5a‐RFP or late endosome rab7‐RFP), for which the intensity in the green channel was also above zero.

Mechanism of Q‐Starch/siRNA Complexes Uptake and Inhibition of Intracellular Transport

Cellular uptake mechanism and intracellular path study were conducted using a small molecule inhibitor, Dynasore. The cells were pretreated by a solution of the inhibitor diluted in RPMI growth media (80 µM) for 30 min. Then, the cells were exposed to Q‐starch/siRNACy5 or Q‐starch5‐DTAF/siRNA complexes for an additional 4 h. At the end of the treatment, the cells were harvested, and uptake efficiency was quantitatively determined by ISX as described above or visualized by live cell imaging microscopy. To exclude internalization by passive diffusion, NAR cells were preincubated at 4 °C to reduce active cell processes including uptake by endocytosis. Following, the cells were incubated with Cy5‐labeled complexes for 4 h at 4 °C. Uptake efficiency was quantitatively determined by ISX as well.

In order to study the intracellular path of the complexes, NAR cells, in which the early endosome was labeled, were incubated in the presence of Bafilomicyn A1 (0.1 µM) and exposed to 5‐DTAF‐labeled complexes. At the indicated time points, live cells were imaged (as mentioned above) and co‐localization with the early endosome was examined.
