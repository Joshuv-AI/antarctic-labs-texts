Prognostic significance of isolated clinically detected regional melanoma metastasis in minor nodal basins
Authors: Mervi Rautalin, Dina Saks, Serigne N Lo, Thomas E Pennington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe first sites of lymphatic drainage from primary melanoma tumour, sentinel nodes, are primarily found at major nodal basins in the axilla, groin or neck. However, there are minor nodal fields at triangular intermuscular space (TIS) in the back, epitrochlear in the arm and popliteal in the lower leg. Background

Sentinel node biopsy remains a valuable tool in management of clinically node-negative melanoma patients with intermediate or thick melanomas. Experience with lymphoscintigraphy has greatly increased our knowledge of the lymphatic drainage of the skin and has shed light on smaller node fields [1, 2]. These “minor” node fields at the popliteal fossa, epitrochlear and triangular intermuscular space (TIS) represent occasional sites of isolated nodal metastasis, yet little is known whether these instances carry a similar prognosis to metastasis to larger (and vastly more common) node fields of the neck, axilla and groin.

Previous studies have presented data on finding first tier sentinel nodes in lymphoscintigrams at minor nodal fields especially with patients with melanoma on the posterior trunk, but also at other sites. Incidences reported 12.7% on the posterior trunk and 5 to 8% at other sites, but only 1% on the lower limb [3]. It was suggested that recurrence in these minor field nodes is uncommon. These interval nodes were associated with 14% incidence of micrometastases in the nodes [3]. Previous studies have argued both for and against further mainstream nodal field dissection in case of minor nodal field metastases; a study from early 2000 recommended completion dissection of major downstream nodal basing only in cases where metastasis was clinically detected at the major nodal basin [4]. Another study suggested that in case a nodal field metastasis was detected particularly in the popliteal nodal field, an upstream inguinal nodal field dissection was of benefit [5]. Of course, such recommendations need to be interpreted in light of changes to the surgical management of nodal disease in the (neo)adjuvant immunotherapy era, and the practice-changing results of the MSLT-2 and DeCOG-SLT trials [6, 7].

The documented data for isolated metastasis in minor nodal fields is scarce. We address this subgroup of minor nodal field metastasis in this study; we include patients with metastasis at epitrochlear/brachial, popliteal and TIS on the upper back. Data concerning recurrence-free (RFS) and overall survival (OS) were analysed and presented.

Methods

Aims

To compare recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) between patients with isolated metastasis detected at minor field versus major node field. Secondary considerations include investigating the pattern of disease recurrence after management of the nodal metastasis in this subpopulation and providing guidance on management.

Study design

A database review of patients treated at Melanoma Institute Australia from 1980 to 2020 was performed. Inclusion criteria included patients aged 18 or older with isolated metastasis detected at minor or major field on physical examination or imaging, either at the time of diagnosis of the primary melanoma or during follow up.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics and pathological features were summarized using frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables. To address potential imbalances between patients with minor and major nodal fields, we performed propensity score matching at a 1:1 ratio, incorporating age, primary site, ulceration, year of diagnosis, and stage (AJCC 8th edition) [8]. Following the matching process, the Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) for each group. Time-to-event outcomes for both RFS and OS were measured from a common clinical starting point, defined as the date of melanoma diagnosis for each patient. This approach ensured appropriate handling of differences in the timing of nodal presentation (synchronous vs. metachronous).

Survival differences between minor and major nodal field patients were assessed using the log-rank test.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.3 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Thirty six patients met the study criteria. 20 were male and 16 were female. The median age at diagnosis was 53 years (range 22–87). Primary site was unknown in 5 patients (14%), where the first presentation of melanoma was a metastasis in a minor nodal field. The primary site was on the upper limb in the majority (18/36, 50%), followed by trunk (9/36, 25%), lower limb (3/36, 8%) and head and neck (1/36, 2.8%). Sites of the metastases were mainly epitrochlear/brachial (21/36, 58%) followed by TIS (11/36, 31%) and popliteal (4/36, 11%). Median Breslow thickness was 2.1 (range 0 to 8.2). Ulceration was present in 5/25, 20%. Nine out of 33 patients were at Stage I (27.3%) at time of diagnosis, 13 at Stage II (39.4%) and 11 at Stage III (33.3%). In three patients (8.3%), missing data precluded accurate staging.

Eleven patients (33.3%) presented with synchronous melanoma metastasis in the minor field at time of diagnosis, the remainder were detected at follow up at median time of 1.2 years (95% CI: 0.5–2.4) post-treatment of the primary melanoma. The majority of metastases were detected by physical examination. Eight were detected by imaging modalities (five by ultrasound, three by PET/CT scan). Follow-up ranged from 2 to 15 years.

Treatments consisted of resection of the minor nodal field metastasis for 23/36 (63.9%) patients. Two (5.6%) patients received adjuvant radiation therapy and one (2.8%) adjuvant anti-PD1 immunotherapy after resection. Radical lymphadenectomy of the onwards major nodal basin was performed for 1 (2.8%) patient in the ilioinguinal field and for 9 (25%) patients in the axilla.

Fifteen out of 36 (41.7%) patients developed a further recurrence. Distribution of recurrence in relation to minor nodal field location was 10 out of 21 patients having epitrochlear (47.6% recurred), 2 out of 4 popliteal (50%) and 3 out of 11 TIS (27.3%). The recurrence appeared in a minor nodal field in 3 patients (20% of recurring patients), 6 (40%) developed a downstream major nodal field recurrence and 6 (40%) developed distant metastases (Tables 1 and 2). In matched cohort comparison there were no statistically significant difference in recurrence sites between groups. Further analysis of the patients who developed a recurrence found that all of them had a minimum of 2 identified risk factors: one risk factor being a minor field metastasis (n = 15), age over 50 at time of minor field nodal disease detection (n = 11), ulceration present at primary site (n = 2), BT (Breslow Thickness) over 3.5 (n = 3), mitotic rate 1 or higher (n = 12), size of nodal deposit reported extensive and/or number of positive nodes 3 or higher (n = 7) or ENE (extra nodal extension) positive (n = 6). 6 patients had 3 of these risk factors, four had 4 and four had 5 risk factors. Discussion

Melanoma management continues to evolve as we understand more of the biology of the disease and as new treatment approaches undergo scrutiny. Several prospective international trials have enabled management of nodal disease to drastically change in the last 30 years. Elective lymph node dissection for higher risk melanomas made way for sentinel node biopsy and selective lymphadenectomy for micrometastatic disease, which in turn ceded to nodal observation in these patients after the results of MSLT2 and DeCog trials were released [6, 7, 9]. The current accepted management for clinically detected nodal metastasis is a therapeutic lymph node dissection. However, new studies are examining the safety and efficacy of more limited lymphadenectomy in these patients [10, 11]. Such an approach has been made possible due to multidisciplinary advancements, most significantly the discovery and application of immune checkpoint inhibitors in melanoma management, which are now an integral part of management of regionally advanced disease.

This study presents novel data of melanoma patients with isolated metastasis in minor fields treated at a large melanoma treatment centre (Melanoma Institute Australia/Sydney Melanoma Unit) over a forty-year period. The retrospective study covers a substantial register of melanoma patients over decades of changing treatment of melanoma, and yet, found only 36 patients with isolated minor nodal field metastasis. Our data suggests a distinct difference in RFS and OS between patients with isolated minor node field metastasis and a matched cohort with the more common pattern of nodal disease. It bears noting the relatively modest group size which limits the strength of these observations. However, these patients may represent a less aggressive subgroup with a more favourable clinical course.

The main focus of this study was to investigate OS and RFS of minor nodal field patients, but in addition a notion was also made of the high-risk factors as per MSLT II study group and AJCC7 and AJCC8 diagnostic guidelines have stated [9, 12, 13, 14, 15].

Identifying risk factors and evaluating significance is intriguing. It is worth noting that even though the patients with minor nodal field metastases had several high risk-factors, they still had more favourable RFS than matched patients with major nodal field metastasis.

The small size of the cohort, and retrospective nature of the study do both limit the power of these observations. Nevertheless, there were some intriguing findings. Three patients who had 5 risk factors recurred less than 12 months from minor field metastasis (two epitrochlear and one popliteal nodal field) but one patient with epitrochlear field metastasis and 5 risk factors recurred more than 5 years later. Group comparisons for all these risk factors were not performed, as the matching process performed in this study did not take into account the size or number of metastatic nodes, extranodal extension or mitotic rate. These were examined as descriptive clinicopathological characteristics rather than used as covariates for analysis. Also, comorbidities were not addressed in the group comparisons as the data is not available for the whole study period for all patients. Whether this affected the treatments given, remain debatable. There is scarce data presenting high risk features for minor nodal fields, such as number of affected nodes in the field. This study considered three or more affected nodes in line with the MSLT group’s description of high-risk features in melanoma [9]. This of course is arbitrary when translated to the minor node field, as there are much smaller number of total nodes at these sites. Due to the rarity of isolated minor node field metastasis, it is unlikely that a sufficiently powered prospective study could be carried out to more clearly answer prognostic and management questions. Therefore, studies like ours may be of benefit to guide treatment decisions in these patients. This may be of greatest value in patients whereby adjuvant immunotherapies are being considered, but perhaps a relative contraindication is present. In this scenario, the treatment decision may fall in favour of observation, and certainly in favour of a personalised, neoadjuvant approach with the extent of surgery dictated by the clinicopathological response to immunotherapy.

The correlation between the extent of treatments given and OS or RFS was not statistically tested as the group sizes were small. In this study only one patient received adjuvant immunotherapy and did not have recurrence. The treatment era in the world of changing melanoma treatment as such was not included in the analysis – only one patient in minor nodal field group received modern adjuvant therapy thus indicating the favorable outcome of RS and OS were not dependent of these therapies. These patients did not receive modern treatments and still their OS and RFS outperformed similar patients with major nodal field metastasis. So, we could argue that more likely these patients genuinely are less likely to recur or die of melanoma close to diagnosis phase. When looking at the long timespan of this study; from 1980 to 2020 the treatment scheme of metastatic melanoma has certainly changed. In our study, only one patient received adjuvant treatment other than radiation therapy, the long study period is unlikely to affect the findings of this study of recurring later and having better OS. Nevertheless, as the field of melanoma treatment is changing, this should be noted – immunotherapy is changing the treatment scheme for Stage III patients [10]. Only two patients in this study received adjuvant radiation therapy and both had a recurrence. Ten patients had complete nodal field dissection and half of them had a recurrence. The primary sites, minor nodal field sites and pattern of recurrence to either local, regional or distant sites were evident in all different minor fields, ostensibly independent of the treatment provided. With more data on this small subgroup of patients, it may be possible to better identify patients at highest risk of recurrence and to also determine the extent of required surgical treatments [5]. As previously stated, regional nodal dissection was only recommended if a patient had a minor nodal field metastasis and proven metastasis in the regional field [4]. A prospective study from the USA found that minor nodal field metastasis is likely the sole site of metastasis when detected by lymphoscintigraphy and sentinel node biopsy [16]. Therefore, for isolated minor nodal field patients it remains unclear whether regional nodal dissection should be recommended or not. One study from Italy discussed the regional nodal dissection for minor nodal field patients might improve the prognosis of this patient group through improved node field control [17]. In our study, the fewest recurrence occurred in patients with isolated metastases in the TIS. This might be a subgroup of patients who could be safely placed on active surveillance protocols. We are eager to see what the recently started MSLT3 study will reveal in this subgroup of melanoma patients [10].

Conclusion

This study presents data of small subgroup of melanoma patients, who develop isolated metastatic nodes in the minor node fields of epitrochlear, popliteal and triangular intermuscular space node fields. These patients might have better prognosis in terms of disease recurrence compared to those with nodal metastasis to major fields of head and neck, axilla or inguinal sites. It is suggested that these uncommon scenarios may be best managed with a personalised approach combining neoadjuvant immunotherapy where indicated, and limited surgery, with expected good outcomes in patients achieving a major pathological response. For those achieving a lesser response, close observation, with or without adjuvant immunotherapy, rather than further dissection, might seem a reasonable and balanced approach.
