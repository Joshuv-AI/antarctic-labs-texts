GWAS and genomic prediction of crown rust resistance in the southern US elite oat (Avena sativa L.) germplasm
Authors: Janam Prabhat Acharya, Md Ali Babar, Kathy Esvelt Klos, Stephen Harrison, Noah DeWitt, Amir M. H. Ibrahim, Paul Murphy, Raja Sekhar Nandety, Jason Fiedler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrown rust (CR), caused by Puccinia coronata f. sp. avenae, is a major constraint to oat (Avena sativa L.) production in the southern United States. We dissected the genetic architecture of CR resistance and assessed genomic prediction in 234 winter and facultative oat lines adapted to the southern United States. Using multi‐environment phenotyping across five CR‐prone sites and 8,234 high‐quality single‐nucleotide polymorphisms, we conducted genome‐wide association study (GWAS) and genomic prediction analyses. GWAS detected 13 significant loci, nine of which co‐localized with reported quantitative trait loci or contained plausible resistance candidates such as leucine‐rich repeat (LRR), nucleotide‐binding site‐LRR, and serine/threonine kinases. A stable locus on chromosome 3D was repeatedly identified in Baton Rouge, LA (2016); Castroville, TX (2016); Winnsboro, LA (2017); and in the combined analysis, indicating cross‐environment consistency. Additional loci were supported on chromosome 4C (Citra and Quincy, FL, in 2017, and the combined analysis) and 2A (Winnsboro, LA, in 2017 and the combined analysis). Allele stacking was associated with large phenotypic gains. Lines with five or more favorable alleles showed >56% lower crust and 63% lower Crust_Per than lines with none, supporting marker‐assisted pyramiding. Genomic prediction models achieved high prediction accuracy. Parametric models, particularly BayesA and RRBLUP, performed strongly across cross‐validation schemes; random forest was broadly comparable; and gradient boosting was lower. These results underscore the effectiveness of using GWAS and genomic prediction to enhance breeding for durable CR resistance in oat and provide a genomic framework for developing resilient cultivars suited to southern United States growing conditions.

INTRODUCTION

Oat (Avena sativa L.), a member of the genus Avena in the grass family, is the sixth most important cereal crop globally, following rice, wheat, maize, barley, and sorghum (Yan et al., 2021). Common oat plays a crucial role in agriculture, serving as a staple for human consumption and a valuable feed source for livestock. Oat grains are renowned for their elevated protein and soluble dietary fiber contents (Gorash et al., 2017). Oats contain a significant amount of a soluble fiber called β‐glucan, which can lower cholesterol and blood sugar levels (Fardet, 2010; Othman et al., 2011; Pomeranz et al., 1971). Oat is used as a traditional animal feed or as an additional source of nutrients. They are also frequently used as grain and forage for livestock, horses, and poultry (Federizzi & Mundstock, 2004; Winkler et al., 2018). In the United States, harvested area has contracted markedly (from ∼19.1 million acres in 1973 to ∼2.5 million acres in 2017), underscoring the need for cultivars that combine resilience and milling quality (USDA Crop Reporting Board, 1975, 2017; McNish et al., 2020).

Among production constraints, crown rust (CR) caused by Puccinia coronata f. sp. avenae (Pca) is the most significant disease and is the central target of this study, driving recurrent yield and quality losses across oat‐growing regions (Nazareno et al., 2018). Historical records from the USDA Agricultural Research Service, Cereal Disease Laboratory (USDA‐ARS CDL), indicate that from 1981 to 2013, CR epidemics in the US generally resulted in <10% yield loss, except in 1991 and 1993, when North Dakota experienced 15% losses, and in 1997, when Minnesota and Louisiana reported losses of up to 20% (Long and Hughes, 2007). Under conditions unfavorable for infection or when inoculum pressure is low, CR‐related yield losses in oats can be minimal, around 5% (Nazareno et al., 2018). However, a severe outbreak in 2014 led to the loss of over 13 million bushels (18.7% of total US oat production) with yield losses (Nazareno et al., 2018) reaching 50% in Minnesota and 35% in South Dakota (USDA‐ARS CDL, 2016; Nazareno et al., 2018).

Genetic resistance is the primary strategy for managing CR in oat, complemented by foliar fungicide applications (Klos et al., 2017; McNish et al., 2020). Race‐specific (qualitative) resistance follows a gene‐for‐gene system (Jones and Dangl, 2006), often triggering a hypersensitive response (Klos et al., 2017), but it is frequently short‐lived as virulence evolves (typical durability ∼5 years; Carson, 2008; Chong and Kolmer, 1993; Leonard, 2003). Numerous race‐specific genes (Pc genes) have been identified and deployed, including Pc38, Pc39, Pc45/PcKM, Pc48, Pc53, Pc58, and Pc91 (Admassu‐Yimer et al., 2018; Hoffman et al., 2006; Kebede et al., 2019; McCartney et al., 2011; Wight et al., 2005). In contrast, adult‐plant (partial) resistance (APR) is quantitative, multigenic, and typically more durable, exhibiting lower infection frequency, longer latent period, and smaller uredinium size (Díaz‐Lago et al., 2002; Leonard, 2003).

Winter and facultative oat (intermediate between spring and winter types that requires less vernalization to induce flowering) germplasm with CR tolerance or resistance has been developed over the past decade by southern US oat breeding programs, including those at the University of Florida, Louisiana State University, North Carolina State University, and Texas A&M. However, the genetic basis of this resistance remains largely unexplored, as neither the underlying mechanisms of action nor the genes governing these disease reactions have been mapped. Given that the germplasm developed by southern US breeding programs is genetically distinct and possesses unique agronomic traits (Esvelt Klos et al., 2016), it represents a valuable but underutilized resource for enhancing CR resistance. Oat cultivars developed for different production zones may lack the resistance alleles present in southern germplasm, highlighting the importance of identifying and utilizing this untapped genetic diversity. Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) can be employed to dissect the genetic architecture of CR resistance in this panel, allowing the identification of loci associated with resistance traits. Furthermore, genomic prediction (GP) offers a complementary approach to accelerate breeding efforts by predicting breeding values based on genome‐wide marker effects (Meuwissen et al., 2001). Taken together, the focus on underexplored southern United States winter and facultative germplasm and the integration of GWAS for locus discovery, GP for deployment, and resistance pyramiding constitute a comprehensive framework for breeding.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and field experiment

The southern oat association panel (SOAP), consisting of 234 winter and facultative oat lines, was evaluated for resistance to CR. These lines were developed by southeastern US breeding programs, including the University of Florida, Louisiana State University, North Carolina State University, and Texas A&M. The SOAP panel includes mostly elite germplasm (good agronomic performance, adaptation, and maturity) and commercial varieties and some historically important lines that grow in the southern and some other areas in the United States. Field trials were conducted in 2016 and 2017 across multiple locations recognized as CR hotspots. In 2016, trials were established in Castroville, TX (CT_2016); Baton Rouge, LA (BL_2016); and Citra, FL (CF_2016), while in 2017, experiments were conducted in Citra, FL (CF_2017); Quincy, FL (QF_2017); Castroville, TX (CT_2017); and Winnsboro, LA (WL_2017). Each trial followed a randomized complete block design with two replications. To promote uniform disease pressure, spreader rows (Brooks [Murphy, 1979], a CR‐susceptible oat variety) were planted along the perimeter of each experimental plot. Oat lines were sown using a headrow planter in single‐row plots measuring 1.2 m in length, with a row spacing of 15 cm. Each plot was seeded with approximately 3 g of seed to ensure consistent plant density. At the CF_2016 location, unintended herbicide drift from an adjacent peanut trial resulted in damage to the second replication, preventing reliable phenotypic data collection; therefore, it was excluded from subsequent analyses. Similarly, herbicide damage at the CT_2017 location compromised the second replication, which was also omitted from further analysis due to the lack of usable data. We excluded entire site years with off‐target herbicide injury to avoid confounding of disease phenotypes by non‐pathogen stress. Because the exclusion was applied uniformly at the environment level and the injury was not genotype specific, the missingness is effectively non‐differential with respect to genotype and should not bias estimated genotype effects. The main consequence is reduced sample size and slightly narrower environmental representativeness. Retaining those trials would have depressed vigor and inflated apparent disease, which would bias effects downward and obscure true genetic differences.

Genotyping

The panel was genotyped by GBS (PstI/MspI) (Poland et al., 2012). at the USDA‐ARS Genotyping Laboratory, Fargo, ND. Reads were trimmed with BBduk and aligned to the PepsiCo oat reference OT3098v2 using Bowtie2 (Poland et al., 2012). Variants were called with SAMtools/BCFtools (Danecek et al., 2021; Li, 2011) and imputed using LD‐kNNi in TASSEL 5.0 (Bradbury et al., 2007). From 51,534 initial single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), 8,234 biallelic, chromosome‐assigned markers were retained after QC (mapping quality ≥ 30, depth ≥ 4, MAF ≥ 0.05, observed heterozygosity ≤ 0.10, per‐marker missing ≤ 10%; that is, call rate ≥ 90%).

Population structure and linkage disequilibrium

Population structure in the oat panel was assessed using principal component analysis (PCA) in R with the “prcomp” function. A scree plot was utilized to determine the appropriate number of principal components for inclusion in the genotype–phenotype association analyses. Pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) between SNP markers (r 2) was estimated in TASSEL v5.0 (Bradbury et al., 2007) using a sliding window of 50 markers. Decay of LD over physical distance (bp) was estimated in R by fitting a nonlinear model following the Hill and Weir method (Hill and Weir, 1988). The pairwise LD values (r 2) were plotted against genetic distance using the R statistical package (R Core Team, 2020), and a locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) regression curve was fitted. The pattern of LD decay was determined based on the point where the LOESS regression of mean r 2 between SNP pairs intersected the threshold of 0.2, which is commonly applied in many related studies as a practical benchmark for useful LD, approximating the distance at which marker–causal correlations cease to be reliably tagged in GWAS and enabling cross‐study comparability (Bazzer et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2008).

Phenotyping and statistical analysis

The CR infection was assessed at the milk to early dough growth stage. Disease severity was evaluated on a whole‐plot basis using two scoring methods: Crust score (Crust), rated on a 0–9 scale, based on pustule size and the extent of chlorosis or necrosis. Response categories included 0 (no visible symptoms), resistant (1–2), moderately resistant (3–4), moderately susceptible (5–6), and susceptible (7–9), and Crust percentage (Crust_Per), visually estimated based on the 0–100 modified Cobb scale (Peterson et al., 1948). Summary statistics for the two variables were calculated for each location‐year combination using the dplyr package in R.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess the effects of genotype and replication on the traits Crust and Crust_Per for each location‐year combination separately. The replication effect was significant in certain environments; therefore, least squares (LS) means were estimated using the lmer package and utilized as phenotypic data for subsequent analyses. LS means they were calculated separately for each location‐year and across all location‐years combined.

To estimate the combined LS means for the traits Crust and Crust_Per, a linear mixed‐effects model was fitted using the lmer() function from the lme4 package in R. Each location‐year combination is considered one environment. The model was specified as follows: (1) yijkl=μ+Gj+Ei+E:Ril+G:Eji+εijkl where, yijkl is the observed phenotypic value (Crust or Crust_Per) of genotype j in environment i, μ is the overall mean, G j is the fixed effect of the jth genotype, Ei is the fixed effect of the ith environment (combined location‐year), (E:R) il is the random effect of lth replication nested within ith environment, assumed ∼N(0,σR2), (G:E) ji is the fixed effect genotype × environment interaction effect, and εijkl∼N(0,σε2) is the residual error.

For each location‐year combination, adjusted genotype means for Crust and Crust_Per were estimated using a linear model: (2) yij=μ+Gi+Rj+εij where yij denotes observed trait value for the genotype i in the replication j, Gi is the fixed effect of genotype, Rj is the fixed effect of replication, and εij∼N(0,σR2) is the residual error.

Phenotypic distributions were visualized using the ggplot package in R (R Core Team, 2020). This model was implemented using the lm() function in R, and LS means for each genotype were extracted using the emmeans package. Broad‐sense heritability (H 2) was estimated to assess the genetic influence on phenotypic variation. H 2 was estimated using Equations (3) and (4) for each location year and across all year‐locations, respectively. (3) H2=σG2σG2+σer2 (4) H2=σG2σG2+σGEe2+σeer2 where σG2 represents the genotypic variance, σGE2 denotes the genotype‐by‐environment interaction variance, and σe2 corresponds to the error variance. The terms r and e refer to the number of replications and environments, respectively. Variance components were estimated using the Asreml‐R package (Butler et al., 2009). In addition, narrow‐sense heritability (h 2) on a line‐mean basis was estimated using additive genetic variance (σA2) obtained from a genomic relationship matrix (G) fitted in ASReml‐R. For individual environments, h 2 was calculated as follows: (5) h2=σA2σA2+σer2

Genome‐wide association study

GWAS was run for each site year and for the combined dataset a using generalized linear model (GLM), MLM, fixed and random model circulating probability unification (FarmCPU), and Bayesian‐information and Linkage‐disequilibrium Iteratively Nested Keyway (BLINK) in GAPIT (Lipka et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2018). Marker–trait associations were tested on LS means for Crust and Crust_Per. Model performance was evaluated with Q–Q plots, and significance was declared at a Bonferroni‐corrected threshold. Population structure was accounted for using principal components from the genotype PCA, and relatedness was modeled with a kinship matrix in the MLM. To reduce confounding from very large loci, we also performed conditional scans in which SNPs with phenotypic variance explained (PVE) >25% were included as fixed covariates within the same site year; conditional terms were not carried to other environments unless they independently met the threshold. Full model specifications, including the MLM and conditional formulations, are provided in the Supporting Information Methods.

After GWAS, we quantified the cumulative effect of favorable alleles by counting homozygous favorable genotypes at loci with PVE greater than 20%, grouping individuals by count, and comparing group means for Crust_all and Crust_Per_all using one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's honestly significant difference. Details of GWAS are described in the Supporting Information Methods.

Genomic prediction

To evaluate the prediction accuracy (PA) of different GP strategies for Crust and Crust_Per, we compared five GP models: Genomic Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (GBLUP), ridge regression best linear unbiased prediction (RRBLUP) (Meuwissen et al., 2001), BayesA (Meuwissen et al., 2001), random forest (RF) (Chen and Ishwaran, 2012), and gradient boosting regression (GBR) using packages Asreml‐R (Butler et al., 2009), BGLR (Pérez and de Los Campos, 2014), randomForest (Liaw and Wiener, 2002), and gbm (Ridgeway, 2007). From a breeding perspective, we compared parametric models with machine learning models because the genetic architecture of CR resistance can vary across traits and environments. Parametric methods such as GBLUP, RRBLUP, and BayesA are standard in GP and are efficient when additive effects predominate, providing stable predictions and straightforward deployment in routine pipelines (Meuwissen et al., 2001; Pérez and de Los Campos, 2014). Machine learning methods such as RF and Gradient Boosting can model non‐linear responses, local interactions, and heterogeneous marker importance, which may improve ranking when architecture departs from strictly additive assumptions (Chen and Ishwaran, 2012; Liaw and Wiener, 2002; Ridgeway, 2007). Details of each prediction model are described in the Supporting Information Methods.

Model performance was assessed using three distinct cross‐validation (CV) schemes, CV1, CV2, and CV0, following the framework established by Jarquín et al. (2014). CV1 withholds entire genotypes during training and predicts their performance, mimicking selection for new, untested lines. CV2 withholds random plot records while allowing the same genotype to appear in both training and testing, reflecting incomplete trials with missing observations. CV0 withholds one environment at a time and predicts in a previously unobserved environment, assessing the portability of models across target environments. CV1 and CV2 used fivefold splits; CV0 rotated each environment as the test set. Details of CV schemes are also in the Supporting Information Methods.

RESULTS

Disease infection and heritability

The summary statistics for Crust and Crust_Per traits across different locations years separately are presented in Table 1. The mean Crust scores ranged from 2.50 (CF_2017) to 5.09 (CT_2016), while Crust_Per mean values ranged from 18.26 (CF_2017) to 43.19 (CT_2016). Broad‐sense heritability (H 2), representing the proportion of phenotypic variance explained by genetic factors, was relatively high, ranging from 0.75 to 0.86 for Crust and 0.74 to 0.83 for Crust_Per. Narrow‐sense heritability (h 2) was moderate to high and consistently lower than broad sense, as expected: for Crust it ranged from 0.55 in QF_2017 to 0.68 in WL_2017, and for Crust_Per it ranged from 0.54 in QF_2017 to 0.65 in BL_2016 (Table 1). The highest broad‐sense heritability estimates for both traits were observed in WL_2017, indicating a strong genetic influence in this environment. High broad‐sense heritability estimates were observed in the combined analysis across locations and years, with values of 0.88 for Crust_all and 0.87 for Crust_Per_all (Table S1).

Marker coverage, population structure, and LD

The distribution of SNP markers across the 21 oat chromosomes varied, ranging from 120 markers on chromosome 3A to 831 markers on chromosome 6C. The C subgenome exhibited the highest SNP density, followed by the D and A subgenomes. A lower density of SNP markers was observed in the centromeric regions compared to the telomeric regions (Figure S3).

PCA revealed the presence of three subpopulations, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 7.1% and 6.5% of the total genetic variation, respectively (Figure 1a). To determine the optimal number of principal components, a scree plot was constructed by visualizing the variance explained by the first ten principal components. The inflection point occurred around the third principal component (Figure 1b), supporting the existence of three genetically distinct clusters within this oat panel. Notably, lines from Texas formed a separate cluster, while those from North Carolina grouped independently. In contrast, lines from Florida and Louisiana exhibited substantial overlap, forming a single cluster. Overall, the modest variance explained by PC1 and PC2 and the limited separation among groups indicate weak population structure, which is advantageous for GWAS because it reduces confounding from stratification and lowers the risk of spurious associations.

LD decay was assessed using the squared correlation coefficient (r 2) between marker pairs. Genome‐wide LD decayed to r 2 = 0.2 at ∼8.6 Mb (Figure 2). Among the three oat sub‐genomes, LD decay was approximately 3.6 Mb for genome A, 20.2 Mb for genome C, and 4.1 Mb for genome D (Figure S4). Notably, sub‐genome C exhibited a slower LD decay rate compared to sub‐genomes A and D.

Association analysis

We conducted GWAS separately for each environment, across environments within each year, and across all years. To identify the most suitable GWAS model, we evaluated the performance of GLM, MLM, FarmCPU, and BLINK using Q‐Q plots. Among these, the BLINK model demonstrated superior performance, exhibiting fewer false associations, and was consequently selected for downstream association analysis (Figure 3; Figures S5 and S6). Thirteen SNPs were significantly associated with CR resistance in one or more locations (Table 2; Table S5). These comprised 23 significant MTAs for Crust and Crust_Per (Figure 4; Figures S7 and S8). The phenotypic variation accounted for by the significant SNPs was between 6.92% and 53.15% (Table 2; Table S5). Two SNPs located on chromosome 4C, separated by only 92 base pairs, were identified as significantly associated with the trait of interest. To determine whether these SNPs represented independent quantitative trait locus (QTL) or were tagging the same underlying locus, we conducted a conditional association analysis. Specifically, the SNP with the smaller p‐value was incorporated as a fixed‐effect covariate, and the genome‐wide association analysis was re‐run (Dahleen et al., 2015). The SNPs were considered statistically non‐independent and thus likely tagging the same QTL if the inclusion of one SNP in the model resulted in an increase in the p‐value of the other SNP to above the threshold of 0.001 (Dahleen et al., 2015). Including the SNP Schr4C_73642432 increased the p‐value of the other SNP Schr4C_73642524 > 0.001 in all the instances. So, these two SNPs represent the same QTL. This reduces the total number of loci from 13 to 12. Several SNPs were associated with both Crust and Crust_Per across multiple environments (Figure 4; Figures S7 and S8). On chromosome 2A, the SNP Schr2A_363649087 was consistently associated with both Crust_Per_all, the overall LS means for crust percentage, and Crust measured at the WL_2017 location, indicating its stable effect across environments (Table 2; Figures S7 and S8). To further evaluate the stability of this association, we examined the marker's effect across all environments at a nominal significance threshold of p < 0.05 (Table S6). Schr2A_363649087 showed significant or suggestive effects in Crust_CT_2016 (p = 0.0034), Crust_BL_2016 (p = 0.0189), and Crust_CF_2017 (p = 0.0215), with consistent positive effect estimates. Although it did not surpass Bonferroni correction in every environment, the direction and magnitude of its effects remained stable across locations, reinforcing its potential as a broadly effective locus. This suggests that the QTL tagged by Schr2A_363649087 may contribute to resistance across diverse environments or act against a subset of Pca races common across sites.

A particularly important locus was identified on chromosome 3D, where Schr3D_474416215 was significantly associated with Crust_all, Crust_Per_all, and location‐specific traits, including Crust_Per_WL_2017 and Crust_Per_CT_2016 (Table 2; Figure 4; Figures S7 and S8). To assess the consistency of this association, we evaluated the marker across all environments using a nominal threshold (p < 0.05). Schr3D_474416215 remained significant or marginally significant in Crust_Per_QF_2017 (p = 0.015), Crust_Per_CF_2017 (p = 0.041), Crust_CF_2017 (p = 0.0156), and Crust_BL_2016 (p = 0.0415), with negative effect sizes throughout (Table S6). The consistent direction of effect across all five environments supports the hypothesis that this SNP tags a QTL with broad resistance potential, or alternatively, a race‐specific response effective against pathogen variants common to multiple locations.

Among the identified SNPs, several explained a substantial proportion of PVE, indicating the presence of major‐effect QTLs. For example, Schr6A_436031934 accounted for up to 37.43% of the variance in Crust_Per_all, while Schr3C_40606299 explained over 50% of the variance in both Crust_BL_2016 and Crust_Per_BL_2016.

To account for the confounding influence of these large‐effect loci in downstream association analyses, SNPs with PVE greater than 25% were incorporated as fixed‐effect covariates in the model. This strategy was employed to control for major genetic effects and to reduce the risk of model overfitting or inflation of association signals due to a few disproportionately large‐effect loci. Including these SNPs as covariates improved the detection of additional minor‐effect loci and enhanced model interpretability by adjusting for their dominant contributions. Specifically, we included Schr2A_363649087 (PVE = 52.89%) as a fixed effect for both traits in WL_2017, Schr3C_40606299 (PVE = 53.15%) for BL_2016, Schr6A_436031934 (PVE = 37.43%) for Crust_all and Crust_Per_all, and Schr6C_12072836 (PVE = 39.75%) for both traits in CF_2017.

Conditional analyses confirmed the utility of this approach. Specifically, including Schr6A_436031934 as a fixed‐effect covariate revealed a previously undetected signal at Schr2A_363649087, but this association was previously detected for Crust_Per_all. Subsequent conditioning on Schr2A_363649087 led to the identification of two additional loci: Schr5D_427288167 and Schr6C_621136873. Likewise, incorporating Schr3C_40606299 as a covariate uncovered novel associations at Schr2D_112928098 and Schr7D_7926136 (Table S7).

To evaluate the cumulative effect of pyramiding favorable alleles from major loci, we focused on six SNPs with the highest phenotypic variance explained (PVE > 20%) across traits. Each line in the panel was assigned a value based on the number of homozygous favorable alleles present, ranging from 0 to 6. For both Crust_all and Crust_Per_all, mean disease severity declined with increasing numbers of favorable alleles (Table 3). Lines carrying five or more favorable alleles showed a reduction of >56% in Crust and 63% in Crust_Per compared to the group with no favorable alleles (Table 3). These results demonstrate the additive effects of stacking resistance loci and highlight the potential of pyramiding strategies to enhance CR resistance in oat.

Candidate gene identification

Thirteen significant SNPs identified through association analysis were flanked by genomic regions extending ±8.6 Mb (Figure 2), collectively spanning 2377 predicted gene models in the hexaploid oat genome. Of these, 1799 genes were functionally annotated using the OT3098 v2 reference genome (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov/jb/?data=/ggds/oat‐ot3098v2‐pepsico). A network‐based functional analysis prioritized 328 candidate genes with putative roles in disease resistance (Table S8). These candidates were distributed across 10 chromosomes: 1C, 2A, 2D, 3C, 3D, 4C, 5C, 5D, 6A, and 6C, with the highest concentrations observed on chromosomes 2D (53), 3D (47), and 5D (45). The identified genes encompassed several well‐known resistance‐related families, including leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) proteins, nucleotide‐binding site‐lrr (NBS‐LRR) proteins, and serine/threonine protein kinases, key regulators of pathogen recognition and defense signal transduction. Noteworthy examples included Xa21‐like receptor kinases (1C and 3C), RPM1 and XIAO homologs (2A), RGA1 (2D and 5D), RGA4 (4C and 5D), and leaf rust 10‐like kinases (3D and 6C). Additional defense‐related candidates, such as F‐box/LRR‐repeat proteins, NB‐ARC domain‐containing proteins, and zinc finger–type resistance proteins, were identified across nearly all implicated chromosomes, indicating a diverse and widespread disease resistance gene repertoire.

Furthermore, the inclusion of large‐effect markers (PVE > 25%) as fixed‐effect covariates in conditional association models led to the identification of four additional loci. Candidate genes underlying these regions included well‐characterized resistance‐associated genes such as RGA4, RGA5, and members of the LRR and NBS‐LRR families. Specifically, these new loci harbored two genes on chromosome 2D, 38 on 5D, 20 on 6C, and 27 on 7D (Table S9), further expanding the catalog of potential disease resistance genes and highlighting the value of conditional modeling for uncovering additional loci masked by major‐effect variants.

GO enrichment of genes within the ±8.6 Mb windows around lead MTAs (Table S10) showed strong over‐representation of kinase activity/protein phosphorylation (GO:0016301, GO:0006468; high fold enrichment), phosphotransfer/transferase functions (GO:0016772/GO:0016773), and cell‐surface receptor signaling (GO:0007166), together with defense/response to stress categories (GO:0006952/GO:0006950). Additional enriched terms included recognition (cell recognition) and nucleotide/lectin‐type binding (ADP binding, carbohydrate binding). These patterns are consistent with the observed concentration of NBS‐LRR and LRR‐RLK/kinase (where RLK is receptor‐like kinases) candidates near association peaks, supporting a mechanism of pathogen perception at the plasma membrane followed by downstream phosphorylation cascades.

Genomic prediction

In CV1 (Figure 5), the predictive abilities of GBLUP, RRBLUP, BayesA, and RFR for Crust were comparable, with a mean value of 0.53, indicating their robustness in GP. For Crust_Per, predictive abilities were slightly lower, with a mean of 0.51 across all models except GBR. Notably, GBR exhibited the lowest predictive accuracies for both traits, with mean values of 0.46 for Crust and 0.43 for Crust_Per. A similar trend was observed in CV2 (Figure S9), where GBLUP, RRBLUP, BayesA, and RFR maintained nearly identical predictive abilities of 0.61 for Crust, while GBR again exhibited lower performance (0.54). For Crust_Per, predictive abilities remained consistent across the four models (0.59), with GBR showing reduced accuracy (0.51).

In CV0 (Figure S10), predictive abilities for Crust ranged from 0.61 to 0.71, with BayesA and RRBLUP achieving the highest values (0.71), followed by GBLUP (0.70) and RFR (0.67), while GBR exhibited the lowest (0.61). For Crust_Per, predictive abilities ranged from 0.57 to 0.68, with BayesA, GBLUP, and RRBLUP achieving the highest accuracy (0.68), followed by RFR (0.64), with GBR again demonstrating the lowest performance (0.57). Incorporating high PVE SNPs as fixed‐effect covariates did not lead to improved prediction accuracy in any of the CV schemes evaluated.

DISCUSSION

Phenotypic analysis and heritability

This study assessed variation in Crust and Crust_Per within the SOAP across two environments in 2016 and three in 2017, resulting in a comprehensive evaluation over five location‐year combinations. Both traits exhibited substantial phenotypic variation across environments. The consistently high heritability estimates for both Crust and Crust_Per across environments highlight a strong genetic basis for CR resistance in the SOAP. Notably, heritability increased when data were pooled across all environments, indicating that the expanded dataset improved the model's ability to detect and partition genetic variance. Rather than being masked by genotype‐by‐environment interactions, genetic effects remained stable and became more evident with increased replication and environmental diversity. This increase in heritability across environments is also a strong indicator of stable, non‐race‐specific resistance, suggesting that the underlying genetic effects are consistent across diverse rust races and environmental conditions. CR has been reported to have moderate heritability in other species, such as Lolium (Arojju et al., 2018). High heritability estimates for CR have been reported in other studies conducted by Holland and Munkvold (2001), Zambonato et al. (2012), and Barbosa‐Prestes et al. (2008). This pattern suggests that the resistance traits are robust across locations and years, supporting their suitability for selection in multi‐environment breeding programs.

Population structure and LD

PCA revealed a low level of genetic differentiation among the oat lines. The first two principal components accounted for 13.6% of the total genetic variation (PC1: 7.1%, PC2: 6.5%), which is relatively low compared to other crops such as barley, where the first two PCs can explain up to ∼90% of the variation (Hamblin et al., 2010). The PCA plot showed partially overlapping clusters, suggesting some population stratification likely driven by geographic or breeding program origin (Figure 1a). This may be due to the sharing of germplasm between the breeding programs. The scree plot showed a rapid decline in variance explained after the first few components, indicating that most of the genetic variation is captured by the initial principal components (Figure 1b). In a study conducted on beta‐glucan concentration in oats, the first two principal components accounted for 13.42%–20.68% of variation (Bazzer et al., 2025), which is slightly higher than what we have observed in our population. Similar results were observed in a study conducted by McNish et al. (2020). Although accounting for population structure in GWAS is important to minimize false‐positive associations, it can also reduce the power to detect true associations when trait variation is confounded with population structure (Zhao et al., 2007). Therefore, the relatively low level of population structure observed in this panel is advantageous, as it lowers the risk of spurious associations while preserving the ability to identify true marker–trait relationships (Bazzer et al., 2025; McNish et al., 2020). The extent and rate of LD decay across the genome play a critical role in determining both the power and mapping resolution of GWAS (Bazzer et al., 2025). Reported estimates of LD decay in oat vary widely, from 1.4 to 4.1 cM, though translating these into physical distances (in megabases, Mb) remains challenging due to differences in recombination landscapes (Chaffin et al., 2016; Esvelt Klos et al., 2016; Newell et al., 2011; Sunstrum et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023). Notably, previous studies have consistently reported faster LD decay in North American oat germplasm, which aligns with our observation of an averaged LD decay of approximately 8.6 Mb in our southern panel. This is in contrast to a more rapid decay rate of 2.29 Mb observed in a globally diverse oat panel (Peng et al., 2022). Such discrepancies may be attributed to differences in genetic diversity among panels, particularly the inclusion of landraces and the effects of historical selection pressures (Flint‐Garcia et al., 2003). In the northern oat germplasm, LD decay was found at 28 Mb (Bazzer et al., 2025). As a predominantly self‐pollinating species, oat exhibits extended LD, a pattern also observed in barley, which can reduce mapping resolution but increases power for detecting associations over broader genomic regions. In our study, LD decay patterns varied across subgenomes, with the C subgenome showing the highest extent of LD decay (20.2 Mb), followed by D (4.1 Mb) and A (3.6 Mb) (Figure S4). The elevated LD decay in subgenome C likely reflects its greater marker density and more uniform genome coverage compared to the A and D subgenomes (Figure S4). Conversely, genomic regions within the A and D subgenomes, where we observed fewer significant associations, may have had lower marker density and less uniform coverage, which could reduce the power to detect loci in those regions. This lack of signal may reflect not only underlying biology but also technical limitations in genome coverage that constrain detection of associations in these subgenomes. Moreover, because GBS is a reduced‐representation approach with uneven site discovery, our SNP panel may not tag all LD blocks, particularly in sparsely covered intervals, so some true associations could have been missed.

Association analysis and candidate gene identification

This study identified a significant MTA on chromosome 1C at position 33,499,379 bp (Schr1C_33499379). This marker is located within the previously reported QTL QPc.NSGC‐Mrg28 (chr1C:45,013,006–443,226,303), which has been associated with seedling resistance to CR (Winkler, Michael Bonman, et al., 2016). The gene Pc91 has been proposed as a candidate underlying this QTL based on prior mapping studies (Gnanesh et al., 2015; Winkler, Bonman, et al., 2016). Pc91 confers race‐specific resistance effective at both seedling and adult plant stages and was originally derived from Avena magna × Avena longiglumis (Rooney et al., 1994). It was first deployed commercially in the oat variety “HiFi,” released in 2001 in the United States and in 2006 in western Canada (McCartney et al., 2011). Early surveillance data suggested Pc91 was highly effective, with virulence observed in only 1 of 77 US spring oat isolates and 1 of 61 winter oat isolates in 2008 (Carson, 2008), and in just 1 of 859 Canadian isolates collected between 2007 and 2009 (Chong et al., 2008). However, long‐term USDA CR survey data show that Pc91 has undergone rapid resistance erosion over the past decade (Moreau et al., 2024). Specifically, Pc91 falls into a group of resistance genes (Group C) that maintained low virulence frequencies through the early 2000s but experienced a sharp increase in virulence from 2015 onward (Moreau et al., 2024). By 2020–2022, 75%–100% of isolates surveyed were virulent to Pc91, especially in the Northern United States, suggesting widespread adaptation of the P. coronata population (Moreau et al., 2024). Nevertheless, during the 2016 and 2017 field seasons, the years from which phenotypic data were collected in this study, Pc91 may still have conferred partial resistance in the southern panel, as the frequency of virulent races had not yet reached fixation. This temporal context supports the biological relevance of the detected association and the candidacy of Pc91 as a functional contributor to resistance in this environment. In a differential study conducted at CF 2023, the line that carried Pc91 received a rating of 2 (resistant), and the check HiFi got a score of 3 (moderately resistant), suggesting that the races that we might have are still ineffective against the resistance gene, Pc91.

Taken together, the erosion of Pc91 underscores the inherent vulnerability of single, race‐specific all‐stage resistance (ASR) genes under sustained pathogen selection (Moreau et al., 2024). In contrast, adult‐plant resistance tends to be quantitative, reduces infection efficiency and pustule development, and has shown greater durability across pathosystems because it imposes weaker directional selection on any single avirulence factor (Díaz‐Lago et al., 2002; Klos et al., 2017; Leonard, 2003). In practical terms, deploying Pc genes like Pc91 alone is unlikely to provide long‐term protection once virulence frequencies rise, whereas stacking Pc genes with multiple APR loci can buffer against shifts in race composition and slow resistance breakdown. The loci we detected that do not co‐localize with known Pc genes are candidates for such APR, and their additive effects are consistent with the allele‐stacking patterns observed in this panel. For breeding, this argues for a two‐track strategy: maintain Pc gene utility for near‐term protection where effective, but prioritize pyramiding of APR QTL using genomic selection (GS) to accumulate small effects across the genome, supported by ongoing race surveillance and regional deployment to avoid over‐reliance on any single gene.

Candidate gene identification within a ±8.6 Mb window around this marker revealed 29 putative resistance‐related genes (Table S8), including well‐known disease resistance genes such as Xa21, RGA1, and Lr10, alongside multiple RLKs, which are key components of plant immune signaling in species like rice, wheat, and potato (Feuillet et al., 2003; Ivaschuk et al., 2016; Song et al., 1997).

Similarly, a significant marker on chromosome 2A (Schr2A_363649087) was found to overlap with the previously reported QTL QCR.TxH‐Mrg33 (chr2A:351,451,840–365,501,335), which has been associated with CR resistance (Sunstrum et al., 2019). This marker is located 4.17 Mb downstream of the QTL peak and has been previously identified as resistant across multiple experimental time points (McNish et al., 2020). Analysis around this region also revealed the presence of disease resistance‐related genes, including LRR proteins and several kinase genes (Table S8).

On chromosome 2D, the marker Schr2D_152314935 co‐localized with the resistance QTL QCR.ProxBoy‐12D (chr2D:115,185,096–183,493,256), previously identified by Babiker et al. (2015). This region contains resistance gene analogs such as RPM1, RGA2, RPS2, and RGA5, known to confer disease resistance in Arabidopsis, banana, and rice (Axtell and Staskawicz, 2003; Boyes et al., 1998; Cesari et al., 2013). A significant association on chromosome 3C (Schr3C_40606299) corresponded to a QTL identified in oat cultivars Ogle and TAMO‐301, known to confer resistance to Puccinia coronata pathotypes BQMG and LGCC (Jackson et al., 2008). On chromosome 3D, marker Schr3D_474416215 showed consistent associations with the traits Crust and Crust_Per across environments (CT_2016, WL_2017, and combined analysis) and overlapped with multiple known Pc genes, including Pc44, Pc46, Pc50, Pc68, Pc95, and PcX (Chong et al., 1994; Fleischmann et al., 1971; Martens et al., 1968; Penner et al., 1993; Wong et al., 1983). Candidate genes within the flanking LD region include RGA1, RGA5, F‐box/LRR proteins, and several serine/threonine kinase genes (Table S8). On chromosome 5C, the significant marker Schr5C_523508959 was located within the QTL QPc.CORE.03.1 (chr5C:455,910,165–528,123,171), previously linked to CR resistance (Klos et al., 2017). Likewise, on chromosome 5D, marker Schr5D_477572861 overlapped with QTLs QPc‐APR.MakxMN‐1 and QPc‐APR.AsbxMN‐17 (chr5D:476,697,226–495,061,066), also associated with CR resistance (Lin et al., 2014). Another notable association was detected on chromosome 6A (Schr6A_436031934), within a genomic region harboring QTLs QPc.CORE.05, Pc50‐5, KasxPc50‐5, and Pc71 identified in resistant oat germplasm, including D526 × Lang and Y345 × C237‐89 (Bush and Wise, 1998; Bush et al., 1994; Klos et al., 2017). In contrast, significant markers located at Schr2D_82939886, Schr4C_73642524, Schr5D_68556351, and Schr6C_12072836 were not found to be associated with any previously reported QTLs, suggesting that these loci may represent novel sources of resistance to CR.

Incorporating markers that explained >25% of the phenotypic variance (PVE) as fixed‐effect covariates led to the identification of four significant loci. One such marker, Schr2D_112928098, located on chromosome 2D, lies approximately 2.25 Mb upstream of the previously reported QTL QCR.ProxBoy‐12D, which overlaps with the region harboring another significant SNP, Schr2D_152314935, and was identified in this study. Similarly, the significant marker Schr7D_7926136, mapped to chromosome 7D, resides within a genomic region densely populated with previously reported QTLs and known CR resistance genes. This region spans the interval chr7D:215,569–527,312,738, encompassing several resistance loci, including QCR.TxH‐Mrg02 (Sunstrum et al., 2019); QPcd.OxT2007‐32 and QPcDLA‐05‐LA.2010‐OxT_32 (Jackson et al., 2007); and QPcU‐NQMG‐05‐FD.2008‐OxT_32 (Jackson et al., 2008). Furthermore, the region includes characterized resistance genes such as Pc54 (Admassu‐Yimer et al., 2022), localized to chr7D:2,142,910–502,217,010, as well as Pc38, Pc62, Pc63, Pc39, and Pc48 (Harder et al., 1980; Wight et al., 2005), highlighting the genetic richness of this region for CR resistance. In contrast, significant SNPs Schr5D_427288167 and Schr6C_621136873 were not found in previously reported studies, suggesting they may represent novel loci contributing to CR resistance.

Our results suggest that pyramiding favorable alleles may contribute to improved resistance to CR in oat. A gradual reduction in both Crust and Crust_Per values was observed as the number of homozygous favorable alleles increased, indicating possible additive effects. In our panel, stacking favorable alleles was associated with a 56%–70% reduction in mean Crust and Crust_Per. In practical terms, that magnitude typically shifts entries from susceptible toward moderately resistant field reactions, which translates to substantial yield protection when epidemics develop. Using the historical loss ranges already documented for CR in US oats (≈5%–20% in most years, up to 35%–50% in severe outbreaks), a 60% reduction in severity would be expected to cut yield loss by roughly one‐half to two‐thirds under comparable disease pressure. This pattern supports the broader concept that combining multiple resistance loci can enhance the durability and effectiveness of disease control. Resistance gene pyramiding has been proposed as a strategy to provide broad‐spectrum and long‐lasting protection against diverse pathogen populations (Fuchs, 2017). In oats, previous studies have reported additive effects from stacking minor APR loci (McCartney et al., 2011). The development of molecular markers linked to resistance genes, such as Pc68, Pc71, Pc91, and Pc94, has improved the efficiency of assembling such multigenic combinations (Bush and Wise, 1998; Chen et al., 2006; Chong et al., 2004; Rooney et al., 1994). With the recent availability of reference genomes and gene‐based cloning tools (Steuernagel et al., 2017), the identification and deployment of additional resistance loci is likely to become more precise and efficient. Together, these findings highlight the potential value of pyramiding resistance alleles in oat breeding as a step toward more durable management of CR.

Genomic prediction

Traditionally, the improvement of disease resistance in crops has relied heavily on phenotypic selection, often centered around the identification and incorporation of well‐characterized ASR genes. The integration of molecular tools over the past few decades has revolutionized this process, with marker‐assisted selection (MAS) providing a targeted and efficient means of selecting traits with known genetic markers (Poland and Rutkoski, 2016). Building on these advances, GS represents a paradigm shift by enabling the simultaneous assessment of genome‐wide marker effects, capturing both large‐effect loci and the polygenic background underlying complex traits. By leveraging dense marker data, GS enhances PA and selection efficiency, offering a powerful tool for accelerating genetic gain (Heslot et al., 2012, 2015). Its utility has been increasingly validated across diverse crop species, particularly for improving quantitative traits such as disease resistance (Daetwyler et al., 2014; Juliana et al., 2017; Lorenz et al., 2012; Poland and Rutkoski, 2016; Rutkoski et al., 2011; Rutkoski et al., 2012). We examined the prediction accuracy of different models and used different CVs for the Crust and Crust_Per. These prediction models are based on differing assumptions regarding the genetic architecture of traits, and the relative advantage of one approach over another remains an active area of investigation. The comparative analysis of GP models across multiple CV scenarios revealed critical insights into their reliability and practical utility in plant breeding contexts. In CV1, which simulates the prediction of entirely untested genotypes, akin to predicting the performance of newly developed lines, GBLUP, RRBLUP, BayesA, and RFR demonstrated robust and comparable predictive abilities for both Crust and Crust_Per, with mean values around 0.53 and 0.51, respectively. Similar results were obtained in a study conducted on wheat for rust (Azizinia et al., 2020; Juliana et al., 2017), whereas some studies have shown non‐parametric models perform better than the parametric ones (Crossa et al., 2010; Gianola et al., 2006; Howard et al., 2014). Across all CV schemes, GBLUP, RRBLUP, and BayesA models that assume additive genetic effects consistently outperformed GBR in predicting both Crust and Crust_Per. This pattern suggests that the underlying genetic architecture of these traits may be primarily additive, with limited contribution from complex, non‐additive interactions such as dominance or epistasis. GBR, which is particularly well‐suited for capturing non‐linear patterns and interactions, may have been disadvantaged in this context due to the absence of strong interaction signals or threshold effects (Perez et al., 2022). Additionally, GBR's tendency to overfit when faced with high‐dimensional but largely additive data may have contributed to its reduced PA (Cheng and Wang, 2024). These findings align with the hypothesis that the majority of genetic variance for these traits is additive in nature and can be effectively captured by simpler, linear models.

CV1 simulates the prediction of entirely untested genotypes, reflecting scenarios commonly encountered in breeding programs where phenotypic data for newly developed lines is unavailable. The strong predictive performance observed in CV1 indicates that the models can effectively capture genetic signals underlying CR resistance, even in the absence of prior phenotype data for those genotypes. This underscores the potential of GS to accelerate breeding decisions by enabling early and accurate identification of resistant lines. In CV2, which mimics incomplete multi‐environment trials where data gaps exist for certain genotype‐environment combinations, the predictive performance of GBLUP, RRBLUP, BayesA, and RFR remained consistently high (mean ∼0.61 for Crust and ∼0.59 for Crust_Per). This demonstrates the models’ robustness in leveraging cross‐environment information, even when phenotypic records are missing for some environments. Notably, this finding suggests that disease phenotyping data from CR nurseries can be economically incorporated into breeding pipelines. Because prediction accuracy remains stable despite partial phenotyping, it may not be necessary to evaluate every genotype in every environment to accurately estimate their resistance profiles. This has important practical implications, as it enables efficient resource allocation and supports the use of sparse testing strategies in oat breeding programs. The superior performance observed in CV0 is an extrapolative scenario where predictions are made for environments entirely excluded from model training, further highlighting the practical potential of GS in anticipating genotype performance in novel or future conditions. Here, BayesA and RRBLUP achieved the highest predictive abilities (0.71 for Crust and 0.68 for Crust_Per), emphasizing the value of incorporating models that accommodate differing marker effect variances when genotype‐by‐environment interactions are prominent. This highlights the capacity of GS to extrapolate across environmental conditions. It also raises important biological questions about the stability and generalizability of resistance loci across diverse environmental contexts. The ability to predict phenotypes in novel environments may reflect the contribution of non‐race‐specific resistance, environmental buffering, or shared race profiles across regions.

From an operational standpoint, accuracies near 0.6–0.7 indicate that GPs can shoulder a substantial share of selection decisions. In CV1 and CV2, this level of predictive ability supports preselection of the top 30%–50% of lines before expensive disease nurseries, which can reduce total plots per cycle or hold footprint constant and expand the number of entries or families screened. Because CV2 accuracies remained high with incomplete data, programs can adopt sparse testing strategies, evaluating only a subset of lines per site and using GPs to fill gaps across environments. In cycle time terms, selecting parents on genome estimated breeding values (GEBV) at harvest can advance crosses without waiting for a full year of phenotyping; with r ≈ 0.65, the expected response approaches two‐thirds of the gain from a fully observed cycle while saving a season. The CV0 results suggest that predictions have useful portability to unobserved environments, which helps prioritize broadly resistant candidates for multi‐location yield testing. For disease resistance specifically, GEBV‐based culling shifts the distribution toward more resistant material entering advanced trials, which can lower phenotyping and plant protection costs and concentrate resources on validation rather than initial screening. Using high PVE markers as a fixed effect in the model did not change the prediction accuracy because most of their signal was already captured by the genome‐wide marker effects and the genomic relationship matrix. In shrinkage models such as GBLUP, RRBLUP, and BayesA, markers in strong LD with the major locus collectively absorb that effect, so adding the top SNP as a fixed term contributes little new information. In addition, the apparent large effects were estimated with sampling error and can vary across environments. Treating them as fixed and constant reduces the shrinkage on an uncertain effect and can lower robustness, especially with a modest training set. Uneven GBS coverage also means a top SNP is often a proxy rather than the causal variant; its LD with the causal site can change across folds and sites, which limits transfer of a fixed effect. Finally, the proportion of variance explained by the trait was largely polygenic, so overall predictive ability was driven by the many small effects that the genomic models already model well. Under these conditions, models that allow heterogeneous shrinkage across markers, such as BayesA, recover major effects without requiring explicit fixed covariates, which explains the lack of gain when we forced them as fixed terms. For a study conducted for beta‐glucan concentration in oats, using markers as a fixed effect in the model did not result in the drastic improvement of the result; that is, it increased from 0.38 to 0.39 for one scenario and 0.71 to 0.70 in another scenario (Bazzer et al., 2025).

These results underscore the capacity of parametric and non‐parametric models to generalize well even in the absence of phenotypic information for target genotypes. Collectively, these findings validate the application of GS for improving complex traits such as Crust and Crust_Per in oats and demonstrate that traditional linear models, particularly BayesA and RRBLUP, remain highly competitive against more complex machine learning algorithms. The consistent underperformance of GBR suggests that while machine learning holds promise, its implementation must be carefully optimized for specific traits and data structures.

CONCLUSION

This study used genome‐wide association mapping and GP to dissect the genetic architecture of CR resistance in a diverse panel of southern United States winter and facultative oats. Through multi‐environment phenotyping and high‐density SNP genotyping, we identified 13 loci significantly associated with CR resistance, several of which co‐localized with known QTLs or harbored candidate resistance genes such as LRR, NBS‐LRR, and serine/threonine kinases. Notably, regions on chromosomes 3D and 2A demonstrated stable effects across environments, highlighting their potential utility in MAS. Complementing these findings, GP analyses revealed that parametric models, especially BayesA and RRBLUP, consistently achieved high predictive accuracies across diverse CV scenarios, outperforming more complex machine learning approaches. The integration of GWAS and GS underscores the feasibility of accelerating resistance breeding in oats by identifying durable loci and predicting performance in untested genotypes and environments. These findings provide a critical foundation for deploying genomic tools in breeding pipelines to enhance CR resistance and advance the development of resilient oat cultivars.
