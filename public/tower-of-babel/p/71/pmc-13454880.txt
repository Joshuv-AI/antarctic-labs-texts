Integrated DNA methylome and transcriptome signatures of lung CD4+ T-cells in sarcoidosis
Authors: Camille M. Moore, Shu-Yi Liao, Li Li, Jonathan Cardwell, Arunangshu Sarkar, Kristyn MacPhail, Margaret M. Mroz, Christina Riley, Nancy W. Lin, Elizabeth Davidson, Cheyret Wood, Clara I. Restrepo, Ivana V. Yang, Lisa A. Maier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RationaleActivated pulmonary CD4+ T lymphocytes are a critical part of sarcoidosis pathogenesis. To identify molecular changes associated with sarcoidosis risk primarily and progression secondarily, we profiled and integrated DNA methylome and transcriptome data in lung CD4+ T-cells.MethodsBronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) CD4+ T-cells were isolated from 29 sarcoidosis (16 progressive sarcoidosis (SarcP) and 13 non-progressive sarcoidosis (SarcNP)) cases and 19 healthy controls. mRNA was sequenced and DNA methylation profiled on Illumina HumanMethylationEPIC BeadChips. Linear models were fitted to test for effect of diagnosis, adjusting for age and sex. Methylation models were adjusted for unknown batch effects, inflation and bias. We used Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent cOmponents (DIABLO) to integrate methylome and transcriptome datasets and validated targets using single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq).Measurements and main resultsUsing transcriptome-wide significance, differentially expressed (DE) transcripts were identified in participants with sarcoidosis (39), SarcNP (23) and SarcP (7), compared with controls. Using genome-wide significance, 37 CpGs and 47 regions were identified as differentially methylated (DM) in sarcoidosis participants, compared with controls. Focusing on DE genes, we identified DM CpGs in 11 genes associated with sarcoidosis, 3 with SarcNP and 7 with SarcP. In DIABLO, the first latent component distinguished cases and controls based on transcripts and CpGs associated with T-cell signalling, T-cell differentiation, apoptosis and epigenetic regulation. Among the DE/DM transcripts identified by integrative omic analysis and validated by single-cell RNA-seq is interleukin 18 receptor accessory protein (IL18RAP).ConclusionsIntegrative methylome–transcriptome profiling of lung CD4+ T-cells identified coordinated immune and epigenetic alterations, including IL18RAP, as candidate biomarkers.

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous disease affecting approximately 45–300 per 100 000 individuals in the USA [1], with a rising mortality rate [2]. While exact aetiologies are unknown, exposure to an as yet unidentified antigen(s) appears to result in a T-helper cell (Th) 1/Th17.1 immune response, activation of CD4+ T-cells and granulomatous inflammation [3]. Perpetuation of granulomatous inflammation may result in sarcoidosis progression. Immune dysregulation seems to predispose to disease, probably with imbalances in T-cell signalling [4], regulatory T-cell (Treg) number and function [5–7], and cytokines. Genetic risk is strongly linked to sarcoidosis disease risk [8–10] and disease severity [11–14] in the human leukocyte antigen and other regions; however, no genetic risk fully explains susceptibility or clinical variability, thereby implicating environment and epigenetics. Epigenetic alterations in combination with genetic susceptibility may provide a crucial link between exposure and disease risk as they impact gene expression and disease pathogenesis [15].

Previously, we identified DNA methylation and gene expression changes associated with disease risk and progression in mixed bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) cells [16, 17]. However, epigenetic changes and their impact on expression are cell specific. Studies of DNA methylation in lupus [18], rheumatoid arthritis [19] and juvenile idiopathic arthritis [20] have revealed new genes and regulatory elements associated with disease and severity in CD4+ T-cells. In this study, we 1) performed immunophenotyping of BAL cells, 2) determined DNA methylation and transcription associated with sarcoidosis risk and progression in CD4+ BAL T-cells, 3) conducted multi-omic integration with DNA methylation to identify key regulatory genes and pathways associated with sarcoidosis and, secondarily, progression and 4) used our single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset to validate target genes.

Methods

We include here a brief overview of the methods used; additional details can be found in the supplementary material.

Study population

Sarcoidosis cases (n=29) and non-diseased controls (n=19) were enrolled at National Jewish Health. Sarcoidosis subjects met American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society criteria [21] for tissue biopsy confirmation of diagnosis and had not been treated for 3 months before and at enrolment. Medical records were reviewed for organ involvement, pulmonary function tests (PFTs), chest imaging and immunosuppressive treatment at time of BAL (confirming no treatment) and up to 2 years after BAL to evaluate progressive sarcoidosis (SarcP)/non-progressive sarcoidosis (SarcNP). SarcP was defined based on any of the following up to 2 years after BAL: PFT showing ≥10% decline in forced vital capacity or forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) or ≥15% decline in the diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO); worsening chest imaging (chest X-ray or chest computed tomography), per interpreting radiologist; and/or initiation of systemic immunosuppression. SarcNP was defined by all of the following at 2 years after BAL: PFTs that did not meet SarcP criteria, stable/improved chest imaging and no immunosuppressants. A flow chart outlines participants with flow cytometry, RNA-seq and methylation data (supplementary figure 1).

Bronchoscopy and cell processing

Bronchoscopy with BAL was performed as previously described [22, 23]. CD4+ lymphocytes were isolated from BAL utilising EasySep™ Human CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). BAL cells were stained and analysed using multiparameter flow cytometry to acquire cell counts and the relative frequencies and numbers of Th cells (Th1, Th17, Th17.1 and Th2) and Tregs, based on previously published methods [24], which are described in the supplementary material (for the gating strategy, see supplementary figure 2A).

Transcriptome and DNA methylome data collection

CD4+ DNA and RNA were extracted using the QIAGEN AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini Kit. We obtained high-quality DNA to collect Illumina Infinium HumanMethylationEPIC BeadChips DNA methylation data on 27 sarcoidosis cases and 18 controls and high-quality RNA to collect RNA-seq data on 19 sarcoidosis cases and 14 controls. Data are available in Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE285769).

Transcriptome data analysis

After quality control and filtering, 16 482 genes and 30 samples were available for analysis. We compared gene expression between sarcoidosis participants (n=17) and healthy controls (n=13), adjusting for sex, using negative binomial regression models in DESeq2 [25]. Before differential testing, we removed lowly expressed genes without at least one count per million in ≥13 samples, resulting in 15 365 genes for testing. Similar models were used to compare those with SarcP (n=11), those with SarcNP (n=6) and controls, with adjustment for age included in SarcP/SarcNP comparisons. A similar gene filtering strategy was used, excluding lowly expressed genes without at least one count per million in ≥6 samples, yielding 16 482 genes included in testing. The Benjamini–Hochberg method [26] was used to control the false discovery rate (FDR).

DNA methylation data analysis

After quality control and filtering, 710 615 CpG probes and 45 samples were analysed. To remove known and unknown batch effects, we used Probabilistic Estimation of Expression Residuals (PEER) [27]. A scree plot indicated that the first two PEER factors accounted for major sources of variation in DNA methylation (supplementary figure 3). Principal components regression analysis determined that PEER factor 1 was highly associated with disease (supplementary figure 3); we thus adjusted only for PEER factor 2 in statistical models. Linear models were fitted to each CpG testing for an effect of sarcoidosis diagnosis or progression, adjusting for sex, race/ethnicity and PEER factor 2. We adjusted for age in the comparison of SarcP with SarcNP. Null test statistic distributions were derived for each analysis using the bacon R package to reduce inflation [28] (supplementary figure 3). Differentially methylated CpGs (DMCs) were deemed significant based on FDR-adjusted p-values. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified using comb-p [29] with a window size of 300 bases on linear model results. DMRs were deemed significant based on regional Stouffer–Liptak–Kechris correction (slk) followed by Šidák adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Integration of gene expression and DNA methylation

15 participants with sarcoidosis (9 SarcP and 6 SarcNP) and 12 healthy controls had both methylation and gene expression data and were included in multi-omics analyses. We included expression of the 15 000 most variable genes and methylation at the 15 000 most variable CpG sites in multi-omics analyses. Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent cOmponents (DIABLO) [30] was used for multi-omics analysis. We used a design matrix with values of 0.5 for off-diagonal elements to achieve balance between prediction and strength of correlation between features in the network. Details of the multi-omic model are described in the supplementary material.

Single-cell RNA-seq validation

We looked up expression patterns of select genes in our recently collected and analysed single-cell RNA-seq dataset [31].

Results

Enrolled subjects

We enrolled 29 sarcoidosis cases (16 SarcP and 13 SarcNP) and 19 healthy controls. Demographics at BAL collection (table 1) showed no significant differences in race or ethnicity. Sarcoidosis cases were significantly older than controls. Those with SarcP were more likely to be male than SarcNP cases and controls, and presented with lower FEV1 and DLCO values than those with SarcNP. Immunophenotyping was available for all subjects, whereas DNA methylation data were obtained on 27 cases and 18 controls; three subjects had insufficient DNA quantity. RNA-seq data were available for 17 cases and 13 controls; 15 RNA samples had low-quality RNA data, or a low quantity of it, and three were removed as outliers (see Methods section). 15 cases and 12 controls had both methylation and gene expression data (supplementary figure 1).

Immunophenotyping of BAL cells by flow cytometry

A significant increase in the CD4+ T-cells Th17 and Th17.1 and a decrease in Tregs were noted in sarcoidosis BAL cells, compared with control BAL cells (figure 1). CD4+ T-cells were significantly increased in SarcNP cases than in controls and Th17.1 cells were significantly increased in SarcP cases than in controls (supplementary figure 2B).

Transcriptional changes associated with sarcoidosis

Comparing sarcoidosis (n=17) with controls (n=13) identified 39 differentially expressed (DE) transcripts at FDR-adjusted p<0.05 (supplementary table 1). Upregulated transcripts were enriched in pathways related to apoptosis while downregulated transcripts were enriched in pathways related to interleukin (IL)-4 regulation of apoptosis, adaptive and innate immunity and type I interferon signalling, among others (supplementary table 2).

The top significant upregulated genes in sarcoidosis (figure 2a) were related to immune function and T-cell differentiation, such as IL17RE, receptor for IL-17C (4.9-fold change), IL18 receptor accessory protein (IL18RAP, 3.9-fold change) and IL12RB2 (3.6-fold change) [32]. Other upregulated genes include those without immediately clear, but with potentially novel, function in sarcoidosis, such as chimerin 1 (CHN1), encoding a GTPase-activating protein for Ras-related p21-rac and phorbol ester receptor (5.8-fold change), and transient receptor potential cation channel, subfamily C, member 3 (TRPC3), encoding a protein that forms channels regulating calcium (3.9-fold change). Due to differences in sex, age and smoking status between cases and controls, we performed sensitivity analyses stratified by sex, age and smoking status; while power to detect significant differences was reduced with lower sample sizes, fold changes comparing sarcoidosis and controls were concordant with primary analyses (supplementary figure 4).

Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) of the extended DE gene list (500 genes ranked by p-value) identified inhibition of type I interferon signalling, activation of viral infection signalling and CD28 co-stimulatory molecule, an interesting observation considering that CD4+CD28− cells have been identified as expanded in the lungs and blood of those with sarcoidosis [33] (figure 2b). More novel observations include activation of atypical chemokine receptor D6 (ACKR2), mitochondrial polyribonucleotide nucleotidyltransferase 1 (PNPT1) and transcription factor ETV5. Canonical pathway analysis identified 38 enriched pathways at Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p<0.05 and z-score >1.5, 33 of which are activated and five inhibited (figure 2c, supplementary table 3). Many identified pathways are known in sarcoidosis, such as pathogen-induced cytokine storm signalling, IL1 signalling, tumour necrosis factor signalling and Toll-like receptor-related pathways. We also identified novel associations, such as MITF-M-dependent gene expression, found in cardiac [34], but not pulmonary, sarcoidosis; metabolism of noncoding RNAs; and non-canonical NF-ĸB signalling.

Transcriptional changes associated with disease progression

At adjusted p<0.05, we identified four DE transcripts in SarcP versus SarcNP, nine DE transcripts in SarcP versus controls and 25 DE transcripts in SarcNP versus controls (supplementary table 4). The most significant DE gene in SarcP was IL17RE (upregulated 6.5-fold compared with controls). Genes differentiating SarcNP included activator protein (AP)-1 transcription factor subunit JUNB, suppressor of cytokine signalling SOCS3 and other JAK/STAT signalling pathway members, which were downregulated in SarcNP versus controls. JUNB was also downregulated in SarcNP, compared with SarcP. Genes significantly upregulated in SarcNP included IL12RB2 (4.4-fold change compared with controls).

Differential methylation analysis

Genome-wide DNA methylation analysis of 27 cases and 18 controls identified 37 DMCs (bacon- and FDR-adjusted p<0.05; supplementary table 5A) and 47 DMRs (region- and multiple-comparison-adjusted p<0.05; supplementary table 5B) associated with sarcoidosis. The genomic inflation factor was 0.914, indicating slight deflation rather than inflation of p-values. Focusing on DE genes, DNA methylation analysis identified multiple (≥3) significant DMCs (bacon-adjusted p<0.05; supplementary table 5C) in 11 out of 39 genes in sarcoidosis, compared with controls, including CHN1, IL12RB2, IL17RE and TRPC3. 12 out of 25 DE genes in those with SarcNP had multiple DMCs, including SOCS3, and three of the DE genes in those with SarcP had multiple DMCs, including IL17RE (bacon-adjusted p<0.05; supplementary table 6).

Integration of DNA methylation and gene expression

The integration analysis focused on 15 cases and 12 controls with DNA methylation and gene expression data. The DIABLO model was built using the 15 000 most variable features from expression and DNA methylation data. Using 0.5 design value to equally balance sarcoidosis prediction and correlation of DNA methylation and gene expression features, component 1 provided some distinction between sarcoidosis and controls while component 2 had a small amount of separation between SarcP and SarcNP (supplementary figure 5). However, this model provided high correlation of DNA and gene expression datasets (0.96). Component 1 of the DIABLO model (figure 3a) captured CpGs and transcripts associated with T-cell signalling, including cg00520410 in c-Maf-inducing protein (CMIP), NCR3 transcript that interacts with CD3-zeta/CD247 and ZNF683 or Hobbit transcription factor that regulates cellular response to ionomycin. They were also associated with T-cell differentiation: cg20891481 in FOXP1, and IL12RB2. Apoptosis CpG and transcripts were noted in component 1: cg03963977 in BCL2 apoptosis regulator; cg14025589 in serine/threonine kinase MELK; FAS ligand FASLG; DAPK2 in the serine/threonine kinase family; negative regulation of apoptotic process NUGGC involved in cellular response to lipopolysaccharide; and two granzymes, GZMB and GZMH. Importantly, IL18RAP was downregulated with a hypermethylated CpG (cg03453574) in people with sarcoidosis, compared with controls, and there was a strong correlation between DNA methylation and gene expression across sample groups (figure 3b).

Component 2 of this model captured additional genes related to T-cell immunity: TCF7, a member of the T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer-binding factor family of high-mobility group box transcriptional activators; CCR7; cg20817580 IKZF3 or Ikaros transcription factor involved in regulation of lymphocyte development; lymphocyte-specific interferon response factor IRF4; cg22395761 in CD27; and cg11963452 in FOSL2, an AP-1 transcription factor subunit.

Features related to epigenetic regulation were also captured by component 1, such as DPF3, a transcriptional regulator that binds acetylated histones and is part of the BAF chromatin remodelling complex, and component 2 with cg19324997 in histone deacetylase HDAC4.

Using 0.1 design value to explore phenotype prediction, component 1 provided prominent separation between people with sarcoidosis and controls with decreased correlation between DNA methylation and gene expression (supplementary figure 5). This model retained several CpG features identified in the 0.5 design, including cg03453574 (IL18RAP), cg20891481 (FOXP1), cg19324997(HDAC4) and cg02387618 (TCF7). However, this design had only five transcripts, none of which have an obvious role in sarcoidosis. Changing the design to 0.75 to focus on correlation of methylation and expression features, the separation of samples in component 1 and component 2 appeared nearly identical (supplementary figure 5), with a similar list of features to that of design 0.5, supporting the use of 0.5 design for biological interpretation of multi-omic analyses.

Single-cell RNA-seq validation

We used our recently assembled BAL cell single-cell dataset consisting of independent sarcoidosis (SarcNP, n=8; SarcP, n=8) and controls (n=14) to validate gene expression [31]. Of the 39 CD4+ T-cell genes that were significantly DE in sarcoidosis versus controls, three were validated in our single-cell data (upregulated in both datasets): CHN1 (7.4-fold upregulation; p=6.4×10−4), IL18RAP (3.9-fold upregulation, p=3.2×10−4; figure 3c) and NCR3 (2.8-fold upregulation; p=1.8×10−4).

Discussion

Our study identified disease risk and progression-associated changes in T-cell immunophenotypes, DNA methylation and gene expression; to our knowledge, it is the first study to focus on omic integration in a sarcoidosis BAL CD4+ T-cell subset. Specifically, sarcoidosis BAL CD4+ T-cells have higher proportions of Th17 and Th17.1 but a lower proportion of Tregs than the cells of healthy controls. They also displayed DE (n=39) transcripts, and about 30% of these (n=11) demonstrated significant DMCs, including CHN1, IL12RB2, IL17RE and TRPC3. We also identified DE transcripts associated with SarcP, including IL17RE. Using DIABLO multi-omic integration, we found that CpGs and transcripts (latent component 1) distinguished sarcoidosis from controls, capturing changes in T-cell signalling, T-cell differentiation and apoptosis. Latent component 2 provided some separation between SarcP and SarcNP in CpGs and transcripts related to T-cell immunity. The IL18RAP gene was identified as a novel target in DE, differentially methylated (DM) and DIABLO analyses. We validated transcriptional changes in IL18RAP, CHN1 and NCR3 in an independent cohort of sarcoidosis cases and controls in BAL single-cell RNA-seq data.

Our findings of increased Th17.1 in sarcoidosis are consistent with those of previous studies [24, 35]. Th17.1 cells appear to be a subgroup of classical Th1 cells that secrete interferon gamma and play a central role in sarcoidosis. We did not observe differences in Th17.1 between SarcP and SarcNP cases, similar to Broos et al. [35], but we did observe a significant difference in SarcP cases compared with controls. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have shown that BAL Th17.1 increases with sarcoidosis radiologic stage [36], with one study finding that higher levels of Th17.1 are associated with favourable sarcoidosis progression [37]. A novel finding of our study is differential methylation of IL17RE in sarcoidosis, a receptor for one of six members of the IL-17 family. Unlike IL-17A, which is increased in granuloma and circulating memory T-cells in sarcoidosis [38], IL-17C and its receptor IL17RE have not been studied. The IL-17C/IL-17RE axis is important in the development of T-cell-dependent imiquimod-induced psoriatic dermatitis [39], although its role in other inflammatory diseases, including sarcoidosis, is unknown.

Our study identified a decrease in Tregs in those with sarcoidosis, compared with controls, consistent with previous BAL and peripheral blood studies [5, 36, 40, 41]. Tregs regulate and suppress Th cells; they present an immune paradox in sarcoidosis, as they only partially inhibit production of tumour necrosis factor-α while simultaneously demonstrating robust antiproliferative effects on T-cells, resulting in an inability to effectively regulate local inflammation [6].

In addition to finding pathways associated with adaptive and innate immunity, we found that pathways of apoptosis and programmed cell death (YWHAQ, PSMD5, DAPK2, PSME1, GZMB, FASLG) and IL-4 regulation of apoptosis (VCAN, ITGAM, RSAD2, RIPK2, F13A1 ID3, TLR2) were DE in sarcoidosis, compared with controls. We also found apoptosis-related pathways, such as BCL2 apoptosis regulator, serine/threonine kinase MELK, FAS ligand FASLG, DAPK2 in the serine/threonine kinase family and two granzymes (GZMB and GZMH – markers of cytotoxicity or cell lysis), in our DIABLO integrative analysis of gene expression and methylation data. Apoptosis resistance has been observed in a study that examined BAL lymphocytes, along with a non-apoptotic morphology in sarcoidosis [42]. Apoptosis is a key mechanism in resolving inflammation. The inability of cells to undergo apoptosis might play a role in granuloma development, although other studies have suggested that enhanced apoptosis may reduce the number of Tregs [43]. Although apoptosis-related pathways were enriched in our analysis, this finding alone does not indicate defective apoptosis, and a link between apoptosis and granuloma formation in sarcoidosis remains speculative. These findings emphasise the importance of evaluating the complex interplay of differential methylation/differential expression and cell interactions in cell or animal models of sarcoidosis in future studies.

Our integrative analysis also identified IL18RAP as upregulated with canonical hypomethylated CpG (cg03453574) in those with sarcoidosis, compared with controls. We validated this novel finding using single-cell RNA-seq. The product of this gene is an accessory subunit of the heterodimeric receptor for IL-18, a proinflammatory cytokine-stimulating cell-mediated immunity, which augments IL-18-binding capacity of the IL18 receptor. A defect in IL18RAP (or IL-18Beta) phosphorylation impairs natural killer cell function in systemic-onset juvenile idiopathic arthritis [44] and was proposed as a biomarker for rheumatoid arthritis treatment response [45]. Moreover, a polymorphism in IL18RAP is a shared risk locus for Crohn's disease and coeliac disease [46]. Its role in sarcoidosis is still to be determined.

While we studied CD4+ BAL cells, data increasingly show that findings from one organ or tissue type may have implications for other organs in sarcoidosis [47]. We previously showed that transcriptomic and, to a lesser degree, proteomic signals from blood cells, BAL cells or fluid, lung tissue, microdissected lung granulomas, lymph nodes or an in vitro granuloma model shared many pathways, including those in T-cell activation, antigen presentation, mechanistic target of rapamycin signalling, IL-4 signalling and apoptotic pathways; we found some of these signals and pathways in our current study [47]. While we found that Th17.1 cells were increased with TBX21 as the most upstream activated transcription factor in sarcoidosis CD4+ BAL cells, similar findings were noted when comparing pulmonary with cardiac sarcoidosis single-cell sequencing data [48]; like in our study, Daoud et al. found a reduction in autophagy and cytokine signalling in cardiac and pulmonary sarcoidosis. Some differential expression was present only in BAL cells and not cardiac cells, such as the increased apoptosis genes that we found in our study. Other studies have found similar results, with prominence of TBET-positive Th17.1 cells with high TBX21 gene expression in skin granulomas, and GZMB and GZMH, markers usually present on cytotoxic T-cells that we found in our analysis [49, 50]. Shared pathways were found across skin and lung granulomas, fibrotic lung tissue and ocular tissues, including pathways we found in this study, such as autophagy, apoptosis genes and IL-12, IL-4 and IL-13 signalling [49]. Interestingly, IL18RAP gene expression was upregulated in sarcoidosis skin granulomas and plasma levels reduced with treatment [50], suggesting it might serve as a marker of treatment response.

Our study used a systems biology approach integrating differential expression/differential methylation in sarcoidosis lung CD4+ T-cells to identify several key genes/pathways associated with sarcoidosis. Findings in those with SarcP were limited, potentially due to smaller sample sizes, yet are consistent with those of previous studies. This may suggest the need for additional approaches to define disease progression phenotypes. Our integrative analysis of DE/DM data highlighted genes showing correlations between expression, methylation and disease risk, including IL18RAP. However, given that our single-cell validation was restricted to gene expression and did not assess DNA methylation, the role of IL18RAP remains preliminary and requires further investigation. Functional validation in cell and animal models remains an important future direction for research. Although histone modifications were not assessed in this study, post-translational histone marks (e.g., acetylation and methylation) are established modulators of CD4+ T-cell differentiation and function – acting through chromatin remodelling and transcriptional regulation in disorders such as asthma and allergies [51]. Investigating these histone modifications in future work could uncover how multiple epigenetic layers interact with differential expression/differential methylation in sarcoidosis pathogenesis. We acknowledge the limitation that the control group was younger, which may influence differential methylation. In addition, the relatively small sample size limits power, model stability and generalisability; thus, validation in larger independent cohorts will be essential to confirm these findings.
