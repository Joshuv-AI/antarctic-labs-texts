Immunological features of acquired pure red cell aplasia: Specific human leucocyte antigen alleles, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 mutations and a unique T‐cell receptor beta motif
Authors: Naruaki Yamashita, Toru Kawakami, Shuji Matsuzawa, Shotaro Miyairi, Masae Maruyama, Fumihiro Kawakami, Shunsuke Kojima, Ryu Yanagisawa, Yumiko Higuchi, Hideki Makishima, Hideyuki Nakazawa, Fumihiro Ishida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryT‐cell abnormalities have been implicated in the pathogenesis of acquired pure red cell aplasia (PRCA), particularly in its major subtypes such as idiopathic PRCA, thymoma‐associated PRCA and large granular lymphocytic leukaemia (LGLL)–associated PRCA, and the precise details remain unclear. Furthermore, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) mutations are frequently detected in PRCA patients, but their association with cellular immune abnormalities is not well understood. In order to elucidate the immunogenetic backgrounds of PRCA, we conducted human leucocyte antigen (HLA) typing in 39 PRCA patients. The results showed a significantly higher allele frequency of HLA‐B*44:03:01, ‐C*14:03, ‐DRB1*13:02, compared to HLA database. Additionally, analysis of inferred HLA haplotypes revealed significantly increased frequencies of two haplotypes: HLA‐A*24:02‐C*07:02‐B*07:02‐DRB1*01:01 and ‐A*33:03‐C*14:03‐B*44:03:01‐DRB1*13:02. Clonotypic analyses of T‐cell receptor beta (TCRβ) chain revealed that 80% of the PRCA cases possessed mono‐ or oligoclonally expanded T cells. Particularly among those with STAT3 mutations, the ‘QGXG’ motif in 15 AA sequences of TCRβ complementarity‐determining regions 3 (CDR3) regions was specifically recognized. These findings suggest that a specific immunogenetic background defined by particular HLA alleles, the expansion of somewhat limited T‐cell clones and STAT3‐mutated T cells are involved in the pathogenesis of chronic acquired PRCA.Keywords: HLA, pure red cell aplasia, STAT3, T‐cell receptor

INTRODUCTION

Acquired pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) is a haematological disorder characterized by anaemia, reticulocytopenia and a significant decrease in erythroid precursor cells in the bone marrow. It has been considered that immune dysregulations including T‐cell–mediated cellular abnormalities and/or autoantibody‐dependent immune mechanisms contribute to impaired erythropoiesis. 1 , 2 Idiopathic PRCA, thymoma‐associated PRCA and large granular lymphocytic leukaemia (LGLL)–associated PRCA are three major subtypes of PRCA, particularly based on T‐cell–mediated pathogenesis. 3 , 4 , 5 Skewed T‐cell receptor variable beta (TCR Vβ) repertoire, clonal expansions of T cells and somatic mutations of certain genes have been shown especially in CD8+ T cells in PRCA. 6 In vitro erythropoiesis of PRCA patients was suppressed by cytotoxic T cells through major histocompatibility complex class I antigen recognition. 7 , 8 , 9 However, the roles of human leucocyte antigen (HLA) have not been investigated except for cases of anti‐recombinant human erythropoietin (anti‐rHuEpo)‐associated PRCA. 10 , 11 , 12 Furthermore, the relationship between clonal T‐cell changes and gene mutations in acquired PRCA has not been fully explored.

This study aims to elucidate the immunogenetic mechanisms of acquired PRCA through analysis of HLA alleles and comprehensive studies of TCRβ chain clonotypes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

Thirty‐nine patients with acquired PRCA were recruited for this study. The diagnostic criteria for acquired PRCA were applied based on previously reported guidelines (Table S1). PRCA subtypes, clinical information and laboratory data were collected from medical records. Target sequencing analysis was performed as previously reported on 37 cases 13 (Figure S1). The remaining two cases were examined for signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) Y640F and D661Y mutations using allele‐specific polymerase chain reactions (AsPCRs). 14 , 15 This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shinshu University School of Medicine (approval number 723) and each participating centre. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

HLA typing

HLA typing was performed using the polymerase chain reaction‐sequence specific oligonucleotide (PCR‐SSO) method. Analysis was conducted using the WAKFlow HLA typing Kit (Wakunaga, Hiroshima, Japan) and the Luminex 100 system (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The primer sequences of the WAKFlow HLA typing Kit were specifically designed to identify alleles with an incidence frequency of 0.1% or higher reported in the Japanese population. 16

HLA haplotypes inference

HLA haplotypes for HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐C and ‐DRB1 were inferred from the four loci using the Asian/Pacific Islander dataset of Easy‐HLA's HLA‐2‐Haplo (https://easyhla.univ‐nantes.fr/prediction/prediction.php), a web application specialized in HLA haplotype inference using probabilistic inference models, compared with publicly available reference population data containing over 600 000 haplotypes. 17

Computation of HLA evolutionary divergence

HLA evolutionary divergence (HED) scores between the two HLA alleles within each patient, as proposed by Pierini and Lenz, were used as a method to quantify the evolutionary diversity of HLA class I genotypes. 18 The computation method considered the physicochemical properties of amino acids between two HLA alleles, utilizing the Grantham distance to indicate qualitative differences. The calculation of HED scores from Grantham distances was performed using an integrated web tool (https://hladiv.net/). 19 HLA class I HED scores for HLA‐A, ‐B and ‐C were computed due to the involvement of HLA class I in immunological mechanisms with CD8+ T cells, and comparisons were made among PRCA subtypes, STAT3 gene mutational states and treatment responses.

TCRβ chain clonotype analysis

To further investigate the T‐cell immune mechanisms in PRCA, a comprehensive clonotype analysis of the TCRβ chain variable region was conducted. A total of 14 patients, comprising 9 treatment‐naïve and 5 treated cases, were selected for TCRβ chain clonotype analysis based on sample availability. Among them, 10 cases showed skewing towards specific TCR Vβ chains by flow cytometry (FCM) analysis, as previously described. 6 Demographic characteristics and FCM analysis results are shown in Table S2. Sequences of TCR variable region including complimentary‐determining region (CDR) 3 were determined using a method from Repertoire Genesis (Ibaraki, Osaka, Japan). Briefly, total Ribonucleic acid isolated from PBMCs was reverse‐transcribed to complementary Deoxyribonucleic acid and amplified using a non‐biased gene amplification method with Adaptor‐Ligation PCR (AL‐PCR). After AL‐PCR, index sequences were added to PCR products using Nextera XT Index Kit v2 setA (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and sequencing was performed on Illumina Miseq (2 × 300 bp). Subsequently, a bioinformatics analysis was performed using repertoire analysis software provided by Repertoire Genesis. 20 Monoclonal pattern was defined as a pattern where T‐cell receptor beta variable (TRBV) region usage frequency was ≥20% limited to one region. Oligoclonal pattern was defined as a pattern where TRBV region usage frequency was ≥20% observed in two or more regions. Polyclonal patterns were defined as patterns other than monoclonal and oligoclonal patterns (Figure S2).

Statistical analysis

Fisher's exact test was used for comparative analysis of categorical variables, and two‐sided t‐test or Mann–Whitney U‐test was used for continuous variables. For comparisons among three or more groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For HLA typing data, statistical analysis was performed in comparison with Japanese Society for Histocompatibility and Immunogenetics' data (https://www.jmdp.or.jp/pdf/medical/familydoctor/cordinate/jshi_hla.pdf). Detailed information on healthy individuals registered in the HLA data bank is provided as donor registration criteria for the Japan Marrow Donor Program (https://www.jmdp.or.jp/reg/requirement/). For comparisons with the HLA database, Bonferroni correction was applied, and a Bonferroni‐corrected p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. EZR software program was used for statistical analysis, 21 and plots were generated using Python 3.9.7, numpy 2.0.2, pandas 2.2.3, matplotlib 3.9.4 and seaborn 0.13.2 (https://www.python.org/).

RESULTS

Demographics of patients with acquired PRCA

The clinical background of 39 patients with acquired PRCA is shown in Table 1. Main PRCA subtypes included idiopathic PRCA, thymoma‐associated PRCA and T‐LGLL‐associated PRCA. There were no significant differences in age, sex, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, haemoglobin concentration and platelet count among these subtypes. However, lymphocyte count was significantly higher in LGLL‐associated PRCA patients than in patients with other subtypes (p = 0.0036). STAT3 gene mutations were found in 13 patients (33.3%), with a median variant allele frequency (VAF) of 2.6% (range: 1.1–60.7) (Table S3).

HLA alleles and HED scores

When compared with the Japanese Society for Histocompatibility and Immunogenetics (JSHI) data, the frequencies of certain HLA alleles differed significantly in the PRCA group (Table S4). HLA‐B*44:03:01 (PRCA 20.5% vs. JSHI 6.7%, corrected‐p = 0.0038), HLA‐C*14:03 (PRCA 20.5% vs. JSHI 6.6%, corrected‐p = 0.0030) and HLA‐DRB1*13:02 (PRCA 20.5% vs. JSHI 6.4%, corrected‐p = 0.0020) were significantly more frequent in PRCA patients (Table 2). Furthermore, subgroup analyses comparing each PRCA subtype with the JSHI data revealed subtype‐specific HLA alleles that were significantly enriched in idiopathic PRCA and LGLL‐associated PRCA (Table S4). Additionally, all three cases of autoimmune disease‐associated PRCA were negative for the HLA alleles; therefore, these three HLA alleles showed strong correlation in the remaining 36 patients with major three PRCA subtypes including idiopathic, thymoma‐associated and LGLL‐associated PRCA (Table S5). We also investigated the impact of HLA class I HED scores on disease characteristics (Table S6). No significant differences were observed in HLA class I HED scores in relation to treatment response (Figure S3A) or PRCA subtypes (Figure S3B). In contrast, STAT3‐mutated PRCA showed significantly higher HLA‐B HED scores than STAT3 wild‐type PRCA (p = 0.046) (Figure S3C).

HLA haplotypes in PRCA

The results of HLA haplotype prediction from the four HLA allele loci in PRCA patients are shown in Figure 1. Compared with the general Japanese population (Table S7), the inferred frequencies of A*24:02‐C*07:02‐B*07:02‐DRB1*01:01 haplotype (15.4% vs. 3.8%, corrected‐p = 0.0018) and A*33:03‐C*14:03‐B*44:03:01‐DRB1*13:02 haplotype (14.1% vs. 4.5%, corrected‐p = 0.037) were significantly increased.

The usage of TRBV–TRBJ pairing in PRCA

Among the 14 patients with TCRβ clonotypes analysed, the median number of in‐frame reads was 98 827 (range: 37 508–167 028), and the median number of unique reads was 9254 (range: 4166–19 982). Compared to the diversity scores of bulk T cells in peripheral blood of healthy individuals, shown in a previous report with this approach 20 (Method S1), the median inverse Simpson's index score was 12 (range: 4–498) in PRCA patients, which was lower than in healthy individuals. Similarly, the median Shannon–Weaver Index H′ of 5.21 (range: 2.94–8.51) was also lower compared to healthy individuals. TRBV–T‐cell receptor beta joining regions (TRBJ) pairing and TRBV usage patterns according to clonal pattern are shown in Figure 2. PRCA patients exhibited heterogeneous TRBV–TRBJ region usage patterns, corresponding to mono‐, oligo‐ and polyclonal T‐cell expansions (Figure 2A–C; Table S8; Figure S4). Among the TRBV regions, TRBV9 showed the highest usage (11.9%) (Figure S4C). TRBV9 was predominant in the monoclonal pattern (42.9%), whereas TRBV28 and TRBV5‐1 were frequently utilized in the oligoclonal pattern (28.6%) (Figure 2D,E). Following the analytic method of Bhattacharya et al., 22 we focused on clones with a clone size of ≥5% and categorized them into three groups: 5%–10%, 10%–20% and ≥20% (Figure 3). According to PRCA subtypes, all patients with idiopathic PRCA (n = 4, 100%) and all patients with LGLL‐associated PRCA (n = 7, 100%) harboured clones with a clone size of ≥5% and a total of 24 clonal populations were identified.

Characteristics of CDR3 sequences of TCRβ chain in relation with STAT3 mutations

Across PRCA patients (n = 14), the length distribution of the TCRβ chain complementarity‐determining regions 3 (CDR3) region showed a prominent peak at 15 amino acid (AA) residues, with an additional peak at 13 AA residues, resulting in a bimodal distribution (Figure 4A). Furthermore, STAT3‐mutated cases showed a trimodal distribution with peaks at 15, 13 and 18 AA residues (Figure 4B). Interestingly, glycine (G) at positions 111 and 113 tended to be enriched (Figure 4C). STAT3‐mutated cases showed an increase with the ‘QGXG’ motif at positions 110, 111 and 113. This motif was found in 50% and 0% of cases with STAT3‐mutated and ‐non‐mutated PRCA, respectively, suggesting a strong trend towards enrichment in STAT3‐mutated PRCA (p = 0.0549) (Figure 4D,E). In three patients exhibiting this ‘QGXG’ motif (Table S9), the STAT3 mutations were D661Y in two and Y640F in one respectively. All three patients with this motif had the PRCA subtype of CD8+TCRαβ+LGLL. Two of these (Case 23 and 29) were elderly females carrying STAT3 D661Y mutation, both refractory to immunosuppressive therapy. Notably, in these two cases, the ‘QGXG’ motif was shared by clonally expanded T‐cell populations. In contrast, while the same motif was detected in one additional case, it was observed only at a low frequency within the non‐expanded population.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we identified a significantly increased frequency of HLA‐B*44:03:01, ‐C*14:03 and DRB1*13:02 alleles in Japanese patients with PRCA. A substantial proportion of patients carried one or more of these alleles or the corresponding inferred haplotypes, which were markedly enriched in PRCA compared with their frequencies in the general Japanese population.

Previous studies identified HLA associations in anti‐rHuEpo antibody‐associated PRCA; 10 , 11 , 12 however, immunogenetic backgrounds in other subtypes of PRCA remain unclear. Our findings, for the first time, demonstrated an association between specific HLA alleles and inferred haplotypes in immune‐mediated acquired PRCA. A recent study suggested that inborn errors of immunity (IEI) may underlie the pathogenic background of LGLL, including cases complicated with PRCA. 23 Taken together, our findings support the notion that shared immunogenetic backgrounds, such as those related to HLA or IEI, may predispose individuals to the development of PRCA.

Furthermore, comprehensive analysis of the TCRβ chain demonstrated a less diverse T‐cell repertoire characterized by mono‐ or oligoclonal T‐cell expansion in many cases, even outside of LGLL‐associated PRCA. A notable ‘QGXG’ motif was detected in the CDR3 region of the TCRβ chain, particularly among patients with STAT3‐mutated PRCA. These findings suggest that STAT3 mutations, particularly STAT3 D661Y, may drive selective expansion of T‐cell clones bearing specific TCRβ amino acid motifs. Further studies are warranted to elucidate how STAT3 mutations contribute to the expansion of ‘QGXG’ motif‐positive T cells.

In aplastic anaemia, cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) inhibit haematopoietic progenitor cells in an HLA‐B*40:02‐restricted 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 and HLA‐DR15 is associated with responsiveness to immunosuppressive therapy. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 HLA‐B*44:03:01, ‐C*14:03 and DRB1*13:02 haplotypes have been previously associated with immune‐medicated conditions such as Stevens–Johnson syndrome and rheumatoid arthritis as well as viral infections including cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Human immunodeficiency virus‐1. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 Notably, these two haplotypes were the third and second most frequent haplotypes in Japanese individuals, respectively. The A*33:03—C*14:03—B*44:03:01—DRB1*13:02 haplotype has been mostly reported in East Asia, particularly in Japan and Korea. 37 Consequently, the association found in our study suggests a regionally heterogeneous immunogenetic contribution to PRCA susceptibility, as these HLA types are considerably less prevalent in other regions.

A meta‐analysis of TCRβ chain Vβ region usage in LGLL reported that Vβ13.1 usage accounted for 12.5% of all cases. 38 Given the frequent detection of clonal T cells not only in LGLL‐associated PRCA but also in other PRCA subtypes, there is a need to further explore the similarities and distinctions between LGLL and non‐LGLL PRCA, particularly when small clonal T‐cell populations are observed in idiopathic or thymoma‐associated PRCA. Although our study analysed bulk T cells without differentiating between CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in TRBV usage, CD8+ T cells constitute the dominant subpopulation in all cases (Table S10). Moreover, concordant results were observed between flow cytometry‐based Vβ usage and TRBV analysis, strongly suggesting that the TRBV usage reflected CD8+ T‐cell clones in PRCA (Table S2). Notably, STAT3 VAF was substantially lower than the proportion of expanded clones and was also significantly lower in LGLL‐associated PRCA compared to those without PRCA. 13 , 39 These results imply that STAT3 mutations might develop in subclonal populations among clonally expanded cells or in different cells from dominant clones, including those with TCRβ ‘QGXG’ motif, in PRCA.

We previously identified clones harbouring the ‘QGRG’ motif in a patient with CD8+ TCRαβ+ LGLL, although the case was not included in the current cohort. 40 Validation in external cohorts, comprising datasets from independent studies of bone marrow failure syndrome, confirmed that this motif is exceptionally rare in AA. 41 , 42 , 43 Conversely, public database analysis linked the motif to CMV‐ and Epstein‐Barr virus‐specific T‐cell populations in healthy donors. 44 , 45 These findings suggest that the ‘QGXG’ motif reflects a selection driven by potentially viral antigens that is preferentially expanded in the context of STAT3 mutations. Consistent with this, STAT3‐mutated cases exhibited a trend towards higher HLA‐B HED scores, suggesting a potential for enhanced capacity for peptide presentation under chronic antigenic stimulation. Several viral antigen epitopes were predicted to bind the ‘QGXG’ motif using the Immune Epitope Database (Table S11). 46 AlphaFold 3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/) modelling suggested a high‐confidence interaction between the ‘QGXG’ motif and a CMV epitope in Case 23, visualized with PyMOL (https://pymol.org/) (Figure S5), though these computational predictions warrant experimental confirmation. Based on the present findings and previous evidence, we propose an immunopathogenic model of PRCA (Figure 5). 1 , 4 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 23 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 In this model, motif‐bearing CTLs, potentially driven by chronic antigenic stimulation and somatic genetic alterations such as STAT3 mutations, contribute to erythropoietic dysfunction. Further studies are warranted to elucidate how these T‐cell subsets mediate haematopoietic suppression in PRCA.

A schematic model of molecular pathogenesis of immune‐mediated PRCA. In individuals with an immunological background, such as specific HLA and/or inborn errors of immunity, stimuli from viruses self‐antigen may trigger cytokines release, leading to CTL activation. 1 , 4 , 23 Initially, activated CTLs proliferate polyclonally and undergo clonal expansion through cytokines stimulation and somatic gene mutations. 1 , 47 , 48 During clonal expansion, STAT3 mutations may arise in subclone populations, and these STAT3‐mutated T‐cell clones might promote the survival and persistence of major CTL clones. 49 , 50 , 51 This process potentially drives the selective expansion of clones with biased TRBV gene usage or the ‘QGXG’ motif in the CDR3β region. These changes may be a consequence of clonal selection induced by STAT3 mutations. Ultimately, it is hypothesized that through molecular mimicry between a target antigen and an erythroid precursor surface antigen, or via other immunological mechanisms, the selectively expanded CTL clones attack a BFU‐E and subsequent maturing erythroid precursors such as CFU‐E and proerythroblasts mediated by cytokines and other factors, causing haematopoietic suppression culminating in the development of PRCA. 7 , 8 , 9 , 49 , 52 , 53 , 54 The figure has been created by BioRender.com. BFU‐E, burst forming unit‐erythroid; CDR3, complementarity‐determining regions 3; CFU‐E, colony forming unit‐erythroid; CTL, cytotoxic T lymphocyte; HLA, human leucocyte antigen; PRCA, pure red cell aplasia; RBC, red blood cell; STAT3, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3; TCR, T‐cell receptor; TRBV, T‐cell receptor beta variable regions.

In conclusion, acquired PRCA is characterized by a high frequency of specific HLA alleles, biased TCRβ chain usage and, in some cases, the presence of distinct motifs within the TCRβ chain CDR3 region. STAT3 mutations appear to be associated with particular immunological features, implicating clonal T cells in the pathogenesis of PRCA.
