Severe Anaphylaxis Induced by Turkey Egg With Tolerance to Hen’s Egg and Poultry Meat: A Species-Specific Reaction
Authors: Pelin Korkmaz
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Food allergy to hen’s egg is well recognized and extensively studied; however, allergic reactions to eggs from other avian species are rarely reported.123 We describe a case of life-threatening anaphylaxis triggered by a turkey egg in a patient who tolerates hen’s eggs and poultry meat, illustrating a highly unusual form of selective avian egg allergy.

A 28-year-old male with no history of atopic disease presented after developing rapidly progressive shortness of breath, throat tightness, dysphonia, flushing, and generalized erythema approximately 20 minutes after consuming a mixed meal containing baked turkey egg. On arrival at the emergency department, he was hypotensive (70/40 mmHg) with oxygen desaturation and marked oropharyngeal edema, requiring intramuscular epinephrine followed by endotracheal intubation and one day of mechanical ventilation. The patient clearly stated that he had never consumed turkey eggs before this episode. He had previously consumed turkey meat without symptoms and continued to tolerate hen’s egg in both raw and cooked forms, as well as chicken, after the index episode.

A diagnostic evaluation showed an elevated total immunoglobulin E (IgE) of 380 IU/mL, but no evidence of asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, or mast cell disease. Prick-to-prick skin testing with fresh turkey egg white and yolk provoked immediate generalized symptoms—pruritus, flushing, dyspnea, and hypotension—requiring 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine. Wheal sizes were 40 × 32 mm (egg white) and 30 × 18 mm (yolk) (Figure). Serum-specific IgE testing demonstrated sensitization to hen’s egg white (44.8 kUA/L), egg yolk (34.6 kUA/L), and hen’s meat (6.16 kUA/L), despite his continued and unremarkable consumption of all three. In contrast, specific IgE to turkey meat was negative, and an oral food challenge with turkey meat was tolerated without symptoms.

Species-specific egg allergy has been previously reported, including cases of quail egg allergy with preserved tolerance to hen’s egg, suggesting that sensitization to non-hen avian eggs may occur independently of clinically relevant reactivity to chicken egg proteins.45 Such reports highlight that phylogenetic proximity and protein homology among avian species do not always predict clinical cross-reactivity. Experimental immunochemical studies have demonstrated that turkey egg white contains proteins that cross-react with major hen’s egg white allergens, including ovalbumin, ovomucoid, and ovotransferrin, and that these proteins are capable of binding IgE from patients with hen’s egg allergy.6

However, clinical reports specifically describing turkey egg–induced allergy or anaphylaxis are essentially absent from the indexed literature. In the present case, high serum-specific IgE levels to hen’s egg white and yolk despite daily tolerance may reflect sensitization to heat-labile or clinically irrelevant egg proteins, whereas the severe reactions to turkey egg point toward IgE binding to distinct, clinically relevant turkey egg components within this shared allergen family, resulting in a species-specific pattern of reactivity.

Despite no known consumption of turkey eggs, primary sensitization through cutaneous or environmental exposure cannot be excluded.7

A limitation of this report is that allergen component analysis could not be performed, as assays for turkey egg components are not commercially available and hen’s egg/meat and turkey meat component testing is not accessible in our clinical setting.

This case illustrates the complexity of avian egg allergy, showing that serologic sensitization does not invariably predict clinical reactivity. Management should therefore focus on documented culprit allergens to avoid unnecessary elimination of well-tolerated foods such as hen’s eggs and poultry meat.
