AaSIZ1‐Mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31 Positively Regulates Freezing Tolerance in Actinidia arguta
Authors: Linyao Liu, Lei Cao, Na Tang, Wangmei Ren, Zhili Zhu, Rong Wang, Minghao Xiang, Jinliang Sun, Xuan Zhang, Feng Zhang, Yunzhi Lin, Yuting Kong, Zhihui Jiang, Lihuan Wang, Yongsheng Liu, Cheng Zhang, Pengpeng Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeveral studies revealed that wax accumulation enhances plant tolerance to cold stress. However, the molecular regulation mechanism of wax synthesis under cold stress is still unclear in Actinidia arguta , a hardy kiwifruit species. Here, we found that cold stress significantly induces wax accumulation in Actinidia arguta leaves, which is driven by the robust upregulation of wax biosynthetic genes, particularly AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1. Overexpression of AaKCS2/6.1 in Actinidia chinensis not only elevated wax biosynthesis but also enhanced freezing tolerance. Furthermore, we identified that the R2R3‐MYB transcription factor AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 are transcriptionally activated by cold stress and directly bind to the promoters of AaKCS2/6.1 to activate their expression. Overexpression of AaMYB31s in Actinidia chinensis significantly promoted wax biosynthesis under cold stress, thus enhancing the freezing tolerance of kiwifruit. Besides, we identified that AaSIZ1, a small ubiquitin‐like modifier (SUMO) E3 ligase from Actinidia arguta , interacts with AaMYB31s to mediate the SUMOylation of AaMYB31s to enhance their protein stability. The functional validation in Actinidia chinensis further revealed that overexpression of AaSIZ1 robustly upregulated the cuticular wax biosynthesis and enhanced freezing tolerance. Importantly, our evidence suggests that the interaction between AaMYB31s and AaSIZ1 and the AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s were significantly enhanced under cold stress. Therefore, we conclude that the AaSIZ1‐AaMYB31s‐AaKCSs module positively regulates wax synthesis in response to freezing stress.

Introduction

Kiwifruit has high vitamin C content and balanced nutritional composition, and its global consumer demand is also growing (Huang et al. 2013; Richardson et al. 2018; Ma et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2021). In recent decades, kiwifruit cultivation has expanded rapidly across temperate and subtropical regions. However, low temperature is still a major abiotic constraint in its production, severely limiting the growth, yield and geographical distribution of kiwifruit (Sharma et al. 2005; Theocharis et al. 2012; Lesk et al. 2016; Sun, Qi, et al. 2020). In particular, sudden early‐winter frosts and late‐spring cold events frequently cause floral and vegetative tissue injury, leading to significant economic losses in major production areas such as northern China and northeastern Asia (Lin et al. 2021). Among Actinidia species, the branches of Actinidia deliciosa and Actinidia chinensis are highly susceptible to freezing, whereas the branches of Actinidia arguta exhibit remarkable cold tolerance, surviving temperatures as low as −30°C (Sun, Qi, et al. 2020). When the young shoots of Actinidia arguta , Actinidia eriantha, Actinidia deliciosa , Actinidia chinensis and Actinidia macrosperma were exposed to low temperature during spring, the tissue activity was severely reduced at 0°C in all species except Actinidia arguta (Jo et al. 2008). In addition, Actinidia arguta exhibits a lower tissue browning temperature in its dormant buds and shoots than Actinidia eriantha, Actinidia deliciosa and Actinidia chinensis , and no tissue browning was observed in Actinidia arguta even at −20°C (Dozier Jr. et al. 1992; Cho 2005; Jo et al. 2008). Under −20°C treatment, 14% of Actinidia eriantha, 25% of Actinidia deliciosa , and 80% of Actinidia chinensis failed to survive, whereas all individuals of Actinidia arguta survived (Chat 1995; Jo et al. 2008). Therefore, Actinidia arguta is a valuable genetic resource, which can be used to explore the molecular mechanisms of cold adaptation, and then provide strategies for breeding cold resistant kiwifruit varieties. Although there are a few studies have emerged to explore the molecular mechanism of cold resistance regulation in Actinidia arguta , including AaERF64‐AaTPPA module participated in cold acclimation of Actinidia arguta (Eh et al. 2024), AaMYC2a‐AaMYB5a‐AaLAR1 module enhanced freezing tolerance in Actinidia arguta (Sun et al. 2024), and AaCBF4 enhanced the freezing tolerance of Actinidia arguta by activating AaBAM3 to increase the content of soluble sugar (Sun et al. 2021), the molecular mechanism of cold resistance is still largely unclear.

Cold stress will trigger the accumulation of specific metabolites that mitigate cold‐induced damage and maintain membrane stability (Khan et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2023; An et al. 2024; Hou et al. 2024; Shu et al. 2024; Sun et al. 2024; Xiao et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024). For instance, soluble sugars, proline and other amino acids act as osmoprotectants, ascorbate and anthocyanins contribute to redox homeostasis, as well as modifications of lipid metabolism enhance membrane stability under chilling and freezing stress (Khan et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2023; An et al. 2024; Hou et al. 2024; Shu et al. 2024; Sun et al. 2024; Xiao et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024). Recently, studies have found that improving plant wax accumulation can enhance plant tolerance to cold stress (Chen et al. 2020; Rahman et al. 2021; Hou et al. 2024). For instance, overexpression of KCS1 or its interacting partner AKR1 enhances VLCFA biosynthesis and cold tolerance in Arabidopsis (Chen et al. 2020). In rice, OsMTACP2‐mediated wax ester biosynthesis is essential for cold tolerance at the booting stage (Hou et al. 2024). Cuticular wax, mainly composed of very‐long‐chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) and their derivatives acts as a protective barrier on the surface of plants to prevent ice crystals from penetrating into plants (Riederer and Schreiber 2001; Bernard and Joubès 2013; Yeats and Rose 2013). Recently, studies have shown that over‐accumulation of wax can significantly prevent plants from freezing, while wax defects cause plants to freeze at warmer temperatures (Rahman et al. 2021). Even though the important role of wax in cold stress has been found, the molecular regulation mechanism of wax synthesis under cold stress is still poorly understood.

Several studies have found that MYB transcription factors play important regulatory roles in wax synthesis (Raffaele et al. 2008; Seo et al. 2011; Lee and Suh 2015; Lee et al. 2016; Zhang, Zhang, et al. 2019; Xiong et al. 2020; Shi et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2025). For example, in citruses, CitMYB16 positively regulates the accumulation of cutin and wax by directly binding to the promoters of CitDCR and CitKCS2 to activate their expression (Zhu et al. 2025). In apple, MdMYB30 positively regulates wax biosynthesis by directly binding to the MdKCS1 promoter to activate its expression (Zhang, Zhang, et al. 2019). In tomato, SlMYB31 positively regulates cuticular wax biosynthesis by activating CER6 expression (Xiong et al. 2020). In Arabidopsis, AtMYB96, AtMYB94 and AtMYB31 positively regulate wax biosynthesis by directly activating key genes of wax synthase, such as KCS1/2/6 and CER3 (Seo et al. 2011; Lee et al. 2016; Shi et al. 2022). Previous studies have also found that MYB transcription factors are involved in the regulation of plant cold stress tolerance (Lee and Suh 2015; An et al. 2018, 2020; Yang et al. 2022; Zheng et al. 2023; Luo et al. 2024). In apple, MdMYB308L positively regulates cold tolerance by directly activating MdCBF2 and MdDFR (An et al. 2020). In chrysanthemum, DgMYB interacts with DgATX, promoting the specific targeting of DgPOD by DgATX, enhancing DgPOD expression and thereby reducing the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under cold stress (Luo et al. 2024). Recently, SlMYB15 in tomato has been demonstrated to positively modulate cold stress tolerance by regulating the CBF pathway, ABA signal and redox‐related genes (Zhang et al. 2020, 2022, 2025). In kiwifruit, AceMYB102 associates with AcePosF21 that transcriptionally activate AceGGP3 promoting L‐ascorbic acid (AsA) biosynthesis and enhancing cold tolerance (Liu et al. 2023). However, whether MYB of Actinidia arguta responds to cold stress by regulating wax synthesis remains unknown.

Small ubiquitin‐related modifier (SUMO) modification is a reversible posttranslational modification that affects many different biological processes by regulating the subcellular localisation, stability, protein–protein interaction and the transcriptional activity of its target proteins (Gill 2003; Terui et al. 2004; Miura, Jin, and Hasegawa 2007; Gareau and Lima 2010; Miura and Ohta 2010; Kong et al. 2020; Zheng et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021). SUMOs are attached to protein substrates through three reaction steps. Initially, SUMO E1 activates SUMO by a two‐step process of adenylation and thioester bond formation, which is then transferred to the E2 SUMO enzyme to generate E2 ~ SUMO thioesters (Verger et al. 2003; Hay 2005; Gareau and Lima 2010). Subsequently, E3 SUMO ligases, including SIZ1, PIALs and MMS21, promote activated SUMOs' specific binding to substrates by recruiting E2 ~ SUMO thioesters and substrates, or enhance the binding of SUMO to substrates by stimulating the ability of E2 enzymes to release SUMO to substrates (Verger et al. 2003; Hay 2005; Miura, Jin, Lee, et al. 2007; Gareau and Lima 2010; Tomanov et al. 2014; Augustine and Vierstra 2018). Several studies have found that SIZ1 is involved in the regulation of plant cold stress tolerance (Miura, Jin, Lee, et al. 2007; Miura and Ohta 2010; Jiang et al. 2022). In Arabidopsis, AtSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation at K393 of AtICE1 represses polyubiquitination of the protein, thus enhancing the stability of AtICE1 at low temperatures (Miura, Jin, Lee, et al. 2007). In apple, MdSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of MdMYB2 maintains its protein stability, thereby promoting the expression of MdDFR and MdANS and enhancing cold stress tolerance of apple (Jiang et al. 2022). Besides, previous studies have also found that SIZ1 is involved in the regulation of wax synthesis. In apple, MdSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of MdMYB30 inhibits its degradation through the 26S proteasome pathway, thereby positively regulating wax biosynthesis in apple (Zhang et al. 2023).

In this study, we show that AaSIZ1‐AaMYB31s‐AaKCSs module positively regulates wax synthesis under cold stress. Firstly, we found that cold stress can induce the expression of wax synthesis genes AaKCSs and wax synthesis, thereby improving the freezing resistance of Actinidia arguta . Subsequently, we found that AaMYB31s could directly bind to the promoter of AaKCSs to activate its expression. Moreover, we found that cold stress can significantly upregulate the expression of AaMYB31s, thereby enhancing the activation of AaKCSs and wax synthesis, as well as significantly improving the freezing resistance of plants. In addition, we found that AaSIZ1 interacts with AaMYB31s to mediate the SUMOylation of AaMYB31s, thereby enhancing its protein stability. More importantly, the AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s was significantly enhanced under cold stress, which would significantly enhance the activation of AaKCS genes by AaMYB31s, thereby increasing the freezing tolerance of Actinidia arguta mediated by wax synthesis.

Results

Wax Synthesis of Actinidia arguta Was Induced by Cold Stress

Actinidia arguta is a valuable cold‐tolerant fruit tree resource with extremely strong low‐temperature tolerance (Calugaru‐Spataru et al. 2014). To explore its cold resistance related genes, we analysed the transcriptome data of the cold treated Actinidia arguta cv. ‘Longcheng No. 2’ based on our group's database (Table S2). We found that there were 21 differentially expressed genes related to wax synthesis in a single haplotype, of which 16 genes were upregulated by cold induction (Figure 1A). To validate the transcriptome data, we detected the expression level of AaKCS6.1/6.2, AaKAS1.1/1.3/1.4, AaKCS4.2/4.3, AaKCS19, AaKCS2, AaKCS1, AaKCS11.1/11.2/11.3/11.4, AaKCS3.2 and AaCER3 using qPCR. We found that the expression level of AaKCS1 and AaKCS11.1/11.2/11.3/11.4 increased rapidly and peaked at 3 h, AaKCS6.1/6.2, AaKAS1.1/1.3, AaKCS4.2, AaKCS19 and AaKCS2 peaked at 12 h, and AaKAS1.4, AaKCS3.2, AaCER3 and AaKCS4.3 peaked at 24 h (Figure 1B; Figure S1). These results demonstrate that cuticular wax biosynthesis genes were induced by cold stress in Actinidia arguta . To investigate whether cold stress‐induced wax synthesis genes could enhance wax accumulation, we checked the deposition of epidermal wax crystals on the surface of Actinidia arguta leaves under cold stress using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). It was found that the amount of epidermal wax crystals on the leaves surface increased significantly with the increase of cold stress time, and the shape of the epidermal wax crystals changed gradually from granular to sheet (Figure 1C). Subsequently, we determined the total amounts of wax on leaves using gas chromatography with flame ionisation detection (GC‐FID). Consistent with deposition of epidermal wax crystals, the total wax increased rapidly within 48 h (Figure 1D), revealing that cold stress induces wax synthesis in Actinidia arguta .

Among these genes, AaKCS6.1 showed the most significant upregulation at 12 h, followed by AaKCS2 (Figure 1B; Figure S1). We thought AaKCS6.1 and AaKCS2 are the representatives that may play a critical role in mediating the cold stress response of Actinidia arguta , so these two genes were selected as the main targets for subsequent functional characterisation. Immediately, we generated AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit lines (Figure S2), and determined the total amounts of wax on leaves of AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1‐OE transgenic kiwifruit. The results showed that the total wax of AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1‐OE transgenic kiwifruit leaves were higher than that of wild‐type (WT) Actinidia chinensis under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure 2A). Subsequently, we checked the deposition of epidermal wax crystals on the surface of AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis leaves and found that the epidermal wax crystals on the leaves surface of AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis leaves were significantly more than that of WT under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure 2B). These results indicate that wax accumulation of Actinidia arguta under cold stress is indeed directly related to the upregulation of wax synthesis genes.

Recently, studies have found that wax synthesis can enhance the freezing tolerance of plants (Chen et al. 2020; Gao, Jiang, et al. 2024; Gao, Pan, et al. 2024; Hou et al. 2024). Therefore, we speculated that Actinidia arguta might enhance cold stress tolerance by enhancing wax synthesis. To prove this speculation, we analysed the freezing phenotype of AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants. The results showed that AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants exhibited a freezing tolerance phenotype (Figure 2C,D). Consistent with the phenotype, the ion leakage, MDA content, H2O2 content and O2 ·− content of AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants were significantly lower than those in WT under freezing stress, and the chlorophyll content of AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants was significantly higher than that of WT under freezing stress, whereas there was no difference before the freezing stress (Figure 2C–F). These results indicate that the increased wax synthesis due to the overexpression of AaKCS2‐OE and AaKCS6.1 genes significantly improved the tolerance of kiwifruit to freezing stress. Taken together, these results implied that cold stress‐induced wax biosynthesis enhanced the freezing resistance of kiwifruit.

AaMYB31s Directly Activate the Expression of AaKCSs

Previous studies have found that MYB transcription factors, such as AtMYB30/31/94/96, SlMYB31 and MdMYB30, are key regulators of wax synthesis (Raffaele et al. 2008; Seo et al. 2011; Lee and Suh 2015; Lee et al. 2016; Zhang, Zhang, et al. 2019; Xiong et al. 2020; Shi et al. 2022). Therefore, we wondered whether the upregulation of wax synthesis genes under cold stress was activated by MYB transcription factors in Actinidia arguta . To this end, we analysed all cold‐upregulated MYB transcription factors within a single haplotype. We identified 27 cold‐upregulated MYB transcription factor genes, of which 16 exhibited upregulation followed by downregulation, while 11 displayed irregular changes (Figure 3A). Subsequently, we conducted a phylogenetic tree comprising 16 cold‐upregulated MYB transcription factors from Actinidia arguta and all MYB transcription factors of Arabidopsis, and found that 4 MYB transcription factors are closely related to AtMYB31, which were named as AaMYB31.1 (AaLCAc16p19g22728), AaMYB31.2 (AaLCAc26p14g37699), AaMYB31.3 (AaLCAc19p22g27763) and AaMYB31.4 (AaLCAc23p04g32360) (Figure 3B). 1 MYB transcription factor is closely related to AtMYB86, and the remaining ones are clustered separately (Figure 3B). Then, we compared the protein sequences of AaMYB31s with MYB31 of other species and found that these 8 MYB31 were highly similar (Figure S3). Although the cold‐responsive AaMYB31.1, AaMYB31.2, AaMYB31.3 and AaMYB31.4 are clustered with the wax synthesis transcription factor AtMYB31 of Arabidopsis thaliana in the same branch, the self‐expansion values of AaMYB31.1/2 are > 50% in the evolutionary tree and more closely homologous to AtMYB31 than that of AaMYB31.3/4 (Figure 3B). Further expression pattern analysis under cold stress conditions showed that the expression levels of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 were significantly higher than those of AaMYB31.3 and AaMYB31.4 (Figure 3C; Figure S4), likely implying their more important roles in mediating the cold stress response of Actinidia arguta . Thus, we chose to focus on AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 for follow‐up analyses to verify their functions in response to cold stress. The subcellular localisation and tissue‐specific expression patterns results showed that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 were localised to the nucleus (Figure 3D), and the expression of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 genes was the highest expression in leaves of Actinidia arguta , followed by stems and petiole (Figure 3E).

Previous studies have reported that several MYB transcription factors, including MYB96, MYB94, MYB31 and MYB30, positively regulate wax synthesis by activating the expression of wax synthesis genes KCSs and CERs (Raffaele et al. 2008; Seo et al. 2011; Lee and Suh 2015; Shi et al. 2022). Notably, both our RNA‐seq and qRT‐PCR data demonstrated that the expression of AaMYB31s also peaked at 12 h under cold stress conditions (Figure 3A,C). Based on this temporal synchronisation in expression, we focused our subsequent research on the AaKCS genes with peak expression at 12 h under cold stress. In addition, dual‐luciferase reporter (DLR) assays assay consistently showed that AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1 genes were profoundly activated by AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 (Figure 4A,B; Figure S5). Hence, we choose AaKCS6.1 and AaKCS2 as the representatives to explore their regulation by AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2. To further examine whether AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 could directly bind to the promoters of AaKCSs and regulate their expression, we performed yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays. The results showed that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 are indeed bound to the promoters of AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1 genes (Figure 4C). According to MYB recognition sequences (A/TAACCA) (Abe et al. 2003), we truncated the promoters of AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1 into five and four short fragments and performed Y1H assays again (Figure 4C). The results showed that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 could directly bind to the ProAaKCS2‐P3, and ProAaKCS6.1‐P2, ProAaKCS6.1‐P3 and ProAaKCS6.1‐P5 promoter regions of AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1, respectively (Figure 4C). Moreover, we cloned the promoter sequence of AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1, which contains the AaMYB31s binding cis‐element, and constructed the reporter constructs minProAaKCS2‐P3::LUC, minProAaKCS2‐P4::LUC, minProAaKCS6.1‐P4::LUC and minProAaKCS6.1‐P5::LUC (Figure S6A). The results of DLR assays showed that the luminescence intensity increased significantly when minProAaKCS2‐P3::LUC or minProAaKCS6.1‐P5::LUC co‐expressed with 35S::AaMYB31.1/2 (Figure S6B–E), while there was no difference when minProAaKCS2‐P4::LUC or minProAaKCS6.1‐P4::LUC co‐expressed with 35S::AaMYB31s (Figure S6B–E). LUC activity further confirmed these results (Figure S6B–E). Besides, electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) demonstrated that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 could bind to the ProAaKCS2‐p3, ProAaKCS6.1‐p2, ProAaKCS6.1‐p3 and ProAaKCS6.1‐p5 sites on the promoter of AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1 (Figure 4D). These results indicate that AaMYB31s directly bind to the promoters of AaKCSs genes to activate their expression.

AaMYB31s Enhance Freezing Tolerance by Positively Regulating Wax Synthesis

Since AaMYB31s can directly activate the expression of AaKCSs genes, we wondered whether AaMYB31s promote wax synthesis under cold stress. We generated AaMYB31.1‐OE and AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit lines (Figure S7), and determined the total amounts of wax on their leaves. The results showed that the total wax of AaMYB31.1‐OE and AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis leaves were higher than WT Actinidia chinensis under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure 5A). Besides, we analysed the wax composition of AaMYB31.1‐OE and AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis , and found that compare to WT, the increased total wax content in the leaves of AaMYB31.1‐OE or AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis was primarily attributable to the increase in C24, C26, C28 and C30 fatty acids, C28, C30 and C32 aldehydes, C26, C28, C30 and C32 primary alcohols and C29, C31 and C33 alkanes under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure S8). Subsequently, we checked the deposition of epidermal wax crystals on the surface of AaMYB31.1‐OE and AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis leaves (Figure 5B). The SEM analysis indicated that the epidermal wax crystals on the leaves surface of AaMYB31.1‐OE and AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis leaves were significantly more than that of WT Actinidia chinensis under normal and cold stress (Figure 5B). These results indicate that AaMYB31s positively regulates wax synthesis in kiwifruit during cold stress.

To detect the expression levels of downstream AcKCSs genes in AaMYB31‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis , we initially determined the expression patterns of AcKCSs genes in Actinidia chinensis under cold stress conditions. The results showed that the expression levels of AcKCS1.1, AcKCS6.1, AcKCS12.1 and AcKCS19.1 genes increased rapidly and peaked at 6 h, and then gradually decreased (Figure S9). The expression levels of AcKCS4.1 and AcKCS7.1 were significantly reduced under cold stress, and AcKCS10.1 and AcKCS15 were irregular changes (Figure S9). Immediately, we detected the expression levels of AcKCS1.1, AcKCS6.1, AcKCS12.1 and AcKCS19.1 in AaMYB31.1‐OE and AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis leaves under normal and cold stress conditions. We found that the expressions of AcKCS1.1, AcKCS6.1, AcKCS12.1 and AcKCS19.1 genes in AaMYB31.1‐OE and AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis were significantly increased compared with WT under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure S10). Besides, we analysed the freezing phenotype of AaMYB31.1‐OE and AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants. We found that AaMYB31.1‐OE and AaMYB31.2‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants exhibited a freezing tolerance phenotype with lower ion leakage rate, MDA content, H2O2 content and O2 ·− content, and higher chlorophyll content compared to WT under freezing stress, whereas there was no difference before the freezing stress (Figure 5C–F), proving that AaMYB31s positively regulates freezing tolerance in kiwifruit. Taken together, these results indicate that AaMYB31s can enhance the freezing tolerance by promoting wax synthesis.

AaMYB31s Interact With AaSIZ1

To further explore the regulatory network of AaMYB31s regulating wax synthesis in response to cold stress in Actinidia arguta , we performed Pull‐down followed by mass spectrometry (Pull‐down MS) screening using AaMYB31.1 as the bait to identify potential interacting candidate proteins. Interestingly, we identified a small ubiquitin‐like modifier (SUMO) E3 ligase SAP AND MIZ1 DOMAIN‐CONTAINING LIGASE1 (AaLCAc26p18g38086), an interacting candidate protein that may interact with AaMYB31.1 (Supporting Information S1 and S2). This protein has significant sequence similarity with Arabidopsis AtSIZ1, which is characterised by the presence of an SAP domain, PHD finger and SP‐RING domain; hence we designated this protein as AaSIZ1 (Figures S11 and S12). We found that the expression levels of AaSIZ1 genes increased gradually and picked at 6 h, and then gradually decreased (Figure S13). As a SUMO E3 ligase, SIZ1 typically modulates the function of target proteins by adding the SUMO modification and plays an important role in various biological processes (Kurepa et al. 2003; Miura et al. 2005; Catala et al. 2007; Miura, Jin, and Hasegawa 2007; Miura, Jin, Lee, et al. 2007; Benlloch and Lois 2018). Therefore, we are particularly interested in the interaction between AaMYB31.1s and AaSIZ1. We validated the interaction between AaMYB31.1/2 and AaSIZ1 by yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assays. Since full‐length AaMYB31.1/2 and AaSIZ1 all have autoactivation activity (Figure S14A), we truncated AaSIZ1 into AaSIZ1‐N (AaSIZ1‐N, amino acids 1–664 of AaSIZ1) and AaSIZ1‐C (AaSIZ1‐C, amino acids 664–872 of AaSIZ1) (Figure S14B), and found that residues 1–664 of AaSIZ1 were not self‐activating (Figure S14C), which was consistent with previous reports on similar domains in homologous proteins (Jing et al. 2025). Then, we performed Y2H assays again using AaSIZ1‐N, and found that AaSIZ1‐N interacted with AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 (Figure 6A). In addition, a robust luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) signal was observed when AaSIZ1‐nLUC and AaMYB31.1‐cLUC or AaMYB31.2‐cLUC were co‐expressed, further validating their interaction (Figure 6B). Furthermore, an in vivo coimmunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assay showed that AaSIZ1‐HA was specifically detected in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves co‐expressing AaMYB31.1‐FLAG or AaMYB31.2‐FLAG and AaSIZ1‐HA, but not in the group of GFP‐FLAG and AaSIZ1‐HA (Figure 6C), confirming the interaction between AaMYB31.1 or AaMYB31.2 and AaSIZ1. Moreover, an in vitro pull‐down assays demonstrated that glutathione s‐transferase (GST)‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.2 bound to His‐AaSIZ1 but not to GST alone (Figure 6D). Taken together, these results demonstrate that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 interact with AaSIZ1 in vivo and in vitro.

AaSIZ1‐Mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s Enhances Their Protein Stability

Previous studies have demonstrated that SUMO E3 ligase SIZ1 interacts with target proteins and mediates their SUMOylation (Zheng et al. 2020), and our evidence supports that AaSIZ1 is a homologue of the established SUMO E3 ligase AtSIZ1 and interacts with AaMYB31s, so we asked whether AaSIZ1 functions as an E3 SUMO ligase for the SUMOylation of AaMYB31s. To test this hypothesis, we first performed an in vitro SUMOylation assay to determine whether AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 are SUMO substrates. The GST‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.1 protein were incubated with E1 (His‐SAE1b and His‐SAE2), E2 (His‐SCE1), His‐SUMO1GG (the mature form of SUMO1, with the C‐terminal double‐Gly motif exposed) or His‐SUMO1AA (a conjugation‐defective SUMO1 mutant, with the double‐Gly motif mutated to double‐Ala), and immunoprecipitation analysis was performed. Anti‐GST and anti‐SUMO1 antibodies detected SUMOylated GST‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.2 bands only in the reaction containing His‐SUMO1GG, but not in His‐SUMO1AA (Figure 7A), suggesting that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 could be SUMOylated in vitro. To confirm whether AaMYB31s are SUMOylated in vivo, Myc‐AaMYB31.1 or Myc‐AaMYB31.2, FLAG‐SUMO1GG or FLAG‐SUMO1AA were transiently expressed in Actinidia arguta mesophyll protoplasts isolated from WT leaves, and subsequently, a Co‐IP assay was performed. The anti‐FLAG antibody detected SUMOylated AaMYB31.1 or AaMYB31.2 bands when Myc‐AaMYB31.1 or Myc‐AaMYB31.2 was co‐expressed with FLAG‐SUMO1GG, but not with FLAG‐SUMO1AA (Figure 7B). We also tested the SUMOylated of AaMYB31s in vivo via co‐expression of AaMYB31.1‐GFP or AaMYB31.2‐GFP and FLAG‐SUMO1GG or FLAG‐SUMO1AA in N. benthamiana leaves. Immunoblotting analysis showed that SUMOylated AaMYB31.1‐GFP or AaMYB31.2‐GFP bands were detected when co‐expressed with FLAG‐SUMO1GG, but not when co‐expressed with FLAG‐SUMO1AA (Figure 7C). These results indicate that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 are SUMOylated in vivo. To validate whether AaMYB31s are SUMOylated in planta, we extracted total proteins from 35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP or 35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP transgenic plants and performed immunoprecipitated with an anti‐GFP antibody. As shown in Figure 7D, the anti‐SUMO1 antibody detected the SUMOylated AaMYB31.1 or AaMYB31.2 bands in 35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP or 35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP transgenic plants but not in WT plants. Taken together, these results demonstrate that AaMYB31s are SUMO substrate.

Given the covalent SUMO modification of target proteins occurs at lysine residues, we identified three and one potential SUMOylation consensus motif, ψ‐K‐X‐E/D (ψ, hydrophobic amino acid; K, acceptor lysine; X, any residue; D, aspartic acid; E, glutamic acid) on AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2, respectively (Figure S3), using GPS‐SUMO2.0 software (Zhao et al. 2014). To explore the role of the potential SUMO sites of AaMYB31s, SUMOylation assays were conducted in vivo. The AaMYB31.1‐GFP or AaMYB31.2‐GFP, AaMYB31.1K76R‐GFP or AaMYB31.1K173R‐GFP or AaMYB31.1K281R‐GFP or AaMYB31.2 K76R‐GFP and FLAG‐SUMO1GG or FLAG‐SUMO1AA were co‐expressed in N. benthamiana leaves, and Co‐IP assay was performed using anti‐FLAG antibody. The results showed that the K76R substitution strongly affected the SUMO conjugation of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 (Figure 7E), but the K173R and K281R substitution did not affect the SUMO conjugation of AaMYB31.1 (Figure S15). To validate that K76 as the specific residue for AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 SUMOylation in planta, we generated AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐GFP and AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐GFP transgenic Actinidia chinensis ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit (Figure S16). The in vivo SUMOylation assay revealed that the K76R substitution impaired the SUMO conjugation of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 in the transgenic Actinidia chinensis (Figure 7F). These results indicate that the K76 residue is the target for AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 SUMOylation.

To investigate whether AaSIZ1 mediates the SUMOylation of AaMYB31s, we performed an in vitro SUMOylation analysis. The GST‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.2 protein were incubated with E1, E2, SUMO1 and His‐AaSIZ1, and immunoprecipitation analysis was performed. The SUMO‐conjugated AaMYB31.1 or AaMYB31.2 was not detected using either anti‐GST or anti‐SUMO antibodies in reactions lacking AaMYB31.1 or AaMYB31.2, but it was clearly observed when AaMYB31.1 or AaMYB31.2 was included. Notably, AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 was more SUMOylated in vitro in the presence of AaSIZ1 (Figure 7G). Then, we transiently coexpressed AaMYB31.1‐GFP or AaMYB31.2‐GFP, HA‐AaSIZ1 and FLAG‐SUMO1 in N. benthamiana leaves and performed a Co‐IP assay. Consistently, the presence of AaSIZ1 enhanced the SUMOylation of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 (Figure 7H). Furthermore, we extracted total proteins from WT and 35S::AaSIZ1‐GFP transgenic plants and incubated with GST‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.2. We found that more AaMYB31.1 or AaMYB31.2 was SUMOylated in the 35S::AaSIZ1‐GFP transgenic plants compared with WT plants (Figure 7I). Besides, we transiently expressed Myc‐AaMYB31.1 or Myc‐AaMYB31.2 and FLAG‐SUMO1 were in Actinidia chinensis mesophyll protoplasts isolated from WT or AaSIZ1‐OE leaves, and subsequently, followed by Co‐IP assay. The results showed that AaMYB31.1 or AaMYB31.2 were more SUMOylated in 35S::AaSIZ1‐GFP protoplasts than WT protoplasts (Figure 7J). Taken together, these results suggest that AaSIZ1 mediates the SUMOylation of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2.

To investigate whether AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation affects the activation of AaKCSs genes by AaMYB31s, DLR assays were performed using ProAaKCS2::LUC or ProAaKCS6.1::LUC reporter vectors co‐transfected with either empty vector or 35S::AaMYB31.1, 35S::AaMYB31.1K76R or 35S::AaMYB31.2, 35S::AaMYB31.2K76R effector vector, respectively. The results of DLR assays showed that the K76R mutation of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 does not influence the transcriptional activation activity of AaMYB31s (Figure S17A,B). To further study whether the AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s affects the subcellular localisation of AaMYB31s, we coexpressed AaMYB31s‐GFP or AaMYB31sK76R‐GFP with AaSIZ1‐RFP in N. benthamiana leaves. The results showed that neither AaMYB31s‐GFP nor AaMYB31sK76R‐GFP coexpressed with AaSIZ1‐CFP did not change their nuclear localisation (Figure S17C).

Several studies have found that SUMOylation modification could enhance the stability of target proteins (Park et al. 2011; Jing et al. 2025). Therefore, we compared the protein stability of AaMYB31s and AaMYB31sK76R using AaMYB31s‐GFP and AaMYB31s K76R ‐GFP transgenic Actinidia chinensis ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit. The results showed that the degradation of AaMYB31sK76R‐GFP was significantly accelerated compared with AaMYB31s‐GFP protein (Figure 8A,B), suggesting that the SUMOylation of AaMYB31s affected their protein stability. To further investigate whether the stabilities of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 are altered by AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation, we transiently co‐expressed Myc‐AaMYB31.1 or Myc‐AaMYB31.2, AaSIZ1‐GFP or 35S::GFP, and HA‐RFP in N. benthamiana leaves and extracted the total proteins for degradation testing. The results showed that the AaMYB31.1‐Myc and AaMYB31.2‐Myc proteins were degraded more slowly in the AaSIZ1‐GFP group than in the GFP group, suggesting that the AaMYB31s were more stable in the presence of AaSIZ1 (Figure 8C,D). However, treatment with MG132 significantly decreased the degradation rate of Myc‐AaMYB31.1 and Myc‐AaMYB31.2 in both groups, and this inhibitory effect was further enhanced in the AaSIZ1 group, suggesting that AaSIZ1 likely functions by inhibiting the degradation of AaMYB31s by the 26S proteasome pathway. Subsequently, we incubated GST‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.2 protein with total proteins extracted from WT and 35S::AaSIZ1‐GFP transgenic plants for cell‐free protein degradation assays. The results showed that GST‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.2 protein was more stable when incubated with the protein extracts of 35S::AaSIZ1‐GFP transgenic plants, while GST‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.2 protein degraded faster in WT (Figure 8E,F). In addition, when MG132 was present, the degradation rate of GST‐AaMYB31.1 and GST‐AaMYB31.2 incubated with both crude proteins was significantly slowed down, especially when coincubated with the total protein of 35S::AaSIZ1‐GFP transgenic plants, indicating that AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation increased the protein stability of AaMYB31s and blocked their degradation via the 26S proteasome pathway (Figure 8E,F). Taken together, these results indicate that AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s enhances their protein stability.

SUMOylation Of AaMYB31s Was Enhanced Under Cold Stress

Previous study demonstrated that SIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of ICE1 can improve the stability of ICE1, and this modification is enhanced under cold stress, thus promoting the expression of CBF3/DREB1A and improving the cold stress tolerance (Miura, Jin, Lee, et al. 2007). To investigate whether AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s was enhanced under cold stress, we detected the SUMOylation levels of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 in 35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP and 35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP transgenic Actinidia chinensis at 22°C and 4°C. The results showed that cold treatment enhanced SUMOylation levels of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 (Figure 9A). Subsequently, the ubiquitination of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 in 35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP and 35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP transgenic Actinidia chinensis with or without cold treatment were examined. The results showed that the levels of poly‐ubiquitinated AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 were decreased in both transgenic plants at 4°C compared to 22°C (Figure 9B), indicating that cold treatment could reduce the ubiquitination modification level of AaMYB31s. Next, we assessed the stability of AaMYB31s in 35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP and 35S::AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐GFP plants at 4°C and 22°C. The results revealed that the AaMYB31.1‐GFP and AaMYB31.2‐GFP protein degraded slower at 4°C than at 22°C (Figure 9C,D). In addition, AaMYB31.1K76R‐GFP and AaMYB31.2K76R‐GFP proteins were highly unstable and degraded significantly faster than AaMYB31.1‐GFP and AaMYB31.2‐GFP proteins at both 4°C and 22°C, and their degradation rate is not affected by temperature (Figure 9C,D). These findings demonstrate that the SUMOylation of AaMYB31s was significantly enhanced under cold stress, while ubiquitination was significantly weakened. Moreover, SUMOylation modification could effectively slow down the degradation rate of AaMYB31s protein, thereby improving the cold stress tolerance of Actinidia arguta . Moreover, we conducted a Co‐IP assay in Nicotiana benthamiana to explore the effects of cold stress on the interaction between AaSIZ1 and AaMYB31s in vivo. The results showed that when the same amounts of AaMYB31s were detected by IP assay, a higher abundance of AaSIZ1 could be captured at 4°C. This observation suggests that cold stress could enhance the interaction between AaSIZ1 and AaMYB31s (Figure 9E,F). Taken together, these results indicate that the increased SUMOylation of AaMYB31 mediated by AaSIZ1 under cold stress was due to the enhanced interaction between AaSIZ1 and AaMYB31 induced by cold stress.

AaSIZ1 Positively Regulates Freezing Tolerance by Promoting Wax Synthesis

Since AaSIZ1 enhances protein stability of AaMYB31s by mediating their SUMOylation under cold stress, we asked whether AaSIZ1 regulates freezing stress tolerance by mediating wax synthesis in Actinidia arguta . To this end, we generated AaS1Z1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis lines (Figure S18) and analysed their wax synthesis and freezing phenotypes. The results showed that the total wax levels were higher in AaS1Z1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants than in WT Actinidia chinensis under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure 10A). Besides, we analysed the wax composition of AaS1Z1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis and found that compared to WT, the increased total wax content in the leaves of AaS1Z1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis was primarily attributable to the decreases in C28 and C30 fatty acids, C28, C30 and C32 aldehydes, C26, C28, C30 and C32 primary alcohols and C29, C31 and C33 alkanes under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure S19). Consistent with the total wax, the deposition of epidermal wax crystals on the leaves surface of AaS1Z1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants was significantly increased compared with that of WT Actinidia chinensis under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure 10B), revealing that AaS1Z1 positively regulates wax synthesis under cold stress conditions. The freezing phenotypic analysis showed that AaS1Z1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants exhibited an enhanced freezing tolerance phenotype, with lower ion leakage rate, MDA content, H2O2 content and O2 ·− content, and higher chlorophyll content compared to WT under freezing stress, whereas there was no difference before the freezing stress (Figure 10D). Taken together, these results indicate that AaS1Z1 positively regulates wax synthesis under cold stress, thereby enhancing freezing stress tolerance.

We found that the total wax content of AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐OE3 or AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐OE1 was higher than that of WT, but significantly lower than that of AaMYB31.1‐OE5 or AaMYB31.2‐OE3 transgenic Actinidia chinensis under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure S20A). Consistent with the total wax, the deposition of epidermal wax crystals on the leaves surface of AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐OE3 or AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐OE1 transgenic Actinidia chinensis was more than that of WT, but significantly less than that of AaMYB31.1‐OE5 or AaMYB31.2‐OE3 transgenic Actinidia chinensis under normal and cold stress conditions (Figure S20B). Besides, the freezing phenotypic analysis showed that AaMYB31s K76R ‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants exhibited a freezing tolerance phenotype compared with WT, but the freezing tolerance of AaMYB31s K76R ‐OE was weaker than that of the AaMYB31.1‐OE5 or AaMYB31.2‐OE3 transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants (Figure S20C–E), suggesting that the K76R mutation diminished the freezing tolerance of AaMYB31s‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants by reducing was synthesis. Taken together, these results support that AaS1Z1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s positively regulates freezing tolerance by modulating wax synthesis.

Discussion

Several studies have found that cuticular wax plays an important role in plant abiotic stress tolerance. For example, MYB94/96, DREB26/ERF12, MYS1/2, EsWAX1, SPL9, MdCER2, TaMYB31 and PtoMYB142 can promote the biosynthesis of cuticular wax, thus enhancing the drought tolerance (Zhu et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2016; Zhao et al. 2018; Zhong et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022; Song et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2024; Urano et al. 2024). In recent years, researchers began to explore the role of wax in the response of plants to cold stress. For example, cold stress can induce the expression of wax related synthesis genes, such as TsKCS1, TsKCS2, TsKCS3, TsCER3 and CsKCS2 and CsKCS9, and increase the wax content, thereby enhancing the cold tolerance of Thellungiella salsuginea and tea plants (He et al. 2019; Zhu et al. 2022). In Dianthus spiculifolius, the high‐wax mutant exhibited significantly enhanced cold tolerance compared with the WT, while removing surface wax eliminated this difference, indicating that cuticular wax plays a crucial role in conferring cold tolerance to Dianthus spiculifolius (Zhang, Song, et al. 2022; Zhang, Wang, et al. 2022). OsCYCBL1 can promote the expression of wax biosynthesis‐related genes to facilitate the biosynthesis of wax, thereby improving the cold stress tolerance of rice (Ma et al. 2025). These studies imply that wax should play an important role in cold stress tolerance of plants.

Actinidia arguta , among Actinidia species, has excellent cold resistance (Sun, Fang, et al. 2020; Sun, Qi, et al. 2020; Sun et al. 2021, 2024), but knowledge relevant to molecular mechanisms is still limited. Here, we found that many wax synthesis related genes were upregulated by cold stress induction, especially AaKCSs (Figure 1A,B; Figure S1). KCSs are the rate‐limiting enzyme for wax synthesis and determine the substrate specificity for fatty acid elongation in FAE complexes (Lewandowska et al. 2020). Our evidence proved that the wax synthesis of Actinidia arguta significantly increased under cold stress (Figure 1C,D). Recently, several studies have implied that the biosynthesis of wax can improve cold stress tolerance of plants (Chen et al. 2020; Tian et al. 2022; Gao, Jiang, et al. 2024; Gao, Pan, et al. 2024; Hou et al. 2024). For example, AKR1 enhances cold tolerance by interacting with KCS1 to elevate wax biosynthesis (Chen et al. 2020). OsMTACP2 positively regulates the expression of OsCER1 and DsWSL2, thereby mediating biosynthesis of wax ester in response to cold stress (Hou et al. 2024). ZmHSF21 positively regulates the cold tolerance of maize by directly promoting the expression of GPATs, FADs and SADs, and inhibiting the expression of KCS29 (Gao, Jiang, et al. 2024; Gao, Pan, et al. 2024). Due to the stable genetic transformation system of Actinidia arguta has not yet been established at present, we can only rely on Actinidia chinensis's stable genetic transformation system to verify the tested genes' functions. Hence, we carried out genetic transformation in Actinidia chinensis and found that overexpression of AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1 confers cold tolerance to Actinidia chinensis by increasing wax deposition, suggesting that Actinidia arguta does enhance cold tolerance by promoting wax synthesis (Figure 2). Notably, obvious differences in cuticular wax accumulation and freezing tolerance were observed between Actinidia arguta and Actinidia chinensis , which further supports the functional association between wax synthesis and freezing tolerance in kiwifruit (Figure S21). Under normal and cold stress conditions, the basal wax crystal content of Actinidia arguta leaves was significantly higher than that of Actinidia chinensis (Figure S21A). The accumulation rate and coverage of wax crystals and in Actinidia arguta were significantly higher than that in Actinidia chinensis under cold stress (Figure S21A,B). Actinidia arguta thus exhibited excellent freezing tolerance, while Actinidia chinensis showed severe freezing damage (Figure S21C,D). Therefore, we speculate that the difference of freezing phenotype of Actinidia arguta and Actinidia chinensis may originate from the difference in the regulation of cuticular wax biosynthesis. Actinidia arguta has evolved a more efficient regulatory system to drive wax crystal biosynthesis and accumulation under cold stress, the key molecular and physiological basis for its superior freezing tolerance over Actinidia chinensis .

Previous studies have found that temperature variation induced alterations in the total wax amount and the reorganisation of wax crystal structures, and cold treatment significantly increased the total wax content in the leaves of B. napus , Arabidopsis, and Thellungiella salsuginea (Whitecross and Armstrong 1972; Shepherd and Wynne 2006; He et al. 2019; Ni et al. 2024). It is well known that the plant cuticle is a multifunctional extracellular membrane composed of cuticular waxes and cutin (Kolattukudy 1980) and is widely distributed over the plant surface, covering organs such as shoots, fruits, flowers and leaves (Kolattukudy 1980; Riederer and Schreiber 2001; Leide et al. 2007). Besides, cuticular waxes mainly consist of hydrophobic very‐long‐chain fatty acids (VLCFAs, ≥C20) and their derivatives (Riederer and Schreiber 2001; Bernard and Joubès 2013; Yeats and Rose 2013). These studies show that wax is an important physical protective layer for plants. Notably, recent studies have found that wax‐deficient cer3‐6 froze at warmer temperatures, while wax‐overaccumulated dewax mutants displayed freezing exotherms at colder temperatures compared to WT, indicating that cuticular wax deposition can facilitate avoidance of ice formation (Rahman et al. 2021). These findings imply that we can enhance the cold stress tolerance of fruit trees through bioengineering to improve wax synthesis.

Previous studies have found that Actinidia arguta possesses a thick cuticle (Hallett and Sutherland 2005), and our evidence also supports that Actinidia arguta enhances cold tolerance by promoting wax synthesis (Figures 1 and 2). However, the regulatory molecular mechanisms of cuticular wax biosynthesis in Actinidia arguta are largely unclear. Here, we found that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 could directly bind to the promoters of AaKCSs to activate their expression, thereby enhancing wax synthesis (Figures 4, 5A,B, S6). Previous studies have found important regulatory functions of MYB transcription factors in wax synthesis (Raffaele et al. 2008; Seo et al. 2011; Lee and Suh 2015; Lee et al. 2016; Zhang, Zhang, et al. 2019; Xiong et al. 2020; Shi et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2025). For instance, MdMYB30 directly binds to the MdKCS1 promoter to activate its expression and promote wax biosynthesis (Zhang, Zhang, et al. 2019). SlMYB31 regulates cuticular wax biosynthesis by activating CER6 expression in tomato (Xiong et al. 2020). Although these studies have largely explored the important role of MYB transcription factors in the regulation of plant wax synthesis, it is still unclear how MYB transcription factors regulate wax synthesis to respond to and combat cold stress. Here, our data showed AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 enhance freezing tolerance by positively regulating wax synthesis (Figure 5), and AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 were upregulated under cold stress; AaMYB31s may be important regulators to regulate wax synthesis in Actinidia arguta under cold stress.

Besides, we found that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 can be modified by SUMOylation (Figure 7A–D). Previous studies identified that the covalent SUMO modification of target proteins occurs at lysine residues of the SUMOylation consensus motif, ψ‐K‐X‐E/D (Seeler and Dejean 2003; Hendriks and Vertegaal 2016; Augustine and Vierstra 2018). We found that the single substitution of K76‐to‐R in AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 blocked their SUMOylation, indicating that the K76 residue is the target site for SUMO conjugation of AaMYB31s (Figure 7E,F). In plants, SUMOylation is rapidly triggered under diverse stress conditions, such as heat, phosphate deficiency, drought and cold stress (Kurepa et al. 2003; Miura et al. 2005; Miura, Jin, Lee, et al. 2007; Benlloch and Lois 2018). For instance, Arabidopsis exhibits a significant increase in SUMO1 and SUMO2 conjugation to protein substrates under heat stress (Kurepa et al. 2003). Our evidence showed that SUMOylation of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 was significantly enhanced under cold stress (Figure 9), suggesting that SUMOylation is involved in the regulation of wax synthesis in Actinidia arguta under cold stress.

SUMOylation, as a reversible post‐translational modification, affects many aspects of substrate proteins, including localisation, function, activity, stability and protein–protein interactions, which in turn regulate a variety of physiological processes (Verger et al. 2003; Miura and Ohta 2010; Li et al. 2022; Sahin et al. 2022). However, SUMO E3 ligases are usually required to mediate the SUMOylation of specific substrate proteins (Park et al. 2011; Zhou et al. 2019). Currently, SIZ1, MMS21 and PIALs are well‐characterised SUMO E3 ligases (Miura, Jin, Lee, et al. 2007; Tomanov et al. 2014; Augustine and Vierstra 2018). Our data show that AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 interact with AaSIZ1 (Figure 6). Several studies have revealed that SIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation can maintain the stability of substrate proteins and the strength of their protein–protein interactions. For instance, SUMOylation of MYB75 by SIZ1 increases MYB75 stability, which is essential for anthocyanin accumulation (Zheng et al. 2020). SIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of ROS1 promotes its stability and positively regulates active DNA demethylation (Kong et al. 2020). MdSIZ1 enhanced the conjugating enzyme activity of MdSCE1 to form a MdSCE1‐MdSIZ1‐MdARF8 complex, thereby facilitating SUMO modification (Zhang et al. 2021). Our evidence suggests that AaSIZ1 mediates the SUMOylation of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 at K76 (Figure 7). In addition, we found that the SUMOylation of AaMYB31s mediated by AaSIZ1 enhanced their protein stability (Figure 8C–F). Previous studies have explored the involvement of SIZ1 in the regulation of cold stress and wax synthesis. SIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of ICE1 activates the expression of CBF3/DREB1A, thereby enhancing chilling and freezing tolerance in Arabidopsis (Miura, Jin, Lee, et al. 2007). Besides, MdSIZ1 mediates the SUMOylation and accumulation of MdMYB30 by inhibiting its degradation through the 26S proteasome pathway, thus regulating biosynthesis of apple cuticular wax (Zhang et al. 2023). However, how SIZ1 regulates cold stress tolerance by mediating wax synthesis remains unclear. Here, our data show that AaSIZ1 positively regulates freezing tolerance by promoting wax biosynthesis (Figure 10). This is because it can enhance the SUMOylation of AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 under cold stress (Figure 9). In turn, AaMYB31.1 and AaMYB31.2 can significantly activate KCSs and enhance wax synthesis.

Based on the data in this work and previous, we propose a working model that AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s enhances freezing tolerance by improving wax synthesis (Figure 11). Under normal conditions, AaMYB31s were maintained at low expression levels, and AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s is also at a low level. In this case, only low levels of AaKCSs are activated by AaMYB31s, thereby limiting wax synthesis to promote energy conservation and optimise plant growth and development. When exposed to cold stress, the expression levels of AaMYB31s and AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s were significantly enhanced under cold stress, thus enhancing the transcriptional activation of AaKCSs. The significant upregulation of AaKCSs promoted wax synthesis, thereby improving plant freezing stress tolerance of Actinidia arguta .

Proposed a working model that AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31 regulates cuticular wax synthesis of Actinidia arguta to improve freezing tolerance. Under normal temperature conditions, low‐level expression of AaMYB31s and AaSIZ1 leads to low AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s, so that less AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1 are activated by AaMYB31s, which was sufficient to maintain normal plant growth and development. Under cold stress, the expression levels of AaMYB31s and AaSIZ1, as well as the interaction of SIZ1 and MYB31 were significantly enhanced under cold stress. Thus, AaSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation of AaMYB31s was significantly increased, which enhanced the transcriptional activation of AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1. The significant upregulation of AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1 promoted wax synthesis, thereby improving plant freezing stress tolerance of Actinidia arguta .

Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

In this study, plants of Actinidia chinensis ‘Hongyang’ and Actinidia arguta cv. ‘Longcheng No. 2’ were selected after 3 months tissue culture and then the tissue culture seedlings were transferred to the rooting medium. The rooted Kiwifruit ( Actinidia chinensis and Actinidia arguta ) plants were transferred to a mixture of nutrient soil and vermiculite (2:1, v/v) and cultivated under the same conditions. All plants were grown at 22°C under long‐day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark cycles), with an illumination intensity of 150 μmol m−2 s−1.

Plasmid Construction and Genetic Transformation

The coding regions of AaKCS2, AaKCS6.1, AaMYB31.1, AaMYB31.2, AaSIZ1, AaMYB31.1 K76R , AaMYB31.1 K173R , AaMYB31.1 K281R and AaMYB31.2 K76R were cloned into the pCAMBIA‐2300‐35S::GFP plasmid to generate 35S::AaKCS6.1‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP, 35S::AaSIZ1‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.1K76R‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.1K173R‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.1K281R‐GFP and 35S::AaMYB31.2K76R‐GFP constructs. Transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants were generated using the Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation method. The hypocotyl with leaf and petiole from tissue cultured ‘Hongyang’, were immersed in a bacterial solution of K599 containing target genes for 12–15 min. This procedure follows the method previously described by Li et al. (2024). Transgenic plants were obtained including AaKCS2‐OE (35S::AaKCS2‐GFP), AaKCS6.1‐OE (35S::AaKCS6.1‐GFP), AaMYB31.1‐OE (35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP), AaMYB31.2‐OE (35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP), AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐OE (35S::AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐GFP), AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐OE (35S::AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐GFP) and AaSIZ1‐OE (35S::AaSIZ1‐GFP).

Cold Treatment

For kiwifruit plants, freezing stress was performed as previously reported with minor modifications (Yang et al. 2022, 2023; Mei et al. 2023). Seven‐week‐old wild‐type and transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants were initially placed at 4°C for 48 h. The freezing procedure starts at 0°C, and the temperature decreases by 2°C per hour until the −4°C is reached, and then it is maintained for 2 h. After freezing treatment, Actinidia chinensis plants were transferred to 4°C for 12 h recovery. Subsequently, the plants were cultured at 22°C for 2 days and then phenotypic images were obtained. Three independent biological replicates were conducted, each consisting of 10 seedlings from each line in every replicate.

Determination of Physiological Indexes

Ion leakage assays were performed as described by Wang et al. (2019) and Mei et al. (2023). Specifically, Actinidia chinensis leaves were discs with a diameter of 1 cm punched out and collected and placed into 15 mL tubes containing 10 mL of deionised water, and the tubes were shaken at 22°C for 30 min, during which the electrical conductivity (EC) was measured as S1. Subsequently, the samples were boiled for 40–50 min and then shaken at 22°C for an additional 30 min. After this step, EC was measured again as S2. Ion leakage was calculated as (S1–S0)/(S2–S0). S0 represents the conductivity of deionised water.

The content of MDA, total chlorophyll content, H2O2 content, and O2 ·− content in the leaves of Actinidia chinensis plants was determined as described in the kit (Nanjing jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China; Beijing solarbio science technology Co. Ltd). Three independent biological replicates were conducted each.

Subcellular Localisation

The coding sequences of AaMYB31.1, AaMYB31.1 K76R , AaMYB31.2, and AaMYB31.2 K76R were cloned into pCAMBIA‐2300‐35S::GFP. Additionally, nuclear localisation signals (NLS) were inserted into pCAMBIA‐2300‐35S::RFP, which served as a marker for nuclear localisation. Transient expression assays were performed as previously described (Zheng et al. 2023). Fluorescence observations were performed 48 h postinjection using an Axio LSM 700 microscope. GFP fluorescence was excited with a 488 nm laser and detected within the 500–550 nm range, while RFP fluorescence was stimulated by a 532 nm laser and observed within the 550–590 nm range.

RNA Extraction and Real‐Time Fluorescence Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (R401, Vazyme) and the RNA was then used for the synthesis of first‐strand cDNA with the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix reverse transcriptase (R223, Vazyme) following the manufacturer's protocol. Subsequently, quantitative real‐time PCR assays were conducted with the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix Reagent kit (Q711, Vazyme) to detect specific genes. ACTIN was used as an internal control. The relative expression levels were calculated as described previously (Zheng et al. 2022). The primers used for RT‐qPCR are presented in Table S1.

Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) Analysis

The crystallisation patterns of epicuticular wax were observed by SEM as described by Lü et al. (2009). The 7‐week‐old wild‐type (WT) Actinidia arguta , wild‐type (WT) Actinidia chinensis , AaKCS2‐OE, AaKCS6.1‐OE, AaMYB31.1‐OE, AaMYB31.2‐OE, AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐OE, AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐OE and AaSIZ1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants were treated with or without cold 4°C for 0 h and 24 h and collected for SEM assays. The leaves were subjected to electron microscope fixative (G1102, Servicebio) for 24 h, and then dehydrated in a gradient series of aqueous ethanol solutions. Subsequently, the samples were rinsed with deionised water and dehydrated in a gradient series of aqueous ethanol solutions. The dehydrated samples were dried on the critical point dryer (K850, Quorum) and placed on the sample stage of the ion sputtering instrument (MC1000, HITACHI) for gold spraying. The surfaces of the coated samples were observed under 4000 × ( Actinidia arguta ) and 20 000 × ( Actinidia chinensis ) magnification (HITACHI Regulus 8100).

Cuticular Wax Analysis

Cuticular wax was extracted from the leaves of 7‐week‐old wild‐type (WT) Actinidia arguta , wild‐type (WT) Actinidia chinensis , AaKCS2‐OE, AaKCS6.1‐OE, AaMYB31.1‐OE, AaMYB31.2‐OE, AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐OE, AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐OE and AaSIZ1‐OE transgenic Actinidia chinensis plants as described (Go et al. 2014). Briefly, immerse the leaves in chloroform and allow them to soak at room temperature for 30 s. n‐octacosane, docosanoic acid and 1‐tricosanol were added as internal standards. The extracted solution was evaporated by nitrogen gas. The dried residue was re‐dissolved in 100 μL pyridine and 100 μL bis‐N, N‐(trimethylsilyl)‐trifluoroacetamide, and the mixture was incubated for 20 min at 100°C. The cuticular wax content was re‐dissolved in CH2Cl2 and detected by GC–MS system (Agilent Technologies, USA). Cuticular wax content was expressed as μg mg−1 FW.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

For the dual‐luciferase reporter (DLR) assays, the promoter regions (2000 bp for AaKCS2, 2000 bp for AaKCS4.2, 2000 bp for AaKCS6.1, 2000 bp for AaKCS6.2, 2000 bp for AaKCS19, 2000 bp for AaKAS1.1, 2000 bp for AaKAS1.2, 247 bp for AaKCS2‐P3, 343 bp for AaKCS2‐P4, 374 bp for AaKCS6.1‐P4 and 365 bp for AaKCS6.1‐P5) were cloned into pGreenII 0800‐LUC at the BamHI site to generate ProAaKCS2::LUC, ProAaKCS2‐P3::LUC, ProAaKCS2‐P4::LUC, ProAaKCS6.1::LUC, ProAaKCS6.1‐P4::LUC and ProAaKCS6.1‐P5::LUC, respectively (Cao et al. 2024). The CDS of AaMYB31s were cloned into pART27‐GFP at the KpnI site to generate constructions of 35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.3‐GFP and 35S::AaMYB31.4‐GFP, respectively. The plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium GV3101 and subsequently transiently expressed in the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana for 36–48 h. Following this, Nicotiana benthamiana leaves were injected with 1 mM luciferin (LUCK‐100, GoldBio), and images were captured using a charge‐coupled device (CCD) cold imaging system (4600SF, Tanon). Then, the infected Nicotiana benthamiana leaves were collected, the firefly luciferase (LUC) and renilla luciferase (REN) activity were measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (DL101–01; Vazyme).

Yeast One‐Hybrid Assay

For the yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assay, The CDS of AaMYB31s were cloned into pJG4‐5 vector at the EcoRI site (Zhang, Zhao, et al. 2019). The full length and truncated fragments of the AaKCS2 and AaKCS6.1 promoters were cloned into pLACZI vector at the KpnI site. In brief, various combinations of plasmids were co‐transformed into yeast strain EGY48 and spread on SD/‐Trp/‐Ura plates and left at 28°C for 2–3 days. Positive transformants were subsequently inoculated onto SD/‐Ura/‐Trp medium supplemented with SD/‐Trp/‐Ura/Gal/Raf/X‐Gal for blue colour development to assess binding.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay

The electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was performed as previously described (Kang et al. 2024). Firstly, the DNA probe was biotin‐labelled using the Thermo Scientific Biotin 3′ End DNA Labeling Kit (89 818, Thermo Fisher). The recombinant GST‐AaMYB31.1 and GST‐AaMYB31.2 proteins were incubated with the biotin‐labelled probe, competition probe or mutant probe using a LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (20 148, Thermo Scientific) at room temperature for 30 min. The reactions were separated on a 6% PAGE using 0.5X Tris borate EDTA running buffer and detected by chemiluminescence. The probe sequences used for EMSA are listed in Table S1.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid Assay

The yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay was performed as previously reported (Zheng et al. 2022; Cao et al. 2024). The CDS of AaMYB31s was cloned into pGADT7 and pGBKT7 at the EcoRI site to generate the AD‐AaMYB31s and BD‐AaMYB31s fusion vectors, respectively. The full length and truncated fragments of the CDS of AaSIZ1 were cloned into pGBKT7 at the EcoRI site to generate the BD‐AaSIZ1, BD‐AaSIZ1‐N and BD‐AaSIZ1‐C fusion vectors, respectively. These constructs were cotransformed into the AH109 yeast strain utilising the specified combinations of plasmids. Subsequently, the yeast cells were cultured on SD/‐Trp/‐Leu medium and incubated at 28°C for 3 d until clones appeared. The interaction was assessed by spotting equal amounts of cells onto SD/‐Trp/‐Leu/‐His/‐Ade medium.

Luciferase Complementation Imaging Assay

The luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay was conducted according to previously published protocols (Cao et al. 2024). The CDS of AaMYB31s and AaSIZ1 were cloned into JW771‐nLUC vector at the KpnI and SalI site or JW772‐cLUC vector at the KpnI site to generate the AaSIZ1‐nLUC or AaMYB31.1‐cLUC and AaMYB31.2‐cLUC fusion vectors, respectively. The plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium GV3101 and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves for 36–48 h. After that, these leaves were injected with 1 mM luciferin (LUCK‐100, GoldBio), and images were captured with the Tanon 4600SF Chemiluminescent Imaging System.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation Assay

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays were performed as previously reported (Zheng et al. 2023). The CDS of AaMYB31s and AaSIZ1 were cloned into pBTEX‐Flag at the BamHI site and pBTEX‐HA at the KpnI site to generate AaMYB31.1‐FLAG, AaMYB31.2‐FLAG and AaSIZ1‐HA, respectively. The plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium GV3101 and transiently expressed in the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana for 36–48 h. Protein extraction buffer (2.7 mM KCl, 140 mM NaCl, 0.012 mM EDTA, 0.05% NP40, 25 mM Na2HPO4, 1.5 mM KH2PO4, 5 mM DTT and 0.5 mM PMSF) was used to extract total protein and incubated with anti‐FLAG magnetic beads (AE037, Abclonal) at 4°C for 2 h. After being washed with washing buffer (50 mM pH 7.5 Tris–HCl, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol and 1 mM PMSF), the captured proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and detected by immunoblotting with anti‐HA (Abmart, M20003) and anti‐FLAG (Abmart, M20008).

Pull‐Down Assay

The pull‐down assay was conducted according to previously published protocols (Zheng et al. 2022). The CDS of AaMYB31s and AaSIZ1 were cloned into pGEX‐4T‐1 and pET‐30a at the EcoRI and at the KpnI site to generate GST‐AaMYB31.1, GST‐AaMYB31.2 and His‐AaSIZ1 constructs, respectively. The Escherichia coli Rosetta transformed with different vectors was induced by 0.1 mM isopropylthio‐β‐galactoside (IPTG) and incubated in a shaker at 28°C for 8 h. Recombinant protein was purified through affinity chromatography with glutathione agarose (A10018S, Abmart) and amylose resin (E8021V, NEB), respectively. The purified GST‐AaMYB31.1, GST‐AaMYB31.2 or GST were mixed with His‐AaSIZ1 protein and incubated with amylose resin at 4°C for 2 h. The beads were washed with pull down buffer (1 M pH = 8.0 Tris–HCl, 0.2 M NaCl, 0.5 M EDTA, 0.25% NP‐40 and 0.5 mM PMSF) and eluted into SDS loading buffer. Meanwhile, eluents were analysed by immunoblot probed with anti‐GST (M20007, Abmart; dilution 1:2000) and anti‐His (M20001, Abmart; dilution 1:2000).

SUMOylation Assay

The SUMOylation assay was conducted according to previously published protocols (Zheng et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021; Jing et al. 2025). For in vitro SUMOylation assays, GST‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.2 was incubated in reaction buffer (50 Mm Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM ATP, 10 mM MgCl2, 15% glycerol, E1 (0.5 μg human SAE1 full and 0.5 μg His‐AtSAE2), E2 (2 μg His‐AtSCE1), pH 7.8) with or without His‐SUMO1GG, His‐SUMO1AA and His‐AaSIZ1 for 3 h at 37°C. Next, the samples were subjected to the subsequent separation on an SDS‐PAGE gel and immunoblotting analysis using anti‐GST and anti‐SUMO1 antibodies (A2130, ABclonal; dilution 1:1000).

For in vivo SUMOylation assays, the AaMYB31.1‐Myc, AaMYB31.2‐Myc, AaMYB31.1‐GFP, AaMYB31.2‐GFP, AaMYB31.1K76R‐GFP, AaMYB31.2K76R‐GFP or HA‐AaSIZ1 were transiently coexpressed with FLAG‐SUMO1GG (FLAG‐SUMO1) or FLAG‐SUMO1AA in Actinidia arguta mesophyll protoplasts or N. benthamiana leaves. The total protein was extracted with protein extraction buffer (20 mM pH = 7.6 Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 2 mM DTT, 10% Glycerol, 1% PVPP, 1 mM PMSF and 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail). Then the protein was immunoprecipitated with anti‐GFP (AE012, ABclonal; dilution 1:2000) and anti‐Myc (AE010, ABclonal; dilution 1:2000) antibodies, and the immunoprecipitated proteins were detected with anti‐Myc, anti‐FLAG and anti‐GFP antibodies. To determine SUMOylation of AaMYB31s in planta, total proteins were extracted from freshly harvested leaves of 35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐GFP or 35S::AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐GFP plants with protein extraction buffer. Then the protein was immunoprecipitated with anti‐GFP antibody, and the immunoprecipitated proteins were detected with anti‐SUMO1 and anti‐FLAG antibodies.

Protein Degradation Assay

The in vitro protein degradation assay was conducted according to previously published protocols (Zhang et al. 2023). The AaMYB31.1‐Myc or AaMYB31.2‐Myc, AaSIZ1‐GFP or 35S::GFP were transiently co‐expressed with HA‐RFP in N. benthamiana leaves and extracted the total proteins. The GST‐AaMYB31.1 or GST‐AaMYB31.2 protein incubated with total proteins extracted from WT and 35S::AaSIZ1‐GFP plants for protein degradation assays. Then the total protein treated with or without 50 μM of Z‐Leu‐Leu‐Leu‐CHO aldehyde (MG132, 26S proteasome inhibitor) for 3 h were incubated with degradation buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 4 mM phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride (PMSF), 5 mM DTT and 10 mM ATP, pH 7.5) and sampled at 0, 1, 2 and 3 h. Then the protein was separated by SDS‐PAGE and detected by immunoblotting with anti‐Myc antibody and anti‐GFP antibody. HA‐RFP and actin were used for internal control.

The in vivo protein degradation assay was conducted according to previously published protocols (Zhang et al. 2021). The 35S::AaMYB31.1‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.2‐GFP, 35S::AaMYB31.1 K76R ‐GFP or 35S::AaMYB31.2 K76R ‐GFP plants were treated at 22°C or 4°C and sampled at 0, 1, 2 and 3 h and the total proteins. Then the protein was separated by SDS‐PAGE and detected by immunoblotting with anti‐GFP antibody and anti‐Actin. Actin was used for internal control.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0. Student's t‐test was used for comparisons to the control. Asterisks indicate significant differences assessed by Student's t‐test: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01. For comparisons among multiple groups, one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD was performed. Different lowercase letters indicate statistically significant differences at p < 0.05 as determined by ANOVA.
