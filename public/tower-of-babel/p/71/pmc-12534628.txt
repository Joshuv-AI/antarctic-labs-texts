The effects of genetically determined plasma methyltransferases on digestive system cancers: a comprehensive bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Congcong Zhang, Xueying Li, Zhebin Dong, Yihui Weng, Weiming Yu, Xianlei Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe causal relationship between plasma methyltransferase protein concentrations and the onset of digestive system cancers remains unclear. This study employed a Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to explore potential causal relationships.MethodsA bidirectional two-sample MR analysis was conducted to systematically investigate causal associations. Data from the FinnGen Biobank and the European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI) were used for verification. This analysis focused on 14 types of methyltransferases and 5 types of digestive system cancers.ResultsThe analysis identified specific methyltransferases associated with increased risks for certain digestive system malignancies. Notably, dual verification confirmed that HEMK2 is associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer [Odds Ratio (OR) = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.06–1.55 for FinnGen; OR = 1.18; 95% CI: 1.07–1.29 for EBI] and liver cancer (OR = 2.31; 95% CI: 1.29–4.14 for FinnGen; OR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.08–1.76 for EBI). NTMT1 was also associated with colorectal cancer (OR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.03–1.30 for FinnGen; OR = 1.10; 95% CI: 1.04–1.17 for EBI) and liver cancer (OR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.11–2.27 for FinnGen; OR = 1.21;95%CI: 1.04–1.40 for EBI). We also found that some methyltransferases exhibit either harmful or protective effects in digestive system cancers; however, these findings have not been validated by another independent database. Therefore, the interpretation of these results requires caution.ConclusionsThis study reveals that certain methyltransferases are associated with an increased risk of some digestive system cancers. HEMK2 and NTMT1 emerged as candidate genetically predicted biomarkers, meriting further research to elucidate their mechanistic functions and potential clinical applicability. Furthermore, as all genetic information in this study originates from European cohorts, the conclusions are primarily relevant to this population and necessitate validation in diverse ethnic groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03758-8.Keywords: Methyltransferase, Plasma protein, Digestive system diseases, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Epigenetic regulation involves chemical modifications or alterations in chromatin structure without changing the DNA sequence. This regulation critically affects species adaptability, individual development, and disease aetiology [1, 2]. Key epigenetic regulatory mechanisms include DNA methylation, histone modification, non-coding RNA regulation, and chromatin structure remodelling, and methyltransferases are crucial in these processes.

Methyltransferases represent a diverse group of enzymes responsible for transferring methyl groups to a broad spectrum of substrates, with S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) commonly serving as the primary methyl donor. Widely distributed across biological systems, they play critical roles in numerous fundamental biochemical pathways [3]. Methyltransferases are primarily classified into the following four types: (i) DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), a crucial subset of methyltransferases, convert cytosines to 5-methylcytosines, predominantly at CpG dinucleotides. DNMTs maintain methylation patterns following DNA replication, balancing gene expression crucial for gene silencing and genomic stability. Aberrant DNA methylation profiles, such as hypermethylation or hypomethylation, are linked to various diseases, including cancers, neurodegenerative disorders, and metabolic dysfunctions [4, 5]. (ii) RNA methyltransferases modify mRNA, rRNA, and tRNA by introducing methyl groups, affecting RNA stability, processing and translational efficiency. RNA methylation is integral to intracellular signaling and developmental control, and is associated with disease mechanisms, particularly carcinogenesis [6, 7]. (iii) Protein methyltransferases (PMTs) catalyze the methylation of amino acid residues (such as arginine and lysine) in proteins, influencing protein activity, stability, and interactions. Disrupted protein methylation states are implicated in diseases, including cancers, neurodegenerative disorders and cardiovascular diseases [8, 9]. (iv) Small molecule methyltransferases participate in the synthesis and metabolism of compounds such as catecholamines, choline, and alkaloids. Abnormal activities of these enzymes are linked to neuropsychiatric disorders and other diseases, underscoring the importance of methylation in maintaining physiological equilibrium [10].

Malignant tumors of the digestive system include malignancies of the alimentary canal, such as esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer, as well as malignancies of the liver, gallbladder, and pancreas [11]. These cancers are characterized by high incidence rates, high malignancy, and poor prognosis [12]. Numerous studies have indicated that methyltransferases play a significant role in the initiation and progression of malignant tumors of the digestive system [13–15]. However, current research is mainly limited to some common methyltransferases, such as those related to N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification [11]. Extensive research has been conducted on m6A methyltransferases owing to their critical involvement in the modulation of RNA stability, translation efficiency, and degradation rates, thereby influencing cellular activities, and physiological functions [16]. The m6A modification has also been found to be closely related to the occurrence and development of many malignant tumors of the digestive system [17–19]. The role of many other types of methyltransferases in malignant tumors of the digestive system remains unknown.

In this context, our study employs bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) to explore the causal relationships between various plasma methyltransferases and digestive system cancers. The aim is to identify novel genetically predicted biomarkers and establish a solid theoretical foundation for basic research, preventive strategies, and therapeutic approaches in this domain.

Method

Study design

This bidirectional two-sample MR study adhered to the STROBE-MR guidelines [20, 21]. MR analysis was based on three key assumptions: (i) instrumental variables (IVs) must be correlated with the exposure. IVs represent attributes that demonstrate a strong correlation with the exposure under study, enabling the examination of causal relationships between the exposure and the outcome without the influence of confounding factors; (ii) IVs should affect the outcome solely through the exposure; and (iii) IVs must be independent of any factors that could confound the exposure-outcome relationship, whether measured or unmeasured. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the study utilised a bidirectional two-sample MR strategy to investigate the correlation between plasma methyltransferase protein concentrations and the risk of digestive system cancers. The study was conducted utilizing a bidirectional Mendelian randomization framework, wherein single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) served as IVs due to their minimal association with confounding factors. This methodology enabled the evaluation of the causal effects between plasma methyltransferase concentrations and the risk of developing digestive system cancers, as well as the reciprocal, the influence of such cancers on plasma methyltransferase levels.

Study population and data sources

The analysis focused on 14 plasma methyltransferases and 5 digestive system cancers (Tables 1 and 2). Data on plasma methyltransferase proteins were obtained from a large proteomics project involving 3,301 European individuals as IVs [22]. This study elucidated the genetic profile of the human plasma proteome among healthy blood donors in the INTERVAL study. The dataset on digestive system diseases was sourced from the FinnGen Biobank Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) (https://finngen.gitbook.io/documentation/v/r5/data-download) and the European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI) [23] databases, utilizing summary statistics from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) Open GWAS project. All participants, of homogeneous European heritage, provided voluntary consent.

Selection of genetic variations

To ensure the robustness of the study, a stringent screening criterion was applied for SNPs used as IVs. The basis for these stringent standards is outlined as follows: (1) Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) Constraint (r² < 0.001): SNPs in LD were selected to diminish LD’s impact on our analysis, thus confirming their role as independent genetic indicators; (2) Spatial Proximity Criterion (window size = 10,000 kb): This criterion was utilized to assess SNPs that could be in LD due to their physical proximity, ensuring the independence of the selected SNPs; (3) Statistical Significance Threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸): Adhering to the standards of GWAS, this threshold was applied to mitigate the risk of type I errors and to verify the genuine association of SNPs with the exposure under investigation; (4) Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) cut-off (MAF < 0.01): SNPs exhibiting a low MAF were not included to guarantee their presence in the population’s genetic diversity and their significant contribution to the exposure’s variance; (5) Variance Explained (PVE) Calculation: Utilizing the formula PVE = 2 × MAF × (1 − MAF) × beta² [24], we evaluated the SNPs’ capacity to account for the exposure’s variance, confirming the IVs’ substantial influence on the exposure; (6) F-statistics Determination [F = PVE × (N − 2)/(1 − PVE)] [24]: F-statistics were employed to gauge the robustness of the SNPs, with an F-statistic exceeding 10 signifying a strong association and the IV’s adequacy for MR analysis; (7) MR-PRESSO assessment: SNPs failing the MR-PRESSO test at the 0.05 significance level were excluded, a measure to identify and adjust for pleiotropy, which could otherwise skew the causal effect estimation through multiple pathways [25–27].

Statistical analysis

The MR analysis followed a sequential methodology, as depicted in Fig. 1. Suitable IVs were identified, and the MR Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) methodology was used to remove pleiotropic outliers before conducting the main MR analysis. Following this, MR-Egger regression analysis was performed to assess horizontal pleiotropy, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 indicating negligible horizontal pleiotropic effects [28]. Potential confounding factors were evaluated using the online tool PhenoScanner V2 [29].

Following the elimination of pleiotropic effects, Cochran’s Q test was used to detect heterogeneity among SNPs. Multiple MR methodologies were employed, including weighted median, inverse variance weighting (IVW), and weighted mode analyses, to assess consistency in effect directions. The primary causal relationship was determined using the IVW methodology. To ensure robustness, three analytical strategies were employed to derive odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Additionally, a sensitivity analysis utilizing the leave-one-out approach was conducted to further validate the IVW effect estimates. In cases where the instrumental variable consisted of a single SNP, only the Wald ratio approach was used [12].

A stringent significance threshold of P < 3.57 × 10− 3(0.05/14) was applied to denote a substantial association, while P values between 3.57 × 10− 3 and 0.05 indicated a suggestive association. The Bonferroni correction was employed owing to its conservative properties. All statistical analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.1) within the R software (version 4.0.3).

Result

Baseline participant characteristics

To investigate the causal associations between genetically predicted methyltransferase and digestive system cancers, we evaluated 14 types of methyltransferases: Glycine N-methyltransferase (GNMT), Histone-lysine N-methyltransferase 2 C (KMT2C), Set1/Ash2 histone methyltransferase complex subunit ASH2 (ASH2L), tRNA (guanine-N(7)-)-methyltransferase (METTL1), N-acetylserotonin O-methyltransferase-like protein (ASMTL), HemK methyltransferase family member 2 (HEMK2), N-terminal Xaa-Pro-Lys N-methyltransferase 1 (NTMT1), Protein arginine N-methyltransferase 3 (PRMT3), rRNA methyltransferase 3, mitochondrial (MRM3), Histone-lysine N-methyltransferase SETD2 (SETD2), Histone-lysine N-methyltransferase SETMAR (SETMAR), N-lysine methyltransferase SMYD2 (SMYD2), Histone-lysine N-methyltransferase SUV420H2 (KMT5C), and Histone-lysine N-methyltransferase EHMT2 (EHMT2) (Table 1). The study included 5 types of digestive system cancers, involving a total of 14,979 cases (Table 2). These cancers included esophageal cancer (n = 1,230), gastric cancer (n = 1,662), colorectal cancer (n = 9,603), hepatocellular carcinoma (n = 683) and pancreatic cancer (n = 1,801). The selected SNPs associated with methyltransferases and digestive system cancers are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. All F-statistics exceeded a threshold of 10, corroborating the effectiveness of our instrumental approach. For exposures proxied by a single genetic variant (see Supplementary Tables 2–6), causal estimates were obtained via the Wald ratio method. Supplementary Table 1 provides detailed information on the proportion of variance explained (PVE) and F-statistic for each SNP, facilitating thorough evaluation of instrumental variable strength. Results from the bidirectional two-sample MR analysis are illustrated in Figs. 2 and 3, with detailed outcomes presented in Supplementary Tables 2–10.

The causal effect of multiple methyltransferases on digestive system cancers

Supplementary Tables 2–6 summarize the causal associations between genetically predicted methyltransferases and digestive system cancers, using data from both the FinnGen Biobank and the EBI database (Fig. 2). Additionally, forest plots in Fig. 4 visually represent the causal relationships, including the magnitudes and certainty of these effects.

Causal effects of genetically determined plasma methyltransferases on malignant digestive system cancers by MR analyses. This forest plot summarizes the specific results of the study (IVW: inverse variance weighted; OR: Odds Ratio; CI: confidence interval)

Increased levels of specific methyltransferases were associated with an elevated risk of digestive system cancers. In the FinnGen Biobank, HEMK2 and NTMT1 were identified as risk factors for colorectal cancer (OR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.06–1.55 for HEMK2; OR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.03–1.30 for NTMT1) and liver cancer (OR = 2.31; 95% CI: 1.29–4.14 for HEMK2; OR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.11–2.27 for NTMT1). These associations were confirmed by the EBI database (HEMK2: OR = 1.18; 95% CI: 1.07–1.29; NTMT1: OR = 1.10; 95% CI: 1.04–1.17 for colorectal cancer; HEMK2: OR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.08–1.76; NTMT1: OR = 1.21, 95%CI: 1.04–1.40 for liver cancer). Additionally, ASH2L was linked to an increased risk of esophageal cancer (OR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.09–3.27), PRMT3 to gastric cancer (OR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.05–3.49), and NTMT1 to pancreatic cancer (OR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.11–2.27). However, these findings were not reproducible in the EBI datasets and the results of ASH2L, PRMT3, and NTMT1 should be interpreted cautiously.

Exploratory analyses indicated several potential associations that merit further independent validation. These comprised elevated risk of esophageal cancer linked to ASH2L (OR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.09–3.27) in the FinnGen Biobank, gastric cancer associated with PRMT3 (OR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.05–3.49) in the EBI database, and pancreatic cancer related to NTMT1 (OR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.11–2.27), also within FinnGen. It should be noted, however, that these associations were not consistently observed across both datasets and did not withstand stringent multiple testing adjustment. Thus, these results must be interpreted prudently as hypothesis-generating.

Conversely, exploratory analyses also suggested that elevated levels of certain methyltransferases were associated with reduced risks of specific digestive system malignancies. Specifically, increased plasma concentrations of KMT5C were linked to a protective effect against esophageal cancer (OR = 0.22; 95% CI: 0.10–0.50) and pancreatic cancer (OR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.51–0.98). KMT2C was associated with a reduced risk of gastric cancer (OR = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.45–0.89), while METTL1 and MRM3 were linked to a lower risk of colorectal cancer (OR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.65–0.89 for METTL1; OR = 0.92; 95% CI: 0.85–0.99 for MRM3) in the FinnGen Biobank. These preliminary observations indicate that the aforementioned methyltransferases could play a mitigating role in tumorigenesis and disease advancement, suggesting possible protective functions. Nevertheless, it is essential to underscore that these correlations were not replicated in an additional independent cohort and must be regarded as purely exploratory until substantiated by subsequent research. These findings suggest that these methyltransferases may exert inhibitory effects on tumor initiation and progression, potentially offering protective benefits. However, these associations were not reproducible in the other datasets. The results should be interpreted cautiously.

The causal effect of digestive system cancers on multiple methyltransferases

Supplementary Tables 7–10 outline the causal effects of genetically predicted malignant digestive system diseases on plasma methyltransferase levels (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

We observed positive correlations between specific malignant digestive system diseases and certain methyltransferases. Notably, an elevated plasma concentration of PRMT3 was associated with esophageal cancer (OR = 1.14; 95% CI: 1.01–1.28) in the EBI database, However, there were not datasets available in the FinnGen Biobank to repeat the result. An increased levels of SETMAR were linked to colorectal cancer (OR = 1.15; 95% CI: 1.01–1.30) in the EBI database. However, these associations were not reproducible in the FinnGen Biobank. The results should be interpreted cautiously.

Interestingly, a decrease in plasma levels of MRM3 was noted in colorectal cancer (OR = 0.90; 95% CI : 0.81-1.00), while reductions in PRMT3 and SETMAR levels were observed in hepatocellular carcinoma (OR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.76-1.00 for PRMT3 ; OR = 0.91; 95% CI: 0.84-1.00 for SETMAR). However, these associations were not reproducible in the other datasets. The results should be interpreted cautiously.

We did not obtain relevant IVs related to pancreatic cancer from both databases, thus precluding a bidirectional MR analysis for pancreatic cancer. Additionally, no relevant SNPs for esophageal cancer were available from the Finnish database.

Discussion

This study employs bidirectional MR analysis to investigate the causal relationship between methyltransferases and digestive system cancers. The findings reveal a complex and nuanced association, offering novel insights into the pathogenesis of these conditions. Specifically, the dual verification establishes that HEMK2 and NTMT1 promote the development of colorectal cancer and liver cancer.

DNMTs, RNA methyltransferases, and PMTs are the three most common types of methyltransferases [30, 31]. DNMTs facilitate the conversion of cytosine to 5-methylcytosine in DNA, predominantly at CpG dinucleotides. DNMT1 functions as a maintenance methyltransferase, preserving existing methylation patterns during DNA replication, whereas DNMT3A and DNMT3B serve as de novo methyltransferases, adding methyl groups to previously unmethylated DNA, thereby influencing gene expression [6]. RNA methyltransferases, on the other hand, modify RNA molecules, primarily by introducing m6A into mRNA. This modification can affect mRNA stability, localization, and translation efficiency [32, 33]. Protein methyltransferases (PMTs) are enzymes that catalyze the transfer of methyl groups to specific amino acid residues, such as arginine and lysine, in target proteins. This modification regulates various cellular processes, including gene expression, signal transduction, and protein stability. Dysregulation of protein methylation is linked to diseases like cancer and neurodegenerative disorders [8, 9].

Our investigation reveals the intricate and multifaceted causal relationships linking methyltransferases to malignant digestive system diseases. Elevated expression levels of specific methyltransferases appear to contribute to tumor progression. In particular, HEMK2 and NTMT1 demonstrate consistent risk-enhancing effects across two distinct datasets, thereby validating the robustness of our results and emphasizing their pivotal roles in tumor formation. This concordance highlights the causal connections between these methyltransferases and digestive malignancies, implicating them as key factors in disease pathogenesis. HEMK2 is a highly conserved protein methyltransferases found throughout evolution, from yeast to humans. It has been shown to play a critical role in germ cell development in Drosophila, modulating protein synthesis, and mRNA stability via methylation of the eukaryotic release factor 1 (eRF1) [34]. NTMT1 is also a protein methyltransferase and it is implicated in processes such as mitosis, DNA damage repair, neurogenesis, and stem cell maintenance [35]. Additionally, NTMT1 is frequently overexpressed in various cancers, including melanoma, colorectal carcinoma, and cervical cancer [36]. Experimental and clinical research on HEMK2 and NTMT1 in malignant tumors of the digestive system is still relatively scarce. A conference paper has reported that NTMT1 promotes the development of colon cancer in both in vitro and in vivo experiments [37]. Additionally, a multicenter clinical study has found that DNA methylation detection of plasma NTMT1 is conducive to the diagnosis of colorectal cancer [38]. These results are consistent with our findings. Existing literature also supports the associations between methyltransferases and malignancies. It should be emphasized, however, that the present study constitutes a hypothesis-generating investigation rooted in genetic instrumental variable analysis. Although it provides robust evidence supporting a potential causal link, it does not supply empirical functional or mechanistic evidence. The precise biological mechanisms through which HEMK2 and NTMT1 contribute to oncogenesis remain incompletely characterized and warrant further in-depth experimental exploration. For example, Chen et al. reported that tRNA m7G methyltransferase METTL1 promotes head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) development and aggressiveness by regulating global mRNA translation, including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, and altering the immune environment [39]. Similarly, Pan et al. observed elevated levels of RNA N6-methyladenosine (m6A) and methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) in CRC tissues, with METTL3 enhancing CRC progression [40]. Moreover, certain methyltransferases, including ASH2L [41, 42], PRMT3 [43, 44], HEMK2 [45] and NTMT1 [46, 47], are associated with an increased risk of malignant digestive tumors. These findings suggest that m6A modifications influence tumor cell behavior and biological characteristics by affecting mRNA stability, processing and translation efficiency. Notably, specific methyltransferases such as KMT2C, KMT5C, and MRM3 demonstrate protective effects. This suggests that these methyltransferases may inhibit tumor initiation and progression through mechanisms that are not yet fully understood, thus presenting potential anticancer properties.

In the reverse MR analysis, we uncovered a noteworthy phenomenon: the plasma levels of specific methyltransferases exhibited both increases and decreases across various digestive system cancers. This duality underscores the intricate mechanisms through which these enzymes influence disease progression, suggesting that they may operate through multiple pathways, exerting both protective, and risk-increasing effects. The presence of this bidirectional impact indicates that the role of methyltransferases in digestive system cancers is more complex than previously understood. Elevated levels of some methyltransferases may serve as a protective mechanism against disease progression, while reduced levels might indicate insufficient protective action, thereby increasing disease risk.

The observed causal relationships between methyltransferases and digestive system cancers underscore the potential of these enzymes as valuable genetically predicted biomarkers. Methyltransferases that demonstrate significant risk-enhancing or protective effects could be pivotal in early diagnosis, prevention, and therapeutic strategies. For instance, enhancing the expression of methyltransferases with protective effects might offer therapeutic benefits, while inhibiting those associated with increased risk could mitigate disease progression. Epidemiological research has already highlighted the role of methyltransferases in drug metabolism, efficacy, and toxicity. For example, Weinshilboum et al. [48] explored the impact of catechol O-methyltransferase (COMT), thiopurine S-methyltransferase (TPMT) and histamine N-methyltransferase (HNMT) on drug metabolism, revealing how these enzymes can affect drug efficacy and toxicity. This suggests their potential as therapeutic targets. Additionally, Anglin et al. [49] reviewed the biochemistry, cancer biology, and recent advances in the disruptor of telomeric silencing 1 like (DOT1L) inhibitors. DOT1L, a histone methyltransferase, inhibitors have been reported to hold potential applications in cancer therapy.

This study possesses several strengths. Firstly, the use of a bidirectional MR design allowed us to explore the causal relationship between methyltransferases and digestive system cancers in both directions, reducing the likelihood of confounding factors, reverse causality and differential exposure [50]. Additionally, we leveraged two independent GWAS datasets from the EBI and FinnGen, with consistent results across these populations, further validating our findings.

However, these strengths must be considered alongside certain limitations. Firstly, it is important to note that all genome-wide association data utilized in this analysis originate exclusively from European-ancestry populations and have not been validated in other ethnic groups, such as East Asian or African cohorts. Consequently, the general applicability of our results is constrained to populations of European descent, and extrapolation to other ethnicities should be approached with caution. Secondly, the SNP selection criteria used in the study may not have captured all relevant genetic variations, potentially excluding biologically significant SNPs that did not meet the screening threshold. Furthermore, the selected SNPs account for only a small portion of the variance in plasma levels of methyltransferases, which could bias the interpretation of causal effects. Thirdly, although we have established a bidirectional causal relationship between methyltransferases and digestive system cancers, this does not imply a comprehensive understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms. Fourthly, important limitations in the reverse MR analysis merit consideration. The absence of adequate genetic instruments for pancreatic cancer in both datasets, as well as for esophageal cancer in the FinnGen cohort, prevented bidirectional causal assessment for these cancers. This scarcity of instruments may indicate underlying divergence in the genetic architecture influencing both cancer susceptibility and methyltransferase abundance — such as a lack of common genetic variants exerting robust effects on both traits, or limited power of current GWAS to detect such associations. Moreover, the inconsistency of certain associations (e.g., SETMAR with colorectal cancer, PRMT3 with esophageal cancer) across databases underscores the heterogeneity potentially introduced by differences in study design, population structure, or phenotype classification across consortia. Thus, results from the reverse MR analysis, especially those lacking consistent support, should be regarded as preliminary and in need of confirmation in future studies. Fifthly, as a characteristic of proteomics and pQTL analysis, it is challenging to identify a substantial number of SNPs that are statistically associated with a particular protein. The number of SNPs also limits the stability of statistical results. In addressing the issue of multiple testing, a Bonferroni adjustment was applied to control the incidence of false positives. Other methods such as false discovery rate (FDR) were not used, which may increase the risk of false positives. It should be noted, however, that some results which reached nominal significance — particularly those not consistently replicated across both datasets (such as for ASH2L and PRMT3) — are of an exploratory nature. These outcomes warrant cautious interpretation and should be viewed primarily as hypothesis-generating for subsequent studies rather than as conclusive evidence. Finally, the lack of experimental validation is a major limitation of this work. As an MR analysis, this study provides genetic evidence supporting a causal relationship but does not elucidate the specific biological mechanisms through which HEMK2, NTMT1, or other methyltransferases promote or suppress tumorigenesis. Moreover, although all included instrumental variables had F-statistics exceeding 10, most methyltransferases were instrumented by only 1–3 SNPs, limiting the explained variance (low PVE) for certain exposures. This increases the potential for weak instrument bias and may result in underestimation of the true causal effects. Further validation through cellular and animal models, along with detailed molecular investigations, is needed to confirm these findings and clarify the underlying functional pathways.

Therefore, although our study identifies HEMK2 and NTMT1 as promising genetically predicted candidates, their clinical applicability as biomarkers depends critically on subsequent validation via plasma enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, tissue immunohistochemistry, or prospective cohorts.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the pathogenesis of digestive system cancers and suggests new avenues for prevention and treatment. HEMK2 and NTMT1 emerged as novel genetically supported biomarker candidates. This study is a causal inference study, and mechanistic studies are needed for further validation.

Future research should aim to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which these methyltransferases influence the progression of digestive system diseases. Additionally, larger-scale clinical studies are needed to confirm these findings and to assess the potential of methyltransferases as therapeutic targets. It should be noted that the generalizability of our results may be limited by the exclusive use of genetic data from individuals of European descent. Consequently, subsequent replication in multi-ethnic cohorts is essential.
