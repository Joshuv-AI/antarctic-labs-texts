Hitching a ride across the continental divide of Mexico: the twospot livebearer Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus carried its Gyrodactylus spp. when translocated from the Atlantic to the Pacific Ocean versant
Authors: Ismael Edoardo Sánchez-González, Miguel Rubio-Godoy, Carlos Daniel Pinacho-Pinacho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe studied flatworm parasites of the genus Gyrodactylus associated with twospot livebearer Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus both in this host’s native distribution in the Gulf of Mexico (Atlantic Ocean) versant, as well as in its invasive range across the continental divide, in the Pacific Ocean watershed. We surveyed 12 river basins, six on each slope of the continent, and recorded –apparently– overdispersed parasite distributions, both in terms of localities within basins and on fish hosts. Six gyrodactylid species were previously known to infect this poeciliid host, and we add five taxa to the list, three known and two undescribed species. From the 11 parasite species we characterized molecularly (cox 2 mDNA and ITS rDNA sequences), 9–10 species (depending on the markers used for species delimitation; cox 2 + ITS, nine species; cox 2, 10 species) were recorded in river basins draining into the Atlantic, and only four in rivers flowing into the Pacific. Only one species, Gyrodactylus takoke, was recorded frequently on both sides of the country, and cox 2 haplotype networks suggest that parasites co-translocated with their fish hosts originated from the central Gulf of Mexico slope and successfully invaded two river basins draining into the Pacific. We hypothesize that following the translocation of P. bimaculatus across the continental divide of Mexico, different types of host-parasite interactions (enemy release, spill-over and spill-back) may involve particular parasite species, some of which are known to be pathogenic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-026-08701-6.Keywords: Twospot livebearer, Monogenea, Enemy release, Spill-over, Spill-back, Invasive parasite

Introduction

Poeciliids are among the most common and speciose freshwater fish families in the Americas, with members distributed at low elevations along the Atlantic Ocean coast from the eastern United States to northeastern Argentina (Froese and Pauly 2025). In Mexico, the most cosmopolitan poeciliid in the lowlands draining into the Gulf of Mexico is the twospot livebearer, Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus (Heckel 1848) (syn. Heterandria bimaculata), whose natural distribution spans from the river Nautla, Veracruz, Mexico to the river Prinzapolka in Nicaragua (Miller et al. 2005; Agorreta et al. 2013). This species forms a complex containing at least eight lineages distributed in different regions of Tropical Middle America that have not been assigned to nominal species (Agorreta et al. 2013). Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus has been used as an ornamental aquarium fish, as live food for larger fish and for mosquito biological control programs, and starting in the late XXth century, it was translocated from the Atlantic Ocean versant to the central highlands of Mexico, to rivers draining to the Pacific Ocean (Gaspar-Dillanes 1987; Contreras-MacBeath et al. 1998; Gomez-Maldonado et al. 2023). The first record of translocated twospot livebearers past the continental divide was in the river Balsas in 1987, and their distribution has steadily expanded northwest-ward to other river basins, including rivers Cutzamala (Paulo-Maya and Ramírez-Enciso 1997), Lerma-Chapala (Ramírez-Herrejón et al. 2012) and Teuchitlán (Mar-Silva et al. 2021). The success of P. bimaculatus as an invasive species arises from its year-round high fecundity, adaptive trophic strategies, flexible behavior, tolerance to pollution and to a wide range of adverse biotic conditions (Ramírez-Carrillo and Macías-García 2015; Mar-Silva et al. 2021; Gomez-Maldonado et al. 2023; Salazar-Rueda et al. 2024).

Poeciliid fishes are hosts to several monogenean ectoparasites of the genus Gyrodactylus von Nordmann, 1832 – many of which have been translocated along with their fish hosts, that have been widely used in the ornamental fish trade and mosquito-control programs. In fact, several of the first gyrodactylid species described from poeciliids were collected from ornamental fish acquired far from their native distribution range in the Atlantic coast of the Americas and the Antilles (e.g., Gyrodactylus bullatarudis Turnbull, 1956 collected from aquarium guppies, Poecilia reticulata Peters, 1859 in Canada; G. rasini Lucký, 1973 from green swordtail, Xiphophorus hellerii Heckel, 1848 in the Czech Republic; and G. turnbulli Harris, 1986 from P. reticulata in Britain), which illustrates the ability of monogenean parasites to persist after translocation and despite fish husbandry procedures, including quarantine. Subsequently, several species of Gyrodactylus have been described from wild poeciliid populations in the Americas (e.g., Harris and Cable 2000; Cable et al. 2005; Rubio-Godoy et al. 2010; García-Vásquez et al. 2015); in Mexico, five species are known to infect P. bimaculatus within its native distribution range in the Gulf of Mexico versant, and a further one in its translocated range in the Pacific Ocean versant (Table 1).

Of particular note are three of these gyrodactylids collected in Mexico from the twospot livebearer, because they are generalist parasites with quite low host specificity, since they have not only been recorded from hosts from different poeciliid genera but also from different fish families. Thus, G. bullatarudis was described from P. reticulata (and was probably co-introduced to Mexico with this invasive host) but is also known to infect other species from the poeciliid fish genera Gambusia, Poecilia, Pseudoxiphophorus and Xiphophorus (Rubio-Godoy et al. 2016), and is also recorded as a parasite of the non-poeciliid, cyprinodontiform killifish, Anablepsoides hartii (Boulenger, 1890) (syn. Rivulus hartii) (Cable et al. 2013). Gyrodactylus cichlidarum is a parasite of African cichlid fishes of the genera Coptodon, Oreochromis, Pelmatolapia (syn. Tilapia) and Sarotherodon (colloquially called “tilapias”) but has also been recorded infecting P. bimaculatus and other poeciliids in Mexico, both within their native as well as their human-translocated distributions on both sides of the continental divide (García-Vásquez et al. 2017). Finally, G. chiapaneco was described as a parasite of profundulid fishes in Chiapas, southern Mexico, on the Pacific Ocean versant (García-Vásquez et al. 2019), but is also known to infect P. bimaculatus in the central highlands of Mexico (Guanajuato) as well as other poeciliid, characid and clupeid fishes in Veracruz, on the Gulf of Mexico watershed (Pinacho-Pinacho et al. 2026). Previous studies in the central highlands of Mexico suggested that gyrodactylids infecting translocated poeciliid fishes have not spilled over to infect endemic goodeid fishes (Rubio-Godoy et al. 2016).

Most surveys of gyrodactylids infecting poeciliid fishes in Mexico have been conducted in river basins in central Veracruz, the northern edge of the twospot livebearer’s natural distribution. Ten species of Gyrodactylus associated with poeciliids have been described in this region (Rubio-Godoy et al. 2010; García-Vásquez et al. 2015), with G. takoke being the most abundant and widely distributed species, having been recorded from two poeciliid fish (P. bimaculatus and Poeciliopsis gracilis) in three river basins draining into the Atlantic Ocean.

Considering that P. bimaculatus is a common species in the Gulf of Mexico versant, which has been widely translocated by humans in Mexico and is host to several different species of gyrodactylid parasites (including generalist species with low host specificity), we studied Gyrodactylus associated with twospot livebearer in: (1) its native range, in rivers draining to the Gulf of Mexico (Atlantic Ocean) in basins that have not been surveyed yet, to assess the distribution range of known parasite species and to determine whether unknown taxa are associated with this host; and (2) in its translocation range, in rivers draining to the Pacific Ocean, to establish whether the same parasite community is found on both sides of the continental divide and hence to identify potentially translocated gyrodactylids.

Materials and methods

Host and parasite collection

In a period of seven years, from January 2018 to December 2024, we sampled 72 localities across Mexico, belonging to 12 hydrological basins on the Atlantic and Pacific Ocean slopes (Supplementary Table S1; Fig. 1). Hosts were captured using an electrofishing apparatus and nets, and subsequently fishes were anesthetized following the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA 2020) with an overdose of 2-phenoxyethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA), prior to their preservation in labelled bottles containing 96% ethanol. In the laboratory, preserved fishes were inspected with aide of a dissection microscope (Leica EZ4, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and gyrodactylids were removed with surgical needles. The bottom of the bottles was inspected to collect worms dislodged during transportation, which enabled us to determine the total amount of parasites per site, although not per host. Collected gyrodactylids were preserved in 96% ethanol in 1.5 ml labelled Eppendorf tubes and stored at -20 °C until further morphological and molecular analyses.

Parasite preparation

Individual worms were placed on a slide with 96% alcohol and labeled with the parasite number and locality. Surgical needles were used to separate the haptor from the trunk, which was removed from the semi-permanent preparation and stored in 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes with 96% ethanol for DNA extraction. The haptor was treated with 2.5 µl of a proteolytic digestion solution composed of 75 mM Tris HCL, 10 mM EDTA, 5% SDS, and proteinase K (1.4 mg/ml) to remove excess tissue from the marginal hooks, hamuli, and bars; the digestion reaction was stopped after approximately 30 s by adding 3.5 µl of a STOP solution composed of 4% formalin and glycerol in a 1:1 ratio. Finally, a coverslip was placed on each haptor (Harris and Cable 2000). In addition, some individuals were mounted on slides following the Gray & Wess technique proposed by Vidal-Martínez et al. (2001) to clarify internal structures, such as the male copulatory organ (MCO). Once the previous steps were completed, the preparations were examined using a Leica DM 750 light microscope. We used the Leica Application Suite software (V. 4.12.0, Leica Microsystems, Switzerland) and a Leica ICC50 HD camera (Driver Version 3.0.0.25) to capture images of structures such as the hamuli, bars, and marginal hooks.

Molecular data: DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 120 Gyrodactylus spp. individuals, of which 86 yielded successful amplifications. The process was performed using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, California, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Additionally, the DNAzol extraction protocol (Molecular Research Center, Ohio, USA) was used; for this method, worms were digested overnight at 56 °C in a solution composed of 10 µl of 100 mM TrisHCl (pH = 7.6), 10 µl of 200 mM NaCl, 20 µl of 0.5 M EDTA (pH = 8.0), 10 µl of 10% Sarkosyl, 1.4 µl of 20 mg/µl proteinase K, and 48.6 µl of distilled water. After digestion, DNA was isolated from the supernatant using DNAzol reagent, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following DNA extraction, amplification of two molecular markers was performed using Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR); the regions amplified for this study were internal transcribed spacers (ITS1-2, rDNA) and cytochrome oxidase subunit II (cox 2, mtDNA). The ITS region was amplified using the primer pairs: BD1 (5’-GTCGTAACAAGGTTTCCGTA-3’) and BD2 (5’-ATCTAGACCGGACTAGGCTGTG-3), as proposed by Bowles et al. (1993; 1995). A partial region of the cox 2 gene was amplified using the primer pairs COX2F1 (5-TACATAYCGCCCGTCAATYT-3) and COX2R1 (5-TCARTAYCACTGDCGDCCYA-3), as described by Xavier et al. (2015). Additionally, for some samples, the primers COX2F2 (5’-TTTCACTGAGATAAGTCGTAAC-3’) and COX2R (5’-AATAMWKATWGGCATRWAAGARTG-3’) (Bueno-Silva and Boeger 2014) were used.

PCR amplification reactions were carried out in a volume of 25 µl, consisting of 1 µl of each primer at a concentration of 10 µM, 2.5 µl of 10x buffer (200 mM Tris HCL (pH 8.4), 500 mM KCl), 1.5 µl of MgCl2 solution (50 mM), 0.5 µl of dNTP mix (10 mM), 0.125 µl of Platinum Taq DNA polymerase (1U/µl) (Invitrogen Corporation, São Paulo, Brazil), and 2 µl of previously extracted DNA. The PCR parameters were as follows: for cox 2, denaturation at 94–95 °C for 5–15 min, 40–41 cycles of 94–95 °C for 0.5–1 min; annealing at 45–50 °C for 0.5–1.5 min; extension at 72 °C for 0.5–1.5 min; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min; and for ITS, denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, 35 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min; annealing at 50 °C for 1 min; extension at 72 °C for 1 min; and a final extension at 75 °C for 10 min. All ITS and cox 2 amplifications were visualized on 1% agarose gel stained with GelRed (Biotium, California, USA). Once positive reactions were obtained, the sequencing service at Macrogen (https://dna.macrogen.com/) was used to purify and sequence each sample. Contigs were assembled, and base-calling differences were resolved using Geneious 8.1.8 software.

Phylogenetic analyses

The new ITS rDNA and cox 2 mtDNA sequences were compared with other sequences available in GenBank using BLAST. Some sequences available for both molecular markers were downloaded and incorporated into two databases: one for ITS and the other for cox 2. Gyrodactylus mojarrae was used as outgroup for the analysis. The species added to the phylogenetic analyses were: G. turnbulli (ITS: AJ001846; cox 2: KP168391, KP168394, KP168403, KP168404, KP168406, KP168410); G. guatopotei (ITS: MK912263; cox 2: MN927193); G. marplatensis (ITS: MK965393; cox 2: MN927194, MN927195); G. decemmaculati (ITS: MK299423, MK299424, MK299425, MK312457, MK312458; cox 2: MN066554, MN066555, MN066556); G. poeciliae (ITS: AJ001844; cox 2: KP168345, KP168285, KP168327, KP168328, KP168329, KP168335, KP168344); G. xtachuna (ITS: KM514446, KM514442, KM514445; cox 2: PZ316034, PZ316035); G. bullatarudis (ITS: AY692024, AJ011410; cox 2: KP168379, KP168380, KP168350, KP168373); G. pseudobullatarudis (ITS: KM514436, KM514439; cox 2: PZ316036, PZ316037); G. breviradix (ITS: MK312260, MK312259, MK965396, MK965397, MK965395; cox 2: MN927192, MN066552, MN066553); G. chiapaneco (ITS: MK912265, MK912264; cox 2: PX098335); G. tlaloci (ITS: PX096128, MK912268, MK912267; cox 2: PX098461, PX098462, PX098463); and G. zapoteco (ITS: MG883704; cox 2: PX098452, PX098453). The independent databases were aligned using MEGA12 software available at: https://www.megasoftware.net/ (Kumar et al. 2024) with the MUSCLE V5.1 algorithm (Edgar 2004) and the default parameter settings. The alignments were manually reviewed and edited to obtain the highest possible quality of genetic sequence data for each gene. Once the alignment was completed, the evolutionary model for each database was calculated using JModelTest Ver. 2.1.10 software (Posada 2008) according to the Akaike information criterion (AIC). ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) implemented in the IQ-TREE 2 software (Minh et al. 2020) available at http://www.iqtree.org/, was also used to estimate the optimal evolutionary model. Subsequently, phylogenetic relationships were inferred using Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) trees for the ITS and cox 2 markers and the concatenated dataset (ITS + cox 2).

ML phylogenetic trees were generated using IQ-TREE 2 and RaxML V8.2.12 software (Stamatakis 2014) available at: https://github.com/stamatak/standard-RAxML with 1000 bootstrap replicates. Bayesian Inference trees were constructed using MrBayes Ver. 3.2.7a software (Ronquist et al. 2012). The configuration parameters modified in the BI analysis were 10,000,000 MCMC generations, 4 chains, a temperature of 0.2, and a sampling frequency of every 1000 generations. The fraction discarded as burn-in was 25%. The model calculated using Jmodeltest was added for each individual marker and the concatenated dataset. Chain convergence was visualized with Tracer software Ver. 1.7.2 (Rambaut et al. 2018) to verify that the effective sample size (ESS) parameter value was greater than 200. Values from additional independent runs were added to improve the ESS. Posterior probabilities for the clades were obtained using the majority consensus rule (50%), excluding the 25% burn-in. For visualization and editing of the phylogenetic trees, the software FigTree V.1.4.4 (Rambaut 2018) was used, which is available at: https://github.com/rambaut/figtree/releases. The genetic divergence among species of Gyrodactylus was estimated using Kimura two-parameter (K2P) distances in MEGA12 (Kumar et al. 2024). Phylogenetic trees and matrices are deposited in Mendeley Data at: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/f9nr8b8tvz/1.

Ecological analyses

We are aware that ecological parameters defined by Bush et al. (1997) refer to parasite species; and that the highest possible taxonomic resolution should be used for ecological analyses of parasite communities (Poulin 2019). However, as these authors specify, some ecological parameters referring to parasite counts per host can under certain circumstances be applied to taxonomic groups. Poulin (2019) suggests that the only instance when parasite counts can involve pooled parasite species is when: (1) the question posed justifies this approach; and (2) the species pooled are taxonomically very close. We pooled all Gyrodactylus spp. recovered from each collection site: (1) to assess and contrast parasite counts for different localities within river basins; and (2) considered that the species pooled are congeneric, i.e., very close taxonomically – thus fulfilling Poulin’s criteria. We calculated the mean abundance of Gyrodactylus spp. for each locality, by dividing the total number of members of the genus Gyrodactylus by the total number of hosts examined. Mean abundance of Gyrodactylus spp. was the only parameter that we could analyze because of the impossibility (1) to accurately quantify the number of Gyrodactylus on each host to calculate both prevalence and mean intensity of infection for each sampled locality, as parasites often dislodge during transport; (2) to delimit taxonomically 100% of collected parasites without molecular characterization of individual worms (e.g. Razo-Mendivil et al. 2016; Pozos-Carré et al. 2025); and (3) to calculate the variance to mean ratio to describe parasite overdispersion, in the absence of intensity of infection values. The mean abundance of Gyrodactylus spp. was used to compare the number of parasites in the natural and translocated distribution areas of the host P. bimaculatus. The calculation and comparison of mean abundances were carried out using the Excel X (Microsoft Office) software package.

Translocation of Gyrodactylus spp. by Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus

With records and evidence of multiple translocation events of twospot livebearer to central Mexico and along the Pacific Ocean slope, it was hypothesized that Gyrodactylus spp. were also translocated alongside their host. To test this hypothesis, a haplotype network was constructed for Gyrodactylus species using cytochrome oxidase subunit II (cox 2) sequences, which were mapped onto the natural and introduced distribution of P. bimaculatus following the studies by Miller et al. (2005) and records by Gaspar-Dillanes (1987) for this host. The sequences of each species, aligned with MEGA12, were formatted with DnaSPV6 (Rozas et al. 2017), in which the “mtDNA flatworm” genetic code assignment available in the software was used for analysis. The sequence set was divided into groups according to the corresponding hydrographic systems. Haplotype analysis was performed including invariant sites, and a haplotype matrix was built for the “Traits” configuration using the data obtained. Based on this information for Gyrodactylus, haplotype networks were constructed using the PopArt program (Leigh and Bryant 2015) employing the “TCS Network” method (Clement et al. 2000), and were mapped in QGIS.

Results

Parasite collection and ecological data

Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus (n = 1047) were collected from 72 localities on the two versants of Mexico, in six rivers draining into the Gulf of Mexico (Atlantic Ocean) and six to the Pacific Ocean (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1).

A total of 561 gyrodactylids were recovered, and at genus level (Gyrodactylus spp.) infection parameters were comparable on both sides of the continental divide: in the Gulf of Mexico drainages, no parasites were recorded in 30% (16/53) of localities sampled and the mean parasite abundance in the remaining localities was 0.82 worms/host (range 0.15–3.08); in rivers flowing to the Pacific Ocean, no parasites were recorded in 26% (5/19) of localities sampled and the mean parasite abundance in the remaining localities was 0.76 worms/host (range 0.1–4.15). The mean abundance of Gyrodactylus spp. infection recorded in different localities on both watersheds of the country is shown in Fig. 2. Although no parameters could be calculated to quantify aggregation (see materials and methods), parasite distribution appears to be overdispersed both within river basins and between hosts: for instance, various localities in river Papaloapan had abundances of zero (not shown in Fig. 2, but see Supplementary Table S1), some very low (e.g., localities Papaloapan 12 and 8) and others intermediate (e.g., localities Papaloapan 16 and 1) values, and also included some of the highest recorded mean abundance values (Papaloapan 18). Individual parasite burdens also appear to be overdispersed (Supplementary Table S1): no parasites were recorded from individual fish in several localities in river Papaloapan (e.g., sites 13–15), some had quite low values (e.g., Papaloapan 12, where 1 parasite was recorded from 24 hosts), some had intermediate (e.g., Papaloapan 25, where 20 parasites were recovered from 17 hosts), and some had higher values (e.g., Papaloapan 18, where 3 worms were recovered from 1 host).

Molecular data

A total of 61 partial mitochondrial DNA cox 2 gene sequences were generated for Gyrodactylus spp. parasitizing P. bimaculatus on both sides of the continental divide (Supplementary Table S2). The approximate length of these sequences was 256 bp. In addition, 39 sequences from other Gyrodactylus species available in GenBank were added to our dataset; these represent phylogenetically close species that parasitize hosts from the Poeciliidae and Profundulidae families. Once the database was aligned with the outgroup, 101 sequences with lengths of 262 bp were obtained. The alignment contained 85 conserved, 177 variable, and 166 parsimony-informative sites. On the other hand, a total of 60 partial internal transcribed spacer (ITS1-2 rDNA) sequences were generated. The approximate length of these sequences was 870 bp. These new sequences were aligned with 42 Gyrodactylus spp. sequences available in GenBank that parasitize hosts from the Poeciliidae and Profundulidae families. After aligning the database, 102 sequences with an approximate length of 1012 bp were obtained. The alignment contained 295 conserved, 696 variable, and 511 parsimony-informative sites.

Phylogenetic analyses

cox 2 tree: The phylogenetic hypothesis using Bayesian inference for cox 2 recovered 10 clades/species that parasitize P. bimaculatus, including eight nominal species: G. bullatarudis, G. chiapaneco, G. mojarrae, G. pseudobullatarudis, G. takoke, G. xalapensis, G. xtachuna, and Gyrodactylus sp. lineage “montana”, as well as two undescribed species, Gyrodactylus sp. 1 and Gyrodactylus sp. 2. The clades corresponding to these ten species were recovered with high posterior probability values (pp > 0.99). Phylogenetic relationships among these 10 species that parasitize P. bimaculatus and other Gyrodactylus species parasitizing poeciliid and profundulid fishes, were not resolved; particularly the relationships between species and nodes near the root (Fig. 3). The interspecific divergence values (K2P) for the cox 2 gene ranged from 8.80% to 53.46% (G. chiapaneco vs. G. mojarrae with the highest values), and the intraspecific values reached up to 16.26% divergence (with G. pseudobullatarudis having the highest value) (Supplementary Table S3).

cox 2 sequence phylogenetic tree for Gyrodactylus spp. recovered from Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus in the two versants of Mexico

ITS tree: The phylogenetic hypothesis for the ITS rDNA recovered only seven Gyrodactylus species that parasitize P. bimaculatus, including G. chiapaneco, G. mojarrae, G. pseudobullatarudis, G. xalapensis, G. xtachuna, Gyrodactylus sp. lineage “montana” and Gyrodactylus sp. 1. These species formed monophyletic groupings with relatively high posterior probability values (pp > 0.91). The phylogenetic relationships among Gyrodactylus species that parasitize P. bimaculatus, as well as other poeciliids and profundulids, are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Compared to the phylogenetic hypothesis of the cox 2 gene, the ITS hypothesis did not recover G. bullatarudis, G. takoke, nor Gyrodactylus sp. 2. The highest percentage of intraspecific divergence (K2P) calculated for the ITS marker is 1.29% in the species G. takoke, while the highest reported interspecific divergence values are between G. chiapaneco and G. mojarrae (ranging from 75.11% to 78.05%). The lowest interspecific divergence was 1.09% between G. xtachuna and Gyrodactylus sp. 1 (Supplementary Table S3).

Concatenated tree (ITS + cox 2): The phylogenetic hypothesis based on the concatenated data recovered nine species that parasitize P. bimaculatus in Mexico: G. chiapaneco, G. mojarrae, G. pseudobullatarudis, G. takoke, G. xalapensis, G. xtachuna, Gyrodactylus sp. lineage “montana”, Gyrodactylus sp. 1, and Gyrodactylus sp. 2. Regarding previous phylogenetic hypotheses using cox 2 and ITS, G. bullatarudis is not represented in this concatenated hypothesis because no ITS sequences are available for this species. A group of species composed of G. pseudobullatarudis, G. xtachuna, Gyrodactylus sp. lineage “montana”, and Gyrodactylus sp. 1 were retrieved as phylogenetically close species that parasitize P. bimaculatus and other poeciliid and profundulid fishes. On the other hand, three species (G. takoke, G. xalapensis and Gyrodactylus sp. 2) are retrieved as monophyletic groups that show a clear geographic structure, where Gyrodactylus sp. 2 restricts its distribution to the Coatzacoalcos River hydrological system, G. xalapensis is distributed in Lake Catemaco and the La Antigua River, and finally, G. takoke is widely distributed in most of the sampled systems, forming small subclades that are phylogeographically consistent with their distribution area, except for possible cases of translocation (Fig. 4).

Translocation and haplotype network

Of the 10 parasite species identified, only G. chiapaneco, G. pseudobullatarudis, G. takoke and Gyrodactylus sp. lineage “montana” were found on both sides of the continental divide (Fig. 4).

Gyrodactylus takoke was the most abundant species recorded (37/60 worms sequenced) in this survey and a haplotype network was constructed with its cox 2 sequences (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Table S4). Analysis of the geographical distribution of the 10 G. takoke haplotypes recovered indicates that two haplotypes (Hap_3, Hap_6) were recorded on both sides of the continental divide. Haplotype Hap_6 was particularly abundant and was recorded in P. bimaculatus’ native range in central Veracruz, as well as in several localities within two different basins on the Pacific Ocean versant, the Atoyac-Verde river catchment located in southern Mexico and the more northwesterly Balsas river basin (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

Gyrodactylus spp. infecting Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus in Mexico

Four native and two invasive gyrodactylid species had been reported to infect twospot livebearer in Mexico (Table 1), and this study increases that figure to 11 by adding five taxa – three known and two undescribed species. This survey also provides data to analyze the geographical distribution of several species of Gyrodactylus across the country, both in the Gulf of Mexico and across the continental divide, on the Pacific Ocean watershed.

This investigation increases the known distribution of five parasite species, of which three (G. bullatarudis, G. xalapensis, G. xtachuna) were found in previously unrecorded river basins draining into the Gulf of Mexico in the state of Veracruz, and two (G. chiapaneco, G. takoke) were recorded in different basins emptying into the Pacific Ocean.

Regarding previously recorded species, both G. xalapensis and G. xtachuna are restricted to rivers draining into the Gulf of Mexico. The introduced species G. bullatarudis, originally a parasite of guppy P. reticulata in Trinidad, had been previously recorded from P. bimaculatus in the La Antigua river (Salgado-Maldonado et al. 2014) and we recovered it in the Papaloapan river basin. The specimens we characterized molecularly (cox 2) correspond to G. bullatarudis lineage II recorded in Trinidad (Xavier et al. 2015); this supports the hypothesis that this is an invasive parasite, co-translocated from the Antilles with its host – and it will be interesting to determine whether the cryptic G. bullatarudis lineage I recorded in Trinidad has likewise been translocated.

Gyrodactylus takoke is a parasite of poeciliids in their native distribution range in the Gulf of Mexico (García-Vásquez et al. 2015), where it was previously recorded from P. bimaculatus in three river basins, and from porthole livebearer Poeciliopsis gracilis (Heckel, 1848) in one basin. In this survey, G. takoke was recorded widely in several basins, on both sides of the continental divide – this suggests it may be part of the “biogeographical core parasite fauna” of the twospot livebearer, sensu Pérez-Ponce de León & Choudhury (Pérez-Ponce de León and Choudhury 2005).

Gyrodactylus chiapaneco is a parasite of profundulid fishes in the Río Grijalva basin, which drains into the Atlantic Ocean, and was previously recorded from P. bimaculatus in the Río Lerma basin, which drains into the Pacific Ocean (García-Vásquez et al. 2019). In this study, we found it infecting twospot livebearer in the Río Papaloapan basin (Gulf of Mexico) and in three basins on the Pacific Ocean slope, in rivers Tehuantepec, Atoyac-Verde and Balsas. This species is common in poeciliids and is also able to infect characid and dorosomatid fishes (Pinacho-Pinacho et al. 2026).

New parasite records for the twospot livebearer include G. pseudobullatarudis, G. mojarrae, Gyrodactylus sp. lineage “montana” and Gyrodactylus sp. 1 and Gyrodactylus sp. 2. Gyrodactylus pseudobullatarudis was recorded on both slopes of the country, in Río Jamapa (Gulf of Mexico) and Río Balsas (Pacific Ocean). This species is phylogenetically close and morphologically similar to G. bullatarudis, with which it may form a complex of cryptic species.

This survey represents the first record of G. mojarrae infecting P. bimaculatus. This gyrodactylid has previously only been recorded from cichlid fishes in southern Mexico (Mendoza-Palmero et al. 2019; Pinacho-Pinacho et al. 2026) Gyrodactylus sp. lineage “montana” was recorded from twospot livebearer on the two watersheds of the continent. Previously, this gyrodactylid had been recorded from profundulid fishes in rivers draining into the Pacific Ocean (Calixto-Rojas et al. 2025).

Finally, undescribed Gyrodactylus sp. 1 and Gyrodactylus sp. 2 were both recorded in the high reaches of the Río Coatzacoalcos basin, on the Gulf of Mexico catchment.

Ecological data

Previous studies analyzing the parasite fauna of P. bimaculatus throughout Río La Antigua from its mouth into the Gulf of Mexico up to elevations > 1400 m above sea level (m.a.s.l.), indicate that monogeneans are the most prevalent, abundant and widely distributed helminth group in the basin, with these flatworms recorded in eight out of 11 sampling sites and four monogenean species constituting 43% of all helminth species identified (Salgado-Maldonado et al. 2020). Both G. takoke and G. xalapensis are common in the Río La Antigua basin, and all Gyrodactylus spp. are particularly abundant at medium elevations (ca. 1100 m.a.s.l.). Recently, an analysis of parasite metacommunities identified medium-elevation localities in Río La Antigua as potential fish parasite diversity hotspots (López-del-Monte et al. 2025). Helminth metacommunities associated with P. bimaculatus were shown to be strongly structured along environmental gradients, and to contain a core group of widespread helminth species characterized by high ecological tolerance, broad dispersal and transmission capacities, and occurrence across multiple hosts and sites – both G. takoke and G. xalapensis are included in this core group.

Other than the higher abundance recorded at medium elevations in Río La Antigua basin (Salgado-Maldonado et al. 2020), parasite burdens seem to be stochastically distributed, as illustrated by the apparently random distribution of parasite burdens in different localities within the same river basins depicted in Fig. 2 – clearly, no basin has remarkably higher parasite burdens. Moreover, parasites recorded here for 12 different river basins across the country are apparently aggregated: hosts in 26–30% of localities harbored no parasites, and in localities where infections were recorded, most fish had no or only a few parasites while a handful of hosts carry most of the parasite population (Poulin 2026) – this strongly overdispersed distribution has been shown to be an almost-universal characteristic of helminth parasite infections, and may be strongly shaped by host immunity (Tinsley et al. 2020, 2025; Ieshko et al. 2024).

Translocation

The introduction of non-native, invasive fishes is one of the leading causes of biodiversity decline and disruption of freshwater aquatic ecosystems (Gomez-Maldonado et al. 2023; Esposito et al. 2024; Díaz-Morales et al. 2025). Apart from the direct effects caused by translocated fishes, their co-translocated parasites can also exert several different ecological impacts (Llaberia-Robledillo et al. 2022; Díaz-Morales et al. 2025; de Moraes et al. 2025; Tkachenko et al. 2025). Parasitological information on both the native and the new distribution ranges of translocated hosts is essential to characterize parasite-host interactions in invaded ecosystems. Here, we present a perspective of the gyrodactylid species infecting twospot livebearer on both sides of Mexico’s continental divide and provide evidence suggesting that three types of ecological host-parasite interactions may occur following the translocation of P. bimaculatus to the Pacific Ocean versant.

Enemy release

The first potential ecological impact of the translocation of P. bimaculatus is enemy release, where the invasive host has an advantage as it is freed from its natural enemies in the recipient environment (Torchin et al. 2003). Natural enemies include parasites and this poeciliid has been recorded to host (depending on the marker used for species delimitation) nine (concatenated tree) or 10 (cox 2) gyrodactylid taxa in its native range in Atlantic Ocean drainages (García-Vásquez et al. 2015; this study). In contrast, only four parasite species were found in its invasive range – of which only G. takoke was recorded at several localities and in comparatively higher numbers, albeit parasite abundance was low overall. Considering that G. xalapensis and G. xtachuna are prevalent, abundant and distributed in several basins draining into the Atlantic Ocean (Rubio-Godoy et al. 2010; García-Vásquez et al. 2015; Salgado-Maldonado et al. 2020), it is conceivable that these species were co-translocated (transported) with their hosts and were also introduced to the Pacific Ocean versant, but apparently failed to establish.

Although high host mortality has been observed in experimental settings (e.g., Houde 1997), there are few examples documenting the impact of gyrodactylid infection on wild poeciliid (or other) fishes – among these is the demonstration that G. turnbulli and G. bullatarudis have a significant impact on the survival and migration of wild guppies (P. reticulata), particularly males (van Oosterhout et al. 2007). Thus, if the gyrodactylids recorded in its native range do exert a negative impact on the twospot livebearer, translocated fish are mostly free from this particular impact as most parasite species were lost during transport/failed to establish, which could give hosts an advantage facilitating their success as invasive species (Torchin et al. 2003; Díaz-Morales et al. 2025).

Spill-over

Gyrodactylus takoke was successfully co-translocated with the twospot livebearer and apparently managed to establish and spread, as indicated by its presence in two basins draining into the Pacific Ocean –suggesting this gyrodactylid, like its fish host, has become invasive. The same applies to guppy, P. reticulata and its parasite G. turnbulli, which have both established across Mexico (Contreras-MacBeath et al. 1998; Mendoza Alfaro and Koleff Osorio 2014); and G. bullatarudis has spilled-over to native poeciliids including twospot liveberarer (Salgado-Maldonado et al. 2014), as confirmed here with the demonstration that parasites characterized molecularly in Mexico are similar to those collected in Trinidad (Xavier et al. 2015). We hypothesize that given their wide distribution and relatively low host specificity, both G. turnbulli and G. takoke may spill-over to poeciliids – and potentially other fishes, considering the first gyrodactylid has been shown to also infect other non-poeciliid cyprinodontiformes (Cable et al. 2013).

In this survey we focused on the twospot livebearer, so we cannot assess the presence of gyrodactylids on other poeciliids introduced to central Mexico (Contreras-MacBeath et al. 1998), which include guppy P. reticulata, platy Xiphophorus variatus (Meek, 1904), swordtail X. hellerii, shortfin molly Poecilia mexicana Steindachner, 1863 and porthole livebearer P. gracilis – all of which host different species of Gyrodactylus. There is a high probability that some poeciliid fish-gyrodactylid parasite associations have persisted after their translocation to central Mexico, considering that some of these fishes have conserved their gyrodactylids following their introduction to distant places; for instance, mosquito fish Gambusia affinis (Baird & Girard, 1853) has kept its association with Gyrodactylus gambusiae in New Zealand (Renner and Duggan 2023); the guppy P. reticulata with G. bullatarudis in India (Tripathi et al. 2025); and the ten spotted livebearer Cnesterodon decemmaculatus (Jenyns, 1842) with 4 gyrodactylid species following its introduction to Argentinian Patagonia (Vega et al. 2019; Taglioretti et al. 2020). Further, although gyrodactylids are assumed to be specialists with a narrow host range, when this has been tested, some species have been shown to be generalists able to infect fish from different species, genera or even families (King and Cable 2007; Calixto-Rojas et al. 2025), which facilitates host switches and thus spill-over events. The role of the twospot livebearer and other poeciliid fishes in the potential overspill of parasites is not necessarily limited to their “natural” parasites, considering that the African tilapia parasite G. cichlidarum has been recorded infecting poeciliid fishes on both versants of Mexico (García-Vásquez et al. 2017).

Spill-back

In this survey, G. chiapaneco was recorded at low abundance (n = 4) in the twospot livebearer’s invasive range on the Pacific Ocean watershed, and a single specimen was recovered from the Gulf of Mexico versant. This gyrodactylid is a parasite of profundulid fishes in Chiapas (southeastern Mexico, in the Pacific Ocean versant), suggesting its primary host may be a fish other than P. bimaculatus; and that the twospot livebearer acquired this gyrodactylid following translocation. Similarly, Gyrodactylus sp. lineage “montana” was recorded in this study at very low abundance on both versants of the country, and this taxon is associated with profundulid fishes in the Río Atoyac-Verde which drains into the Pacific Ocean (Calixto-Rojas et al. 2025). These observations support the hypothesis that profundulid fishes are the primary hosts of these gyrodactylids, and that both spilled-back to poeciliids following their translocation.

We acknowledge that our data are insufficient to properly support or refute any of these scenarios, but they provide a baseline to assess these hypothetical ecological outcomes of the translocation of fish hosts and their parasites – which is particularly relevant given the recognized status of the twospot livebearer as one of the most successful invasive freshwater fishes and responsible for serious ecological impacts like the almost complete replacement of the native fish fauna in localities in Central Mexico (Córdova-Tapia et al. 2024). The potential role of the twopost livebearer’s associated and co-translocated parasites has not been properly investigated – particularly gyrodactylids, whose direct transmission between hosts is favored by the twospot livebearer’s con- and heterospecific social shoaling behavior (Salazar-Rueda et al. 2024). Gyrodactylids are among the most successfully invasive and potentially pathogenic fish parasites known (Huyse et al. 2015; Vancheva et al. 2020; Shinn et al. 2023; Maduenyane et al. 2023; Esposito et al. 2024), which makes their study a relevant topic.
