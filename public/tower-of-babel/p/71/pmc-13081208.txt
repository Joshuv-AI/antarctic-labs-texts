A Demonstration of Propensity-Score Weighting to Adjust a Social Media Nonprobability Sample Survey of Political Attitudes
Authors: Michael S Pollard, Michael W Robbins, Max Griswold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterest in using nonprobability online samples continues to grow despite concerns about selection bias. Many methods exist for adjusting nonprobability data so it may yield generalizable inferences. Here we investigate whether a propensity score weighting method can balance differences between a probability sample and a nonprobability sample of Twitter (now X) users to evaluate the feasibility of using social media data for producing generalizable inferences on public opinion. We fielded identical surveys to 2,001 probability-sampled respondents (June 30–July 22, 2022) and 949 Twitter users (March 1–July 13, 2022); final analytic sample sizes were 1,972 and 822, respectively. The nonprobability sample differed significantly in demographic characteristics (younger, lower income, higher educational attainment), and broadly endorsed significantly more liberal attitudes toward a range of political and policy issues than the probability sample. We show that the propensity score weighting procedure, using demographics, techno/psychographics, and political ideology, reconciles differences between the samples for 25 of the 27 attitudes assessed. The results demonstrate the feasibility and utility of the propensity score weighting procedure to replicate a probability sample with nonprobability social media data and add to the literature on the use of nonprobability samples to draw population-level inferences.

Introduction

Large social media data sources such as Twitter (now X) are promising means to gauge public opinion. Efforts to use social media data for this purpose are hindered since social media users constitute a nonprobability sample and may not accurately reflect national demographic groups. Probability samples (even those with very low response rates) continue to be the most accurate method for assessing public opinion (Dutwin and Buskirk 2017; MacInnis et al. 2018) and are the most likely to yield generalizable inferences about a population of interest.

Differences between a population of interest and the respondents who take a survey may induce non-response bias, and as such may negatively impact the ability to draw inferences from the survey data (Groves 2006; Peytchev 2013; Wright 2015). The AAPOR Task Force on Online Panels concluded that researchers “should avoid nonprobability opt-in panels when a key research objective is to accurately estimate population values” (Baker et al. 2010), and the AAPOR Task Force on Nonprobability Sampling noted that numerous obstacles, such as selection bias, are inherent to most nonprobability sampling methods (Baker et al. 2013).

However, the factors driving the appeal of nonprobability online sampling methods that were noted by the AAPOR Task Force on Online Panels in 2010 are even more salient today; in the face of increasing nonresponse in traditional methods and continued increases in internet penetration, they can be conducted on shorter cycles, and at lower costs (Baker et al. 2010). This has contributed to increasing interest in using social media as a way to recruit survey respondents (Reveilhac, Steinmetz, and Morselli 2022; Vicente 2023) despite repeated evidence that nonprobability survey data are less accurate than data collected from probability samples when measuring demographics, consumption behaviors, public opinion, and political attitudes and intentions (Malhotra and Krosnick 2007; Chang and Krosnick 2009; Couper 2013; Szolnoki and Hoffmann 2013; Sturgis et al. 2016).

Twenty-two percent of American adults used Twitter/X in 2023 (McClain, Anderson, and Gelles-Watnick 2024), and most use one or more social media platforms (Auxier and Anderson 2021). These platforms can provide relatively low-cost and convenient sources of survey recruits. However, it is well established that the demographics of social media users differ from the national population, and that social media surveys are not typically generalizable beyond users of that platform (Vicente 2023).

Multiple methods exist for adjusting nonprobability data so it may yield generalizable inferences, and recent evidence suggests weighting techniques may be the most promising (Lee 2006; Lee and Valliant 2009; Elliott et al. 2010; Elliott and Valliant 2017). These approaches commonly use methods such as propensity scores (PSs) (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983) or entropy balancing (Hainmueller 2012) to account for demographic differences between two samples to enhance comparability.

We aim to determine how opinions on public policy and political issues differ between a nonprobability and a probability sample and then investigate how weighting methods can balance differences between the two samples. Using a probability survey of American adults as a benchmark of the general US adult population and an analogous, contemporaneous survey of Twitter users, we apply PS weighting techniques (Elliott et al. 2010; Robbins, Ghosh-Dastidar, and Ramchand 2021) to evaluate the feasibility of using social media data for producing generalizable inferences on public opinion.

Survey Data

Our probability sample was recruited from NORC’s AmeriSpeak panel. Separately, we constructed a nonprobability sample by recruiting participants through promoted Tweets on Twitter. Respondents provided informed consent online, and study materials and procedures were approved by the study’s institutional review board. The IRB determined that the consent language precludes sharing of data (even in anonymized form), given that they did not explicitly consent to data sharing, as well as the statements “No information pertaining to you specifically (either your survey responses or publicly available Twitter posts) will be listed in any of our reports or publications” and “All reporting of survey data will be done of the group of participants as a whole.”

Probability Sample

AmeriSpeak is a probability-based panel of 43,000 US households. Participating households are selected at random from across the United States to form a representative cross-section of US households. These sampled households are then contacted by US mail, telephone, and field interviewers to be recruited into the panel. Once included, they are then invited to participate in online and telephone surveys (NORC 2024). Some 2,001 panelists (7,016 invited) ages 18 and older completed the survey from June 30 through July 22, 2022. The survey-level response rate was 28.5 percent. The weighted AAPOR 3 response rate (American Association for Public Opinion Research 2023) to the recruitment is 19.5 percent, and the weighted household panel retention rate is 77.0 percent, yielding a weighted cumulative response rate of 4.3 percent. The margin of error for the weighted sample of 2,001 respondents was plus or minus 2.8 percentage points. NORC derives panel study-specific weights to adjust for the probability of selection into the panel, panel recruitment nonresponse, probability of being invited to the survey, and to match population demographic and voting behavior benchmarks (NORC 2024). To be included in the final analytic sample, respondents must have answered at least half of the questions, resulting in 1,972 respondents.

Nonprobability Sample

We collected roughly 20 million Twitter usernames using the Twitter “Spritzer” Application Programming Interface (API) to query the 1 percent Twitter sample stream over the span of six months in 2021. Because the API provides a sample of Tweets rather than users, the data heavily overrepresent users who post frequently.1

We then collected profile information for these usernames to identify users reporting a US location. These accounts were targeted for recruitment via promoted tweets on Twitter to participate in an online survey. The accounts were shown an ad reading “Share your opinions to receive a $15 Amazon.com Gift Card” and included an image of the gift card. Users who clicked the ad were directed to a landing page with additional information about the survey, human subjects protection information, and other study details. From this page, participants could begin the survey if they submitted a username that matched a username from the invited list to ensure that respondents were only those included in the sampling frame and to limit repeat participation from users seeking the incentive.2 Participants were recruited between March 1 and July 13, 2022.3 Of a total of 949 survey completions (0.3 percent of all accounts shown the ad), 822 comprise our analytic nonprobability sample after meeting eligibility requirements and completing more than half of the questions.4

Measures

The same survey (included in Appendix A) was given to both samples. It elicited sociodemographic and COVID-19 vaccination information, attitudes concerning a range of current public policies and political issues, perceptions of critical threats to the United States, and opinions on specific politicians.

There are substantial differences between the samples in demographics, vaccination status, technology use, and political ideology. The nonprobability sample is younger, more male, less non-Hispanic White, and lower income but more likely to hold a college degree. The nonprobability sample is more likely to report having received the COVID vaccine and a booster. Only 17.3 percent of the probability sample reported using Twitter daily, compared to 97.6 percent of the nonprobability sample. Similarly, time spent using technology was lower in the probability sample. The probability sample also reported being less likely to agree they “try new products before others do” and “when I shop I look for what is new.” The nonprobability sample identified as liberal/extremely liberal substantially more than the probability sample.

Propensity Score Weighting to Achieve Representativeness

To align the two samples, we apply the PS weighting procedure (Elliott et al. 2010; Robbins, Ghosh-Dastidar, and Ramchand 2021), which uses a set of auxiliary variables (xi for case i) to adjust for differences between the nonprobability and probability samples. We assume the sampling mechanism for the nonprobability sample is ignorable with respect to the auxiliary variables (meaning that conditional on the auxiliary variables, the probabilistic distribution of a survey outcome of interest, yi, is the same in both samples). Next, we used a logistic regression to estimate the PSs γi, which is the likelihood of case i being contained within the nonprobability sample, given that it is contained within one of the two samples and given its values of the auxiliary variables xi. For the auxiliary variables, we use: age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, income, political ideology, vaccination status, time spent using technology, and how much they like to try new products and look for what’s new. Variances are estimated using a jackknife, as suggested by Elliott and Valliant (2017).

The bars displayed in the figure correspond to the 95 percent confidence interval (CI) on the respective estimated difference. If CIs were to not overlap with zero following PS adjustments, that would be evidence that the ignorability assumption required by the procedure is not met (i.e., the set of auxiliary variables used was insufficient).

Differences in sample means are substantially reduced following adjustment. Before adjustment, there are significant differences in average responses between the two samples for 24 out of 27 survey questions. All but two attitudes become non-significantly different after the weighting procedure (censorship of social media users, which increases mean difference, and the critical threat posed by terrorism). CIs are wider when weighting adjustments are made, which is commonly observed when using propensity weights (Duffy et al. 2005). This loss of precision is due to the design effect induced by weighting (which can be large when the nonprobability sample is notably different from the probability sample). Two items (China and Taliban critical threats) change only slightly, but the CIs now include zero. However, the lack of statistical significance differences in responses is not primarily due to the wider CIs; with three exceptions (support social media user censorship, Russian’s military power, and foreign interference in US elections), the point estimates of the differences are systematically closer to zero after weighting.

Political ideology is the most important variable within the PS model for weighting (i.e., 14 attitudinal items remain significantly different between the samples when we exclude political ideology from the propensity weight model and the K-S p-value drops to <0.0001). Income and time spent using technology are the next most important items for inclusion. The DEFF increases as variables are added into the blending model—this is as expected since alignment across additional characteristics will likely yield weights of higher variance.

Discussion and Conclusion

This study demonstrated the feasibility of the PS weighting procedure to replicate a probability sample with social media data, adding to a growing literature on the use of nonprobability samples to draw population-level inferences. It may be possible to recruit relatively inexpensive samples via social media and supplement them with small sets of contemporaneous data from a probability sample to derive inferences that can be generalized beyond the nonprobability sample.

In contrast to our results, a recent review of the use of propensity weights on nonprobability samples (Cornesse et al. 2020) concluded that although bias was generally reduced, propensity weights were not sufficient to address differences between nonprobability and probability samples in most studies. It is possible that weighting performed better here due to our choice of auxiliary variables. Political ideology in the PS weighting procedure accounted for the most items reconciled between the samples, and time spent using technology was third most impactful. Most previous studies rely only on demographic variables (Pasek 2016; Dutwin and Buskirk 2017). However, one (Mercer, Lau, and Kennedy 2018) also included a set of variables associated with political attitudes and engagement and was unable to reduce the majority of bias across 24 behavioral and attitudinal benchmarks—though it noted the political variables were more useful for adjusting differences in political and civic engagement topics than differences in personal finance questions.

The collection of our social media sample is subject to several limitations and potential concerns. First, although we attempted to limit the social media survey to specific accounts from our sample, we observed some clearly malicious behavior where individuals attempted to bypass our verification protocol by entering many usernames in order to match the invitation list. To limit this possibility, invitations were sent to subsets of the invitation list over time so that fewer accounts could proceed to the survey at any time. We cannot conclusively reject the notion that some attempts were successful.

How our study generalizes to other US social media platforms needs further investigation. The demographic compositions of users of other social media platforms are fluid and known to differ widely (Auxier and Anderson 2021). Whether the same set of auxiliary variables can successfully reconcile samples obtained from platforms such as Facebook, for example, is an empirical question. Further, the conservative Twitter user base expanded following the rebranding to X, although the platform is still primarily liberal (McClain, Anderson, and Gelles-Watnick 2024).

The surveys did not include nonpartisan questions on topics other than politics, and it is unknown whether the PS procedure used here would successfully replicate those types of items in the probability sample. Our preference was for parsimony, so we excluded higher-order interactions in the PS weighting model given the sufficient performance of the weighting procedure (although more complex PS models may have been precluded by the relatively small size of our nonprobability sample). Similarly, issues with precision of estimates from the nonprobability sample were exacerbated by variance inflation from weighting. A large increase in variance due to weighting may be necessary to reduce bias in nonprobability samples. Thus, we cannot conclusively reject the possibility that underlying differences between our two samples remain after weighting but are masked by the increase in variance. Replicating the procedure with larger samples and obtaining similar results would bolster conclusions about the ability of PS models to adjust social media data.

Despite these concerns and limitations, inclusion of weighting variables pertaining to the subject area and social media–based source of the sample showed strong potential in adjusting a nonprobability sample to mirror a benchmark probability sample.
