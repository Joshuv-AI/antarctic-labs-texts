Machine learning estimation of FVIII pharmacokinetic parameters in Chinese children with severe Hemophilia A
Authors: Yuntian Wang, Di Ai, Shuo Wang, Zekun Li, Gang Li, Yunqi Zhu, Yuqi Zhong, Wensheng Zhang, Runhui Wu, Zhenping Chen, Yongqiang Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hemophilia A is a rare inherited bleeding disorder typically managed with coagulation factor VIII (FVIII) replacement therapy. Designing personalized prophylactic regimens requires accurate pharmacokinetic (PK) characterization; current population PK (popPK) and Bayesian approaches provide a principled framework for individualized dosing, but their routine clinical implementation may still be constrained by model specification requirements and practical considerations in data collection and analysis. Here we present a machine learning (ML) framework, incorporating state-of-the-art language models, to predict individual FVIII PK parameters in pediatric patients. Using minimal sampling and routinely collected clinical data, our approach achieves superior performance over the widely adopted WAPPS-Hemo platform in predicting in vivo recovery (IVR) and FVIII half-life. These findings highlight the potential of AI-driven methods to reduce patient burden while improving accuracy in individualized treatment planning for children with severe hemophilia A.

Introduction

Hemophilia A is an X-linked recessive bleeding disorder caused by a deficiency of coagulation factor VIII (FVIII), with severe cases (FVIII < 1%) at high risk of spontaneous bleeding, chronic arthropathy, and reduced quality of life1. Although non-factor prophylactic agents, such as FVIIIa mimetics like emicizumab and rebalancing therapeutic products like fitusiran, which are utilized in routine clinical practice with less frequent subcutaneous doses2,3, FVIII replacement remains indispensable, particularly to control major bleeding or during surgery. Due to its predictable dose-response profile and global availability, FVIII prophylaxis continues to be the primary treatment worldwide4. Maintaining adequate FVIII activity above a defined threshold is essential for effective prophylaxis. However, significant variability in individual pharmacokinetic (PK), including differences in half-life, makes standard weight-based dosing suboptimal, risking both undertreatment and overtreatment5,6. Personalized PK profiling has therefore been recommended by the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) and the World Federation of Hemophilia (WFH) for tailoring prophylaxis7, enabling simulation-based adjustment of doses and intervals to achieve individualized FVIII levels8. Traditional PK assessment requires a long washout period (minimum 72 h) and a total of 5 to 11 blood samples drawn at specific time points following an infusion of FVIII with the subject in a non-bleeding state9. Such intensive sampling can be challenging for both caregivers and patients, particularly in children10. Population pharmacokinetic (popPK) models, first introduced by Sheiner et al. using nonlinear mixed-effects analysis11, reduce sampling burden via Bayesian forecasting12,13. Clinical tools like WAPPS-Hemo and MyPKFiT, allowing for sparse sampling are widely used for dose optimization14,15. Methodologically, Bayesian estimation relies on predefined parametric and structural assumptions, which provide interpretability and physiological grounding. A rich body of work has developed mechanistic models of the blood coagulation system, including reduced-complexity formulations that retain essential degrees of freedom and have been successfully applied in clinical settings16,17. Importantly, the motivation for exploring alternative modeling approaches in this study does not arise from theoretical shortcomings of these mechanistic frameworks. In fact, the predefined parametric and structural assumptions of Bayesian and PK modeling are a fundamental scientific strength: they reflect the inherent PK disposition of drugs across populations, and this structural consistency precisely enables principled generalization and extrapolation across different cohorts. Instead, under real-world clinical conditions characterized by sparse sampling, substantial inter-individual variability, heterogeneous patient features, and practical workflow constraints, the deployment and routine application of fully specified PK models may remain challenging in certain settings. These challenges are mainly contextual and operational, rather than conceptual. In this context, exploring complementary, data-efficient modeling approaches that can leverage clinical features and sparse samplings may provide additional flexibility for individualized PK assessment while reducing clinical burden, and further help bridge mechanistic PK understanding and data-driven inference.

Machine learning (ML) models are transforming biomedical prediction tasks by enabling data-driven modeling of complex, non-linear relationships. ML techniques, ranging from classical models18–23 to deep neural networks24–27, have shown promise in PK modeling, particularly to estimate drug concentration profiles and identify patient-specific variability28,29. However, their application in predicting the PK parameters of FVIII in hemophilia A remains unexplored, especially in pediatric settings where sample availability is limited. Recent developments in Transformer-based30 language models, such as BERT31 and GPT32, have expanded their applicability beyond natural language processing to structured clinical data in tabular format. When tabular data are serialized into sequences similar to natural language, language models can capture latent feature interactions and context-dependent patterns that traditional ML may ignore33–36. These capabilities are particularly attractive for small-sample, high-dimensional datasets common in rare disease contexts. Despite this potential, little prior work has explored the use of LMs to predict individual PK parameters in hemophilia. For instance, existing clinical tools such as WAPPS-Hemo rely on population PK frameworks and Bayesian estimation, which are well-established for individualized dosing; yet their routine clinical application may still be constrained by practical factors including software accessibility, workflow complexity, and the need for model-specific infrastructure.

In this study, we propose a novel machine learning framework to predict two critical PK parameters of FVIII, in vivo recovery (IVR) and half-life (HL) for children with severe hemophilia A. While conventional population PK approaches are specifically designed to operate under sparse sampling, they typically rely on mechanistic model specification and dedicated software platforms. In contrast, our method learns direct mappings from sparse clinical features to key PK parameters without explicit model formulation. We systematically compare a spectrum of models, from conventional machine learning algorithms to transformer-based language models adapted for tabular clinical data, and demonstrate that our approach performs competitively with population PK tools under the evaluated sparse-sampling settings (see Fig. 1). Results

We first evaluated the predictive performance of multiple machine learning models for estimating FVIII PK parameters in children with severe hemophilia A. Specifically, we developed six traditional machine learning models: Lasso, Ridge, Random Forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and FT-Transformer, as well as a fine-tuned language model (LM), to predict FVIII PK parameters in children with severe hemophilia A. In addition to data-driven models, we implemented a scientific machine learning (SciML) model for HL estimation, which embeds the exponential elimination dynamics into the training process. WAPPS-Hemo14, a widely used population PK tool in clinical practice, was included as a baseline comparison: for HL, estimation results are directly generated by WAPPS-Hemo; for IVR, we calculated IVR using the WAPPS-Hemo predicted FVIII activity corresponding to 1 h after infusion. To ensure robustness and reduce the impact of data-splitting variability, the data set was randomly partitioned 10 times into training and testing sets. For each split, the performance of the model was evaluated in the testing set, and the mean and standard deviation of key metrics were calculated. The results for the estimation of IVR and HL are shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. Across all evaluation settings, both traditional machine learning models and the language model consistently outperformed WAPPS-Hemo, demonstrating competitive predictive performance under the evaluated settings. In the IVR estimation task, all evaluated models demonstrated strong predictive capabilities, as reflected by their performance improvements over the WAPPS-Hemo baseline across multiple evaluation metrics. Notably, the traditional ML models, Lasso and Ridge regression, consistently outperformed not only the baseline method but also more complex models such as ensemble methods (Random Forest, XGBoost) and deep learning architectures (MLP, FT-Transformer, LM), particularly in terms of MAE and R2. From a clinical perspective, this pattern is reasonable, as IVR is a dose-normalized metric that primarily reflects early post-infusion response and is driven by a limited set of dominant factors. In such settings, simpler models are sufficient to capture the clinically relevant signal, whereas additional model complexity does not necessarily translate into improved performance, highlighting that model suitability depends on the clinical task, and that increased model complexity is not always advantageous for IVR estimation. In summary, the performance of ML approaches highlights their potential as complementary methods under the evaluated settings in this context.

In the HL estimation task, model performance was generally lower than in the IVR task, reflecting the increased complexity and variability associated with estimating this PK parameter. Several models exhibited diminished accuracy and reduced consistency across evaluation metrics. This decline highlights the inherent challenge of HL estimation, which may involve nonlinear relationships and greater individual variability. In addition, comparing performance between developed conventional ML models, the SciML model achieved predictive performance comparable to that of conventional ML baselines in HL estimation, demonstrating that incorporating PK structure does not compromise accuracy under the same sampling conditions. However, LM demonstrated a marked and consistent advantage in this task, outperforming every other model. In particular, its improvements in MAE and RMSE exceeded 90% over the WAPPS-Hemo baseline. Experimental results suggest the potential applicability of language models in PK-related tasks.

Across the evaluated experimental settings, ML models trained with three post-infusion FVIII measurements and corresponding activity levels demonstrated good predictive performance under sparse-sampling conditions, and in several settings achieved comparable or improved accuracy relative to the WAPPS-Hemo approach. Notably, linear models, despite their simplicity, achieved competitive performance, particularly for IVR estimation, reflecting the relatively limited information requirements of this clinical task. In contrast, HL estimation represents a more challenging problem, as it reflects elimination kinetics influenced by multiple interacting patient-specific factors. In this setting, more expressive models, including Transformer-based architectures, showed superior performance in our experiments. Overall, these results indicate that feasible PK parameter estimation may be achievable with reduced sampling burden, supporting the potential of machine learning approaches as a complementary and data-efficient option for individualized PK profiling in routine pediatric hemophilia clinical practice.

We further conducted residual analyses for both IVR and HL estimation tasks (Figs. 4 and 5). Linear regression and neural network models exhibited relatively low residual dispersion, suggesting stable predictive performance across the test samples. In contrast, ensemble-based models such as RF and XGB showed higher residual variability, indicating less consistent estimations. Notably, the neural networks and SciML model demonstrated residual distributions that are around zero with moderate dispersion, indicating stable error behavior without pronounced systematic bias. Compared with WAPPS-Hemo, these models exhibited reduced residual spread, suggesting improved individual-level consistency under sparse sampling conditions. Besides, it should be noted that the residual plot of the language model revealed a noticeable linear trend. While the overall variance remained low, this pattern suggests potential model bias, where estimations may be systematically over- or under-estimated depending on the true target range. This could indicate that the language model, despite its strong representation capacity, may not fully capture certain non-linear or latent patterns inherent in the data. Alternatively, the trend may reflect the influence of unobserved confounders or missing input variables that contribute to systematic error. These findings highlight the importance of integrating both performance metrics and residual diagnostics in model evaluation to uncover potential sources of bias and improve clinical interpretability. Notably, all cross-validation analyses in this study were performed within a single dataset, and cross-validation alone cannot fully preclude overfitting, particularly given the small sample size inherent to this rare pediatric disease cohort. For IVR estimation, SHAP analysis revealed that early post-infusion FVIII activity (1h FVIII activity) was the main contributor to model output, followed by dose-normalized exposure (IU/kg). This pattern is consistent with the clinical definition of IVR as a dose-derived metric. Baseline FVIII activity, age, and dose showed secondary contributions, while sampling time variables exhibited minimal impact, indicating that the model did not rely on a specific optimized time point but instead learned robust relationships between FVIII activity levels and IVR.

While HL estimation was primarily driven by late post-infusion FVIII activity (24 h FVIII activity), reflecting its dependence on the elimination phase of FVIII disposition. Baseline FVIII activity, age, blood group, and vWF:Ag level also ranked among the most influential features, aligning with established PK knowledge regarding individual variability in FVIII clearance. Notably, dose-related variables played a comparatively minor role in HL estimation, highlighting the mechanistic distinction between IVR estimation and HL modeling.

Overall, these interpretability results demonstrate that the FT-Transformer model captures clinically meaningful and plausible relationships rather than spurious correlations. The identified feature contributions may also provide useful insights for refining existing population PK models by highlighting features with strong influence on FVIII exposure and elimination.

Furthermore, to evaluate the influence of blood sampling strategies on model performance, we systematically evaluated all models developed under varying sampling conditions. To reflect realistic clinical practice and feasibility considerations, we reduced the number of post-infusion FVIII activity measurements from three to two and one, while keeping all other input features fixed. The sampling time points were selected from commonly used post-infusion follow-up windows in routine hemophilia management, informed by expert consensus and WAPPS-Hemo guidelines37,38, and taking into account patient burden and outpatient capacity constraints. The one-point sampling scenario was included as an intentionally constrained and exploratory setting, designed to assess the extent to which informative clinical features can partially compensate for minimal PK information, rather than as a recommended clinical sampling strategy. Model performance was assessed separately for IVR and HL estimation under each sampling scenario. For IVR estimation, all models showed improved accuracy with increasing sampling frequency, though the degree of improvement varied. Linear models (Lasso, Ridge) performed notably well under the three-sampling setting, surpassing the WAPPS-Hemo baseline. Tree-based models (RF, XGB) demonstrated more gradual improvements, reaching performance comparable to WAPPS-Hemo only under the full three-sample configuration. In contrast, deep learning models, including MLP, FT-Transformer, and the language model, exhibited reduced performance as sampling frequency decreased. Under sparse sampling (i.e., one or two measurements), their accuracy dropped below that of WAPPS-Hemo, suggesting that advanced neural architectures require richer input information to maintain robustness. These results indicate that a three-sampling strategy may represent the optimal balance between input sufficiency and estimation accuracy for ML-based IVR modeling.

For HL estimation, performance trends were more heterogeneous across model types. Tree-based models (RF, XGB) showed minimal improvement with increased sampling, this observation may be explained by the supervised learning setting of this study. For tree-based models, a substantial portion of the predictive signal is already captured by clinical features. Additional post-infusion measurements may provide limited incremental information. Moreover, tree-based structures do not explicitly model continuous temporal trends or slopes, which reduces their sensitivity to additional time-ordered measurements. This explains the limited benefit observed with increased sampling for these models. The SciML model exhibited relatively stable performance across different sampling strategies. Although its accuracy did not always surpass the best-performing data-driven models, the degradation in performance with reduced sampling was less pronounced, suggesting that integrating PK elimination constraints helps stabilize HL estimation under sparse and non-ideal sampling conditions. These results may also indicate that SciML is particularly suitable for estimating mechanistic PK parameters. In addition, the FT-Transformer demonstrated consistent performance gains from one to three samples and outperformed WAPPS-Hemo under all sampling conditions. In particular, the language model achieved the lowest MAE and RMSE values under sparse sampling, although its R2 remained moderate, highlighting its strong error minimization capability but limited variance explanation. Importantly, all deep learning models (MLP, FT-Transformer, LM) exceeded the performance of WAPPS-Hemo even when restricted to a single FVIII measurement, suggesting their potential to reduce sampling burden under the evaluated experimental conditions.

Additionally, we conducted statistical analyses to compare the performance of machine learning models against WAPPS-Hemo under different sampling strategies. A non-parametric Mann−Whitney U test was employed for each evaluation metric, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05 at a 95% confidence level. All developed models were tested against WAPPS-Hemo across the full set of metrics and sampling configurations. Detailed results are presented in the Supplementary Table 1. For IVR estimation, most machine learning models achieved statistically significant improvements over WAPPS-Hemo under the three-sampling condition, while this advantage diminished with one or two sampling points. In contrast, for HL estimation, the majority of machine learning models maintained superior performance and demonstrated statistically significant differences from WAPPS-Hemo across most evaluation metrics, even under reduced sampling conditions.

Through extensive experiments and in-depth analysis, we have summarized the following findings: Language models exhibited substantial performance improvements within HL estimation, with improvements in MAE exceeding 90% and p-values consistently below 0.01. However, their performance in IVR estimation is mixed, with some results not reaching statistical significance; FT-Transformer models demonstrate robust and consistent improvements in both tasks across all metrics, particularly in HL, often outperforming traditional models. Traditional regression models (Lasso, Ridge) and ensemble methods (RF, XGB) generally show modest to moderate improvements, with Ridge slightly outperforming Lasso in most metrics; MLP shows significant variability, while it achieve large improvements in HL (especially in MAE), its performance in IVR (e.g., RMSE and R2) is less stable and sometimes worse than baseline.

In addition to sampling strategies, we further explore model optimization for the LM. Due to the complex architecture and large number of trainable parameters, LM typically requires substantial data for effective training and optimization39. However, given the limited sample size of our dataset, full fine-tuning of the model may lead to overfitting and suboptimal predictive performance in the current small-sample setting. To improve predictive performance while mitigating over-parameterization, we investigated selective fine-tuning of key components within the model to identify the most effective optimization strategy. Prior studies have shown that in BERT-based encoders, the lower layers primarily capture general syntactic or grammatical features, while the upper layers encode task-specific semantic representations40,41. The embedding layer, in turn, contains foundational language information learned during large-scale pretraining, which may be misaligned with domain-specific terminology, such as clinical and PK terms, present in our dataset but absent from the pretraining corpus. Based on these insights, we implemented a partial fine-tuning strategy, selectively unfreezing the embedding layer (Lemb), the first encoder layer (L1st), and the final encoder layer (Llast), while freezing all other components of the BGE encoder. We evaluated multiple combinations of these layers to assess model performance under different fine-tuning configurations.

The results of these experiments are shown in Figs. 9 and 10, corresponding to IVR and HL estimation tasks, respectively. The ablation analysis indicates that partial fine-tuning consistently outperforms full fine-tuning on both tasks. Notably, the best performance was achieved by unfreezing only the embedding layer and the first BERT layer, while keeping the remaining layers frozen. Discussion

Pharmacokinetics describes the time course of drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) in the human body, and is traditionally modeled using differential equation-based mechanistic frameworks42–44. These models offer strong interpretability and theoretical rigor, and form the foundation of model-informed precision dosing. To improve individualization under sparse sampling, population PK (popPK) approaches, including Bayesian forecasting45 and likelihood-based estimation46, have been widely adopted in clinical practice.

However, despite their methodological maturity, the deployment of popPK-based software in real-world pediatric settings may still face practical constraints. Notably, recent work has demonstrated that a population PK model specifically developed for Chinese pediatric patients achieved improved predictive performance compared with WAPPS-Hemo in this population47. This finding further highlights the importance of population and region-specific model calibration when deploying PK tools in pediatric clinical practice. Widely used platforms such as myPKFiT and WAPPS-Hemo were primarily developed and validated in specific drug contexts and healthcare systems. For example, myPKFiT is restricted to specific FVIII products (e.g., ADVATE)5, while WAPPS-Hemo operates as a centralized online platform with servers hosted outside China, which may limit real-time accessibility and raise data governance and regulatory concerns in certain regions48. Moreover, although popPK models are capable of handling sparse sampling, their performance and usability may still be affected by clinical heterogeneity, workflow constraints, and the availability of locally validated pediatric models. In this context, ML approaches provide a complementary strategy by directly learning associations between individual-level features (e.g., age, body size, dose, laboratory biomarkers, and sparse FVIII activity measurements) and PK parameters, without requiring explicit specification of compartmental structures28,29,49. Rather than replacing popPK frameworks, ML-based models may serve as pragmatic alternatives or adjunctive tools in settings where rapid estimation, reduced sampling burden, or local deployment is prioritized, particularly in pediatric populations.

This study demonstrates that ML models can accurately predict FVIII PK parameters in pediatric patients with hemophilia A using only sparse clinical data for the first time. Compared to WAPPS-Hemo, a widely used popPK tool, all ML models achieved improved performance in predicting both IVR and HL, despite requiring only three FVIII activity measurements post-infusion. These findings support the feasibility of data-efficient, individualized PK profiling that significantly reduces sampling burden in children. Notably, all reported results should be interpreted as exploratory and dataset-specific, and external validation will be required before any claims of population-level generalizability can be made.

Across model types, linear models (Lasso, Ridge) performed reliably across both tasks, due to their regularization capacity and inherent robustness in small sample tabular settings. Tree-based ensemble methods (RF, XGB) exhibited less stability, possibly owing to increased variance under small data regimes. Deep learning models yielded more diverse results. The FT-Transformer, with its attention-based architecture and feature tokenizer design for tabular data, demonstrated competitive performance in both tasks, suggesting its suitability for structured clinical data. The language model (LM) achieved particularly strong performance in the more challenging HL estimation task, highlighting its potential to capture subtle semantics and context beyond what is accessible to conventional architectures.

The inclusion of a SciML model further clarifies the role of mechanistic knowledge in individualized PK estimation. HL is a primary PK parameter directly determined by the elimination rate constant, making it particularly well-suited for physics-informed modeling. By embedding PK constraints, the SciML approach reduces the hypothesis space of the learning task and improves robustness under sparse sampling conditions. The SciML approach provides a complementary perspective by explicitly linking estimations to PK structure rather than relying solely on flexible function approximation. From a clinical and methodological perspective, SciML should be viewed as complementary to both conventional machine learning and popPK approaches. While popPK frameworks remain the gold standard for reconstructing full concentration-time profiles, SciML offers an alternative for estimating key PK parameters, such as HL, when blood sampling is limited. Together, these approaches provide a spectrum of tools for personalized FVIII therapy, balancing mechanistic interpretability, data efficiency, and clinical feasibility.

We additionally performed exploratory experiments using the reciprocal of half-life (1/HL) as an alternative prediction target, as 1/HL is more directly related to the elimination rate constant in classical PK theory. However, the results showed inferior performance across most models, with increased MAE and RMSE and universally reduced R2 values, indicating that the reciprocal transformation amplified estimation noise in this small-sample, sparse-sampling cohort. This finding suggests that HL estimation involves a trade-off between mechanistic alignment and numerical stability. While transforming PK parameters to better reflect underlying kinetic mechanisms is theoretically appealing, its practical utility depends strongly on data quality and sample size. These results further highlight the value of incorporating mechanistic priors or physics-informed constraints (as in the SciML approach) to stabilize learning, and support prioritizing clinically interpretable parameters such as HL in data-limited settings.

We also explored multi-label regression to simultaneously predict IVR and HL, aiming to leverage their statistical correlation. However, this joint modeling approach did not yield consistent performance improvements—IVR prediction remained stable or slightly degraded, while HL estimation showed clear declines in MAE, RMSE, and R2 for both Ridge and Lasso models. This outcome reflects the fundamental heterogeneity of the two PK parameters: IVR is a dose-normalized, approximately linear metric, whereas HL reflects complex elimination kinetics dependent on multiple interacting patient-specific factors. These results highlight that for heterogeneous PK targets with distinct underlying mechanisms, separate modeling tailored to each parameter’s characteristics yields more robust and interpretable performance than enforcing shared linear representations. This insight provides practical guidance for future PK modeling efforts, emphasizing the importance of aligning model structure with the inherent nature of the parameters being predicted.

Residual analysis further revealed differences in model behavior. Linear and neural network models showed relatively low residual dispersion, indicating stable generalization. However, ensemble models exhibited larger residual variability, reflecting estimation inconsistency across cases. Interestingly, the residuals from the language model displayed a linear trend, which may indicate homoscedasticity but also suggests limited calibration in certain estimation ranges. This pattern implies that, while the LM produces smooth estimations, it may lack granularity in capturing individual-level outliers. Future work may address this limitation through uncertainty modeling or task-specific loss functions.

The study also highlights the critical role of sampling strategies in model performance. Ablation experiments demonstrated that increasing the number of sampling points enhanced accuracy across all models, although the magnitude of improvement varied. Notably, the language model and other deep architectures were more sensitive to reduced sampling frequencies, performing substantially better with three measurements than with one or two. In contrast, simpler models such as Ridge regression were less affected by sparse input. These results underscore the need to balance data efficiency with information sufficiency, especially in clinical scenarios where frequent blood draws are infeasible. In addition, while population PK frameworks naturally accommodate arbitrarily timed samples through continuous-time modeling, the present ML approach operates on fixed-dimensional inputs and gains practical flexibility by explicitly including sampling times and evaluating diverse sparse-sampling patterns within clinically feasible windows.

To adapt the language model for small-sample, domain-specific tasks, we explored fine-tuning strategies. In line with prior findings40,41, partial fine-tuning, specifically updating only the embedding layer and the first BERT encoder block, significantly outperformed full fine-tuning. This strategy likely preserves general language representations while allowing task-relevant adaptation, particularly in the embedding space, which must bridge the semantic gap between general-domain pretraining and specialized medical terminology. These results offer a practical solution for applying large pretrained models in data-constrained clinical contexts.

From a clinical perspective, the utility of the proposed ML framework lies in its ability to provide patient-specific PK parameters that are directly actionable in routine care. Although the ML approach does not reconstruct the full FVIII concentration-time profile as population PK or Bayesian forecasting models do, the two predicted parameters: HL and IVR are the key PK quantities routinely used by clinicians for individualized prophylaxis. HL determines the rate of FVIII elimination and therefore directly informs dosing interval selection and expected trough levels. A shorter predicted HL suggests the need for more frequent dosing to maintain FVIII activity above the patient’s protective threshold, whereas a longer HL allows extension of the dosing interval. IVR is commonly used to individualize the dose required to achieve a target peak level. Together, HL and IVR provide clinically actionable information for practical decisions such as adjusting prophylactic schedules and optimizing dosing intensity, particularly in routine care and reduced-sampling settings, without requiring dense blood sampling. The ML-derived PK parameters can be applied in the same way as those obtained from reduced-sampling popPK tools or Bayesian forecasting, while offering a lightweight and locally deployable alternative in settings where full PK curve reconstruction is not feasible.

Despite the encouraging results, several limitations merit consideration. Hemophilia A is a rare condition, and model development is inherently constrained by limited sample size. Although cross-validation and repeated random splitting were employed to improve robustness, external validation across independent cohorts and clinical settings remains essential. In addition, this study is focused on predictive modeling of FVIII under sparse-sampling conditions, and we therefore did not conduct non-compartmental assessment or cross-population PK analyses to compare FVIII exposure profiles between Chinese pediatric patients and other populations (e.g., Chinese adults, international pediatric or adult cohorts). Such analyses would be highly valuable for placing the current cohort within a broader biological and clinical context and for understanding population-level differences in FVIII disposition. However, rigorous cross-population comparison would require access to well-matched external reference cohorts and standardized exposure data. We therefore consider cross-population non-compartmental assessment and population-level PK characterization as important directions for future work. Furthermore, all PK data in this study were collected from a single pediatric hemophilia center; therefore, the findings may not fully represent the FVIII disposition profiles of Chinese children with hemophilia more broadly. To address this limitation, we are currently coordinating a multicenter collaboration to expand the sample size and to prospectively validate the proposed models across diverse clinical environments. Moreover, from a methodological perspective, the current ML framework relies on fixed-dimensional tabular inputs and therefore requires a predefined number of post-infusion measurements during training and deployment. While this design enables robust learning under clinically realistic sparse-sampling scenarios, it does not provide the same degree of flexibility as popPK models in accommodating arbitrary sampling numbers and timings at inference. Besides, the extrapolation capability of the models to outlier cases remains uncertain, particularly for language models trained on sequences with limited diversity. Future research should explore domain-adaptive pretraining strategies and multimodal data integration to further enhance generalizability and clinical reliability. Finally, although we incorporated SHAP-based analyses to improve the interpretability of Transformer-based models, further work is needed to develop clinically aligned explanation frameworks and to ensure regulatory readiness for broader clinical adoption.

In conclusion, this study presents a data-efficient machine learning framework for predicting FVIII PK parameters in Chinese children with severe hemophilia A. By utilizing sparse blood sampling and routinely collected clinical features, a range of models—from linear regression to ensemble methods and deep learning architectures—consistently outperformed the current clinical standard, WAPPS-Hemo. Among these, transformer-based models, including FT-Transformer and pretrained language models, demonstrated particular advantages in HL estimation when combined with appropriate fine-tuning and sampling strategies. These findings support the integration of machine learning into individualized PK modeling, offering a scalable and patient-friendly approach toward personalized medical care for children with severe hemophilia A.

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital (Approval Number: 2020-Z-095). The study’s registration number is ChiCTR2000037821, which can be found at https://www.chictr.org.cn.Allpatients and/or legally authorized guardians gave written informed consent before any data collection. All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations.

Pediatric patients with severe hemophilia A were enrolled at Beijing Children’s Hospital from June 2018 to June 2023. The inclusion criteria were age < 18 years old, severe and moderate hemophilia A (FVIII activity < 2 IU/dL). After a washout period of at least 72 h, each patient received a single-dose Kovaltry infusion of 50 IU/kg. Peripheral venous blood samples were drawn using Vacutainer tubes (3.2% trisodium citrate, 2 mL). A predefined blood sampling schedule, including pre-dose and 1, 3, 9, 24, 48, and 72 h after concentrate infusion, was implemented as previously described50. Blood samples were immediately centrifuged at 2500 g for 15 min at 20–25 ∘C. Plasma was aliquoted into three Eppendorf tubes (600 μL per tube) and stored at −80 ∘C until further analysis. For each patient, FVIII activity, FVIII inhibitor status, ABO blood type, and von Willebrand factor antigen (vWF:Ag) levels were assessed.

FVIII activity was measured using a one-stage activated partial thromboplastin time assay (Instrumentation Laboratory, MA, USA) in a multidilution mode with FVIII-deficient plasma. FVIII inhibitor levels were determined using the Nijmegen-modified Bethesda assay. All coagulation measurements were performed on an ACL TOP-700 analyzer (Instrumentation Laboratory, MA, USA). vWF:Ag levels were quantified using a latex particle-enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay with the HemosIL von Willebrand factor antigen kit (Instrumentation Laboratory).

Based on the collected clinical data, we next defined the model input features, target labels, and preprocessing strategy to ensure consistency across different modeling approaches. The initial cohort consisted of 91 children with 18 features, which included demographic, clinical, and treatment-related variables, including six precise blood sampling times and corresponding FVIII activity after concentrate infusion. We cleaned the dataset by removing the subjects with over 50% missing features and removing features with over 50% missing values. The final dataset contains 88 children with 16 features and does not have missing data.

To align model development with realistic clinical settings, we designed the input and sampling strategy to accommodate sparse and non-ideal FVIII activity measurements. To reflect routine clinical practice, in which post-infusion FVIII activity measurements are often collected at non-ideal and variable time points, each post-infusion measurement was represented as a (sampling time, FVIII activity) pair, with sampling time encoded explicitly as an input feature. Pre-infusion FVIII activity was treated as a baseline clinical variable and was consistently included across all models. For model development under sparse-sampling conditions, three post-infusion FVIII measurements were selected from clinically available time windows (1, 3, 9, 24, 48, and 72 h after infusion). Rather than relying on a single optimized sampling schedule, multiple combinations of these sampling points were systematically evaluated while keeping all other patient features unchanged. This design preserves the fixed input dimensionality required by tabular machine learning models, enables fair comparisons across modeling approaches, and allows the models to accommodate varied yet clinically realistic sampling patterns. Among these configurations, the (1 h, 3 h, 24 h) sampling scheme yielded the best predictive performance, consistent with established clinical practice and expert consensus, in which early post-infusion samples capture distribution dynamics and a later sample reflects the elimination phase of FVIII37,38.

Although neural networks and Transformer-based models are capable of end-to-end learning without explicit feature engineering, this study is not intended to learn unconstrained representations from dense PK profiles. Instead, we focus on clinically realistic scenarios in which only a limited number of post-infusion measurements are available, and explicitly evaluate model performance under such sparse-sampling conditions.

PK analysis was performed using clinical measurements and Phoenix WinNonlin (https://www.certara.com), a software package widely used in PK research and adopted by both pharmaceutical companies and academic institutions. In this study, HL values estimated by Phoenix WinNonlin using the full six-point sampling scheme (1, 3, 9, 24, 48, and 72 h) were used as ground-truth labels for machine learning model development. IVR values were calculated directly from FVIII activity measurements obtained from multiple clinical blood samples. To further investigate the impact of reduced sampling on model performance, we designed two scenarios: a two-sampling strategy and a one-sampling strategy. Notably, in this study, the number of sampling points refers exclusively to post-infusion FVIII activity measurements. Pre-infusion FVIII activity is treated as a baseline clinical feature rather than as a PK sampling point. Importantly, pre-infusion FVIII activity is included consistently as an input feature in all modeling approaches, including both machine learning models and popPK analyses. In both scenarios, only the sampling times and corresponding post infusion FVIII activity levels were varied; all other patient features remained unchanged. For the two-sampling strategy, we explored three combinations: 1 h & 3 h, 1 h & 24 h, and 3 h & 24 h. For the one-sampling strategy, we evaluated 1 h, 3 h, and 24 h individually. After comparative experiments, the 3 h & 24 h combination showed the best performance in the two-sampling setting, while 24 h alone was optimal in the one-sampling setting. These findings align with clinical experience and demonstrate the model’s robustness under reduced sampling conditions. They also suggest that reliable PK estimation may be achievable with fewer blood samples, which is particularly important for pediatric patients with severe hemophilia A.

During the model training and evaluating procedures, the dataset was partitioned into the training set and the testing set. Due to the limitation of sample size and to evaluate model convergence and select optimal hyperparameters, the 5-fold cross-validation is conducted during the training process. The training set is divided into 5 folds; 4 folds of samples are used for training, and 1 fold for validation, while the testing set remains invisible throughout the model training process. Min-Max scaler and Standard scaler from the Scikit-Learn51 library were implemented for features and labels, respectively, for data normalization.

Employed configurations for traditional machine learning models

In addition to conventional machine learning models, we incorporated a scientific machine learning (SciML) approach to integrate domain knowledge of FVIII pharmacokinetics into the learning process. The goal of this model was not to replace established PK theory, but to provide a mechanistically informed regularization that complements data-driven estimation under sparse clinical sampling. HL was selected as the target for SciML modeling because it is a fundamental PK parameter that reflects the elimination behavior of FVIII and is directly related to the underlying disposition process. In contrast to purely data-driven models, the SciML framework introduces a mechanistic inductive bias consistent with the expected decay behavior of FVIII, while allowing patient-specific variability to be learned from clinical features and limited FVIII activity measurements. In practice, the SciML model combines a neural network with mechanistic constraints that enforce consistency with established PK processes, thereby balancing interpretability and flexibility. This approach enables the model to leverage known scientific structure while remaining robust in small data and sparse-sampling settings. SciML modeling was applied exclusively to HL estimation. IVR was not included in the SciML framework since it represents a dose-normalized derived exposure metric rather than a primary kinetic parameter governed by the FVIII disposition process.

Finally, we explored the use of LM to leverage textual representations of tabular clinical data for PK parameter estimation. To enable the application of language models to structured clinical tabular data, we transformed tabular features into natural language text through a feature serialization process. The effectiveness of language models is known to be highly sensitive to the structure and informativeness of textual input55,56. In this study, we adopted a simple yet effective serialization strategy known as Text-Template33, in which each feature is formatted as a natural language phrase of the form “The [column name] is [value]”. All serialized feature phrases were concatenated using the conjunction “and” to construct a single input sequence (e.g., “The dosage is 500 IU and the blood group is A”), resulting in a complete textual representation that contains all feature names and their corresponding values for a given patient sample. An example of the serialization process is illustrated in Fig. 1e. To improve readability and semantic clarity of the serialized inputs, selected feature names and values were manually mapped to human-interpretable forms with input from clinical experts. For example, the column name “FFM” was replaced with “Fat-Free Mass” and one-hot encoded blood type variables were reverted to their original categorical labels (e.g., “AB”). Numerical feature values were retained in their original form without additional transformation.

The serialized textual representations were then used as input to a pre-trained language model to generate latent feature embeddings. Specifically, we adopted the BAAI General Embedding (BGE) encoder57 as the backbone language model. BGE is a BERT-based31 text embedding model designed to produce high-quality semantic vector representations and is built upon the Transformer architecture30. Owing to its strong representation capacity, BGE has demonstrated competitive performance across a wide range of text-based tasks58. In our framework, the encoder was used to extract latent representations from the serialized clinical data. The BGE encoder employs a bidirectional Transformer architecture composed of multiple Transformer encoder layers. Given the limited sample size of the clinical dataset, we used a simplified version of the BGE encoder consisting of 12 Transformer encoder layers, each with a hidden dimension of 768 and 12 attention heads, resulting in approximately 110 million trainable parameters. The BGE model consists of the encoder and decoder during the pre-training process. A novel pre-training strategy called Retrospective Masked Auto Encoder (RetroMAE)59 was introduced during the optimization process of the BGE model, enhancing context modeling capabilities via a masking-reconstruction mechanism. First, the input text t is masked with a 15% ~30% moderate masking ratio, and encoded by BERT Encoder. Then the latent embedding hcls from [CLS] token, which aggregates global representations of input text is generated. Then, a heavily masking strategy with 50% ~70% masking ratio to the input text is implemented, the masked text \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{t}$$\end{document}t^ and the embedding hcls generated by the encoder are combined used to input the decoder for text reconstruction, making the model reconstruct the original text based on extremely sparse context with a lightweight decoder (single Transformer layer), forcing the encoder to learn stronger representations. After pretraining, we obtain a BGE encoder with strong representation extraction capabilities for downstream tasks.

In the fine-tuning stage, the encoder is further optimized by introducing a contrastive learning strategy, enabling it to extract more discriminative representations. A triplet (Q, P, N) is designed to build text pairs for contrastive learning, where Q denotes Query, which is a text input in the dataset (usually the Text matching or Question-Answer datasets), P denotes a semantically similar positive sample, and N denotes a semantically unrelated negative sample. The fine-tuning goal is to align semantically similar text embeddings while separating unrelated ones. The model leverages in-batch negatives and cosine similarity with temperature scaling, following the contrastive loss paradigm, such as NT-Xent loss60. More details on pre-training and fine-tuning can be found in the original research paper58. After the above pre-training and fine-tuning steps, we obtained an encoder suitable for text data, and we can directly use this encoder to generate embeddings from serialized tabular data for the PK parameter estimation task.

After generating a 768-dimensional latent text representation using the encoder, the next step is to convert this high-dimensional embedding into a single, continuous value corresponding to the predicted PK parameter. To achieve this, we employ a straightforward yet effective approach: a linear transformation layer, also known as a fully connected layer, which serves as the regression head. This linear layer takes the 768-dimensional input vector and projects it down to a one-dimensional output. The output of this layer represents the model’s estimation for the target PK parameter value. This lightweight regression head enables efficient computation and facilitates end-to-end learning from serialized clinical features to quantitative PK outcomes.
