Predictive diagnostic models for newly diagnosed diabetes mellitus in moderate to severe COVID-19: the role of TyG Index, BMI, and inflammatory markers
Authors: Farzahna Mohamed, Sulé Gunter, Bezalem E Yirdaw, Frederick J Raal, Aletta ME Millen, Ismail S Kalla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between COVID-19 and newly diagnosed diabetes mellitus (DM) remains uncertain. This cross-sectional study examines the role of insulin resistance (IR) and selected inflammatory markers in COVID-19 associated newly diagnosed DM.Research design and methodsA cross-sectional pilot study was conducted at an academic tertiary hospital and a primary healthcare facility, with COVID-19 patients additionally followed for three months post-discharge. Participants included patients hospitalised with moderate to severe COVID-19 during the third wave of predominantly the delta variant. Diagnostic markers predictive of newly diagnosed DM were assessed using logistic regression analysis. Four predictive diagnostic models were developed, incorporating combinations of triglyceride-glucose index (TyG index), homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), body mass index (BMI) and inflammatory cytokines. Model performance and optimal cutoff values were determined using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis and the Youden index.ResultsA total of 127 individuals were evaluated, consisting of 84 patients admitted with moderate to severe COVID-19 and 43 healthy controls. Among the 84 COVID-19 participants, 45 were newly diagnosed with DM, 20 had no DM, and 19 had pre-existing DM. Those with newly diagnosed DM exhibited significantly higher BMI and IR markers (HOMA-IR, and TyG index) compared to those without newly diagnosed DM (p < 0.001, p = 0.05 and p = 0.002, respectively). The predictive diagnostic model for newly diagnosed DM included the TyG index, BMI, IL-10 and IL-1β, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.91 (95% CI, 0.84–0.98). The TyG index was strongly associated with newly diagnosed DM (Crude Odds Ratio [COR] 11.25 (95% CI, 2.80-76.28; p-value = 0.01); Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) 6.83 (95% CI, 1.57, 42.96; p-value = 0.01) and showed improved predictive accuracy when used with BMI (AUC 0.86; 95% CI, 0.77–0.95), compared to the TyG index alone (AUC 0.73; 95% CI, 0.59–0.86). These findings support the potential role of the TyG index as a practical alternative to HOMA-IR in resource-limited settings where insulin measurement may not be feasible.ConclusionsIn our study population, IR rather than insulin deficiency was more strongly associated with newly diagnosed DM in patients with COVID-19. The TyG index may serve as a practical diagnostic marker for predicting newly diagnosed DM in resource-limited settings, with BMI and inflammatory markers further improving model accuracy. However, given our predominantly Black African study population, validation in larger and more diverse populations is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02056-2.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, Type 2, Insulin resistance, Triglyceride-glucose index, Fasting glucose, COVID-19 severity, Biomarkers

What is already known on this topic

Insulin resistance (IR) and obesity are key risk factors for the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with viral infections like SARS-CoV-2 linked to newly diagnosed DM, especially in individuals with a high body mass index (BMI). Inflammatory markers such as IL-6 are associated with increased severity of COVID-19, yet there is a lack of research on its association with newly diagnosed DM and IR.

What this study adds

IR is a key factor driving newly diagnosed DM in COVID-19, and utilising markers such as the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG index) can significantly improve the predictive diagnostic accuracy, particularly when combined with BMI and inflammatory cytokines. The findings also highlight the strong association between an elevated BMI and the increased risk of newly diagnosed DM in COVID-19 patients.

How this study might affect research, practice or policy

The study emphasises the need for cardiometabolic monitoring in COVID-19 survivors, particularly those with an elevated BMI and risk factors for IR, while highlighting the importance of integrating obesity management and using cost-effective markers like the TyG index. Similarities between SARS-CoV-2 and other viruses suggest that DM prediction models for COVID-19 may be widely applicable. However, further validation of these models is required to effectively estimate diabetes risk in vulnerable populations and implement strategies in clinical practice and policy to reduce this risk.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has had a significant global impact, not only due to acute respiratory illness but also because of its broader metabolic consequences. Obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are interrelated risk factors for complications from SARS-CoV-2. However, there is a bidirectional relationship, as COVID-19 has also been causally linked with hyperglycemia. This may occur directly due to the virus, since viruses have been associated with both non-autoimmune and autoimmune-mediated DM or as a side effect of treatment interventions such as glucocorticoids [1–4]. Insulin resistance (IR) and β-cell dysfunction contribute to glucose intolerance in this context [3].

Our previous work during the first wave of the pandemic in South Africa, predominated by the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 variant with the Asp614Gly mutation, highlighted the prognostic value of fasting plasma glucose (FPG) in predicting COVID-19 outcomes in hospitalised patients [5]. Additionally, DM has been shown to be associated with higher mortality rates during SARS-CoV-2 and other viral outbreaks, such as SARS and MERS [5–7].

Studies have indicated a higher risk for newly diagnosed DM following COVID-19 compared to non-COVID-19 respiratory infections [8]. The United States Veterans Affairs cohort study found that individuals recovering from COVID-19 had an increased risk of developing newly diagnosed DM and requiring anti-hyperglycemic medications [9, 10]. A meta-analysis estimated a 16% prevalence and 1.6% incidence of newly diagnosed DM post-COVID-19 infection, with significantly higher rates among hospitalised patients [10, 11]. Hyperglycemia at admission has also been identified as an independent predictor of mortality [5, 12]. Despite these findings, little is known about the mechanisms contributing to newly diagnosed DM in COVID-19 in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in sub-Saharan Africa.

Several factors have been proposed in the pathogenesis of DM in COVID-19, including direct pancreatic β-cell injury, stress-induced IR, the pro-inflammatory milieu, and glucocorticoid therapy. Viral entry through angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors in pancreatic tissue may lead to β-cell dysfunction and insulin deficiency, while cytokine driven systemic inflammation can impair insulin signaling and contribute to IR [8, 10, 13–15]. IR and inflammation are increasingly recognised as central components in the development of newly diagnosed DM during and after COVID-19. IR may lead to hyperinsulinemia and elevated C-peptide levels, while inflammation can provoke stress hyperglycemia and β-cell stress [10, 13, 16]. The relative contributions of insulin deficiency versus IR to COVID-19-associated hyperglycemia remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Numerous DM risk prediction models have been developed in recent years using clinical and biochemical markers [17–19]. Although machine learning has advanced model performance, few efforts have focused specifically on newly diagnosed DM in the context of COVID-19, particularly in LMICs. Our prior research has shown that incorporating routine biochemical markers such as FPG improves the prognostic accuracy of pneumonia severity scores in hospitalized COVID-19 patients during the first wave [20]. This highlights the utility of using accessible metabolic indicators in predictive diagnostic models, especially in resource-limited settings.

IR has been established as a key pathophysiological pathway for the development of T2DM and cardiovascular disease, yet its gold-standard assessment via the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HIEC) is not feasible in routine clinical settings due to complexity and cost [19, 21, 22]. The HOMA-IR index also has limitations, especially in patients on insulin or with severely impaired β-cell function [10, 23]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has emerged as a practical surrogate, not requiring insulin measurement and applicable across treatment statuses, enhancing its versatility across diverse patient populations [10, 22, 23]. While evidence indicates that the TyG index effectively estimates IR compared to HIEC and outperforms HOMA-IR in both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals, varying cutoff values highlight the need for further validation and definitions for clinical use, particularly in LMIC settings [10, 22, 24, 25]. Its simplicity and cost-effectiveness enhance its potential utility for IR screening [10, 22].

Considering the link between metabolic dysfunction and immune activation, inflammation must also be considered in the pathogenesis of IR and newly diagnosed DM, particularly in the COVID-19 context. Inflammation is a key contributor to both IR and T2DM, and its role in newly diagnosed DM in the context of COVID-19 is increasingly recognised. Limited studies suggest early β-cell dysfunction during SARS-CoV-2 infection, warranting further investigation [26–28].

Our focus on a predominantly Black African population is particularly relevant given evidence that this group may exhibit heightened chronic systemic inflammation and IR, potentially predisposing them to a more severe COVID-19 phenotype [29]. Acute inflammation from SARS-CoV-2 infection may amplify these pre-existing risk factors, contributing to worse outcomes.

This pilot study aimed to investigate surrogate markers of IR and characterise inflammatory pathway activity (including Th1, Th2, and Th17 associated cytokines) in a predominantly Black African population with newly diagnosed DM admitted with moderate to severe COVID-19. This reflects South Africa’s demographic and epidemiologic context, where urbanisation and metabolic disease contribute to the rising incidence of DM. The study also evaluates how these markers, combined with elevated BMI, may improve DM risk assessment in this sample population. Given the limited utility of traditional markers like HOMA-IR and HOMA-CP in this setting, particularly among patients with variable β-cell function, our findings offer valuable regional insight. Furthermore, the persistence and remission of newly diagnosed DM post-COVID-19 remain poorly understood, therefore, we evaluated outcomes at three-month follow-up to assess the trajectory of dysglycemia in this population.

Methodology

Study design

This pilot study was designed as a cross-sectional analysis with three subgroups (COVID-19 patients, diabetic controls, and healthy controls). Data for the COVID-19 group were collected prospectively during hospitalisation, with follow-up actively timed from the date of discharge, and all patients were monitored for three months post-discharge to assess remission or persistence of metabolic abnormalities. The study was carried out at two centres: Charlotte Maxeke Johannesburg Academic Hospital (CMJAH), a tertiary academic facility, and Bertha Gxowa Hospital (BGH), a level 1 district hospital within the same referral cluster, where the same patient population, access to care, and standard operating procedures were maintained during the recruitment period. Participants were recruited from July 6 to August 31, 2021, during the peak of the Delta variant wave in Johannesburg. A total of 169 participants were enrolled in a 2:1:1 ratio, comprising hospitalised COVID-19 patients (n = 84), diabetic controls (n = 42), and healthy controls (n = 43). COVID-19 patients were enrolled consecutively at admission, while diabetic and healthy controls were purposively sampled to achieve approximate age- and sex-matching. Controls had no prior history of COVID-19 requiring hospitalisation and no additional comorbidities (Supplementary Fig. 1). For this sub-analysis, only the hospitalised COVID-19 patients were included in the predictive diagnostic modelling, with analyses restricted to two admission groups: those with newly diagnosed DM and those without DM. The healthy control groups were included for comparative analyses of metabolic and inflammatory markers but were not used in the construction of the diagnostic models.

Study population

The study included patients who exhibited symptoms and tested positive for COVID-19 via reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Participants were eligible if they experienced moderate to severe symptoms necessitating hospitalisation during the third wave of COVID-19 (May to September 2021), which was dominated by the delta variant (B.1.617.2), as reported by the National Institute for Communicable Diseases [30]. Severity was classified as moderate in patients with an oxygen saturation (SpO₂) above 90% and no evidence of severe pneumonia, such as a respiratory rate exceeding 30 breaths per minute, severe respiratory distress, or SpO₂ below 90% on room air [31]. Enrollment occurred at the time of presentation to the emergency department and within two days of hospital admission, prior to the initiation of glucocorticoids or other therapies that could influence glycaemic or inflammatory markers. Exclusion criteria included individuals under 18 years of age, pregnant patients, those already initiated on glucocorticoid therapy, individuals with COVID-19 symptoms lasting 21 days or more, and those with features of long COVID-19. Long COVID, or post-acute COVID-19 syndrome, refers to symptoms that persist or emerge at least three months after infection and last for two months or more without another clear cause [32]. Patients with a history of pre-existing DM or prior use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic medications were excluded from the sub-analysis of the study population based on their status of newly diagnosed DM. Healthy controls were confirmed to be free of DM at recruitment based on a glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), FPG and absence of symptoms.

Data collection

Participant characteristics and processing of blood samples

Demographic data and clinical information, including medical history, glycaemic status, comorbidities, treatment, vaccination status, and symptom duration, were recorded at admission and at 3-month follow-up. Blood samples were obtained from each patient within two days of their admission to the emergency department or inpatient ward and before the initiation of glucocorticoid treatment. All samples collected as part of the standard of care were processed at a central laboratory on admission and follow-up. Additional blood samples were centrifuged within two hours and subsequently stored at -80 °C until further analysis.

Cytokines

The protein levels of 8 serum cytokines representing the Th1, Th2 and Th17 immune pathways were assessed utilising the MILLIPLEX® Human Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel from Millipore™, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Each sample was analysed in duplicate using a Luminex® 100™ Bioanalyzer (Luminex Corp., Austin, TX, USA).

Triglyceride measurement

Blood samples were collected to measure lipid profiles, including triglycerides, using a colorimetric enzymatic assay on the Roche Cobas automatic analyser (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland), following the manufacturer’s standard protocols.

HbA1c

HbA1c was measured using the Biorad Variant II, which makes use of ion exchange high performance liquid chromatography. The method is traceable to both the National Glycohemoglobin Standardisation Program (NGSP) and the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (IFCC) reference methods.

Fasting plasma glucose and markers of insulin resistance

Fasting serum insulin and C-peptide levels were measured using the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) method with Roche Diagnostics kits on the Roche/Hitachi Cobas e-602 analyser (Roche Diagnostics, Germany). Plasma glucose levels were determined on the Roche enzymatic assay using the hexokinase method on the Roche Cobas c-702 analyser (Roche Diagnostics, Germany).

Autoimmune status

Autoimmune-mediated DM was assessed by evaluating the glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) and protein tyrosine phosphatase (IA2) antibody status in all patients diagnosed with newly diagnosed DM. Patient samples were analysed using ELISA kits for anti-GAD and anti-IA2 (EUROIMMUN, Germany), which quantitatively detect human autoantibodies against GAD and protein tyrosine phosphatase (IA2) with a specificity of 99%. According to EUROIMMUN guidelines, results below 10 IU/ml are considered negative, while those above this threshold are classified as positive.

Definitions

Newly diagnosed DM was defined as a HbA1c level of ≥ 6.5% (≥ 48 mmol/mol) at the time of initial presentation, utilising an NGSP-certified method that was standardised according to the DCCT assay [37]. Additionally, DM was confirmed by an FPG level of ≥ 126 mg/dL (≥ 7.0 mmol/L) or the presence of classic symptoms of hyperglycemia or hyperglycemic crisis, evidenced by a random plasma glucose level of ≥ 200 mg/dL (≥ 11.1 mmol/L) [37]. Remission of DM was defined as having an HbA1c level of < 6.5% (48 mmol/mol), assessed at least three months after stopping glucose-lowering medication [38]. Persistence of newly diagnosed DM was defined as the continued presence of sustained hyperglycemia and adherence to diagnostic criteria (HbA1C ≥ 6.5% or FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL (≥ 7.0 mmol/L) at a three-month follow-up, despite attempts at intensive lifestyle changes or pharmacotherapy [37]. Considering the variability of triglyceride levels across ethnicities, further research is needed to evaluate the TyG index in different populations, with a proposed cutoff of 4.68 suggestive of IR [27, 39]. Additionally, while HOMA-IR lacks a universally defined cutoff, a value ≥ 2.5 is commonly utilised for the general population [33, 34, 40]. BMI was classified as follows: underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) [37]. For the purpose of subgroup analysis, the non-obese group was defined as participants with normal weight or overweight BMI classifications [37].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.4.1). Pearson’s Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to determine differences in categorical variables. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Student’s T-test were used to compare quantitative variables between individuals with and without newly diagnosed DM, as appropriate. The performance of continuous clinical predictors potentially associated with the onset of new DM was assessed through Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the Youden index was employed to determine the cutoff values. Univariable logistic regression analysis was performed to explore potential risk factors associated with newly diagnosed DM. However, since univariate analysis may overlook the combined predictive value of multiple weakly associated variables, multivariable logistic regression was employed. Variables with a p-value ≤ 0.25 in the univariate analysis were considered for the multivariable logistic regression model. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all included predictors showing VIFs < 5. A backward elimination method was applied to identify significant predictors, and statistical significance in the multivariable model was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. In addition to backward elimination for model selection, we assessed potential confounding by examining changes in estimated coefficients when specific covariates were removed or retained. The logistic regression model included only fixed effects, and odds ratios were interpreted while keeping other variables constant. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and likelihood ratio tests to compare nested models, with the best-fitting model selected accordingly. To address potential bias due to small sample size, Firth’s penalised logistic regression was applied after excluding multicollinearity and separation issues. A 10-fold cross-validation was conducted to evaluate the model’s predictive performance and generalisability, using Cohen’s kappa coefficient and area under the curve (AUC), as the Hosmer-Lemeshow test is unreliable with smaller sample sizes (n < 400). This approach allowed for the evaluation of agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, accounting for consistency beyond chance. Although age and sex were initially considered for inclusion based on clinical relevance, they did not substantially improve model performance or alter effect estimates and were therefore excluded to preserve model parsimony. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics among COVID-19 patients

The demographic characteristics and comorbidities at baseline are detailed in Table 1. Among the 84 patients hospitalised with moderate to severe COVID-19, 53.6% (n = 45) were diagnosed with newly diagnosed DM. Those diagnosed with newly diagnosed DM were predominantly female and of black ethnicity. Patients with newly diagnosed DM exhibited a significantly higher BMI (median (IQR: 37 (30,41) kg/m2) compared to those without DM and coexisting COVID-19, (26 (24,32) kg/m2, p < 0.001) (Table 1). Additionally, a higher proportion of patients with obesity had newly diagnosed DM (83%) as compared to those without DM (17%, p = 0.002) (Table 1). BMI was significantly greater in those admitted with COVID-19 (median (IQR: 33 (26,40) kg/m2) compared to healthy controls (25 (22,29) kg/m2, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Glycaemic characteristics, autoimmunity and markers of insulin resistance

Glycaemic markers, including FPG and HbA1c, along with markers of IR (HOMA-IR, HOMA-CP and TyG index), were significantly higher in patients admitted with COVID-19 compared to healthy controls (Supplementary Table 1). Patients with COVID-19 had three times higher insulin and C-peptide than those in the healthy control group (Supplementary Table 1). Sub-analysis based on the status of newly diagnosed DM in those with COVID-19 showed significantly higher levels of FPG, HbA1c, and TyG index compared to those without DM infected with COVID-19, while all glycaemic markers (FPG, HbA1c, HOMA-IR, HOMA-CP, and TyG index) were significantly lower in the healthy control group (p < 0.001; Table 2, Supplementary Table 2). Haemoglobin levels were similar in COVID-19 patients with and without DM (Table 2), and although lower than in healthy controls (Supplementary Table 1), median values in both groups remained within the normal range, making it unlikely to influence the interpretation of HbA1c results. Among those admitted with COVID-19, patients with newly diagnosed DM displayed a significantly higher HOMA-IR and TyG index compared to those admitted with COVID-19 without DM (Table 2). Of the participants with newly diagnosed DM, only two tested positive for anti-GAD antibodies; one of these patients was also positive for both anti-GAD and anti-IA2 antibodies. Both individuals were over 50 years of age, had a BMI greater than 30 kg/m2, and exhibited biochemical features indicative of IR (HOMA-IR 13.4 and 75.8). On admission, a higher TyG index was significantly associated with a higher HbA1c (ρ = 0.76, 95% CI, 0.52–0.89; p < 0.0001), ferritin (ρ = 0.60, 95% CI, 0.27–0.81; p = 0.001) and TNF-α (ρ = 0.42, 95% CI, 0.03–0.70; p = 0.03) (Supplementary Table 4), but not with other inflammatory markers (Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, the TyG index was not significantly correlated with HOMA-IR and HOMA-CP (p = 0.10; Supplementary Table 4).

Inflammatory profile in patients admitted with COVID-19

Patients admitted with COVID-19 had significantly higher markers of inflammation, including CRP, ferritin, LDH and higher Th1 (IL-2, IL-12, IFN- γ, TNF-α), Th2 (IL-10) and Th17 (IL-6, IL-1β, IL-17) mediated immune responses, as compared to healthy controls (Supplementary Table 1). TNF-α concentrations were significantly higher in COVID-19 patients with newly diagnosed DM compared to healthy controls (p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 2). Among those with COVID-19 and newly diagnosed DM, significantly lower IL-10 concentrations were observed compared to patients with COVID-19 without DM (Table 3, Supplementary Table 2). IL-1β in those with COVID-19 without DM (median (IQR: 1.8 (0.50, 3.42) pg/ml) was comparable to healthy controls (median (IQR: 1.5 (1.2, 2.1) pg/ml) (Supplementary Table 2).

Predictive diagnostic models of newly diagnosed DM

Univariate and multivariable regression analysis were performed to identify factors that predict newly diagnosed DM. The ROC for HOMA-IR, TyG index, IL-10 and IL-1β for predicting newly diagnosed DM is represented in Fig. 1. A TyG index > 5.08 had an AUC of 0.73 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.86) in predicting newly diagnosed DM. In univariate analysis, the TyG index had a crude odds ratio (COR) of 11.25 (95% CI, 2.80–76.28.80.28, p = 0.01) (Supplementary Table 3). For this study, we considered four predictive diagnostic models to identify potential predictors of newly diagnosed DM. The first model of predicting newly diagnosed DM included markers of IR, TyG index, HOMA-IR, IL-10 and IL1-β (Table 4). Considering the high prevalence of obesity in those with COVID-19 and newly diagnosed DM within our sample, we aimed to assess whether incorporating BMI could enhance the model’s performance (Tables 5, 6 and 7). When BMI was added to Model 2 (M_2), HOMA-IR was no longer significantly associated with newly diagnosed DM (Table 5). In addition, the predictive performance of the model increased from 0.87 (AUC = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.79–0.95) for Model 1(M_1) to 0.90 (AUC = 0.90; 95% CI, 0.83–0.97) for M_2 (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the removal of HOMA-IR did not reduce the model’s discriminative ability (AUC = 0.91; 95% CI, 0.84–0.98) (Table 6; Fig. 2). The odds of developing newly diagnosed DM increased by 14% for every 1-unit increase in BMI (AOR = 1.14; 95% CI: 1.04–1.28) (Table 6). When assessing the impact of BMI on model performance, we found that incorporating BMI with the TyG index in Model 4 (M_4) showed an AUC of 0.86 (95% CI, 0.77–0.95), demonstrating improved predictive accuracy. As illustrated in Table 8, Model 2 (M_2) and Model 3 (M_3) achieved the lowest AIC values and the highest log-likelihood estimates, indicating improved model performance compared to Model 1 (M_1). However, the addition of HOMA-IR in Model 2 (M_2) did not significantly improve model fit (likelihood ratio test, p = 0.07), consistent with the results shown in Fig. 2. The inclusion of IL-10 and IL-1β in Model 3 (M_3) further improved model performance compared to Model 4 (M_4), as supported by a significant likelihood ratio test (p = 0.022). Figure 2 shows that Model 3 had the highest area under the curve (AUC), with a 10-fold cross-validated AUC of 0.91 and a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.40.

Outcomes and follow-up of newly diagnosed DM in patients admitted with COVID-19

Of those diagnosed with newly diagnosed DM, 12 (27%) had been initiated on metformin during the admission and 15 (33%) required insulin, specifically human insulin. Overall, 27 out of the 84 patients admitted with COVID-19 (32%) died during the study period. Of the 57 patients who were discharged, 44 (77%) were followed up at three months. Among those with newly diagnosed DM, 13 (29%) died, and 32 (71%) were discharged, with 7 (22%) lost to follow-up (Supplementary Fig. 1). Of the remaining 25 (78%) patients with newly diagnosed DM who were followed up, 64% met the criteria for remission of DM. Those with persistent disease (36%) exhibited significantly higher FPG and HbA1c levels at baseline compared to those who achieved remission, along with a greater reduction in FPG at follow-up (Supplementary Tables 5, 6 and 7). Patients who experienced remission of DM had a decrease in HbA1c of 1.1%, whereas those with persistent DM showed a decrease of 0.80%. (Supplementary Table 7). Only 5 patients with newly diagnosed DM were discharged on insulin therapy, and all had persistent DM on follow-up. Additionally, patients with persistent DM had higher BMI measurements at both admission and follow-up (Supplementary Tables 5, 6 and 7), as well as higher markers of IR. Significantly higher levels of HOMA-IR (p = 0.02), HOMA-CP (p = 0.01), TyG index (p = 0.03), and C-peptide (p = 0.02) were observed in patients admitted with newly diagnosed DM with persistent DM at follow-up as compared to those achieving remission (Supplementary Table 6). Significantly higher levels of HOMA-IR, HOMA-CP, TyG index, insulin, and C-peptide were observed in patients admitted with newly diagnosed DM with persistent DM at follow-up (Supplementary Table 6). Although not statistically significant, an overall downward trend in IR markers (HOMA-IR and TyG index) was noted in those with newly diagnosed DM at follow-up, across both the remission and persistent dysglycaemia subgroups (Supplementary Table 7). In all those admitted with COVID-19 without DM, there were no reported cases of new DM at follow-up.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional pilot study of patients hospitalised with moderate to severe COVID-19, over half developed newly diagnosed DM after excluding those with pre-existing DM. Our findings support the hypothesis that IR, rather than insulin deficiency, is the predominant mechanism of newly diagnosed DM in this urban South African population. The TyG index, a pragmatic surrogate of IR, together with BMI, improved the diagnostic accuracy of predicting newly diagnosed DM, offering a cost-effective alternative to more complex measures such as HOMA-IR. These markers are especially practical in resource-limited settings, given their established links to T2DM and cardiovascular risk [41, 42]. The addition of BMI and selective inflammatory markers further enhanced diagnostic performance, yielding a robust model for stratifying risk of newly diagnosed DM in hospitalised COVID-19 patients.

SARS-CoV-2 has been implicated in the triggering of autoimmune responses, including T1DM, as viral infections can induce autoimmunity through mechanisms like molecular mimicry and bystander activation [8]. However, current evidence shows no significant association between SARS-CoV-2 and the presence of islet autoantibodies [8, 43]. In our sample population, pancreatic autoantibodies were predominantly negative, with only two individuals with newly diagnosed DM testing positive for anti-GAD antibodies. This suggests that autoimmune mechanisms are unlikely to be the main contributors of newly diagnosed DM in this population. These findings are consistent with national data, where type 1 DM affects approximately 32 000 of over 2.3 million people living with DM in South Africa [44, 45]. The identification of two autoimmune DM cases in our study population remains consistent with existing incidence rates and supports the need for continued surveillance and immunological profiling in COVID-19-associated dysglycaemia. Individuals with positive anti-GAD antibodies demonstrated features suggestive of IR, as reflected by elevated HOMA-IR scores, further supporting IR as the dominant mechanism in newly diagnosed DM among COVID-19 patients. In contrast, HOMA-CP proved to be an ineffective predictor of DM in this setting.

Analysis of glycaemic characteristics in our study population revealed significantly higher FPG and HbA1c levels in patients hospitalised with COVID-19 compared to healthy controls. These findings are consistent with our previous study from the first wave of the pandemic, which also identified hyperglycaemia as a key predictor of poor outcomes in COVID-19 [5]. Markers of insulin secretion and resistance, including insulin, C-peptide, HOMA-IR, HOMA-CP, and the TyG index, were significantly elevated in COVID-19 patients relative to healthy controls, with HOMA-IR and TyG index particularly increased in those with newly diagnosed DM. Collectively, these findings support IR as central to the pathogenesis of COVID-19-related hyperglycaemia. IR develops when insulin fails to act effectively on skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue, typically due to post-receptor signalling defects rather than receptor abnormalities [15]. Although glucocorticoid therapy can exacerbate hyperglycaemia through both IR and β-cell dysfunction, emerging evidence suggests that IR occurs independently of steroid use [10, 16]. Severe COVID-19, particularly in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), is associated with more pronounced IR and hyperglycaemia, accompanied by elevated C-peptide and amylin levels, indicative of β-cell hypersecretion rather than failure [16]. These observations highlight the metabolic disturbances associated with moderate-to-severe disease and provide mechanistic insight into one of the principal pathways contributing to newly diagnosed DM in this context.

Within our study sample, a TyG index ≥5.08 was associated with 11.25 times increased odds for newly diagnosed DM. Although HOMA-IR was significantly higher in those with newly diagnosed DM compared to those without, it did not remain significant when combined with BMI in the diagnostic models. These findings highlight the potential utility of the TyG index as a simple and accessible marker for identifying individuals at risk of COVID-19 related dysglycaemia. Observational and case-control studies have similarly reported higher HOMA-IR, FPG, and insulin levels in COVID-19 patients compared to controls, supporting the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and IR [10, 46]. However, while HOMA-IR has been associated with COVID-19 severity and long-term metabolic dysfunction, it primarily reflects hepatic rather than peripheral IR, and its predictive value for newly diagnosed DM appears limited once confounders such as BMI are considered [10, 47–49]. This divergence may be attributed to methodological differences and the physiological variability of fasting insulin, which can be affected by stress responses, β-cell hypersecretion, and glucocorticoid use, common features in acute illness. In contrast, the TyG index, derived from fasting triglycerides and glucose, may provide a more stable reflection of peripheral IR that may be less vulnerable to acute fluctuations. Large scale data from the PURE study demonstrated that the TyG index is significantly associated with incident T2DM and cardiovascular disease, particularly in LMIC populations, where vulnerability to IR may be heightened [22]. TyG index also correlates with future cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, and stroke, highlighting the role of IR in the pathogenesis of cardiometabolic disease [22]. The TyG index is a simple and cost-effective marker that serves as an independent predictor for the onset of metabolic syndrome and T2DM in patients with COVID-19, as well as for the risk of severe illness and mortality associated with the virus [50]. Furthermore, it has been shown to independently predict metabolic syndrome, T2DM, and severe COVID-19 outcomes with greater accuracy than traditional methods like HOMA-IR [27, 41, 50]. Together, these findings highlight the TyG index as a low-cost and reliable marker for early detection of metabolic complications, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Future research should investigate the mechanisms by which COVID-19 induces IR, possibly via adiposity, inflammation, or direct β-cell effects, to refine prevention and treatment strategies.

In our study, BMI was significantly higher in patients with COVID-19 compared to healthy controls, consistent with international cohorts reporting a 1.31-fold higher pooled risk of severe COVID-19 among overweight individuals [51, 52]. The significantly higher median BMI of 37 kg/m2 in patients with newly diagnosed DM, compared to 26 kg/m2 in those without, emphasises the strong association between obesity and dysglycaemia. Furthermore, a significantly greater proportion of patients with newly diagnosed DM were obese, reinforcing obesity as a critical risk factor. Combining BMI with the TyG index further improved diagnostic performance, supporting the value of these simple, non-invasive surrogates of IR, both of which have been linked to T2DM and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [53]. Although visceral obesity is traditionally considered a superior marker of IR, recent data suggest that BMI performs comparably, with no added predictive benefit when substituting visceral fat area (VFA) for BMI [18]. Obesity, now recognised as a chronic metabolic condition, contributes to the development of IR through several mechanisms [1]. Adipose tissue acts as an active endocrine organ and expresses high levels of ACE-2, potentially facilitating SARS-CoV-2 entry [14, 54, 55]. The accumulation of excess adipose tissue also promotes inflammation via dysregulated adipokines, characterised by reduced anti-inflammatory adiponectin and increased pro-inflammatory leptin [1, 8, 16, 55]. Other proposed mechanisms include the upregulation of the RE1-silencing transcription factor (REST), which may alter metabolic pathways and contribute to long-term complications independent of pre-existing metabolic status [10, 13, 54].

The absence of pre-COVID-19 insulin sensitivity data limits our ability to exclude pre-existing IR, especially since the baseline BMI was higher in infected patients compared to healthy controls. Within the follow-up study group, patients with persistent DM had greater baseline BMI than those who achieved remission. Interestingly, despite BMI increasing at follow-up in both groups, markers of IR (HOMA-IR and TyG index) decreased, with no significant correlation observed between BMI and IR indices. This pattern aligns with studies reporting that COVID-19 can increase IR prevalence independently of BMI or obesity, with inconsistent associations between IR and adiposity [1, 10, 54, 56]. These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of COVID-19-related dysglycaemia, where systemic inflammation and immune-mediated metabolic disruption may contribute beyond adiposity alone.

In our study, inflammatory markers, including CRP, ferritin, IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β were increased in individuals with COVID-19 compared to healthy controls. Among those with newly diagnosed DM, IL-6 and TNF-α levels were significantly higher compared to healthy controls, while IL-10 levels were significantly lower in those with COVID-19 without DM. Our study demonstrated that lower IL-10 concentrations were independently associated of newly diagnosed DM when assessed alongside IR markers, including the TyG index and BMI. Individuals with IL-10 levels ≥ 67.69 pg/mL had 88% lower odds of developing newly diagnosed DM, while elevated IL-1β was associated with a five-fold increased risk. These findings highlight the contribution of pro-inflammatory cytokine activity, alongside reduced anti-inflammatory signalling, to the pathophysiology of COVID-19-associated dysglycaemia.

These findings are consistent with studies demonstrating that COVID-19 provokes a cytokine storm that contributes to IR and hyperglycemia, partly by impairing pancreatic β-cell function, particularly in individuals with underlying metabolic risk factors [1, 8, 57]. Chronic IL-6 exposure has been shown to impair insulin sensitivity [1, 16], while TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1, secreted from adipose tissue and elevated in severe COVID-19 further disrupt glucose metabolism [1, 54, 57]. Elevated CRP and IL-6, in combination with reduced IL-10, have been associated with reduced insulin sensitivity [58]. Mechanistically, pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-1β contribute to IR by activating protein kinase cascades that induce serine/threonine phosphorylation of IRS-1/2, impairing insulin signalling and glucose uptake [59]. IL-1β has been implicated in β-cell dysfunction and impaired glucose tolerance, with meta-analyses supporting its association with an increased risk of newly diagnosed DM, including T1DM, T2DM, and GDM [60–63]. Additionally, IL-1β may contribute to disease progression from prediabetes to overt T2DM by promoting β-cell failure and inflammation [50, 61].

Evidence suggests that an imbalance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines plays a central role in obesity and the development of IR and T2DM, however, there is limited research on the association of anti-inflammatory markers in newly diagnosed DM and COVID-19 [1, 26, 54, 57]. IL-10, a key anti-inflammatory cytokine, regulates immune responses by inhibiting pro-inflammatory mediators in macrophages and lymphocytes [26, 64]. Reduced IL-10 has been linked to IR, obesity and T2DM, with studies reporting IL-10 hypo-responsiveness or “IL-10 resistance” and reduced receptor expression in hyperglycemic states, potentially contributing to the progression of DM [26, 64, 65]. Our findings are consistent with these observations, supporting IL-10 as a potential biomarker for early detection of COVID-19 related DM and its utility in risk stratification. The lack of significant differences in other inflammatory markers between COVID-19 patients with and without newly diagnosed DM suggests that additional mechanisms, such as genetic predisposition, or variation in ACE2 and DPP4 polymorphisms, may also influence susceptibility and warrant further investigation.

Longitudinal studies report that nearly 50% of COVID-19 patients without prior DM develop new-onset hyperglycemia during acute illness, with many sustaining dysglycaemia for months post-recovery, highlighting the need for ongoing DM surveillance [13, 54, 66]. The natural history of COVID-19 associated DM remains uncertain, as most available data are derived from hospitalised patients with severe disease. In our sample population, 64% achieved remission of DM at 3-month follow-up. This aligns with previous reports showing that 41% to 79% of hospitalised COVID-19 patients, including those initially presenting with diabetic ketoacidosis, regress to normoglycemia or pre-diabetes after discharge [8, 10, 66–69]. It is important to acknowledge, however, that stress hyperglycaemia may persist beyond three months, and a longer follow-up is required to definitively distinguish transient from persistent DM. These observations suggest that COVID-19 and its treatments, such as glucocorticoids may unmask predisposition to DM or trigger transient stress hyperglycemia through exacerbation of IR. Clinically, this highlights the importance of post-discharge monitoring to avoid unnecessary long-term therapy and reduce hypoglycaemia risk.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strengths lie in its prospective assessment of IR as a key pathophysiological mechanism for newly diagnosed DM, coupled with follow-up evaluations to assess remission. Such research is limited, particularly in resource-constrained settings, providing important insights into disease dynamics in LMICs. However, the single-centre design, potential selection bias due to non-randomisation, modest sample size, lack of pre-COVID-19 baseline data, and the potential contribution of comorbidities to inflammation may restrict the generalisability of the findings and complicate causal inference. The predominantly Black African sample population, with a high prevalence of obesity among newly diagnosed DM cases, reflects the real-world demographic and disease burden locally but may reduce applicability to more diverse populations. Inclusion of both general ward and ICU patients introduces clinical heterogeneity. However, all participants had moderate to severe COVID-19 and were enrolled at the point of emergency department presentation, with sampling conducted before ICU-specific interventions or treatment initiation, thereby minimising treatment-related confounding. As the study was conducted before the widespread vaccine rollout, the impact of COVID-19 vaccination on disease severity and metabolic outcomes could not be fully assessed. Only 9% of individuals with newly diagnosed DM and 10% of those without DM had received at least one vaccine dose prior to admission, with no significant difference in vaccination status between subgroups, making it unlikely to have influenced or biased the results.

Several factors can influence HbA1c measurements, including variations in haemoglobin, differences in glycation rates, and changes in erythrocyte lifespan related to various medical conditions [38]. Consequently, some individuals may present with normal HbA1c levels despite having elevated glucose levels or vice versa [38]. However, as haemoglobin levels in our study remained within normal reference ranges across groups, this is unlikely to have significantly biased the interpretation of HbA1c. Another potential limitation of our study lies in how we assessed IR. While the TyG index is a well-validated and pragmatic tool, outcomes may differ if alternative methods such as the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp or Matsuda index were used. Additionally, although we referenced the commonly used HOMA-IR threshold of ≥ 2.5, this lacks universal standardisation, and its interpretation should consider population-specific validation. Similarly, the TyG index cutoff for IR may vary across ethnic groups, and further validation in African populations is warranted to establish appropriate thresholds. The reliance on a single follow-up after three months limited our ability to evaluate long-term remission, and no serial HbA1c data were collected thereafter to assess for recurrence or delayed conversion to DM. Although predictive diagnostic modelling identified factors associated with newly diagnosed DM in COVID-19, it was based on baseline cross-sectional data; therefore, temporality and causal inference cannot be established, and validation in larger prospective cohorts is required. Furthermore, the TyG index may be influenced by acute stress-related hyperglycaemia and lipid fluctuations in severe illness, and results should be interpreted with this context in mind. Validation in larger, longitudinal cohorts, including post-acute and recovery phases, will be important to confirm its utility. Finally, the wide 95% confidence intervals in our regression models likely reflect the modest sample size and number of outcome-positive cases. Although model complexity was minimised to avoid overfitting, the small number of patients remains a key limitation, and this should be considered when interpreting the generalisability of the findings.

Recommendations and future studies

This pilot study highlights the need for larger, longitudinal studies with serial monitoring of glycaemic indices and diabetes-related complications [38]. To build on these findings, future research should focus on larger cohorts with extended follow-up periods to accurately assess the long-term outcomes associated with newly diagnosed DM in COVID-19 patients. The TyG-BMI index, a simple and reliable marker reflecting both IR and obesity, has recently shown superior predictive value for 365-day mortality and major cardiovascular events in severe coronary heart disease [42], highlighting the need for future studies to validate its accuracy and utility in diverse populations like ours, for improved risk stratification and individualised care. Investigating interventions aimed at reducing IR in these individuals will be essential for enhancing prevention and management strategies. Additionally, developing community-based programs that address both obesity and metabolic health post-COVID-19 could be beneficial in reducing the incidence of DM in vulnerable populations. Ongoing studies within our study population are evaluating ACE-2 and DPP4 concentrations, as well as genetic variants such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which may influence susceptibility to COVID-19-related dysglycemia and metabolic dysfunction persistence. Understanding these polymorphisms could elucidate host response variability and inform targeted prevention strategies. Future directions should also include larger randomised controlled trials and the inclusion of additional metabolic and inflammatory markers to refine and validate predictive diagnostic models.

Conclusion

This pilot study highlights IR as a key mechanism associated with newly diagnosed DM among hospitalised patients with moderate-to-severe COVID-19, without pre-existing DM, within a predominantly Black African study population. The TyG index and BMI may serve as simple, accessible tools for risk stratification during viral pandemics and guide targeted post-COVID-19 interventions. However, validation in larger, more diverse cohorts is required.
