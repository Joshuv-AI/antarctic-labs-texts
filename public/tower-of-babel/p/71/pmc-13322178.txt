Asymmetric niche partitioning in large omnivores in response to anthropogenic disturbances within subarctic ecosystems
Authors: Ludovick Brown, Chrystal Mantyka‐Pringle, Joël Potié, Pascale Savage, Megan Hornseth, Jason T. Fisher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Niche partitioning is an evolutionary process that allows the coexistence of multiple species in a landscape. However, human disturbance can interfere with this process. For instance, humans may displace competitors and favour species with a greater tolerance for disturbances. American black bears (Ursus americanus) and grizzly bears (U. arctos) are two large omnivores that share similar ecological niches, but that can also coexist by partitioning resources.Understanding the consequences of anthropogenic disturbance on niche partitioning in these two species is important to preserve biodiversity and ecosystem function. This importance is particularly relevant in landscapes located in subarctic biomes where resource availability is limited.In this study, we used data from wildlife cameras deployed at 194 sites in central Yukon during 2020–2023 to determine whether anthropogenic disturbances facilitated, or disrupted, spatiotemporal niche partitioning in American black bears and grizzly bears.Kernel density functions revealed that grizzly bears modified their diel activity pattern in the higher‐disturbance landscape during the afternoon, presumably to avoid interactions with humans. This new peak coincidentally promoted temporal niche partitioning with black bears, whose activity pattern remained static across the gradient of disturbance. The bear species spatially segregate, but anthropogenic disturbances had a much stronger effect on grizzly bear occurrences compared to black bears, signalling an asymmetric response.Anthropogenic disturbances constrained grizzly bears to the lower‐disturbance landscape. Black bears could take advantage of reduced competition and partially fill the spatiotemporal niche of grizzly bears, but our results did not show that black bears concentrated their activity in areas avoided by grizzly bears. The displacement of grizzly bears may lead to shifts in prey populations or vegetation communities, with unknown consequences for ecosystem structure.

INTRODUCTION

Anthropogenic disturbances are major drivers of environmental change and have the potential to interfere with behavioural strategies evolved to promote stable coexistence within animal communities (Gilbert et al., 2022). The evolved strategy niche partitioning allows multiple species to coexist by feeding on different resources, using different habitats, or spatially or temporally segregating. Anthropogenic disturbances can influence the spatial, trophic and temporal dimensions of niche partitioning by altering resource availability or interfering in interspecific interactions (Gilbert et al., 2022; Sévêque et al., 2020; Thakur et al., 2023). Large carnivores and low fecundity species are often disproportionately affected by anthropogenic disturbance because of their greater space requirements, inelastic population growth and more frequent conflicts with humans (Suraci et al., 2021), thereby leading to competitive exclusion or even local extinction of species with low tolerance to human disturbances (Grimm et al., 2008). These species are often replaced by smaller, lower‐trophic, more fecund species, which constitutes losses of biodiversity and ultimately reduces the complexity of mammalian communities (Sévêque et al., 2020; Suraci et al., 2021). Avoidance of human disturbance or disturbed sites may also increase the risk of predation (Patten et al., 2019) or promote niche overlap (Manlick & Pauli, 2020), which may ultimately jeopardize the mechanisms that regulate coexistence in mammal communities.

In addition to human presence, the anthropogenic spatial footprint left by resource extraction impacts wildlife by altering resource availability and distribution, especially in northern boreal environments (Fisher & Burton, 2018; Fisher & Wilkinson, 2005). Far northern landscapes are mineral‐rich and resource extraction activities—particularly mining—are major drivers of landscape change. In Norway and northern British Columbia (Canada), reindeer (Rangifer tarandus) and northern mountain caribou (R. tarandus) avoid mines, especially during high‐disturbance periods (Eftestøl et al., 2019; Polfus et al., 2011); in addition, caribou avoid major and minor roads (Polfus et al., 2011). In the Canadian Northwest Territories, mining development had the strongest negative effect on the occurrence of large mammals, but responses varied among species (especially large carnivores) and seasons (Johnson et al., 2005). This is an example of an asymmetric response, which can be defined as unequal or opposite responses to the same disturbance (Burdon et al., 2023). A disturbance may indeed have very different consequences on the species within a community because of different degrees of behavioural plasticity, which for instance, may allow some species to persist in highly disturbed landscapes (Caspi et al., 2022). The consequences of anthropogenic disturbances disparately affect interacting species, often with asymmetric outcomes (Fisher & Ladle, 2022).

In the western subarctic, American black bears (Ursus americanus; ‘black bears’) and grizzly bears (U. arctos) are two large omnivores that can occupy similar ecological niches across their range, but they are able to coexist by partitioning all niche components (Apps et al., 2006; Merkle et al., 2017; Schwartz et al., 2010). In Alaska, both species eat salmon when they are abundant, whereas only grizzly bears eat salmon when they are scarce (Belant et al., 2006). Black bears are more adaptable to human disturbance than grizzly bears, which typically exhibit lower spatiotemporal overlap with humans (Apps et al., 2006; Ladle et al., 2018). A higher tolerance for human disturbance could favour black bears and lead to competitive exclusion of grizzly bears as food supplies become functionally lost due to the presence of humans on the landscape; however, even tolerant species can reach a threshold of human disturbance at which their habitats become unsuitable (Suraci et al., 2021). In such cases, similar species may be forced to share a smaller portion of the landscape when developed areas become functionally lost, thereby exacerbating the competitive interactions between the species (Gilbert et al., 2022). It is unclear how anthropogenic disturbance could affect the dynamic between the two large omnivores in subarctic ecosystems, where food supplies are limited.

We ask whether anthropogenic disturbances affect niche partitioning in sympatric grizzly and black bears in a northern, low‐productivity landscape. In this subarctic ecosystem, anthropogenic disturbances are primarily associated with mineral exploration and extraction through hard‐rock (e.g. quartz) mining and placer mining, where minerals are extracted from streambed deposits. Despite the global spatial extent of subarctic biomes, they are markedly understudied. In particular, studies in landscapes where disturbance is largely due to mineral extraction are rare, despite the extent of mining activities in northern ecosystems.

We used camera traps (Burton et al., 2015; Rovero & Zimmermann, 2016) to quantify grizzly and black bear occurrence across a wide disturbance gradient. We hypothesized that human disturbance affects spatiotemporal niche partitioning in bears: We predicted that grizzly bears would be detected less frequently at camera traps during the day (when humans are mostly active), whereas black bears would be detected more frequently during the diurnal period presumably to avoid interactions with grizzly bears. We also predicted that areas with higher densities of linear features (associated with resource extraction and human activity) would be associated with lower occurrence probabilities of grizzly bears and that black bear occurrence would increase in the same areas. Alternatively, we predicted that both bear species would display similar avoidance of areas with higher densities of linear features, which would force them to share a smaller portion of the landscape. In summary, our conjecture is that anthropogenic disturbance reshapes the spatial and temporal outcomes of risk–reward trade‐offs differently for each bear species, mediated by each species' perception of (or tolerance to) risk.

METHODS

Study area

Our study took place between July 2020 and September 2023 over a ~5000 km2 area within the Traditional Territory of the First Nation of Na‐Cho Nyäk Dun in central Yukon, Canada (Figure 1), with the centroid of the study area approximately 52 km northeast of the community of Mayo. The study area spans the Beaver River (lower disturbance) and Stewart River (higher disturbance) watersheds. It sits within the boreal cordillera and taiga cordillera ecozones and is characterized by diverse habitats, including wide lowland and upland forests in the southern extent of the study area and extensive rugged mountains with valleys, wetland complexes and alpine tundra across the north. The vegetation is predominantly composed of black spruce (Picea mariana), white spruce (P. glauca), aspen (Populus tremuloides), sub‐alpine fir (Abies lasiocarpa), willow (Salix spp.), shrub birch (Betula nana) and patchy distribution of berry shrubs (Shepherdia canadensis, Vaccinium spp. and Arctostaphylos spp.).

Map of the study area in central Yukon. The map shows camera locations (Points, n = 194), anthropogenic linear features (lines) and water bodies (polygons). Orange and blue points correspond to the higher‐ (Stewart River) and lower‐disturbance (Beaver River) regions of the study area, respectively.

The study area features a gradient of landscape disturbances within the Silver Trail Region, historically known for the silver mining boom and bust of the 1920s. This resource‐rich area continues to attract mineral exploration and extraction activities (gold, silver, lead and zinc), contributing to ongoing landscape changes and increasing human impacts. The study area includes the footprints of two quartz mines: Keno Hill mine operated from 1913 to 1989 and became active again in 2020. Eagle Gold mine was constructed in 2018 and operational until 2024; it is currently under remediation and monitoring. Keno Hill is an underground quartz mine and Eagle Gold is an open‐pit quartz mine. When active, these quartz mines operate 24‐h a day and include a high volume of traffic (e.g. ore transport, contractors, monitors and workers) associated with the mine sites. Roads are gated at quartz mine sites to limit recreational access. Vegetation is removed from the mine site, which also includes camp facilities for over 150 people, tailings ponds, and, at Eagle Gold, a heap leach facility. There are approximately 15 active exploration quartz permits in the study area in both the higher‐ and lower‐disturbance areas, as well as adjacent to the quartz mines. Disturbance associated with advanced quartz exploration includes frequent helicopter traffic, trenching, small camps (<20 people) with crews active 12–24 h/day, core extraction, survey lines, and in some cases access roads. Quartz exploration and placer gold mining create roads and trails that are accessible by hunters and recreationalists.

Disturbance associated with placer mining includes vegetation and overburden removal within the stream bed and adjacent riparian areas, as well as smaller camps (typically supporting 10–50 people), cut lines, trails, access roads and tailings ponds. A placer is a deposit of sand or gravel in the bed of a river or lake, containing particles of valuable minerals (e.g. gold). The density anthropogenic features both historical (inactive) and active (e.g. roads, trails, cutlines) is highest in the southwestern portion of the study area, while the northeastern section remains relatively intact, with minimal human development and no road access. Helicopters are the only means of transportation to conduct quartz exploration in the lower‐disturbance area.

Study design and camera trapping

We employed a spatially stratified sampling design in which we overlaid a 5 × 5 km2 grid array to summarize anthropogenic and natural disturbance features: a cell size that traded off target species' home‐range size and the resolution of landcover heterogeneity (Burton et al., 2015). We allotted grid cells to strata defining natural and anthropogenic (areal and linear) features, road access, proximity to a proposed 65‐km access road and fire regrowth stages. We deployed wildlife cameras (Reconnyx HyperFire 2 Professional, Holman, WI) at 194 sites, with 87 sites accessible by road, 5 accessible by boat, and 102 requiring helicopter access. Selected grid cells contained up to two cameras spaced ≥1.5 km apart and positioned ≥1 km from active dwellings and camps, ≥50 m from roads. Camera sites were selected based on the presence of a game trail; helicopter access sites were in nearby proximity to a clearing (<400 m). Most cameras were active June 2020 – August 2023 with annual checks to retrieve data and change batteries. Cameras were moved if >5 independent photos of humans were captured, or if there was an active dwelling or camp within 1 km to minimize human–wildlife conflicts and avoid interfering with mining activities.

Cameras were mounted on trees approximately 1.7 m above ground, oriented in a northerly direction (range: east to northwest) to minimize false triggers, with the field view capturing the trail and a designated scent lure tree 4–7 m from the camera. Scent lure (O'Gorman's Long Distance Call, Broadus MT USA) was applied annually to holes drilled into a tree. Cameras were programmed to take a single time‐lapse photo daily at noon to monitor environmental changes and verify functionality. Upon heat or motion activation, cameras captured a burst of five consecutive photos with no delay between activations.

This study did not require ethical approval and fieldwork permits.

Image processing

Photos were downloaded and processed annually using the MegaDetector object detection model (Beery et al., 2019). The resulting image recognition data were used to filter out empty frames by retaining capture events (i.e. a series of images captured within less than 1.5 min of one another) containing an identified animal with a confidence threshold of 75% or greater. Each image was manually reviewed using TimeLapse2 (Greenberg et al., 2019) and bears were identified to the species level and counted. We defined independent detections as all images of a species at a site within a 30‐min window.

Covariates

We quantified landscape‐scale vegetation data from Macander et al. (2022) including the cover of coniferous trees, broadleaf trees, graminoids, forbs, evergreen shrubs and deciduous shrubs. We derived the Terrain ruggedness index (TRI) from a 30 m digital elevation model (Government of Yukon, 2021) by using the TRI tool in QGIS version 3.34.1 (QGIS Development Team, 2023). Wildfire areas were obtained from the Yukon fire maps (Government of Yukon, 2023a) and were classified into three categories following (Fisher & Wilkinson, 2005): old burns (<2000), regenerating burns (2000–2015) and more recent burns (>2015). Anthropogenic disturbances were quantified from a surface disturbance linear features dataset (Government of Yukon, 2023b) including cutlines, trails and roads. All linear features in the database are >3 m wide. Cutlines are linear features cleared of vegetation and are associated with mining claims; trails are linear features used for travel, but not for resource extraction. We defined roads as all other linear features associated with transportation and resource extraction. Polygonal features, while present in the database, had high collinearity with linear features and could not be included in the model.

We extracted the mean percentage of cover for each type of vegetation within circular buffers (radius = 250, 500 and 1000 m) centred on camera traps, sensu Holland et al. (2004) and Fisher et al. (2011). We also calculated the total burnt surface (m2) as well as the density of linear features (total km/km2) within these buffers. To select the most appropriate scale for each variable, we built univariate models comparing the same variable at the three different scales and compared them by using Akaike information criterion (Burnham & Anderson, 2002) corrected for sample size (AICc) from the package AICcmodavg (Mazerolle, 2023). We selected the scale with the lowest AICc value for each species and season (Spring, Summer and Fall) and used them in the models described in Table 1. See Figures S1–S6 for detailed results regarding univariate scale selection. All analyses were carried out in R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024).

Statistical analyses

Temporal niche partitioning

Diel activity patterns are quantifiable from camera traps using several methods (Frey et al., 2017). We used kernel density functions to compare the daily activity patterns of black and grizzly bears within lower‐disturbance and higher‐disturbance areas of the study region (Ridout & Linkie, 2009). The probability density functions were obtained by using the timestamps (i.e. hours) of independent observations by using the overlapPlot function from the overlap package Version 0.3.9 (Meredith et al., 2024). We also obtained coefficients of overlap from the overlapEst function to quantify the extent of the overlap in the activity patterns of bears.

We compared the annual activity patterns of black and grizzly bears across a gradient of disturbance by fitting a generalized additive mixed effect model (GAMM) with a binomial family (logit link function) on weekly occurrences (gam function; mgcv package Version 1.9‐1; Wood, 2011). This modelling approach allowed us to account for nonlinearity in weekly occurrences during the year. Thus, we were able to model seasonal variations continuously instead of discretizing bear occurrences into seasons. The GAMM included species, week of the year with a cubic cyclic spline and road density within 250 m of camera sites, as a continuous gradient of disturbance. We tested for road density at 500 and 1000 m scales (Table S1) but we only present the results at 250 m in the main text (Table S1). We also included an interaction term for species with the ‘by’ function (for week and road density) and added the camera ID as a random intercept. We included sampling year as a fixed effect (4‐level factor) to test for between‐year variability, but the beta estimates were not concordant with an important effect (0.709 ≥ p ≥ 0.463) and this term was thus excluded from subsequent analyses.

Spatial niche partitioning

We created a set of biologically plausible competing models with a combination of vegetation, topography, fire history and linear features, for each species during the spring, summer and fall (Table 1). We defined the spring as weeks 15 to 24, the summer as weeks 25 to 34 and fall as weeks 35 to 45. These three seasons correspond to the approximate active period (i.e. den exit to den entry) for bears. Winter weeks (46 to 14 of the following year) were excluded from this analysis to accommodate the approximate hibernation period for bears. We used generalized linear models (binomial family; logit link function; glm function, stats package; R Core Team, 2024) to predict the probability of weekly bear occurrence at camera traps (1 = detected; 0 = not detected). We did not use occupancy models to estimate false absences as past research shows bear probability of detection approaches 1.0 after 3 months (Fisher et al., 2014) and we sampled much longer. The set of models contained a Null model (intercept only, no effect) and a Landscape model that included only plant functional types and terrain ruggedness index (TRI). We included a quadratic term for TRI to account for suspected nonlinearity. The other models were a combination of the Landscape model with either the fire history (Fire model) or the density of linear features (Disturbance model) around camera traps. A Full model containing all the variables was also included in the set.

We identified the best‐supported model for each species during each season (spring, summer and fall) using AICc and model validation. We considered models with ΔAICc <2 of each other to be equivalent (barring uninformative parameters; Arnold, 2010), but we only discuss the results of the top model. We used variance inflation factors (VIF) to ensure low multicollinearity (check_collinearity function; performance package Version 0.12.0; Lüdecke et al., 2021); all VIF were below 5. We also used a correlation matrix to identify highly correlated variables prior to constructing models, and all variables had |r| < 0.70. We assessed the predictive performance of our models by using fivefold cross‐validation (cv.glm function; boot package Version 1.3‐30; Canty & Ripley, 2024).

RESULTS

We obtained 1040 and 324 independent occurrences of black bears and grizzly bears 2020–2023. Grizzly bears rarely occurred in the higher‐disturbance landscape (n = 27) of the study area compared to the lower‐disturbance landscape (n = 297). We obtained similar numbers of black bear occurrence in the lower‐ (n = 576) and higher‐disturbance (n = 464) regions of the study area.

Both species were mainly active between 10:00 and 20:00 in the lower‐disturbance area; activity peaked between late afternoon and early evening. The activity patterns of bears were almost identical in the lower‐disturbance region [coefficient of overlap (Δ) = 0.95]. The diel pattern remained largely unchanged for black bears in the higher‐disturbance area (Figure 2). In contrast, grizzly bears in the higher‐disturbance area exhibited a bimodal activity pattern, with a depression during mid‐day. Even though the overlap between the activity patterns of bears decreased in the higher‐disturbance region, they still overlap substantially [coefficient of overlap (Δ) = 0.81].

Black bears were overall more likely to occur at camera traps during their active period compared to grizzly bears (Figure 3); however, these two species showed strong contrasting responses to anthropogenic disturbance. A combination of landscape (i.e. vegetation and topography) variables and anthropogenic linear features best explained the probability of bear occurrence at camera traps; the best‐supported model for each combination of species and season was in all cases the Full (including fire history) or the Landscape + Disturbance model (Table 2). Both bear species reacted negatively to the density of roads on the landscape, but this response was stronger in grizzly bears during the spring and fall (Figure 4a). Trail density had a marginal effect on bear occurrence at camera traps in all seasons, except for black bears during the summer, which displayed a positive association with this variable (Figure 4b). The effects of cutline density were negative for both species during each season (Figure 4c). The fire history around camera traps also had a marginal or no effect on bear occurrence in all seasons, except during the summer. During this season, grizzly bears displayed positive associations with patches that burnt before 2015 (Figure 4e,f), and black bears were also associated with patches that burnt before 2000 (Figure 4f).

DISCUSSION

Anthropogenic disturbances associated with mineral extraction (placer and quartz) influenced space use and activity patterns in grizzly bears, and to a much lesser extent in black bears, in this subarctic ecosystem, signalling an asymmetric response. Following predictions, the probability of grizzly bear occurrence decreased during the day in the higher‐disturbance landscape of the study area; however, contrary to predictions, black bears maintained their diel activity patterns in areas affected by anthropogenic disturbance. We also found support for our second prediction that grizzly bears avoid the higher‐disturbance landscape. However, we did not find evidence that black bears concentrated their activity in sites that were less likely to be visited by grizzly bears in response to disturbances. Bear species did spatially segregate at the local scale by using different habitats, but there was no evidence of a human‐shield effect; likewise, Granados et al. (2023) in their multi‐landscape analysis found limited support for this process. We did not find support for our third prediction that both bear species would be forced to share a smaller portion of the landscape due to the asymmetric response to human disturbance.

Temporal niche partitioning

Grizzly bear activity decreased during the afternoon in the higher‐disturbance landscape, thereby suggesting temporal avoidance of anthropogenic disturbances. The changes in the activity pattern of grizzly bears in the higher‐disturbance landscape are consistent with other studies, which showed that mammals, including grizzly bears, modified their activity patterns to reduce encounters with humans by, for example, switching to nocturnality (Gaynor et al., 2018; Lamb et al., 2020). Interestingly, black bears did not appear to change their activity patterns to avoid being active at the same time as humans (i.e. during the day) and grizzly bears. Similar to our observations, motorized activities did not strongly affect the activity pattern of black bears in the central Alberta's Rocky Mountains and foothills (Ladle et al., 2018). Likewise, Fortin et al. (2013) reported that black bears from Wyoming (USA) did not avoid being active at the same time as more dominant grizzly bears. Our results illustrate an example of asymmetric response to anthropogenic disturbance, thereby reinforcing the general idea that different species can react differently to the same stressor (Burton et al., 2024; Suraci et al., 2021). Nevertheless, the activity patterns of both species considerably overlapped across the study area despite divergent responses to anthropogenic disturbance on the landscape, suggesting that bears likely coexist by partitioning the trophic or spatial dimensions of their ecological niches, rather than temporal.

Spatial niche partitioning: Vegetation

Grizzly and black bear occurrences were typically associated with different types of vegetation and landscape features, which supports the contention that bear species coexist by partitioning the trophic or spatial dimensions of their niche. Fine‐scale resource partitioning is a mechanism that promotes the coexistence of sympatric carnivores (Müller et al., 2022). Despite great overlap in all dimensions of their ecological niches, subordinates (i.e. smaller species) may be able to persist by adjusting their diet or space use to reduce niche overlap with more dominant species (Petroelje et al., 2021), which is consistent with our results. Black bears were more likely to occur at sites with higher proportion of both coniferous or deciduous trees in all seasons, whereas these variables had either no or negative effects on grizzly bear occurrence (Figure 4). Grizzly bears were instead positively associated with patches that were affected by wildfires before 2015 during the summer (Figure 4), consistent with Milakovic et al. (2012) who also found that grizzly bears in northern British Columbia were positively associated with burnt patches. We observed that grizzly bear occurrence was also positively associated with the forb and deciduous shrub cover during the summer (Figure 4). These types of vegetation can represent important food resources (i.e. roots, berries) for grizzly bears and are generally more prominent in the lower‐disturbance landscape, which may in part explain why the probability of grizzly bear occurrence was higher in this part of the study area.

Black bears were generally more likely to occur at camera traps compared to grizzly bears and the patterns that we observed (Figure 3) are in accordance with known density estimates (6 grizzly bears/1000 km2 and 25 black bears/1000 km2; Boulanger et al., 2023) for both species in our study area. To maintain optimal foraging efficiency and avoid exploitation competition from more abundant black bears, grizzly bears are typically associated with open habitats, forest edges and regenerating forests with berry shrubs (Apps et al., 2006; Sells et al., 2022). These open habitats that are typically considered as ‘grizzly habitats’ can also be used by black bears when having access to abundant resources outweighs the costs of interspecific competition or when grizzly bears are extirpated due to anthropogenic disturbances (Apps et al., 2006). Black bears are typically associated with forest cover and may be able to outcompete grizzly bears when foraging on dispersed food resources in forested habitats (Apps et al., 2006; Garneau et al., 2008). Black bears were also associated with more rugged terrain (i.e. slope) around camera locations, which has been found to promote the richness of understory vegetation by allowing sunlight to penetrate dense tree stands (Coop et al., 2010). Both bear species coexist by spatially segregating at fine spatial scales, but human disturbance may be beneficial for black bears by allowing them to access habitats that would otherwise be occupied by grizzly bears.

Spatial niche partitioning: Anthropogenic disturbances

Fine scale spatial partitioning and an asymmetric response to disturbance may have allowed both species to coexist on the landscape. Black bears in this subarctic landscape were more tolerant to human disturbances, and did not avoid linear features, as strongly as did grizzly bears. This asymmetric response had consequences for spatial overlap at the local scale by possibly allowing black bears to avoid interference competition from grizzly bears around trails and, to some extent, other linear features. Black bear occurrence increased with trail density around camera traps during the summer and trails can provide early seral vegetation forage and more efficient travel paths (Dickie et al., 2017; Roever et al., 2008; St‐Pierre et al., 2022). On the other hand, roads have a negative impact on grizzly bear mortality due to facilitating development that leads to human‐grizzly bear conflict, vehicular accidents, habituation to food sources and increased hunter access (Boulanger et al., 2013; Nielsen et al., 2004). Increased mortality resulting from human–bear conflicts, and overall low bear densities in the study area (Boulanger et al., 2023), could limit the persistence of grizzly bears in the higher‐disturbance landscape. A general decrease in groundberry cover in some parts of our study area (Krebs et al., 2023) combined with low ungulate densities (O'Donoghue et al., 2019) also likely contributed to low probabilities of grizzly bear occurrence in the higher‐disturbance region. Lamb et al. (2018) showed that reducing human access by limiting road densities below 0.6 km/km2 is an effective management tool for conserving grizzly bears, and our results align with this recommendation. The average road density around camera traps was 3.10 km/km2 in the higher‐disturbance landscape compared to 0.11 km/km2 in the lower‐disturbance landscape, which suggests that the higher‐disturbance landscape may be unsuitable for grizzly bears and that is also supported by the numbers of occurrences in each landscape (lower‐disturbance, n = 297; higher‐disturbance, n = 27). It is also possible that the lower‐disturbance landscape contains a different composition of vegetation that attracts grizzly bears; however, it appears more likely that grizzly bears are avoiding the higher‐disturbance landscape in response to anthropogenic disturbance. We observed both spatial avoidance and reduced activity during the day, which is a well‐documented mechanism that allows coexistence between humans and large mammals (Gaynor et al., 2018). Our study suggests that anthropogenic disturbances largely constrained grizzly bears to the lower‐disturbance regions of the study area.

Larger animals, such as grizzly bears, are severely affected by human disturbance and are often extirpated from disturbed areas (Haight et al., 2023). Their ecological niche can be partially filled by smaller carnivores and omnivores that are more flexible (Suraci et al., 2021) and our results support this general trend. The probability of black bear occurrence was on average lower, but more uniformly distributed within the higher‐disturbance relative to the lower‐disturbance landscapes (Figure 5). It is also important to note that even species that are deemed tolerant to anthropogenic activities may eventually reach a threshold of disturbance beyond which habitats become unsuitable (Suraci et al., 2021). Thus, increasing the level of human disturbance on the landscape may further alter the structure of the mammal community by excluding black bears and favour smaller carnivores or omnivores (Sévêque et al., 2020; Suraci et al., 2021); however, smaller species may not act as adequate substitutes for large carnivores. For example, coyotes (Canis latrans) did not elicit the same spatial avoidance behaviour as pumas (Puma concolor) in communities of meso‐carnivores (Avrin et al., 2023). Black bears and other species may not be able to fully substitute grizzly bears once they have been displaced from an area and the ecological consequences of such a shift in functional roles are unknown.

Caveats

In our analyses, we could not consider demographic groups, which may have masked contrasting results from the different groups. Age, sex and reproductive status are known for affecting space use, activity patterns and responses to human disturbance in bears and other species. For instance, females with dependent offspring may seek closer proximity to humans to protect their young from infanticide (Steyaert et al., 2016) and subadults may avoid dominant adults (Gende & Quinn, 2004). Additionally, we assumed that the avoidance of disturbance by grizzly bears was caused by a top‐down causal chain, whereby human disturbance directly affects grizzly bears. Our analyses could not confirm the possibility that human disturbance reduced the number of potential prey, thereby indirectly affecting grizzly bears through lower food availability. However, considering the large body of literature on the effect of human disturbance on grizzly bears (Fortin et al., 2016; Ladle et al., 2019; Lamb et al., 2020; Proctor et al., 2020), we believe that direct avoidance of human disturbance remains the most plausible explanation for the lower grizzly bear occurrence in some parts of our study area.

CONCLUSIONS

We showed that anthropogenic disturbance associated with mineral exploration and extraction is spatially and temporally displacing grizzly bears in this subarctic ecosystem, whereas black bears did not exhibit temporal avoidance of grizzly bears. Both bear species were associated with distinct habitat features on the landscape, and there was a strong contrast in their response to anthropogenic disturbance. Black bears may be better adapted for coexistence with humans than grizzly bears, as indicated by the greater number of black bear occurrences at camera sites in the higher‐disturbance landscape of the study area. The displacement of grizzly bears from anthropogenic disturbance may trigger functional change because black bears may not be able to fill the ecological role of grizzly bears in the ecosystem, particularly as apex predators (Avrin et al., 2023). Considering that it may be difficult to revert into grizzly landscape once black bears are well established in a system (Apps et al., 2006), future development scenarios and environmental assessments should prioritize managing linear feature density within established thresholds (e.g. Lamb et al., 2018). Future work should focus on identifying whether the displacement of grizzly bears in this system affected interspecific interactions within the mammal community and ecosystem functions.
