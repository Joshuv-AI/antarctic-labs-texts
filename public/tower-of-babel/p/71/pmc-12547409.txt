Efficacy and Safety of Tirzepatide in Refractory Hypoglycemia Associated with Esophagojejunostomy
Authors: Mariangela Caporusso, Ludovico Di Gioia, Mariangela Manicone, Lucrezia Matera, Francesco Giorgino, Sebastio Perrini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present the case of a 40-year-old woman with severe recurrent hypoglycemia, unresponsive to dietary glucose intake and occurring in the context of chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction, esophagojejunostomy, and long-term parenteral nutrition. Extensive endocrine and imaging evaluations excluded insulinoma, autoimmune hypoglycemia, and counterregulatory hormone deficiencies, suggesting impaired incretin function as the likely mechanism. Conventional treatments—including acarbose, glucocorticoids, somatostatin analogues, and a long-acting glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA)—failed to provide adequate glycemic control. Given the exclusion of the proximal duodenum from nutrient transit and its key role in glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) secretion, therapy with tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist, was initiated. Tirzepatide led to near-complete remission of hypoglycemic episodes and substantial improvement in glycemic variability and quality of life. Due to recurrence of symptoms before the next scheduled dose, the interval was shortened from 7 to 5 days, maintaining therapeutic efficacy. This case highlights the potential of tirzepatide in treating refractory hypoglycemia related to altered incretin signaling, particularly in patients with complex gastrointestinal surgical histories and parenteral nutrition. While currently approved for type 2 diabetes and obesity, tirzepatide may offer a novel off-label approach in select hypoglycemic syndromes in which GIP deficiency plays a central role.Keywords: hypoglycemia, tirzepatide, GIP/GLP-1 dual agonist, parenteral nutrition

Introduction

Hypoglycemia in people without diabetes poses a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge [1]. In particular, altered nutrient transit, extensive gastrointestinal surgeries, and parenteral nutrition can impair glucose metabolism through various mechanisms [2]. Incretins, primarily glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), play a pivotal role in postprandial glucose homeostasis by regulating insulin and glucagon secretion in a glucose-dependent manner [3, 4]. We report a unique case of recurrent severe hypoglycemia in a patient with chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction who had undergone esophagojejunostomy, successfully managed with tirzepatide.

Case Presentation

A 40-year-old woman was referred to our clinic for frequent, severe symptomatic hypoglycemic episodes, unresponsive to food intake and requiring intravenous glucose administration.

Her medical history began at age 3 years, when she underwent atrioventricular canal defect repair. In 2010, she underwent Nissen fundoplication for severe gastroesophageal reflux disease. Following this procedure, she was unable to maintain adequate oral intake and required daily parenteral nutrition in addition to limited liquid or semisolid foods.

Since 2013, she had recurrent episodes of diarrhea (∼20-30 bowel movements per day) associated with fecal incontinence. Colonoscopy ruled out inflammatory bowel disease but showed ileitis with an eosinophilic inflammatory infiltrate. In 2014, she underwent appendectomy followed by duodenogastrectomy and transmesocolic esophagojejunal anastomosis due to gastroparesis, which in retrospect was likely an early manifestation of chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction (CIPO) [5]. In the years that followed, she experienced multiple episodes of intestinal obstruction requiring endoscopic dilation, and in 2019 a formal diagnosis of CIPO, a rare gastrointestinal motility disorder, was established. Due to persistent diarrhea unresponsive to corticosteroids, treatment with long-acting octreotide (octreotide LAR) was initiated at a dose of 20 mg intramuscularly every 6 weeks, with mild symptom improvement. Following her initial surgeries, she developed malabsorption syndrome, resulting in severe recurrent micronutrient deficiency anemia requiring blood transfusions, hypocalcemia leading to osteoporosis and pathological fractures, and functional amenorrhea. In 2024, thrombosis of the central venous catheter occurred, necessitating anticoagulant therapy with apixaban (2.5 mg twice daily).

Since 2020, the patient began experiencing frequent and clinically significant neuroglycopenic and adrenergic symptoms, including weakness, fatigue, confusion, tremors, and palpitations. These episodes were associated with markedly low blood glucose levels (often <55 mg/dL [SI: < 3.06 mmol/L], occasionally <30 mg/dL [SI: < 1.67 mmol/L]) (reference range, 70-99 mg/dL [SI: 3.9-5.5 mmol/L]), unresponsive to oral intake of simple carbohydrates, and requiring intravenous boluses of 33% glucose solution. Given that she was chronically treated with bisoprolol, opioids, and cannabinoids, agents that may blunt the perception of hypoglycemia, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) was deemed essential for timely detection and management of hypoglycemic episodes (Fig. 1A).

Diagnostic Assessment

At biochemical evaluation, fasting blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels were within the normal range, while liver enzymes, including aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), and γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), were mildly elevated.

During hospitalization for diagnostic work-up, the patient developed a spontaneous symptomatic hypoglycemic episode approximately 4–5 hours after a dinner consisting of small semiliquid portions. Blood tests confirmed hypoglycemia with a plasma glucose level of 37 mg/dL (SI: 2.06 mmol/L), accompanied by insulin at 2.8 µU/mL (SI: 19.3 pmol/L) (diagnostic threshold for endogenous hyperinsulinism, ≥ 3 µU/mL [SI: ≥ 18 pmol/L]) and C-peptide at 2.18 ng/mL (SI: 0.72 nmol/L) (diagnostic threshold, ≥ 0.6 ng/mL [SI: ≥ 0.2 nmol/L]) [1]. Anti-insulin antibodies were negative, effectively ruling out insulin autoimmune syndrome (Hirata disease). Basal growth hormone (GH) and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) concentrations were within the normal range. Morning serum cortisol levels were below the reference range, likely due to recent prednisone treatment that had been discontinued a few days before hospital admission. However, the 250-mcg Synacthen (cosyntropin) stimulation test demonstrated an adequate cortisol response (>18 µg/dL [SI: > 500 nmol/L]), ruling out adrenal insufficiency and other counterregulatory hormone deficiencies (Table 1).

Advanced imaging, including total-body computed tomography (CT) and 68Ga-edotreotide positron emission tomography/CT, revealed no lesions suggestive of insulinoma or other neuroendocrine tumors. In the absence of identifiable organic causes of hypoglycemia, and in light of the patient's complex gastrointestinal history and dependence on parenteral nutrition with limited oral intake, a defect in incretin secretion or action was hypothesized as a potential contributor to her glycemic instability.

Treatment

Since the patient was not consuming regular meals but instead receiving long-term parenteral nutrition combined with small liquid or semiliquid meals, dietary modifications could not be recommended as a first-line therapeutic approach. Parenteral nutrition was administered through an admixture tailored to individual requirements and periodically adapted by a specialized dietitian. The formulation included glucose, amino acids, electrolytes, calcium gluconate, magnesium chloride, and a 20% lipid emulsion, providing approximately 850 kcal/day (500 kcal from carbohydrates, 180 kcal from lipids, and 170 kcal from amino acids; total volume 1453 mL, osmolarity 981 mOsm/L). Although this relatively high carbohydrate content may have contributed to glycemic variability, hypoglycemic episodes occurred irrespective of infusion timing or composition changes. Similarly, acarbose, an α-glucosidase inhibitor used to attenuate postprandial glucose excursions, was deemed inappropriate in this context. The patient was also being treated with octreotide LAR for diarrhea (20 mg intramuscularly every 6 weeks in the years preceding our observation), without experiencing any notable improvement in glycemic control. Therefore, modified-release prednisone was initiated at gradually increasing doses (up to 7 mg/day), aiming to reduce hypoglycemic episodes by promoting hepatic gluconeogenesis and decreasing peripheral insulin sensitivity. This intervention yielded only mild benefit; although there was some attenuation in hypoglycemia, the patient continued to experience episodes of both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, with glucose peaks exceeding 200 mg/dL (SI: 11.1 mmol/L). Given the suspected impairment in incretin secretion or signaling, prednisone was discontinued, while octreotide LAR was maintained (as previous withdrawal had led to worsening diarrhea), and the introduction of a long-acting GLP-1 RA was considered. On April 29, 2025, the patient received dulaglutide 1.5 mg. In the days following administration, no significant reduction in hypoglycemic episode frequency was observed. However, spontaneous recovery of blood glucose levels occurred during episodes, eliminating the need for intravenous glucose administration (Fig. 1B). Based on this partial response, a second dose of dulaglutide was administered earlier than scheduled, on day 5. Despite this, the patient experienced a relapse of symptomatic hypoglycemia after hospital discharge, which significantly affected her quality of life. Considering the limited efficacy of GLP-1 RA monotherapy and the potential advantage of GIP in stimulating glucagon secretion during hypoglycemia, treatment with tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist, was initiated June 2, 2025, at a dose of 2.5 mg once weekly.

Outcome and Follow-up

The patient experienced significant glycemic improvement following tirzepatide administration, with complete resolution of symptomatic hypoglycemic events and no episodes of hyperglycemia during the initial days post injection (Fig. 2A). However, mild hypoglycemic episodes reappeared in the days preceding the next scheduled dose (Figs. 2B and 3A), prompting an early readministration on day 5, starting from June 21, 2025 (Fig. 3B). This therapeutic effect was reinforced with subsequent weekly doses of tirzepatide 2.5 mg [6, 7], resulting in sustained glycemic stability with no further hypoglycemic episodes. Additionally, a moderate reduction in body weight was observed, from 60 kg to 54.5 kg. The weight loss, which had begun during treatment with dulaglutide, continued following the initiation of tirzepatide, with the overall decrease being evenly distributed across both therapies. According to the patient's report and confirmed by retrospective CGM analysis (see Fig. 3), tirzepatide was the only therapy that achieved near-complete remission of hypoglycemic episodes, eliminated glycemic variability, and resulted in a marked improvement in quality of life. At the most recent follow-up, the patient remained free from hypoglycemia with stable glucose profiles and reported sustained clinical benefit.

Discussion

This case describes a rare and challenging presentation of recurrent, severe hypoglycemia in a patient with a history of gastric resection and esophagojejunal anastomosis, who was dependent on parenteral nutrition and unable to consume regular meals. The late onset of hypoglycemic episodes, occurring approximately 6 years after esophagojejunostomy, may be related to progressive adaptive changes in the gastrointestinal and endocrine systems over time. Following surgery, alterations in nutrient transit, absorption, and hormonal regulation may develop gradually, with hypoglycemic events potentially appearing well beyond the immediate postoperative period [8, 9].

The complexity of this case lies in the multifactorial nature of the hypoglycemia and the partial or absent response to conventional pharmacological and dietary interventions. A possible contributor to this presentation is impaired glucagon secretion in response to hypoglycemia, potentially due to disrupted incretin signaling following gastrectomy [10].

In addition, altered nutrient transit and reduced exposure of enteroendocrine cells to nutrients, related both to parenteral nutrition and limited oral intake, may have further impaired incretin secretion. Physiologically, GLP-1 and GIP are secreted in response to enteral nutrient intake, by L cells in the distal ileum and colon, and by K cells in the duodenum, respectively, and play a central role in postprandial glucose regulation [3]. While GLP-1 RAs have been proposed in the postbariatric setting [11], dulaglutide, a long-acting GLP-1 RA, yielded insufficient clinical benefit in this patient. Importantly, unlike most postbariatric cases, the primary challenge here was parenteral nutrition and the exclusion of the duodenum from nutrient transit due to esophagojejunostomy. This anatomical alteration likely resulted in reduced GIP secretion, while GLP-1 secretion may have remained intact, given that hypoglycemic episodes occurred several hours after ingestion of small liquid meals, suggesting an exaggerated insulin response possibly driven by GLP-1, unopposed by glucagon due to insufficient GIP activity. A pivotal therapeutic shift occurred with the introduction of tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist recently approved for the treatment of obesity and type 2 diabetes [12-15]. GLP-1 stimulates insulin secretion and suppresses glucagon in hyperglycemic states [4, 16], whereas GIP enhances insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner and stimulates glucagon secretion, especially under hypoglycemic conditions [4, 16] (Fig. 4). Interestingly, tirzepatide has been shown to stimulate glucagon secretion similarly to native GIP in isolated human islets, indicating that its GIP component remains functionally active [17]. Moreover, repeated exposure to tirzepatide and GIP may attenuate glucagon release through receptor desensitization, though tirzepatide alone continues to exert significant glucagonotropic effects [17].

Effect of altered intestinal transit on incretin hormone secretion and glycemic regulation. In healthy individuals (left panel), nutrient intake stimulates the release of glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) from duodenal K cells and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) from jejunal L cells. Both incretins enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, and GIP also promotes glucagon release during hypoglycemia, thereby contributing to glycemic stability. In patients with altered intestinal transit following upper gastrointestinal surgery (eg, esophagojejunal anastomosis) and receiving parenteral nutrition (right panel), exposure of the proximal gut to nutrients is markedly diminished or absent (dashed arrows). This results in impaired GIP secretion with preserved GLP-1 release, disrupting the insulin-glucagon regulatory balance and increasing the risk of hypoglycemia due to an insufficient counter-regulatory glucagon response. GI, gastrointestinal; GIP, glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide; GLP-1, glucagon-like peptide-1. Pointed arrows indicate a stimulatory effect; blunt-ended arrows indicate an inhibitory effect; dashed arrows indicate reduced gut nutrient exposure.

These findings support the hypothesis that tirzepatide may enhance endogenous glucagon responses during hypoglycemia, contributing to improved counterregulation in this patient. In the clinical context of our patient, this mechanism may have been crucial in restoring glycemic stability. Notably, in our case, hypoglycemic episodes reappeared toward the end of the weekly dosing interval (days 5 and 6), prompting a reduction in the dosing interval to every 5 days, starting from June 21, 2025. This adjustment was likely necessary due to tirzepatide’s approximate half-life of 5 days [3, 6], which may result in waning therapeutic efficacy before the next scheduled dose in certain clinical contexts. Although tirzepatide is currently approved only for type 2 diabetes and obesity, its dual incretin mechanism may also offer therapeutic benefits in select hypoglycemic disorders. In fact, a previous case report has described successful tirzepatide use in a patient with postbariatric dumping syndrome and severe hypoglycemia, suggesting potential off-label utility in similar settings [18].

In summary, this case highlights a rare clinical scenario of severe, recurrent hypoglycemia likely related to incretin deficiency secondary to anatomical and nutritional alterations. Standard therapies, including GLP-1 RA monotherapy and corticosteroids, were only partially effective. In contrast, tirzepatide significantly improved glycemic stability, likely via its ability to activate both incretin pathways and support glucagon counterregulation. This experience underscores the need for further research into the potential role of GIP/GLP-1 RAs in the management of complex hypoglycemic syndromes, particularly in patients with altered gastrointestinal anatomy or impaired nutrient absorption, extending beyond their established indications in diabetes and obesity.

Learning Points

Altered nutrient transit and limited exposure of enteroendocrine cells to nutrients, as seen in patients receiving parenteral nutrition, may impair incretin release, leading to disrupted glucose homeostasis and severe hypoglycemic episodes.
