Furosemide plus oral sodium chloride for syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis after fluid restriction failure
Authors: Kittiphan Chienwichai, Pitsinee Laipanngam, Sirin Jiwakanon, Kamonrat Chaiviriyawong, Jananya Wattanakul, Arunchai Chang, Pannawat Mongkolrattanakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSyndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis (SIAD) is a common cause of hyponatraemia, with fluid restriction (FR) failing in nearly half of patients. Although furosemide combined with oral sodium chloride (NaCl) tablets is widely used as second-line therapy, evidence supporting its effectiveness after FR failure remains limited, and predictors of response have not been established.MethodsThis prospective multicentre cohort study enrolled hospitalized adults with SIAD who failed FR at two tertiary centres in Thailand between October 2022 and September 2025. Patients received oral NaCl tablets (3.6 g daily) and furosemide (40 mg daily) while continuing FR. The primary outcome was the daily rate of serum sodium correction. Predictors of response were identified using multivariable linear mixed-effects models.ResultsAmong 88 patients (68% male; median age 65 years), the median baseline serum sodium was 124 mmol/l. Serum sodium increased by 1.51 mmol/l per day [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.37–1.66], reaching a median of 130 mmol/l by day 4 and 134 mmol/l by day 7. Three factors independently predicted faster correction: higher body weight (β 0.85 mmol/l per 10 kg; 95% CI 0.28–1.42), lower baseline sodium (β −0.58 per 1 mmol/l lower; 95% CI −0.77 to −0.39), and drug-induced aetiology (β 7.17 mmol/l; 95% CI 3.48–10.85). Adverse events included hypokalaemia (51%), hypomagnesaemia (29.5%), and acute kidney injury (18.2%). Overcorrection occurred in one (1.1%) patient.ConclusionsFurosemide combined with oral NaCl tablets is a practical second-line option for SIAD after FR failure, achieving sodium correction of 1.5 mmol/l per day. Higher body weight, lower baseline sodium, and drug-induced aetiology predict faster correction. Randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm efficacy.

INTRODUCTION

Hyponatraemia, defined as a serum sodium concentration below 135 mmol/l, is the most frequently encountered electrolyte disturbance, with a prevalence of up to 30%–40% among hospitalized patients [1–4]. In the hospital setting, hyponatraemia has been linked to worse clinical outcomes, including prolonged length of stay [3–7], increased healthcare costs [3, 7], higher readmission rate [3, 6], and elevated mortality [3–5, 7, 8].

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis (SIAD) is a common cause of hyponatraemia, accounting for approximately 40% of cases among hospitalized patients with hyponatraemia [9, 10]. SIAD occurs when arginine vasopressin is inappropriately secreted in response to nonosmotic stimuli such as malignancy, medications, and disorders of the central nervous system or lungs [11].

Although clinical guidelines recommend fluid restriction (FR) as first-line therapy for SIAD [12, 13], its efficacy is limited, with treatment failure in approximately 50% of patients [14]. Consequently, second-line therapeutic interventions are frequently required. Available options include vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans), urea, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), or a combination of oral sodium chloride (NaCl) supplementation and loop diuretics.

Combination therapy with NaCl tablets and furosemide offers a physiologically rational approach: loop diuretics promote excretion of dilute urine, while oral NaCl tablets replace urinary sodium losses, thereby facilitating net free water clearance and raising serum sodium. However, evidence supporting this combination remains limited [15]. A case series reported successful treatment in seven of nine patients (78%) with chronic SIAD who had failed FR [16]. One randomized controlled trial (RCT) found no significant difference between NaCl tablets plus furosemide and FR alone at day 4, but a significant difference favouring combination therapy was observed by day 7 [17]. Notably, this trial was not designed to evaluate combination therapy specifically as second-line treatment after FR failure. Despite its widespread clinical use, NaCl tablets combined with furosemide have not been rigorously evaluated in patients with documented FR failure.

Personalizing hyponatraemia treatment requires identifying which patients are likely to respond to specific interventions. For FR, the Furst equation can predict treatment failure [18]. For urea therapy, age, baseline serum sodium, urea dose, and concurrent FR are associated with the magnitude of sodium correction [19]. In contrast, no study has evaluated predictors of response to NaCl tablets combined with furosemide. This study aimed to evaluate NaCl tablets combined with furosemide as second-line therapy for SIAD after FR failure, quantify the rate of serum sodium correction, and identify predictors of treatment response to guide patient selection.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted this prospective multicentre cohort study at two tertiary referral centres in Thailand: Hatyai Hospital and Phanatnikhom Hospital. Between October 2022 and September 2025, patients were consecutively recruited from general internal medicine and subspecialty wards. The study population comprised hospitalized adults admitted primarily for medical conditions, including pulmonary disease, malignancy, and neuropsychiatric disorders.

This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cohort studies [20].

Study population

Eligible patients were adults aged ≥18 years with hypotonic hyponatraemia (serum sodium <130 mmol/l and serum osmolality <275 mOsm/kg) attributed to SIAD who demonstrated an inadequate response to initial FR therapy.

SIAD was diagnosed according to the Bartter and Schwartz criteria [21], requiring (i) serum osmolality <275 mOsm/kg; (ii) urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg; (iii) clinical euvolaemia independently confirmed by two physicians through history and physical examination; (iv) urine sodium concentration >30 mmol/l; and (v) eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) ≥45 ml/min per 1.73 m2. Patients with acute kidney injury (AKI), hypothyroidism, glucocorticoid deficiency, or current diuretic use were excluded.

FR failure at our institution was defined by any of the following criteria: (i) a serum sodium increase of ≤3 mmol/l on day 4; (ii) a serum sodium increase of ≤5 mmol/l on day 7 or failure to achieve a serum sodium >130 mmol/l by day 7; or (iii) a serum sodium decrease of >2 mmol/l during the initial FR treatment period. The day 4 threshold was selected to facilitate comparison with a previous study [14], whereas day 7 was considered a reasonable timeframe for achieving near-normal sodium levels. Patients were eligible for enrolment at any point after meeting the FR failure criteria, provided they satisfied all other inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients who had received SIAD-specific treatments before enrolment—including NaCl tablets, diuretics, SGLT2i, urea, or vaptans—were excluded.

Treatment protocol

The institutional protocol for SIAD management began with FR (<1000 ml/day). For patients who met FR failure criteria, therapy was escalated to a fixed-dose regimen of oral NaCl tablets (3.6 g daily, administered as 1.2 g three times daily) and furosemide (40 mg once daily), while continuing FR. Third-line therapies—including tolvaptan, urea, SGLT2i, or hypertonic saline—were permitted at the treating physician’s discretion for patients who failed second-line therapy.

Data collection

Data collection included patient demographics, vital signs, and the aetiology of hyponatraemia. Following a comprehensive review of the medical history, standardized clinical and biochemical assessments were performed before initiating second-line therapy. Venous blood samples were collected to measure glucose, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, uric acid, electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate), and osmolality. Concurrent spot urine specimens were obtained to measure urinary sodium, potassium, osmolality, uric acid, and creatinine. During second-line therapy, serum sodium concentrations were measured at least every other day, with additional measurements at the treating physician’s discretion. Monitoring continued until day 7 or discharge, whichever occurred first. Third-line therapies were permitted at the treating physician’s discretion; no predefined failure criteria were specified. Serum sodium measurements obtained after initiation of third-line therapy were excluded from analysis. Fluid intake and output were recorded for 4 days after initiating FR and for 4 days after initiating second-line treatment. Adverse events were prospectively recorded and included electrolyte disturbances (hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia), overcorrection (defined as an increase in serum sodium >10 mmol/l in 24 h or >18 mmol/l in 48 h) [13], osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS), and AKI. AKI was defined according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes guidelines [22].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the rate of increase in serum sodium during second-line therapy, expressed as mmol/l per day from the initiation of combination therapy. The secondary outcome was to identify clinical and biochemical factors associated with the magnitude of change in serum sodium from baseline (ΔNa) during follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables are reported as medians with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms.

To evaluate the rate of increase in serum sodium during second-line combination therapy, we analyzed repeated serum sodium measurements using a linear mixed-effects model with patient as a random effect and treatment day as a fixed effect. To visualize serum sodium trajectories before and after treatment escalation and to assess potential nonlinearity over time, we fitted a separate linear mixed-effects model incorporating a natural cubic spline for treatment day, with patient included as a random intercept.

To identify factors independently associated with the magnitude of ΔNa during follow-up, we fitted a multivariable linear mixed-effects model with ΔNa as the outcome and a random intercept for the patients to account for within-patient correlation. Fixed effects included age, sex, body weight, baseline serum sodium, eGFR, urine osmolality, mean urine output before initiation of combination therapy, and aetiology of SIAD. These variables, measured before initiation of combination therapy, were selected a priori based on their reported associations with hyponatraemia correction rates in the literature [13]. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors; all values were below 5, indicating no significant collinearity among predictors (Supplementary Table 1).

To explore potential risk factors for electrolyte-related adverse events, we performed multivariable logistic regression analyses with hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia as the dependent variables. Body weight (per 10 kg) was the primary predictor, with adjustment for age, sex, baseline serum sodium, and eGFR. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding patients with drug-induced SIAD to assess the robustness of the findings. Missing values were not imputed. Linear mixed-effects models were used to analyse all available data, assuming the data were missing at random. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [23]. Linear mixed-effects models were fitted using the lme4 package [24]. All tests were two-tailed, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics and fluid balance

We included 88 hospitalized patients receiving furosemide with NaCl tablets for SIAD (Fig. 1). The cohort was predominantly male (68%), with a median age of 65 years (IQR 58–74) (Table 1). The median baseline serum sodium was 124 mmol/l (IQR 121–126). Laboratory findings consistent with SIAD included concentrated urine (mean osmolality 437 ± 141 mOsm/kg) and elevated urine sodium (91 mmol/l, IQR 61–123). Baseline renal function was preserved (eGFR100 ml/min per 1.73 m2, IQR 83–115). The median ratio of urinary sodium plus potassium to serum sodium was 0.9 (IQR 0.7–1.2), and 35 patients (40%) had a ratio exceeding 1. Using the cut-offs proposed by Winzeler et al. [25], 18 patients (20%) had urine sodium ≥130 mmol/l, 26 patients (30%) had urine osmolality ≥ 500 mOsm/kg, and 33 patients (38%) met either criterion. Patients had a median hospital stay of 6 days and contributed a median of 6 serum sodium measurements (IQR 5–7) to the analysis.

Change in serum sodium after treatment with furosemide and NaCl tablets

After treatment with oral NaCl tablets, furosemide, and FR, the rate of increase in serum sodium was 1.51 mmol/l per day (95% CI 1.37–1.66). Compared with the fluid-restriction-only phase, combination therapy was associated with a 1.21 mmol/l per day faster increase in serum sodium (95% CI 0.77–1.64; P < .001). After 72 h of treatment, serum sodium rose from a baseline of 124 mmol/l (IQR 121–126) to 130 mmol/l (IQR 125–133) (Fig. 3). By day 7, it had reached 134 mmol/l (IQR 130–137). Before discharge, 70 patients (80%) achieved serum sodium >130 mmol/l, and 36 (41%) achieved >135 mmol/l. In a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with drug-induced SIAD, the rate of serum sodium correction remained similar at 1.52 mmol/l per day (95% CI 1.38–1.66).

Predictors of serum sodium response to furosemide and NaCl tablets

Multivariable linear mixed-effects analysis identified several factors independently associated with greater ΔNa during follow-up (Table 2). Each 10-kg increase in body weight was associated with 0.85 mmol/l higher ΔNa (95% CI 0.28–1.42; P = .004). Each 1 mmol/l lower baseline serum sodium was associated with 0.58 mmol/l higher ΔNa (95% CI 0.39–0.77; P < .001). Drug-induced SIAD was associated with 7.17 mmol/l higher ΔNa compared with malignancy (95% CI 3.48–10.85; P < .001), whereas other aetiologies showed no significant differences. Age, sex, baseline eGFR, urine osmolality, and urine output were not significantly associated with mean ΔNa. Sensitivity analysis showed results consistent with the primary findings (Supplementary Table 3).

Adverse events during treatment with furosemide and NaCl tablets in patients with SIAD

Adverse events were common during treatment (Supplementary Table 4). Electrolyte disturbances predominated, with hypokalaemia in 45 patients (51.1%) and hypomagnesaemia in 26 (29.5%). Potassium fell below 3.0 mmol/l in 33 patients (37.5%) and below 2.5 mmol/l in 8 (9.1%). AKI developed in 16 patients (18.2%): stage 1 in 8 (9.1%), stage 2 in 5 (5.7%), and stage 3 in 3 (3.4%). Furosemide was discontinued in all patients with AKI; NaCl tablets were continued in 10 cases.

Overcorrection of sodium occurred in one patient (1.1%). NaCl tablets and furosemide were discontinued, and a hypotonic solution and desmopressin were administered. No cases of suspected ODS were identified.

In exploratory analyses, higher body weight was independently associated with lower risks of both hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia after adjustment for age, sex, and eGFR (Supplementary Table 5).

DISCUSSION

Our study shows that combining NaCl tablets with furosemide—a widely used, low-cost therapy—is associated with an increase in serum sodium among patients with SIAD who do not respond to FR. Factors associated with a higher rate of serum sodium correction included higher body weight, lower baseline serum sodium, and drug-induced aetiology of SIAD. Adverse events were common, with electrolyte disturbances and AKI being the most frequent.

The discrepancy between our study demonstrating benefit and the previous RCT showing no significant effect [17] may be explained by several factors. First, our higher initial dosing regimen may have enhanced treatment response. We initiated furosemide at 40 mg once daily—the dose previously shown to be effective in case series [16]—rather than the 20 mg/day starting dose used in the RCT. This, combined with higher doses of NaCl tablets (3.6 g vs. 3 g daily in the RCT), may explain the more robust early response in our cohort compared with the delayed effect observed in the RCT, where significant serum sodium elevation was not apparent until day 7. Second, the small sample size in the RCT (approximately 30 patients per group) may have been insufficient to detect a treatment effect. Lastly, the RCT evaluated NaCl tablets plus furosemide as initial therapy, whereas our study specifically enrolled patients who had already failed FR—the clinically relevant population for whom second-line therapy is indicated according to current guidelines [12, 13]. By selecting patients who had failed FR, we may have enriched for those with SIAD physiology more amenable to pharmacological intervention with sodium supplementation and diuretics.

Our observation that lower baseline serum sodium was associated with a greater change in serum sodium has also been reported in previous studies in which hyponatraemia was treated with SGLT2i [26], tolvaptan [27], hypertonic saline [28, 29], or urea [19]. This inverse relationship likely reflects both a ceiling effect, in which patients with less severe hyponatraemia have limited potential for absolute change, and regression to the mean, a statistical phenomenon in which extreme values tend to move towards the mean over time, independent of treatment effects. Furthermore, patients with severe hyponatraemia may have multiple mechanisms contributing to SIAD, some of which may be transient and resolve spontaneously during the observation period, thereby contributing to the observed higher rate of correction. Higher body weight independently predicted faster sodium correction in our cohort. This finding contrasts with data from fixed-dose hypertonic saline, in which heavier patients are at risk of relative undertreatment due to larger total body water [30]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in mechanism, as furosemide combined with oral NaCl primarily increases electrolyte-free water excretion rather than directly expanding extracellular sodium. Lower body weight may reflect a greater chronic disease burden and more severe SIAD physiology, both of which could contribute to reduced treatment responsiveness. In contrast, relatively higher body weight within this predominantly normal-to-underweight cohort may serve as a surrogate for better nutritional status and higher solute intake, which can enhance free-water clearance and thereby improve response to oral NaCl tablets plus loop diuretic therapy. This finding should be considered hypothesis-generating, as relevant physiological and nutritional parameters were not directly measured in this study.

Lastly, drug-induced SIAD was also associated with greater sodium correction. In these patients, the offending drug (all were selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) was discontinued before initiation of combination therapy; however, all met predefined criteria for FR failure prior to enrolment. Delayed resolution of SIAD physiology after drug withdrawal, which may take days to weeks [31], may explain why sodium did not normalize during FR alone. Partial resolution combined with combination therapy may explain the particularly effective response in these patients.

Our study found a high rate of adverse events with combination therapy, similar to a previous RCT [17], predominantly related to diuretic effects. The high rates of hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia likely reflect a combination of diuretic-induced renal losses and limited nutritional intake during hospitalization. Exploratory analyses demonstrated that lower body weight—a potential surrogate for poorer nutritional status—was independently associated with higher risks of both hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia. AKI likely resulted from concurrent medical conditions and volume depletion. The sodium overcorrection rate of 1% was lower than that reported with both urea therapy (3%–8%) [19, 32] and tolvaptan (33%) [27]. However, differences in patient populations and baseline sodium levels limit direct comparison. Importantly, no cases of ODS were observed, likely due to the relatively high baseline serum sodium in this cohort [33].

Notably, net fluid balance did not differ significantly between the fluid-restriction-only and combination-therapy phases. The absence of a marked increase in urine output despite furosemide administration may be explained by postdiuretic sodium retention. In our protocol, furosemide was administered once daily, a dosing strategy known to induce compensatory sodium retention during the postdiuretic period [34].

This study has several clinical implications. Combination therapy with furosemide and NaCl tablets offers a practical second-line option for SIAD after FR failure, achieving a sodium correction rate of approximately 1.5 mmol/l per day. This rate is moderate and may be considered suboptimal, given that current guidelines permit correction of up to 8 mmol/l per day [12, 13] and meta-analyses suggest that lower rates of sodium correction are associated with increased mortality [35]. Although comparable to oral urea [19] and some tolvaptan studies [36, 37], this rate is lower than that achieved with tolvaptan in other reports (6–12 mmol/l per day) [27, 38, 39] and SGLT2 inhibitors [26]. However, these therapies differ in their predictors of response, adverse event profiles, and costs. The high rate of electrolyte disturbances and AKI necessitates close laboratory monitoring—a burden not observed with urea or SGLT2i. This combination may be particularly valuable in settings where other second-line therapies are unavailable, poorly tolerated, or cost-prohibitive.

From a resource perspective, this combination is substantially less expensive than vaptans and more accessible than pharmaceutical-grade urea, which is often unavailable or limited by poor palatability [11, 15]. This is particularly relevant in low- and middle-income countries, where vaptans are prohibitively expensive or inaccessible. The low cost and universal availability of furosemide and NaCl tablets make this combination a practical second-line option in resource-limited settings.

This study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to evaluate furosemide and NaCl tablets as second-line therapy following failure of FR, consistent with current guidelines recommending FR as first-line treatment for SIAD [12, 13]. Second, the prospective design with standardized treatment protocols ensures high-quality data collection and minimizes bias. This study also has several limitations. First, the absence of a control group precludes establishing a causal relationship between the intervention and sodium correction. We cannot definitively exclude spontaneous improvement due to the resolution of transient SIAD as the primary mechanism of sodium normalization. Although all patients met predefined criteria for FR failure prior to enrolment, the underlying stimulus for SIAD—such as infection, drug exposure, pain, or acute illness—may have partially resolved during follow-up. Consequently, some degree of sodium normalization may have occurred independently of furosemide and oral NaCl therapy. An RCT comparing furosemide plus NaCl tablets with alternative second-line therapies such as urea or SGLT2 inhibitors would provide stronger evidence; however, resource constraints, combined with the widespread clinical use of furosemide plus NaCl tablets at our institutions, informed our decision to conduct a prospective cohort study. Second, as an observational cohort, the study may be confounded by nutritional or solute intake, or by illness severity, rather than reflecting a direct physiological effect. Future studies should incorporate a standardized dietary regimen to better control for these factors. Third, this study did not include standardized assessments of clinical symptom resolution, precluding evaluation of whether sodium correction paralleled clinical improvement. Fourth, although net fluid balance did not differ significantly between phases, visual inspection suggests modestly lower fluid intake after initiation of combination therapy, which may confound the observed sodium correction. Fifth, the short-term follow-up, limited to hospital discharge, prevents evaluation of long-term outcomes, such as hyponatraemia recurrence and long-term complications associated with therapy. Finally, the relatively small sample size may limit the precision of effect estimates and the ability to detect associations with less common outcomes.

In conclusion, combination therapy with furosemide and NaCl tablets is a potential second-line option for patients with SIAD who have failed FR, with a median sodium correction rate of 1.5 mmol/l per day. Higher body weight, lower baseline sodium, and drug-induced SIAD predict faster correction. However, the high rate of adverse events necessitates close monitoring. RCTs are needed to confirm efficacy, compare with alternative second-line therapies, and optimize the benefit–risk balance.
