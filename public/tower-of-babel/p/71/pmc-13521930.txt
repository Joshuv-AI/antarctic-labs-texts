Comparative performance of the ahmed and vertucci systems in classifying mandibular premolar canal morphology: a Bayesian and information-theoretic analysis

ABSTRACT
This multinational cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) study evaluated the root canal morphology of mandibular first premolars (M1Ps) from 21 countries and compared how well the Ahmed and Vertucci classification systems represented these patterns. A total of bilateral mandibular first premolars from eligible CBCT scans were assessed using both systems, and the data were analyzed with a hierarchical probabilistic approach to account for population and imaging-related variation. Across the overall dataset, a simple single-canal configuration was the dominant pattern, and this remained the most common finding in all populations despite limited but structured variation in less frequent subclasses. The Ahmed system classified all observed configurations, whereas the Vertucci system left approximately 10.2% of cases without an equivalent category. In addition, Ahmed preserved more anatomical detail, while Vertucci compressed several distinct configurations into broader groups. Demographic and imaging-related factors had modest effects, with voxel size showing the clearest technical influence on the detection of more complex patterns. Overall, M1P morphology showed a stable global pattern with secondary heterogeneity, and the Ahmed system provided a more complete and informative representation for CBCT-based morphology assessment across diverse populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-52409-7.

Introduction

A thorough understanding of root canal morphology is essential for successful endodontic treatment, as accurate identification of the canal system directly influences disinfection, canal shaping, and obturation. Over the years, various methods have been used to investigate root canal anatomy, including clearing and staining, diaphanization, conventional radiography, micro-computed tomography, and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)1. Conventional radiographic methods are limited by their two-dimensional projection of complex anatomical structures, whereas laboratory-based techniques such as clearing and staining and diaphanization require specimen preparation and are mainly applicable to ex vivo research. Importantly, although the clearing technique does not generate volumetric datasets comparable to micro-computed tomography or CBCT, it still allows three-dimensional visualization of the internal canal system. In contrast, both micro-computed tomography and CBCT enable true three-dimensional evaluation of root canal morphology. Micro-computed tomography provides high-resolution imaging and is particularly valuable for ex vivo anatomical research, whereas CBCT has become the principal three-dimensional imaging modality in clinical endodontics and large-scale morphological studies because it provides multiplanar, distortion-free, and clinically applicable imaging2–4. This makes CBCT especially useful for identifying complex anatomical features such as bifurcations, merging and splitting patterns, accessory canals, and C-shaped configurations.

As imaging methods have advanced, root canal classification systems have also needed to evolve. The Vertucci classification, originally developed from observations obtained through clearing techniques, has long served as a standard reference in endodontics5. However, this system has limited ability to represent the full range of three-dimensional configurations revealed by contemporary imaging, particularly in teeth with complex canal pathways, accessory canals, or continuous anatomical variations6,7. To address these limitations, the Ahmed classification was introduced as a hierarchical system that assigns a unique code to each root and canal pathway, thereby enabling a more detailed and structured representation of root canal anatomy8. Although the Vertucci system remains widely used, the Ahmed classification is still relatively underrepresented in the literature, particularly in clinical and CBCT-based applications. This limited representation itself provides a clear rationale for further comparative investigation, especially in mandibular first premolars (M1Ps), where anatomical variation is common and may not be fully captured by conventional classification systems6,8,9.

Previous studies comparing these classification systems have largely relied on descriptive analyses and have mainly reported frequencies and proportions of canal configurations7,8. Although such data are informative, they provide limited insight into how the relationship between classification systems may vary according to population differences, imaging-related factors, and study setting. This limitation becomes more important in multinational CBCT datasets, where observations are inherently hierarchical and may be influenced by country-level heterogeneity, technical variability, and the low prevalence of certain morphological patterns10. Under these conditions, descriptive comparisons alone may be insufficient to quantify uncertainty or distinguish true structural differences from sampling fluctuation.

For this reason, a more flexible analytical framework is required not only to compare classification systems, but also to model root canal morphology in a way that reflects the complexity of the data. Bayesian mixed-effects modeling is particularly suitable in this context because it allows simultaneous evaluation of individual-level observations and higher-level sources of variation within a unified probabilistic framework11,12. In addition, it provides a robust approach for estimating rare outcomes, accounting for between-group heterogeneity, and generating probability-based inferences with explicit quantification of uncertainty. Therefore, the use of Bayesian modeling in the present study was directly motivated by both the limitations of previous descriptive studies and the hierarchical structure of the multinational CBCT dataset10,13.

Accordingly, the aim of this study was to model the root canal morphology of M1Ps in a multinational CBCT dataset and to evaluate how this anatomical structure is represented by the Ahmed and Vertucci classification systems. Using Bayesian hierarchical modeling, we assessed classifiability, posterior morphology distributions, and the effects of demographic and imaging-related covariates across countries. In addition, the informational correspondence between the two systems was examined using entropy measures, mutual information, and mapping-based analyses. The null hypothesis was that there would be no credible difference between the Ahmed and Vertucci systems in terms of classifiability, morphological information content, or structural overlap in the distribution of commonly observed canal configurations.

Methodology

This large-scale multinational cross-sectional study, carried out between June 2023 and April 2024, received ethical approval from Necmettin Erbakan University’s ethics committee (protocol number: 2023/290). Each researcher followed their country’s specific ethical regulations and obtained necessary approvals. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology’ (STROBE) guidelines were followed. The study conducted a thorough examination of the root and canal morphology of permanent Mn1Ps using the classification systems developed by Vertucci and Ahmed et al.14. Led by a multidisciplinary team of Endodontics and Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology specialists from various regions, the study adhered to guidelines set by the American Association of Endodontists and the American Academy of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology15.

Based on a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), 5% precision, and 50% expected prevalence (which was adjusted to maximum due to its unpredictability), a minimum inclusion target of 384 teeth (192 CBCT scans) per country were established. In the final dataset, this threshold was exceeded, and 600 teeth (300 CBCT scans) were included from each participating country.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study focused on CBCT images obtained for various reasons, including orthodontic treatment planning, tooth impaction, implant surgery, and trauma cases. These images were accompanied by demographic details such as the individual’s gender and age. To ensure the quality of the analysis, only high-resolution CBCT scans with fully-formed teeth that provided clear visualization of the bilateral Mn1Ps were selected. This included the ability to observe individual roots, canals, the entire pulp chamber, and the apex.

Exclusion criteria were rigorously applied, omitting cases where visualization was impaired or altered. Excluded were teeth with previous endodontic treatments, immature teeth with open apices, teeth where the full pulp chamber to apex could not be visualized, and those with extensive metal restorations, crowns, bridges, or implants that obscured the anatomy. Additional exclusions included teeth with metal or fiber posts, those showing evidence of prior apicectomy or periodontal surgery that changed the original anatomy, teeth with significant resorption affecting root number or canal configuration, as well as teeth with calcifications or image scatter that hindered proper visualization. Finally, individuals missing one or both Mn1Ps were also excluded.

Study population and sampling frame

This multinational cross-sectional study was based on retrospective convenience samples of archived CBCT scans collected from the participating centers between 2018 and 2024. Only scans meeting the predefined eligibility criteria and showing bilateral M1Ps were included. Because bilateral M1Ps were required, each included CBCT scan corresponded to one patient and contributed two teeth to the final analytic dataset.

The power analysis established a minimum inclusion target of 384 teeth (192 CBCT scans) per country. However, the final dataset was standardized across countries and consisted of 300 CBCT scans (300 patients; 600 teeth) from each participating country. Therefore, the final analytic sample comprised 6300 CBCT scans/patients and 12,600 teeth across 21 countries.

Because the study was retrospective and complete screening logs were not retained in a standardized format across all participating centers, the exact numbers of screened and excluded CBCT scans and the detailed exclusion flow could not be reconstructed uniformly across countries.

Calibration, intra- and inter- reliability

To ensure consistency before study initiation, two researchers (G.D.B. and G.M.) prepared a training video for standardized analysis. All participating examiners were calibrated simultaneously using CBCT scanning methodology, anatomical landmark definitions, bibliographic references, and written step-by-step instructions. The CBCT imaging approach followed Fernandes et al.16, whereas canal configuration classification followed Ahmed et al.14. One examiner from each participating country was responsible for the study evaluations. For calibration, all examiners assessed the same set of 20 identical CBCT scans (40 teeth) using both the Vertucci and Ahmed classification systems. The same dataset was re-evaluated after four weeks to assess intraobserver agreement. The purpose of this procedure was to evaluate reliability within each classification system separately rather than agreement between the two systems. Accordingly, intraobserver agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa, and interobserver agreement across examiners was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa for each classification system independently. The corresponding agreement values are presented in the Supplemental Table.

Assessing the root canal configurations

Each CBCT scan of the M1Ps was evaluated in the coronal, sagittal, and axial planes to determine root and canal morphology, and a single classification score was assigned to each tooth for each system. In each participating country, one calibrated examiner was responsible for the study evaluations. To improve inter-site consistency, all examiners followed a common written assessment protocol based on predefined anatomical landmark definitions, step-by-step classification instructions, and the reference frameworks of Vertucci and Ahmed. Before formal data collection, all examiners completed a shared calibration process using the same reference training materials and calibration cases. During image assessment, observers were allowed to adjust visualization settings, including noise reduction and image filters, to optimize anatomical interpretation according to local viewing conditions. Root canal morphology was classified using both the Vertucci and Ahmed systems, and demographic and technical variables, including age, sex, ethnicity, field of view, and voxel size, were recorded for subsequent analyses. Statistical analysis

The classifiability performance of the Ahmed and Vertucci systems was compared using a Bayesian logistic mixed-effects model based on tooth-level observations from the same CBCT dataset. The classification system (Ahmed vs. Vertucci) was included as the main predictor, with country modeled as a random intercept and sex, side, field of view (ordered), and voxel size (z-score) included as covariates. Weakly informative priors were used [Intercept student-t (3, 0, 2.5); slopes normal (0, 1.25); random-effect SDs student-t (3, 0, 2.5)], and models were estimated in brms/rstan with four chains (2000 iterations; 1000 warm-up; adapt_delta ≈ 0.95). Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% credible intervals (CrI). In addition, the probability distributions of individual Ahmed classes were modeled using a Bayesian multinomial mixed-effects framework, with country included as a random intercept, to estimate the posterior probabilities of each class across relevant covariates.

In the secondary analysis, the relationship between both systems was examined using information-theoretic measures (Shannon entropy, mutual information, and normalized indices) and a data-driven Ahmed → Vertucci mapping, which was visualized through alluvial/Sankey diagrams and Top-K cumulative coverage curves. The uncertainty of these metrics was assessed using a cluster bootstrap by country (B = 1000, BCa intervals). All analyses were carried out in R (tidyverse, brms, tidybayes, ggalluvial). Evidence for a directional effect was considered strong when the 95% credible interval excluded the null value (OR = 1 or difference = 0, as appropriate) and the posterior probability for the reported direction exceeded 0.95. When these conditions were not met, effects were described as uncertain, weak, or not credibly different from the null.

Results

Calibration and observer agreement

The calibration analysis showed good to excellent reproducibility for both classification systems. Across countries, intraobserver agreement ranged from 0.76 to 0.93 for the Vertucci system and from 0.63 to 0.79 for the Ahmed system. Pooled interobserver agreement across all participating examiners was Fleiss’ κ = 0.81 (95% CI 0.77–0.85) for Vertucci and Fleiss’ κ = 0.70 (95% CI 0.65–0.75) for Ahmed, indicating substantial overall agreement for both systems, with higher reproducibility for Vertucci than for Ahmed.

Global and country-level distribution of Ahmed root canal configurations

Across the total dataset, the posterior marginal probabilities revealed that the Ahmed configuration 1MP1 was by far the most prevalent pattern, with a global mean probability of 0.75 (95% CrI 0.69–0.79), followed by 1MP1–2−2 (0.04; 0.02–0.08), (CsC) 1 MP1 (0.04; 0.02–0.08), and 2MP B1 L1 (0.03; 0.02–0.06). Collectively, the top six Ahmed classes accounted for more than 90% of all observations (Fig. 1).

At the country level, this distribution remained remarkably stable. Syria (0.94; 0.91–0.95), India (0.90; 0.81–0.95), Spain (0.89; 0.76–0.95), Croatia (0.86; 0.76–0.92), Greece (0.86; 0.70–0.93), and Turkey (0.83; 0.71–0.90) showed slightly higher posterior probabilities for 1MP1, reaching up to 0.18 above the global mean, whereas Uzbekistan (0.52; 0.47–0.57), Kazakhstan (0.55; 0.34–0.71), and Yemen (0.59; 0.38–0.75) showed relatively lower frequencies, reaching up to 0.23 below the global mean. Interestingly, several countries with reduced prevalence of 1MP1, such as South Africa, Poland, and Kazakhstan, displayed higher probabilities for more complex forms like 1MP1–2−1 and 1MP1–2−2 (Fig. 2). Taken together, these posterior country-level estimates suggest that M1P morphology is not strictly invariant across populations, yet neither is it strongly population-specific; rather, it is characterized by a stable global dominant pattern with limited but structured secondary heterogeneity.

Covariate effects on Ahmed morphology distribution

Across all covariate strata, the Ahmed pattern 1MP1 was the dominant configuration, with predicted probabilities ranging from approximately 0.83 to 0.90, whereas all other classes remained individually uncommon and generally did not exceed 4% to 5%. Small but directionally consistent shifts were observed across covariates. Females showed a slightly higher probability of 1MP1 than males, with an absolute difference of approximately 0.03, accompanied by correspondingly lower probabilities for the minor classes. With increasing age, from − 1.0 to + 1.5 standard deviations, the predicted probability of 1MP1 increased modestly from approximately 0.87 to 0.90, whereas the probability of the two-rooted configuration 2MP B1 L1 decreased from approximately 1.9% to 0.9%. FOV had little overall influence on the leading class, although a slight decrease was observed at medium FOV, where the probability of 1MP1–2−1 became comparatively higher at approximately 4.2%. Voxel size demonstrated the clearest technical effect. At higher voxel values, approximately + 1.8 standard deviations, the model predicted a lower probability of 1MP1, around 0.82, together with a reciprocal increase in 2MP B1 L1 to approximately 3.5% and 1MP1-2-1 to approximately 2.1%, whereas low and average voxel values preserved the baseline distribution. Differences according to tooth side were negligible. CrI were narrow for 1MP1−1−1 and wider for the low-prevalence classes (Table 2).

Mapping and coverage comparison between the Ahmed and Vertucci systems

The data-driven mapping between the Ahmed and Vertucci systems demonstrated a strong but asymmetric correspondence. The majority of Ahmed configurations, particularly 1MP1 which alone accounted for 74.8% of cases, were mapped to Vertucci Type I, while additional frequent Ahmed patterns such as 1MP1–2−2 (5.8%, Type V), 1MP1–2−1 (3.5%, Type III), and 2MP B1 L1 (2.4%, Type IV) together covered most remaining observations. However, several Vertucci types were represented by multiple distinct Ahmed subclasses, reflecting the higher descriptive granularity of the Ahmed system and its ability to capture sub-variants within the same Vertucci category. Conversely, certain complex or CsC-modified configurations lacked a Vertucci equivalent and were classified as non-classifiable (Table 3). Consistent with this, coverage analysis showed that while the Ahmed system classified 100% of cases, the Vertucci system left approximately 10.2% unclassifiable (Fig. 3a).

Bayesian classifiability model

The paired mixed-effects logistic regression (Table 4) showed that the Ahmed system was substantially more likely to yield classifiable outcomes than Vertucci (OR 497.7; 95% CrI 195.6–1451.0), confirming a robust global advantage. The very large odds ratio should be interpreted in light of the structural difference between the two systems, because Ahmed classified all observed configurations whereas Vertucci left a subset unclassifiable; accordingly, the effect reflects near-complete separation in classifiability rather than a conventional gradational contrast. The baseline probability of obtaining a classifiable case was high (Intercept OR 24.2; 95% CrI 10.6–66.0). Among covariates, male cases had slightly lower odds of being classifiable compared with females (OR 0.76; 95% CrI 0.66–0.87), while tooth side (44 vs. reference) and voxel size (per 1 SD increase) showed no credible association (ORs ≈ 1.0; CrIs overlapping 1). Country-level posterior estimates (Fig. 3b) consistently favored Ahmed, with differences ranging from modest effects in Spain (+ 0.3 pp) and Greece (+ 0.6 pp) to large improvements in Yemen (+ 37.8 pp), Uzbekistan (+ 36.3 pp), and Kazakhstan (+ 33.8 pp). Overall, Ahmed demonstrated superior and more stable classifiability across all populations, with only minor demographic or technical influences and universally positive country-specific posterior differences.

Information-theoretic comparison of the two classification systems

Information-theoretic analysis quantified how much structural information the two classification systems share (Table 5). The entropy of the Ahmed system was higher (H = 1.73 bits, 95% BCa 1.51–1.98) than that of Vertucci (H = 1.35 bits, 95% BCa 1.19–1.51), confirming that Ahmed captures greater morphological diversity. The negative ΔH (− 0.37 bits) indicates that mapped Vertucci categories contain slightly less information overall. The mutual information between the systems was substantial (I = 1.33 bits, 95% BCa 1.17–1.48), with normalized indices (NMI ≈ 0.87 and 0.77) showing very strong but asymmetric correspondence, most of Vertucci’s variability is explained by Ahmed, yet the reverse is not complete.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first international CBCT-based study that thoroughly and scientifically compares the classification systems of Ahmed and Vertucci based on the evaluation of M1P canal morphology. The combination of Bayesian hierarchical modeling and information-theoretic metrics provides an interpretation that is more refined than in previous studies that were often limited to descriptive proportions or single-population datasets. In summary, the results highlight the predominance of a simple canal configuration throughout the world, the impact of demographic and technical factors on morphology detection, and a substantially higher degree of classifiability and anatomical information content provided by the Ahmed system.

Across the populations examined, M1P morphology was predominantly characterized by a simple root canal configuration, consistent with previous CBCT- and micro-CT-based reports showing a high prevalence of single-canal anatomy in this tooth type. Vertucci5, Cleghorn et al.17, and Sert and Bayirli18 likewise reported that single-canal configurations were the most frequent finding in mandibular premolars, although the reported prevalence varied according to population and evaluation method. In this regard, the present multinational dataset al.lowed a question beyond simple prevalence reporting to be addressed, namely whether M1P morphology behaves as a biologically invariant pattern across populations or as a strongly demographic trait. The posterior results support an intermediate interpretation. A simple single-canal configuration remained dominant across all countries, indicating a reproducible global anatomical baseline; however, less frequent subclasses showed limited but structured variation according to population and imaging context. Thus, M1P morphology appears neither strictly invariant nor fully population-specific, but rather organized around a stable dominant pattern with secondary heterogeneity.

International variations, particularly the relatively lower rates of classic single-canal configurations in Kazakhstan, Yemen, Uzbekistan, are consistent with regional patterns described by Khedmat et al. (Iran)19, Rwenyonyi et al. (Uganda)20, and Sert& Bayirli (Turkey)18 who found greater occurrence of complicated bifurcating or converging designs in Asian, Middle Eastern and some Mediterranean groups. These distinctions have been ascribed by some previous authors to racial, genetic and developmental influences21. Cross-national modeling in the present study supports the idea of stable population differences, but crucially does so by placing the differences in a probabilistic framework, demonstrating that these population differences hold when we control for voxel size, field of view and subject demographics This confirms the assertion of Ahmed et al.14 that canal morphology is a product of both biological variation and the imaging environment through which it is perceived.

The effects of demographics were also shown in a clinically meaningful manner. Female subjects had only a somewhat greater chance of having simpler morphologies, which is in line with a number of previous regional CBCT studies22,23 asserting sex differences in canal anatomy that, however, were not sufficiently powered to allow testing for these associations across populations. Reported explanations in the literature include anthropometric variance in root size and dentin thickness, and females frequently have more convergent and uniform canal morphologies. The age-related prevalence of simpler patterns is also in line with the Martins et al. (Portugal)24 and Ordinola-Zapata et al. (Peru)25 provided evidence that age-related secondary dentin deposition may decrease canal irregularities that can be detected on CBCT. Our findings further support this notion providing the most compelling multinational-based evidence to date that age-related simplification is systematic rather than population-based.

The effects of technical parameters, such as the field of view and voxel size, were small, but not negligible. Prior CBCT methodologies16,26 reported that using smaller voxel size could enhance the identification of minute accessory canals or minor bifurcations while a larger voxel size could hide very fine anatomic pathways. The present study’s Bayesian results confirm these technical findings. Although smaller voxels increased the detection of complex configurations, simpler classifications were preferred with larger voxels. However, the results of the study expand the previous knowledge by evidencing the size of such an effect among 21 countries in different populations and showing a limited influence of voxel size on classification agreement, despite having a non-negligible effect on drifting the worldwide morphological pattern of variation. This result is of particular interest for harmonization of CBCT protocols in large-scale or multicentre endodontic investigations.

The most prominent added value of the study is its comparative assessment of the Ahmed and Vertucci classification systems. Previous studies that compared the two systems14,27–29, usually done in a descriptive manner and on small populations. The present findings concur with previous literature that Vertucci’s system frequently does not classify root canal morphology for complex or transitional morphologies; there are unclassifiable cases, especially in cases where the variation does not fit its eight predefined categories. Previous CBCT and micro-CT investigations have shown that the Vertucci classification frequently fails to categorize a proportion of canal systems, with reports ranging from approximately 2–8% of M1Ps and up to 6–18% in other tooth types being labelled as ‘Vertucci non-classifiable’ when assessed in isolation. This has been documented in studies such as Sierra-Cristancho et al.30 who identified non-classifiable configurations in mandibular premolars, and in the systematic review by Ahmed31, which reported ≈ 8% non-classifiable premolars in a Chilean population, as well as in micro-CT analyses of mandibular incisors demonstrating 6–18% unclassifiable canal systems32. These observations align closely with the ≈ 10% of cases in the present multinational dataset that lacked a Vertucci equivalent.

By contrast, the Ahmed system provided a classifiable category across all evaluated populations, consistent with its hierarchical design and prior applications14. More importantly, the present findings show that the advantage of Ahmed is not limited to a greater number of labels. The mapping analysis demonstrated that several anatomically distinct Ahmed subclasses were compressed into the same Vertucci category, whereas some complex configurations had no Vertucci equivalent. In this context, the higher entropy observed for Ahmed should be interpreted as greater retention of underlying anatomical heterogeneity rather than as a purely mathematical consequence of category count alone. Conversely, the lower entropy of the Vertucci system reflects partial information loss arising from the reduction of distinct canal patterns into broader predefined types. The substantial but asymmetric mutual information between the two systems further indicates that they share a common anatomical core, although part of the detail preserved by Ahmed cannot be recovered once morphology is reduced to Vertucci categories.

From a clinical perspective, the relevance of these findings lies less in the nominal superiority of one classification system over another and more in the reduction of diagnostic ambiguity when complex anatomy is encountered on CBCT. The present results do not demonstrate treatment outcomes directly; however, they do show that the Ahmed system reduces structural uncertainty by assigning interpretable categories to configurations that are partially compressed or left unclassified by the Vertucci system. In practical terms, this means that when preoperative CBCT reveals non-simple mandibular first premolar anatomy, the Ahmed framework may allow clinicians to communicate the canal pattern more precisely, recognize complexity with greater consistency, and anticipate the possibility of bifurcation, convergence, or atypical pathways more explicitly. Thus, the clinical value of the present modeling framework is not that it predicts failure directly, but that it supports more informative CBCT-based anatomical interpretation and may reduce uncertainty in morphology-related diagnostic reasoning.

The Ahmed system has better classifiability, as evident from this study through paired mixed-effects logistic regression with highly increased odds ratios, hence supporting the use of newer classification systems in clinical and research settings. This very large effect size should not be interpreted as a purely incremental performance gain; rather, it primarily reflects a structural property of the classification systems, namely that Ahmed provides a label for all observed configurations whereas Vertucci lacks equivalent categories for a subset of cases. For this reason, the absolute classifiability difference (100% vs. 89.8%) may be more directly interpretable than the odds ratio alone. Previous studies by Ahmed et al.33 and Karobari et al.34 found that clinicians were more accurate using the Ahmed system when classifying multi-rooted posterior teeth. This study expands that evidence base through its application of the system to an international dataset of mandibular premolars and its use of a more robust statistical framework. The implications of such findings for endodontic practice are quite substantial on an international level. Inaccurate canal classification is associated with missed anatomy, inadequate debridement, and a high percentage of failures in treatments, all of which are well documented in the literature35–37. For this reason, the superior classifiability of the Ahmed system may have relevant implications for CBCT-based morphology reporting and anatomical communication in both research and clinical contexts.

An additional point that merits consideration is the difference in observer reproducibility between the two systems. In the present study, interobserver agreement was higher for the Vertucci classification than for the Ahmed classification. This likely reflects the simpler and more condensed structure of the Vertucci system, which may facilitate more consistent assignment across observers. By contrast, the Ahmed system preserves greater anatomical granularity, but this increased descriptive detail may require finer distinctions between morphologic variants and therefore introduce greater observer-dependent variability. Accordingly, the findings suggest a trade-off between reproducibility and anatomical expressiveness. From a practical perspective, this means that the Ahmed system may be more informative when precise morphology description is needed, whereas the Vertucci system may retain some advantage in settings where simplicity and observer consistency are prioritized.

From a translational perspective, the practical adoption of more detailed classification systems such as Ahmed may depend not only on their anatomical completeness, but also on clinician familiarity, training, and usability in routine workflows. Therefore, although the Ahmed framework may provide clear advantages when CBCT-based morphology assessment, complex anatomy reporting, or research standardization is required, simpler systems may still retain some practical value in routine settings where rapid communication and ease of use are prioritized.

The current study highlights the importance of using advanced analytical approaches in anatomical studies. Traditional descriptive statistics have substantial shortcomings in the modeling of hierarchical structure across a multicounty sample. Micro-CT data reveal a level of anatomical detail that complicates traditional notions of classification33,38. Through the use of Bayesian hierarchical models, this study contributes to the methodological trend toward more sophisticated quantitative inference in morphometric classification.

The present study showed that M1P morphology is characterized by a stable dominant pattern with limited but systematic heterogeneity around that baseline. Across countries and covariate strata, the posterior predictive distributions consistently favored simple single-canal anatomy, indicating that the principal morphological signal is reproducible at the population level rather than purely stochastic. At the same time, posterior shifts associated with age and, more notably, voxel size suggest that variation in less frequent subclasses is not random noise but is partly shaped by demographic and technical context. In parallel, the mapping and entropy analyses demonstrated that disagreement between the Ahmed and Vertucci systems is largely structural rather than incidental, because several anatomically distinct Ahmed subclasses were compressed into the same Vertucci category, whereas some complex configurations had no Vertucci equivalent. Accordingly, the main contribution of this study was not merely to show higher classifiability for the Ahmed system, but to quantify how anatomical variability, uncertainty, and information loss are distributed across populations and imaging conditions. These findings have implications not only for morphological research but also for CBCT-based reporting, multicenter data harmonization, and the development of AI-compatible annotation systems, in which preservation of anatomical granularity is essential.

This study has notable strengths, including its unparalleled international scope, a standardized protocol for calibration and analysis, and a coherent analytical framework. However, several key limitations should be acknowledged. CBCT cannot show the finest accessory canals detectable by micro-CT; therefore, even after statistical adjustments for several sources of variation, there may still be residual variability regarding possible differences in CBCT equipment, voxel sizes, and acquisition protocols across 21 participating countries. There is also unequal ethnic distribution across different participating countries, with some regions providing disproportionately large datasets compared to others. Because complete screening and exclusion logs were not retained uniformly across all participating centers, the exact flow of screened and excluded CBCT scans could not be reconstructed retrospectively. Furthermore, bilateral M1Ps from the same individual contributed to the dataset, residual within-subject dependence may have remained. This was not modeled explicitly at the patient level and may have led to some underestimation of uncertainty.

Conclusion

This multinational CBCT study showed that M1Ps predominantly exhibit simple canal morphology across populations. Beyond demonstrating superior classifiability of the Ahmed system, the Bayesian and information-theoretic analyses showed that the additional granularity of Ahmed captures measurable anatomical variation that is partially lost when configurations are reduced to Vertucci categories. These findings support the use of information-rich classification systems not only for more complete morphology description but also for CBCT-based reporting, cross-population comparisons, and future AI-oriented endodontic anatomy research.
