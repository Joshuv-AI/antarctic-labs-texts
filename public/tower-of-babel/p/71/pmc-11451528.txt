Atom-glue stabilized Pt-based intermetallic nanoparticles
Authors: Zhongliang Huang, Yingru Wang, Jing Xia, Shengnan Hu, Nanjun Chen, Tianyi Ding, Changhong Zhan, Chih-Wen Pao, Zhiwei Hu, Wei-Hsiang Huang, Tong Shi, Xiangmin Meng, Yong Xu, Liang Cao, Xiaoqing Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Pt-based nanoparticles (NPs) have been widely used in catalysis. However, this suffers from aggregation and/or sintering at working conditions. We demonstrate a robust strategy for stabilizing PtCo NPs under high temperature with strong interaction between M–N–C and PtCo NPs with Pt–M–N coordination, namely, “atom glue.” Such atom glue for stabilizing Pt-based NPs can be extended to Zn, Mn, Fe, Ni, Co, and Cu, being a versatile strategy for stabilizing PtCo NPs, which substantially promotes the performance toward oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and fuel cell. Impressively, the mass activity (MA) reaches 2.99 A mgPt−1 for ORR over g-Zn–N–C/PtCo, and 79.3% of the initial MA is maintained after 90K cycles in fuel cell. This work provides a versatile strategy for stabilizing Pt-based NPs via atom glue, which is likely to spark widespread interest across various fields.

INTRODUCTION

Noble metals have been extensively used in catalysis because of their appropriate adsorption ability to many molecules and resultant excellent activity (1–3) and have been regarded as the most promising candidates for harvesting energy in a sustainable manner. For example, Pt has been widely used as the catalyst for proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs), a device for using electrochemical reactions to generate electricity (4, 5). Nevertheless, the balance between the activity and stability of noble metals is crucial for the practical application in real devices (6). Over the past decades, substantial efforts have been dedicated to improving the catalytic activity and especially strengthening the stability of noble metals (7–11). Despite the substantial progress on the development of noble metal catalysts, great challenges persist in synergistically balancing the activity and durability of catalysts under severe conditions, such as high temperature, acidic electrolyte, and metal dissolution at high potential. Consequently, it is imperative to develop robust and universal strategies for stabilizing the active noble metal sites.

During the past decades, great attention has been paid to modulating the catalytic activity and stability of Pt-based catalysts via structural engineering and constructing strong metal-support interaction (SMSI) (12–15). Alloying Pt with another metal can construct strong synergy and greatly change the electronic property of Pt, which further contributes to the catalytic performance (12–15). Besides, the construction of SMSI can spatially separate the Pt-based alloy nanoparticles (NPs) and prevent them from aggregation. Moreover, SMSI between Pt and support, such as, TiO2, CeO2, TiN, and Ta3N5, can facilitate the electron transfer from metal to support and enhance the stability of the catalyst under harsh conditions (16–18). Nevertheless, the precise balance of activity and stability requires appropriate binding strength of Pt with support, because weak binding strength is unable to anchor Pt while strong binding strength will deteriorate the activity. Moreover, the current strategy for stabilizing Pt on support is limited to specific chemical modifications (19) and selection of carrier materials (20), lacking a universal strategy for constructing appropriate interaction between Pt and substrate without decay of activity and stability, which is of great importance but formidably challenging yet.

In this work, we propose a robust strategy for the stabilization of Pt-based intermetallics (IMCs) with the assistance of metal-nitrogen-carbon (M–N–C), namely, “atom glue,” where the strong interaction between M–N–C and Pt-based IMC with M–Pt binding can efficiently prevent them from being sintering under harsh conditions. The concept of atom glue in the form of Pt–M–N binding can be extended to various elements including Zn, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu, as a universal strategy for stabilizing Pt-based IMCs. The effect of atom glue on the stabilization of Pt-based IMCs has been successfully demonstrated on oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) over g-Zn–N–C/PtCo with Pt–Zn–N binding. The mass activity (MA) of ORR reaches 2.99 A mgPt−1, which is 11.1 and 4.2 times higher than that of commercial Pt/C and N–C/PtCo without atom glue (i.e., Pt–Zn–N binding). Besides, the promising stability of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo is validated by the slight activity decay of 1.7% for ORR after 10K potential cycles. When g-Zn–N–C/PtCo was integrated into a fuel cell cathode, 79.3% of MA was maintained after 90K potential cycles and without obvious voltage drop at 1.5 A cm−2 in 230 hours. No noticeable voltage drop was observed at 1.5 A cm−2 for over 230 hours, showing fascinating potential in fuel cells. Density function theory (DFT) (21, 22) calculations and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) (23) simulations were further performed to demonstrate the origins of the highly enhanced durability and ORR activity of Zn–N–C/PtCo compared with N-C/PtCo.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

We prepared Pt-based IMC via a classic thermal reduction method, which requires high-temperature annealing to facilitate atomic ordering. However, annealing treatment generally leads to the aggregation of small NPs into large ones, which will severely damage the subsequent application. To avoid the aggregation, we propose a strategy to anchor NPs on carbon support with strong binding, namely, atom glue, to prevent them from being sintered (left of Fig. 1A). With the assistance of atom glue, the aggregation and metal loss of Pt-based NPs can be strongly suppressed, which may endow notable enhancement in fuel cells (Fig. 1A). In this work, we synthesized g-Zn–N–C/PtCo by impregnating Pt(acac)2 and Co(acac)3 on Ketjenblack-300 (KB-300) in the presence of zinc phthalocyanine (ZnPc) at 900°C (left of Fig. 1A). Analysis on high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image indicates that uniform NPs with a mean size of 3.3 ± 1.0 nm are successfully obtained at a Pt loading amount of 19 wt % (Fig. 1B). We attribute the peaks in x-ray powder diffraction (XRD) pattern to PtCo IMC phase (JCPDS No. 43-1358; fig. S1). No significant size increase of the obtained PtCo IMCs was observed when Pt loading amount increased to 39 wt % (fig. S2). After further increasing the loading amount of Pt to 62 wt %, the mean size of PtCo IMCs slightly increased to 5.6 ± 1.5 nm (fig. S3), suggesting that the atom glue can strongly stabilize the Pt-based NPs on support. To demonstrate the significance of atom glue on the stabilization of PtCo IMCs, we synthesized N–C/PtCo (right of Fig. 1A). We observed obvious sintering of PtCo NPs when ZnPc was replaced by Pc (Fig. 1C), suggesting that Zn species play a vital role in the construction of atom glue. In addition, PtCo NPs on C (C/PtCo) experience severe sintering, as revealed by the sharp peaks of Pt in XRD pattern (fig. S4).

To investigate the strength of atom glue, we treated g-Zn–N–C/PtCo with HNO3 (0.5 M) at 70°C for 2 hours (fig. S5). The XRD pattern suggests that the structure of PtCo IMC phase is largely maintained after acid treatment (fig. S6). The atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM image shows that such acid treatment leads to the formation of ordered PtCo core and Pt shell with three atomic layers (Fig. 2A). The fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns show that PtCo is an alternating stacking of Pt and Co columns along the [1¯00] axis of the zone, confirming the L10 ordered structure of PtCo IMC. The atomic ratio of Pt/Co/Zn in g-Zn–N–C/PtCo is determined to be 67.5/31.2/1.3, and the content of Co atoms in g-Zn–N–C/PtCo is higher than that of N–C/PtCo (25.5%), suggesting that more Co atoms were etched by HNO3 in N–C/PtCo (fig. S7). The elemental mapping implies that Co atoms are mainly located in the core area of the etched g-Zn–N–C/PtCo, being consistent with the HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 2B). Note that the presence of isolated atoms in the HAADF-STEM images at support may be ascribed to Zn atoms (fig. S8). Moreover, we further investigated the electronic structure and local environment of Pt, Co, and Zn in g-Zn–N–C/PtCo using x-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended x-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses. Compared to Pt foil, the similar feature in the XANES spectrum of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo at Pt L3-edge suggests that Pt mainly presents as metallic state, while the slight increase of the white line intensity reveals the strong interaction between Pt atom and neighboring atoms (Fig. 2C). The feature in the corresponding EXAFS spectrum and wavelet transform (WT) profile of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo, which exhibits a shorter distance than that of Pt–Pt coordination, can be attributed to Pt–Zn and Pt–Co coordination (Fig. 2D and fig. S9) (24). Compared to Co foil, the reinforcement of peak at 7224 eV (red arrow) and the weakening of peak at 7712 eV (blue arrow) in the Co K-edge XANES spectrum of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo are ascribed to the hybridization of Co 4s and 4p orbitals in PtCo IMCs (Fig. 2E) (24, 25). Given the larger radius of Pt than Co, we attribute the feature at 2.2 Å in the Co K-edge EXAFS spectrum and WT profile of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo to Pt–Co coordination (Fig. 2F and fig. S10) (26). Moreover, analysis on the XANES and EXAFS spectra of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo at Zn K-edge suggests that Zn atoms are coordinated with N atoms in the form of Zn–N (Fig. 2, G and H, and fig. S11). Besides, the features at ~2.18 and ~2.80 Å are ascribed to Pt–Zn and Zn–N–C coordination (Fig. 2, H and I). Given the strong Zn–N coordination in g-Zn–N–C/PtCo, we conclude that partial Zn atoms present as isolated state and that others are coordinated with Pt to form Pt–Zn coordination, being consistent with results from HAADF-STEM images (fig. S8). On the basis of the above results, we conclude that the atom glue for stabilizing PtCo IMCs on support presents as Pt–Zn–N binding. In particular, the deposition of ZnPc on KB-300 under high temperature results in the formation of Zn-modified support (Zn–N–C), while the surface Pt atoms of PtCo IMCs strongly bind with Zn atoms in the form of Pt–Zn–N, namely, atom glue (Fig. 2G). The existence of Pt–Zn and Pt–Co endows the electron transfer from Pt to Zn and Co, being consistent with the increase of the intensity of white line in the XANES spectrum of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo at Pt L3-edge (Fig. 2C).

To demonstrate the versatility of our strategy, we used various metal phthalocyanines to construct the Pt–M–N (M = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu) binding as the atom glue. Notably, we obtained uniform PtCo IMCs with a mean size of ~3 nm, suggesting that the concept of atom glue can serve as a universal strategy for stabilizing Pt-based NPs (Fig. 3A and fig. S12). Besides, we applied the current atom glue of Pt–M–N to stabilize PtxM IMCs (i.e., PtFe, Pt3Fe, Pt3Co, PtCu, and PtZn). Specifically, the atom glue can efficiently stabilize PtFe, Pt3Fe, Pt3Co, PtCu, and PtZn IMCs against high-temperature annealing (figs. S13 to S17), as revealed by the clear increase of mean size without the protection of Pt–Zn–N binding (Fig. 3B). After treating g-Zn–N–C/PtFe, g-Zn–N–C/Pt3Fe, g-Zn–N–C/PtCu, and g-Zn–N–C/PtZn with HNO3 (0.5 M) at 70°C for 2 hours, those IMCs are enclosed by a thin Pt shell consisting of three atomic layers (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, we validated the Pt-rich shell of PtxM IMCs through the elemental mapping (Fig. 3D). The distribution of N on the support suggests that N has been successfully incorporated into carbon support. The high brightness of Zn at the area of PtxM IMCs implies that Zn plays a crucial role in the strong interaction between PtxM and support (Fig. 3D). In addition, the synthesis of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo IMCs with a Pt loading amount of 39 wt % can be scaled up to 10 g with a uniform size distribution of PtCo NPs, showcasing great potential in practical fuel cell applications (fig. S18).

To demonstrate the significance of atom glue in practical catalysis, we evaluated the ORR performance of catalysts using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and linear sweep voltammetry in O2-saturated 0.1 M HClO4 (figs. S19 and S20 and tables S1 and S2), using commercial Pt/C, C/PtCo and N–C/PtCo (without atom glue) as references. We determined the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) and specific activity (SA) using CO tripping (figs. S21 to S23). As shown in Fig. 4A and table S3, both MA and SA of g-M–N–C/PtxM significantly surpass those of commercial Pt/C and N–C/PtxM without atom glue. Compared to other references, g-Zn–N–C/PtCo exhibits the highest MA of 2.99 A mgPt−1 at 0.9 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE), respectively, which 4.2 times higher than those of N–C/PtCo (0.71 A mgPt−1), 8.1 times higher than those of C/PtCo (0.37 A mgPt−1), and 11.1 times higher than those of commercial Pt/C (0.27 A mgPt−1), respectively. We recorded the minimum Tafel slope of 53.7 mV dec−1 for g-Zn–N–C/PtCo, which verifies the improved ORR kinetics (fig. S24). Moreover, we subjected all samples to an accelerated durability test (ADT) at a potential of 0.6 to 1.1 V versus RHE for 10K continuous cycles in 0.1 M HClO4 (figs. S25 to S31). Besides, durability tests show that g-M–N–C/PtxM displays superior stability compared to commercial Pt/C and N–C/PtxM without atom glue after 10K cycles (Fig. 4B and table S3). Furthermore, we used g-Zn–N–C/PtCo as the cathode catalyst for membrane electrode assembly (MEA) of PEMFCs (fig. S32). In H2-air single-cell tests, g-Zn–N–C/PtCo displays a peak power density of 1.45 W cm−2, much higher than that of Pt/C (0.81 W cm−2) and N–C/PtCo (1.06 W cm−2) (Fig. 4C). The initial value of MA of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo reaches up to 0.99 A mgPt−1 at 0.9 ViR-free with a cathode Pt loading of 0.08 mgPt cm−2, already exceeding the 2025 target set by US Department of Energy (DOE; 0.44 A mgPt−1) (Fig. 4, D and E). For ADT, a cycle square-wave protocol was used to keep the cathode under repeated cell voltage sweeps of 0.6 and 0.95 V for 3 s, respectively. Analysis on polarization plots of various catalysts after ADT (fig. S33) suggests that the MA of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo decreases by 7.9% after 30K voltage cycles, which is much lower than that of commercial Pt/C (53.3%) and N–C/PtCo (48.4%) (Fig. 4F). Impressively, 75.9% of the MA of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo is retained even after 90K cycles, far exceeding DOE durability goal (MA < 40% after 30K voltage cycles) (27). Note that the MA of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo is 0.91 A mgPt−1 after 30K cycles, which is much higher than that of reported catalysts (Fig. 4G and table S4). After cycling for 30K cycles, the voltage of g-Zn-N-C/PtCo at 0.8 A cm−2 only drops by 5% (fig. S34), which is much less than that of DOE standard (<30%). Besides, the steady-state cell voltage during chronoamperometric test at 1.5 mA cm−2 for 230 hours further validates the enhanced long-term stability of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo (Fig. 4H). In addition, we also collected in situ electrochemical XRD patterns of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo and N–C/PtCo to evaluate the structural change of PtCo IMCs (Fig. 5, A and B). It is noted that the PtCo IMC in g-Zn–N–C/PtCo was maintained after 5K cycles (Fig. 5A), while PtCo IMC in N–C/PtCo gradually evolved into Pt NPs because of Co dissolution during test, as revealed by the absence of the characteristic peaks PtCo IMC in the in situ XRD patterns (Fig. 5B). Subsequent analysis of in situ electrochemical XRD patterns, using the Scherrer equation, demonstrated that the Pt–Zn–N atom glue exhibited exceptional antiagglomeration properties. Specifically, the analysis revealed that the particle size of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo exhibited a nominal increase of 0.34 nm following 5K potential cycles, significantly less than the increases observed for N–C/PtCo (2.56 nm) and commercial Pt/C (1.92 nm) (Fig. 5C and fig. S35). In addition, corroborative evidence from transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images and energy-dispersive x-ray spectrometry (EDS) spectra substantiated these findings (figs. S36 to S40). The electron transfer number of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo is ~3.99 (fig. S41), which suggests that the stability of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo is unaffected by H2O2, being a highly active and stable electrocatalyst for ORR.

Last, we performed DFT (21, 22) calculations and AIMD simulations to further explore the mechanism for the enhanced durability and activity of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo. First, AIMD simulations were carried out to reveal dynamics of Pt clusters on the Zn–N–C and N–C substrate intuitively (Fig. 5D). With the same initial distance (8.12 Å) between Pt13 and Pt4 clusters, the two Pt nanoclusters on the N–C substrate gradually agglomerate to form a large cluster at 5.6 ps, while the two clusters on the Zn–N–C substrate still remain separately even at 10 ps, indicating that the Zn–N–C has a stronger interaction with Pt clusters than N–C, being consistent with the experimental characterizations. For DFT calculations, a 38-atom Pt34Co4 cluster with a Pt-rich skin on a ZnN4 site was constructed to mimic the structure of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo for understanding the enhanced stability and ORR performance, while Pt34Co4 on an N4 site was constructed as a comparison. As shown in Fig. 5E, the binding energies of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo and N–C/PtCo are −1.99 and −1.02 eV, respectively, indicating a much stronger binding strength of Pt–Co cluster to Zn–N–C than N–C. We utilized X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy to understand the electronic structure of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo (fig. S42). g-Zn–N–C/PtCo exhibits a negative shift in the binding energy of the Pt 4f with respect to N–C/PtCo, indicating that the d band center of Pt in g-Zn–N–C/PtCo shifts away from the Fermi level because of the electron transfer of Co to Pt (fig. S43), which weakens the adsorption of OHad on Pt and improves the ORR performance. Furthermore, we analyzed the activities of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo and N–C/PtCo using the free energy diagram of ORR via the associative mechanism (Fig. 5F, fig. S44, and table S5) (28, 29), in which the rate-determining step (RDS) of g-Zn–N–C/PtCo and N–C/PtCo for ORR is *OH + H+ + e− → * + H2O (6), consistent with previous work (6, 29). The free energy change of RDS for g-Zn–N–C/PtCo is 0.58 eV, which is lower than that of N–C/PtCo (0.67 eV), in line with the experimental observation (Fig. 5F).

In this work, we proposed the concept of atom glue for stabilizing Pt-based NPs on metal/N dual-doped carbon support (M–N–C) under high temperature. Detailed characterizations indicate that M–N–C endows the formation of Pt–M–N coordination (M = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn), resulting in the stabilization of ultrafine Pt-based IMCs. Moreover, such atom glue can stabilize PtCo IMCs with a Pt loading amount as high as 62 wt % and can be scaled up to 10 g. To demonstrate the potential application of stabilized Pt-based IMCs, the optimal g-Zn–N–C/PtCo with Pt–Zn–N coordination displays a MA of 2.99 A mgPt−1 at 0.9 V for ORR, largely surpassing that of commercial Pt/C (0.27 A mgPt−1) and N–C/PtCo (0.71 A mgPt−1). Furthermore, g-Zn–N–C/PtCo displays excellent stability for ORR, with a MA retention rate of 98.3% after 10K potential cycle. Impressively, 79.3% of MA was maintained after 90K potential cycles when g-Zn–N–C/PtCo was integrated into a fuel cell cathode. No noticeable voltage decay was observed at 1.5 A cm−2 in 230 hours, showcasing fascinating potential in fuel cells. Theoretical calculations reveal that the Pt–Zn–N interaction introduced by Zn–N site can stabilize the PtCo IMCs and improve ORR activity. This work proposes a universal and robust strategy in the form of atom glue for stabilizing ultrafine Pt-based NPs, which will greatly contribute to their practical application in fuel cells.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

The following chemicals were used as received without any further purification. The carbon used is KB-300. Pt(acac)2 (97%), Co(acac)3 (98%), iron (III) acetylacetonate [Fe(acac)3, 98%], copper (II) acetylacetonate (Cu(acac)2, 97%), zinc chloride (ZnCl2, 98%), manganese (II) phthalocyanine (MnPc, 95%), iron (II) phthalocyanine (FePc, 95%), cobalt (II) phthalocyanine (CoPc, 95%), nickel (II) phthalocyanine (CoPc, 98%), copper (II) phthalocyanine (CuPc, 95%), and zinc (II) phthalocyanine (ZnPc, 95%) were purchased from Aladdin. Nafion perfluorinated resin solution (5 wt %) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Trichloromethane (CHCl3, AR), and isopropanol (C3H8O, AR) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of g-M–N–C/PtxM

To synthesize g-Zn–N–C/PtCo, a mixture of 5 mg of ZnPc, 50 mg KB-300, 36 mg of Pt(acac)2, 32 mg of Co(acac)3 and 10 ml of CHCl3 were prepared. The resulting suspension was stirred at room temperature overnight to ensure thorough mixing. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to annealing in a 5% H2/Ar atmosphere at 900°C for 2 hours. g-M–N–C/PtCo and N–C/PtCo were synthesized using a similar protocol, except for changing ZnPc precursor with other metal phthalocyanine (MPc) precursors (4.5 mg of Pc, 4.9 mg of MnPc, 4.9 mg of FePc, 4.9 mg of CoPc, 4.9 mg of NiPc, or 5 mg of CuPc). For the synthesis of g-Zn–N–C/PtxM, the stoichiometric molar ratios of platinum and transition metal precursors were carefully controlled at 3:1 or 1:1 at specified temperature (e.g., 700°C for Pt3Fe, 750°C for PtFe, and 800°C for Pt3Co/PtCu/PtZn). For the synthesis at 10-g scale, 1.2 g of ZnPc, 6 g of KB-300, 8.6 g of Pt(acac)2, 7.7 g of Co(acac)3, and 500 ml of CHCl3 were placed in a 1000-ml beaker. The resulting suspension was stirred in a fume hood at room temperature until the solution was evaporated, followed by annealing in a tube under a 5% H2/Ar mixture at 900°C for 2 hours, with a temperature ramping rate of 5°C/min.

The above samples underwent postprocessing involving acid etching with 0.5 M HNO3 at 70°C for 2 hours to remove any unreacted or loosely bound species. Afterwards, the etched samples were washed twice with ethanol through centrifugation and subsequently dried in an oven at 50°C for 24 hours. A final annealing step was performed in a 5% H2/Ar atmosphere at 300°C for 2 hours to enhance the stability of the catalysts.

Characterizations

The structure was studied by XRD (Ultima IV) equipment with a Cu Kα radiation. The TEM, HAADF-STEM, and high-resolution TEM images were collected on FEI Tecnai F30 TEM with an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. Atomic-resolution high-angle ring dark-field images and EDS mapping were captured on a JEM-ARM300F scanning transmission electron microscope/transmission electron microscope operating at 300 kV. The loading amount of Pt was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; ICAP 7000, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nitrogen adsorption isotherms, surface area, and pore width distributions of the samples were performed using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (Micromeritics ASAP 2020 HD88) test system. The x-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) spectra were acquired at the TLS01C1 beamline of the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (Hsinchu, Taiwan).

Rotating disk electrode test

To prepare the catalyst ink for rotating disk electrode (RDE) testing, 2 mg of catalyst powder was dispersed in 0.495 ml of isopropanol and sonicated for 1 hour. Afterwards, 0.005 ml of Nafion (5 wt %) was added into the above mixture and sonicated for an additional 1 hour. The resulting ink was then uniformly dropwise added to the glassy-carbon RDE with a geometric area of 0.196 cm2 and allowed to dry at room temperature. The Pt loading on all catalysts was maintained between 1.6 and 2 μgPt, as determined by ICP-OES. ORR measurements were conducted in a 0.1 M HClO4 solution at room temperature using a typical three-electrode system consisting of a working electrode, a counter electrode, and a reference electrode. CV measurements were collected in a 0.1 M HClO4 solution at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 within a potential range of 0.05 to 1.1 V (versus RHE). The linear scanning voltammetry curves were recorded in an O2-saturated 0.1 M HClO4 solution at a rotation rate of 1600 rpm and a sweeping rate of 10 mV s−1 with internal resistance (iR) correction. ADTs were conducted in 0.1 M HClO4 solution by performing 10,000 potential sweeps between 0.60 and 0.95 V versus RHE at a sweep rate of 0.5 V s−1. The ECSA was determined by integrating the results from the CO stripping test in CV, assuming 420 μC cmPGM−2. By normalizing the kinetic current with mass loading and electrochemical surface area, the MA and SA values at 0.9 V versus RHE were calculated. The kinetic current was calculated by the Koutecky-Levich equation: ik = (il × i0.9)/(il − i0.9), in this equation, ik is the kinetic current, il is the limiting current, and i0.9 is the current at 0.9 V. ADTs were performed at room temperature via cyclic sweeps between 0.6 and 1.1 V (versus RHE) in O2-saturated 0.1 M HClO4 with a sweep rate of 500 mV s−1.

MEA fabrication and MA test

The catalyst ink was prepared by ultrasonically mixing of the catalyst with D2020 perfluorosulfonic acid ionomer (ionomer-to-carbon ratio of 0.7) and a water-isopropanol solvent (the water-isopropanol ratio was 1), followed by sonicating for 1 hour. This fresh ink was then evenly sprayed onto a GORE membrane (~12 μm thick; active area, 5 cm−2) to form a cathodic catalyst layer with a loading of 0.08 mgPt cm−2 for g-Zn–N–C/PtCo and 0.1 mgPt cm−2 for N–C/PtCo and Pt/C. The anode catalyst used JM 40 wt % commercial Pt/C as anode catalyst with the Pt loading of 0.1 mgPt cm−2. The actual loading of noble metals was determined by weighing the membrane before and after spraying. Commercial carbon paper was used as a gas diffusion layer (GDL). The catalyst-coated membrane (CCM) had an active area of 5 cm2 and was not subjected to any additional hot pressing. The prepared CCM was dried until the solvent completely evaporates. The final assembly involved combining the CCM, two GDLs (100 μm), and two polytetrafluoroethylene gaskets (190 μm) to create the MEA. The single-channel serpentine flow field was used in all single-cell tests. The pressure drop between the inlet and outlet of the flow field is negligible. Subsequently, single cell performance test was performed on the Scribner 850 e fuel cell test system. For MEA test, the operating temperature of the fuel cell, back pressure, and relative humidity (RH) were set at 80°C, 150 kPaabs, and 100%, respectively. Before data collection, the single cell was preactivated at 0.85, 0.6, 0.5, and 0.4 V for 5 min each, followed by cycling until a steady current density was achieved.

For H2-O2 single-cell test, the MA was obtained at 0.9 ViR-correct, 80°C, 150 kPaabs, and 100% RH with a flow rate of 500–standard cubic centimeter per minute (sccm) H2, 500-sccm O2, respectively. The H2/O2 polarization curves were recorded using the potential step mode with a constant flow rate of 500/500 sccm at 100% RH.

For the H2-air single-cell test, the test conditions were 80°C, 150 kPaabs, and 100% RH with a flow rate of 500 sccm of H2 and 1000 sccm of air, respectively. The ADT cycle test of a square wave was performed at a voltage of 0.6 V for 3 s and then 0.95 V for 3 s according to the DOE MEA ADT for platinum-group metal (PGM) catalyst protocol. The ADT was carried out at 80°C, 150 kPaabs, and 100% RH with 100-sccm H2/100-sccm N2 for the anode/cathode. In addition, a chronopotentiometry measurement at a constant current density of 1.5 A cm−2 at a H2/O2 flow rate of 200/200 sccm.

Computational details

DFT (21, 22) calculations were performed via Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) (30), with the projector-augmented plane-wave method to represent the ion-electron interaction (31). The exchange-correlation energy was described by Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof function at generalized gradient approximation level (32). Spin polarization was taken into account in the calculations, and the Gaussian smearing scheme was used by setting the partial occupancy for each orbital with a smearing width of 0.10 eV. For geometry optimization, the plane wave cutoff energy was set to be 520 eV, and the Brillouin zone was sampled with 1 × 1 × 1 k-points (33). The convergence criteria for electronic energy and force were 10−6 eV and 0.03 eV/Å, respectively. To improve the electronic structure calculations of transition metal compounds, the Hubbard U value in the Hamiltonian was set to be 5.0 eV for Co, as it removes the self-interaction in the metal d-orbitals, leading to strong charge localization and correction of ground-state structure (34, 35). The AIMD (23) simulations were carried out by sampling the canonical ensemble using Nosé-Hoover thermostats (36, 37), aimed to investigate the dynamics properties for a time period of 10 ps with a time step of 2 fs at a finite temperature of 300 K.
