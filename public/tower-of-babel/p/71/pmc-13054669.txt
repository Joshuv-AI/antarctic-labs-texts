Development and Validation of an Explainable Machine Learning Model for Identifying Hypertension Status in Patients With Obstructive Sleep Apnea
Authors: Feng Wei, Tianyu Wu, Jinggang Deng, Jiaxin Gong, Shuning Zhang, Zhaoqiang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is an independent risk factor for hypertension (HTN) worldwide; however, OSA‐related HTN is frequently overlooked in clinical practice, partly because of the limitations of traditional clinical blood pressure measurements. Our study aimed to develop an interpretable machine learning model for identifying HTN status among patients with OSA. We analyzed data from 1771 diagnosed OSA patients and randomly split them into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%). Using LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm, we identified seven key predictors for prevalent HTN status, namely, age, body mass index, neck circumference, lowest oxygen saturation, percentage of time with oxygen saturation below 90%, the apnea‐hypopnea index, and the triglyceride‐glucose index. Fourteen machine learning models were evaluated using internal validation based on a single random test set. Among the 14 models, the adaptive boosting model demonstrated superior performance, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.830 on the test set. Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) analysis not only confirmed the model's logic but also revealed a dose‒response relationship between each feature and HTN status, highlighting the collective contribution of nocturnal hypoxia burden, metabolic factors, and anatomical factors to the model's prediction. We ultimately developed an online prediction tool to facilitate rapid clinical application. This interpretable machine learning model provides a powerful tool for achieving precise sleep medicine. Our model highlights the strong association of nocturnal hypoxia burden (over respiratory event frequency alone) with HTN status in patients with OSA.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a widespread sleep‐disordered breathing condition that affects approximately 1 billion individuals worldwide and is defined as recurrent collapse of the upper airway during sleep [1, 2, 3]. Numerous studies have shown that OSA is firmly established as an independent risk factor for hypertension (HTN), which is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease that causes considerable global morbidity, mortality, and health burdens [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Observational studies have shown that 50%–70% of OSA patients have HTN, and the prevalence of OSA is more than 30% among HTN patients [9]. Identifying the factors that lead to HTN in OSA patients is crucial for improving outcomes for cardiovascular events in this high‐risk population.

OSA‐related HTN is defined as HTN induced or aggravated primarily by OSA after other obvious secondary causes are excluded [10]. It is characterized by a high incidence of latent HTN, a nondipper pattern of nocturnal HTN, increased nocturnal blood pressure (BP), and increased BP variability. Notably, OSA and HTN appear to be related to worse prognosis, including more severe end‐organ damage and a greater risk of incident heart failure, stroke and renal disease progression. However, OSA‐related HTN is often overlooked by patients [10, 11]. In the general population, BP measurements are performed primarily in the clinic, and this diagnostic approach is not appropriate for OSA patients. Among OSA patients, 30% have masked HTN, and approximately 33% have white coat HTN, which means that OSA patients are at high risk of being misdiagnosed or overdiagnosed with HTN [12, 13, 14]. Therefore, ambulatory BP monitoring should be applied to systematically and correctly evaluate the blood pressure of OSA patients [15]. However, recommending ambulatory BP monitoring for all clinically normotensive OSA patients is challenging because of its low cost‐effectiveness and cumbersome nature. Therefore, a simple and practical tool is needed to assess HTN in OSA patients.

As a powerful tool for predictive model development, machine learning (ML) can reveal complex, nonlinear interactions in high‐dimensional clinical data [16, 17]. ML applications have diversified widely in relation to cardiovascular and sleep medicine. These include predicting coronary artery disease and heart failure from clinical variables, detecting arrhythmias via electrocardiograms, and diagnosing and classifying OSA using polysomnography (PSG) or simpler clinical indicators [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Models for predicting OSA‐related HTN have also emerged [23], but their performance varies significantly, and generalizability and causal interpretation still need to be improved.

Existing models for predicting HTN in OSA patients are limited by population heterogeneity and narrow feature sets. In this study, we constructed and validated an interpretable ML framework that integrates comprehensive clinical, polysomnographic, and biochemical variables to predict HTN in patients with OSA, with the intention of providing a tool to aid in the identification of OSA patients with a higher probability of having HTN, which could inform clinical evaluation and management.

Methods

Study Design

The protocol for this retrospective cross‐sectional study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Hospital Affiliated with Fudan University. All the study subjects were OSA patients who visited the cardiology department from January 1, 2024, to October 31, 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) an apnea‒hypopnea index (AHI) ≥ 5 times/hour; and (3) no previous OSA‐related treatment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete baseline data; (2) a history of snoring shorter than the duration of HTN; (3) complications associated with diseases that may affect blood pressure, such as severe heart failure, uremia, kidney transplantation or heart transplantation, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome; (4) complications associated with secondary HTN, including primary aldosteronism, Cushing's syndrome, pheochromocytoma, renal vascular diseases, renal parenchymal diseases, hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, and aortic coarctation; (5) ongoing pregnancy; and (6) a diagnosis of central sleep apnea. The primary outcome was the presence of HTN at baseline, defined as a recorded clinical diagnosis, current use of antihypertensive drugs (data on medication prescriptions were extracted from pharmacy records), or repeated measurement of systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. Data Collection

Population demographics and anthropometric data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), neck circumference (NC), and smoking and drinking history, were collected. A series of PSG parameters were evaluated, including the AHI, oxygen desaturation index (ODI), mean oxygen saturation (MeanO2) and lowest oxygen saturation (LowestO2), percentage of total sleep time with SpO2<90% (CT90), total sleep duration (TSD), sleep efficiency (SE), and average pulse rate (PR), as well as the Epworth Sleepiness Scale score (ESS). Some important comorbidities and laboratory indicators, including the presence of diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, gout, and cardiovascular disease; total cholesterol (TC), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), triglyceride (TG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), uric acid (UA), and fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels; and the triglyceride‐glucose index (TyG), were also recorded. BP was measured by trained nursing staff using a calibrated electronic sphygmomanometer (Omron Model). Following a 5‐min seated rest, BP was measured three times on the nondominant arm, with 1–2‐min intervals between measurements, and the average value was recorded. All BP measurements used for HTN diagnosis in this study were clinic‐based readings taken during the awake state prior to the PSG study.

Data Preprocessing and Imputation

To ensure data integrity, missing values were addressed using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). All the continuous variables were normalized using Z score transformation (centering and scaling) to harmonize the feature scales. This preprocessing step is crucial for preventing variables with larger magnitudes from disproportionately influencing the model gradients.

Variable Selection

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). The training set was used for variable selection, model development, and hyperparameter tuning, and the test set was used for performance evaluation. Preliminary variable selection was conducted to address issues of data reliability and multicollinearity; the TyG index was prioritized to be retained, thereby excluding the related variables FBG and TG. Any comorbidities with a prevalence of less than 5% were excluded from the analysis. This pragmatic step was taken to improve model stability and parsimony for this initial predictive model, recognizing that it may omit predictive signals from rare but severe conditions. This preprocessed dataset was used as the input for the subsequent dual feature selection stage, using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method and the Boruta algorithm, which is a strategy aimed at identifying a set of simple features and preventing model overfitting. The LASSO method refined these subsets to determine the most discriminative subset for ML model construction and determine the optimal regularization parameter λ through 10‐fold cross‐validation on the basis of the minimum cross‐validation error criterion. The Boruta algorithm was used to iteratively compare the importance of the original features with the randomly permuted “shadow” features within the random forest framework. Only the features whose Z values consistently exceeded the maximum importance of the shadow attributes during the iteration process were considered to have statistical relevance. The variables retained by the two algorithms are considered robust and are used for downstream modeling.

Model Construction and Validation

With a training set of 1241 patients, our sample size substantially exceeded common rules of thumb (e.g., >10 events per predictor) for the final model with seven features, reducing overfitting concerns. Continuous features were standardized using Z score normalization, and categorical variables were converted into binary features via one‐hot encoding. To address potential information bias from extreme values, we applied Winsorization at the first and 99th percentiles to continuous variables. A total of 14 machine learning algorithms were implemented and systematically compared, including neural networks, the k‐nearest neighbor algorithm, the kernel method support vector machine, discriminant analysis, the partial least squares method, the LASSO algorithm, logistic regression, the naïve Bayes algorithm, the adaptive boosting algorithm, the gradient boosting algorithm, the random forest algorithm, the gradient boosting machine algorithm, the CATBoost algorithm, and the LightGBM algorithm. We performed 10‐fold cross‐validation on the training set for hyperparameter optimization to ensure the robustness and generalizability of the algorithms. For each algorithm, a grid search was conducted over a predefined search space to identify the optimal parameter configurations. The specific hyperparameter settings for all the evaluated models are detailed in Table S1. The comprehensive evaluation of the test set included sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score, Youden index, and area under the curve (AUC). The final selected model was determined by using a sorting system, and each model was scored from 14 (the highest ranking) to 1 (the lowest ranking). The model with the highest cumulative score was selected as the final tool for predicting HTN in patients with OSA. Calibration curves were generated to assess agreement between the predicted probability of HTN and the actual observed prevalence. The SHAP model was applied to provide interpretability insights into the prediction mechanism of the selected model.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are presented as numerical values and percentages. The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used to compare the differences between two groups for continuous variables, and the chi‐square test was used to evaluate categorical variables. The DeLong test was used to compare the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC), and the bootstrapping method was used to compare the area under the precision‒recall curve (AUPRC). A two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Prevalence and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 1771 OSA patients were ultimately included in the study. The subjects were randomly divided into a training set (n = 1241, accounting for 70%) for model development and a test set (n = 530, accounting for 30%) for internal validation. Table 1 details the baseline characteristics of the overall population and the comparison results between the training set and the test set. The prevalence of HTN in the overall population was relatively high (77.3%). These differences reflect a typical population seen in specialized cardiovascular centers in China, where the burden of both OSA and HTN is substantial. Compared with those of nonhypertensive patients, the proportion of male hypertensive patients was significantly increased, their BMI and NC was also significantly increased, and the proportion of severe OSA was also greater (all p values < 0.001). The burden of comorbidities in hypertensive patients was also significantly increased, including diabetes, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, chronic kidney disease and gout (all p values < 0.05). As expected, the PSG parameters reflected greater disease severity in the HTN group, with significantly higher AHI, CT90, ODI, and LowestO2 (all p < 0.001). Several metabolic biomarkers, including TG levels, UA levels, FBG levels, and the TyG index, were also significantly increased in the HTN group. Critically, the random split into training and test sets was successful. There were no statistically significant differences in any recorded demographic, clinical, comorbidity, medication, PSG, or biochemical variables between the training and test sets (all p > 0.05), confirming the balanced nature of the data partitioning and ensuring the validity of the subsequent internal validation.

Feature Selection and Covariate Removal

To identify the most informative predictors of HTN in OSA patients, we performed feature selection using the LASSO and Boruta algorithms. As shown in Figure 2A,B, the LASSO model progressively decreased the regression coefficients with increasing penalty strength, and 10‐fold cross‐validation revealed the optimal λ value. On the basis of the minimum binomial deviance criterion, fifteen candidate variables were retained, namely, age, BMI, NC, smoking status, diabetes mellitus status, hyperlipemia status, arrhythmia status, coronary heart disease status, gout status, antidiabetic drug use, AHI, CT90, LowestO2, average PR, and TyG. To further validate feature robustness, we applied the Boruta algorithm, which ranks variable importance using a random forest framework (Figure 2C). Age, BMI, NC, AHI, CT90, ODI, LowestO2, UA, and TyG demonstrated the strongest contributions to hypertensive status in OSA patients. The final intersection of the two algorithms resulted in a set of consistent key predictive factors (Figure 2D), which were incorporated into the model development. These results indicated that the severity of nocturnal hypoxemia (CT90 and the lowestO2), the severity of OSA (AHI), the metabolic burden (BMI and TyG), and demographic factors (age and NC) were significantly associated with HTN status and were important features in the predictive model.

Model Construction and Evaluation

The 14 ML models that used multiple indicators were evaluated in both the training group (Figure S1) and the test group (Figure 3) to comprehensively compare their ability to identify patients with OSA with concurrent HTN. Most of the models demonstrated moderate to high diagnostic performance, but there were significant differences in the discrimination and calibration indices (Figure 3A). On the basis of the comprehensive ranking of all the models based on various evaluation indicators, the adaptive boosting model achieved the highest total score of 71 points (Figure 3B). This superior performance is largely attributed to its well‐balanced sensitivity–specificity profile and optimal Youden index. Furthermore, the ROC curve and PR curve further indicated that this model has the strongest discriminatory ability, with its AUC (0.830) and AUPRC values being the highest among all the models (Figure 3C,D). Calibration was further assessed on the training and test set using reliability diagrams (Figure S2). Both training and validation set curves showed good alignment with the ideal diagonal line, with minor residual deviations primarily in the high‐probability region, and 95% Wilson confidence intervals largely encompassing the ideal line.

Model Explanation and Application

The absolute SHAP values of different variables for the ranking of feature importance are shown in Figure 4A. LowestO2, CT90, TyG, and the AHI were identified as the most important predictors, and nocturnal hypoxemia, OSA severity, and metabolic status were the features most strongly associated with HTN status in our model. BMI, age, and NC also contributed substantially to the predictions. The SHAP summary plot shows the value distribution, indicating that lower values of LowestO2 and higher values of CT90 were associated with a higher predicted probability of HTN. Higher TyG, AHI, and BMI values were also associated with an increased predicted probability of HTN, whereas lower values were associated with a lower probability (Figure 4B). A force plot for a representative 43‐year‐old patient (LowestO2 = 71%, CT90 = 2.5%) is shown in Figure 4C. It decomposed the prediction, revealing that LowestO2 contributed positively (+0.0198) to the predicted probability, whereas the value of CT90 contributed negatively (–0.00896).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated the predictive performance of 14 ML models for HTN in OSA patients, ultimately obtaining an adaptive boosting model that can be used clinically. In our model, measures of nocturnal hypoxic burden (CT90 and LowestO2) ranked as the most important features for distinguishing hypertensive status, ahead of the AHI. Metabolic factors (TyG and BMI), together with age and NC, also featured prominently as key associated factors. These findings collectively support the hypothesis that evaluating HTN in OSA patients on the basis of hypoxic and metabolic phenotypes, rather than relying solely on the traditional, AHI‐centered “one‐size‐fits‐all” framework, could lead to more precise management. While our ML approach focuses primarily on discrimination, the high feature importance of CT90 and the TyG index—even after accounting for age and BMI—suggests that these variables are robust markers of the hypertensive state in OSA patients, which aligns with their established biological roles in sympathetic activation and systemic inflammation. Furthermore, they indicate that interpretable ML tools have the potential to become integral components of future clinical decision‐support systems for individualized cardiovascular risk assessment.

Traditionally, the AHI, which assesses the frequency of apneas and hypopneas during sleep, is considered the gold standard for diagnosing OSA, and it is also commonly used to assess OSA‐related cardiovascular events [24]. A Danish prospective cohort study demonstrated that when OSA severity was stratified by the AHI, individuals with moderate or severe OSA faced a significantly increased 10‐year risk of fatal cardiovascular events [25]. However, the AHI is unable to reflect specific parameters related to the duration and severity of hypoxemic burden and cannot provide a comprehensive explanation of OSA characteristics [26, 27, 28]. Some studies have shown that because OSA is a heterogeneous disease, patients with the same AHI have significant differences in pathogenesis, comorbidity frequency, and prognosis, indicating that the AHI may be insufficient for studying the association between OSA and related comorbidities [29, 30, 31, 32]. Recent studies have shown that some novel predictors, including nocturnal hypoxemic load, ventilatory stress, arousal‐related burden, and the respiratory disturbance index, are more closely related to the risk of complications than the AHI is [33, 34]. In a clinical cohort of newly diagnosed OSA patients, elevated OSA‐specific hypoxic burden and CT90 independently predicted the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events better than the AHI did [35]. Wang et al. reported that severe OSA patients with high CT90 (>25% of sleep) had ∼2.7‐fold greater odds of HTN than those with minimal hypoxia (CT90 ≤5%) did [36]. In our research, LASSO logistic regression and the Boruda algorithm suggested that age, BMI, NC, LowestO2, CT90, AHI, and TyG were variables significantly associated with HTN in OSA patients, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies. The adaptive boosting model constructed on the basis of seven key variables demonstrated excellent predictive performance, but its robustness and clinical application value still need to be verified through prospective clinical studies and in‐depth mechanistic animal experiments.

In terms of pathogenesis, chronic intermittent hypoxia in OSA patients sensitizes the carotid body chemoreflex, triggering sustained sympathetic activation beyond sleep, with subsequent stimulation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS), promoting renin release and vasoconstriction [37, 38]. In parallel, repeated oxyhemoglobin desaturation elevates reactive oxygen species and adipokines, which impair endothelial nitric oxide function and promote vascular inflammation [37, 39]. These processes collectively drive arterial stiffening and enhance aldosterone‐mediated sodium retention, perpetuating HTN [37, 38]. The strong association of CT90 and LowestO2with HTN status, as quantified by their high SHAP values in our model, provides empirical support for the pathophysiological mechanisms linking nocturnal hypoxia to hypertension. While the AHI remains the diagnostic gold standard, our findings underscore that it may not be the most sensitive predictor of cardiovascular complications. Metrics of hypoxic burden have a more direct effect on blood pressure regulation. This finding is consistent with the theory that deep, prolonged desaturation, rather than the frequency of events alone, is the primary driver of sympathetic overactivity and oxidative stress in patients with OSA‐related HTN [26, 35]. This finding reinforces the importance of quantifying hypoxic severity in the clinical evaluation of OSA patients and lends weight to the argument for including CT90 as a key metric in routine PSG reports.

In addition to nocturnal hypoxemia, metabolic dysregulation, especially insulin resistance (IR), critically influences HTN risk in the OSA population. Previous studies have shown that the TyG index, which serves as a practical surrogate marker of IR, is strongly correlated with the severity of OSA and elevated blood pressure [39]. These studies support the “double‐hit” pathophysiological model, in which intermittent hypoxia and metabolic burden act synergistically to accelerate the progression of HTN in patients with OSA. Chronic intermittent hypoxia causes ectopic fat deposition and IR by disrupting metabolic signaling pathways. The resulting IR triggers the activation of the RAAS through neural‐hormonal mechanisms, leading to vasoconstriction and sodium retention [39]. Our analysis revealed that BMI and the TyG index were strongly associated with HTN status in OSA patients. These findings reinforce the existing pathophysiological concept, which is consistent with prior clinical observations, that metabolic dysfunction and hypoxic stress are not independent risk factors but are intertwined in terms of core pathological processes, which together explain the more severe hypertensive burden in patients with elevated TyG and central obesity.

The strong predictive value of nocturnal hypoxic burden and the TyG index in our model aligns with their known role as upstream drivers of HTN in OSA patients. Intermittent hypoxia is a potent trigger of sympathetic activation and RAAS stimulation [40, 41], whereas IR often coexists with and may potentiate aldosterone‐mediated pathways [42, 43]. Thus, while our study did not directly measure these specific neurohumoral biomarkers, our selected features likely serve as integrative, clinically feasible surrogates that capture the downstream phenotypic convergence of these mechanisms.

Large‐scale randomized trials have highlighted the heterogeneity of the cardiovascular benefits of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). The sleep apnea cardiovascular endpoints trial (SAVE) revealed that the primary cardiovascular events in patients treated with CPAP did not significantly decrease over approximately 3.7 years [44]. Similarly, the ISAACC (Impact of Sleep Apnea syndrome in the evolution of Acute Coronary syndrome. Effect of intervention with CPAP) trial revealed no difference in recurrent CV events between the CPAP and usual care groups [45]. These negative findings have been confirmed in meta‐analyses: an individual‐patient‐data meta‐analysis of SAVE, ISAACC and other trials revealed no benefit in intention‐to‐treat analyses but revealed that patients adherent to CPAP (≥4 h/night) had a significantly lower risk of recurrent cardiovascular events (adjusted hazard ratio ≈0.69) [46]. In contrast, trials focused on blood pressure endpoints have shown modest effects. For example, the HIPARCO trial in resistant hypertensive OSA patients revealed that 12 weeks of CPAP lowered 24‐h mean BP by ∼3.1 mmHg (p = 0.02) and improved nocturnal “dipping” status [47]. In summary, CPAP clearly improves OSA symptoms and sleep quality, but its impact on BP/CV outcomes depends on patient factors (baseline BP, sleepiness, and adherence) and selection. High adherence and severe hypoxemia appear to be necessary for CPAP to translate into measurable cardiovascular benefits [44, 46]. While our study is cross‐sectional and cannot directly establish treatment efficacy, the identified hypoxic‐burden phenotype (high CT90/low nadir SpO2) represents a subgroup that may be physiologically more predisposed to HTN. This leads to the hypothesis that these individuals might achieve more meaningful BP improvement with CPAP than those with less severe desaturation. This perspective aligns with emerging evidence that CPAP responsiveness may be phenotype‐dependent and suggests a potential clinical shift for future investigations: prioritizing CPAP for hypoxic‐dominant phenotypes. However, this remains an implication for future prospective interventional trials, which are necessary to determine whether such phenotypic stratification can indeed translate into improved longitudinal cardiovascular outcomes and enable ‘true’ precision sleep medicine.

A major strength of our work is the use of explainable ML and SHAP interpretation, which makes the model not only accurate but also clinically transparent and actionable. ML is poised to transform OSA care through innovative diagnostics and personalized monitoring. For example, ML models are already being embedded into telehealth platforms. One recent study developed ML algorithms (logistic regression, ANN, SVM, etc.) using simple patient data (age, BMI, sleep‐time heart rate) to predict moderate‐to‐severe OSA with ∼80% accuracy and integrated these models into a cloud‐based mobile sleep app [48]. This shows how ML‐driven risk scores can be delivered via smartphones or wearable devices for early screening. In parallel, ML has been applied to electronic health records (EHRs) to identify patients at risk or predict therapy outcomes. For instance, models using routine EMR features have been tested to predict CPAP adherence: a recent US veteran cohort study trained over a dozen algorithms (random forests, SVM, neural nets, etc.) on clinical and polysomnography data to predict 90‐day CPAP use [49]. While current EHR‐based adherence models still lack perfect accuracy [49], they suggest future clinical decision support tools that flag likely nonadherers for early intervention. Similarly, ML incorporating physiological traits (sleep study metrics, loop gain) has improved the prediction of the CPAP response: Lo et al. used random forests combining clinical and respiratory control parameters to predict residual AHI on CPAP, yielding better accuracy than linear models did [50]. Future paths include remote monitoring (using smartwatch PPG or home oximetry with ML algorithms), EHR‐embedded alerts for patients with undiagnosed OSA or uncontrolled HTN, and adaptive learning systems that personalize treatment. For example, continuous home oximetry or BP sensors analyzed by AI could adapt to CPAP pressure or timing. Overall, emerging research suggests that ML will soon enable real‐time OSA management: remote data capture, digital phenotyping and integrated health‐system algorithms will guide tailored therapy and risk stratification [48, 50].

Our study complements recent advancements in the ML‐based assessment of HTN in OSA patients. Martinot et al. demonstrated that mandibular movement signals, reflecting respiratory effort, are robust predictors of HTN [51]. While their work emphasizes the mechanical impact of OSA, our findings underscore the importance of the hypoxic–metabolic axis, in which CT90 and the TyG index emerged as core predictors. These findings suggest that the depth of hypoxic exposure and insulin resistance may be as critical as respiratory effort in driving HTN. Furthermore, compared with the model proposed by Shi et al., which focuses on traditional PSG metrics, our integration of metabolic signatures enhances the clinical relevance of the model, providing a more holistic view of the cardiovascular risk profile [23] of OSA patients. Notably, the primary aim of this study was to develop a pragmatic prediction model using variables readily available in standard clinical practice. While deep phenotyping with specialized biomarkers is crucial for understanding the underlying mechanisms, our model offers a feasible first‐step tool for widespread risk assessment.

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective, cross‐sectional design at a single center precludes causal inference; thus, our model identifies prevalent HTN status rather than forecasting incident risk. Second, the reliance on clinic BP measurements instead of ambulatory blood pressure monitoring may have introduced misclassification bias, potentially overlooking masked or white‐coat HTN. Third, the cohort's recruitment from a cardiology department suggests significant selection bias, limiting generalizability to primary care or community settings. Finally, the absence of neurohumoral biomarkers (e.g., aldosterone, renin, or sympathetic activity) means that the reported contributions of hypoxic burden and IR may be quantitatively overestimated, as they are likely clinical surrogates for these unmeasured pathways. Future multicenter prospective trials are essential to validate these phenotypes and determine whether phenotype‐guided management improves longitudinal cardiovascular outcomes.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight that nocturnal hypoxic burden is more strongly associated with HTN status than respiratory event frequency in patients with OSA is. By identifying a hypoxic‐metabolic phenotype and providing a transparent tool for stratification, this study contributes to a shift toward phenotype‐based OSA management. These results support the routine reporting of CT90, suggest that CPAP prioritization could be informed by phenotypic assessment, and indicate that interpretable ML tools merit further integration into clinical workflows to advance precision medicine in sleep and cardiovascular care.
