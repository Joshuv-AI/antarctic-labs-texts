Securing the Anchor in Pure Native Aortic Regurgitation
Authors: Vyanne Hei-Tung Chan, Kent Chak-yu So
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has transformed the management of patients with aortic stenosis, however pure native aortic regurgitation (PNAR) remains a major challenge for transcatheter therapy. Unlike aortic stenosis, PNAR is characterized by the absence of annular and leaflet calcification, as well as significant annular dilatation. These anatomical features deprive transcatheter heart valves of the friction and radial counterforce required for secure anchoring, resulting in a high risk of device migration and embolization, reported in up to 12% of cases.1 It is associated with an increased need for valve-in-valve implantation, recurrent heart failure hospitalization, and higher procedural mortality, with composite rates of heart failure hospitalization and mortality of up to 25%.1 Consequently, patients with severe PNAR have historically faced limited transcatheter treatment options.

In this context, Dvir et al2 have introduced a novel anchoring strategy that addresses the fundamental limitation of TAVR in PNAR: anchoring via trans-leaflet apposition stent (ATLAS). By intentionally traversing the noncoronary cusp (NCC) with a guidewire and deploying a peripheral stent across the leaflet, the authors created an artificial anchoring scaffold that can provide mechanical grip and friction for the transcatheter valve stent frame. In the reported case, a Palmaz Blue (Cordis) peripheral stent was positioned across the NCC and was subsequently crushed during the deployment of a balloon-expandable Sapien 3 Ultra Resilia valve (Edwards Lifesciences). The crushed stent frame served as a mechanical anchor, enhancing valve stability and preventing embolization.

The ATLAS technique represents a solution for anchoring off-label TAVR devices in PNAR. Nonetheless, it is important to recognize that the field has evolved toward purpose-built solutions for this disease entity. Dedicated devices for PNAR, including the J-valve system (JC Medical Inc) and the JenaValve Trilogy (JenaValve Technology) have been designed to overcome the anchoring challenges in PNAR. Both platforms employ active leaflet-based fixation mechanisms and provide anchoring directly at the level of native aortic cusps, rather than relying on annular calcification or radial force alone. The JenaValve has locators that attach to the native leaflets for secure anchoring, whereas the J-valve incorporates U-shaped nitinol anchor rings that are positioned within the aortic sinuses to provide fixation and stability. Clinical experience with the J-valve reported a procedural success of 81%, with 1 reported case of embolization among 27 patients (3.7%).3 The ALIGN-AR trial of the JenaValve reported a high technical success rate of 95%, with embolization occurring in approximately 2% of patients (4 out of 180).4

Despite these advances, dedicated devices have several limitations. In the case presented by Dvir et al,2 a dedicated device was avoided given the presence of an aortic dissection flap. To minimize interactions with the flap, a Sapien 3 Ultra Resilia valve was selected for its short stent frame height. The ATLAS technique provided a means to enhance anchoring while accommodating the anatomical constraints imposed by the aortic dissection.

Additional patient-specific factors that limit the use of dedicated devices include bicuspid aortic valve anatomy, which was excluded in the initial studies of devices.3,4 Anchoring in bicuspid anatomy inherently relies on engagement of 2 cusps rather than 3, which may compromise the stability of the active leaflet-based fixation system and result in less predictable behavior. This may lead to complications including valve tilting, paravalvular leak, or embolization.5

Device sizing also limits their applicability in patients with extra-large annuli. The J-valve is available in sizes from 22 to 34 mm, corresponding to annular perimeters of 57 to 104 mm, whereas the JenaValve is offered in 23-, 25-, and 27-mm sizes and accommodates annular perimeters up to 90 mm. In patients with annular dimensions exceeding these ranges, dedicated devices may not be suitable. A case report has described the use of the J-valve in a patient with an annular perimeter of 112 mm, however the procedure resulted in valve tilting and significant paravalvular leak and required an additional valve-in-valve procedure for stabilization and sealing of the leak.6

Furthermore, dedicated devices for PNAR are not widely available internationally. The J-valve is currently approved only in mainland China, whereas the JenaValve is available in the United States and Europe and has yet to achieve broader adoption across Asia.

Given these limitations of dedicated devices, the novel ATLAS technique described by Dvir et al2 remains a valuable tool, particularly in cases where dedicated devices are unsuitable. In addition to the ATLAS technique, several other anchoring strategies for PNAR patients have been reported. The “plus anchor valve replacement” technique from China employs a similar principle of deploying a ventricular septal defect occluder across the NCC to provide anchoring for subsequent implantation of a TaurusElite AV31 (Peijia Medical) self-expandable valve.7 Other approaches include the noncoronary sinus pivot implantation (NCPI) technique, which positions the valve at the base of the NCC while allowing the stent frame to extend into the left ventricular outflow tract at the contralateral cusp, hence creating a 3-point pivot for anchoring.8 The “flare the outflow” technique has also been described, in which the deploying balloon of the balloon-expandable valve is reinflated with additional 2 mL volume to flare the outflow portion of the stent frame and improve anchoring.9 These innovations and techniques (Figure 1) aim to compensate for the lack of an intrinsic anchoring mechanism in PNAR, stabilizing transcatheter valves in this challenging population.Figure 1Anchoring Strategies in Pure Native Aortic RegurgitationPure native aortic regurgitation is characterized by lack of calcium and a dilated annulus, which results in difficult anchoring in conventional transcatheter heart valve platforms. Anchoring Strategies in Pure Native Aortic Regurgitation

Pure native aortic regurgitation is characterized by lack of calcium and a dilated annulus, which results in difficult anchoring in conventional transcatheter heart valve platforms. Different anchoring strategies have been developed to address this problem: Dedicated devices (J-valve, JenaValve) have been developed to achieve active leaflet engagements, but they have various limitations. Other anchoring strategies include the anchoring via trans-leaflet apposition stent (ATLAS) technique,2 which anchors the transcatheter valve with a crushed peripheral stent; the plus anchor valve replacement technique, which uses a ventricular septal defect occluder as an anchor7; the noncoronary sinus pivot implantation (NCPI) technique8; and the flare the outflow technique.9

The ATLAS technique, alongside the other anchoring strategies, represents a useful innovation for addressing anatomical challenges in PNAR. Beyond the aortic position, these anchoring strategies may be applied to transcatheter mitral valve replacement, particularly in patients without extensive mitral annular calcification and in whom stable anchoring remains a major challenge. Such innovations will play a crucial role in bridging the gap between dedicated devices and real-world anatomical complexity.
