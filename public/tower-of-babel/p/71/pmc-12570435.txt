Evaluating co-design approaches with parents in paediatric healthcare: a systematic review
Authors: Jessica Suna, Daniel Tanti, Gregory Merlo, Penelope Bryant, Brendan McMullan, Lisa Hall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Co-design is increasingly employed in healthcare to develop interventions that reflect the needs and preferences of patients and families. In paediatric settings, actively involving parents and guardians is particularly critical due to their central role in care delivery and decision-making. Despite growing interest in co-design, its implementation and impact in paediatric healthcare—especially in relation to parental engagement—remain inconsistently reported. This systematic review examines the use of co-design methodologies involving parents and guardians in developing paediatric healthcare interventions.Methods:This review was conducted using Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024550366). Seven databases (Medline, Embase, Global Health, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Cochrane, Web of Science) were searched for studies published from January 2000 to June 2025. Eligible studies employed co-design methods to develop paediatric healthcare interventions involving parents or guardians. Two reviewers independently screened records, extracted data on study characteristics, co-design methods, participants, intervention focus, and outcomes, and appraised quality using a customised JBI checklist. Findings were synthesised narratively to identify key themes.Results:The search retrieved 12,574 citations. Seventy studies met the inclusion criteria, representing diverse designs including Participatory Action Research, Experience-Based Co-Design, Human-Centred Design, and mixed-methods. Across studies, 53,676 participants contributed, including parents/guardians, children, healthcare providers (clinicians, nurses), carers, and hospital policy makers. Interventions frequently addressed digital tools, communication, health education, and psychosocial supports. Outcomes were predominantly qualitative, reporting improved acceptability, usability, feasibility, and early implementation success. Reported challenges included power imbalances and scalability concerns, with limited explicit reporting on diversity, outcome consistency, or sustainability.Conclusion:Co-design with parents in paediatric healthcare supports the development of contextually relevant and acceptable interventions, with evidence of improved relevance, cultural alignment, and satisfaction in a subset of studies. However, reporting on outcome measurement remains inconsistent, and evidence on long-term sustainability and scale is limited. Future research should prioritise inclusive recruitment strategies, robust outcome evaluation (including economic analyses), and integration of co-designed interventions into routine clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13495-x.Keywords: Co-design, Participatory design, Paediatric healthcare, Family-centred care, Parental engagement, Systematic review

Background

Co-design represents a significant evolution in healthcare innovation, shifting away from traditional top-down service development toward collaborative approaches that centre upon the experiences of end-users. In paediatric healthcare, where children may not always be able to articulate their healthcare needs, the active involvement of parents and guardians is essential. As primary caregivers and advocates, they offer valuable insights and support that enhance the relevance, usability, and sustainability of interventions [1]. This review therefore focuses specifically on co-design involving parents and guardians, recognising their critical role in shaping paediatric healthcare interventions.

Co-design, also known as participatory design, emerged from the Scandinavian design movement in the 1960s and 1970s, initially developed to empower workers in shaping workplace technologies [2, 3]. Since then, co-design has been widely adopted across diverse sectors, including healthcare, where it is used to co-create services that better reflect the values, needs, and lived experiences of patients and communities [4]. In adult healthcare, co-design has supported the development of chronic disease management tools, mental health services, and patient education resources, often through frameworks such as Experience-Based Co-Design (EBCD) and Participatory Action Research (PAR) [5, 6]. In paediatrics, co-design has been more recently used to develop digital health platforms, educational materials, psychosocial interventions, and tools for improving communication and shared decision-making between clinicians and families [7, 8].

Evidence suggests that co-designed healthcare interventions can enhance patient satisfaction, improve health outcomes, and foster stronger relationships between families and healthcare providers [9–11]. These benefits are often attributed to increased alignment between services and the real-world needs of end-users, as well as to a sense of ownership and trust cultivated through active participation [9, 10]. Despite its promise, co-design in paediatric settings presents several challenges. These include time and resource constraints, difficulties in maintaining sustained engagement from co-design partners, and power imbalances that may limit the influence of families compared to professionals [11, 12]. In paediatrics, additional complexities arise from the involvement of both children and their parents or guardians in the co-design process. This can include navigating differing priorities between children, parents, and clinicians; ensuring child-friendly engagement methods; and addressing ethical and consent considerations unique to involving minors. These factors require tailored approaches to co-design in paediatric contexts. Evaluating the impact of co-designed interventions is also a challenge, due to the often experiential or relational nature of outcomes, lack of standardised methodologies, and limited long-term follow-up data [7, 13, 14].

This systematic review synthesizes current evidence on the application of co-design methodologies involving parents and guardians in a paediatric healthcare setting. It examines how co-design has been implemented, the types of interventions developed, and the outcomes reported. The review aims to identify best practices, uncover barriers and facilitators, and offer guidance for future research and policy to enhance healthcare outcomes through co-designed solutions.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across seven electronic databases: Medline, Embase, Global Health, PsycINFO, Web of Science, CINAHL and Cochrane. The review protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024550366). The search was restricted to English-language studies published between January 1, 1980, and June 30, 2025. Peer-reviewed journal articles were eligible for inclusion; conference abstracts and unpublished manuscripts were excluded. Studies were excluded if the co-designed activity did not directly address paediatric healthcare delivery or aim to improve health outcomes for children. Eligible study designs included qualitative studies, mixed-methods studies, randomised controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental studies, cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies that used co-design approaches to develop paediatric healthcare interventions involving parents or guardians. The search strategy incorporated keywords and subject headings related to co-design, parents, guardians, paediatric healthcare, patient satisfaction, communication, and care outcomes. A specialist health librarian assisted in refining the search strategy to maximise the retrieval of relevant studies. Covidence systematic review software was used to manage study screening, de-duplication, and the selection process.

For the purposes of this review, co-design was defined as a collaborative, participatory approach to intervention development in which co-design partners—including parents or guardians—are actively involved in one or more stages of the design process [15]. This involvement may include identifying needs, generating ideas, developing solutions, and/or refining interventions.

Studies did not need to explicitly label their approach as “co-design” to be eligible; however, they were required to demonstrate characteristics consistent with co-design principles. Specifically, eligible studies had to:

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using a modified Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist (see Appendix 1), adapted for the purposes of this review to assess co-design methodology across diverse study designs. The customised tool incorporated items from multiple JBI checklists, expanded to include domains specific to co-design.

The adapted checklist was piloted on a sample of five studies (representing a range of study designs) by two independent reviewers (JS, DT) to assess clarity, applicability, and consistency. Minor wording refinements were made following the pilot to ensure criteria were relevant to all study types included in the review.

Two reviewers (JS, DT) independently applied the checklist to each included study, scoring each item as “Yes” [1] or “No/Unclear” (0). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer acting as arbiter when consensus could not be reached. For each study, an overall quality score, quality rating (High, Medium, Low), and key strengths and weaknesses were recorded [16].

Synthesis methods

Studies were selected based on their use of co-design methodologies in paediatric healthcare. A tabulated summary was created to facilitate comparisons across intervention types, participant groups, and co-design approaches, categorized into frameworks such as Experience-Based Co-Design (EBCD), Participatory Action Research (PAR), Mixed-Methods Co-Design (MMCD), and Human-Centered Design (HCD).

Given the heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures, meta-analysis was not possible, as many studies did not report comparable quantitative measures. Instead, a narrative synthesis was undertaken, with quantitative findings summarised descriptively and qualitative findings synthesised using thematic analysis.

The thematic analysis followed an inductive, interpretive approach informed by repeated readings of the included studies and guided by the review’s objectives. Rather than applying a pre-existing coding framework, themes were identified by examining recurring patterns in how co-design processes influenced outcomes across different contexts. This process was informed by established guidance on thematic synthesis in systematic reviews, particularly the approach outlined by Thomas and Harden [17], which supports the use of inductive, comparative methods to identify patterns across heterogeneous qualitative data.

During data extraction, three key dimensions were prioritised: co-design objectives, level of participant involvement, and tools and techniques used. These dimensions were then applied to stratify the findings, enabling comparative analysis of how different approaches shaped feasibility, acceptability, and implementation success. This stratification highlighted consistent thematic trends, such as the association between focused, practical goals, sustained co-design partner engagement, and the production of more actionable outcomes.

Results

Database searches yielded 12,574 records. After removing 5,983 duplicates, 6,591 titles and abstracts were screened, and 472 full-text articles were reviewed. A total of 70 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The 70 included studies were published between 2013 and 2025 and conducted in Australia (n = 13), Canada (n = 17), the United Kingdom (n = 16), the United States (n = 17), Ireland (n = 2), Denmark (n = 3), Sweden (n = 4), Spain (n = 1), Finland (n = 1), Singapore (n = 1), South Africa (n = 1), Iran (n = 1), and the Czech Republic (n = 1). Nine studies were multi country, typically collaborations between the USA, UK, Canada, and Ireland. Most studies were conducted in high-income, English-speaking countries (see Table 1, where co-design methodologies appear to be more established in health and social research. This distribution may reflect differences in research funding, infrastructure, and policy support for co-design practices. Designs included qualitative (n = 58), mixed-methods (n = 25), quantitative (n = 2), and other descriptive/exploratory designs (n = 9), with some studies spanning multiple types. Sample sizes ranged from 10 to 47,450 participants, totalling 53,676 participants across all studies. Settings included hospitals (n = 42), community-based programs (n = 26), online/virtual environments (n = 35) and mixed/hybrid (n = 20). The most frequently represented specialty areas were paediatric oncology (n = 10), cerebral palsy and neuro-disability (n = 9), mental health, anxiety and autism (n = 12), chronic or complex illness (n = 8), neonatal/NICU/prematurity (n = 8), genomics or rare diseases (n = 6), general paediatrics (n = 7), disability or rehabilitation (n = 5) or other including health literacy and communication tools (n = 5).

Co-design methodologies and techniques

All included studies employed co-design or participatory design approaches, though the specific methodologies varied (see Supplementary Table1). The most commonly reported approach was Participatory Action Research (PAR) (n = 19) [18–35], followed closely by Experience-Based Co-Design (EBCD) (n = 17) [36–51] and Human Centred Design (n = 17) [1, 52–65]. Design Thinking was less commonly reported, [66–69]while a number of studies used general co-design methodology (n = 14) without specifying a formal framework [70–83].

To better understand how co-design was operationalised across studies, we further examined the specific methods and techniques used. These were grouped into two categories to distinguish between approaches used during the co-design phase and those applied in post-design refinement and evaluation.

Engagement methods used in the initial co-design phase

Analytical approaches

To evaluate the effectiveness of interventions, studies most commonly employed thematic analysis (n = 24) [19, 20, 24, 25, 28–30, 32, 34, 36, 37, 52, 55, 58, 61, 62, 64, 65, 68, 73, 75, 79, 83], followed by content analysis (n = 7) [20, 24, 45, 48, 56, 60, 81] and qualitative analysis (n = 4) [26, 54, 79, 85]. Reflexive analysis, framework analysis and grounded theory were also used however less commonly. These methods were applied to data generated through both co-design and refinement phases to identify key themes, inform iterative changes, and guide final implementation. In many studies, analysis was embedded within cyclical processes, ensuring that feedback from each stage directly informed subsequent development.

Participants and engagement

Parents and caregivers were involved in all studies (n = 70), while children and youth participated in 62 studies [1, 18, 20–25, 27–32, 34–37, 39–50, 52, 53, 55–69, 71–74, 76–85, 89]and clinicians or allied health professionals were included in 52 [18, 20–22, 24–27, 29, 32, 35–54, 56, 57, 59–65, 67, 69, 71–73, 75–80, 82, 84, 86, 88]. Participant engagement typically spanned multiple phases of the design and evaluation process, including needs identification (n = 38), co-design workshops (n = 35), prototype development (n = 22), and iterative refinement (n = 26). The most common forms of engagement were co-design and ideation activities (n ≈ 30), followed by feedback and refinement (noted widely across the studies), and testing and evaluation. Only a small number of studies explicitly reported participant involvement in priority setting or governance structures, indicating that engagement at the earliest and final stages remains limited.

The level of participant involvement varied across studies. Most adopted a collaborative approach (n = 36), characterised by shared decision-making, co-creation, and ongoing partnership throughout the intervention lifecycle. Fewer studies (n = 8) described consultative engagement, where participants provided input or feedback during design but did not share decision-making roles.

Parents and caregivers played central roles, most commonly providing feedback on usability and content, testing prototypes, and sharing experiential insights. Frequent contributions included participating in workshops, interviews, advisory panels, or journey mapping sessions. A notable number of parents helped co-develop educational materials and intervention components, validate findings, and identify needs. A smaller group contributed to governance, acted as coaches, or shaped priorities, demonstrating opportunities for deeper involvement in leadership and decision-making roles.

Objectives of Co-designed interventions

The objectives of co-designed interventions varied across the included studies but consistently focused on enhancing care experiences and outcomes through meaningful, user-informed innovation. A predominant goal was improving healthcare services (n = 51), including efforts to optimize communication, care transitions, service coordination, and clinical processes [1, 18–27, 29, 30, 34–41, 43, 45–53, 59–67, 69, 70, 72, 75–79, 81–83, 85, 86, 88]

A smaller but notable group of studies focused on designing digital tools (n = 7), such as mobile apps, online platforms, and augmented reality interventions to support care delivery and engagement [54, 55, 57, 58, 73, 74, 80, 84].

Finally, a set of studies had other or uncategorized objectives (n = 8), often context-specific or exploratory in nature, such as understanding lived experiences or developing frameworks [28, 31, 32, 42, 44, 56, 68, 71].

Duration and frequency of Co-design activities

The duration and frequency of co-design activities varied widely across the included studies. A total of 4 studies reported short-term engagements lasting from several weeks to a few months, often focused on rapid prototyping or targeted improvements [20, 32, 59, 62]. In contrast, 8 studies described long-term involvement extending over 12 months or more, incorporating iterative development and evaluation phases [22, 32, 37, 41, 46, 50, 67, 82].

Multi-phase co-design frameworks were employed in 33 studies, incorporating structured stages such as exploration, development, and implementation [18–22, 25–27, 32, 35, 40, 41, 44, 47–49, 53, 56, 58, 59, 63, 65, 67–69, 73, 75, 76, 78, 82, 84, 86, 88]

Frequent and sustained engagement activities—such as iterative workshops, feedback sessions, and ongoing collaboration—were evident in 37 studies, demonstrating the importance of maintaining active involvement throughout the design process [18, 22, 24–26, 29, 31, 38, 42–44, 49, 51–53, 55, 56, 58, 59, 61–64, 66–69, 72–74, 76, 78, 82, 84, 86, 88, 89]

Outcome measures

Across the included studies, outcomes were diverse but most commonly focused on patient and caregiver experiences, usability, and the practical impact of co-designed interventions. For the purposes of synthesis, outcomes were grouped according to the primary phase of the co-design process they addressed: exploration/ideation, development/refinement, and implementation/evaluation. Where studies reported outcomes across multiple phases, each measure was categorised according to the phase for which it was primarily reported.

Exploration/Ideation phase

In the earliest phase, outcome measures typically focused on identifying needs, setting priorities, and assessing the quality of partner engagement. Common methods included qualitative feedback gathered through focus groups, interviews, and surveys to determine whether co-design partner perspectives were adequately captured and whether engagement processes were perceived as inclusive, relevant, and culturally safe [19, 23, 28, 45, 70].

Development/Refinement phase

In this phase, the emphasis shifted to assessing usability, acceptability, feasibility, and content relevance of prototypes or draft interventions. The most frequently used methods were qualitative and mixed-methods approaches. Thematic analysis featured prominently (n = 17) [24, 25, 29, 44, 49, 50, 52, 55, 58, 59, 62, 73, 80, 83–85] and was often combined with qualitative feedback, in-depth interviews, and focus groups (n = 10) [21, 42, 46, 48, 53, 56, 72, 76, 78, 81]. Surveys and questionnaires were also used to collect quantitative data on usability, comprehension, and satisfaction (n = 7) [18, 21, 23, 32, 51, 69, 77].

Implementation/Evaluation phase

Outcome measures in this phase focused on the real-world effectiveness and impact of co-designed interventions. Many studies applied content or thematic analysis methods [24, 25, 31, 37, 39, 41, 44, 47, 48, 58]to evaluate changes in knowledge, behaviour, or service use. Focus groups [25, 37, 44, 48, 82] and participant feedback sessions [24, 38, 47] provided further insight into how interventions worked in practice, while a few studies combined observational methods [41, 47] and communication audits to examine improvements in information provision and family-provider dialogue.

Across all phases, qualitative approaches were frequently used to provide contextual depth, while quantitative tools complemented these findings by capturing standardised measures of usability, comprehension, and effectiveness. This phased synthesis approach highlights how outcome measures evolve alongside the co-design process, reflecting the differing aims and evaluation needs from initial ideation through to full-scale implementation.

Outcome findings

Overall, co-designed interventions were well received across the studies, with many reporting high levels of user satisfaction, engagement, and empowerment (n = 49) [1, 18, 20, 22, 24, 25, 28–32, 35–41, 44, 47–49, 51, 52, 54, 56–58, 61–66, 69–71, 73–79, 82–86, 88] (See Tables 2 and 3). Participants frequently described the interventions as valuable, trustworthy, and aligned with their needs, contributing to increased confidence and willingness to use the tools in practice.

Acceptability and feasibility were commonly assessed, with findings indicating that co-designed tools and processes were generally considered appropriate and practical for implementation (n = 47) [1, 18–27, 30, 32, 36, 39, 40, 43, 44, 46–48, 51–53, 55, 56, 58, 60–62, 65, 67, 70, 71, 73, 75–80, 82–86, 88]. Usability and user-friendliness were consistently highlighted, reinforcing the relevance of these interventions in real-world settings.

Communication outcomes were a major focus of many studies (n = 53), with co-design processes frequently leading to improved understanding, more tailored and accessible resources, and enhanced dialogue between families and healthcare providers [18–23, 25, 26, 28, 29, 31, 35, 36, 38–44, 46, 47, 49–53, 55–59, 61–69, 72–74, 76, 78, 79, 81–83, 85, 86, 88].

These studies consistently reported that co-designed interventions helped families better understand complex medical information, such as genomic test results, discharge instructions, and risk assessments. Tools such as plain-language reports [72], interactive apps [58, 62, 72], and tailored educational resources [66, 76] were found to improve comprehension, recall, and confidence in managing care.

Many interventions also led to the creation of materials that were described as being more culturally relevant and emotionally supportive, particularly for Indigenous families [18, 29] and families navigating sensitive topics like child death review [43]. These resources were described as trustworthy, inclusive, and empowering, often incorporating visual aids, storytelling, and peer perspectives to enhance relatability and reduce distress.

In several cases, co-design improved communication between families and clinicians, fostering shared decision-making and mutual understanding [41, 44, 51, 63]. Structured tools and feedback loops helped clarify roles, expectations, and care pathways, while also addressing systemic barriers such as inconsistent messaging and lack of follow-up [39, 40, 86].

Digital health tools were especially strong on usability and engagement (n = 18), particularly those designed for symptom tracking, self-management, or health education [1, 22, 25, 29, 32, 44, 48, 54, 57, 58, 61, 62, 73, 78, 80, 81, 83, 84, 88]. These studies often found that digital co-designed interventions were easy for families to use and integrated well into daily routines.

System-level impacts were demonstrated in a smaller number of studies (n = 6), where co-design contributed to improved workflow integration, service delivery, and organizational change [27, 38, 41, 44, 50, 82]. These included clearer role definitions, smoother care pathways, and enhanced team communication, showing how co-design can influence broader systems beyond individual tools.

Finally, collaborative engagement emerged as a key factor for success (n = 12), with stronger outcomes reported when families and co-design partners were actively involved throughout planning, design, and testing [18, 20, 21, 23, 25, 30, 32, 38, 41–43, 47, 80]. These studies emphasized the value of inclusive, participatory approaches in developing interventions that are responsive to user needs.

Stratified findings

Outcomes by co-design objectives

Studies with clearly defined and purposeful co-design objectives consistently demonstrated stronger alignment with user needs, resulting in interventions that were more usable, acceptable, and contextually relevant. For example, the neurodiversity-affirmative adaptation of an anxiety intervention for autistic children [42] excluded unsupported components and integrated lived experience perspectives, enhancing relevance and acceptability. Transition support tools for youth with spinal cord injuries [20, 21] were described as empowering and comprehensive, reflecting the priorities of both young people and caregivers.

Culturally tailored resources also benefited from focused objectives. The development of an Indigenous breastfeeding resource [18] incorporated traditional knowledge and community input, resulting in a tool that was well received and considered culturally safe. Similarly, the Growin’ Up Healthy Jarjums intervention [29] emphasized cultural safety, family-centeredness, and simplicity, leading to high acceptability among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander mothers. Social prescribing interventions for families of children with cerebral palsy [48] addressed systemic inequities and were valued for their responsiveness to local needs.

Other examples include the development of a genomic results booklet [76], a paediatric pain risk communication tool [65], and a hospital information system for children [52], all of which were shaped by specific goals to improve understanding, reduce anxiety, and support decision-making. These findings illustrate that co-design objectives emphasizing inclusivity, cultural relevance, and system-level change can significantly enhance the practical utility and emotional resonance of health interventions.

Outcomes by level of participant involvement

The depth and continuity of participant involvement emerged as a critical factor influencing the quality and impact of co-designed interventions. Studies that engaged participants as active and collaborative partners throughout all phases of the design process consistently reported greater satisfaction, empowerment, and usability. For instance, families involved in the development of the HOPE video resource [24] and rehabilitation tools [47] described feeling valued and empowered, while their input directly shaped the final outputs. Similarly, iterative engagement with parents and clinicians in the development of a virtual mental health platform [25] led to improved accessibility and relevance.

High levels of involvement were also associated with successful implementation and uptake. In studies such as those by Gill [44], Wolfe [82], and Brett [72], family engagement contributed to smoother integration into clinical workflows and increased comfort with care processes. In contrast, studies with limited or consultative involvement, such as Dwyer [66], noted missed opportunities for refinement and lower engagement, despite reporting positive usability outcomes. These findings suggest that embedding participants as equal partners—not merely informants—results in more responsive, sustainable, and impactful interventions.

Outcomes by tools and techniques used

The techniques employed during co-design played a significant role in shaping both the technical quality and emotional impact of the resulting interventions. Iterative prototyping and usability testing, including think-aloud sessions and standardized usability scales, were strongly linked to high engagement and user satisfaction [53, 58, 73]. These methods allowed for rapid identification and resolution of usability issues, ensuring that tools were intuitive and aligned with user expectations.

Workshops and advisory groups facilitated consensus-building and emotional validation, particularly in sensitive contexts such as child death review [43] and youth mental health services [46]. Digital tools and simulations expanded participation and enabled real-time feedback, as seen in studies by Wong [83], Ogourtsova [78], and Xu [85]. Creative approaches—such as storytelling, visual mapping, and gamification—were especially effective in enhancing emotional connection and motivation [24, 35, 69].

Other studies used structured frameworks such as EBCD (Experience-Based Co-Design), design thinking, and participatory action research to guide the process [21, 31, 36]. These techniques supported multimodal engagement and flexibility, allowing interventions to be tailored to diverse user needs and preferences.

Barriers and enablers to Co-design

Across the included studies, several recurring barriers and enablers influenced the implementation and success of co-design processes in paediatric healthcare.

Barriers to co-design were diverse and often context-specific. Time and resource constraints were frequently cited, limiting the depth and continuity of stakeholder engagement [18, 36, 37, 52]. Institutional challenges such as staff turnover, scheduling conflicts, and limited availability of participants were common, as were logistical issues including recruitment delays and lack of diversity in participant samples [19, 53, 70, 71]. Underrepresentation of culturally and linguistically diverse families, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, and children with disabilities was noted as a persistent equity issue [23, 24, 29, 42]. In digital contexts, barriers included limited internet access, device compatibility, and digital literacy, which affected engagement and usability [26, 78, 83, 84]. Ethical complexities, especially when involving children, and challenges in maintaining sustained engagement over time were also highlighted [45, 46, 56, 76]. Other barriers included unclear role definitions, resistance to change, and lack of integration with existing clinical workflows [39, 40, 64, 65].

Enablers of successful co-design were consistently associated with inclusive, iterative, and well-supported processes. Early and sustained engagement of stakeholders—including children, parents, clinicians, and community representatives—was a key factor in ensuring relevance and ownership [18, 36, 37, 52]. Studies emphasised the value of iterative feedback loops, flexible participation formats (e.g., online and asynchronous options), and the use of creative, age-appropriate, and culturally responsive methods to facilitate meaningful involvement [19, 53, 70, 71]. Supportive organisational cultures, leadership buy-in, and integration of co-design into existing clinical workflows were critical for embedding participatory practices into routine care [23, 24, 26, 84]. The presence of dedicated facilitators, use of visual and interactive tools, and recognition of lived experience as expertise further enhanced the quality and relevance of co-design processes [43–45, 75]. Additional enablers included trauma-informed approaches, peer support, and the use of structured frameworks such as EBCD, COM-B, and APEASE to guide design and implementation [31, 47, 51, 57].

Quality appraisal of included studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using a tailored JBI critical appraisal tool (see Appendix 1). Of the 70 studies reviewed, 59 were rated as high quality [1, 18–27, 29–32, 34–39, 41–44, 46–55, 57–63, 63–75, 77, 79, 80, 82, 84, 86] and 11 were rated as medium quality [28, 40, 45, 56, 62, 76, 78, 81, 83, 85, 88] (see Table 4). This distribution reflects a strong overall standard in the application of co-design methodologies within paediatric healthcare contexts.

The studies encompassed a range of design approaches, though the majority were exploratory and participatory in nature. Specifically, 68 studies were identified as participatory or co-design studies, often referencing frameworks such as Experience-Based Co-Design (EBCD), Participatory Action Research (PAR), or general co-design approaches. Two studies employed user-centred or human-centered design principles. No studies were explicitly classified as randomized controlled trials, non-randomized trials, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, or mixed-methods designs based on the available titles and descriptions. This suggests a predominant focus on collaborative, qualitative, and design-led methodologies rather than traditional experimental or observational formats.

High-quality studies consistently demonstrated strong alignment with co-design principles, including clear methodological articulation, systematic data collection, relevant outcomes, and robust stakeholder engagement. A key strength observed across high-quality studies was the clarity and structure of co-design methodology. For instance, Castensøe-Seidenfaden [22] and Hackett [46] provided detailed frameworks that guided their participatory processes, while Ali [36] and Aufegger [52] described iterative design cycles involving end-users. Systematic data collection was another common strength, evident in studies such as Campbell-Yeo [38] and Jibb [57, 58], which used structured methods to gather feedback and evaluate usability. Many studies also reported outcomes directly relevant to paediatric healthcare, such as Bradford [53], which developed a remote symptom management app for paediatric oncology, and Murphy [77], which co-designed educational resources for gastrostomy care.

Participant involvement was robust in most high-quality studies. Abbass-Dick [18] and Perkes [29] exemplified culturally responsive engagement with Indigenous families, while Garstang [43] and Scott [31] involved parents and clinicians in shaping interventions for paediatric palliative care and child death review. A smaller subset of studies, such as Bracken [19] and Massey [47], demonstrated comprehensive reporting, offering detailed documentation of design processes, stakeholder roles, and implementation steps.

Despite these strengths, several recurring weaknesses were noted. A significant number of studies lacked discussion of challenges encountered during the co-design process. For example, Biddiss [71] and Banerjee [70] did not reflect on barriers or tensions that may have influenced design decisions. Scalability and sustainability were also underreported; studies by Bray [20], Drake [24], and Reitzel [49] offered limited insight into how their interventions could be maintained or expanded beyond pilot phases. Ethical considerations, including approval processes and participant protections, were also inconsistently addressed. Hackett [46] and Aufegger [52], for instance, did not specify ethical review procedures. Additionally, validity and reliability measures were often absent, particularly in qualitative designs. Studies such as Dosso [74] and Costa [41] did not describe how data quality or methodological rigor was ensured.

Medium-quality studies shared many of the strengths of high-quality ones but often lacked depth in reporting or methodological clarity. For example, Gustavsson [45] and Strand [88] described their co-design approaches and reported relevant outcomes, but provided limited detail on recruitment strategies or participant diversity. Chudleigh [40] and Handra [76] did not include information regarding ethical approval or data analysis methods. Others, like Ogourtsova [78] and Wong [83], failed to reflect on implementation challenges or barriers to engagement, which are critical for informing future practice.

In summary, while the majority of studies demonstrated thoughtful methodological execution and strong engagement with co-design principles, future research would benefit from more consistent reporting on ethical processes, scalability, and methodological rigor. Greater attention to cultural responsiveness and recruitment diversity would also enhance the inclusivity and impact of co-designed interventions in paediatric healthcare.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesised findings from 70 studies exploring the application of co-design methodologies in paediatric healthcare involving parents or caregivers. The evidence supports co-design as a valuable and adaptable approach for developing interventions that are relevant, acceptable, and feasible across a range of clinical settings. These findings are consistent with reviews in adult and general healthcare contexts, which have shown that co-design enhances the relevance and uptake of interventions by aligning them with user priorities and lived experiences [7]. In paediatric contexts, our results mirror prior research demonstrating that participatory approaches can improve family engagement, health literacy, and adherence to care plans [11, 90].

Across the included studies, co-design was most effective when projects had focused, practical objectives and sustained engagement with families and clinicians. This echoes previous implementation studies showing that narrowly defined goals, coupled with iterative stakeholder involvement, are more likely to lead to implementable and sustainable solutions [91, 92]. Interventions developed through multi-phase collaboration—such as those by Campbell-Yeo [38], Jibb [57], and Bradford [53]—were more likely to be usable, acceptable, and culturally appropriate. In contrast, studies relying on single-point consultation or limited engagement, such as Costa [41] and Dosso [74], were less likely to result in meaningful or lasting change. This reinforces that co-design is not a one-size-fits-all method, but one that delivers the greatest value when applied with intentionality, inclusivity, and methodological rigour—especially in paediatric healthcare, where alignment with the lived experiences of children, families, and clinicians is essential for sustainable impact.

Co-design processes were particularly effective for addressing challenges related to communication, care coordination, health education, and digital engagement. Studies such as Walter [51], Reitzel [49], and Ali [36] demonstrated how iterative prototyping, feedback loops, and usability testing with parents and youth led to interventions that were more trustworthy, user-friendly, and contextually appropriate. This is consistent with human-centred design literature, which emphasises that usability testing during development improves both user satisfaction and long-term adoption [93, 94]. Digital tools co-designed with children and adolescents—particularly those managing chronic or complex conditions—showed strong usability, engagement, and self-efficacy outcomes, as seen in Biddiss [71], Hannon [55], Vänskä [35], and Gill [44].

Parents frequently reported increased confidence and satisfaction when actively involved in co-design, noting that interventions better reflected their family’s context and priorities [45, 48]. Clinicians also described improved understanding of patient experiences and felt more confident using co-designed education and communication tools [49, 78]. These findings match the results of co-design projects in adult chronic care, where provider–patient collaboration improved both relational trust and clinical decision-making [95].

A clear relationship emerged between study quality and the depth of co-design application. Of the 70 studies, 59 were rated as high quality, and 11 were rated as medium quality. High-quality studies were more likely to demonstrate meaningful collaboration and power-sharing with families and clinicians, often using established frameworks such as Participatory Action Research (PAR), Experience-Based Co-Design (EBCD), and Human-Centred Design (HCD). These studies also integrated robust user-centred techniques—including simulation, role play, and scenario mapping—to test feasibility and usability in real-world contexts. In contrast, medium-quality studies often relied on consultative or one-off methods, which limited participant influence on final design decisions and reduced the likelihood of sustained impact.

Common barriers to effective co-design included time and resource constraints, institutional challenges, and the complexities of engaging underrepresented populations. These findings are consistent with broader literature on participatory health research, which highlights how limited organisational capacity, rigid governance processes, and inequities in access can undermine sustained engagement [96, 97]. Addressing these barriers requires not only project-level solutions—such as flexible timelines, hybrid participation models, and targeted digital access support—but also system-level commitments to resource allocation and cultural change within organisations.

The enablers identified in this review align with previous research demonstrating the importance of early, sustained stakeholder involvement, trust-based relationships, and creative, culturally responsive engagement [94, 95]. Their consistent association with positive co-design outcomes reinforces the need to embed participatory principles into institutional processes, rather than treating them as optional or project-specific activities. Leveraging these enablers can help ensure that co-design initiatives are more inclusive, contextually relevant, and capable of delivering meaningful, sustainable improvements in paediatric healthcare.

Methodologically, the review highlighted inconsistent outcome measurement and variable reporting of impact. While short-term outcomes like acceptability and usability were commonly assessed, few studies evaluated longer-term effectiveness, sustainability, or cost-effectiveness. This was particularly evident in studies involving complex or underrepresented groups, where small sample sizes and limited follow-up were common. These limitations reflect broader challenges in co-design research noted by Palmer et al. [97] and Greenhalgh et al. [96], who advocate for stronger longitudinal designs and standardised outcome measures to capture sustained impact.

Recommendations for future research

To strengthen the impact, equity, and sustainability of future co-design efforts in paediatric healthcare, several strategic priorities should be considered. First, there is a critical need for the proactive inclusion of underrepresented groups, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander (ATSI) families, culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) communities, and children living with disability. Ensuring that these populations are meaningfully engaged throughout the design process will enhance the relevance and inclusivity of resulting interventions. Second, future studies should adopt long-term evaluation frameworks to assess real-world implementation, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness, moving beyond short-term usability metrics. Third, the development and consistent application of standardised outcome measures tailored to co-design in healthcare would enable more robust comparisons across studies and support evidence-based scaling. Economic evaluations should also be incorporated to better understand the resource requirements and potential return on investment of co-designed interventions. In addition, scalability assessments—particularly for digital and hybrid models—are essential to ensure that solutions can be adapted for rural and resource-constrained settings. Finally, co-design efforts must integrate patient-important clinical outcomes into both the design and evaluation phases. This ensures that interventions address not only acceptability and usability, but also tangible health outcomes that matter to children, families, and clinicians. By embedding rigorous, inclusive, and well-documented co-design practices, future paediatric interventions can be better positioned to deliver meaningful, equitable, and lasting improvements in care.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates the growing adoption and value of co-design in paediatric healthcare, with interventions consistently improving relevance, acceptability, usability, and satisfaction for children, families, and healthcare professionals. When applied with rigour and inclusivity, co-design produces solutions that reflect lived experiences and practical needs, supporting more effective and equitable care.

Tokenistic engagement limits sustainability and uptake. Future research should prioritise transparent reporting, inclusion of underrepresented families, integration of patient-important clinical outcomes, and long-term evaluation of impact, cost-effectiveness, and scalability. Co-design remains a practical, adaptable approach for developing child- and family-centred health innovations in diverse settings.
