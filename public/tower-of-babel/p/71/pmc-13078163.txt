Short versus long corticosteroid therapy for immune checkpoint inhibitors–induced acute tubulointerstitial nephritis
Authors: Pilar Auñón, Vanessa Lopes, Irene Minguez, Pablo Pérez-Romero, Katherine Peña-Ordónez, Mara Serrano-Soto, Mariana Rivera, Fabiola Alonso, Verónica R Mercado, Candela Moliz, Virginia Cabello, Enrique Morales, Eduardo Gutiérrez, Teresa Cavero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundImmune-mediated acute tubulointerstitial nephritis (ICI-AIN) is the most common form of immune-mediated renal toxicity induced by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). It has an excellent response to corticosteroids, but there is no consensus on the most suitable steroid regimen.MethodsA multicentre retrospective study including 62 patients diagnosed with ICI-AIN treated with corticosteroids was conducted. Patients were categorized into two groups based on the duration of corticosteroid treatment: ‘short course of steroids (SCS)’ (<12 weeks) and ‘long course of steroids (LCS)’ (≥12 weeks). The study aims to compare the efficacy and toxicity of these two regimens.ResultsTwenty-eight patients received SCS and 34 received LCS. Renal recovery was achieved in 89% of patients, with no differences between groups (P = 1). Serum creatinine levels were also similar (1.2 mg/dL, P = .71) at the end of follow-up. ICI rechallenge was attempted in 18 patients, and ICI-AIN recurred in 5 (28%). Among these, four recurrences occurred in LCS patients, 67% still receiving ≥10 mg of prednisone. Steroid-related adverse events per patient-month were lower in the SCS group (0.027 vs 0.072, 95% confidence interval 0.20–0.68; P = .001). At 12 and 18 months, progression-free survival and overall survival were significantly better in the SCS group, even after adjusting for confounders.ConclusionsSCS is an effective and safe approach for the management of ICI-AIN. It offers a comparable rate of renal recovery, fewer adverse events and possibly more favourable oncological outcomes, compared with a prolonged regimen. Future prospective studies are warranted.Keywords: check-point inhibitors, corticosteroids, immune-mediated acute tubulointerstitial nephritis, immune-related adverse events, onconephrology

INTRODUCTION

The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has represented a revolution in the field of oncology. The use of these drugs has expanded to many tumours, becoming a first- or second-line option for patients with advanced or metastatic neoplastic disease and achieving substantial improvement in the survival of this population [1]. Despite their proven efficacy and good tolerance, these treatments are not exempt from toxicities. Due to their mechanism of action, by increasing the activity of the immune system to fight tumours, they can produce inflammatory adverse events, called ‘immune-mediated’ (irAE). Any organ can be affected, with skin involvement, endocrinopathies, liver and gastrointestinal involvement being more frequent [2].

The most common form of renal irAE is immune-mediated acute tubulointerstitial nephritis (ICI-AIN), which is histologically characterized by an interstitial inflammatory infiltrate and accounts for approximately 90% of renal toxicity cases [3–6].

Treatment includes stopping ICI (at least temporarily) together with corticosteroids [7, 8]. With this strategy, response rates close to 90% are achieved [9–11]. In steroid-dependent or non-responder cases, mycophenolate mofetil or anti–tumour necrosis factor have been employed, with varying success [11–13]. However, despite being the primary treatment with a very favourable response rate, the most optimal steroid regimen has not yet been established, especially in terms of duration, with considerable heterogeneity between the schemes employed across different centres. The American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines for the management of immune-mediated adverse effects recommend initiating steroid treatment with doses ranging from 0.5 to 2 mg/kg/day, depending on the degree of renal function impairment; once serum creatinine (sCr) fall below 1.5 times the baseline, it is advised to continue with a tapering regimen for another 4–6 weeks [8]. Furthermore, if we extrapolate the existing evidence on immunoallergic tubulointerstitial nephritis steroid treatment, regimens with a duration of <8 weeks could be sufficient [14].

On the other hand, some authors advocate for longer steroid regimens based on the pharmacokinetic properties of ICIs [15]. The clearance of these drugs, primarily through lysosomal proteolytic degradation and partial recycling from endosomes to circulation, results in a prolonged half-life of between 6 and 27 days [16–32]. Additionally, the harmful effects of corticosteroids are well documented and are mostly dose-dependent. The use of high-dose steroids has been linked to an increased risk of severe infections in patients with melanoma, renal, urothelial and lung cancer treated with immunotherapy [33, 34].

Complete renal recovery after immune-mediated damage is critical, often determining the reintroduction of effective treatments such as ICIs, or future antineoplastic therapeutic options. Non-recovery after immune-mediated renal damage has been shown to be an independent predictor of mortality [5]. Therefore, it is imperative to identify the most appropriate steroid regimen that allows for achieving an optimal renal response in patients with ICI-AIN while minimizing the risk of adverse side effects. This study aims to analyse the efficacy and toxicity of long vs short course of corticosteroids as remission induction therapy in ICI-AIN.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

We included 62 patients with a diagnosis of ICI-AIN who received corticosteroids as remission induction therapy identified in seven nephrology departments in Spain from 2017 to 2022. Patients were categorized into two groups based on the total duration of steroid therapy: ‘short course of steroids (SCS)’ (<12 weeks) and ‘long course of steroids (LCS)’ (≥12 weeks). Treatment duration was determined by centre-specific protocols.

This study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board statement was waived given this study involved retrospective review of patient charts. The information was recorded by the investigator in a way that the identity of the human subjects could not be ascertained directly or through identifiers linked to the subjects, the investigator did not contact the subjects and the investigator will not re-identify subjects.

Follow-up and data collection

Baseline and follow-up data were retrospectively collected from medical records following a uniform protocol, including demographics, clinical and analytical variables. Information on comorbidities, concomitant treatment with drugs related to acute interstitial nephritis, cancer type, ICI modality and other irAEs was compiled, along with laboratory and histological data from the acute kidney injury (AKI) episodes. We also recorded information about steroid treatment, including duration, dose, use of intravenous pulses, use of other immunosuppressants, ICI rechallenge and ICI-AIN recurrence.

Definitions and selection criteria

ICI-AIN was considered in acute renal failure after ruling out any other cause of acute renal failure (clinical ICI-AIN) or after having a confirmatory biopsy (histological ICI-AIN).

Baseline sCr was established as the last stable sCr value before ICI initiation. AKI was defined as an absolute increase in sCr of ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 h, or a relative increase in sCr of ≥50% within 7 days [35, 36].

Complete response was defined as a recovery of at least 75% of baseline sCr. Partial response was defined as a decrease of 25%–75% in baseline sCr. No response was defined by a sCr value that was higher than at least 75% of the baseline or need for chronic dialysis. The response was assessed after at least 3 months of corticosteroids treatment.

Relapse was defined as an increase in sCr >0.3 mg/dL attributed to ICI-AIN, occurring after reducing or stopping corticosteroids in patients who had previously achieved a partial or complete response.

End-stage kidney disease (ESRD) was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <15 mL/min/1.73 m2 according to Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation, or need for chronic dialysis.

Patients with ESKD or those who had received a renal transplant, patients with other types of histological involvement related to ICI (such as glomerular involvement), and individuals with <3 months of follow-up after starting steroid treatment were excluded.

Aims and outcomes

Primary outcome was remission rate (complete and partial). Secondary outcomes included cumulative dose of corticosteroids, need for other immunosuppressive therapy, renal function (sCr and eGFR) at 3 months and last follow-up, ICI-AIN relapse rates after ICI rechallenge, cancer progression-free and overall survival, and adverse events related to corticosteroids. Adverse events were defined as infections, steroid-induced diabetes mellitus, worsening of known hypertension, fragility fractures, myopathy, gastrointestinal complications and psychosis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are shown as absolute and relative frequencies (percentage). Two-sample comparisons were performed with the use of the χ2 test or the non-parametric Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used for continuous variables, as appropriate. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to assess the time to reach ESKD, and differences between groups (SCS vs LCS) were compared with use of the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyse risk factors associated with renal response, using a backward stepwise process. The selection of covariates in multivariable models was based on univariate associations and biological plausibility. Poisson regression has been used to compare the event rate between the two treatment groups. All P-values were two-tailed and a value <.05 was considered as criteria for statistical significance. Analyses and plots were performed using STATA version 12.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Outcomes: steroid treatment

Patients included in the SCS group were treated with corticosteroids for a median duration of 8 weeks, compared with a median duration of 25 weeks in the LCS group (Fig. 1). This led to a cumulative prednisone dose that was more than twice as high in the LCS group (4030 vs 1654 mg in the SCS patients, P < .0001). On the other hand, intravenous pulse of steroids was administered to half of the SCS patients, compared with only 15% in the LSC group (P = .002).

Corticosteroid therapy.

Additional immunosuppressant therapy was needed in four patients, all of whom received the LSC regimen. One patient needed it due to steroid intolerance, while the other three were dependent on corticosteroids. None of these patients had histologically confirmed ICI-AIN.

In all but 3 patients the ICI was discontinued, and in 33 (57%) PPIs or other acute tubulointerstitial nephritis-related drugs were stopped with no differences between both groups.

Outcomes: remission rate

Renal recovery was achieved in 89% of patients after steroid treatment. There were no significant differences in terms of complete (35.7% in the SCS group vs 38.2% in the LCS group, P = 1) or partial response (53.6% in the SCS group vs 50% in the LSC group, P = .8) between both groups (Table 4).

Outcomes: rechallenge

ICI rechallenge was attempted in 18 out of 64 patients (29%), 9 from the SCS group (32%) and 9 from the LCS group (27%) (Table 5). Most patients (89%) in the SCS group had completed steroid treatment at the time of rechallenge compared with 33% in the LSC group (P = .05). Outcomes: progression-free and overall survival

Progression-free survival was significantly better in the SCS group compared with the LCS group: 77% (95% CI 57–89) vs 46% (95% CI 28–63) at 12-month, respectively, and 73% (95% CI 51–86) vs 39% (95% CI 22–57) at 18 months (P = .029) (Fig. 3A).

(A) Progression-free survival. (B) Overall survival.

The 12-month overall survival rate was 89% (95% CI 70–96) in SCS group vs 51% (95% CI 31–68) in LCS group, and the 18-month overall survival rate was 84% (95% CI 62–94) vs 43% (95% CI 24–61) (P = .045) (Fig. 3B). During the follow-up period, 23 (37%) patients died: 6 (21%) in SCS group and 17 (50%) in LCS group (P = .019). Most of these deaths were due to oncologic causes.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluates the efficacy and safety of short- vs long-course corticosteroid treatment in patients with ICI-AIN. Our findings prove that SCS, with a median of 8 weeks' duration, provides equivalent renal recovery while significantly reducing corticosteroid-related toxicity. Also, SCS was associated with improved oncologic outcomes, including progression-free and overall survival.

In our cohort, steroid treatment led to renal recovery in nearly 90% of patients, consistent with previous research findings [9–11]. Comparable rates of complete and partial remission between the SCS and LCS groups were observed, hence demonstrating the non-inferiority of short-course corticosteroid therapy in achieving renal response. Importantly, serum creatinine levels improved rapidly over the first 2 weeks and then stabilized similarly in both cohorts over time. This finding highlights that the therapeutic benefit from corticosteroids in ICI-AIN may be achieved early, potentially reducing the need for prolonged exposure.

There is limited research exploring the impact of corticosteroid duration on kidney outcomes after ICI-AIN. Lee et al. [37] evaluated 13 patients with ICI-AIN who were treated with a rapid corticosteroid taper (reduction to 10 mg/day within 3 weeks) and compared them with patients receiving standard of care (taper to 10 mg/day within 6 weeks). The study found no significant difference in time to renal recovery and observed higher rates of recovery by 30 and 60 days in the rapid taper group. Gupta et al. [38] performed a subanalysis of a multicentre study comparing 56 patients with ICI-AIN on a short steroid taper (to 10 mg/day in ≤21 days) with 109 patients on a longer regimen, finding no increased ICI-AIN recurrence with shorter treatment. However, these studies only assessed time on doses above 10 mg/day prednisone equivalent, not total steroid duration [37, 38]. Unlike the Lee and Gupta studies, we considered treatment duration as the time until complete discontinuation of corticosteroids. We think that our approach provides a more comprehensive assessment of therapy exposure and potential side effects; prolonged exposure to corticosteroids, even at low doses, can increase the risk of adverse events.

It is also worth noting the higher frequency of intravenous corticosteroid use in the SCS group, which could have contributed to the similar renal response rate seen in this group compared with the LCS regimen, despite a lower accumulated corticosteroid dose. In this regard, a study by Manohar et al. found that patients who achieved a complete renal response received higher initial doses of steroids compared with those who only achieved a partial response [39].

On the other hand, SCS showed advantage in terms of safety. Patients in the LCS group received more than twice the cumulative corticosteroid dose compared with those in the SCS group. This greater exposure translated into a significantly higher incidence of adverse events, particularly infections and worsening metabolic comorbidities. Notably, the rate of steroid-related adverse events per patient-month was more than double in the LCS group. These findings reinforce the known risks of prolonged corticosteroid exposure, especially in oncologic populations already at risk for immunosuppression-related morbidity [33, 34].

Rechallenge was attempted in approximately one-third of patients and recurrence of ICI-AIN occurred in 28%, a percentage in accordance with what has been previously described by other groups [5, 40, 41]. Most of the recurrences were among patients who had received a long-course regimen. Interestingly, the majority of SCS patients had completed steroid therapy at the time of rechallenge, while most LCS patients were still receiving ≥10 mg/day of prednisone. This observation suggests that prolonged corticosteroid therapy does not prevent recurrence and its use as secondary prophylaxis should not be recommended. In any case, all patients with recurrence responded to retreatment with corticosteroids, supporting the feasibility of ICI rechallenge in selected patients.

The effect of systemic steroids on immunotherapy efficacy remains somehow controversial. Several studies have shown that high-dose corticosteroids can negatively affect ICI efficacy [42, 43]. Faje et al. reported that among melanoma patients who experienced ipilimumab-induced hypophysitis, those treated with higher doses of glucocorticoids had reduced survival [43]. In this regard, our data show a clear survival benefit for the SCS group. At both 12 and 18 months, progression-free (82% vs 39% and 73% vs 43%, respectively) and overall survivals (89% vs 51% and 84% vs 43%, respectively) were notably higher compared with the LCS group (P < .05). These differences remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders in the multivariate analysis. While the exact underlying mechanisms are not fully elucidated, one hypothesis is that prolonged systemic immunosuppression may impair the anti-tumour activity of ICIs, particularly when administered early with immunoteraphy. Steroids impair interleukin-2-mediated effector T-cell activation, increase regulatory T cells, and impact tumour antigens release, lymphocyte trafficking and immune-mediated tumour killing [42]. In contrast, the SCS strategy could help preserve immune surveillance, and consequently, better tumour control. However, our results should be interpreted cautiously due to the observational nature of the data. Prolonged corticosteroid therapy may simply indicate a subgroup with more severe toxicity or poorer health, which could independently lead to worse outcomes even after statistical adjustment.

The present study has various strengths. It addresses a clinically relevant and little-studied question by directly comparing short- vs long-course corticosteroid therapy in patients with ICI-AIN. The use of well-defined cohorts with similar baseline characteristics and AKI severity enhances the internal validity of our findings. Furthermore, our data suggest that a shorter duration of immunosuppression could preserve immune function and enhance oncologic outcomes, supported by the better progression-free and overall survival in the SCS group. These results provide a valuable contribution to the limited literature on optimizing immunosuppressive strategies of renal irAEs management. Finally, it explores the ICI rechallenge after ICI-AIN, a situation that is becoming increasingly common, and the impact of steroid therapy on its outcomes.

However, our study is subject to several important limitations. The retrospective nature of the design inherently restricts causal inference and, together with the modest sample size—particularly in the rechallenge and recurrence subgroups—limits the statistical power and generalizability of the results. In addition, histological confirmation was available in only 36% of cases, similar to other ICI-AIN studies [37–39] and reflecting real-world clinical practice, but substantially reducing diagnostic certainty and increasing the risk of disease misclassification. Furthermore, steroid regimens were not randomized, introducing the possibility of treatmentk selection bias.

In summary, our data suggest that a short course of corticosteroids is effective and safe for the management of ICI-AIN, with a comparable rate of renal recovery, fewer adverse events and potentially more favourable oncological outcomes compared with a prolonged regimen. Future prospective studies are needed to confirm these results and to define the best corticosteroids duration and tapering strategy for patients with ICI-AIN.
