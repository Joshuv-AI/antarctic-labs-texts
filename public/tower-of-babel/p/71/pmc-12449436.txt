Enhancing ATMO-Street model accuracy through emission source analysis using a dense sensor network: a Warsaw case study
Authors: Anahita Sattari, Hans Hooyberghs, Stijn Janssen, Aleksander Norowski, Lisa Blyth, Iwo Augustynski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban air quality models are essential for managing particulate matter (PM) pollution, yet their accuracy is often limited by sparse monitoring networks and outdated emission inventories. This study presents a scalable framework for improving PM10 and PM2.5 modelling through the use of high-resolution emissions inventories and enhanced validation based on calibrated low-cost sensor networks. Using Warsaw city in Poland as a representative case study, we demonstrate that incorporating high-resolution residential heating emissions from the Central Register of Emissions from Buildings (CEEB) and calibrating road dust resuspension parameters led to concentration reductions of up to 20% in urban hotspots and reduced the prediction bias for PM2.5 by 57% at key locations. Notably, the Revised scenario resolved substantial overestimations in districts where incorrect fuel classifications had previously caused overestimations. However, persistent winter overestimations and the inability to fully capture extreme PM10 peaks in dry months highlight ongoing challenges, particularly in modelling resuspension dynamics under dry conditions. Our findings reveal that low-cost sensors, when rigorously calibrated, can extend spatial coverage and improve model validation, though they may underestimate extreme pollution events. The methodological advances presented here are broadly applicable to cities worldwide, particularly those facing similar challenges of diverse emission sources and limited regulatory monitoring. This integrated approach supports more accurate forecasting and targeted mitigation strategies, offering a scalable solution for urban environments seeking to achieve international air quality standards.Keywords: Residential emissions, Resuspension, Particulate matters, Street-canyon modelling, Sensors

Introduction

Urban air pollution is a major threat to public health and the environment. The problem is especially severe in densely populated cities. Warsaw, the capital of Poland, is home to about 1.8 million people (2025/09/19 14:24:25: www.um.warszawa.pl). Located in the Masovian Voivodeship in central-eastern Poland, Warsaw lies within a temperate, transitional mid-latitude climate zone, characterized by prevailing westerly and south-westerly winds. In 2022, the average annual wind speed ranged between 12 and 13 m/s, and the mean annual temperature was between 9 and 10°C (Kalinowska-Witowska et al., 2020).

Among the most critical air pollutants in Warsaw are particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), originating from diverse sources such as household heating, traffic, and industry. PM10, a coarse pollutant, can come from road dust, vehicle exhaust and non-exhaust, industrial activity, and even natural sources like wildfires. In the Mazovia region, the residential heating sector is responsible for 68% and 87% of PM10 and PM2.5 emissions, respectively. In contrast, the road traffic sector accounts for 9.2% and 7.1% of PM10 and PM2.5 emissions, respectively (Kalinowska-Witowska et al., 2024). Short-term exposure to PM10 aggravates conditions such as asthma, while long-term exposure is linked to chronic respiratory issues and premature mortality (Balduzzi, 2003; World Health Organization, 2013).

PM2.5 particles, being even finer, are able to penetrate deep into the respiratory system and enter the bloodstream. These particles mostly result from combustion processes and are considered the most dangerous air pollutants due to their systemic health effects (Sharma et al., 2024). Epidemiological studies suggest PM2.5 exposure leads to about 4 million deaths annually worldwide and reduces average life expectancy in Europe by up to 8 months (Thangavel et al., 2022; Xing et al., 2016).

Mitigating these pollutants through emission control and urban planning is essential for improving public health (Gustafsson, 2022; Kelly & Fussell, 2015; Tsai & Yang, 2023). To support such efforts, this study utilizes the ATMO-Street model chain (Hooyberghs et al., 2022), which integrates multiple components to simulate urban air quality with high spatial resolution. This includes GEM-AQ (Kaminski et al., 2008) for background concentrations, IFDM (Lefebvre et al., 2011) for local emission dispersion, and OSPM (Berkowicz, 2000) for street-canyon effects—providing a comprehensive view of regional and localized pollution interactions.

However, high-quality modelling depends on accurate emission inventories and validated monitoring data (Borge et al., 2014; Khreis et al., 2018; Hood et al., 2018). Discrepancies in emission reporting, especially for residential heating and road dust resuspension, can lead to biases in model predictions (Holnicki et al., 2017).

Low-cost sensors (LCS) have become increasingly prominent in air quality monitoring due to their affordability and ease of deployment, but their accuracy often falls short compared to reference-grade instruments, necessitating robust calibration strategies (Watne et al., 2021; Ravindra et al., 2024). Calibration is critical to correct for systematic biases, environmental influences, and sensor drift, ensuring data reliability for both scientific and regulatory applications (Dong et al., 2025; Koziel et al., 2024). Field studies have demonstrated that careful calibration, often involving co-location with reference stations, can substantially improve the accuracy and practical utility of LCS data in both stationary and mobile deployments (Peters et al., 2021; Wesseling et al., 2021).

Despite advances in calibration, low-cost sensors may still exhibit limitations, particularly under extreme environmental conditions or during high-pollution epi-sodes (Watne et al., 2021). Calibrated sensor data can effectively complement regulatory monitoring networks and support air quality model validation, provided that their limitations are acknowledged and addressed through rigorous calibration and quality control procedures (Dong et al., 2025; Aula et al., 2022; Nalakurthi et al., 2024).

The primary objective of this study is to develop and evaluate a robust, transferable framework for urban air quality modelling that leverages both updated emission inventories and integrated monitoring data from LCS and reference stations. Using Warsaw as a representative case, for the year 2022, we seek to address the following scientific questions:

How does the inclusion of high-resolution, up-to-date emission inventories—particularly for residential heating and road dust resuspension—affect the accuracy and spatial resolution of urban PM10 and PM2.5 modelling?

Data and methods

Emission data

In this study, emissions from the residential sector were derived from the national emission inventory, which is developed and maintained by the Institute of Environmental Protection-National Research Institute (IEP-NRI). The estimation process incorporated a comprehensive set of input parameters, including building-specific characteristics such as location, total area, number of floors, heating degree days (HDD), fuel types (gas, wood, coal, and oil), gas consumption for heating, building age (reflecting insulation levels), heat distribution network structure, and accessibility to these networks. The spatial resolution of these data varied depending on the nature of each parameter. To ensure accuracy, every data point was precisely linked to its corresponding building using detailed geographical coordinates. This bottom-up methodology enabled the calculation of residential emissions at an individual building level within the study domain (Gawuc et al., 2021).

On-road emissions were estimated using GPS-based data from the smartphone navigation app “Yanosik,” which provides detailed insights into user counts and average speeds across all roads within the study area. However, since Yanosik does not reliably capture overall traffic volumes, annual traffic volume estimates from the City of Warsaw’s integrated traffic model (2022 edition) were used as a reference to adjust these data. Specifically, the model outputs by road segment served to calculate scaling factors as the mean ratio of city model traffic volumes to Yanosik user counts for selected road segments. This scaling factor was then applied to the Yanosik-based emission estimates to correct for under- or over-representation of vehicle flows in the app data.

Emission calculations were based on speed-depen-dent emission factors from COPERT (COmputer Programme to calculate Emissions from Road Transport), version 5.7, developed by Emisia SA and coordinated by the European Environment Agency (EEA), accessible at (2025/09/19 14:24:25: https://copert.emisia.com) with fleet distribution assigned according to road class as defined in OpenStreetMap (OSM).1

For major roads, traffic data from the city’s model was intersected with a 500-m grid. Points near highways and express ways were selected, and traffic volumes, along with fleet composition, were interpolated. These values were then assigned to OSM road segments to generate a detailed road network. For other roads, traffic data was categorized into deciles based on intensity, with each decile processed separately to interpolate traffic volumes and fleet composition. Yanosik data was then adjusted to align with the structure and intensity of the city’s traffic model (2025/09/19 14:24:25: https://www.cupt.gov.pl/). Finally, all road data was integrated to produce an accurate emissions estimate.

The traffic emissions calculation included both exhaust and non-exhaust emissions but did not account for road dust resuspension. A previous study (Sattari et al., 2025) identified an underestimation of PM10 concentrations, particularly at traffic monitoring stations. However, model performance improved when resuspension emissions were incorporated into the model chain. Road dust resuspension emissions were calculated separately using the Vehicular Emissions INventories (VEIN) model (Ibarra-Espinosa et al., 2018), applying emission factors from AP-42 Section 13.2.1 for paved roads. The VEIN model utilized the same traffic volume estimates for computation of both exhaust and non-exhaust PM emissions on each road link.

The resuspension calculation was adjusted for precipitation by applying a daily correction term based on the frequency of measurable rainfall (> 0.254 mm). This approach assumes that annual average emissions are inversely proportional to precipitation frequency, as represented in Eq. 1 below (2025/09/19 14:24:25: EPA - AP-42 Section 13.2.1 Paved Roads).

Emission scenarios

The study initially utilized baseline emissions from the national inventory to establish the Base modelling scenario. However, a detailed analysis of PM10 and PM2.5 concentration patterns revealed localized discrepancies between modelled and observed pollution hotspots. This prompted a thorough reassessment of emission inputs, particularly within the residential heating sector—a major contributor to PM in the region.

Our examination identified key discrepancies in the original inventory’s assumptions about heating fuel types. Outdated or incomplete records led to misclassification of coal and wood burning and gas usage reporting, as well as spatial inaccuracies in emission allocations. To address this, we incorporated data from the Central Register of Building Emissions (CEEB)—a national database in which building owners are legally required to declare their primary heating fuel type annually.

The CEEB dataset significantly improved the accuracy of residential heating emissions by refining fuel consumption patterns due to mandatory reporting requirements. This data refinement process led to an average reduction of 22% in modelled PM10 and PM2.5 emissions across pollution hotspots.

Initial model outputs systematically underestimated observed PM10 concentrations during dry months, particularly in high-traffic locations. This discrepancy suggested an under-representation of road dust resuspension—a process highly sensitive to seasonal precipitation patterns. Due to the absence of regional observational data on silt loading factors for Polish road networks, we applied a calibrated adjustment approach to better align temporal PM10 patterns with observed peaks. Resuspension emissions were increased by 17% for both PM10 and PM2.5 by modifying the silt loading factors in the AP-42 13.2.1 emission factors. ATMO-Street model chain

In this study, the ATMO-Street model chain (Hooyberghs et al., 2022) was applied in a customized configuration to simulate street-level PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations in Warsaw, Poland, for the year 2022. This modelling framework provides a detailed assessment of urban air quality by capturing both regional background pollution and localized emission sources (Lefebvre et al., 2013). Typically, the ATMO-Street model chain consists of three core components: the RIO land-use-based interpolation model, which estimates background pollution levels (Janssen et al., 2008); the IFDM bi-Gaussian plume dispersion model, which accounts for emissions from traffic and industry (Lefebvre et al., 2011); and the OSPM street-canyon module, which simulates pollution accumulation within street canyons (Berkowicz, 2000). However, in the Warsaw configuration, the RIO model was replaced by the GEM-AQ model (Kaminski et al., 2008). A double counting correction (Lefebvre et al., 2011) is implemented within the model chain to prevent overlapping contributions from the ATMO-Street local-scale components and the regional background supplied by GEM-AQ. The modified model chain (GEM AQ-IFDM-OSPM) was previously assessed for 2019 to evaluate its performance (Sattari et al., 2025). The ATMO-Street model chain, in (Sattari et al., 2025), showed significant improvements by including detailed emissions and road dust resuspension. While resuspension improved performance in warmer months, it caused PM2.5 overestimation in winter. Moreover, excluding residential heating emissions in the background model notably underestimated PM2.5 at background stations, highlighting the importance of residential sources in urban air quality modelling.

This modelling approach generates hourly pollutant concentrations at various irregularly distributed receptors. Concentration outputs from the IFDM and OSPM models undergo a three-stage post-processing workflow to generate high-resolution pollutant maps. First, IFDM-derived concentrations are spatially distributed using Delaunay triangulation, creating continuous grids of open-street pollution levels. Second, OSPM results are mapped via nearest-neighbour interpolation to preserve street-canyon resolution. Finally, these gridded datasets are combined through a weighted integration process, prioritizing OSPM outputs in densely built areas with adjacent structures and IFDM results in open environments. This merging technique produces a unified 10-m resolution pollution map, balancing the strengths of both models while maintaining an urban morphology-aware approach.

Building data were obtained from the GUGIK Topographic Objects Database (BDOT10k), a vector dataset providing spatial coordinates, building heights, and number of floor for each structure. BDOT10k offers detail comparable to a 1:10,000 scale topographic map (2025/09/19 14:24:25: geoportal.gov.pl). Meteorological variables (temperature, wind speed, and direction) for 2022 were obtained from the GEM-AQ model. The modelling process is summarized in the flowchart shown in Fig. 3.

Simulated background concentration data

Background concentrations were simulated through the GEM-AQ—an online air quality model, which offers a unified approach for integrating meteorological and air quality components within the atmospheric system (Kaminski et al., 2008). GEM-AQ generated hourly concentrations for PM2.5 and PM10 on the original computation grid with a spatial resolution of 2.5km (0.025 degrees). Emission data from the industrial and non-industrial combustion plants, road transport, and residential heating were included in the background concentration model. These data were obtained from the Centre for Emissions Management’s annual assessment, along with the 2022 EMEP inventory for neighbouring countries. The spatial distribution of annual average background concentrations of PM2.5 and PM10 is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Annual average background concentrations of a PM2.5 and b PM10 (μg/m3) obtained from GEM-AQ model

The operational Integrated Forecasting System (IFS) of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) provides meteorological fields for the initial and boundary conditions of GEM-AQ. GEM-AQ is initialized using the IFS 12-h forecast (valid at 00:00 UTC of the following day) and uses IFS boundary data at 3-h intervals. The IFS data have a horizontal resolution of 0.1∘×0.1∘ (∼ 9 km at mid-latitudes) and 28 hybrid vertical levels. Within GEM-AQ, these are dynamically interpolated to the model grid (limited area domain), with meteorological fields constrained in a 10-grid-point nesting/absorber zone along each lateral boundary (Colette et al., 2024; Kaminski et al., 2008).

Measured concentration data

Measured concentration data for PM2.5 and PM10 were obtained from nine reference stations operated by the Chief Inspectorate of Environmental Protection (GIOŚ) and from sensor data provided by Airly (2025/09/19 14:24:25: https://airly.org/map/pl/), collected from 31 distinct locations within the study domain.

Two main types of sensors were used by Airly for these concentration measurements (2025/09/19 14:24:25: https://airly.org/):

Airly Pure: Focuses on particulate matter (PM1, PM2.5, PM10), along with temperature, humidity, and air pressure.

Airly Pure: Focuses on particulate matter (PM1, PM2.5, PM10), along with temperature, humidity, and air pressure.

Airly Aura: Measures PM as well as gaseous pollutants like nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and nitric oxide (NO).

Airly Aura: Measures PM as well as gaseous pollutants like nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and nitric oxide (NO).

PM is measured in these sensors using optical methods. These sensors are compact, weatherproof, and calibrated for local conditions to ensure reliable performance in diverse environments.

Airly employs a rigorous two-step calibration process to ensure the highest accuracy and reliability of its PM sensors. Initially, all PM sensors undergo calibration in a controlled lab environment. This involves exposing the sensors to varying concentrations of PM and diverse environmental conditions within a specialized test chamber. The process includes cross-checking sensor readings against reference-grade equipment to guarantee precision. Only sensors that meet stringent quality standards are approved for deployment in the field. Once installed, the sensors are fine-tuned using geographically specific calibration factors tailored to local environmental conditions. This unique approach ensures optimal measurement accuracy for each location. Furthermore, seasonal variations in pollution sources and characteristics necessitate periodic recalibration throughout the year. Airly dynamically updates these calibration factors to reflect changes in pollution patterns, ensuring consistent data quality over time (2025/09/19 14:24:25: https://airly.org/).

The reference stations operated by GIOŚ determine PM concentrations using reference-grade gravimetric (mass-based) methods, whereas Airly measurements are based on optical light-scattering technology. This difference in measurement principle can influence the comparability of absolute concentration values. To minimize such effects, Airly applies a two-stage calibration protocol: an initial laboratory calibration against reference instruments under controlled aerosol and environmental conditions, followed by in-field calibration against co-located reference stations using geographically specific correction factors. Seasonal recalibration further adjusts for variations in aerosol composition and environmental factors such as humidity. These procedures are designed to align optical measurements as closely as possible with mass-based reference values, although some residual bias may remain due to differences in technique.

Results and discussion

In this study, the model’s performance in predicting PM10 and PM2.5 concentrations was evaluated by incorporating emissions from various sources, including residential heating, transport, and road dust resuspension, under two scenarios. Residential emissions were modelled using data from 228,272 point sources, while traffic emissions were based on 409,697 road segments. Hotspots and uncertainties in emissions were identified, and an updated emission dataset was implemented to enhance the model’s accuracy in predicting PM concentrations.

The model performed well at reference stations, except at MzWarChrosci and MzPiasPulask, where discrepancies were highest. Notably, the model consistently overestimated PM10 concentrations at sensor locations, including those in street canyons, while underestimating concentrations at the only reference traffic station.

Seasonal validation plots (Figs. 20 and 19) show that winter conditions exacerbate overestimations, likely due to increased residential heating emissions and meteorological factors. Conversely, the model’s performance improves in summer, with fewer outliers compared to winter. Monthly profiles

Monthly PM10 and PM2.5 profiles (Fig. 8) at the traffic station reveal a significant anomaly in March 2022, where measured PM10 concentrations peaked at nearly 70 μg/m3, exceeding both modelled scenarios. Similarly, PM2.5 concentrations exhibited a noticeable peak, although to a lesser extent. This pronounced difference between PM10 and PM2.5 suggests a substantial increase in coarse PM, which is strongly associated with road dust resuspension. This is aligned with the study done by Rienda et al. (2023), where it was shown the increase of dust loading by 21% in the traffic site after consecutive days of dry weather. The extreme dryness in March (precipitation of only 0.4 mm on March 7; Fig. 11) likely exacerbated road dust resuspension, contributing to elevated PM10 levels. Several studies confirm that omitting road dust resuspension can lead to underestimation of PM10 in urban environments (Sattari et al., 2025; Linda et al., 2025; Fussell et al., 2022; Amato et al., 2012).

The validity of sensors

Figure 10a, which compares PM10 concentrations measured by sensors and reference stations, shows that sensors consistently underestimate PM10 values during the months of January to April. This discrepancy is particularly pronounced in March, when reference stations recorded significantly higher PM10 levels compared to sensors. The spike in PM10 concentrations observed in March aligns with the fact that this month was extremely dry, suggesting that extreme dryness may exacerbate sensor inaccuracies. Dry conditions often lead to increased PM due to reduced precipitation and higher resuspension of dust particles, which may not be effectively captured by the sensors.

a The comparison of the PM10 concentrations in the reference stations and sensor locations, b the daily quantile mapping of PM10 in the Filtrowa sensor location

The standard deviation analysis (Table 2) further highlights these discrepancies. The values were calculated based on hourly measured concentrations at each sensor and reference station location. For PM10 concentrations, reference stations exhibit much greater variability in March, with a standard deviation of 9.8, compared to sensors, which show a standard deviation of only 4. This indicates that sensors fail to capture the full range of PM10 variability observed by reference stations during high-pollution periods. Similarly, for PM2.5 concentrations in March, reference stations display a standard deviation of 6, while sensors report only 3.3, again underscoring the sensors’ inability to reflect peak pollution levels accurately.

Nevertheless, in the other months—when pollution levels are more moderate and less variable—the lower standard deviation for sensors is less problematic for validation purposes, as the sensor readings are more closely aligned with the reference measured concentrations. This suggests that sensor data can be effectively used for model validation during these periods, providing reliable information about typical air quality conditions. However, during months with peak pollution, the inability of sensors to capture the full range and variability of concentrations limits their usefulness for validating air quality models, especially when it comes to assessing extreme events or high-pollution episodes.

Moreover, the quantile mapping (Fig. 10b) was conducted using average daily values of the concentrations to ensure a comprehensive representation of pollution levels across different time periods. The sensor location shown in Fig. 10b was specifically chosen because it is situated within a street canyon and is closest to the street-canyon reference station. This proximity allows for a direct comparison between sensor and reference station measured concentrations under similar environmental conditions.

While there is a close match between sensor and reference values at lower PM10 levels, the divergence becomes significant at higher concentrations. The combination of underestimations during high-pollution periods and reduced variability captured by sensors suggests that model results cannot be adequately analyzed at sensor locations, particularly for PM10, unless these limitations are accounted for.

While discrepancies between sensor and reference station measured concentrations are often attributed to the sensitivity of the sensors themselves, this is not always the case. Differences may also arise due to calibration algorithms applied to raw sensor data. These algorithms may adjust measured concentrations to align with historical trends or other predefined criteria as part of the calibration process described above. Additionally, variations in measurement conditions—such as differences in sensor placement, installation height, and proximity to pollution sources—can further contribute to these discrepancies.

Moreover, environmental factors play a significant role in sensor performance. Notably, studies (Tagle et al., 2020; Jayaratne et al., 2018; Blaga & Gautam, 2024) have shown that low ambient humidity can exacerbate the underestimation of PM10 concentrations by optical sensors. This occurs because dry particles scatter less light than humidified ones, leading to lower sensor readings during periods of extreme dryness. Such environmental influences, combined with calibration and installation variables, help explain why sensors may fail to fully capture peak pollution levels observed at reference stations.

In addition to our analysis, it is important to compare these findings with results from independent field evaluations of Airly sensors, such as those conducted by the South Coast Air Quality Management District (SCAQMD) (South Coast Air Quality Management District, 2017). In SCAQMD’s extensive field testing of the Airly PM sensors, the evaluation confirmed that, while Airly sensors generally track the temporal trends of PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations, they tend to underestimate particulate matter levels during high-pollution episodes. The report observed increasing negative bias with higher concentrations, particularly for PM10, and highlighted lower precision in capturing pollution peaks and rapid concentration changes during events such as dust storms or extremely dry conditions. These findings are consistent with the discrepancies identified in our study, specifically, the significant underestimation of PM10 concentrations during dry, high-pollution periods, such as in March. Reduced sensitivity at high concentrations, potential influences of environmental factors like humidity and dust resuspension, and limitations in real-time calibration algorithms likely contribute to the observed underestimation. Conversely, both our findings and those from SCAQMD suggest that Airly sensors perform better during periods of moderate and stable air quality, making them suitable for routine monitoring and model validation outside of peak emission events.

The discrepancy between sensor data and reference measured concentrations observed earlier suggests that model results cannot be adequately analyzed at sensor locations. Despite improvements, both scenarios failed to capture the extreme PM10 peak in March at urban stations.

While road dust resuspension was accounted for, the model underestimated emissions under extreme dry conditions, revealing a gap in the OSPM module’s sensitivity to meteorological variability. This is a known limitation of OSPM (Zhong, 2016; De la Paz et al., 2015; Ketzel et al., 2011). In reality, particles resuspended within street canyons tend to remain trapped: they fall to the ground, are resuspended again, and repeat this cycle multiple times. Such repeated resuspension is not accurately represented in OSPM, nor is it fully captured by the current resuspension parametrizations.

The influence of meteorological factors

The temperature data for January, modelled in the study, was compared with observational data from the Institute of Meteorology and Water Management (IMGW). The analysis of one of the background stations (MzWarChrosci), which exhibited considerable overestimation in winter (see Fig. 16), showed that high-pollution periods coincided with very low temperatures—particularly between January 7th and 14th, when modelled temperatures were lower than those observed. These colder conditions increased heating demand, as indicated by heating degree days (HDD) from the GEM-AQ model, a key parameter in calculating residential heating emissions. As a result, the model overestimated pollutant concentrations during this period and throughout January.

Comparison of the temperature and PM10 concentrations in the location of the MzWarChrosci (urban background station) in January 2022

Despite attempts to improve emissions from the residential sector, the difference in pollutant concentrations between the Revised and Base scenarios was minimal during this period. This likely occurred because stations like MzWarChrosci are situated in districts with a high number of single-family homes, where the emission model assumes a mix of heating fuels. Consequently, improvements in the Revised scenario had limited impact. A similar pattern was observed at MzPiasPulask, where both MzWarChrosci and MzPiasPulask consistently showed strong positive NMB values across all seasons, particularly during winter and autumn (see Fig. 21b).

These findings are consistent with previous studies that have identified discrepancies in meteorological input data—especially temperature and wind speed—as a major source of error in urban air quality modelling (Pernigotti et al., 2013; Baklanov et al., 2009).

In this study, robust bias correction for meteorological data was not feasible for 2022 due to the lack of co-located or sufficiently nearby temperature measurements. This limitation is acknowledged, and future modelling work will prioritize access to local meteorological observations or bias-corrected datasets, so as to reduce uncertainty in residential heating emissions and improve wintertime pollutant predictions.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of sensor-based validation in improving urban air quality models, especially in cities like Warsaw where reference stations are limited—particularly within street-canyon environments. Two emission scenarios were evaluated: a Base scenario using the original emission inventory and a Revised scenario that incorporated updated and refined emissions, including high-resolution residential heating data from the Central Register of Emissions from Buildings (CEEB) and calibrated road dust resuspension parameters. The updated emissions dataset in the Revised scenario led to significant improvements in model performance compared to the Base scenario. For example, the Revised scenario resulted in concentration reductions of up to 20% in urban pollution hotspots and reduced the prediction bias for PM2.5 by 57% at key locations. Across most sensors, normalized mean bias (NMB) values dropped from 40–60% in the Base scenario to 10–20% in the Revised scenario. These improvements demonstrate how regulatory data infrastructures like CEEB can help address systemic uncertainties in urban-scale modelling.

Seasonal analysis showed that winter remains the most challenging period for accurate predictions, driven by elevated heating emissions and complex meteorological conditions. In contrast, model-observation agreement was strongest during summer, when emissions were lower and atmospheric conditions were more stable. The model captured annual mean PM10 concentrations reasonably well but struggled with winter outliers, with persistent overestimations of 5–10 μg/m3 at many sensor sites.

PM2.5 predictions were more consistent across both sensor and reference locations compared to PM10. The lower accuracy in PM10 forecasts was primarily due to limitations in OSPM’s ability to represent road dust resuspension under dry conditions. Notably, the extreme PM10 peak observed in March exposed this weakness. Although resuspension was included in the model, OSPM does not account for repeated re-entrainment of particles in street canyons—where dust tends to settle and be resuspended multiple times. This dynamic is not adequately reflected in current parametrizations, leading to underestimation during prolonged dry periods. The exceptionally low precipitation in March further amplified PM10 levels, emphasizing the critical role of wet deposition in mitigating particulate pollution in urban areas.

The analysis also highlighted the growing importance of sensor networks in extending spatial coverage of air quality monitoring. However, sensor data must meet rigorous quality standards to be effectively used for model validation. Sensors tracked seasonal patterns similarly to reference stations but systematically underestimated pollutant concentrations during high-pollution events. The quantile mappings confirm this pattern, showing increasing divergence at higher concentration levels. During winter peaks, sensors consistently underestimated PM10 concentrations and failed to capture the full variability seen in reference data, as further evidenced by standard deviation analysis. Calibration algorithms applied to sensor outputs may dampen fluctuations or bias readings toward historical trends, limiting their responsiveness to extreme events.

While the Revised modelling scenario led to clear improvements—particularly in urban zones—it also exposed ongoing challenges in representing localized effects such as road dust resuspension during dry conditions. Moving forward, continuous model refinement, high-resolution emission inventories, and broader deployment of high-quality sensors are essential. Enhancing the model’s responsiveness to extreme environmental conditions and refining localized emissions, particularly in traffic-heavy areas, will be key to developing targeted mitigation strategies for reducing PM10 and PM2.5 pollution and improving urban air quality.
