Insights into hepatitis B vaccination decision-making: Exploring vaccine preferences, health disparities, and practice patterns among patients and healthcare providers
Authors: Melissa Scherkoske, James Moran, Michael Barnett, Clem Lewin, Stephanie Campbell, Patricia Novy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a public health burden, with non-Hispanic Asian/Pacific Islander and non-Hispanic Black persons disproportionately impacted. Social determinants of health influence vaccination rates and healthcare access. This study used HBV vaccination to identify potential drivers of health disparities associated with multidose HBV vaccines, understand how patients and healthcare providers (HCPs) approach HBV vaccine decision-making and selection, and evaluate the patient and HCP preferences for multidose HBV vaccines. Patients (n = 805) and HCPs (n = 300) were recruited through an online survey. Social vulnerability index was used to identify health disparities linked to demographics and socioeconomic status. Results revealed challenges to vaccination, including lengthy appointment durations (40% spending more than 2 hours), high visit costs (27% paying over $100), and appointment cancellations due to affordability concerns (31%). 38% of patients with higher SVI missed an appointment due to cost. Logistic regression analysis for patients’ likelihood for vaccine dose decisions did not show significance, despite reported preference for a two-dose HBV vaccine. A multinomial logistic regression showed that when HCPs were aware of a two-dose HBV vaccine, they had 78.5% lower odds of recommending a three-dose HBV vaccine (OR = 0.215, 95 CI [0.101, 0.458], p < .001). 97% of HCPs agreed that their responsibilities include discussing vaccination with patients, but only 33% did so. There is an opportunity to improve low HBV vaccination rates through HCP influence. This study exposed barriers that patients face in accessing preventive healthcare. The two-dose HBV vaccine may address healthcare disparities and, by reducing appointment burden, could assist socially vulnerable individuals.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a common cause of chronic liver disease and remains a public health burden globally and in the United States (US).1,2 In 2018, approximately 1.9 million people in the US were living with chronic HBV infection.3 In the US, non-Hispanic Asian/Pacific Islander persons and non-Hispanic Black persons are disproportionately impacted by HBV, both in terms of long-term consequences of chronic infection and mortality.4,5

The introduction of the HBV vaccine has led to a decline in the number of reported HBV cases in the US.6 Since 1991, a three-pronged vaccination strategy comprised of infants, children (catch-up), and at-risk adults (defined as persons at risk of sexual exposure, persons at risk by percutaneous or mucosal exposure to blood, international travelers, persons with hepatitis C, persons with chronic liver disease, persons with HIV infection, and incarcerated persons), successfully reduced the burden of HBV disease by over 90%.6,7 Despite this significant reduction, a slight increase in HBV acute infection has been observed in adults.6 Factors likely contributing to this increase are sub-optimal HBV vaccine coverage rates in at-risk populations and a significant proportion of reported acute HBV cases without an associated risk factor in adults, suggesting the risk-based recommendations might be insufficient at preventing the remaining burden of HBV disease in adults.6 In April 2022, new guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) expanded the adult HBV vaccine recommendations to cover all adults aged 19–59 who do not have documentation of receiving a complete HBV vaccine series.6 The updated recommendations eliminated any potential stigma associated with the vaccine (such as risk factors of injection drug use, incarceration, or multiple sex partners) as well as concerns about the time required for providers to assess patient risk factors. The simplified vaccine recommendations aimed to improve HBV vaccine coverage rates across all adults, ideally before the onset of chronic liver disease and other comorbidities (e.g., obesity or diabetes) that might make vaccination less effective.6 The licensed and available two-dose and three-dose adult HBV vaccine series are recommended for this population.6

Social determinants of health (e.g., social environment, economic stability, and public policies) can negatively impact health across various groups, including vaccination practices and patient healthcare accessibility.8 Poor vaccination coverage in adults and uptake among racial and ethnic minorities in the US illuminate health disparities and a disproportionate burden of preventable diseases, such as that seen with HBV.9 Barriers to healthcare access include a lack of transportation and childcare, inability to take time off from work, unstable housing, communication and language barriers, racism, discrimination, and a lack of trust in healthcare providers (HCPs).10 As the US transitions from risk-based adult vaccination recommendations to a universal approach, research is needed to determine how to optimize implementation. For example, series completion is required to ensure immunized individuals are protected against HBV, and data from multiple studies in high-risk populations suggest series completion is sub-optimal.11-15 It will therefore be critical to understand the barriers to series completion, including costs to patients and how social determinants of health impact HBV vaccination.

A relationship-centered model was used to understand the role of patients and HCPs in addressing social determinants of health.16 A relationship-centered approach focuses on the role of relationships between HCPs and patients and their impact on healthcare delivery and improving patient outcomes.17,18 Studies have shown that HCP recommendations improve patient receptivity to receiving HBV vaccination, and therefore, employing the patient-centered model would require insights from HCPs and patients.19 This study was conducted to 1) identify potential drivers of health disparities associated with multidose vaccines, 2) analyze patient and HCP criteria in HBV vaccine decision-making and selection, and assess their impact on the propensity to obtain HBV vaccination, and 3) evaluate patient and HCP preferences for HBV vaccines (specifically two-dose vs three-dose options).

Methods

Study design and participant eligibility

Participants were recruited via convenience sampling from across the US through Qualtrics (Seattle, WA, USA), a flexible online survey tool. Despite potential biases, convenience sampling allows for a cost-effective and timely approach to fielding surveys across patients and HCPs.20 The patient sample size of 800 was calculated assuming 260 million US adults (over 18 years of age), an estimated margin of error of 3.5%, 95% CI, and a response distribution of 50% for a total of 784 respondents, which was rounded up. Patient respondents were eligible if they were born before 1991 and were aged ≤60 years, spoke and read English, were US residents, and had tested negative for HBV infection (respondents born after 1991 outside of the US were allowed to participate as long as the other criteria were met) (Figure 1(a)). Patient respondents were sampled to ensure reflection of the US population through quotas related to geographical distribution (17% Northeast, 21% Midwest, 24% West, 38% South), to ensure better representation due to convenience sampling. Additionally, respondent data were monitored throughout recruiting to ensure appropriate representation across sex, household income, and education level. HCP sample size of 300 was calculated assuming 200,000 primary-care-based providers in the US, an estimated margin of error of 6%, 95% CI, and response distribution of 50% for a total of 267 respondents, which was rounded up. HCP respondents were eligible if they were US residents, spoke and read English, were board-certified, were in practice from 3 to 40 years, and provided ≥ 20 routine vaccinations per month to adult patients (Figure 1(b)). To enhance reliability, attention and validity checks were included in the survey, and all eligible participants (patients and HCPs) had to pass them. The survey instruments were developed using literature on HBV, health disparities, and behavioral economics (Supplemental materials). Additionally, the Qualtrics ExpertReview validation tool was used during the survey programming to ensure survey questions and logic met data quality standards. The tool also removes potential bot responses, reviews completion rates, and checks the overall quality of the responses. Any flagged respondents were removed from the completed survey data. Both surveys were piloted to ensure instrument reliability and functionality before launching. Participants who completed the study from June 9, 2023, to June 28, 2023, were considered for this analysis. All participants provided informed consent before participating. All research protocols were reviewed and approved by the Castle Institutional Review Board (protocol number: AH-2301Dx). Vaccine demand tests

Decision-making

A behavioral economics framework was used to understand behaviors relevant to preventive vaccination and public health guidance.21 Hypothetical and situational HBV vignettes were developed for patients and HCPs to evaluate their behavioral mechanisms through operant situations, including delay discounting of a two- versus a three-dose series vaccination, probability discounting through the completion of recommended HBV vaccine series in 1 month versus 6 months, and behavioral economic demand by understanding perceived HBV value and its potential impact on health.21 For patients, the vignettes were developed to provide scenarios in which they would be recommended by their HCP or seek out an HBV vaccine, and the time required to obtain and the likelihood they would get a two- versus three-dose series vaccine. HCPs were provided a scenario in which they were a member of their facility’s vaccine task force and must provide clinical, operational, and patient preferences for a two- versus three-dose series vaccine.

Health disparities

Recent research has evaluated a more robust approach to health disparities in quantitative surveys, as understanding has expanded regarding the impact of society, culture, and class on one’s health.22 The survey questions aimed to focus on socioeconomic status (SES) based on: economic capital (traditional wealth and income), cultural capital (educational opportunities and completion, social resources, and engagement with cultural institutions), and social capital (one’s relationships and social and support networks).22 In addition to basic demographics, various proxies for SES were incorporated into the patient survey: economic capital (income [sources of income and potential barriers to accessing basic needs], wealth [money spent on healthcare services], occupation [employment or work status], and neighborhood [type of community]), social capital (social influences [trust of family/friends] and community [connection with others, political engagement, involvement with organizations]), cultural capital (education [educational attainment and health literacy] and cultural engagement [knowledge]), and health (health-related behaviors, self-perceived health, and interactions with HCPs).22

The social vulnerability index (SVI), a tool for evaluating health disparities, was also employed. It was created by the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to understand the vulnerabilities of communities regarding natural disasters or disease outbreaks.23 Unless noted, an overall summary ranking variable was used that encompasses the main elements of the SVI to quantify further the survey results, which were: SES, household characteristics, racial/ethnic minority status, housing type, and transportation. A geographic information system‒based data match was conducted between the SVI census tract data and the master postal code file database to create an average SVI score that was at least partially within the postal code provided by the respondent. Additionally, SVI status was categorized into continuous variables of <0.3, 0.3‒0.5, 0.5‒0.7, and ≥0.7, with SVI being directly proportional to social vulnerability.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Qualtrics QM program, focusing on descriptive statistics using the data and analysis module; secondary analysis and geographic data matching and analysis were conducted using Microsoft Excel and Tableau. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), using the Qualtrics Stats iQ statistical analysis module, was primarily used to determine significant differences between groups (defined as p ≤ .05 at the 95% confidence interval), which provides insights into the impact of various factors on the dependent variables. Additionally, using Python 3.10.1, univariate logistic regression was used to assess the likelihood of decisions for binary dependent variables, and multinomial logistic regression for categorical dependent variables with more than two outcomes. The significance level (α) was also set to 0.05, and 95% confidence intervals were computed.

For current employment status, patients reported being employed full time (48%), employed part time (13%), and unemployed (18%). 55% of patients reported an income of $60,00 or less. 87% of patients reported having health insurance. Of the HCPs surveyed, 66.3% were male. Patient results

Access to healthcare

Several questions related to patients’ resources regarding healthcare visits were asked to understand potential health disparities of the patients. Within the last year, 72% of patients saw an HCP for sickness or a regular checkup (Figure 2(a)). Approximately 33% of patients reported missing a follow-up appointment as requested by their HCP, and 31% reported having to cancel an appointment due to financial constraints. Respondents were asked, in general, how much money it cost for a single patient visit at an HCP office/clinic. Considerations asked in the estimate included: time off work, lost wages, dependent care (e.g., childcare or elder care), transportation (e.g., gas, parking, public transportation), and co-pays, with 27% of patients spending more than $100 per visit (Figure 2(b)). Since the estimate was patient-reported, the total cost may be underestimated. Patient HBV vaccination status

Regarding HBV vaccination status for adult patients (Table 2), only 34% of patients reported being vaccinated for HBV, and 25% were unsure of their vaccination status. Insurance coverage had a strong association with the vaccination status of patients, with those with insurance also displaying high rates of vaccination (p < .001). A chi-square analysis revealed a significant association between sex and vaccination status, indicating that a higher proportion of females were vaccinated (p = .018). Of those who reported being vaccinated for HBV, 71% reported completing the entire vaccine series, 6% had not completed their series, and 23% were unsure. Factors influencing attitudes toward HBV vaccination

Respondents, regardless of vaccination status, were asked about sources of influence when it came to recommendations to get an HBV vaccine. Approximately 75% would agree to get an HBV vaccine if recommended by their HCP. About 51% were likely to get a vaccine at the recommendation of the CDC or other government entity; 23% were neither likely nor unlikely, while 26% were unlikely to follow the recommendation of the CDC or other government entity. Approximately 42% agreed that they would get an HBV vaccine if recommended by a family member or friend; 35% neither agreed nor disagreed, and 23% disagreed.

Patient preference for HBV vaccine options

The 272 respondents who reported being vaccinated for HBV were asked if their HCP offered them a choice between a two-dose and a three-dose series. Of those surveyed, 57% were not offered a choice, while 43% responded that they were allowed to choose. For those given a choice, 58% chose the two-dose option while 42% chose the three-dose option, with reasons for selection shown in Figure 3(a). Of the 166 respondents who reported being unvaccinated but were planning to get an HBV vaccine in the future, 49% preferred getting a two-dose vaccination. In contrast, only 4% preferred receiving a three-dose vaccine (Figure 3(b)). Modeling behaviors related to vaccine choice

Patients were provided three HBV vaccine selection vignettes. For vignette 1, patients were randomly assigned to one of three scenarios and asked to highlight their preferences based on time and effort, decision influence, and location preference for a two-dose versus a three-dose HBV vaccination (Table 5). Patients would make the time and effort to obtain a two-dose HBV vaccine over the three-dose option for all but one variable, traveling 20 miles. When in agreement with their HCPs to receive an HBV vaccination, patients appeared to favor the two-dose option when influenced by factors such as travel to/from the doctor’s office/clinic, more time with the doctor, the need to take time off work, the need to find dependent care, and amount of money required. Patients reported their location preference for receiving a two-dose HBV vaccine at their doctor’s office/health clinic, pharmacy, vaccine event (e.g., at a church), local health department, or a community center. It should be noted that more social locations (e.g., vaccine event at church or a community center) were not preferred by those required to receive the three-dose option. For vignette 2, patients were randomly assigned to two groups, a two-dose group and a three-dose group, to assess potential variables that would impact their decision to be vaccinated against HBV, based on recommendations of the CDC and their agreement with their HCP recommendations (Table 6). The two-dose option had numerically increased likelihood, when compared with the three-dose option, for travel to/from HCP’s office/clinic, more time with HCP, need to take time off work, need to find dependent care, and amount of money required. HCP results

Demographics of adult patient population in HCP practices

HCPs provided insurance coverage estimates for their adult patient population of 45% with private insurance, 29% on Medicare, 16% on Medicaid/state-specific Medicaid, 5% on other government insurance, and 5% uninsured. HCPs were asked to report on their adult population across nine socioeconomic indicators (Table S3). HCPs reported a population of patients under their care, with 52% non-Hispanic White, 17% non-Hispanic Black, and 17% Hispanic or Latino.

HBV vaccination/recommendation volumes in HCP practices

The mean number of HBV vaccinations that HCPs administered to adult patients monthly was 23.1. The mean number of recommendations of HBV vaccination to adult patients was 31.5 per month. The mean percentage of adult patients who received the vaccine based on HCP’s recommendation was 62%. When years in practice were categorized into continuous variables (11–19 years vs ≥27 years), there was a statistically significant negative correlation between years in practice and the number of HBV vaccinations administered (p < .001) and recommended (p < .001).

Likelihood of vaccine dose decisions

To evaluate HCP responses when assessing for choice between two options for their patients, either a two-dose series vs three-dose or two-dose or a combination of three-dose and no preference or two-dose and no preference, only one model met the p-value significance threshold (p < .001). When adjusting for years in practice, confidence in vaccines, reported patient perception for ease of vaccination series, being aware of the two-dose option, and belief patients will complete the entire series, the multinominal logistic regression showed that when providers were aware of the two-dose HBV vaccine series option, they had 78.5% lower odds of recommending the three-dose vaccine ([OR = 0.215, CI 95% [0.101, 0.458], p < .001). Additionally, when providers were aware of the two-dose series option, there were reduced odds of having no preference when compared with the two-dose HBV series (OR = 0.217, CI 95% [0.099, 0.474], p < .001) (Table 8). HCP attitudes about recommendation of HBV vaccination

Most HCPs agreed that it is their responsibility to initiate conversations regarding HBV vaccinations with their adult patients (strongly agree = 43%, agree = 29%, somewhat agree = 25%, somewhat disagree = 2%). When asked if they discuss different HBV vaccine options with their adult patients, 33% of HCPs responded “yes,” 28% responded “no,” and 38% responded “depends.” For those who do not discuss HBV vaccine options, the reasons provided were that their practice or health system provided only one option (56%), most of their patients did not have a preference (54%), patient insurance coverage determined the best option (19%), patient’s clinical sequelae determined which HBV vaccine was best (5%), and not knowing the differences between HBV vaccines (1%). Additionally, there was a statistically significant relationship between total years in practice and the likelihood that an HCP discussed different HBV vaccines with their adult patients. When asked to report the percentage of adult patients likely to get their HBV vaccine, based on the required number of doses, HCPs reported the mean percentage likely to receive one dose (62%), two doses (55%), and three doses (44%). Most HCPs agree that an HBV vaccine with fewer doses might be easier for their patients; 61% of providers strongly agreed, 24% somewhat agreed, and 14% agreed.

HBV vaccine selection by HCPs

Overall, 78% of HCP respondents were aware of a two-dose vaccine option. When asked to select all criteria that are important for their HBV vaccine selection, 77% selected strong efficacy data for at-risk patient subpopulations, 67% selected patient convenience to ensure dose series completion, 64% selected convenience for administration to ensure dose completion, 59% selected simple dosing to assist with patient flow/logistics, 49% selected applicability across adult populations, and 1% provided their criteria, which included affordability, insurance coverage, and safety. A total of 85% of HCPs (42% extremely likely, 43% somewhat likely) reported that, in their experience, adult patients are more likely to complete a full HBV vaccine series when fewer doses were required. Many HCPs (45%) agreed that a two-dose HBV vaccine series is most beneficial for their adult patients, 27% felt that a three-dose vaccine series would be most beneficial, and 28% stated no preference for their adult patients. The primary reasons reported for selecting their most beneficial HBV dose series were seroprotection rates (43%), safety profile (19%), compliance rates (13%), reduction in health disparities (9%), cost (7%), and patient convenience (5%).

Discussion

Our survey of 805 patients and 300 HCPs revealed important insights about patient challenges in accessing healthcare, patient and HCP decision-making related to HBV vaccines, and their preferences among HBV vaccine dosing regimens.

The patient survey data highlight the time and money associated with a healthcare visit. 40% of patients reported spending 2 hours or more going to a healthcare appointment. Regarding time off work, dependent care, transportation, and co-pays, 27% of patients reported spending more than $100 per doctor’s office/clinic visit. In comparison, 31% reported canceling a doctor’s appointment due to financial constraints. The survey data show a correlation between higher social vulnerability and a greater likelihood of missing a doctor’s appointment due to affordability issues. These findings highlight the challenges associated with accessing healthcare; considering the time and money required for a single appointment, vaccination series that require extra visits for series completion could become a hardship for some patients. Indeed, our survey results revealed that patients with insurance had higher rates of having been vaccinated for HBV, and more socially vulnerable respondents were more likely to be unsure of vaccination series completion or to have not completed an HBV vaccine series.

Overall, surveyed patients expressed a preference for a two-dose versus a three-dose HBV vaccine, and based on the survey vignettes, this preference appeared to be influenced by factors such as travel to/from the doctor’s office/clinic, more time with the doctor, the need to take time off work, the need to find dependent care, and the amount of money required. However, logistic regression models did not identify any statistically significant associations between preferences, or lack thereof, and independent variables such as SES, age, or income. This finding is not unexpected and points to a broad preference for a two-dose vaccine compared to a three-dose vaccine, regardless of societal factors.

The HCP survey data echoed the patient data, with HCPs agreeing that a two-dose vaccine is more beneficial/accessible. Like the patients’ logistic regression, most of the independent factors that may influence why a physician would prefer a vaccine series or suggest it to their patients, such as years in practice, did not yield statistically significant predictors of choice and suggestion. However, results suggest that awareness of the two-dose series played a significant role; HCPs familiar with the two-dose vaccine were more likely to prefer or recommend it to their patients, and patient convenience for vaccine series completion was a consideration for many of these HCPs. Among all HCPs (whether aware or unaware of a two-dose option), when selecting whether a two- or three-dose series was most beneficial for their patients, only a small proportion of HCPs considered health disparities, cost, or patient convenience. This data may indicate that HCPs fail to consider patient-centric considerations such as cost and convenience in their vaccine decision-making process. Given our findings on the cost and time for patients associated with making a healthcare visit, HCPs offering a choice of vaccine to their patients could play a role in decreasing health disparities, because the two-versus three-dose vaccine decision may be a more critical factor for people with low SES. Increased educational outreach to providers about the challenges some patients face in accessing healthcare could enhance vaccine series completion and improve public health outcomes, especially for patients who face health disparities.

Despite most HCPs agreeing it is their responsibility to initiate conversations about the HBV vaccine with their adult patients, only a third discussed HBV vaccine options with their patients. The reasons for this discrepancy are likely multi-factorial. They may include insufficient time during the healthcare visit to discuss vaccines, limitations in the documentation of adult vaccine history, and educational gaps among HCPs regarding their patients’ eligibility for HBV vaccination. The recent update of the ACIP guidelines, recommending HBV vaccination with all adults 19–59 years, presents a valuable opportunity to educate and encourage HCPs to integrate these conversations more routinely into patient interactions.6 Our survey found that HCPs with more years in practice were less likely to recommend and administer HBV vaccines. HCPs should assess HBV vaccine history and engage in discussions regarding HBV vaccination with all their adult patients, consistent with ACIP recommendations. The data presented here support the use of the two-dose vaccine based on patient convenience since a healthcare visit for a third dose would involve additional time and money for many patients.

While HCPs remain a source of information and influence for patients, the resources required for those patients to engage with HCPs present limitations on improving HBV vaccination rates to reach equity levels. Exploring a community-based healthcare model has been demonstrated to reduce health disparities.25 For those who rely on their family and friends for health information, engagement with community and faith-based leaders helps to address local social factors impacting the community.10 HCPs can leverage the SVI data to identify potential communities, required resources, and existing infrastructures to partner with community health centers or collaboratives specializing in the medical neighborhood.

This study employed a convenience sampling method, which may limit generalizability and introduce sample bias among the HCPs and participating patients. Self-selection bias should be considered, as the respondents could be financially motivated to participate due to their participation in Qualtrics’ respondent panels. Additionally, since this survey was conducted online, respondents may be more digitally literate and have greater access to technology. While a geographic distribution quota was implemented, the results should not be considered reflective of the US population.

The survey was conducted in June 2023, right after the end of the COVID-19 public health emergency on May 11, 2023. There are possible carryover effects of COVID-19 vaccinations in the responses from HCPs and patients, as well as potential delays in HCPs being made aware of the updated HBV vaccination recommendations. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed and elevated the discussion of health disparities in vaccinations, and learnings from COVID-19 can and should be applied in any intervention related to improving adult vaccination coverage.

In conclusion, survey data indicate that patients strongly favor a two-dose vaccine. Given this study’s findings about the significant time and money associated with a healthcare visit for many patients, a two-dose vaccine may alleviate some of this burden, especially for socially vulnerable patients. This preference for a shorter regimen aligns with data suggesting higher completion rates for two-dose vaccines, making it a potentially more effective strategy. Adult patients’ HBV vaccination coverage remains low; however, adults can be influenced by their HCPs to get vaccinated. The surveyed HCPs recognize their role in encouraging HBV vaccination, with a two-dose HBV vaccination option being the most recommended due to the reduction of barriers with their adult patient populations. By prioritizing discussions about vaccine options with patients, HCPs can contribute to the development of more effective vaccination strategies in the future.
