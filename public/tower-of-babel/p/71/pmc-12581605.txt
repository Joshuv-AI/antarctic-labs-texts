Comparative effectiveness of integrated video-based learning vs. conventional didactic lectures among Indian medical graduates: a multicentric study
Authors: Saumya Singh, Aditya Pratap Singh, Anuj Singh, Harendra Kumar, Monika Sharma, Neha Singh Chauhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe medical field is rapidly evolving, requiring innovative teaching methodologies to enhance comprehension and retention among students. Integrated video-based learning (VBL), which combines audio-visual content with faculty-led discussions, aims to improve understanding through multi-sensory engagement. This study compares the effectiveness of integrated VBL with conventional didactic lectures in medical education.MethodologyThis multicentric study was conducted across five medical institutions in India, involving 608 s-year MBBS students (Phase II) and 12 faculty members. Two topics, tuberculosis and biomedical waste management, were taught using a crossover design, with students randomly assigned to Group A (n = 304) and Group B (n = 304). Integrated VBL combined video content with interactive faculty-led sessions, while didactic lectures relied on traditional lecture-based teaching. Pre- and post-test assessments, using identical multiple-choice questions (MCQs), were conducted, and feedback was collected via a 1–5 Likert scale.ResultsFor tuberculosis, the mean pre-test score was 7.35 ± 1.74. Post-test scores were 13.10 ± 2.05 for Group A (integrated VBL) and 10.25 ± 1.84 for Group B (didactic). For biomedical waste management, the mean pre-test score was 6.31 ± 1.39, with post-test scores of 12.59 ± 2.33 for Group B (integrated VBL) and 8.50 ± 1.67 for Group A (didactic). Over 60% of students reported that integrated VBL improved understanding, retention, and self-directed learning. Faculty feedback corroborated these findings, though they noted increased workload.ConclusionIntegrated VBL significantly outperformed conventional didactic teaching, as evidenced by higher post-test scores and positive feedback. Neither VBL nor didactic teaching alone is optimal; however, their integration leverages visual-aural engagement and faculty guidance to enhance comprehension and retention. This approach is a vital component of modern medical education, fostering deeper understanding and clinical correlation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08116-4.

Introduction

The medical profession demands mastery of a vast and complex syllabus within a limited timeframe, necessitating innovative teaching strategies to enhance comprehension and retention [1]. The rapid evolution of medical science underscores the need for lifelong learning and adaptable educational methods [2]. Traditional didactic lectures, while structured, often fail to engage students fully or accommodate diverse learning styles [3–5]. Integrated video-based learning (VBL), defined as the use of audio-visual content (videos, animations, and graphics) combined with faculty-led interactive discussions, offers a promising alternative by engaging both visual and auditory senses to improve understanding and retention [7–9].

Bloom’s taxonomy categorizes educational objectives into cognitive, affective, and psychomotor domains, providing a framework to optimize medical teaching [6]. Integrated VBL aligns with this framework by enhancing cognitive understanding through multi-sensory engagement, fostering positive attitudes toward learning (affective domain), and supporting skill development (psychomotor domain) [10–13]. Studies have shown that VBL increases motivation, concentration, and satisfaction in knowledge acquisition [10–12].

This study addresses the research gap in evaluating the effectiveness of integrated VBL compared to conventional didactic lectures in Indian medical education, particularly for second-year MBBS students. The objectives were to: (1) compare post-test performance between integrated VBL and didactic teaching, (2) assess student and faculty perceptions of integrated VBL, and (3) determine its potential for integration into the medical curriculum. By focusing on tuberculosis and biomedical waste management, this study explores how integrated VBL can enhance comprehension of complex medical concepts and promote self-directed learning.

Methodology

This multicentric study was conducted over 18 months (July 2023–December 2024) across five medical institutions in India, located in Prayagraj (Uttar Pradesh), Raipur (Chhattisgarh), Bhopal (Madhya Pradesh), Udaipur (Rajasthan), and Mumbai (Maharashtra) Fig. 1. The study involved 608 s-year MBBS students (Phase II, corresponding to the second year of the MBBS curriculum in India, focusing on preclinical subjects such as microbiology and pathology) and 12 faculty members from the departments of microbiology, community medicine, and pathology.

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the lead institution (IEC Reg. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study design

A crossover design was employed to compare integrated VBL with conventional didactic lectures. Integrated VBL involved faculty-led sessions incorporating videos, animations, and interactive discussions, while didactic lectures followed traditional lecture-based teaching without multimedia. Two topics—tuberculosis and biomedical waste management—were selected due to their relevance to the Phase II curriculum, clinical significance, and suitability for visual representation.

A total of 608 students were randomly divided into two groups (Group A: n = 304; Group B: n = 304) using a random number generator to ensure comparable academic achievement, confirmed by reviewing prior exam performance (no significant differences, p > 0.05). Data collection and analysis

Pre- and post-test scores were compared using paired t-tests to assess differences within groups and independent t-tests to compare between groups. Feedback was collected via structured questionnaires on a 1–5 Likert scale, assessing comprehension, engagement, and curriculum integration. Descriptive statistics (percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to analyze feedback. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Of the 750 enrolled students, 608 completed the study (142 were absent). Twelve faculty members from all five institutions participated, including six from microbiology, three from community medicine, and three from pathology.

Academic performance (Table 1)

For tuberculosis, the mean pre-test score was 7.35 ± 1.74. Post-test scores were significantly higher for Group A (integrated VBL: 13.10 ± 2.05) than Group B (didactic: 10.25 ± 1.84; p < 0.001). For biomedical waste management, the mean pre-test score was 6.31 ± 1.39. Post-test scores were significantly higher for Group B (integrated VBL: 12.59 ± 2.33) than Group A (didactic: 8.50 ± 1.67; p < 0.001).

Faculty’s feedback (N=12)

All faculty (100%) agreed or strongly agreed that integrated VBL improved student understanding, promoted interaction, and effectively introduced clinical scenarios. However, 100% noted increased workload. Most (75%) felt it encouraged conceptual learning and engagement, and 100% supported its integration into the curriculum.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that integrated VBL significantly enhances comprehension and retention compared to conventional didactic lectures, addressing the need for innovative teaching methods in medical education. The higher post-test scores for integrated VBL (tuberculosis: 13.10 vs. 10.25; biomedical waste management: 12.59 vs. 8.50) highlight its effectiveness in delivering complex concepts through visual-aural engagement and faculty interaction. These findings align with prior studies showing that multimedia-enhanced teaching improves knowledge and skill retention [14–17].

The positive feedback from students (65% for retention, 66% for curriculum integration) and faculty (100% for improved understanding) underscores the potential of integrated VBL to transform medical education. However, the study emphasizes that neither VBL nor didactic teaching alone is ideal. Integrated VBL combines the strengths of both: videos provide visual clarity and repeatability, while faculty guidance ensures structured learning and clinical correlation. This integration mitigates risks associated with standalone VBL, such as over-reliance on unsupervised online content, which can lead to errors or unsafe practices [18–20].

The crossover design ensured that both groups experienced both teaching methods, reducing bias and confirming the superiority of integrated VBL across topics. The selection of tuberculosis and biomedical waste management was strategic, as their visual representation (e.g., disease progression, waste segregation protocols) enhances understanding through multimedia.

Conclusion

Integrated VBL, combining audio-visual content with faculty-led discussions, significantly outperforms conventional didactic teaching in enhancing comprehension, retention, and engagement among medical students. Neither standalone VBL nor didactic teaching is optimal; the integrated approach leverages multi-sensory engagement and expert guidance to address diverse learning needs. By fostering self-directed learning and clinical correlation, integrated VBL is a crucial component of modern medical education, as evidenced by superior post-test scores and positive feedback. Its adoption into curricula, supported by adequate faculty training, can optimize medical education while ensuring patient safety through supervised learning.
