Electronic medical record system user satisfaction and its implication on individual performance: a case of a university teaching hospital in Rwanda
Authors: Evode Uwamungu, Alpha Arsene Marara, Emmanuel Munyaneza, Jean Baptiste Ndahiriwe, Joel Pinney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to assess the level of user satisfaction with an electronic medical record system (OpenClinic) among healthcare service providers in a hospital setting and its implication on their performance. The study was conducted at the “Centre Hospitalier Universtaire de Kigali”, which has been implementing OpenClinic as an Electronic Medical Record system since 2007. The study was a cross-sectional mixed research using explanatory embedded design. The data was collected on a convenient sample of 217 OpenClinic users through the questionnaires including closed and open ended questions in order to capture both quantitative and qualitative data. The study used the descriptive and inferential statistical tests for analysis for quantitative data, and content analysis for qualitative data. The OpenClinic user satisfaction was found to be high (91%) as well as the proportion of users who perceived it as having positive impact on their performance (94%). The relationship between user satisfaction and perceived impact was statistically significant (p < 0.001), and satisfied users were 20 times more likely to perceive it having positive impact than non-satisfied users (Fisher’s odds ratio = 20.5 [CI: 5.3, 85.1], Wald’s odds ratio = 21.2 [CI: 6.3, 71.1]. Important concerns were expressed by users and the main ones are the poor functionality of the system due to unstable internet, the limited capacity of use and the scarcity of computers. Therefore, the Electronic Medical Record system implementation at the hospital has been successful and its user satisfaction led to perceived positive impact, but it needs further improvements for optimal success.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03597-9.Keywords: Electronic medical record system, OpenClinic, Management information system, User satisfaction, Individual performance

Introduction

Background

Many developing countries suffer from the lack of or poor healthcare Management Information Systems (MIS) [1]. The purpose of MIS in healthcare settings is to improve efficiency and service delivery to the patients in order to achieve positive outcomes, including saving lives [2]. However, achieving that purpose is not straight forward because many systems fail [3–5]. Successful implementation of information systems (IS) requires recurrent and rigorous evaluations that inform decision making and actions of organizations [6]. Many researchers have identified Information Systems (IS) user satisfaction as a key determinant of information systems’ success in achieving organizational goals [6].

In Rwanda, the “Centre Hospitalier de Kigali (CHUK)” is a tertiary hospital providing healthcare services to thousands of population annually including those that are self-referred and those that are transferred from different hospitals across the country. This hospital has been one of the few pioneers in Rwanda to implement OpenClinic as a healthcare MIS since the year 2007 [1]. The system is currently used by over 800 healthcare providers, including physicians, nurses, allied health professionals, and finance department staff.

Nevertheless, one study has been identified that focuses on its evaluation [7]. In addition, no study has yet been conducted in Rwanda to examine the implications of user satisfaction on individual performance, in order to understand the extent of system success or failure and inform the hospital leadership on necessary improvements that would eventually lead to enhanced patient care outcomes. Therefore, this study intends to fill this gap by assessing user experience in terms of satisfaction on using OpenClinic and their appreciation of its impact on work performance. The results of the study will inform the hospital on the interventions needed to ensure successful adoption of MISs and improve services provided to the patients.

Study context and relevance

Information systems (IS) user satisfaction has been documented as a key indicator of IS success, and numerous quantitative studies have been conducted in various countries to explore the factors associated with it. However, a lot of traditional IS studies conducted often overlooked the complex, institutional, and socially embedded nature of IS, raising the need to examine how IS align with context organizational specificities [8]. Previous health IT research has primarily focused on adoption, implementation, and technical effectiveness, and often overlooking user-centered and performance-related outcomes [9].

On the other hand, there have been few user-centered studies on IS success conducted in Rwanda. In addition, few qualitative studies have been conducted in order to deepen the understanding of the lessons learnt and issues associated with user satisfaction, particularly in healthcare settings. In particular, no assessment has been conducted on user experience of OpenClinic at the CHUK over the past seven years to inform its leadership on necessary improvements that could enhance patient benefits.

This study addresses the identified gaps in three ways: First, it contributes to the existing literature on factors affecting IS user satisfaction and performance. Second, it provides the hospital leadership with insights into user satisfaction level as a key component in the successful implementation of its healthcare information system. Thirdly, for staff who are dissatisfied or whose performance is negatively affected by the system, the leadership is informed on reasons of dissatisfaction, which might help in eventual planning for appropriate remedial actions.

Literature review

Nowadays, information systems are an essential component of organizational management aimed at improving efficiency and performance. However, many systems fail to meet user expectations, which negatively impacts both individual and organizational performance [3–5]. Therefore, the need for research on successful operationalization of information systems emerged from these challenges and remains an important subject of inquiry [6]. Thus, evaluating operationalization of an IS, is a worthwhile undertaking to ensure its success and realize its intended benefits [10]. One of the key aspects of IS success is the perceived satisfaction of its users [6].

IS user satisfaction as a measure of IS success

The IS user satisfaction reflects the extent to which a user perceives that the system meets their needs and expectations [11, 12]. The IS user satisfaction has been considered as an important element or even a surrogate of information system’s success, especially when the use of MIS is mandatory [10, 13]. This assertion is grounded in the fact that users are the primary actors who interact with an IS, making them well-positioned to assess its functional strengths and weaknesses, thus playing the role of key evaluators [6, 14]. Beyond this intuitive assertion, extensive research has demonstrated a correlation between user satisfaction and IS success [14]. Therefore, as measuring IS success is asserted to be difficult, satisfaction is usually used as a proxy measurement of IS success [15, 16].

Factors affecting user satisfaction

A variety of models have been developed to identify and explain the factors influencing user satisfaction with an IS. Among these, the most widely used are the DeLone and McLean (D&M) original and revised multi-dimensional and interdependent construct models [10, 17, 18]. According to the updated D&M model, the user satisfaction has been theorized as being associated to System quality, Information quality and Service quality [18, 19]. The model stipulates that the quality dimension of an IS has a positive effect on its actual use or intention to use and user satisfaction, which in turn bring or contribute to the overall benefits of the IS as illustrated in the Fig. 1 below [18].

Updated DeLone and McLean IS model [18]

According to D&M model, the system quality reflects the aspects of usability, availability, reliability, adaptability and response time. Information quality pertains to the interface content issues, including personalization, completeness, relevance, ease of understanding and security. Service quality concerns the support provided to the users whether coming from within or outside the organization. Use measures relate to navigation, retrieval of information and execution of operations. User satisfaction captures users’ opinions based on their experience while interacting with the system; and finally, net benefits refer to impacts realized whether positives or negatives [18, 19].

The model suggests that the results of IS quality are iteratively interrelated [18]. In fact, the system use must precede user satisfaction, but also a good experience of use (user satisfaction) would lead to the intention to and use of the system [10, 18, 19]. On the other hand, the system use and user satisfaction would result in net benefits, but the net benefits would also result in intention to use and user satisfaction [18]. A variety of empirical studies have been conducted and validated the D&M IS model. Most of these studies used quantitative research design and found significant relationships between quality of IS and user satisfaction as well as a positive relationship between user satisfaction and IS benefits [20–26].

D&M IS model was criticized for its difficulties in application across diverse contexts, modelling processes with causal explanations resulting in confusing meanings, and limited coverage of dependent variables as there are many other potential measurement categories that may vary from user to user [14, 18, 19, 27–29]. Thus, a variety of other IS success models were referred to and others surged including technology acceptance model, human-organizational-technology and fit model, technology-organization-environment model, and innovation dissemination theory [10, 17, 30].

Other main models of IS success

Technology acceptance model (TAM)

According to this model, the IS success is characterized by the extent of user acceptance measured through the actual use, while the underlying factors of that acceptance are the perceived usefulness and the perceived ease of use [31, 32]. The mode defines perceived usefulness as the extent to which users believe the information system is important in helping them for completing their job requirements, while perceived ease of use is defined as the extent to which users believe the system is used without efforts [31]. The model recognizes that these two factors are influenced by external factors which can include social, cultural and political factors [31, 33]. As illustrated in the Fig. 2 below, the model stipulates that the perceived usefulness and ease of use influence users’ attitudes towards using the technology. These attitudes, in turn, influence the intention to use the technology, which ultimately leads to the actual usage [31].

The technology acceptance model [31]

Many studies have validated the TAM, demonstrating the significant relationship between perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use on technology acceptance [32, 33]. However, numerous studies have also reported findings that do not align with TAM model [34].

The human-organizational-technology (HOT) fit model

The HOT fit model assumes that the effectiveness of an IS depends on the alignment of human factors, organizational structures and technology, with perspective of understanding IS success through a complex interplay between people, organizations and technology [35]. The human attributes reflect individual characteristics and interaction such as knowledge, skills, capabilities, motivation, position, tasks, collaboration, communication, competition and supervision; the organizational structures encompass internal and external organizational management environment and traits such as organizational size, culture, policies and strategy, practices and government regulations; while technology relates to the IS characteristics and requirements such as software, hardware, network tools, professional skills, systems quality, information quality, service quality and social presence [35].

Numerous studies have been conducted on the hypothesis that, when an information system is well aligned with the individual characteristics, organizational management practices and the existing technological infrastructure, it results in higher user satisfaction, increased productivity, and improved system performance [36, 37]. Thus, the underlying technology adoption factors highlighted in these studies include: (i) training, perception, roles, skills, clarity of system purpose and user involvement as “human attributes”: (ii) leadership and support, process, participation or user involvement, internal communication and inter-organizational system as “organization factors”; and (iii) ease of use, system usefulness, system flexibility, time efficiency, information accessibility and relevancy as “technology factors” [36, 37].

However, the literature emphasizes on the necessity for constant re-alignment of HOT components [35]. In fact, the alignment has to be dynamically adapted due to constant technological changes and evolving needs of the organization [35]. The literature also points out the nuances and complex interactions between HOT components and recommends further research to explore these interactions as well as their implications for emerging technologies and workplace dynamics [35, 38].

The technology-organization-environment (TOE) model

The TOE model focuses on technological, organizational and environmental factors for modeling the technology adoption and innovation within organizations [39]. Technological factors encompass the key characteristics of a given technology, including the complexity of the technology and its compatibility with existing systems [40, 41]. The technological factors relate to internal organizational characteristics and capabilities such as absorptive capacity, leadership and a culture of innovation [40]. The environmental factors include the elements that are external to the organization, but crucial in shaping technology adoption in an organization such as industry regulations, pressure from competitors and market trends [40]. Studies have shown the relevance of the TOE model, highlighting specific elements that influence technology adoption such as connectivity, IT capabilities and management support [40, 41].

Innovation dissemination theory (IDT)

The IDT theory explains the success of an information system in terms of gradual technology adoption. According to this theory, the extent of technology adoption follows a diffusion process and communicated over time throughout members of an organization [42]. In the context of an IS, an innovation is defined as a technology that is perceived as new by its users, even if it has been long on the market, and it is manifested through their knowledge, persuasion and decisions [42]. Thus, the key point of IDT model is one of achieving convergence in understanding and adopting the technology through a framework of information sharing where change agents and opinion leaders can play significant influence towards desired practices [42, 43]. This approach of adoption depends on five main factors: perceived attributes of innovation, type of innovation-decision, communication channels, nature of the social system, and extent of promotion agents’ efforts [43].

User satisfaction and individual performance

The measurement of net benefits in terms of individual or organization impact, as results of system’s qualities, use and user satisfaction, has been advocated for. For instance, D&M have advocated for this measurement, cautioning researchers not to limit their investigations on use and user satisfaction as measures of IS success, but also extending them towards benefits realized in terms of performance [18]. Therefore, measuring the impact of user satisfaction on individual performance is essential and add value to the existing literature on IS success.

IS impact to individual performance is defined as the extent to which the individual perceives the level of productivity due to the use of IS [44]. While many studies found positive impact through increased productivity in both quantity and quality, some studies reported negative or neutral effects on individual performance and users’ social life [11, 22, 44, 45].

Critical analysis and conclusion

The D&M and subsequent models intended to associate IS qualities with user satisfaction or technology adoption in terms of correlation relationship of dependent and independent variables. However, the intuitive nature of the relationship appears to be more internal, shaped by individual or organizational dynamics rather than driven by external factors. When a user expresses appreciation or difficulties regarding aspects of an IS such as ease of use or usefulness of the information it generates, this reflects their satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the system’s use or usefulness. Therefore, this study will focus on exploring the reasons, issues and challenges as well as characteristics underlying a certain level of satisfaction rather than understanding it in terms of correlation and causality.

Most studies conducted are quantitative, exploring the relationship between user satisfaction and the IS qualities through a number of dependent and independent variables [19]. However, understanding challenges and issues with user satisfaction might not be limited to parametric models and pre-defined variables [18, 28]. In addition, there is a divergence on how IS quality variables are measured and critics on their reliability [11]. This implies that research must give the flow to the users to express their concerns as measures of IS qualities. Thus, this study will integrate a qualitative component to enhance the understanding of the challenges healthcare providers face while using the system. Therefore, the D&M and other models that subsequently emerged were adapted in the conceptual framework of this study as illustrated in the Fig. 3 below:

Conceptual model adapted from DeLone and McLean and subsequent IS models

D&M recommended researchers to select a small number of measurements while using their model [18, 19]. However, many of the quantitative studies conducted generally used long questionnaires and many questions had similarities, which led to confusion in distinguishing variables [17–19, 23, 24, 46]. This study used a concise questionnaire with a single question addressing user satisfaction and another assessing the perceived impact of the system on users’ work performance. In this study, the aspects of IS qualities and other issues characterizing user satisfaction and how they affect the individual performance were obtained through qualitative questions. This enabled discerning user judgment, minimizing impulse responses and encouraging a more natural flow of the expression for the qualitative component of the questionnaire.

Summary and gap in the literature

The D&M model has been instrumental in explaining IS success through user satisfaction and various quality dimensions of the IS. In response to critiques on the D&M model, subsequent research has been conducted using a variety of other models to explore other factors that affect IS success including perceived ease of use and usefulness of IS, trustworthiness, top management support, design of user requirement, training, computer literacy, organizational structure and management style [26–30]. Despite a multitude of models, none is claimed to exhaustively explain all the elements of an IS success. However, qualitative studies can effectively capture a wide range of IS characteristics underlying the status of IS success taking into account the specificity of organizations, although this approach remains under-explored. Thus, this study uses a mixed research approach to take advantage of the benefits of both quantitative and qualitative approaches to analyze the extent of an IS success as well as the underlying issues in the context of a specific hospital.

Methodology

Study design

This study was a cross-sectional mixed research method using explanatory embedded design in order to get a deep understanding of key issues affecting OpenClinic user satisfaction and individual performance. The quantitative studies have been useful and extensively used in studies exploring information systems success, but criticized for their underlying assumptions [47]. However, qualitative studies remain limited, yet they have been advocated for their strengths in identifying virtues in their natural context with minimal or no assumptions [47].

The combination of qualitative and quantitative approaches is necessary for studying IS success. Some of the key benefits of this combination are: the value of combining qualitative and quantitative methods is superior to that of using either method alone, as it enables for a more nuanced understanding of context-specific nature and practices within organizations rather than relying solely on standardized, context-independent variables and assumptions. It accounts for the natural processes experienced by users while evaluating information systems, and can capture the multi-dimensional relationships between an IS and user perceptions rather than unidirectional assumptions. This enriches the body of knowledge by bringing in diversity in system research [47–49].

As consequence, this study opted to integrate the two approaches in order to take advantage of each. The purpose of quantitative component was to enable measuring the level of OpenClinic user satisfaction, the level of its perceived impact on individual performance, and the relationship between user satisfaction and perceived users’ performance [50, 51]. On the other hand, the qualitative component was aimed at providing deeper insights into issues affecting user satisfaction and its impact on individual performance [47].

Sample selection

The sampling frame of this study was the list of staff who use OpenClinic at the CHUK. A convenient sample of 217 staff was used for this study, drawn from various service departments of the hospital. It included the main categories of healthcare professionals administered by the hospital including physicians, nurses, allied health professionals and administration staff. This sampling has proved to be effective in exploring information systems success [52].

Data collection and analysis procedure

The data were collected personally by the principal investigator. Prior to data collection, appointments were arranged during the departmental staff meetings. The questionnaires were distributed and collected during these meetings with flexibility allowed for participants to submit them afterward. During the data collection, the investigator provided questionnaire and explained the study to the participants, ensuring that they understand its importance and voluntarily agree to participate. Thereafter, the participants who accepted to participate in the study signed a hard copy of the consent form and completed a self-registered questionnaire.

A self-registered questionnaire was used to collect data from the participants (Ref. Appendix 2 and Appendix 3). This questionnaire was designed specifically for this study based on research questions. The first section of the questionnaire consisted of closed questions inspired by Likert measurement framework and was focused on collecting quantitative data [53]. The second section of the questionnaire consisted of open ended questions and was focused on qualitative data.

Data analysis techniques

An electronic Epi-Info data entry platform was used for data entry of quantitative data. The quantitative data was analyzed using “R” software whereas qualitative data was analysed using “NVivo 12” software.

The quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and statistical tests including Chi-square, Fisher exact and Wald tests. The descriptive statistics provides the proportions of user satisfaction and perceived impact of the system by its users. The Chi-square test provides the significance of the relationship between user satisfaction and perceived impact of the system. Fisher exact test was used to validate the Chi-square findings due to the low absolute numbers of dissatisfied users, and to assess the strength of the association. In addition, the Wald test was used to provide the magnitude and significance of the relationship between user satisfaction and perceived impact of the system and evaluating the consistence of previous results. The analysis of user satisfaction and perceived impact across demographic variables were not undertaken due to presence of empty or very low cell counts in the cross-tabulation of dissatisfaction.

The qualitative data was analyzed using content analysis. The participants’ expressions were grouped and coded into themes and sub-themes over the process of analysis using NVivo12. When participants’ expressions lacked clarity for matching or elaborating the related theme, clarifications were sought from the participants.

The Chi-square, Fisher exact and Wald tests were performed to analyze the significance as well as the strength of association between user satisfaction and perceived performance (ref. Appendix 1). Both Chi-square and Fisher tests confirmed the significance of the association with p-values less than 0.001, whereas the Fisher and Wald tests indicated a high strength of association through odds ratio values.

The Fisher’s test odds ratio value was 20.5 (CI: 5.3 85.1), while the Wald test odds ratio value was 21.2 (CI: 6.3, 71.1). Thus, the respondents who were satisfied were 20 times more likely to have a positive perception of the EMR (OpenClinic) on their performance than those who were not satisfied.

Participants were asked to explain the sources of dissatisfaction with the system and the reasons thereof. Despite the high percentage of users who expressed overall satisfaction, a significant majority (98%) also identified one or more issues that resulted to their dissatisfaction. The main issues for dissatisfaction are the following:

Poor functionality or dysfunctionality due to unstable network or electricity: Participants explained that sometimes, there were issues of internet connectivity and interruption of electricity, which negatively affected the functionality of the system. An example of this explanation is: “When there is no electricity or internet connection, the work stops, but it’s not always”, said the respondent (ID NM012).

Results discussion

Overall, the implementation of OpenClinic for healthcare provision in the CHUK has been successful. This study revealed that a high proportion of employees (91%) were satisfied with the use of OpenClinic. According to D&M and many other models, this is an indication of information system success [13, 10, 14]. In addition, these findings serve as evaluation feedback to the hospital leadership indicating that the implementing of EMR is on track. On the other hand, in general and particularly in developing counties, the literature shows that many systems fail [1, 3–5]. Thus, the operationalization of OpenClinic in the CHUK can serve as a lesson to interested parties for successful implementation of IS in healthcare settings.

The level of satisfaction and its benefits identified throughout Likert-scale grades is not sufficient in characterizing user satisfaction and success [54]. In fact, although 91% were ranked as very or just satisfied through a 5 point Likert-scale in this study, there were even more employees (98%) who expressed some dissatisfactions through qualitative open-ended questions. Therefore, an IS user who provides an overall score of good satisfaction can still have specific concerns of dissatisfaction which would need to be addressed in order to optimize success in IS implementation.

The key drivers of OpenClinic success in the CHUK can arguably reflect the main causes of user satisfaction stipulated by D&M and other IS success models, but the self-expressed appraisals are more specific and clear for understanding success in the context of the hospital. Thus, the reliability of system functionalities, its ease of use, its quick performance, its role in patient data protection and confidentiality, the shifts from paper-based to digital system, its appreciation in time management, and its ability for facilitating and simplifying employees work can reflect the system, information and service qualities as well as its usefulness, acceptability and trustworthiness. However, the self-expressed appraisals identified by the employees are more self-explanatory to understand the reason behind their user satisfaction and IS benefits.

Although the identified triggers of satisfaction might be implicitly multi-matched with suggested models of IS success, especially the D&M model, they are also mitigated by self-expressed dissatisfactions equally identifiable with those models. Thus, the factors such as expressed poor functionality or dysfunctionality due to unstable network or electricity, limited capacity of use due to lack or insufficient training, limited computers, lack of flexibility due to difficulties in correcting errors, incomplete digitization, the perception of its induced high workload, exposure of patients’ info, inconsistent diagnosis coding and many other issues addressed by users can equally be negatively multi-paired with the key components of IS success models through user satisfaction including system quality, information quality, service quality and usefulness of the system, its acceptability and trustworthiness. This suggest that user satisfaction would be evaluated holistically, discerning both positive and negative aspects of satisfaction.

The perceived impact of OpenClinic was expressed by users in terms of efficiency and effectiveness in work performance, which is the purpose of an IS [2]. The positive side of this impact was specifically explained as higher speed of services, improved service quality, increased number (volume) of beneficiaries who receive healthcare services, increased revenue recovery, efficient management and use of resources, and continuity of patient care. The negative side of the impact was expressed at limited extend as delays in service delivery manly due to the interruption of work caused by unreliable internet connection, limited capacity of use, insufficiency of equipment and high workload. Thus, the success of OpenClinic has been achieved beyond satisfaction, but would be more improved if the causes of expressed delays in service delivery are addressed.

The inter-relationship between the components of IS success stipulated in the D&M model was even observed through the interchangeable responses of participants between user satisfaction components and perceived impact. For instance, according to D&M model, the user satisfaction would result in net benefits, but the net benefits would also result in user satisfaction [18]. Through this analogy, some participants expressed interchangeably the reasons for their satisfaction as the impact that the system has on their work such as being satisfied by improved speed or efficiency of services, while others expressed the perceived impact as sources of their satisfaction such as the appraisal of its functionalities and its role in their work performance.

Some of dissatisfaction addressed by users might not have been real challenges, but would have been due to limited capacity of knowledge and use. For instance, according to the system administrators at CHUK, some issues such as the inability to work simultaneously with multiple files and missing of some information in the system would be due to limited capacity of users. In addition, addressing some of the functionalities according to the wishes of the users might cause other challenges which may be difficult to address. This is for example the case of frequent password expiration, difficulties in editing and correcting errors and limitation of access to some data which, if addressed differently, would compromise data security and confidentiality. Furthermore, some challenges are beyond the feasibility by the hospital such as the interoperability with the systems of district hospitals which would require the roll out of the system in these hospitals, while others are difficult to address due to financial constraints and management capacity such as having sufficient computers for every user.

The results of this study highlighted the positive relationship between user satisfaction and individual performance. Consequently, satisfied users were much more likely to have a positive perception of the system on their performance than unsatisfied users. This adds to the many existing literature on IS user satisfaction and individual performance, motivation and wellbeing [20–25, 55–57]. This study also adds to few existing studies that used mixed method research design and this enabled a deeper understanding of the relationship to identify key aspects of user satisfaction as well as IS benefits.

Conclusion

This study reviewed the extent to which the implementation of electronic medical record (EMR) system has been successful at the “Centre Hospitalier Universitaire de Kigali (CHUK)”. The implementation was found to be successful due to high level of overall user satisfaction (91%) and the high proportion of users who felt it to be beneficial (94%) through its perceived positive impact. However, the users also addressed important points of concerns as dissatisfaction which need to be reviewed and addressed appropriately by the hospital leadership for further improvements in service delivery and enhanced patient outcomes. This study also provides insights relevant to the wide audience interested in healthcare information systems management by taking advantage of using both quantitative and qualitative approaches to analyze IS success and issues in the context of healthcare facilities, thus adding on a few existing literatures of this kind.

This study exposed the relevance of considering the constructs as integral aspects of user satisfaction in the IS success model. This study also highlighted the necessity and importance of integrating qualitative approach in research on IS user satisfaction and benefits. In particular, a high level of overall user satisfaction does not preclude the existence of high dissatisfaction on specific aspects of an IS, indicating the need for integration of a qualitative approach to comprehensively understand issues of IS user satisfaction and benefits. Future research using qualitative or mixed approach are recommended, and may explore specific areas such as workflow integration or system usability.

Limitations and recommendations for further studies

The findings from this study suggest the need for using mixed research methods to evaluate information systems’ success. In fact, it is necessary to quantify the overall satisfaction and benefits, but equally important to understand challenges and issues addressed by users. The key aspect in favor of mixed research is the fact that a high level of user satisfaction and perceived benefits does not preclude the existence of many challenges and issues experienced by users which constitute dissatisfaction and hindrance to their performance. Therefore, the inclusion of qualitative component in evaluating information systems’ success brings in a holistic review of both positive and negative aspects of user satisfaction and IS benefits.

This study used a convenient sample of healthcare providers which is prone to selection bias and limits the generalizability of results. This sampling method was used due to challenges with availability and accessibility of the participants who have a busy schedule and work on different shifts of the day. In order to mitigate the selection bias, the sample was enlarged to 217 participants selected from different departmental services of the hospital.

This study assessed the impact of OpenClinic usage on the performance of healthcare providers. However, the assessment was only limited to the identification of what the healthcare providers believe to be the positive and negative impact of the system on their work performance and not a strict experimental impact assessment, which limit the degree of reliability of the expressed impact.

Although this study highlighted important aspects of dissatisfaction and improvements needed, it did not intend to make an objective evaluation of system dysfunctionalities. Thus, the findings elaborated the sources of dissatisfaction and the actions that might be taken to improve based on user opinions and some of the suggestions might not be relevant or easily applicable. Therefore, the hospital is recommended to conduct a deep review of the sources of dissatisfaction expressed as well as recommended improvements, analyze the feasibility of suggested improvements and take appropriate measures to ensure the optimal operationalization of its EMR system.
