Relational Artificial Intelligence as a Developmental Exposure in Adolescence: A Precautionary Perspectives and Controversies Article
Authors: Christopher Rhyss Edwards
Published: 2026

Adolescent mental health is increasingly recognized as a defining public health challenge of the digital era. One in 7 young people aged 10 to 19 years lives with a diagnosable mental health condition, reflecting a substantial and growing burden of disease during adolescence.1,2 Parallel increases in digital media exposure have prompted renewed scrutiny of how emerging technologies shape developmental environments. Over the past decade, social media shifted from a communication tool to a potential public health exposure, with ongoing debate regarding its association with adolescent well-being.3 Population-level analyses have also highlighted broader shifts in adolescent mental health trajectories across the digital era, although interpretations remain contested.4 A new generation of relational artificial intelligence (AI) systems—conversational and companion-oriented systems—may represent the next transition.

Relational AI systems encompass both conversational platforms (eg, ChatGPT), primarily designed to assist with information seeking, learning, and everyday dialog, and companion-oriented platforms (eg, Replika), which are explicitly structured to simulate friendship, emotional support, or romantic connection through sustained interaction. These systems rely on persistent dialog, adaptive language generation, and varying degrees of conversational memory to maintain continuity across exchanges. Unlike earlier chatbots that responded to isolated prompts, contemporary relational AI systems often adopt personas, mirror emotional tone, and encourage ongoing engagement. Adolescents are among the fastest adopters, frequently using conversational systems for emotional disclosure, identity exploration, and social connection.5 For many young people, these technologies function not merely as tools but also as relational environments. However, their rapid diffusion has outpaced longitudinal evidence regarding developmental safety, raising questions about whether widespread adolescent exposure should be understood as a population-level health risk rather than solely a technological innovation. Emerging reports describing suicides associated with chatbot engagement, emotional dependency, and AI-reinforced manic or delusional ideation have drawn increasing attention from clinicians and researchers.6 These cases do not establish causal relationships between AI use and mental health outcomes. However, public health responses rarely wait for definitive proof when exposure is widespread and the population affected is developmentally vulnerable. Adolescence represents a period of heightened social sensitivity and ongoing neurodevelopment, during which digital environments can shape identity formation, emotional regulation, and perceptions of support.7 Technologies that simulate social relationships therefore warrant particular scrutiny.

This Perspectives and Controversies article argues that adolescent exposure to relational AI systems should be understood through a public health lens. We propose that relational AI systems constitute a new category of developmental exposure, characterized by sustained simulated relationships rather than discrete technological interactions. Drawing on developmental science, sociotechnical risk analysis, and regulatory precedents such as Australia’s social media age restrictions, we suggest that relational AI may warrant age-appropriate design limits or limited deployment during adolescence. If adolescent mental health is a defining challenge of the digital era, then relational AI systems must be evaluated not only as innovation but also as a potential population-level exposure requiring the same caution applied to other emerging public health risks.

Relational AI Systems and Adolescent Development

People respond socially to technology. Early research found that individuals attribute social qualities to digital interfaces, even when interactions are clearly artificial.8 Parasocial interaction theory further explains how mediated characters can evoke feelings of intimacy and companionship despite 1-sided communication.9 Relational AI systems extend these dynamics by enabling continuous dialog, memory, and emotional simulation.

For adolescents, whose cognitive and emotional systems are still developing, such relational immersion may carry unique implications. Adolescence is marked by heightened sensitivity to social feedback and ongoing identity formation.7 Digital environments influence how young people perceive validation, belonging, and emotional support. Longitudinal and cohort-based research suggests that digital media exposure interacts with developmental context in complex ways, with both potential benefits and risks depending on individual and environmental factors.10 Research on social media demonstrates complex associations with mental health outcomes, highlighting both potential benefits and risks.3 Relational AI systems differ from social media in an important respect: they replace peer interaction with algorithmically mediated dialog. Instead of navigating unpredictable social relationships, adolescents engage with systems optimized for responsiveness and engagement. Constant availability may feel supportive, particularly during periods of loneliness. However, from a public health perspective, persistent relational immersion raises questions about dependency, emotional calibration, and developmental substitution. Scholars have observed that even earlier digital technologies facilitated a retreat from the friction inherent in human connection toward the apparent comfort of machine-mediated interaction—a dynamic that relational AI now extends into far more intimate territory.11

Clinical Signals and Emerging Risk Pathways

Public health frameworks often treat rare but severe incidents as early indicators of broader risk pathways. Recent clinical and media reports describing adverse outcomes in the context of intensive engagement with relational AI systems should be interpreted as early clinical signals rather than evidence of causation, highlighting the need for careful longitudinal investigation. Nonetheless, such cases highlight scenarios in which relational design may interact with psychological vulnerability. Debate persists regarding the magnitude and direction of digital media effects on adolescent well-being, underscoring the importance of cautious interpretation of emerging signals.4,10

Research has also raised concerns about conversational models reinforcing maladaptive beliefs. Because large language models generate responses that mirror user input, emotionally charged narratives may be validated rather than challenged. Studies examining relational AI systems in mental health contexts have identified instances of inappropriate responses or reinforcement of stigma.12 Emerging observations that relational AI systems may inadvertently mirror or intensify maladaptive cognitive patterns raise clinically relevant questions about how simulated empathy interacts with vulnerability during periods of developmental and psychological instability. Population-level risk is shaped not only by technology but also by patterns of use. Adolescents experiencing social isolation, neurodivergence, or limited access to mental health resources may rely more heavily on relational AI systems. For these young people, relational AI systems may serve as substitutes for unavailable support networks. Public health analysis emphasizes that the same features that increase accessibility may also concentrate exposure among those most vulnerable.

Policy Precedents and the Public Health Framing of Digital Technology

Recent regulatory developments suggest growing recognition of digital environments as public health exposures. Australia’s Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act 202413 introduced age-based restrictions, requiring platforms to prevent access for users under 16 years. Policymakers cited concerns about mental health, addictive design, and algorithmic amplification affecting young people, although debate continues regarding proportionality and potential unintended consequences. Crucially, the legislation placed responsibility on platforms rather than adolescents, reframing safety as an infrastructural design issue. The Australian case illustrates how age-based restrictions can function as public health interventions rather than moral prohibitions. Platforms must redesign onboarding processes and interaction models to comply, reflecting a shift toward systemic accountability. Framing digital environments as modifiable determinants of adolescent health aligns with broader global health strategies that emphasize prevention during sensitive developmental periods.2 Relational AI systems introduce an even more intimate form of digital interaction. If algorithmic social environments are increasingly understood as developmental exposures, relational AI systems may warrant comparable scrutiny.

Relational Asymmetry and Developmental Exposure

We propose the concept of relational asymmetry to describe interactions in which relational AI systems simulate emotional presence without genuine vulnerability or accountability. Human relationships involve mutual obligation and shared risk; relational AI systems do not. Yet, their design encourages users to interpret interactions as socially meaningful. For adolescents, relational asymmetry may influence expectations about intimacy and support. Continuous validation without reciprocal responsibility could shape how young people understand trust, disagreement, and emotional boundaries. Traditional digital safety frameworks focus on harmful content, but relational AI systems introduce risks embedded within interaction structure itself. Exposure occurs through sustained conversational engagement rather than discrete harmful messages. Public health perspectives emphasize cumulative exposure. Just as repeated engagement with social media can shape behavioral patterns over time, prolonged interaction with relational AI systems may influence emotional development in ways that remain poorly understood.

Precaution In The Face of Uncertainty

Public health responses to emerging exposures often operate under conditions of incomplete evidence, prioritizing risk mitigation when potential harms coincide with periods of heightened developmental vulnerability. Adolescent health frameworks increasingly emphasize prevention and early risk mitigation when exposures coincide with periods of heightened neurodevelopmental sensitivity.1 Age restrictions on alcohol and tobacco were introduced before long-term epidemiological data were complete, based on plausible risk and developmental vulnerability. Relational AI systems present similar conditions: rapid adoption, limited longitudinal research, and early signals of potential harm. A precautionary approach does not imply permanent prohibition. Rather, it supports proportionate measures while evidence evolves. Potential strategies include restricting companion-style features for minors, limiting persistent conversational memory, or delaying access to relational AI systems until adulthood. Such measures align with broader trends toward platform accountability and developmental protection. Advocates may argue that relational AI systems provide accessible emotional support. These benefits deserve careful evaluation. However, public health decisions often weigh uncertain benefits against plausible harms. When exposure occurs during adolescence—a sensitive developmental window —caution may be justified.

Implications for Digital Health Research and Policy

Reframing relational AI systems as a public health exposure shifts research priorities. Longitudinal studies examining developmental outcomes should become central to evaluation. Metrics such as perceived intimacy, dependency, and emotional regulation may be as important as traditional measures of usability or efficiency. Policy discussions may also evolve toward age-based design interventions. Delaying exposure during adolescence mirrors established public health strategies aimed at protecting sensitive developmental periods. Such approaches do not reject technological innovation; they recognize that innovation carries responsibility toward population well-being.

Conclusion

If adolescent mental health is a defining public health challenge of the digital era, then relational AI systems should be understood as a new developmental exposure requiring the same level of scrutiny applied to earlier digital environments. By transforming digital interaction into simulated companionship, relational AI intersects directly with the processes through which adolescents form identity, regulate emotion, and learn to relate to others. Evidence remains incomplete, but emerging clinical signals and evolving regulatory precedents indicate that widespread adolescent exposure warrants careful evaluation. Public health history suggests that waiting for certainty can allow preventable harm to scale. Reframing relational AI as a developmental exposure shifts the central question from how engaging these systems can become to whether widespread adolescent exposure is developmentally safe. Technologies designed to imitate relationships must meet a higher standard of safety before becoming embedded within adolescent developmental environments. Precaution in this context is not resistance to innovation—it is the recognition that the same public health caution applied to previous emerging exposures must now be brought to bear on systems that place simulated companionship at the center of adolescent social life.
