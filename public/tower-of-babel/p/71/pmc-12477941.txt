Glyphosate below no-observed-adverse-effect level exacerbates fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome in laying hens
Authors: Jingbo Liu, Guozheng Sun, Hongna Zhang, Hui Liu, Xiaohan Wang, Zengmin Miao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlyphosate (GLY) can disturb gut microbiota homeostasis through inhibiting bacterial shikimate pathway to pose a health challenge to animals and humans. But little information was reported about the influence of GLY on fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) in laying hens, especially the exposure below no-observed-adverse-effect level. This study aimed to investigate the effects of GLY exposure on the progression of FLHS using a laying hen FLHS model induced by high-energy low-protein diet (HELPD). Ninety healthy Hyline brown laying hens were randomly allocated into 3 groups and subjected to the following feed for 6 weeks: Control group (fed with a basal diet), FLHS group (fed with HELPD), and FLHS + GLY group [fed with HELPD containing GLY (equal to 47 mg/kg body weight/d)]. Fresh feces were collected for microbiome and metabolome analysis, as well as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments. The liver was collected for differentially expressed gene analysis and the colon for gut permeability determination. The results revealed that GLY exposure aggravated liver damage and lipid deposition by reshaping gut microbiota and increasing gut permeability in FLHS hens. Specifically, GLY exposure significantly elevated the relative abundance of Escherichia-Shigella, Bacteroides, Erysipelothrix, Desulfobvibrio, and Eggerthellain, while markedly decreased the relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, thus instigating the decline of certain tryptophan metabolites, including 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid, 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid, kynurenic acid, 4-(2-aminophenyl)-2,4-dioxobutanoic acid, tryptamine, and indole-3-carboxylic acid in the gut, which inhibited aryl hydrocarbon receptor pathway to impair gut integrity. The impaired gut barrier led to the increase of serum LPS level, triggering inflammation and oxidative stress in the liver via activating TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway and inhibiting Nrf2/Keap1 pathway. Furthermore, FMT experiments substantiated that GLY exposure aggravated FLHS in a gut microbiota-dependent manner. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GLY exposure aggravates the progression of FLHS in laying hens through modulating the gut-liver axis. This study provides theoretical basis for assessing adverse effects of GLY exposure below no-observed-adverse-effect level on animal welfare.Keywords: Glyphosate, Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome, Gut microbiota, Gut-liver axis, Laying hens

Introduction

Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) is a complex metabolic disorder, with the characteristics of excessive lipid accumulation and obvious hemorrhagic spots in the liver (Zhu et al., 2022). This disease commonly occurs in caged high-production laying hens with a morbidity rate ranging from 5 % to 30 % (Wang et al., 2023b). It is reported that FLHS is the main factor causing non-infectious deaths in laying hens (Shini et al., 2019). Furthermore, laying hens with FLHS display a significant decrease in laying rate and a notable reduction in egg-laying peak period, imposing considerable economic losses on laying hen industry (Wu et al., 2024). Previous study has indicated that the etiological factors of FLHS include improper diets, genetic variations, environmental changes, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and other triggers (Meng et al., 2021). Notably, recent studies have demonstrated that environmental influences and imbalances in gut microbiota are key contributors to the development of FLHS (Hamid et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023a; Yang et al., 2023). Moreover, increasing evidence has substantiated that environmental factors, including chemical pollutants, contribute to the imbalance of gut microbiota (Burgos-Aceves et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Rio et al., 2024), suggesting that environmental pollutants may impact the FLHS progression through disturbing gut microbiota homeostasis.

As one of the common environmental pollutants globally, glyphosate (GLY) serves as an active component of widely used herbicides, applying for weed control and crop desiccant (Benbrook, 2016). Due to its extensive use, GLY is typically detected in post-harvested crops (Duke, 2018). The residual concentrations of GLY are reported for 0.1-100 mg/kg in soybeans, 0.1-25 mg/kg in cereals, 0.1-28 mg/kg in oil seeds, and 1-344 mg/kg in different types of fodder (Bohn et al., 2014; Cetin et al., 2017; Cuhra, 2015; Gélinas et al., 2018), suggesting that livestock and poultry are directly suffered from GLY exposure. The herbicidal action mechanism of GLY attributes to its suppression of 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase, interrupting the shikimate pathway and then killing weed (Mesnage et al., 2015). This pathway is not present in vertebrates, but it exists in bacteria, archaea, fungi, and algae (Serra et al., 2021b), suggesting a mechanism by which GLY exposure negatively affects animal and human health through disturbing the microbiota. A current review reports that GLY exposure triggers gut microbiota dysbiosis (such as increased abundance of Salmonella spp. and Clostridium spp., and decreased abundance of Lactobacillus spp., Bifidobacterium spp., and Enterococcus spp.), as well as gut microbiota-mediated metabolite changes (e.g. carnitine, methylhistamine, butyrylcarnitine, perillic acid, indolacrylic acid, etc.), in different animals (Ignácio et al., 2024). Moreover, broiler hens exposed to GLY showed the disturbance of the cecal microbiota and short-chain fatty acid composition (Fréville et al., 2022). However, few studies are available for the relevance of GLY-caused gut microbiota dysbiosis and related microbiota-derived metabolite alterations on the pathogenesis of FLHS in laying hens.

Several investigations have demonstrated that GLY exposure is linked to liver damage in various animal models and cell lines (Lian et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the views regarding GLY-mediated liver damage for poultry remain controversial. Previous study has indicated that, after long-term GLY exposure lower than a no-observed-adverse-effect level [NOAEL; 100 mg/kg body weight/d for poultry reported by European Food Safety Authority (EFSA)], GLY primarily deposits into the liver of Japanese quails; However, it does not significantly change liver weight (Ruuskanen et al., 2020). Moreover, parental exposure to GLY with the concentration of 46.8 mg/kg body weight/d for 6 weeks does not obviously change the behavior or metabolism for both male and female chicken offspring (Estienne et al., 2022; Serra et al., 2021a). It is worth noting that no significant growth alterations of broiler hens are observed after GLY exposure at the concentration of 47 mg/kg body weight/d (Fréville et al., 2022). Based on the action mechanism of GLY on non-targeted animals and the contribution of gut dysbiosis to the pathogenesis of FLHS, we speculated that GLY exposure can promote the development of FLHS through disordering gut microbiota and related metabolites.

To address this, we established a laying hens FLHS model induced by high-energy low-protein diet (HELPD) to investigate the impacts of GLY exposure on FLHS. The integrative multi-omics analysis revealed that GLY exposure resulted in the gut microbiota-mediated tryptophan metabolite reduction, instigating the gut barrier impairment and causing serum LPS level increase, which enhanced hepatic inflammation and oxidative stress via activating TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway and inhibiting Nrf2/Keap1 pathway in FLHS hens. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments substantiated GLY-exacerbated FLHS in a gut microbiota-dependent manner. This study reveals that GLY exposure exacerbates liver diseases progression via disturbing gut microbiota homeostasis.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

All animal welfare and experimental protocols were carried out in accordance with the guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established in China. The study received approval from the Experimental Animal Ethics of Taishan University (Permit no. 2024051003, approved on May 10, 2024).

Animal treatments

First, the total sample size was determined to be at least 66 laying hens using G*power software (v. 3.1.9.7, https://www.psychologie.hhu.de/arbeitsgruppen/allgemeine-psychologie-und-arbeitspsychologie/gpower) based on an effect size of 0.40, a Type I error probability of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.80. Considering a 10 % dropout rate, the total sample size was finally set at 90.

A total of 90 healthy Hyline brown laying hens (32 weeks old) with similar laying rate (82.638 %) were obtained from the local hen farm. According to our previous study (Zhou et al., 2025), these hens raised in an enclosed chicken house at conditions of the temperature of 19-25°C, a relative humidity of 40 %−60 %, and the lighting program of 16 h light/8 h dark, and admitted to diet and water ad libitum during the acclimation and trial periods. The outline of the experiment was list in Fig. 1A. After 2-week acclimation, these chickens were randomly allocated into 3 groups and subjected to the following feed for 6 weeks: Control group (fed with a basal diet), FLHS group (fed with HELPD), and FLHS + GLY group [fed with HELPD containing GLY (corresponding to 47 mg equivalent/kg body weight/d)]. Each group contained 10 replicates and each replicate included 3 laying hens (3 animals/cage). The compositions of basal diet and HELPD were presented in Table S1 according to previously described (Qiu et al., 2021). The GLY concentration used in this study was based on previous report (Fréville et al., 2022). In the 6th week of the experiment, egg numbers, egg weight, and ADFI were recorded daily. The ratio of feed to egg was worked out.

Effects of GLY exposure on laying performance in FLHS laying hens. (A) The flowchart of experimental design. (B) Laying rate (%). (C) Egg weight (g). (D) ADFI (g/d/hen). (E) Feed/Egg ratio. Data were presented as mean ± SD (n = 7). ANOVA was used to analyze data among three groups. P < 0.05 showed statistical significance (* or # indicates p < 0.05; ** indicates p < 0.01; ns indicates p > 0.05).

Following a 6-week regimen, fresh feces were collected for microbiome analysis and FMT experiments. And then, laying hens were fasted for 12 h and euthanized via cutting the jugular vein for blood collection. Serum samples were collected from each hen after centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 10 min and then analyzed for markers related to liver injury, lipid metabolism, and intestinal barrier function. The liver and colon tissues were collected and part of the liver tissue of each hen was fixed in 4 % formaldehyde for Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Oil red O staining, and the other part was used for further analysis.

FMT experiments

The FMT experiments were designed as per previous studies with minor modification (Liu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023). In brief, fresh feces from the above three groups were immediately collected and transferred into sterile tubes after removing the while fraction of the feces. Then, 10 g of collected feces was added into the tube with 50 mL of sterile PBS and homogenized through the vortex oscillator. Subsequently, the mixture was filtered through a piece of sterile gauze to eliminate small particles, yielding a fecal suspension. After the centrifugation at 5 000 × g for 20 min, the supernatant was collected and combined with 25 % sterile glycerol before storage at −80°C. Prior to FMT experiments, the suspension was thawed and diluted with sterile PBS to a concentration of 1 × 10⁹ colony-forming units/mL for oral gavage at room temperature.

A total of 90 healthy Hyline brown laying hens (32 weeks old) were used to investigate the impact of GLY exposure on FLHS via modulation of the gut microbiota. Following a 2-week acclimation period, all hens received an antibiotic cocktail (Abx) in their drinking water for 1 week—containing neomycin (1 g/L), ampicillin (1 g/L), metronidazole (1 g/L), and vancomycin (0.5 g/L)—followed by 1 week of normal water to eliminate any residual antibiotic effects. The hens were then randomly assigned into 3 groups: namely as FMT-Control group, FMT-FLHS group, and FMT-(FLHS + GLY) group, respectively. Subsequently, each laying hen was gavaged by 1 mL of corresponding bacterial suspension once a day for 4 weeks. During the period of FMT experiments, laying hens were received normal diet and water ad libitum. At the end of the experiments, the serum and liver were collected for further analysis.

Biochemical measurement of serum and liver samples

The serum contents of total protein (TP, A045-2-2), albumin (ALB, A028-1-1), aspartate transaminase (AST, C010-2-1), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, C009-2-1), triglycerides (TG, A110-2-1), total cholesterol (TC, A111-2-1), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, A112-2-1), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, A113-2-1), malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-1-2), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, A015-2-1), superoxide dismutase (SOD, A001-3-2), catalase (CAT, A007-1-1), IL-1β (H002-1-2), IL-6 (H007-1-2), IL-10 (H009-1-2), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, H052-1-2) were measured using commercial assay kits following the manufacturer's instructions (Jiancheng Biotechnology Institute, Nanjing, China). Serum lipopolysaccharide (LPS, C0276S) levels were quantified using an ELISA kit according to the manufacturer's guidelines (Beyotime Biotechnology, Beijing, China). Each assay was carried out at least three times.

The supernatant of liver tissues was obtained though centrifugation after homogenization. The levels of TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, MDA, T-AOC, SOD, and CAT in the liver were also measured using commercial kits following the manufacturer's instructions (Jiancheng Biotechnology Institute, Nanjing, China). All assays were conducted at least triplicate.

Liver and colon pathohistological observation

The parts of fresh samples were fixed in 4 % formaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded liver tissues were then sectioned into 5 μm thick slices. After H&E staining, each slice was visualized and imaged using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Liver Oil Red O staining

After dehydration, the fixed liver samples were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (G6059, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and cut into 10 μm slices at −35°C using a freezing microtome (Leica CM1950, Shanghai, China). Then, each slice was stained by Oil Red O (G1015, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and then visualized and imaged using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Liver RNA sequencing and transcriptome analysis

The liver RNA was extracted by RNAiso Plus (9109, Takala, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were estimated using a MicroDrop spectrophotometer (BIO-DL Corporation, Shanghai, China) and RNA integrity was assessed through a Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA). mRNA was captured using a RiboMinus™ kit (K155002, Thermo Fisher, Shanghai) and then split into 200-300 bp fragments using RNA fragment reagents (AM8740, Thermo Fisher, Shanghai). A total of 5 μg RNA was inversely transcribed into cDNA, and generated cDNA libraries using a CloneMiner™ II kit (A11180, Thermo Fisher, Shanghai). Subsequently, the libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

Raw sequencing reads were processed by removing adapter sequences, poly-N stretches, and low-quality bases (PHRED score ≤ 20) to obtain clean reads. These clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome of Gallus gallus (GRCg7b, downloaded from https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_016699485.2/) using HISAT2 software. The transcriptome files were assembled using StringTie (v. 2.1.6) and gffcompare software (v. 0.9.8). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened based on the criterion: |log2FC| ≥ 1 and p-value < 0.05 using DESeq2 software (v. 1.10.1). These DEGs were enriched into key pathways through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The primers of genes related to lipid metabolism, inflammation, oxidative stress, intestinal barrier, and tryptophan metabolism, were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) (listed in Table S2). Total RNA was extracted from liver and colon tissues by RNAiso Plus (9109, Takala, Beijing, China). One nanogram of RNA per sample was reversely transcribed into cDNA with random hexamer primers using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (11483188001, Roche, Switzerland). Finally, these cDNAs were used for qRT-PCR using LightCycler® EvoScript RNA SYBR® Green I Master (03064760001, Roche, Switzerland) and each reaction volume was 20 µL. Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Each sample was determined at least triplicates.

Fecal 16S rRNA sequencing and microbiome analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples by use of the MagMAX™ DNA Multi-Sample Ultra 2.0 Kit (A36570, Thermo Fisher, Shanghai) and its quality was assessed via NanoDrop spectrophotometry and agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR with primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Amplicons were purified, quantified, and used to construct sequencing libraries for analysis on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, USA).

The high-quality clean data were obtained through quality filter, denoise, merge, and chimera removement of raw sequencing data using the fqtrim (v. 0.94). Taxonomic classification was performed using the classify-sklearn naive Bayes classifier based on the SILVA Release 138 database, and the data were normalized according to the relative abundance of each sample. α- diversity indices, including Chao1, Observed species, Shannon, and Simpson, were calculated to assess microbial community diversity. β-diversity was evaluated based on principle compound analysis (PCA) and Bray-Curtis distance. The differentially abundance in the phylum and genus of microbial community between two groups was computed based on permutational multivariate analysis of variance.

Fecal untargeted metabolomics analysis

A total of 50 mg of each fecal sample was isolated and the fecal metabolites were extracted according to previously described (Wang et al., 2024a). Metabolomic profiling was conducted by Liquid Chromatograph Mass Spectrometer System. The related parameters for the detection of fecal metabolites were set as per a previous study (Wang et al., 2024a). Raw data were converted into an mzXML format using ProteoWizard (v. 3.0.8789), and then detected feature, corrected retention time, and aligned using XCMS R package. Following the normalization, low quality samples (ion peaks ≥ 50 % of quality control) were retained. Differences in fecal metabolite profiles between groups were visualized using partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). Metabolites were considered significantly different if they met the following criteria: log2FC > 1.0, p-value < 0.05, and variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1.0. The enrichment analysis of these differential metabolites was conducted using online platform MetaboAnalyst (v. 6.0, https://dev.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst/). Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was applied to assess associations between intestinal microbes and tryptophan metabolites, as well as between tryptophan metabolites and serum related index.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD or median (Q1, Q3). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed prior to statistical analysis. For normal distribution data, comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test and for non-normal distribution data, comparisons using χ2-test, while differences among three groups were analyzed using ANOVA. All conducted with SPSS software (v. 25.0; Chicago, IL, USA). Graphics were drawn using GraphPad Prism software (v. 8.0; San Diego, CA, USA). P < 0.05 implies statistical significance.

Results

Dietary GLY exposure reduces laying performance of FLHS laying hens

To investigate whether GLY exposure impacts FLHS progression, the laying performance was first evaluated on the 6th week of the experiment. Compared with the Control group, FLHS group exhibited significantly the decrease in laying rate (p < 0.05) and no effect on egg weight (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1B, C), while displayed dramatically the elevation in ADFI (p < 0.01) and Feed/Egg ratio (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1D, E). Moreover, GLY exposure notably reduced laying rate (p < 0.05) and egg weight (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1B, C), whereas remarkedly increased ADFI (p < 0.05) and Feed/Egg ratio (p < 0.05) of laying hens with FLHS (Fig. 1D, E). Collectively, dietary GLY exposure reduces laying performance of FLHS laying hens.

GLY exposure exacerbates liver damage in FLHS laying hens

To explore the influences of GLY exposure on FLHS, liver morphological and pathogenetic changes, as well as the biomarkers related to the liver function, were first evaluated. The results showed that laying hens in FLHS group had obviously morphological changes with yellowish-brown and punctate spots, and even some hens had various degrees of steatosis, and laying hens in FLHS + GLY group displayed the worsening morphological changes caused by GLY (Fig. 2A). H&E staining of liver tissues displayed severe vacuolation and disordered structure among hepatocytes in FLHS group, and GLY exposure worsened the pathological alteration in FLHS laying hens (Fig. 2B). Moreover, TP and ALB contents in the serum were remarkably reduced in FLHS chickens relative to those in normal chickens (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 2C, D), and FLHS hens displayed more increased activities of ALT and AST in the serum than the normal hens (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 2E, F). Whereas, compared with FLHS hens, laying hens in FLHS + GLY group exhibited further significantly reduced levels of TP and ALB in the serum (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 2C, D), and dramatically elevated ALT and AST activities (p < 0.05 and p < 0.05, respectively) (Fig. 2E, F). Overall, GLY exposure exacerbates liver damage in FLHS laying hens.

(D) Serum ALB level. (E) Serum ALT activity. (F) Serum AST activity. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Each dot indicates an individual hen (n = 30). ANOVA was used to analyze data among three groups. P < 0.05 showed statistical significance (# indicates p < 0.05; ** or ## indicates p < 0.01; *** indicates p < 0.001).

Next, liver lipid metabolism was then assessed and determined. Oil red O staining revealed that FLHS laying hens displayed the significant increase of liver lipid droplets relative to the control laying hens, and GLY exposure led to further obvious increase of lipid deposition in laying hens with FLHS (Fig. 3A). Compared with the control, HELPD resulted in the marked elevation in TG, TC, and LDL-C (all p < 0.001) while notable reduction in HDL-C (p < 0.001 or p < 0.01) in both serum and liver (Fig. 3B–I). Furthermore, laying hens in FLHS + GLY group exhibited more change trend of these lipids than those in FLHS group (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) (Fig. 3B–I). Moreover, hepatic mRNA expressions of ACACA, FASN, and SCD1 were notably upregulated in FLHS hens in comparison to those in the normal hens (all p < 0.001), while these gene expressions in FLHS hens exposed to GLY were more significantly increased than that in FLHS hens (p < 0.01 or p < 0.001) (Fig. 3J). Additionally, hepatic mRNA levels of LPL and FABP-1 were dramatically decreased in FLHS hens compared with those in the control hens (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05), and the mRNA levels of these genes (LPL, FABP-1, and CPT1A) were further more reduced in FLHS + GLY group than FLHS group (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p < 0.05, respectively) (Fig. 3K). Nevertheless, no markedly differences were observed in the mRNA expression of CD36 among the three groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3K). In summary, these data indicate that GLY exposure promotes lipid deposition in the liver of FLHS laying hens.

GLY exposure increases hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation in FLHS laying hens

We then investigated whether GLY exposure aggravated liver oxidative stress and inflammation in FLHS laying hens. Compared with the control, HELPD significantly elevated serum levels of MDA (p < 0.001) while dramatically decreased serum levels/activities of T-AOC, SOD, and CAT (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 4A–D). In paralleling with the changes of redox parameters in the serum, laying hens in FLHS group displayed overtly liver increased contents of MDA (p < 0.001) whereas markedly liver decreased levels/activities of T-AOC, SOD, and CAT (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p < 0.05, respectively) compared with laying hens in Control group (Fig. 4E–H). Relative to FLHS laying hens, the levels of MDA (p < 0.05) were further obviously elevated and the levels/activities of T-AOC, SOD, and CAT (all p < 0.01) were also dramatically reduced in the serum of FLHS laying hens exposed to GLY (Fig. 4A–D). Similarly, chickens in FLHS + GLY group exhibited higher hepatic contents of MDA (p < 0.05) and lower levels/activities of T-AOC, SOD, and CAT (all p < 0.05) than those in FLHS group (Fig. 4E–H). Altogether, these findings imply that GLY exposure worsen the imbalance of redox homeostasis in FLHS hens.

Next, the alterations of inflammatory cytokines were estimated. FLHS hens had higher contents of pro-inflammatory cytokines (including IL-1β, p < 0.001; TNF-α, p < 0.01; and IL-6, p < 0.001; respectively), while lower contents of anti-inflammatory cytokine (such as IL-10, p < 0.001) in the serum than control laying hens (Fig. 4I–L). Likewise, the mRNA levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 (all p < 0.001) were notably elevated whereas the mRNA levels of IL-10 (p < 0.001) were dramatically reduced in the liver of laying hens with FLHS compared with those in the liver of normal laying hens (Fig. 4M–P). However, when exposed to GLY, FLHS laying hens displayed further increased levels/expressions of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 (all p < 0.05 in serum; p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.05 in the liver, respectively), while decreased levels/expression of IL-10 (p < 0.05 in the serum and p < 0.01 in the liver) (Fig. 4I–P). Conclusively, GLY exposure facilitates the mRNA expressions and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines in FLHS laying hens.

GLY exposure alters the composition and structure of gut microbiota in FLHS laying hens

Based on the action mechanism of GLY, the changes in gut microbiota between FLHS group and FLHS + GLY group were analyzed to determine which gut microbes took part into GLY-exacerbated FLHS. GLY exposure significantly reduced α-diversity (Chao1, p < 0.05; Observed species, p < 0.05; Shannon, p < 0.05; and Simpson, p < 0.01; respectively) of feces in FLHS hens (Fig. 5A). PCA and Bray-Curtis distance analysis showed that the samples of FLHS + GLY group were clearly separated from those of FLHS groups (Fig. 5B, C). Then, the relative abundance of fecal microbiota was evaluated using QIIME2 software. In the phylum level, the top 5 changed phyla were Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, Desulfobacterota, Firmicutes, and Actinomycetota, with decreased Bacteroidota abundance, and elevated Proteobacteria, Desulfobacterota, Firmicutes, and Actinomycetota abundance (Fig. 5D). Firmicutes/Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio was notably higher in FLHS + GLY group than that in FLHS group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5E). In the genus level, the top 5 changed genera were Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, Escherichia-Shigella, Erysipelothrix, and Desulfobvibrio (Fig. 5F). The relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium was significantly descended creased while the relative abundance of Escherichia-Shigella, Bacteroides, Erysipelothrix, Desulfobvibrio, and Eggerthellain was notably ascended in FLHS + GLY group relative to FLHS group (Fig. 5G–M). These results indicate that GLY exposure reduces beneficial bacterial abundance and elevates harmful bacterial abundance in FLHS laying hens.

GLY exposure reduces intestinal tryptophan metabolism in FLHS laying hens

The changes of gut microbiota concurrently instigate the alterations in intestinal metabolites. To disentangle the impacts of GLY exposure on the alterations of intestinal metabolites in FLHS laying hens, fecal untargeted metabolome analysis was conducted between FLHS + GLY group and FLHS group. The results of PLS-DA indicated the metabolic differences between the two groups, demonstrating that GLY exposure triggered the changes of the intestinal metabolic profile in FLHS laying hens (Fig. 6A). Based on PLS-DA model, under the condition of the log2FC > 1.0, p < 0.05, and VIP > 1.0, 100 differentially changed metabolites were identified among 415 metabolites, including 57 downregulated metabolites and 43 upregulated metabolites in FLHS + GLY group compared to FLHS group (Fig. 6B). KEGG analysis showed that the mainly enriched pathways included tryptophan metabolism, glutathione metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, ether lipid metabolism, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (Fig. 6C). Notably, the tryptophan metabolic pathway displayed dramatically higher enrichment than other pathways (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6D). Specifically, the abundance of 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (p < 0.01), 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (p < 0.05), kynurenic acid (p < 0.01), 4-(2-aminophenyl)-2,4-dioxobutanoic acid (p < 0.001), tryptamine (p < 0.01), and indole-3-carboxylic acid (p < 0.01) were notably reduced in FLHS + GLY group relative to FLHS group (Fig. 6E–J). These findings indicate that GLY exposure significantly results in tryptophan metabolism disorders in the gut of FLHS hens.

Then, Spearman correlation analysis was employed to explore the association between intestinal microbes and tryptophan metabolites, as well as between intestinal tryptophan metabolites and serum related index. The results showed that, among the top 5 changed microbiota genera, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium positively related to that of 4-(2-aminophenyl)-2,4-dioxobutanoic acid, 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid, indole-3-carboxylic acid, and kynurenic acid, indicating that GLY exposure reduces the levels of certain tryptophan metabolites by inhibiting the growth of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium (Fig. 6K). Altogether, these results demonstrate that GLY exposure triggers liver damage through disturbing intestinal tryptophan metabolism, owing to GLY-inhibited the growth of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium in the intestine of FLHS laying hens.

GLY exposure increases gut barrier permeability in FLHS laying hens through inhibiting aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway

Tryptophan-derived metabolites serve as the ligands for AhR, which plays a key role in maintaining intestinal barrier function. Therefore, we examined the changes of the AhR pathway and evaluated the alterations of gut barrier permeability among the three groups. The lower mRNA levels of AhR and its downstream genes, Cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily A member 2 (CYP1A2) and IL-22, were observed in the gut of FLHS laying hens than those in Control laying hens (all p < 0.001), while GLY exposure further significantly reduced the expression levels of the three genes in the gut of FLHS laying hens (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 7A). Then, the alterations of the gut barrier function were assessed and H&E staining of colonic tissues displayed destructed arrangement of villi, as well as a remarkable decrease in villous height and crypt depth in FLHS laying hens compared with the health laying hens, and GLY exposure showed more destructed colonic tissue structure (Fig. 7B). Moreover, serum LPS levels were remarkably increased in FLHS hens in comparison to the normal hens (all p < 0.001), and GLY exposure further dramatically increased serum levels of LPS (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, HELPD notably lowered the expression levels of gut barrier-associated genes ZO-1, Claudin-1, Claudin-5, Occludin, and Mucin-2 relative to the control (p < 0.001, p < 0.01, p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.01, respectively), while GLY exposure farther remarkably downregulated the mRNA levels of ZO-1, Claudin-5, and Occludin in the colons of FLHS laying hens (all p < 0.001) (Fig. 7D). Conclusively, GLY exposure augments the gut barrier permeability in FLHS laying hens likely through inhibiting AhR pathway.

GLY exposure promotes the progression of FLHS in laying hens through activating TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway and suppressing Nrf2/Keap1 pathway

To disentangle the mechanisms by which GLY exposure accelerated HELPD-induced FLHS, liver transcriptome analysis of laying hens was conducted between FLHS group and FLHS + GLY group. First, PCA result showed obvious distance between both groups, indicating that GLY exposure significantly changed HELPD-induced gene expressions in the livers of laying hens (Fig. 8A). And then, DEGs were screened between two groups and the results revealed that 783 DEGs were identified, with 383 upregulated and 400 downregulated (Fig. 8B). Based on these DEGs, we conducted enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways to construct the gene set and the results of KEGG pathway analysis primarily enriched into fatty acid biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism, toll-like receptor signaling pathway, and Nrf2 signaling pathway (Fig. 8C). Moreover, qRT-PCR results substantiated that the mRNA levels of crucial genes involving TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway (such as TLR4, MyD88, NFKB1, and IKKA; all p < 0.001) were significantly upregulated while the mRNA expressions of critical genes of Nrf2 pathway (including NRF2, HMOX, and NQO1; p < 0.01, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001, respectively) were markedly downregulated in FLHS + GLY group relative to FLHS group (Fig. 8D, E). Collectively, based on the results of liver transcriptome, GLY exposure exacerbates FLHS progression by regulating pathways associated with lipid metabolism, inflammation, and redox homeostasis in laying hens.

FMT substantiates the exacerbation effects of GLY exposure on FLHS in a gut-microbiota dependent manner

FMT experiments were carried out to substantiate whether GLY-exacerbated FLHS was dependent on gut microbiota dysbiosis induced by GLY in laying hens (Fig. 9A). H&E staining indicated that hepatocytes in the FMT-(FLHS + GLY) group exhibited more severe vacuolation and structural disarray compared to those in the FMT-FLHS group (Fig. 9B). Oil Red O staining further demonstrated a greater accumulation of lipid droplets in the liver of FMT-(FLHS + GLY) hens than in FMT-FLHS hens (Fig. 9C). Moreover, compared with hens in FMT-FLHS group, laying hens in FMT-(FLHS + GLY) group exhibited further decreased levels of TP and ALB (p < 0.05 and p < 0.001, respectively), while increased activities of ALT and AST (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively) in the serum (Fig. 9D). Furthermore, laying hens received fecal microbiota from the FLHS + GLY group had higher levels of TG, TC, and LDL-C (p < 0.001, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001, respectively), while lower levels of HDL-C (p < 0.01) in the serum than laying hens received fecal microbiota from the FLHS group (Fig. 9E). Additionally, serum levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 were notably increased (all p < 0.01), while serum levels of IL-10 were evidently decreased (p < 0.01) in the FMT-(FLHS + GLY) group in comparison to the FMT-FLHS group (Fig. 9F). Serum levels of MDA were markedly elevated (p < 0.01), whereas serum levels/activities of T-AOC, SOD, and CAT were dramatically reduced (p < 0.05, p < 0.001, and p < 0.01, respectively) in FMT-(FLHS + GLY) hens relative to FMT-FLHS hens (Fig. 9G). These findings demonstrate that dietary GLY exposure promotes the progression of FLHS in a gut microbiota-dependent manner.

Discussion

Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) in laying hens is mainly manifested in pathological liver damage, liver function impairment, and lipid metabolism disorder (Gallo et al., 2012; McGill, 2016). GLY exposure markedly increased the levels of ALT and AST, two key biomarkers for liver damage, in the serum of FLHS chickens, indicating that hepatocyte structure is severely damaged (Gallo et al., 2012; McGill, 2016), which was evidenced by the examination results of H&E staining, oxidative stress and inflammation factors. The simultaneous decrease of TP and ALB concentrations in the serum shows liver function impairment (Agrawal et al., 2016). Our findings indicated that serum contents of ALB and TP were significantly lowered in FLHS + GLY group compared with those in FLHS group, suggesting that GLY exposure aggravates liver function disorder. TG and TC are two key ingredients of lipids, and their levels in the serum and liver are commonly used as important index of lipid homeostasis (Hu et al., 2021). HDL-C and LDL-C are two key lipoproteins responsible for transporting cholesterol: The former transports cholesterol into the liver to facilitate its metabolism whereas the latter brings cholesterol from the liver into other tissues to create its deposition (Hu et al., 2021). In the serum and liver samples, the contents of TC, TG, and LDL-C were significantly elevated while HDL-C levels were notably reduced in FLHS + GLY hens compared with FLHS hens, suggesting that GLY exposure destroyed lipid homeostasis.

Additionally, ACACA, FASN, and SCD1 are important enzymes for de novo lipid synthesis. ACACA functions as key rate-limiting enzyme in de novo fatty-acid synthesis; FASN is indispensable for driving the overall production of fatty acids; SCD1 is centrally involved in catalyzing the lipogenic pathway (Lv et al., 2024). In this study, the hepatic mRNA levels of lipogenesis-related genes (ACACA, FASN, and SCD1) was significantly up-regulated, while hepatic mRNA levels of lipolysis, fatty acid transport and regulation-related genes (LPL, FABP-1, and CPT1A) were significantly down-regulated in FLHS + GLY group in comparison with FLHS group, suggesting that GLY exposure increased lipid deposition in the livers of FLHS hens, which was evidenced by Oil red O staining. These aforementioned findings were consistent with the previous results, reporting that GLY exposure can induce pathological liver damage, liver function impairment, and lipid metabolism disorder in roosters and carps (Liu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024).

GLY exposure below no-observed-adverse-effect level can directly trigger gut microbiota dysbiosis via inhibiting bacterial shikimate pathway, thus negatively affecting animal health (Mesnage et al., 2015). Gut microbiota dysbiosis plays key roles in development and progression of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) (Aron-Wisnewsky et al., 2020). The F/B ratio is commonly regarded as a sign of gut microbial imbalance, and its elevation is considered a hallmark of microbiota-induced MASLD (Li et al., 2018; Valdes, 2018). The elevated F/B ratios were positively involved in increased gut permeability, lipid levels, and liver injury (Wang et al., 2024b). Escherichia_Shigella and Bacteroides are able to induce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the hepatocytes (Liang et al., 2022; Lo Presti et al., 2019). Erysipelothrix can disrupt choline metabolism, leading to serum high trimethylamine contents, which is a high risk of FLHS development (Vallianou et al., 2021). Desulfovibrio has been reported to be associated with with the gut and liver inflammation response (Wang et al., 2024b). Eggerthella involves in host fatty acid metabolism, and its increased abundance has been observed in mice with fatty liver disease (Chen et al., 2023). But Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium are known as probiotics, which can enhance liver health via enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, regulating metabolism, as well as decreasing inflammatory response and oxidative stress (Lau et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024).

A recent report showed that GLY decreases the abundance of Lactobacillus, and increased proportions of pathogens in the ileum of chicks (Dai et al., 2024). It is worth noting that pathogenic bacteria such as Salmonella spp. and Clostridium spp. were found to be resistant to GLY, while beneficial bacteria (including Lactobacillus spp., Staphylococcus spp., and Enterococcus spp.) were reported to be sensitive to the herbicide, suggesting that GLY may cause a reduction in beneficial bacteria in the microbiota of the gastrointestinal tract, disrupting the normal bacterial community and favoring pathogenic bacteria in the gut in various animal models (Ignácio et al., 2024). In this study, the FLHS + GLY group showed an increased F/B ratios and a considerably greater abundance of pathogen bacteria, including Escherichia-Shigella, Bacteroides, Erysipelothrix, Desulfobvibrio, and Eggerthella, while a notably lower abundance of probiotics, e.g. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, relative to the FLHS group. These findings showed that GLY exposure below no-observed-adverse-effect level exacerbated liver damage in FLHS hens via disturbing chicken gut microbiota homeostasis, which was evidenced by FMT experiments and was in agreement with our previous studies in GLY-induced testis injury in rats (Liu et al., 2022a, 2022b).

A growing body of research has shown that the gut microbiota impacts liver function by modulating its metabolites, including tryptophan metabolites, which thus regulate liver lipid metabolism and inflammatory response (Pabst et al., 2023). In this study, chicken fecal metabolomics showed that tryptophan metabolism was the most significant enrichment pathway and the contents of tryptophan metabolites were remarkably decreased after GLY exposure in FLHS laying hens. Spearman correlation analysis indicated that the abundance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium positively related to 4-(2-aminophenyl)-2,4-dioxobutyric acid, 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid, indole-3-carboxylic acid, and kynurenic acid. Previous research has revealed that the increased abundance in Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium positively linked to the elevated contents of tryptophan metabolites (Dang et al., 2023; He et al., 2023). Our findings showed GLY exposure can result in the decreased levels of certain tryptophan metabolites through reducing the abundance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium in FLHS laying hens.

Tryptophan metabolites can act as ligands of AhR to activate AhR and its downstream target genes, such as CYP1A2 and IL-22 (Dang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2023), which can maintain the intestinal barrier function through up-regulating the tight junction proteins (Postal et al., 2020). In this study, GLY exposure inhibited the mRNA expressions of AhR, CYP1A2, IL-22, ZO-1, Claudin-5, and Occludin in the colon of FLHS laying hens, suggesting that GLY-induced the decreased levels of tryptophan metabolites suppressed AhR pathway and destroyed chicken intestinal barrier integrity. Similar results were also found in the rodent animals which displayed the decreased expression of tight junction proteins after long-term exposure to GLY (Del Castilo et al., 2022; He et al., 2024).

Increased gut barrier permeability can cause the development of systemic disorders (Chelakkot et al., 2018). LPS, a common endotoxin, originated from gram-negative bacteria (Saltzman et al., 2018). Accumulating studies have demonstrated that the increased abundance in Desulfovibrio, Clostridium, Helicobacter, and Escherichia was positively linked to the elevated contents of LPS in feces, serum, and liver (Fuke et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). Similarly, our study also showed that GLY exposure significantly increased the relative abundance of LPS-producing bacteria, such as Escherichia-Shigella and Desulfovibrio. Additionally, serum LPS can be transferred to extraintestinal organs via the damaged gut barrier and trigger inflammatory response in various distal organs, including the liver (Ferro et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022c). As expected, GLY exposure caused increased levels of serum LPS in FLHS laying hens. LPS can bind to TLR4 and activate MyD88/NF-κB pathway to express and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, thus triggering liver dysfunction (Feng et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2020). Chicken liver transcriptome profiles showed that, apart from fatty acid metabolism-related pathways, toll-like receptor signaling pathway and Nrf2 signaling pathway were significantly enriched in this study, which was validated by the mRNA level examination, including the significant up-regulation of TLR4, MyD88, NFKB1, and IKKA, as well as the significant down-regulation of NRF2, HMOX1, and NQO1 in the FLHS + GLY group relative to the FLHS group. These above findings demonstrated the GLY exposure aggravated inflammation response and redox imbalance in FLHS chickens via producing LPS, which has been confirmed in our previous study: Chronic GLY exposure induced hepatotoxicity through causing oxidative stress and inflammatory response (Liu et al., 2021).

Conclusion

In summary, GLY exposure below no-observed-adverse-effect level disrupts gut microbiota homeostasis, exacerbating FLHS in laying hens via three different pathways, including tryptophan metabolites/AhR pathway inhibition, LPS/TLR4/NF-κB pathway activation, and the suppression of Nrf2/Keap1 pathway (Fig. 10). These findings provide novel insights into GLY-induced health risks via gut-liver axis.
