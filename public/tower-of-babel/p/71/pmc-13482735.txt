An unexpected pulmonary pathology in a 26-year-old man with recurrent syncope
Authors: Cameron Forward, Ciara McCormack, Caitriona Minnock, Brian McCullagh, Sean Gaine, Sarah Cullivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Shareable abstractPulmonary arterial hypertension is a rare diagnosis and, when present with pulmonary arteriovenous malformations, a diagnosis of hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia should be considered. This case highlights the difficulties in this form of PH. https://bit.ly/4tAmW9R

A 26-year-old man was referred to Mater Misericordiae University Hospital (Dublin, Ireland) in 2009 following recurrent syncopal events. He recently immigrated to Ireland from India and reported a 1-year history of presyncope, syncope and progressive dyspnoea on exertion. His background was significant for an admission to the intensive care unit in 2007 in India for typhoid fever. During that admission he had an echocardiogram that demonstrated raised right heart pressures and he was initiated on a combination of medications including amlodipine, warfarin and sildenafil, which he still took at the time of this presentation. He was a never-smoker, did not consume alcohol, and denied any illicit drug use or relevant family history. His clinical examination revealed a prominent second heart sound and a tricuspid regurgitation murmur and he appeared euvolaemic. There was no evidence of digit clubbing, telangiectasia or any stigmata of connective tissue disease.

He was admitted electively for a panel of investigations to assess for suspected pulmonary hypertension (PH). His echocardiogram suggested a high probability of PH, with normal left heart characteristics. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated restrictive spirometry with a forced vital capacity (FVC) of 3.31 L (72.9%), a forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) of 2.71 L (69.6%), with a FEV1/FVC ratio of 81% and a diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) of 56%. His computed tomography (CT) scan of the thorax is shown in figure 1. A ventilation/perfusion scan was indeterminate.

An axial slice from a computed tomography scan of the thorax of a 26-year-old man with pulmonary hypertension. The arrow highlights a large pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVM) in the posterior segment of the left upper lobe. There are extensive ground-glass changes present and PAVMs involving all lobes. The majority of PAVMs lie in the lung periphery.

A right heart catheterisation (RHC) was performed, which revealed a pre-capillary pattern of PH with a mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) of 61 mmHg, a pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) of 9 mmHg, a pulmonary artery oxygen saturation of 64% and a pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) of ∼11 Wood units (WU). There were some missing data, but it was reported that the vasoreactivity test with nitric oxide was negative and there was no evidence of a left-to-right shunt on the saturations run. A follow-up RHC performed ∼4 years later, on dual oral targeted pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) therapies, demonstrated a mean right atrial pressure of 4 mmHg, mPAP of 60 mmHg, PAWP of 9 mmHg, a reduced cardiac output of 3.2 L·min−1 and PVR of 15 WU.

A liver ultrasound was normal and routine biochemistry revealed a normal B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) level of <5 ng·L−1, and a negative connective tissue disease screen and HIV test. The patient's 6-min walk distance was 510 m.

Answer 1

a) Correct. IPAH is possible in this case, as there is a pre-capillary pattern of PH at RHC and no clear aetiology for this. However, pulmonary arteriovenous malformations (PAVMs) are not associated with IPAH and therefore require further investigation.

b) Correct. PVOD is a subgroup of group 1 PAH that is associated with specific characteristics including hypoxia, marked reductions in DLCO, and CT features such as adenopathy, septal line thickening and ground-glass opacities. Some of these features are present in this case, such as ground-glass opacities and a reduced DLCO; therefore, this diagnosis warrants consideration. However, PAVMs are not associated with PVOD and therefore warrant further investigations.

c) Correct. HHT is an autosomal dominant disease characterised by vascular dysplasia and is an important consideration in this case as it is associated with both PAH and PAVMs.

d) Incorrect. Schistosomiasis is not endemic in Ireland or India and there is no history of travel to endemic regions, making this diagnosis less likely in this case.

Following the results of the RHC and the negative vasoreactivity test, the calcium channel blocker amlodipine was stopped. An endothelin receptor antagonist (ERA) was added to his regimen, which already included the phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitor (PDE5i) sildenafil. The patient was given specific information regarding special considerations for PAVMs and important measures that should be taken to mitigate potential complications.

Answer 2

a) Incorrect. Air travel is not contraindicated in individuals with PAVMs who are otherwise well, with normal peripheral oxygen saturations.

b) Correct. Antibiotic prophylaxis is an important consideration for patients with PAVMs, and is advised before any dental or surgical procedures, due to the risk of bacteraemia and subsequent right-to-left shunting and paradoxical septic embolism.

c) Correct. When feasible, an in-line filter should be used for intravenous access for patients with known PAVMs, to avoid potential complications such as air embolism and cerebral infarction.

d) Incorrect. There is no evidence for empirical anticoagulation for patients with PAVMs, without an alternative indication.

e) Correct. Patients with PAVMs are advised not to scuba dive, due to the theoretical increased risk of complications associated with decompression.

In this case, warfarin was stopped as it was no longer indicated for the specific treatment of PAH, and additional investigations were requested to assess for suspected HHT. The Curaçao criteria were employed to assess the probability of HHT using four established clinical criteria. In this case, the patient had a Curaçao score of 1 out of 4, in light of the presence of PAVMs and the absence of epistaxis, telangiectasia at characteristic sites, or a first-degree relative with HHT. This suggested that a diagnosis of HHT was unlikely. Genetic analysis was then performed using a targeted panel that included ACVRL1 (activin receptor-like kinase 1) and ENG (endoglin) mutations. However, no pathogenic variants associated with HHT or PAH were identified. Lung biopsy was considered too dangerous in this case due to the presence of severe PH, and is generally not indicated in PAH, and PAVM embolisation was not pursued due to concerns regarding potential worsening of PH.

Despite a low Curaçao score and negative genetic tests, there was persistent clinical suspicion for HHT, as it would provide a unifying diagnosis for the PAVMs and PAH. Therefore, additional tests were requested to assess for complications associated with HHT, such as MRI of the brain and a liver ultrasound, and these were reassuring, with no evidence of extrathoracic arteriovenous malformations (AVMs). He was given a diagnosis of group 1 PAH with comorbid PAVMs, and treated according to the PAH guidelines. He continued double combination therapy until 2023 when he began to deteriorate, 14 years following his initial diagnosis. Due to the presence of high-risk features, he was initiated on parenteral prostacyclin with subcutaneous treprostinil and he was referred for lung transplant assessment, where he remains on the active lung transplant list.

This is an interesting case of a young patient with severe PAH and diffuse PAVMs. It highlights important considerations for patients with PAVMs and the typical assessment of patients with suspected HHT. While the clinical phenotype in this case was atypical for IPAH, the allocation of this patient to a clinical group facilitated treatment decisions and guided subsequent escalation of care.

Discussion

This 26-year-old man with progressive dyspnoea and syncope was diagnosed with PAVMs and group 1 PAH. This case highlights important concepts surrounding the management of PAVMs and pulmonary vascular disease.

PAVMs are abnormal vascular connections between pulmonary arteries and pulmonary veins of varying size and complexity. The prevalence of PAVMs is estimated at one case per 2600 persons, and is attributed to HHT in ∼70–93.6% of cases [1, 2]. PAVMs are present in ∼15–50% of patients with HHT and are more prevalent in HHT associated with ENG mutations in comparison to ACVRL1 mutations [3, 4]. PAVMs create a right-to-left shunt, which may result in complications such as hypoxaemia and paradoxical embolism. Furthermore, rupture can result in haemoptysis and haemothorax. However, PAVMs are frequently asymptomatic and therefore routine screening is advised at baseline in all patients with HHT. This can be performed with TTCE and/or CT of the thorax, depending on local screening guidelines and preferences. Patients with radiologically visible PAVMs should be referred to a multidisciplinary team including an interventional radiologist to determine if they are suitable for embolisation. Embolisation is typically advised if technically feasible, in both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients, as it can mitigate symptoms associated with shunts, attenuate epistaxis and migraines and reduce the risk of paradoxical embolism. It is postulated that migraines are ameliorated through the reduction of shunting of vasoactive substances and microemboli into the systemic circulation. Complications are rare and occur in 1% of cases when performed by an experienced radiologist and include infarction, haemoptysis, air embolus and migration of the embolisation material. In patients with PAVMs and comorbid PH, decisions regarding embolisation are more complex due to the theoretical risk of worsening pulmonary haemodynamics. Embolisation of PAVMs in patients with PH could potentially increase right ventricular afterload, by reducing right-to-left shunting [5–7]. There are case reports documenting fatal increases of pulmonary artery pressures following embolisation [8]. Despite this, the presence of PAH is not an absolute contraindication to embolisation. Before proceeding to embolisation, patients with PH should be established on targeted vasodilator therapies to improve haemodynamics. Decisions to proceed with embolisation should be made on a case-by-case basis by an expert team [4].

Patients with PAVMs should be educated regarding the risk of air embolism and the importance of antibiotic prophylaxis in circumstances that are associated with an increased risk of bacteraemia, such as dental procedures. Finally, surveillance of PAVMs is guided by local guidelines and individual patient characteristics, but follow-up imaging is generally advised following intervention, if there is a clinical change, prior to potential pregnancy if there was no recent screening, and in the post-partum period, irrespective of prior screening results. Physiological changes associated with pregnancy including an increase in cardiac output and circulating blood volume and effects of progesterone on venous distensibility may result in enlargement or rupture of PAVMs, thus promoting judicious screening prior to pregnancy and in the post-partum period [3, 9].

All patients diagnosed with PH should undergo a thorough workup including a detailed history, physical examination and investigations to determine the underlying aetiology of their PH, including any potential genetic cause. While the final diagnosis in this case was not HHT, the presence of concomitant PAVMs and PAH should prompt the physician to consider this diagnosis. HHT is an autosomal dominant disease characterised by vascular dysplasia in various organs. Clinical criteria and genetic tests are typically used to diagnose the condition, and a number of subsequent screening tests are advised to assess for potential complications. The Curaçao criteria were first published in 2000 and are four clinical criteria that are used to assess the probability of HHT [10]. HHT is considered definite if three or more of the following criteria are present: recurrent spontaneous epistaxis, the presence of mucocutaneous telangiectasias, visceral AVMs and the presence of HHT in a first-degree relative. Genetic tests are increasingly employed to aid diagnosis, and mutations in ENG and ACVRL1 are responsible for the majority of HHT cases [11]. Once HHT is confirmed, a panel of screening tests are performed to assess for potential complications, such as MRI of the brain to screen for CAVMs and TTCE and/or CT of the thorax to screen for PAVMs. Pulmonary vascular manifestations of HHT include PAVMs, high-output cardiac failure (HOCF) and PAH.

PH is a potential complication of HHT with two characteristic phenotypes. The more common phenotype is post-capillary and is caused by hepatic AVMs. These AVMs can result in large hepato-systemic shunts associated with increased cardiac preload and decreased peripheral vascular resistance. This can result in HOCF characterised by an increased cardiac output and elevations in the mPAP. This results in a post-capillary PH, characterised by a PAWP >15 mmHg and a PVR <2 WU at RHC. Treatment is usually supportive and includes salt restriction and diuretics. In cases refractory to supportive measures, anti-vascular endothelial growth factor therapies such as bevacizumab may be considered, or liver transplantation. PAH-targeted medical therapies are not indicated in this group [3, 4, 6]. This phenotype was not observed in our patient.

The second relevant pulmonary vascular disease phenotype is PAH, which is a progressive disease of the pulmonary vasculature that may be idiopathic or associated with specific exposures and conditions such as HHT. PAH is rare in patients with HHT and it has an estimated prevalence of <1% of all patients with HHT. The pulmonary vascular changes observed in patients with PAH associated with HHT are similar to those seen in other PAH subgroups. Hypertrophy and thickening of intimal and medial layers of pulmonary endothelial cells and characteristic plexiform lesions are frequently noted, which result in progressive narrowing of pulmonary vessels and the loss of pulmonary vascular beds [3]. When PH is suspected, a baseline echocardiogram is advised to look for suggestive features. The peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV) should be measured to stratify patients into low (TRV <2.8 m·s−1), intermediate (TRV 2.8–3.4 m·s−1) or high (TRV >3.4 m·s−1) probability of suspected PH, in conjunction with other suggestive features including a dilated pulmonary artery, right atrium, right ventricle and inferior vena cava. The diagnosis of PAH is then confirmed at RHC, which demonstrates a pre-capillary pattern of PH, defined as mPAP >20 mmHg, PVR >2 WU and PAWP ≤15 mmHg. A clinical risk assessment should be performed to stratify patients into low, intermediate or high risk of mortality, as this information can be used to guide treatment decisions and prognostication. Current approved therapies target four treatment pathways and include the nitric oxide, endothelin, prostacyclin and activin signalling pathways [6, 12]. The first-in-class activin signalling inhibitor, sotatercept, is a new addition to the therapeutic options for patients with PAH. However, treatment with sotatercept has been associated with side-effects including telangiectasia and epistaxis, and for this reason it is not currently recommended for patients with PAH associated with HHT, who already experience these complications [4, 6].

In this case, our patient was established on maximum available therapy, including a PDE5i, an ERA and parenteral prostacyclin, and he was referred for consideration for lung transplantation in light of high-risk features. Despite suspicion for HHT, he did not meet the necessary clinical criteria and targeted genetic analysis was normal. This is an interesting case of a 26-year-old man with progressive dyspnoea and syncope who was subsequently diagnosed with PAVMs and PAH and is on the active lung transplant waiting list. This case highlights important concepts regarding PAVMs, PAH and HHT and the importance of a holistic approach to the management of such complex cases.
