Symbiont‐Mediated Detoxification of Xenobiotics in Honey Bees
Authors: Yao Liu, Esther du Rand, Christian Pirk, Huoqing Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe active foraging behaviour of honey bees frequently exposes them to various xenobiotics. Honey bees rely primarily on endogenous enzymatic detoxification systems to metabolise these compounds; however, this capacity is constrained by limitations in their genomic detoxification repertoire. The gut microbiota may partially compensate for this deficiency through two complementary mechanisms: directly transforming or sequestering xenobiotics, and modulating host detoxification pathways. We therefore propose that the gut microbiota should be regarded as an extended detoxification organ in honey bees. This perspective also points to a microbial biotechnology agenda for pollinator protection, including precision probiotics, microbiome‐informed breeding and engineered symbionts. Viewing detoxification as a holobiont trait provides a more comprehensive framework for understanding bee resilience and for developing microbiome‐based interventions under real‐world chemical stress.

Introduction

The foraging activities of honey bees expose them to a wide range of xenobiotics, including agricultural pesticides, environmental contaminants, plant secondary metabolites and veterinary chemicals (Johnson 2015; Gekière et al. 2023). This multi‐source chemical exposure contributes to sublethal impairments, immune suppression and colony collapse (Johnson et al. 2009; Glavan and Bozic 2013; Gong and Diao 2017; de Beer et al. 2023). Selective foraging to avoid toxic nectar, together with the processing and storage of collected food, can substantially reduce colony‐level exposure to toxins (Berenbaum and Johnson 2015; Liao et al. 2017; Walton et al. 2024). Their contribution is therefore preventive rather than directly detoxifying, and it is strongly context‐dependent because toxin avoidance may fail when floral resources are limited or physiological demand is high (Desmedt et al. 2016; Liao et al. 2017).

The main mechanism by which honey bees detoxify ingested toxins is the enzymatic detoxification system, primarily orchestrated by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (Berenbaum and Johnson 2015). Despite the presence of this system, comparative genomic analyses reveal that honey bees possess a significantly reduced repertoire of detoxification genes compared with other insects (Claudianos et al. 2006). This limited enzymatic toolkit may constrain substrate coverage and functional redundancy, especially under chronic or mixed chemical exposure (Claudianos et al. 2006; Johnson et al. 2012; Berenbaum and Johnson 2015; Maiwald et al. 2023).

The apparent mismatch between complex xenobiotic exposure and genomic deficit in detoxification repertoire directs our attention to another layer of protection: the gut microbiota. For xenobiotics that are ingested despite colony‐level avoidance, the gut microbiota may help to ease the burden on the host enzymatic detoxification system by directly metabolising or sequestering selected compounds, while also modulating host detoxification pathways (Figure 1; Wu et al. 2020; El Khoury et al. 2022). Honey bee detoxification should therefore be viewed as a set of complementary layers rather than independent alternatives, with the relative contribution of each layer depending on the compound, dose, exposure route and ecological context.

The enzymatic and symbiont‐mediated detoxification systems of honey bees. In the enzymatic detoxification system, xenobiotics undergo initial biotransformation by enzymes such as the cytochrome P450 superfamily and carboxylesterases in Phase I, which modify their structures to facilitate subsequent conjugation. In Phase II, members of the glutathione S‐transferase (GST) enzyme superfamily conjugate with the Phase I metabolites, thereby increasing their solubility. In Phase III, ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) proteins transport these conjugated products out of the cell. In the symbiont‐mediated detoxification system, gut microbiota modulates host endogenous detoxification pathways by regulating the expression of detoxification‐related genes such as cytochrome P450. In addition, gut microbiota can directly degrade or sequester certain xenobiotics.

The Gut Microbiota as a Detoxification Partner

Honey bees harbour a distinctive, relatively simple and conserved gut microbiota, dominated by a handful of core bacterial species including Snodgrassella alvi, Gilliamella apicola, Lactobacillus Firm‐4 and Firm‐5 and Bifidobacterium asteroides (Kwong et al. 2017). Long studied for their roles in nutrient processing, pathogen exclusion and immune development, this microbial consortium is now recognised as a symbiotic interface that complements host physiology by directly processing toxins and modulating the host's own enzymatic responses (Wu et al. 2020; El Khoury et al. 2022), thereby bridging individual resilience and colony‐level survival.

Direct Biotransformation of Toxins by Gut Symbionts

Beyond passive absorption by the host, gut microbes engage in active biotransformation and sequestration of xenobiotics. For example, Gilliamella degrade pectin and metabolise toxic monosaccharides (e.g., mannose, xylose) found in some nectars, thereby reducing the burden of plant‐derived dietary toxicants (Engel et al. 2012; Zheng et al. 2016, 2017). Other strains isolated from the honey bee gut, such as Enterobacter sp. and Pantoea sp., can degrade clothianidin and probiotic supplementation with such strains improves survival under pesticide exposure (El Khoury et al. 2022, 2024). Furthermore, bee‐associated gut microbes also show potential to mitigate metal stress, primarily through metal sequestration processes such as biosorption and bioaccumulation (Rothman et al. 2019; Zaghloul and El Halfawy 2023).

Modulation of Host Detoxification Pathways

Perhaps more intriguingly, the microbiota can systemically influence the host's own detoxification machinery. Wu et al. (2020) demonstrated that gut microbiota colonisation significantly upregulated the expression of key cytochrome P450 genes (CYP9Q subfamily) in the honey bee midgut. This microbiota‐induced P450 expression was functionally critical, enhancing the metabolism of, and tolerance to, pesticides such as thiacloprid (Wu et al. 2020). This establishes a paradigm in which gut symbionts actively modulate host detoxification capacity. More recently, the core symbiont Gilliamella sp. G0441 was reported to modulate the expression of the detoxification‐related gene esterase E4 in Apis cerana (Zhao et al. 2025), further supporting the view that gut microbes do not merely remove toxicants but actively shape host detoxification capacity. The precise signalling molecules (e.g., microbial metabolites) and host pathways involved in this cross‐talk are an exciting area of research, suggesting that the microbiome primes the host's chemical defence system.

Together, these findings indicate that microbial detoxification and host enzymatic responses are connected rather than isolated processes. Viewing the gut microbiota as an extended ‘detoxification organ’ therefore offers an integrative framework for understanding how honey bees cope with chemical pressures in their environment.

Protecting and Harnessing Microbial Potential: From Probiotics to Engineered Symbionts

Before microbial functions can be harnessed for xenobiotic detoxification, the native gut microbiota of honey bees should first be protected. Some xenobiotics can perturb gut microbial homeostasis and thereby amplify their toxic effects on honey bees (Wu et al. 2020; Dong et al. 2022; Li et al. 2026). This protected microbial baseline provides the ecological foundation on which more targeted interventions can build.

Building on this foundation, one promising direction is the development of precision probiotics (Daisley et al. 2020; Motta et al. 2022). This involves creating defined mixtures containing bacterial strains known to upregulate host detoxification enzymes or degrade specific, locally prevalent toxins, thereby moving beyond generic probiotics towards tailored solutions. Furthermore, selecting bee lineages that support robust, detoxification‐capable microbial communities could be a long‐term strategy that integrates microbiome resilience as a heritable trait into breeding programmes.

A more targeted frontier is the use of engineered symbionts (Chhun et al. 2024). Utilising synthetic biology to modify native colonising bacteria such as Snodgrassella alvi to express and deliver specific detoxifying enzymes (e.g., for degrading prevalent miticides) directly within the bee gut presents a highly precise strategy (Leonard et al. 2018). This approach, akin to creating ‘living bioreactors’, mitigates ecological concerns because the engineered symbionts are confined to the host. Critical research will focus on ensuring the stability, efficacy and biocontainment of such engineered organisms. Before field application, rigorous biosafety assessment and containment strategies must be developed to prevent unintended horizontal gene transfer.

Future Research Horizons and Integrated Application

Translating symbiont‐mediated detoxification into practical tools will require progress on two fronts. First, the mechanistic basis of microbial detoxification must be resolved more precisely. Future research should prioritise identifying the specific bacterial metabolites that signal host detoxification genes. Concurrently, systematic screening of the gut microbiome's catabolic potential against major pesticide classes is needed. Understanding how xenobiotic exposure reshapes microbial community networks, potentially favouring consortia with complementary detoxification functions, is crucial for predicting and bolstering resilience.

Second, microbial interventions must be validated under hive‐relevant conditions. For probiotics, ensuring that introduced strains colonise and persist in the gut of recipient bees will require optimised formulations and delivery methods, such as incorporation into pollen patties (Daisley et al. 2023). For engineered symbionts, beyond biocontainment, assessing the fitness costs of expressing heterologous enzymes is vital to ensure ecological safety and persistence. Additionally, developing rapid microbiome profiling techniques could provide beekeepers with an early‐warning system for chemical stress, enabling proactive management.

Conclusion: Toward a Holobiont Approach to Bee Health

In conclusion, viewing the honey bee as a holobiont—an integrated unit of host and microbiota—is essential to understanding its detoxification resilience. The gut microbiome is not merely an accessory but a key metabolic organ that is capable of transforming or sequestering selected xenobiotics and enhancing host defence pathways. By shifting our focus and investing in microbial strategies—from precision probiotics and engineered symbionts to microbiome‐assisted breeding—we can develop next‐generation tools to fortify honey bees. Integrating microbiome monitoring into apicultural practise and pesticide risk assessment may further strengthen pollinator protection. This perspective bridges insect toxicology and microbial biotechnology, opening a critical frontier for pollinator conservation and sustainable agriculture in the face of global chemical challenges.
