Exploiting conformational changes in a caffeine aptamer to engineer synthetic RNA devices
Authors: Leon Kraus, Vincent J Gunawan, Robin Johannson, Sarah Hallstein, Sina Block, Gaynor A Campbell, Alya Alameen, Susan J Rosser, Adam A Mol, Beatrix Suess
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA devices, including riboswitches, aptazymes, and RNA-based biosensors, have become essential components in synthetic and molecular biology. These systems make use of RNA’s modularity and structural diversity to build programmable tools for sensing, regulation, and cellular computation. To drive these systems, RNA aptamers usually need to undergo a conformational change upon ligand binding. However, the limited availability of such aptamers has restricted the development and application of RNA devices, particularly in mammalian systems. Traditional selection methods often prioritize high-affinity binding, resulting in a scarcity of conformation-switching aptamers. Here, we addressed this limitation by selecting a caffeine-binding aptamer with RNA Capture-SELEX and in vivo screenings. This aptamer functioned as a modular regulator of riboswitches and ribozymes in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and mammalian cells. Through new optofluidic screenings, we overcame throughput and sequence-function challenges inherent in ribozyme screening for mammalia. Additionally, a straightforward grafting method transformed the aptamer into a fluorogenic iSpinach aptasensor. This work demonstrates the successful selection of a modular and communicating aptamer for a challenging target like caffeine and establishes robust strategies for their modular integration into diverse RNA platforms. These strategies pave the way for broadening the repertoire of aptamers and expanding the potential of RNA-based synthetic biology.

Introduction

Synthetic RNA devices have established themselves as versatile tools that go beyond RNA’s traditional role as a simple messenger between DNA and proteins. Over the past two decades, many studies explored the structural and functional diversity of RNA to engineer sequences capable of sensing a variety of molecules [1–4], regulating cellular functions [5–12], and driving biochemical devices in vitro [13, 14]. These programmable and structure-based systems are powerful tools for applications ranging from diagnostics [15, 16] and therapeutics [17, 18] to metabolic engineering, and synthetic cellular computation [19–21]. Often these tools rely on the modular nature of RNA and the diverse structures it can form [22–24].

Fundamental building blocks of many RNA devices are aptamers, which are synthetic, structured oligoribonucleotides that form specific and high-affinity interactions with their binding partner. While aptamers have evolved naturally, they can also be developed through in vitro selection strategies, most of which are based on the well-established Systematic Evolution of Ligands by EXponential Enrichment (SELEX) method [25, 26].

Aptamers that undergo inherent conformational changes through ligand binding are crucial for the engineering of synthetic RNA devices. Previous studies made use of a handful of well-characterized aptamers—such as the tetracycline, theophylline, and guanine-binding aptamers—that exhibited such conformational changes to drive riboswitches, aptazymes, RNA-based biosensors, and more [27–30]. But the limited number of appropriate aptamers constrains RNA device engineering to a few select ligands. Since traditional selection strategies focus on high binding affinities, they often yield aptamers with pre-formed structures that bind ligands without an entropic penalty associated with structural reorganization. Expectedly, while these protocols can lead to highly affine aptamer-ligand pairs, they have limited success in enriching conformation-switching aptamers. RNA Capture-SELEX followed by in vivo screening has proven to be an effective protocol for the identification of conformation-switching aptamers. This pipeline selects for RNA aptamers that undergo structural change and subsequent release from a capture oligonucleotide [31].

The majority of conformation-switching aptamers that have been selected in vitro recognize antibiotics, such as tetracycline, neomycin, paromomycin, tobramycin, and ciprofloxacin [12, 27, 31–33]. Antibiotics possess favorable attributes for aptamer selection, including multiple hydrogen bonding sites and positive charges that facilitate interactions with negatively charged RNA and sufficient solubility in water. Antibiotics—like aminoglycosides—have been amongst the first targets for aptamer selections in the 1990s and early 2000s [31, 34, 35], establishing a foundation for subsequent riboswitch development. Later, the tetracycline aptamer has proven itself as a highly capable platform for RNA device development [27, 29]. However, the use of antibiotic-binding aptamers hinders the spectrum of biological applications due to their antimicrobial activity on bacteria and side effects in eukaryotic cells and organisms. In contrast, aptamer domains that were discovered in naturally evolved riboswitches usually have the innate ability to switch their conformation but have evolved to sense metabolites that are usually already present in the cell in varying concentrations and are parts of complex metabolic networks. In this context, the popularity of the theophylline-binding aptamer in synthetic RNA engineering can be explained through its capacity to regulate and the fact that it is neither part of a common metabolic pathway nor does it show antimicrobial properties [36].

Caffeine, like theophylline, is a water-soluble xanthine derivative with limited capacity to form polar interactions, though it allows hydrophobic interactions, including pi-stacking with nucleobases [37]. There has been a recent interest in caffeine as a selection target due to its drug-like properties, relevance in medicine, and use as an indicator in wastewater monitoring [38–40]. A grafting approach based on the guanine riboswitch sequence allowed the development of a caffeine-responsive riboswitch for Bacillus subtilis [41]. Furthermore, through a randomization approach, another study succeeded in repurposing the theophylline riboswitch to also accept caffeine as its ligand alongside the original affinity to theophylline [42].

While these caffeine-responsive RNA devices have shown promise in prokaryotic systems, translating similar selection and engineering approaches to mammalian cells presents significant challenges. Compared to the engineering advances of RNA devices in bacteria, development of new devices in mammalian cells has proven labor-intensive and limited in throughput, since the sequence-function relationship is poorly maintained outside stable integrations, single construct transfections, or self-regulating RNA elements. Established medium-throughput methods like randomized fluorescence-activated cell sorting screenings and next-generation sequencing (NGS) approaches fall short in their capacity to distinguish regulatory active sequences from selection artifacts. This is due to their inability to link the observed phenotype to single sequences over time. In contrast, observing single clones in real-time microscopy or similar experiments restricts the number of individual sequences that can be observed at a time. Here, new technologies and protocols for higher-throughput screenings can be highly valuable.

One of these more recent technologies is a chip-based, optofluidic system, which allows the simultaneous cultivation, sorting, and constant optical monitoring of tens of thousands of cells while allowing constant control of the cellular environment. Originally devised for therapeutic antibody discovery, antibody engineering, vaccine development, and cell line development, systems like the Beacon® Optofluidics by Bruker can be considered a powerful tool for the selection of RNA devices in mammalian cell lines [43].

In this study, a new caffeine-binding RNA aptamer was selected using RNA Capture-SELEX. It was thoroughly analyzed and then successively engineered into various RNA devices. It was employed as a well-behaved “ribosome roadblock” style riboswitch [44] with high expression levels and used for a gene-regulating hammerhead aptazyme [45] in S. cerevisiae. Grafting the aptamer onto the fluorescent iSpinach aptamer [2, 46] turned it into a caffeine-detecting RNA biosensor. Beyond that, it was also engineered into a controllable hammerhead aptazyme in mammalian cells using chip-based optofluidics screening. For each RNA device the aptamer’s sequence only needed to be minimally adapted (Supplementary Fig. S1).

With this study we demonstrate that the de novo identification of capable aptamers that can be interchangeably implemented in multiple RNA device platforms is now accessible for challenging ligands. We are convinced that this expansion of suitable modules will further increase the popularity and wider adoption of RNA device development and application.

Materials and methods

In vitro transcription

RNA was transcribed in vitro and purified for isothermal calorimetry (ITC) and in-line probing. Sequence-complementary DNA oligonucleotides were ordered and then annealed by heating them to 90°C for 5 min and letting them cool to room temperature. These annealed double-stranded DNA sequences contained a T7 promoter ending in three Gs upstream of the sequence to be transcribed. Transcription ran overnight with the following conditions: 200 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 20 mM Mg-acetate, 2 mM spermidine, 4 mM of each ribonucleoside triphosphate and 20 mM dithiothreitol (“DTT”), and 50 U/ml T7 RNA polymerase (homemade). The product of this reaction was then precipitated and washed using ethanol and dissolved in ultrapure water. It was then mixed with an equal volume of 2× RNA loading dye [deionized formamide with 0.25 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.02% bromophenol blue, and 0.01% xylene cyanole] and run on a denaturing 10% polyacrylamide (PAA) gel containing 8 M urea. RNA was made visible using ultraviolet shadowing, and the respective parts of the gel were cut out and sliced into small pieces. They were then left overnight in a solution of 300 mM sodium acetate (pH 6.5). The supernatant containing RNA that diffused out of the gel pieces was recovered, and the RNA was precipitated and washed using ethanol. The pellet was then dissolved in ultrapure water.

RNA Capture-SELEX

RNA Capture-SELEX was performed as described before [12, 47] and according to the recently developed in vitro selection guidelines (Minimal Aptamer Publication Standards) [48]. The pool template was designed with two constant regions at the 5′ and 3′ ends to allow polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, in vitro transcription, and reverse transcription (see Table 1). A constant, 13 nt long docking sequence was located between a 40 nt randomized region (5′) and a 10 nt randomized region (3′). This relatively long library was chosen to foster more complex RNA folds and to increase the chances of selecting sequences that may sequester the docking sequence and thus release binding aptamers. The DNA template pool was supplied by Microsynth (Balgach, Switzerland). The single-stranded DNA of the template pool was amplified using PCR: 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 9.0, 50 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.2 mM of each deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate, 200 nM template pool, 2 μM primer pool_fwd, 2 μM primer pool_rev, and 50 U/ml Taq DNA polymerase (NEB). A calculated total of 1015 template molecules were used for the PCR in a volume of 15 ml. The volume was distributed into 96-well plates, and only five cycles were used for the PCR using the following settings: (95°C for 2 min, five cycles of {95°C for 1 min, 54°C for 1 min, 72°C for 1 min}, 72°C for 3 min). After the PCR, all reaction batches were combined, and the DNA was precipitated by adding 2.5 volumes of ethanol. The pellet was washed twice using 70% ethanol and then dissolved in ultrapure water. Concentration of the purified PCR product was measured using photometric analysis, and ~5 × 1015 molecules were transcribed in vitro with the following conditions: 200 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 20 mM Mg-acetate, 2 mM spermidine, 4 mM of each ribonucleoside triphosphate and 20 mM DTT, 50 U/ml T7 RNA polymerase (homemade), and 40 U/ml ribonuclease inhibitor (moloX). The reaction was placed at 37°C and ran overnight. It was then precipitated with ethanol as described earlier. One volume of RNA loading dye was added, and the mixture was loaded onto a denaturing 6% PAA gel (8 M urea). After the gel ran, the RNA band was visualized using ultraviolet shadowing and sliced out. It was then cut up into smaller pieces and left overnight in a solution of 300 mM sodium acetate at a pH of 6.5. The supernatant was taken and the RNA precipitated using ethanol as described earlier.

SELEX pool template and oligonucleotide sequences

Concentration of the purified RNA pool solution was determined using photometric analysis and a volume containing 1015 RNA molecules was used for the initial round of SELEX. Two nanomoles of biotinylated capture oligonucleotide and 40 µl of 5× Capture-SELEX buffer (“CSB”; final concentration of the 1× buffer: 40 mM HEPES, 250 mM KCl, 20 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, pH 7.4) were added. Buffer conditions were chosen to mimic the cytosolic ionic conditions of S. cerevisiae with elevated Mg2+ concentration. The solution was then filled with ultrapure water to 200 µl. Capture oligonucleotide and RNA sequences were then hybridized by being heated to 65°C for 5 min and then cooled to 21°C for 30 min. The hybridized sequences were then mixed with 1 ml Dynabeads® M-270 (ThermoFisher) and gently mixed on a lab rotator for 1 h at room temperature. Beforehand, the beads were washed three times with 500 µl of Bind & Wash Buffer each (5 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 0.5 M EDTA) and then suspended in 300 µl of 1× CSB. The tubes containing the beads, which held the immobilized RNA pools, were then placed in a magnetic rack. After the beads had collected at the side of the tube, the supernatant was removed with a pipette. The beads were then suspended in 500 µl of 1× CSB and agitated. This wash step was then repeated twice for a total of three washes. The second wash step included heating the beads to 30°C for the removal of weakly hybridized sequences. The beads were then incubated with the target compound of the selection to specifically elute target-binding RNAs. The supernatant, which now contained eluted RNA, was recovered. The RNA was then precipitated by adding a solution of sodium acetate (pH 6.5) to a final concentration of 0.3 M and adding 2.5 volumes of ethanol. 15 µg of GlycoBlue™ (Ambion) were added to aid the precipitation. After centrifugation, the pellet was washed twice with 70% ethanol and two more centrifugation steps. The pellet was then left to dry in an open 1.5 ml tube and then dissolved in 50 µl of ultrapure water. Next, reverse transcription followed by PCR amplification was performed with the following conditions: 20 mM Tris–HCl, 0.15 mM KCl, 2.25 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, 1.5 µM Pool_rev oligo, 1.2 mM of each deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate, 200 U of Superscript™ II reverse transcriptase (ThermoFisher), and 1 µl Taq (homemade). For reverse transcription the mixture was incubated for 10 min at 54°C and then at 95°C for 1 min to inactivate the reverse transcriptase. Then, 1 µl of 100 µM Pool_fwd oligo was added, and PCR was performed with the following settings: (95°C for 10 s, 58°C for 10 s, and 72°C for 20 s). The number of cycles was adjusted depending on amplification progression, which was monitored using agarose gel electrophoresis. After the first round of SELEX, the transcription was performed in the presence of radioactively labeled uridine triphosphate (UTP). Phosphorus-32 was thereby incorporated in the sugar-phosphate backbone of the RNA molecules. The transcription reactions in following rounds were done by using 10 µl of the reverse-transcription-PCR product and mixing it with 40 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 5 mM DTT, 2.5 mM of each ribonucleoside triphosphate, 15 mM MgCl2, 100 U T7 RNA polymerase (homemade), 40 U ribonuclease inhibitor (moloX), and 33 nM [α32P]-UTP (3000 Ci/mmol, Hartmann Analytics) in a total volume of 100 μl. The mixture was then incubated overnight at 37°C. The product was then precipitated using ammonium acetate and ethanol. The RNA was washed twice with 70% ethanol and then dissolved in 50 µl of ultrapure water. After the first selection round, 100 kCPM of radiolabeled RNA and smaller volumes of consumables were used. One hundred and fifty microliters of magnetic beads, 0.2 nmol of biotinylated_CO, and 100 kCPM of RNA diluted in 50 μl of 1× CSB were used. Starting with the second SELEX round, each wash and each elution step were monitored by measuring the radioactivity using a Tri-Carb 2800TR Liquid Scintillation Analyzer (PerkinElmer).

Next-generation sequencing analysis

The 15 rounds of RNA Capture-SELEX were analyzed using NGS. The RNA pools of each SELEX round were first reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA). Illumina adapter sequences were then incorporated through PCR amplification using Q5 DNA polymerase and primers designed to anneal to the constant regions of the pool. For each round a unique, barcoded forward primer was used, which included a distinct 6-nucleotide sequence to enable sample multiplexing. The prepared samples were then submitted for sequencing to GENEWIZ (Leipzig, Germany), using an Illumina NovaSeq X sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, USA). The fastq files were then processed using a custom Python script. After a demultiplexing step, the sequences were ranked for each SELEX round, and the top 100 most abundant sequences were extracted. Within these, the minimum, maximum, median, and first and third quartile reads per million (RPM) were determined, as well as the ranking of the caffeine aptamer sequence. Secondary structure probing and prediction

RNA was prepared through in vitro transcription from PCR-amplified DNA-template oligos and purified by PAA-gel excision. One hundred twenty-five picomoles of RNA in 150 µl ultrapure water were heated to 95°C for 5 min and subsequently cooled on ice for 2 min before adding 75 µl folding buffer (333 mM NaCl, 333 mM HEPES pH 8, 33 mM MgCl2) and incubating at 37°C for 20 min. Ninety microliters of refolded RNA was added to 10 µl of 100 mM 1-methyl-7-nitroisatoicanhydride (1M7, Sigma–Aldrich) in water-free dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma–Aldrich) and incubated at 37°C for 2 min. Reactions were purified by gel filtration using G-25 columns (Cytiva). Ten microliters of the purified RNA was used as a template for reverse transcription using SuperScript IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription products were barcoded by PCR with the primers Pool_I5_fw and Pool_I7_rev (Table 2), purified using the Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up kit (Promega), and sent for amplicon sequencing (Azenta). NGS data were analyzed using ShapeMapper v2.3, and reactivities were used for shape-assisted RNA secondary structure predictions using RNAfold from the ViennaRNA Package version 2.4.17 [49, 50]. The resulting dot-bracket annotations were visualized as 2D structures.

In-line probing

RNA for in-line probing was prepared as described earlier with an additional 9 nt long unstructured but transcribed sequence (CAACAACAA) upstream of the actual riboswitch sequence. This leading sequence was added to increase resolution of the cleavage pattern at sequence positions close to the 5′ end of the riboswitch sequence. Very short cleavage products are hard to resolve, which is mitigated by this sequence addition. Transcribed and purified RNA was dephosphorylated using alkaline phosphatase (from cow intestine, Roche). The phosphatase was heat-inactivated and the RNA was 5′ labeled in a total reaction volume of 20 µl containing 20 U of T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB), 0.04 mCi of [γ32P]-adenosine triphosphate (3000 Ci/mmol, Hartmann Analytic), and the manufacturer-supplied buffer. The RNA was then loaded onto a denaturing 10% PAA gel containing 8 M urea and run. The RNA was visualized through exposing and developing an X-ray film, after which the correct band was cut from the gel, and the RNA was recovered as described earlier and measured using a scintillation counter. Thirty-five kilo-counts-per-minute (kCPM) of RNA in in-line buffer (20 mM MgCl2, 50 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM KCl) were then incubated at 22°C for 72 h. A size marker of all possible cleavage positions was generated by incubating the RNA at 96°C in 50 mM Na2CO3 (pH 9.0). A marker that indicates every G-position of the sequence was prepared by a T1-RNase reaction. For this, the RNA was treated with T1-RNase (Roche) under denaturing conditions (5 M urea, 0.75 mM EDTA, 25 mM sodium citrate) for 5 min at 55°C. The results of these reactions were solved in a 5 mM urea solution and equal amounts of RNA (as measured by radioactivity) were run on a 10% PAA gel (8 M urea). The gel was then dried on a heated vacuum bed, after which the gel was used to expose a phosphoimaging screen, followed by phosphoimaging on an Amersham Typhoon™ imager (GE Healthcare).

Isothermal calorimetry

Aptamers were transcribed in vitro and purified through polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis as described earlier. Purified aptamers were buffer exchanged three times by adding 3.5 ml of 1× CSB using Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filter units (Millipore) with a 10 kDa cutoff and spinning at 5000 × g until ~500 µl remained. RNA concentrations were determined by measuring the absorption at 260 nm using a NanoPhotometer® (Implen) and dividing it by the calculated molar extinction coefficient for each sequence. The molar extinction coefficients were calculated by summing up the extinction coefficients for the isolated ribonucleotides (AMP: 15.02 mM−1cm−1, GMP: 12.08 mM−1cm−1, UMP: 9.66 mM−1cm−1, CMP: 7.07 mM−1cm−1) and multiplying by 0.9 to account for base stacking effects and 1000 M−1cm−1 for correct unit conversion [51]. The aptamers were then diluted to a final concentration of 20 µM with 1× CSB. The respective ligands were dissolved in 1× CSB and diluted to a final concentration of 200 µM. ITCs were performed using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC following a 13-injection protocol with a first injection of 0.4 µl and 12 subsequent injections of 3.0 µl injections or a 19-injection protocol with a first injection of 0.4 µl and 18 injections of 2.0 µl, with a spacing of 150 s in between injections. The data were analyzed using the MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis Software. A titration of buffer into RNA was included as a control and subtracted as a baseline. For theophylline, theobromine, and paraxanthine, a low-c analysis was performed and n set to 1. All titrations were performed twice.

Light up aptasensor

Aptamers were in vitro transcribed from PCR products using T7 RNA polymerase and purified by gel extraction as described earlier. RNA concentrations were determined by measuring the absorption at 260 nm and dividing it by the calculated molar extinction coefficient for the respective sequence. Aptamers were diluted to 1 µM using ultrapure water and denatured at 85°C for 2 min and left to cool at room temperature. DFHBI-1T [(5Z)-5-[(3,5-difluoro-4-hydroxyphenyl)methylene]-3,5-dihydro-2-methyl-3-(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl)-4H-imidazol-4-one] (Sigma–Aldrich) was used as fluorophore for all measurements. Fifteen microliters of the diluted aptamers were added to an equal volume of 2× iSpinach folding buffer (200 mM KCl, 80 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 20 mM MgCl2, 20 µM DFHBI-1T) containing the respective ligand at 2× concentration in a MicroAmp 96-Well reaction plate (Applied Biosystems). The plates were measured using a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR cycler (Applied Biosystems). After 1 h of incubation at 25°C, the fluorescence intensity of each well was measured five times at 5-min increments, and mean values were presented as a percentage relative to the values obtained for the iSpinach aptamer without any ligand aside from DFHBI-1T.

Cultivation of yeast and cytometric measurements

Untransformed S. cerevisiae RS453 cells were cultured using yeast peptone dextrose medium [“YPD”: 1% (w/v) yeast extract, 2% (w/v) Bacto Peptone, 4% (w/v) glucose] as liquid media or containing 1.5% (w/v) agar for plates. Transformed cells carrying the URA3 gene were cultured in synthetic complete dextrose medium lacking uracil [“SCD-Ura”: 0.55% (w/v) (NH4)2SO4, 0.2% (w/v) yeast nitrogen base, 2% (w/v) glucose, 2% (v/v) MEM Amino Acids Solution (50×), 4% (v/v) adenine stock solution (300 µg/ml), and 1.5% (w/v) agar for plates]. To measure the fluorescence of individual constructs by flow cytometry, yeast cultures were inoculated from single colonies, cultured for 24 h at 30°C, and subsequently split 1/20 with fresh media with and without 250 µM caffeine. After 24 h, 20 µl of each culture was diluted 1/10 with sterile phosphate-buffered saline, and 10 000 cells were measured using a Cytoflex-S Flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Background fluorescence within the channel for eGFP (Ex: 488/8 Em: 525/40) was accounted for in all cells by subtracting the mean fluorescence intensity values of cells transformed with the pCBB06 (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Sequences) plasmid, which lacks the start codon for eGFP. eGFP fluorescence values were then divided by the corresponding mCherry fluorescence (Ex: 561/10 Em: 610/20). The obtained values were then divided by the mean values obtained for cells carrying the plasmid pCBB05 (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Sequences), which constitutively expresses eGFP and carries no structured element within the 5′ untranslated region (UTR).

Background fluorescence was accounted for by measuring S. cerevisiae RS453 cells carrying the control plasmid pCBB06, which lacks the start codon for GFP, and subtracting their average FITC-A values from each measured well. Normalized GFP values were then generated by dividing the background-subtracted FITC-A value for each well by the corresponding ECD-A value.

Dynamic ranges were calculated by dividing the average normalized GFP fluorescence value of each construct in the absence of the ligand by the average in its presence.

Construction of expression vectors for yeast

Vector fragments were generated by performing an AgeI-HF and NheI restriction digest of the plasmid pCBB06 followed by gel extraction. Individual sequences were inserted by ligating hybridized forward and reverse oligonucleotides carrying the respective aptamer sequences and corresponding sticky ends into the vector fragment, followed by transformation of E. coli DH5α. Plasmids were isolated and S. cerevisiae RS453 cells transformed using the Frozen-EZ Yeast Transformation Kit (Zymo Research).

Yeast libraries were generated by PCR amplifying the enriched SELEX rounds using the primers CBB213 and CBB215 to attach 100 bp long homologous ends and purifying the product using the Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up Kit (Promega). Afterwards, S. cerevisiae RS453 cells were transformed via electroporation following a modified protocol of Benatuil et al. [52]. Four milliliters of YPD were inoculated from a fresh streak out of S. cerevisiae RS453 and incubated at 30°C and 150 rpm overnight. This culture was then used to inoculate 100 ml of YPD, which was grown at 30°C and 115 rpm to an OD600 of 1.6 before being harvested in two 50 ml centrifugation tubes for 3 min at 3000 × g and 4°C. Cells were washed twice with 25 ml of ice-cold, ultrapure water. Cell pellets were resuspended in 10 ml of LiAc-DTT solution (0.1 M LiAc, 10 mM DTT) each and transferred into a single flask. Cells were conditioned for 30 min at 125 rpm before harvesting at 3000 × g at 4°C for 3 min. The cell pellet was washed once with 25 ml of ice-cold electroporation buffer (sorbitol 1 M, CaCl2 0.1 mM) before being resuspended in electroporation buffer to a final volume of 1.5 ml. Four hundred microliters of the cell suspension was transferred to a Gene Pulser® cuvette (0.2 cm, Bio-Rad) together with 1 µg of the AgeI-HF and NheI digest and 1 µg of insert fragments. Transformations were kept on ice for 5 min before electroporation at 2.5 kV and 25 µF using a MicroPulser (Bio-Rad). Electroporations were transferred to 10 ml of Sorbitol-YPD mix (Sorbitol 0.5 M, 0.5× YPD) and cultured at 30°C and 125 rpm. One hundred microliters of the culture was plated onto SCD-Ura plates and incubated at 30°C for 2 days to determine transformation efficiencies. The remaining culture was transferred to a 50 ml centrifugation tube, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 3 min, and the supernatant was removed. Cells were then resuspended in SCD-Ura and transferred to a 250 ml culture. Transformants were cultured at 30°C and 115 rpm for 48 h before 25 ml of the culture was transferred to 225 ml of fresh SCD-Ura. After another 24 h of culturing at 30°C and 115 rpm, part of the culture was diluted 1000-fold with fresh media and plated onto SCD-Ura plates.

Mutational studies

Partially randomized aptamer pools were generated by amplifying a semi-randomized oligonucleotide from Microsynth (Balgach, Switzerland) by PCR using the primers CBB213 and CBB215 (Table 3). Each underlined nucleotide was partially randomized with a chance of 1.5% for each of the other nucleobases. This 4.5% chance of mutation per nucleotide during synthesis results in a theoretically expected 2.07 mutations per sequence in the 46 nt long library. However, actually observed mutation rates are often a little below the theoretical value. The generated fragments were inserted into the cloning vector pCBB06 using yeast homologous recombination as described earlier. Individual clones were screened for their regulatory activity by flow cytometry, and plasmids from exhibiting a regulatory fold between 0.96 and 1.04 were isolated, retransformed into E. coli DH5α, and sent for sequencing. Identified single point mutations were mapped onto the predicted aptamer secondary structure using a floating-point average for each position and both neighboring nucleotides.

Cell analysis on the Beacon® platform

Suspension Chinese hamster ovary K1 cells (CHO-K1) were routinely cultured in 125 ml Erlenmeyer flasks (Biofil) containing CD CHO medium (Life Technologies, 10743-029) supplemented with 8 mM GlutaMAX (Life Technologies, 35050-061) and 1/500 Anti-Clump (Life Technologies, 0010057AE). Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ and agitated at 130 rpm. Conditioned media was prepared from untransfected host cells grown for 48 h, then centrifuged, sterile filtered, and frozen at −20°C before use. For cell loading onto a 1750b OptoSelect chip on the Beacon® Optofluidic System (Bruker), media was additionally supplemented with 20% conditioned media and 1/50 B27 (A1486701) to reduce free radical formation during optoelectric positioning. A pool of stably transfected cells was loaded onto the chip, and a Target Penning Strategy (TPS) workflow was used to selectively pen only mCherry-positive cells into individual nanopens. A total of 1388 single cells were successfully penned across the chip. Media was changed to growth media plus 20% conditioned media, plus 1 mM caffeine. The Beacon® Optofluidic System allows constant perfusion of media across the chip, warmed to 37°C and 5% CO2. Brightfield and GFP images were taken each hour for a total of 8 h, then every 4 h. The caffeine-containing media was removed after 24 h, and cells were imaged every 4 h for a further 3 days to observe cell growth and GFP fluorescence changes. Image Analysis Software (Bruker) was used to analyze individual nanopen GFP fluorescence changes over time and determine the cells that responded to caffeine. A total of 38 nanopens were selected for export into a 96-well plate for expansion or cDNA extraction. Cells were exported using optoelectric positioning into media supplemented with 20% conditioned media, 1/50 B27 (A1486701), and 1/40 ClonaCell ACF (StemCell Technologies 03820) to aid outgrowth. cDNA was extracted from the exported cells and used for PCR amplification and subsequent sequencing. Twenty selected aptazyme sequences were cloned and transiently tested in CHO-K1 cells to assess their switch behavior in the presence of 1 mM caffeine. The media compositions were as follows. Culture media: 80% growth media, 20% conditioned media, 1/500 anti-clump (Invitrogen 0010057AE); Load media: 80% growth media, 20% conditioned media, 1/50 B27 (A1486701); Export media: 80% growth media, 20% conditioned media, 1/50 B27 (A1486701), 1/40 Clonacell ACF (StemCell Technologies 03820).

Single-cell cDNA recovery

Single cells from selected nanopens were exported by optoelectric positioning into 5 µl of 2× Buffer TCL (Qiagen) overlaid with mineral oil (Sigma–Aldrich) in a 96-well plate. Plates were removed, centrifuged briefly, and stored at −80°C until further processing. Plates were thawed on ice, and 10 µl of AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) were added to each well, mixed, and incubated at RT for 20 min. Samples were magnetically separated on the MagDTR 96-well separator for 5 min. Supernatants were removed and bead pellets washed twice with 80% ethanol. Bead pellets were then resuspended in a PCR master mix prepared as instructed in the Opto B Discovery cDNA synthesis kit (Bruker). First strand cDNA synthesis was carried out for 12 cycles: [42°C for 90 min, 12 × (50°C for 2 min, 42°C for 2 min), heat inactivation at 75°C for 15 min]. The cDNA was used to amplify the aptazyme variant sequence using primers pF and pR (pF: AGCGCGATCACATGGTCCTGCTGGAG, pR: TCATGTCTGGATCTCCATCACCG), and the PCR product was sent for sequencing (Genewiz) using primer pF.

CHO single-cell fluorescence measurements

CHO-K1 cells were cultured in CD CHO medium (Gibco) supplemented with 8 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco), 0.01% (v/v) Pluronic F68 (Thermo Scientific), 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma), and 1% (v/v) Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Gibco). Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. For transfection, 120 000 CHO-K1 cells were seeded per well in a 24-well plate. Cells were transfected with 100 ng of a reporter plasmid encoding mCherry and eGFP, with expression regulated by caffeine-responsive aptazyme variants in the 3′ UTR. Transfections were performed using 1 μl Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher) per well, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Four hours post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced with CD CHO medium supplemented with or without 1 mM caffeine (Sigma).

Reporter gene expression was analyzed 24 h post-transfection using flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa™ Cell Analyzer). eGFP and mCherry were excited using a 488 nm laser with a 550/30 filter, and a 561 nm laser with a 670/30 filter, respectively. Data were acquired using BD FACSDiva software. Background fluorescence was gated out based on untransfected controls, and populations were selected accordingly. Mean fluorescence intensity and standard deviations were calculated from three biological replicates and normalized to mCherry fluorescence. Each experiment was independently repeated three times. The inactive aptazyme variant (iHHR), set at 100%, served as the reference for maximum gene expression. Fold changes in gene expression were calculated by comparing fluorescence intensity in the presence and absence of caffeine.

Caffeine toxicity assay

For the caffeine toxicity assay, 2 ml of CHO-K1 suspension cells at a density of 3 × 10⁵ cells/ml were seeded into non-treated six-well plates (Nunc™ Non-Treated Multidishes, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were diluted from a culture with a density of 2.03 × 10⁶ cells/ml and a viability of 98%. Caffeine was added to final concentrations of 0.0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0, and 10.0 mM. Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ with agitation at 130 rpm. Cell growth and viability were monitored by direct cell counting at 24, 48, and 72 h. For each measurement, 10 µl of cell suspension was mixed with 10 µl of 0.4% Trypan blue stain (Invitrogen). Subsequently, 10 µl of the mixture was loaded onto a Countess™ cell counting chamber slide (Invitrogen), and total and viable cell numbers were determined using a Countess™ 3 FL automated cell counter (Invitrogen).

The 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was used to assess cell metabolic activity as an indicator of cell viability and proliferation. Viable cells reduce the yellow tetrazolium salt MTT to insoluble purple formazan crystals, which are subsequently solubilized and quantified spectrophotometrically. For the assay, 2 ml of CHO-K1 suspension cells at a density of 3 × 10⁵ cells/ml were seeded into non-treated six-well plates. Caffeine was added to final concentrations of 0.0 or 1.0 mM, and cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ with agitation at 130 rpm. After 24 h, 100 µl of cell suspension (with or without caffeine) was transferred to a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 10 µl MTT labeling reagent [final concentration of 0.5 mg/ml; Cell Proliferation Kit I (MTT); Roche]. The plate was incubated for 4 h at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Subsequently, 100 µl of solubilization buffer was added to each well, and the plate was incubated overnight under the same conditions. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (FLUOstar Omega, BMG Labtech). Formazan absorbance was recorded at 570 nm.

Caffeine-dependent synthetic riboswitches and aptazymes in S. cerevisiae

A caffeine-dependent riboswitch was identified by in vitro selection of caffeine-binding aptamers by RNA Capture-SELEX (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S3), followed by a screening of the enriched pool for regulating aptamers that act as a roadblock for the scanning of the ribosomal preinitiation complex in yeast (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. S4). A thereby identified aptamer sequence was subjected to a stepwise truncation and an apical loop replacement, resulting in the riboswitch sequence Caff-2.1 (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. S5). Analyzing Caff-2.1 with Vienna Package RNAfold [49] generated a secondary structure prediction with a long stem P1 connected to a shorter P2-L2 stem loop via a large internal asymmetrical bulge (joining region J1-2). P1 is interrupted by two bulged-out nucleotides (C7 and A8). The riboswitch Caff-2.1 achieves a dynamic range of 5.3. Replacing the apical loop L2 (UUCG) with a less stable AAAG- or GUUCG-loop, respectively (Caff-2.2 and -5), resulted in a slightly lower dynamic range of 4.8- and 4.7-fold, but at about double the expression levels (Fig. 1d). Notably, a deletion of U16 led to a total loss of regulation, while expression levels remained similar (Supplementary Fig. S5).

To explore the modularity and portability of the aptamer, a hammerhead aptazyme was constructed and tested for its capacity to control gene expression in yeast. The aptamer sequence was grafted onto a type 1 hammerhead ribozyme, replacing its stem P3. The aptazyme constructs were placed into the 3′ UTR of a gfp reporter gene. The optimized aptazyme showed improved switching and was termed HHA-2.3 (Fig. 1c and f, Supplementary Fig. S6). It showed a caffeine-dependent dynamic range of 2.2. An inactive aptazyme variant (iHHR), which served as a control, barely reacted to caffeine (Fig. 1f). The combination of the riboswitch construct located in the 5′ UTR with the aptazyme located in the 3′ UTR resulted in an overall improved dynamic range of 6.5 (HHA-2.3 & Caff-2.2) (Fig. 1e and f).

Analysis of the caffeine aptamer and riboswitch

The successful implementation of the caffeine-binding aptamer was then followed by a detailed biochemical and structural investigation, as well as an NGS analysis of the RNA Capture-SELEX pools.

First, the nucleotide positions that were critical for function/structure formation were identified. For this, we generated a doped library of Caff-2. Each position had a randomization probability of 4.5%, which is expected to result in about one or two mutations per aptamer sequence. The pool was screened in vivo, and 100 loss-of-function mutants were analyzed (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table S3). Interestingly, not a single mutant with increased riboswitch functionality was identified, which could mean that the combination of RNA Capture-SELEX and in vivo screening already yielded a close-to-optimal sequence. Critical positions were predominantly found in J1-2, indicating the importance of this region for ligand binding and/or switching.

This also hints at the existence of sequence pairings throughout P2, but only for the basal part of the proposed P1 structure. This goes in line with the removal of a C nucleotide within P2 that was deleted in the course of the initial truncation studies (Supplementary Fig. S5a). The nucleotide was removed to allow for an uninterrupted helix with a non-Franklin–Watson–Crick U:U pair. This deletion improved regulation, indicating the importance of a P2 stem that provides a scaffolding function (Caff-5). Interestingly, when considering the top predicted minimum free energy (MFE) structures of the aptamer, it appears as if J1-2 may be able to adopt an alternative secondary structure and form a larger internal junction while P2 is extended by two base pairs and P1 is drastically shortened to only six base pairs. This proposed alternative folding was explored by two sequences containing base pair inversions (Caff-6: U18A|A38U, Caff-7: C17G|U18A|A38U|G39C) that were hypothesized to force the formation of a large internal junction (Fig. 2b). Fluorescence measurements of yeast cells transformed with the respective plasmids were performed. Sequences Caff-6 and Caff-7 both retained similar expression levels. Caff-6 kept most of the dynamic range (factor 3.1); however, Caff-7’s dynamic range dropped to 1.9 (Fig. 2c). This shows that the formation of a proposed, single junction with a drastically shorter P1 stem can still facilitate regulation of the riboswitch. The scaffolding function of P2 is further supported by SHAPE-Map and in-line probing experiments performed with the candidates Caff-2.1 and Caff-2.2 (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. S7). The results hint at the existence of the paired regions P1 and P2 but indicate a substantially shorter P1 next to a large, flexible junction (nucleotides 6–16, Fig. 2d) that did not match the RNAfold-predicted secondary structures. Addition of caffeine during the in-line reaction protected large parts of the junction from cleavage, indicating its importance in ligand binding. (Supplementary Fig. S7). While secondary structure predictions supported pairing of nucleotides 9–13 with nucleotides 44–48, SHAPE-Map did not confirm this. Interestingly, an alternate base pairing that allows a longer P2 stem formation (extended by C17:G38 and U18:A37) is possible as depicted in Fig. 2d. In contrast to the top prediction, this further supports the propensity of the sequence to form a larger junction with longer P2, but shorter P1 regions, as illustrated in Fig. 2b.

Next, the specificities and affinities of the riboswitch toward caffeine and the closely related compounds theobromine (ThBr), theophylline (ThPh), and paraxanthine (PaXa) were explored (Fig. 2e). Yeast cells with gfp expression controlled by the riboswitch sequence Caff-2.1 were grown in the presence of all four compounds in separate cultures. Unlike caffeine, neither theobromine, theophylline, nor paraxanthine achieved considerable dynamic ranges (Fig. 2f). Evaluating the ligand recognition of the riboswitch by ITC revealed an affinity of Caff-2.1 for caffeine with a KD value of 4.8 µM. Surprisingly, its affinities toward theobromine and theophylline are only slightly lower with respective KD values of 18.8 µM (ThBr) and 20.1 µM (ThPh). Due to the apparent KD for theophylline and theobromine resulting in low c-values at or below one, resulting in low confidence measurements, we re-performed and validated the ITC measurements under low-c conditions with excess ligand while fixing the stoichiometry at 1:1. No binding could be detected for paraxanthine even when titrating high concentrations of ligand into the aptamer (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table S4). Despite their similar binding affinities, the binding of caffeine is associated with a higher change in temperature than theobromine and theophylline. It is curious that the three compounds share somewhat similar binding affinities but lead to starkly different regulation.

An NGS analysis of the RNA Capture-SELEX pools of all 15 rounds shows a clear sequence enrichment, as can be seen from the increasing median reads starting at round 7 and the drastic increase of top 100 reads after round 6 (Supplementary Fig. S8a). A drop in Shannon entropy past round 7 also supports this (Supplementary Fig. S8b). The identified caffeine aptamer sequence can already be detected in round 1 and reaches its highest rank of 94th most detected sequence in round 8, after which it starts to decline again (Supplementary Fig. S8a).

Sensing caffeine with an iSpinach aptasensor

The next step to explore the aptamer’s modularity was to prove its ability to function outside the context of a cellular system. For this, a biosensor was developed by grafting the caffeine-binding aptamer onto the fluorogenic iSpinach aptamer. The sensor is based on iSpinach’s capacity to bind DFHBI-1T with help of a G-quadruplex structure, prompting it to exhibit green-fluorescent-protein-like fluorescence when assuming a correct fold (Fig. 3a). For the caffeine biosensor presented here, the apical stem-loop of iSpinach was replaced with a truncated version of Caff-2.1 connected through an AUUU–AUUU linker, a linker that was previously identified to work well for the theophylline aptasensor [2] (Fig. 3a). The resulting aptasensor and the original iSpinach sequence were transcribed in vitro. The purified RNA was incubated with increasing caffeine concentrations, and the fluorescence output was measured. The unmodified iSpinach sequence served as a control for unspecific effects of caffeine on fluorescence and as a benchmark. It displayed constantly high fluorescence levels with slight fluctuations between 90% and 110%. An unspecific signal reduction occurred only at caffeine concentrations above 500 µM (Fig. 3b, gray). The signal response of the sensor was approximately sigmoidal. Its baseline fluorescence started out at ~15% of the iSpinach control’s fluorescence and achieved a first doubling of the signal at around 100 µM of caffeine. The highest observed signal reached ~80% of the control’s brightness at 2.5 mM of caffeine (Fig. 3b, ochre). The aptasensor was tested for its response to theophylline at 500 µM, where a comparatively very low signal response was observed (Fig. 3c). The presented sensor therefore achieved good specificity for caffeine, which is in line with the results obtained in the reporter gene assays.

Selection of caffeine-dependent hammerhead aptazyme in CHO cells

The next challenge of the aptamer’s modularity was whether it could perform in the complex environment of a mammalian cell. The design HHA2.3 (Fig. 1c) did not show detectable regulation when tested in mammalian cells. So, to adapt the aptamer for use in this context, we opted for an improved aptazyme design, which necessitated a novel screening strategy. An aptazyme pool was generated by replacing the P1 stem of a type 3 hammerhead ribozyme with a truncated variant of Caff-2.1 while replacing the L2 loop with five randomized nucleotides (Fig. 4a). This design targeted the loop–loop interaction that is known to be crucial for hammerhead cleavage in vivo. The resulting pool was inserted into the 3′ UTR of an eGFP reporter within a genomic integration cassette. The cassette additionally encodes mCherry as an internal normalization control and a puromycin resistance gene for selection. The entire cassette is flanked by inverted terminal repeats required for stable genomic integration via the piggyBac transposon system [54] into suspension CHO-K1 cells (Fig. 4b). Since this transposon-based system does not allow control of the number and location of genomic integration, the mCherry signal served as an internal standard for expression levels of the reporter construct. The Beacon® optofluidic system by Bruker Cellular Analysis (formerly Berkeley Lights) was then used for live-cell high-throughput screening [43]. The optofluidics chip enabled the cultivation, continuous optical measurement, and selection of individual clones over an extended period of time (Fig. 4c). The chip allows the positioning of individual cells using light-induced electrical fields that occur when its semiconductive surface is illuminated.

Single cells were loaded into NanoPen® chambers, and 1 mM caffeine was added to the culture medium. A concentration of 1 mM caffeine was selected, as it represented the highest concentration that did not significantly affect CHO-K1 cell viability (Supplementary Fig. S9). Following a 24-h incubation, the medium was replaced with caffeine-free medium. The Beacon® system enabled continuous monitoring of cell fluorescence over a period of 87 h. Multiple clones exhibited a strong increase in fluorescence in the presence of caffeine, which was reversible upon caffeine removal. This controllable behavior is depicted for one selected candidate in Fig. 4d (see also SI_video.mp4 for a video; time scale is not identical). Out of 1388 screened cells, 38 clones showing caffeine-induced fluorescence were exported from the chip and prepared for sequencing. To ensure that no undesired genomic mutations influenced the observed, caffeine-responsive phenotype, twenty selected sequences were recloned and transiently expressed in CHO-K1 cells. Fourteen of the 20 aptazyme variants showed increased eGFP fluorescence in the presence of caffeine. Clones B4 and D9 demonstrated tight off-regulation, with fluorescence levels comparable to the active Hammerhead ribozyme control. Clone B4 showed an average dynamic range of 3.7, while clone D9 showed an average of 3.6 (Fig. 4e). Analysis of the 14 caffeine-responsive L2 loop sequences revealed no clear nucleotide preference at position 1, a preference for G or U at position 2, and a strong preference for G at positions 3, 4, and 5 (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). However, given the limited number of sequences analyzed, additional variants will be required to determine whether this pattern represents a genuine sequence preference underlying caffeine-dependent aptazyme switching.

The implementation as a hammerhead aptazyme in mammalian cells completed the exploration of the modularity of the caffeine-binding aptamer and proved that it can function in various designs and contexts.

Discussion

In this study we report the de novo selection, characterization, and modular implementation of a new caffeine-specific RNA aptamer capable of regulating RNA devices. It was applied for the engineering of caffeine-responsive riboswitches, aptazymes, and biosensors in vitro, in yeast, and in mammalian cells and proved to be an effective and novel building block for engineering with high specificity for its target molecule caffeine (Supplementary Fig. S1).

While the aptamer’s binding affinity is comparable to that of previously reported caffeine-binding DNA aptamers [56], one would still expect it to perform poorly at driving a specific conformational change within a riboswitch or aptamer due to its limited capacity to form hydrogen bonds restricting its interaction pattern. However, RNA Capture SELEX presumably selects for aptamers with ligand-binding sites that are not highly pre-formed. Such aptamers may undergo binding free energy changes penalized for unfavorable entropic contributions of reorganization. This may be compensated by more favorable enthalpic binding contributions such as base stacking or Franklin–Whatson–Crick like base-pairing, which caffeine can indeed form with nucleobases [37].

Remarkably, the observed expression levels and dynamic ranges fall well within the magnitude observed in similar, previous designs using antibiotic-respondent aptamers. Furthermore, since reporter genes and their corresponding products such as luciferases or fluorescent proteins were developed for their sensitivity and stability, such dynamic ranges correspond to perfectly suitable regulation of genes of interest [57].

Along this line, while apparent KDs between the closely related compounds theophylline, theobromine, and caffeine are in the same range (with 20.1 µM, 18.8 µM, and 4.8 µM, respectively, determined by ITC) and paraxanthine is unable to bind entirely, only caffeine has the potential to control. This was apparent both in gene regulation and biosensing. We attribute this to subtle differences in binding of the three ligands to the aptamer, where only caffeine may trigger a substantial stabilization of the aptamer despite two of the other compounds also being able to bind. Since the similar binding affinities imply similar binding free energy changes, only caffeine binding might be able to overcome a larger entropic penalty associated with binding and restructuring of the aptamer through an increased enthalpic component, while theophylline and theobromine do not trigger such structural changes.

We have observed similar differences in binding modes in previous studies that showed that the regulatory activity of riboswitches is not solely determined by their binding affinities, but rather a complex interplay of binding kinetics and conformational changes. Examples include the binding mode between neomycin and its riboswitch, or ciprofloxacin aptamers, of which only one displayed riboswitch regulation [58, 59]. In recent years, we have been able to prove that aptamer selection by Capture-SELEX (the method applied in this study) is extremely effective in finding precisely such conformation-switching aptamers [12, 32]. Importantly, these and other aptamers that are commonly used for RNA device engineering need KDs in the lower nanomolar range to drive RNA devices [24]. With just two ketone groups available for hydrogen bonding, caffeine is a more difficult target for specific aptamer selection, as it has a constrained interaction profile with a potential RNA binding site. Nevertheless, its performance is close to other aptamers that have much lower apparent KDs.

The caffeine-binding aptamer folds into a stem-loop structure that is disrupted by a prominent internal flexible region. SHAPE-Map, in-line probing, and mutational studies confirmed the overall structure but failed at uncovering alternative folding within the internal loop structure. At this time the tertiary structure of the aptamer remains unresolved, but future work might uncover the exact binding mode between caffeine and the aptamer. It may also explain how caffeine is able to activate the riboswitch selectively, while theophylline and theobromine fail to do so despite somewhat similar binding affinities. Interestingly, the docking sequence important for the immobilization of RNAs during SELEX was not necessary for the aptamer to function as a roadblock-style riboswitch or to drive other RNA devices. This is something we also observed with the previously published tobramycin riboswitch [12].

Previously, another caffeine-responsive riboswitch for E. coli was identified by using a library of a partially randomized theophylline aptamer [42]. However, the developed riboswitch responded to theophylline with almost identical regulation efficiency. A different study made use of the natural guanine riboswitch as a scaffold for in vitro selection to develop a caffeine-responsive riboswitch in B. subtilis [41]. The switch showed good dynamic behavior but with a low overall expression level. The specificity toward closely related compounds was not tested. This “Café” aptamer and the “Tonic” aptamer published in the same study were also selected using a Capture-SELEX approach, though using a substantially different pool design. The B. subtilis riboswitches engineered from these aptamers also did not require the docking sequence for switching.

The riboswitch we present here is not only remarkable for its specificity, but also for its modularity, as the aptamer was applied in different RNA devices (Supplementary Fig. S1). It communicates structural changes to attached RNA sequences, making it highly versatile. The straightforward approach of replacing the apical stem loop above the central, DFHBI-1T binding G quadruplex structure of the fluorogenic iSpinach aptamer with the caffeine-binding aptamer yielded a functional biosensor. Without the necessity of further optimization, the sensor responded to caffeine concentrations above ~2 mg/l (corresponding to 10 µM), which is way below the typical concentrations in caffeinated energy drinks (300–320 mg/l) and around the observed blood serum concentrations after ingestion [39, 60].

The modularity of the aptamer was also shown in hammerhead aptazyme designs that were capable not only in yeast, but also in the highly complex environment of a mammalian cell. RNA-based devices are increasingly gaining in importance in the medical field, such as control elements for various gene therapeutic applications [61]. Hence, there is a need for a wider range of applicable ligands in mammalian cells. Here, caffeine presents itself as an intensively researched, safe, and well-accepted compound that can be administered systemically to selectively control heterologous gene expression in vivo. To improve functional aptazymes for the regulation of gene expression in mammalian cells, we employed an optofluidic-based screening approach, which enabled the rapid selection of desirable clones from a large library with high confidence in the observed phenotype due to the simultaneous and continuous monitoring and sorting of cells based on their fluorescence.

In sum, we have developed a capable caffeine-respondent aptamer that can communicate with a variety of RNA devices in diverse and complex environments. As such, it is comparable in modularity to the theophylline and tetracycline aptamers used for this type of engineering. Beyond this individual aptamer we demonstrate that the de novo development of such regulating RNA modules is possible, even for highly challenging targets.
