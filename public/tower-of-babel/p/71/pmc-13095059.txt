Arterial Versus Mixed Venous Lactate Levels in 1526 Cardiac Intensive Care Patients
Authors: Christophe Vandenbriele, Eftychia Galiatsou, Alex Rosenberg
Published: 2026

Time is critical in the diagnosis and treatment of cardiogenic shock, even more so than in septic shock.1 Early identification enables prompt circulatory support and restoration of tissue oxygenation. Baseline arterial lactate is an early and prognostically relevant biomarker of impaired cellular metabolism and anaerobic glycolysis,2 with levels ≥2 mmol/L considered clinically significant.3 However, access to arterial lactate measurements is not always straightforward (eg, during cardiopulmonary resuscitation/extracorporeal membrane oxygenation-cannulation, loss of pulsatility, severe vasoconstriction/vasculopathy), and valuable time is often lost in differentiating arterial versus venous samples. This raises the question of the utility of central venous lactate measurements in these critically ill patients for diagnosing cardiac circulatory failure and assessing its severity. Or: what is the correlation between mixed venous and arterial lactate levels in patients with impaired cardiac function?

We performed simultaneous (radial) arterial and (mixed) venous blood gas lactate measurements in 1526 patients at a quaternary cardiac intensive care unit (ICU; both surgical [N=1356, 86%] and nonsurgical [N=170, 14%] cardiac patients), all monitored with a pulmonary artery catheter; From the initial cohort of 1579 patients, 53 patients receiving veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support were excluded. Local ethical approval was obtained, with a waiver of consent due to the retrospective, observational nature of the study. Data available on request from the authors. Analysis included 4801 paired samples from the same blood gas analyzer, collected within a median of 8 minutes (interquartile range [IQR], 3–18). Values were compared using simple linear regression, Pearson correlation coefficient (r), and Bland-Altman tests. Data are expressed as median (25th–75th percentile) for non-normally distributed variables.

Patients were predominantly male (67%) with a median age of 62 years (IQR, 49–73), and 63% had no previous cardiac history at admission. Overall ICU-mortality was 31.4%, and the median vaso-inotropic score was 16 (IQR, 8–26), indicating a very sick cardiac patient population under severe support. Arterial and venous lactate values highly correlate in the overall patient population (1526 patients, 4801 samples; P<0.0001, r=0.99; Figure [A]), and differences between radial arterial and mixed venous lactate values were 0 (IQR, −0.1 to 0.1) mmol/L (Figure [B]). In total, 1250 samples (from 739 patients) showed an arterial lactate level of 2 to 5 mmol/L.3 Here, the median mixed venous lactate level was 2.80 (IQR, 2.3–3.6) versus 2.80 (IQR, 2.4–3.7) for radial arterial lactate and, a strong correlation was found between arterial and venous lactate levels (P<0.0001, r=0.94; 0 [IQR, −0.2 to 0.1]); 591 samples (376 patients) showed an arterial lactate level >5 mmol/L,3 with a median mixed venous lactate level of 7.4 (IQR, 6.1–9.6) compared with 7.6 (IQR, 6.1–9.7) for radial arterial lactate. Again, a strong correlation was preserved between arterial and venous lactate levels (P<0.0001, r=0.97; median difference 0 [IQR, −0.3 to 0.1] mmol/L). Given the presence of repeated measurements and an unbalanced data set (4801 samples in 1526 patients), a mixed-effects model was performed. Although lactate concentrations changed significantly over time (P<0.001), there was no significant effect of sampling site (arterial versus venous; P=0.653) and no significant interaction between sampling site and time point after admission (P=0.219), indicating stable agreement between radial arterial and mixed venous lactate measurements during the patient’s ICU stay in this large cardiac cohort.

A, Linear regression analysis demonstrating a very strong association between arterial and mixed venous lactate concentrations in 4801 paired samples (Pearson r=0.995, P<0.0001). B, Bland-Altman bias plot for paired mixed venous and arterial lactate measurements within the same population group, indicating a low median difference, with very narrow limits of agreement and no significant outliers (median 0 with an interquartile range of −0.1 to 0.1 mmol/L). Overall, these results highlight mixed venous lactate as a highly valuable replacement method for arterial lactate levels in critically ill patients with cardiogenic shock.

To date, persistent correlation between arterial and venous lactate has only been shown in septic shock.4 Although lactate clearance in septic and cardiogenic shock is comparable, lactate production in vasodilatory shock is mainly attributable to microcirculatory/mitochondrial dysfunction, whereas in cardiogenic shock, it is largely due to macrolevel hypoperfusion and low cardiac output. Both shock-types also produce lactate due to stress-induced aerobic glycolysis and often overlap (mixed shock-state). Therefore, results from septic shock cannot automatically be extrapolated to cardiogenic shock and vice versa.5

In conclusion, this is the first study to demonstrate a strong and consistent correlation between mixed venous and arterial lactate in a large population of cardiac ICU patients with pulmonary artery-catheter monitoring (1526 patients; 4801 paired samples), with robust agreement across all lactate ranges. Our findings demonstrate that radial arterial and mixed venous lactate levels are effectively interchangeable across a wide range of concentrations in critically ill cardiac patients (although it should be noted that these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to peripheral venous lactate). The demonstrated equivalence obviates the need to distinguish between the 2, supporting broader clinical flexibility and faster decision-making based on available access.

This has pivotal implications during cardiopulmonary resuscitation and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation-cannulation, where—amid urgent, often chaotic conditions—it is frequently unclear whether a central sample obtained under ongoing resuscitation is arterial or venous. Also, avoiding duplicate arterial and central venous sampling when lactate is the sole indication for an arterial specimen may help reduce iatrogenic blood loss and the associated transfusion requirement during ICU stay. Moreover, a bit more out of the box, this interchangeability provides a compelling rationale for embedding continuous lactate sensors—next to central venous sensors—within pulmonary artery catheters, enabling real-time metabolic monitoring at the bedside and advancing precision care during cardiac arrest and circulatory failure.
