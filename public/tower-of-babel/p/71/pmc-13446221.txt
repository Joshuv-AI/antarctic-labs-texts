First evidence of the circulation of induced pluripotent stem cell-derived platelets in humans
Authors: Kazumasa Takao, Yoshihiro Kumagae, Junya Kanda, Hidenori Hirose, Hiromichi Konno, Tomoyuki Inoue, Yuya Sato, Yasuko Hazama, Masaichi Hasegawa, Asano Asami, Mitsuhiro Nishimura, Akira Sawaguchi, Akifumi Takaori-Kondo, Kenichi Akamatsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlatelet products are essential for preventing and treating bleeding in patients with thrombocytopenia. However, their short shelf life and reliance on voluntary blood donations pose significant challenges to maintaining a stable supply. To overcome these limitations, induced pluripotent stem cell-derived platelets (iPSC-PLTs) have emerged as a promising alternative. The clinical application of iPSC-PLTs succeeded in demonstrating safety in an autologous transfusion setting; however, allogeneic applications remain unexplored. Here, we report the world’s first clinical evaluation of an allogeneic iPSC-PLT product. An immortalized megakaryocyte cell line (imMKCL) was established from an iPSC line by introducing 3 inducible genes—c-MYC, BMI1, and BCL-XL—and subsequently generating master and working cell banks. Using the working cell bank and turbulent flow bioreactors, an allogeneic iPSC-PLT product, MEG-002, was successfully produced with clinically relevant quality and yield. MEG-002 underwent comprehensive structural and functional characterization, including in vivo efficacy testing in rabbit models, which confirmed its functionality. Preclinical safety studies revealed no concerns. A clinical trial was conducted in accordance with ethical and regulatory standards in Japan. MEG-002 was infused into a patient with aplastic anemia at a dose of 6 × 1010 platelets. No adverse events were reported, and no clinically significant changes were observed in any assessments. Furthermore, a transient increase in platelet count and evidence of iPSC-PLT circulation were observed. Despite being descriptive observations from a single subject, these findings suggest the safety and potential efficacy of allogeneic iPSC-PLTs in humans. The clinical trial is registered with the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT2053210068).Keywords: induced pluripotent stem cells, megakaryocytes, blood platelets, regenerative medicine, transfusion medicine, platelet transfusion

This study reports the first-in-human clinical transfusion of an allogeneic induced pluripotent stem cell-derived platelet product, MEG-002. The absence of adverse events and clinically significant changes in laboratory parameters following MEG-002 administration indicates a favorable safety profile, at least at the tested dose. Furthermore, the detection of circulating induced pluripotent stem cell-derived platelets after MEG-002 infusion—reported here for the first time globally—provides evidence supporting the potential in vivo efficacy of an induced pluripotent stem cell-derived platelet product in humans. These findings mark a major milestone in regenerative medicine and transfusion therapy.

Introduction

Platelet transfusions are essential for both treating and preventing hemorrhage in patients with thrombocytopenia.1 To preserve their functional integrity, platelet products are stored at room temperature with gentle agitation. However, prolonged storage can lead to platelet activation and the proliferation of bacterial contaminants, limiting their shelf life to only 5-7 days.2 This short shelf life, combined with reliance on voluntary blood donations, poses significant challenges to maintaining a stable supply—particularly in aging societies where the number of eligible young donors continues to decline.3,4

Platelet transfusion refractoriness (PTR) represents another challenge and refers to the repeated failure to achieve an adequate increase in platelet count following transfusion. PTR often develops due to alloantibody formation, most commonly against human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules. Although immune-compatible platelets are recommended for patients with alloimmune PTR, timely identification of suitable donors is often impractical.5

Given these limitations of donor-derived platelet products, ex vivo—produced human platelets offer promising advantages in both safety and supply stability.6,7 Over the past decade, substantial progress has been made in generating induced pluripotent stem cell-derived platelets (iPSC-PLTs) at clinically relevant scales. This progress culminated in the first-in-human study of iPSC-PLTs, termed iPLAT1 (Japan Registry of Clinical Trials identifier: jRCTa050190117), conducted between 2019 and 2021.8 The iPLAT1 study involved autologous transfusions of iPSC-PLTs in a patient with aplastic anemia complicated by alloimmune PTR, for whom securing compatible blood donors was not feasible. The transfusions were completed without adverse events, demonstrating the safety of autologous iPSC-PLT administration.9

While significant progress has been achieved, autologous iPSC‑PLT production is inherently constrained by the substantial time and cost required, limiting its scalability and broad clinical implementation. In contrast, allogeneic iPSC‑PLTs can be manufactured as standardized, readily available products. Such off‑the‑shelf platelet products may provide an alternative approach to platelet transfusion practice.

Nevertheless, the clinical application of allogeneic iPSC-PLTs remains unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, no clinical trial evaluating allogeneic ex vivo—produced platelets has previously been reported. In this study, we describe the development of MEG-002, a novel iPSC-PLT product intended for allogeneic use, and report the results of the world’s first clinical trial assessing the safety and feasibility of allogeneic iPSC‑PLT transfusion.

Methods

Establishment of imMKCL and production of MEG-002

An induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) line, YZWJs513, homozygous for the most frequent HLA haplotype in the Japanese population, was established and provided by CiRA Foundation.10 An immortalized megakaryocyte cell line (imMKCL) was generated from YZWJs513 by introducing 3 doxycycline (Dox)-inducible transgenes—c-MYC, BMI1, and BCL-XL—according to previously described methods.11 Subsequently, master cell banks (MCBs) and working cell banks (WCBs) of imMKCL were constructed and cryopreserved.

Each batch of MEG-002 was manufactured at the facility for iPS cell therapy (FiT) of the CiRA Foundation using the WCB as the starting material, following previously published protocols.8,12 Briefly, a vial from the WCB was thawed and expanded under Dox-on conditions to induce transgene expression. Upon completion of the expansion phase, the cells were transferred to turbulent-flow bioreactors for Dox-off differentiation culture, where the expanded imMKCLs matured to produce platelets. The platelets in the culture suspension then underwent purification and concentration, followed by packaging into blood transfusion bags. The final MEG-002 product was released after exposure to 25-50 Gy of X-ray irradiation to eliminate the tumorigenic potential of residual nucleated cells.

Platelet count and Annexin V binding of MEG-002

To determine the platelet count in MEG-002, samples were stained with allophycocyanin (APC)–conjugated anti-human CD41 antibody (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) and analyzed using Trucount tubes (BD Biosciences) on a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). The Annexin V binding ratio was assessed on the same flow cytometer by staining with APC—conjugated anti-human CD41 antibody and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)—conjugated Annexin V (BD Biosciences). Ionomycin (final concentration, 20 μmol/L) was added to the positive control sample to define the maximum Annexin V binding.

Platelet size and immature platelet fraction

The size of iPSC-PLTs was analyzed using a laser diffraction and scattering method with a SALD-2300 particle size analyzer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The immature platelet fraction (IPF, %) was measured using an XN-1000 hematology analyzer (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan).

Electron microscopic observation of MEG-002

MEG-002 samples were fixed in 1% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer, followed by postfixation with 1% osmium tetroxide. After dehydration, the samples were infiltrated with and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared and stained with 2% uranyl acetate in 70% methanol and Reynolds’ lead citrate. The stained sections were examined using a transmission electron microscope (HT-7700; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 80 kV.

Platelet aggregation assay

An in vitro functional assay of MEG-002 was performed using light transmission aggregometry. An aliquot of iPSC-PLTs suspended in 70% human plasma (3 × 108 platelets/mL) containing 2 mM CaCl2 was mixed with a solution of collagen and adenosine diphosphate (ADP), resulting in final concentrations of 5 μg/mL and 5 μM, respectively. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 7 minutes. Changes in light transmission were recorded and analyzed using an aggregometer (PRP313M; TAIYO, Osaka, Japan).

PAC-1 binding and P-selectin expression

To assess ligand-induced activation of MEG-002, changes in PAC-1 binding and P-selectin expression following stimulation with ADP and thrombin receptor activator peptide-6 (TRAP-6) were evaluated. MEG-002 samples were stimulated with 100 μM ADP and 40 μM TRAP-6. The cells were then stained with APC-conjugated anti-human CD62P (BioLegend), phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-human CD41 (BioLegend), and FITC-conjugated PAC-1 (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using a FACSCanto II or FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Hemostatic efficacy and circulation of MEG-002 in rabbits

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of CMIC Pharma Science Co., Ltd. (approval numbers: IACUC-CBR-2008-008, -2008-016, -2008-025 and -2010-053) and were conducted in accordance with ethical standards.

The hemostatic efficacy of MEG-002 was evaluated in a rabbit model of thrombocytopenia following previously described methods.13 Thrombocytopenia was induced in New Zealand White rabbits (Japan SLC, Shizuoka, Japan) by intraperitoneal administration of busulfan. To inhibit the rapid clearance of infused xenogeneic platelets by blocking the reticuloendothelial system (RES), the animals received an intravenous injection of emulsified ethyl palmitate (EP) 1 day prior to platelet infusion and the hemostatic test. On the day of testing, rabbits with platelet counts below 2.5 × 104/μL were selected, and an incision was made with a lancet (Quikheel Lancet; Becton Dickinson) in the marginal vein of the ear to assess bleeding time. Animals with prolonged bleeding times greater than 600 seconds were selected for further experimentation. After hemostasis was achieved by applying pressure, the animals were intravenously infused with either vehicle or MEG-002 (1 × 1010 platelets per 2.5 kg of body weight). Two new incisions were then made in the ear marginal veins, and bleeding time was measured for up to 600 seconds at each site. Data were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test, implemented in GraphPad Prism version 7.05 (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, MA, USA).

The circulation of iPSC-PLTs following MEG-002 infusion was evaluated in splenectomized rabbits as previously described.13 The animals received an intravenous injection of emulsified EP 1 day prior to platelet infusion. MEG-002 was administered intravenously at a dose of 2 × 1010 platelets per 2.5 kg of body weight. Blood samples were collected at multiple time points up to 24 hours after infusion. The samples were fixed with ThromboFix (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA) and stained with FITC–conjugated anti-human CD41 antibody (Beckman Coulter) and PE–conjugated anti-human CD61 antibody (BioLegend). Flow cytometric analysis was performed using Trucount tubes and a FACSLyric flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Single-dose toxicity study in mice

A Good Laboratory Practice—compliant study was conducted to evaluate the toxicity of a single intravenous dose of MEG-002 in immunocompromised mice. NOG mice (NOD/Shi-scid, IL-2Rγnull; n = 10/sex/group) received a single intravenous injection of either vehicle or the maximum feasible dose (MFD) of MEG-002 (2 × 108 platelets per 200 μL per mouse). In-life assessments included clinical signs, body weight, food and water consumption, urinalysis, hematology, clinical chemistry, and respiratory function. Mice were euthanized at 1 and 2 weeks post-dosing, and additional assessments included gross pathology, organ weight measurement, and histopathological examination of a comprehensive panel of organs.

In vitro tumorigenicity studies of imMKCL and MEG-002

Prior to product release, MEG-002 undergoes X-ray irradiation at 25-50 Gy to eliminate tumorigenic potential. Because platelets lack nuclei, they do not possess inherent tumorigenic capacity. However, residual imMKCL cells remain in the final formulation, posing a theoretical risk. Therefore, a series of in vitro tumorigenicity studies were performed in a manner similar to previously reported methods.8

To confirm the loss of proliferative capacity following irradiation, a colony formation assay was performed using imMKCL cells. imMKCL cells exposed to 25 Gy of X-ray irradiation were seeded into 15-cm culture dishes (2 × 107 cells per dish) containing soft agar medium supplemented with Dox, either without or with the addition of nonirradiated imMKCL cells (2 × 102 cells per dish), corresponding to the test and spiked groups, respectively. For the positive control group, nonirradiated imMKCL cells (2 × 102 cells per dish) were seeded under identical conditions. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 2 weeks, and colony formation was assessed by counting visible colonies.

An in vitro colony formation assay was also conducted using the irradiated final product, MEG-002. To detect tumorigenic cells with different proliferative characteristics, both adherent and suspension culture conditions were employed. MEG-002 (2 mL, irradiated with 25 Gy of X-rays; approximately 2 × 109 platelets per dish) was seeded into 15-cm culture dishes and cultured for 2 weeks under each condition. For the suspension culture condition, HL60 cells (2.0 × 102 cells per dish) were used as a positive control. The cells were cultured in soft agar medium, and colony formation was evaluated by counting colonies after a 2-week incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2. For the adherent culture condition, HeLa cells (1.0 × 102 cells per dish) served as a positive control, and colony formation was assessed by counting colonies following Giemsa staining after a 2-week incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2. For each culture condition, positive control cells were added to irradiated MEG-002 as a spiked group, and colony formation was similarly evaluated.

Clinical trial

A nonrandomized, single-arm, open-label exploratory clinical trial was conducted to evaluate the tolerability, safety, and efficacy of MEG-002. The study was performed in accordance with the principles of Good Clinical Practice and the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. A clinical trial notification was submitted to the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency and subsequently accepted. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyoto University Hospital (reference number: 2184). At the commencement of the study, registration was completed in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials under the identifier jRCT2053210068 (https://jrct.mhlw.go.jp/en-latest-detail/jRCT2053210068). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. Thrombocytopenic patients aged 18 years or older, under stable medical conditions, and diagnosed with one of the following disorders were considered eligible: aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, leukemia (excluding acute promyelocytic leukemia and patients in the early cytopenic phase after induction or consolidation therapy), or solid cancer associated with prolonged thrombocytopenia after chemotherapy. Eligible patients were required to have platelet counts of ≤20 × 109/L, be expected to reach this level without transfusion, or be at risk of bleeding with platelet counts ≤30 × 109/L. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, hemophilia or other coagulation disorders, thrombotic risk, splenomegaly, fever >38°C, and active infection.

Safety assessments included monitoring of vital signs, electrocardiography, chest radiography, serum chemistry, coagulation and fibrinolysis parameters, hematology, urinalysis, and adverse events. An independent data safety monitoring board (DSMB) was established to review all safety data.

To determine platelet counts, whole blood samples were fixed with ThromboFix (Beckman Coulter) and stained with FITC–conjugated anti-human CD41 and CD61 antibodies (BD Biosciences), and APC-conjugated anti-human CD36 antibody (BioLegend). Flow cytometric analysis was performed using Trucount tubes and a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). The FITC-positive population was defined as total platelets (total PLTs), while CD36-negative events within the FITC-positive population were identified as iPSC-PLTs. The mean and median forward scatter area (FSC-A) values of CD36-positive “endogenous platelets” and iPSC-PLTs were also measured as indicators of platelet size. The count increment (CI) and corrected count increment (CCI) of total PLTs were calculated using the following formulas: CI (/μL) = (platelet count after MEG-002 dosing) − (platelet count before dosing). CCI (/μL) = CI × (body surface area in m2)/(number of platelets infused ×1011).

Results

Manufacturing of MEG-002

Cells of imMKCL were differentiated from YZWJs513 iPSCs, and cell banks of a competent clone were established. The MCB underwent comprehensive evaluations, including sterility testing and a wide-spectrum viral safety panel, in accordance with relevant regulatory guidelines. Short tandem repeat analysis was performed on both the MCB and WCB, confirming a genetic match with the original iPSC line (data not shown).

A good manufacturing practice—compliant manufacturing process for MEG-002 was successfully developed. The process yielded batches containing more than 2 × 1011 platelets, meeting the requirements for clinical application. It was also confirmed that process-related impurities, such as chemical components added to the culture media, were sufficiently reduced during the purification and washing steps of the manufacturing process (data not shown). In 3 consecutive batches of MEG-002 produced to verify process consistency, the final product exhibited a mean concentration of iPSC-PLTs of 0.99 × 109/mL and an average Annexin V—positive ratio of 33.4%, demonstrating the reproducibility of production (Table 1). The average platelet size ranged from 5.5 to 6.9 μm, and the IPF(%) was 55.7% for these batches (Table 1).

Hemostatic effect of MEG-002 in a rabbit model of thrombocytopenia

MEG-002 demonstrated hemostatic efficacy in a rabbit model of thrombocytopenia induced by busulfan (Figure 2A). No spontaneous hemostasis was observed in vehicle-treated rabbits. In contrast, most incisions in animals treated with MEG-002 achieved hemostasis within 600 seconds. The hemostatic effect of MEG-002 was statistically significant compared with the vehicle group (P < .0001, log-rank test). This efficacy was reproducible across multiple manufacturing batches.

Platelet circulation of iPSC-PLTs in RES–blocked rabbits

The circulation of iPSC-PLTs in rabbit blood following MEG-002 infusion was evaluated (Figure 2B). Immediately after completion of the MEG-002 infusion (0 minutes post-dosing), the mean iPSC-PLT count in peripheral blood reached 5.40 × 104/μL. This was followed by a transient decline, after which a gradual increase was observed, peaking at 8.08 × 104/μL at 4 hours post-dosing. The concentration remained relatively stable up to 7 hours, then declined by 24 hours post-dosing. No significant changes were observed in endogenous rabbit platelet counts over time (data not shown). The mean and median FSC‑A values of iPSC‑PLTs and rabbit platelets at each time point were also determined and are summarized in Table S1. In contrast to rabbit platelets, which exhibited stable FSC‑A values over time, iPSC‑PLTs demonstrated a time‑dependent reduction, suggesting a decrease in platelet size during circulation.

Preclinical safety studies

In the single-dose general toxicity study in immunocompromised mice, no mortality was observed in any group during the study period. Moreover, no treatment-related changes were detected in any of the evaluated clinical, hematologic, or biochemical parameters.

In the in vitro colony formation assay using imMKCL cells, colony formation was observed in both the positive control and spiked groups. In contrast, no colony formation was detected in the irradiated imMKCL group, confirming that exposure to 25 Gy of X-ray irradiation effectively eliminated the proliferative capacity of imMKCL (Table 2). Similarly, tumorigenicity assessment of MEG-002 using an in vitro colony formation assay revealed no colony formation under any culture condition when the product had been irradiated with 25 Gy of X-rays (Table 3).

Exploratory clinical study of MEG-002 in patients with thrombocytopenia

One patient diagnosed with aplastic anemia was enrolled and completed the study without any protocol deviations or violations. The patient had no prior history of PTR, and anti-HLA antibody testing yielded a negative result. HLA typing confirmed that the patient’s HLA type did not match that of the MEG-002 platelets. MEG-002 was administered via intravenous infusion at a dose of 0.6 × 1011 platelets, corresponding to approximately one-third of the standard platelet transfusion dose used in Japan. The dose was selected with due consideration of safety, as this study was the first‑in‑human study of MEG‑002. The patient was monitored and followed for 4 weeks after administration.

No adverse events were reported during the study period. Furthermore, no clinically significant changes were observed in any assessments, including vital signs, blood chemistry, or coagulation and fibrinolysis parameters. Although persistent abnormalities attributable to the underlying disease (ie, aplastic anemia) were observed in hematological parameters, no clinically significant changes were noted (Table S2). Follow‑up testing after infusion revealed no new anti‑HLA antibodies. No bleeding episodes occurred throughout the observation period.

As shown in Figure 3A, iPSC-PLTs were distinguished from endogenous platelets (ie, patient-derived platelets) and quantified separately by flow cytometry using CD36 as a discriminating marker. iPSC-PLTs were detected at 1 hour post-dosing and subsequently showed a slight increase. Their counts remained relatively stable between 2 and 6 hours post-dosing, ranging from 3100 to 3400/μL. By 24 hours post-dosing, the count had decreased to 1800/μL, although trace levels were still detectable at 48 hours (Figure 3A and B). The size of iPSC-PLTs, indicated by the mean and median FSC-A values, was greater than that of endogenous platelets at 1 hour following completion of MEG-002 transfusion; however, these values gradually approached those of endogenous platelets over time (Table 4). The total platelet (PLT) count exhibited a trend similar to that of iPSC-PLTs, increasing from 18 800/μL at baseline to 21 000/μL at 1 hour post-dosing, peaking at 22 800/μL at 2 hours, and slightly decreasing to 21 500/μL at 6 hours. By 24 hours, the count had returned to approximately the pre-dose level (Figure 3B). The CCI exceeded the threshold of 7500/μL—commonly used as a criterion for early post-transfusion efficacy assessment5—between 2 and 6 hours after completion of MEG-002 infusion (Table S3).

Discussion

A highly competent imMKCL was successfully established from HLA-homozygous iPSCs. The optimized manufacturing process enabled large-scale production of a platelet product, MEG-002, suitable for clinical application, and ensured consistent quality and functionality.

MEG-002 platelets exhibited a larger particle size compared with peripheral blood platelets (2-4 μm). In addition, the IPF(%) of MEG-002 was markedly higher than that of peripheral blood platelets, for which the IPF% in healthy adults has been reported to be approximately 2%-4%.14 The IPF is largely synonymous with reticulated platelets, which are newly produced in the bone marrow and characterized by a larger cell volume and higher RNA content. Therefore, the high IPF% observed in MEG-002 is consistent with its larger platelet size and suggests that the ex vivo—produced iPSC-PLTs share key biological characteristics with newly generated platelets in vivo.

In a rabbit model of chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia, MEG-002 demonstrated robust hemostatic activity. When extrapolated to a 60-kg human, the dose of MEG-002 that achieved hemostatic effects in rabbits (1 × 1010 platelets/10 mL/2.5 kg) corresponds to approximately 2.4 × 1011 platelets per person, closely matching the anticipated clinical dose (2 × 1011 platelets per person). Circulation studies in RES-blocked rabbits showed that iPSC-PLTs reached a plateau level comparable to that of donor-derived platelets, as reported previously.13 Collectively, these findings suggest that MEG-002 may demonstrate hemostatic efficacy comparable to that of donor-derived platelets.

The general toxicity study conducted in immunocompromised mice revealed no significant safety concerns at the MFD of MEG-002 (2 × 108 platelets per 200 μL per mouse). Assuming a mouse body weight of 20 g and a human body weight of 60 kg, this dosage corresponds to approximately 6 × 1011 platelets per person, which is 3 times the anticipated clinical dose and ten times the dose evaluated in the current clinical trial. Furthermore, although tumorigenicity remains a major concern in regenerative medicine—particularly for iPSC–derived products—in vitro colony formation assays confirmed that irradiation of the final product at 25 Gy effectively eliminates this potential risk. A similar irradiation dose using gamma or X-rays is routinely applied to donor-derived blood products to prevent post-transfusion graft-versus-host disease caused by contaminating lymphocytes. It is widely accepted that such irradiation exerts negligible effects on platelet functionality or clinical outcomes.15

In the clinical study, no adverse events were reported, and no clinically significant changes were observed in any of the parameters evaluated. These findings indicate a favorable safety profile of MEG-002, at least at the tested dose. Notably, while slight elevations in D-dimer levels—measured as one of the coagulation and fibrinolysis parameters—were reported across 2 dose cohorts in the iPLAT1 study,9 no significant change in D-dimer concentration was detected in the present study.

To quantify MEG-002-derived platelets in blood samples while distinguishing them from endogenous platelets, a flow cytometry-based method utilizing an anti-CD36 antibody was developed, based on the observation that MEG-002 platelets lack surface expression of CD36. This approach enabled specific and accurate quantification of iPSC-PLTs. It is worth noting that CD36 is a well-characterized scavenger receptor, and although its deficiency has been predominantly documented among Asian and African populations, it does not significantly affect physiological hemostasis or fundamental platelet functions.16,17 It has been reported that approximately 3%-11% of the Japanese population lacks CD36 expression on platelets.18,19 In this study, although CD36 expression status was not evaluated prior to enrollment, post-transfusion analysis confirmed that the patient was CD36-positive. Following MEG-002 administration, a transient increase in total platelet count was observed, which correlated with fluctuations in MEG-002 platelet levels. Platelet counts gradually increased, reaching a plateau between 2 and 6 hours post-infusion. At this plateau, the CCI exceeded 7500/μL, indicating a measurable transfusion effect. A delayed peak in platelet count was also observed in the current rabbit circulation study of MEG-002, consistent with findings from previous animal studies using other iPSC-PLT strains.8,12 Because iPSC-PLTs display a size distribution skewed toward larger diameters compared with normal peripheral blood platelets, it has been speculated that the larger fraction of iPSC-PLTs undergoes fragmentation in circulation, thereby contributing to the moderate elevation in platelet count.8,12 The changes in FSC-A observed in the current clinical trial as well as the rabbit study support the plausibility of this hypothesis. Since similar trends were observed across species, this phenomenon is unlikely to be driven by species-specific factors. To date, no correlation has been established between the reduction in size of iPSC-PLTs during circulation and their functional status. Although size reduction progressed for several hours post-infusion, no clear correlation was observed with temporal changes in platelet counts. This suggests that fragmentation is not directly associated with platelet activation or clearance.

Despite confirmation of the circulation of iPSC-PLTs in humans, it must be acknowledged that the overall transfusion effect did not fully meet expectations. When platelet concentrates are transfused, approximately one-third of the platelets are sequestered in the spleen.20,21 Thus, the expected CI can be estimated by dividing the total number of transfused platelets by the circulating blood volume and subsequently multiplying the value by two-thirds. Applying this estimation to the present case yielded an expected CI of approximately 7000/µL. In contrast, the maximum observed CI was 4000/µL, corresponding to approximately 60% of the expected increment. Moreover, whereas the effects of platelet transfusion are generally expected to persist for several days, platelet counts returned to baseline levels within 24 hours after infusion of MEG-002. These findings raise the possibility that the in vivo survival of MEG‑002 may be limited, and the possibility of early post‑transfusion clearance of iPSC‑PLTs cannot be excluded.

It is worth noting that an increase in platelet count after infusion of iPSC-PLTs was not observed in the iPLAT1 study. Although the underlying reason for this discrepancy remains unclear, differences in blood sampling time points and/or measurement methodologies may have contributed. In the iPLAT1 study, blood samples were not collected between 1 and 24 hours post-dosing, potentially missing the optimal detection window. Furthermore, the quantification by a conventional blood cell counter used in the iPLAT1 study may have lacked the sensitivity to detect relatively small changes against the high pre-dose baseline, as previously discussed.9 The differences between the 2 studies are summarized in Table S4.

Conclusion

The current findings are compelling, as they provide the first direct evidence of circulation of iPSC-PLTs in humans and offer preliminary insight into their potential utility in transfusion therapy. Nevertheless, because this study involved only a single participant and a single infusion at a suboptimal dose, the clinical data are descriptive in nature and do not permit statistical generalization. Further investigations are warranted to evaluate safety and therapeutic efficacy in a larger cohort, involving higher and multiple-dose administrations.

Another critical consideration is the development of “universal” platelet products lacking HLA class I expression. Immune rejection represents a major barrier to allogeneic transplantation; accordingly, genome‑editing strategies aimed at generating HLA‑deficient cells have attracted considerable interest in regenerative medicine.22 Efforts to generate HLA class I-deficient iPSC-PLTs are currently underway, and although still at an early stage, a preliminary proof‑of‑concept study demonstrating the feasibility of universal platelet production has been reported.23 Such products could substantially reduce the risk of alloimmunization and broaden applicability across diverse patient populations, representing a distinct advantage over conventional platelet transfusions. Given the difficulty of procuring HLA-matched platelets for patients with PTR, expectations for the clinical implementation of universal products are high.

Finally, the quantification method for iPSC-PLTs should be further refined. In the present clinical trial, the patient was CD36-positive, allowing CD36 to be used as a discriminatory marker. However, reliance on a single marker has inherent limitations. Specifically, CD36 is deficient in a subset of individuals, as described above. Platelet CD36 levels have been shown to remain stable within individuals over extended periods.24 Accordingly, it is unlikely that circulating levels of iPSC-PLTs were overestimated in the present study. However, the possibility that CD36 expression on iPSC-PLTs may change during in vivo circulation cannot be completely ruled out. Although alternative markers were not evaluated in this study, HLA may serve as a more robust indicator in the context of the future development of universal platelets.

Ultimately, optimization of dosing strategies, expanding to larger clinical trials, and advancing toward universal, off-the-shelf products will be essential for translating this platform into routine clinical practice.
