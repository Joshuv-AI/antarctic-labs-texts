Family and teachers lead the way: How different information sources about COVID-19 influenced children's well-being during the pandemic
Authors: Wolfgang Messner, Julia Sojka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe study evaluates how children's information needs about COVID-19 during the pandemic were met through various channels, including family, peers, teachers, social media, and news outlets. By examining differences across countries, the research aims to be informative for public health practitioners involved in managing and delivering public health services during an emergency.Study designUtilizing data from the International Survey of Children's Well-Being (ISCWeB), the study analyzes responses from 25,184 children in urban schools across 22 countries. It explores the impact of different information sources on children's well-being.MethodsSeparately for each country and at the individual level of analysis, the study regresses variables related to COVID-19 information sources, support structures, information processing, demographics, online access, and first-hand pandemic experiences on children's subjective well-being. A structural interaction matrix assesses the combined effects of the information sources, while elastic net regression is employed to enhance interpretability and contain multicollinearity issues.ResultsThe impact of various information sources on children's well-being varied significantly by country. Family and teachers were identified as the most positively influential sources across countries, while the effects of peers, social media, and news were more mixed.ConclusionThe findings offer actionable insights for public health practitioners, underscoring the importance of choosing appropriate channels for delivering information to children based on their cultural contexts. Family and teachers are identified as key influencers across different countries.

Introduction

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the proportion of confirmed cases among children was relatively small and deaths were uncommon [1]. Yet, lockdowns, school closures, and other public health measures to reduce the disease's spread fundamentally impacted the life and subjective well-being of the pediatric population, likely depending on social determinants and available support structures, again likely with considerable variation between countries. Because children are marginalized in adult society and differ inherently from adults [[2], [3]][ public health program managers must be educated on how to effectively communicate information to children during a public health emergency. The value that children sense from such support is not only different from adults but will likely also change with their age [4].

Several prior studies analyzed the impact of COVID-19 on the well-being of children during the pandemic and highlighted worrisome trends in children's behavioral health, such that a prevalence of depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and sleep disturbances among children and adolescents under 19 years was observed in a meta-review of 191 studies across multiple countries [5]. The prevalence estimates for all assessed outcomes differed significantly across geographical regions, often originating from variations in mental health coverage. Notably, another meta-analysis of 53 longitudinal studies from 12 countries found good evidence that the increase in depressive symptoms was most severe among female children and those from relatively higher-income backgrounds [6]. A study from Norway revealed a significant decrease in physical and psychological well-being, particularly affecting females and older children [7]. Children in the UK perceived their lives as monotonous and felt a sense of missing out on opportunities [8]. A study from the US illustrated the dual impact of COVID-19 on both child and family well-being, suggesting that the immediate adverse effect on children is addressable through social interventions [9]. Generally, studies concluded that the unique biological and social contexts surrounding the vulnerable pediatric population demand support programs that are specifically tailored to their needs [5].

However, our review of the literature revealed that most prior studies on children's experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic have been confined to single-country contexts. Yet, directly understanding cross-cultural differences is important, as they can inform more effective strategies for supporting children in future crises. Our exploratory study addresses this gap by examining how information provision influenced children's well-being across 22 diverse national contexts.

Methods

Research focus

In our present exploratory study, we aim to offer a unique perspective by examining how information provision influenced children's well-being during the COVID-19 pandemic with a focus on cross-cultural differences, drawing on available but non-representative samples from 22 countries across Western and Eastern Europe, Africa and the Middle East, Asia, and South America. Unlike the meta-analyses mentioned earlier, our research was based on a consistent set of questions and a common methodological framework across all participants.

This study is exploratory rather than theory-building for several reasons. First and foremost, the cultural and institutional variables commonly used in cross-national research are designed to capture adult environments and may not be directly applicable to children [3]. That is, these variables cannot constitute a theoretical framework that can systematically explain children's well-being in a crisis. Second, the COVID-19 pandemic was an unexpected and unpredictable event, which did not allow for proper timing of a survey. Moreover, after the WHO declared the outbreak as a pandemic on March 11, 2020, the course of the pandemic and countermeasures implemented by authorities showed high variation across countries and even within countries [10,11]. This variability makes it challenging to build a consistent theoretical multilevel model across countries. Third, with data collected in 22 countries, the present study does not have the necessary level-2 sample size to support a robust multilevel research model. A larger number of countries would be required to systematically link national-level cultural and institutional factors to individual-level well-being outcomes for testing multilevel hypotheses [12].

As such, we aim to identify patterns that can inform public health practitioners, rather than propose a formalized theoretical model.

Survey data

We used survey data from the International Survey of Children's Well-Being (ISCWeB) [13], which is the largest and most comprehensive global study on childhood from children's perspectives [14]. The ISCWeB specifically focuses on middle childhood (ages 7 to 15) with age-appropriate questions.

Children are the primary focus of analysis, acknowledging them as social actors rather than just members of households. In line with the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, the surveys by the ISCWeB prioritize children's direct input, offering them a platform to express themselves. Moreover, the surveys emphasize positive evaluative indicators of well-being, rather than focusing on problematic behaviors [15]. By focusing on children aged 7 to 15, we capture a distinct developmental stage: unlike younger children who rely almost entirely on parents, and older adolescents who navigate information more independently, this group is beginning to seek and interpret information while still depending on their immediate social environments. Studying their experiences during a global crisis thus provides unique insights for policymakers and public health officials in developing effective information strategies.

The fourth wave (2020 and 2022) included responses from 25,856 children aged 7 to 15 across 22 countries, focusing on the urban school context during the COVID-19 pandemic (see Table 1). By urban school context, we refer to children enrolled in schools located in cities or larger towns, rather than rural or remote areas, thereby reflecting experiences shaped by urban educational and social environments. With the purpose of creating awareness about children's well-being, the ISCWeB collected data by means of group-administered questionnaires on children's daily lives, time use, and, most importantly, their own perceptions and evaluations of their well-being [13]. The survey was adapted to different countries using back-translation, cognitive testing, and expert-translation methods. Data collection commenced in March 2021, continued through to August 2021, and was supervised by an international committee to ensure appropriate geographical representativity. During this period, countries faced various levels of lockdowns and social restrictions, which made it necessary for the ISCWeB team to administer the survey as a mix of paper-and-pencil (34.810 % of Mapping of ISCWeB questions to variables.

This table outlines the correspondence between ISCWeB questions and the variables in the research model. The numbered references align with the ISCWeB dataset. The well-being variable serves as the dependent variable in the model; all other variables are independent variables.

The dependent variable, children's overall well-being, was derived as a composite score following Huebner's methodology [18] for assessing student life satisfaction. It is based on six items (“I enjoy my life,” “My life is going well,” “I have a good life,” “The things that are happening in my life are excellent,” “I am happy with my life,” and “I like being the way I am”) and provides a general evaluation of a child's life quality, beyond the assessment of specific domains like family, friends, or school.

Independent variables included various sources of information about COVID-19, such as family, other children, teachers, social media, and news.

Additionally, to ensure robust results, we analyzed the support structure and information processing of children. As outlined at the bottom of Table 2, we included several control variables: demographic factors (such as age, gender, and living conditions), the extent of their online access (across different media), and their first-hand experiences with COVID-19 (whether they had experienced an infection and whether their schools were closed). Including a variable on COVID-19 infection helped account for differences in the timing and severity of the pandemic across countries, ensuring a more accurate comparison of the pandemic's impact [19].

Because of missing data in the dependent variable, we deleted 2.599 % of the records, resulting in a final dataset of 25,184. We then used multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) separately for each country to handle the 1.718 % missing values in the independent variables. MICE fills in missing data by iteratively modeling each incomplete variable as a function of the others using regression models.

Elastic net regression

At the individual level and separately for each of the 22 countries, we regressed children's well-being on the independent variables. However, traditional OLS regression was not well suited here: single predictors showed only small associations with well-being, statistical significance was largely a product of the large sample sizes (Table 1), and multicollinearity further complicated interpretation [[20], [21], [22], [23]].

To overcome these issues, we applied elastic net regression, a regularized machine learning method that reduces multicollinearity, eliminates redundant variables, and identifies the most informative predictors [24]. Coefficients are regularized (and therefore small), but can still be interpreted as effect sizes and compared between countries. To avoid information loss and capture dependencies between predictors, we computed a structural interaction matrix by multiplying relevant variables, with elastic net regularization controlling the resulting multicollinearity [25,26].

Data preparation and analysis were performed in Python: MICE imputation with NumPy, elastic net regression via SciKit-Learn, and hyperparameter tuning with GridSearchCV.

A lot of information about the Coronavirus coming from family members or caregivers significantly increased children's well-being across most countries. This channel was clearly a very important source of information in the countries of Western Europe, Africa and the Middle East, Asia, and South America. However, variations existed, such that information from family was less important in Germany (with a low positive coefficient of 0.004) and not important at all in all but one of the Eastern European countries (Estonia 0.062).

Information provided by other children showed mixed effects. There was a strong positive effect in Asia (Bangladesh 0.035; South Korea 0.081; Sri Lanka 0.056), and Chile in South America (0.048). Though, information coming from peers had a negative effect in Germany (−0.023), Albania (−0.027), and Algeria (−0.011).

Receiving information from teachers notably increased the well-being of children in the Asian countries. However, it had rather minimal positive or no effects in Türkiye, across Western Europe (exception Finland 0.050), Eastern Europe (exception Albania 0.034), and South America. There was even a small negative effect in Israel (−0.010).

Social media as a source of COVID-19 information tended to negatively affect well-being in all Western European and many other countries. Again, exceptions existed. For example, there were slight positive effects in Albania (0.010), Indonesia (0.017), South Korea (0.026), and Sri Lanka (0.001).

Receiving information from the news generally increased well-being, particularly in Finland (0.058), Germany (0.096), Spain (0.062), UK (0.031), Estonia (0.050), Algeria (0.068), Bangladesh (0.029), Sri Lanka (0.109), and Colombia (0.054). Notably, in Russia (0.027), the news was the only information source with a significant coefficient in this country. However, it had adverse effects in Israel (−0.018), South Korea (−0.048), and Chile (−0.057).

The interaction variables between family as an information source and other channels generally showed positive coefficients, further underscoring the importance of family for children during the pandemic. However, exceptions with negative coefficients existed, notably in the UK, Israel, South Korea, and Chile. The interactions between information received from peers and other sources tended to be either insignificant or negative. Similarly, the interactions with teachers and other sources also showed insignificant or negative coefficients.

Support from family, friends, and teachers was important in all countries, though its overall significance varied, being less crucial in some Asian, African, and Middle Eastern countries. Generally, having sufficient information about COVID-19, discussing it at home, and valuing children's opinions contributed to their well-being. However, there were exceptions. In Germany, having enough information (−0.005) and discussing it at home (−0.017) negatively impacted children's well-being. In Algeria, taking children's opinions seriously was linked to reduced well-being.

Younger children were generally less affected by the pandemic across countries, with exceptions in Algeria (0.015) and Sri Lanka (0.024); the effect was insignificant in Belgium, Russia, Hong Kong, South Korea, Taiwan, and Colombia. The gender control coefficient was mostly positive or insignificant for female children in Western and Eastern Europe, Israel, and South America, but negative in most African and Asian countries.

In Western Europe, living in a house with a garden improved well-being, while living conditions were less relevant in Eastern Europe and many other regions. Access to online information typically boosted well-being, whereas time spent on social media tended to reduce it – except in Russia (0.007), Algeria (0.004), Indonesia (0.048), and South Korea (0.082).

First-hand experience with the pandemic, through infection or witnessing infections, generally decreased well-being, except in Germany (0.036) and Italy (0.030), where it increased well-being. School closures either had a positive or negligible effect on well-being, except in Germany, where being unable to attend school slightly reduced well-being (−0.004).

Discussion

Although children represented a smaller percentage of COVID-19 infection and fatality cases, they were deeply affected by the pandemic. This exploratory study provides new insights into how children across 22 countries experienced different sources of COVID-19 information and how these shaped their subjective well-being. Overall, three findings stand out. First, family members and teachers emerged as the most consistently positive influences, underscoring their importance as trusted and proximate information conduits during crises. Second, the effects of peers, social media, and traditional news outlets were highly variable across countries, reflecting cultural differences in trust, communication norms, and media regulation. Third, contextual factors – including the quality of support at home, children's opportunities to discuss the pandemic, and their access to digital technologies – shaped how information was processed and integrated into children's well-being. These findings highlight that the channels through which children receive information matter greatly, and that these channels must be tailored to cultural and institutional contexts.

Family and teachers as primary conduits

Across most countries, family members were pivotal to sustaining children's well-being, consistent with developmental theories that highlight the role of trusted caregivers in supporting emotional stability and coping during crises, often by filtering media content into age-appropriate messages. Yet our findings also revealed cross-national variation: in Germany and some Eastern European countries, family information had little impact on well-being, even though German children reported receiving more COVID-19 information from family than in any of the other countries.

Meanwhile, teachers played a critical role in several Asian and African contexts, underscoring their traditionally high social status and moral authority. By contrast, their influence on well-being was slightly negative in Spain and Israel. In Germany, Estonia, and Russia, children reported receiving very little information from teachers.

These variations suggest that differences in family communication styles, school engagement, and cultural orientations toward authority [27] may condition the impact of these sources.

Peers, social media, and news media

Information obtained from peers showed mixed effects: beneficial in several Asian countries but negative in Germany and Albania. This may reflect broader cultural orientations, such as individualism versus collectivism [27], and cultural differences in whether peer networks serve primarily as arenas for social comparison or supportive communities [28].

Social media generally correlated with lower well-being in Western Europe, consistent with evidence that unmoderated online content during the pandemic often amplified misinformation, uncertainty, and anxiety [29]. Yet in contexts such as Indonesia and South Korea, social media use was linked to positive outcomes.

Traditional news media likewise showed varied effects: beneficial in many countries, especially those with strong public service broadcasting traditions, but negative in Chile and South Korea. In Chile, declining trust and political polarization of news outlets [30] meant that children's exposure may have lead to confusion or anxiety rather than support. South Korea presents a paradox: despite extensive government-led efforts to counter misinformation through fact-checking platforms and media collaborations [31], news use was still negatively associated with children's well-being. This highlights that even robust institutional responses do not always translate into positive outcomes for younger audiences.

Integrating the channels

Taken together, these results show that no single information source operates in isolation. Families often mediate by filtering news and social media before children engage with it, teachers provide structured opportunities for dialogue, and peers shape emotional interpretations through shared experiences. Yet, in Germany, Russia, Israel, and Estonia, children reported receiving relatively little information across all channels.

The balance between sources varied substantially across cultural contexts, underscoring why interactions were emphasized in our research model. For practitioners, this suggests that interventions should strengthen the relational ecosystems through which children process crisis information, rather than relying on a single channel.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its unique cross-national scope: using harmonized data from more than 25,000 children in 22 countries allowed for systematic comparisons beyond the single-country focus of much prior research. By applying elastic net regression with an interaction matrix, we were able to capture the combined effects of multiple information sources.

The study also has limitations. First, the dataset only covers urban school contexts and may not reflect rural or marginalized children's experiences. Second, while we measured reported exposure to information sources, we could not capture the content or how children interpreted it, for example, whether news was consumed directly or through adults. Nor do we know which specific social media platforms were most influential. Third, the social media item specified Instagram, WhatsApp, and Facebook to make the question more easily understandable for children. We acknowledge that these platforms are used differently across contexts (e.g., WhatsApp for family communication, Instagram for following broader networks). Finally, as the data are cross-sectional, correlations with well-being cannot be taken as causal.

Implications

Our study has clear implications. Public health practitioners should prioritize parents and teachers as key messengers, equipping them with accessible and developmentally appropriate resources. Media literacy programs for children can help navigate content. Policymakers must account for cultural variation: communication strategies effective in one context (e.g., leveraging teacher authority in Asia, or public service media in Europe) may be less effective elsewhere.

Middle childhood is a crucial period for developing socio-emotional and self-help skills. While many children are resilient and capable of recovering, the way societies support them in future crises will be a testament to our commitment – specifically, adult's commitment – to safeguarding children's well-being.
