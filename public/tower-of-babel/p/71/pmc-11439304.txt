A protracted war against cancer drug resistance
Authors: Yuan Tian, Xiaowei Wang, Cong Wu, Jiaming Qiao, Hai Jin, Huafei Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Currently, even the most effective anti-cancer therapies are often limited by the development of drug resistance and tumor relapse, which is a major challenge facing current cancer research. A deep understanding of the molecular and biochemical bases of drug efficacy that can help predict the clinical drug resistance, coupled with the evolution of systematic genomic and proteomic technologies, have facilitated studies identifying and elucidating the underlying mechanisms. In this review, we focus on several important issues on cancer drug resistance and provide a framework for understanding the common ways by which cancers develop resistance to therapeutic agents. With the increasing arsenal of novel anticancer agents and techniques, there are now unprecedented opportunities to understand and overcome drug resistance. The proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC) technology, immunotherapy, nanomedicine, and real-time monitoring of drug response all provide effective approaches for combating drug resistance. In addition to the advancement of therapeutic technologies, the revolution of treatment concept is also of great importance. We can take advantage of the interplay between drug sensitive and resistant subclones for combating cancer. However, there remains a long way to go in the protracted war against cancer drug resistance.

Background

Despite remarkable progress has been made for prolonging the survival of cancer patients through traditional and novel therapeutics, the development of drug resistance still represents a major barrier to long-term survival [1]. Cancers adopt plenty of different strategies to escape therapy-induced cell killing and acquire drug resistance. A comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms contributing to the complex story of tumor progression and drug resistance will help facilitate the development of effective approaches that are aimed at preventing the clonal selection of drug-tolerant cancerous cells and maximizing patient benefits.

The phenomenon of drug resistance, which was firstly considered when bacteria became resistant to certain antibiotics, is defined as the decrease in the efficacy and potency of a drug to produce therapeutic merits at clinically relevant pharmaceutical concentrations and represents a major impediment to the disease treatment and patient overall survival (OS) [2]. In general, anti-cancer drug resistance can be classified as intrinsic (or de novo) and adaptive (or acquired) resistance [3]. A variety of treatment-naive malignancies exhibited primary resistance to certain cytotoxic drugs and combinations due to a pre-existing genetic alteration or cell-state, being termed as intrinsic drug resistance [2]. While adaptive resistance is generally regarded as a sophisticated evolutionary process that occurs during treatment via distinct pathways, as cancerous cells adaptively evolve themselves to survive therapeutics [4]. Adaptive resistance also arises by therapy-induced selection of pre-existing genetic alterations within the original malignancies [3]. Indeed, majority of patients showed cross-resistance to a broad range of anti-cancer agents with varying chemical structures and distinct mechanisms of action, the phenomenon of which known as multidrug resistance (MDR) [5]. MDR, which is the principal limiting factor to achieve cures in cancer patients, remains a reasonable explanation for why malignant variants that relapsed after initial therapy typically showed acquired resistance to both the index and alternative regimens [6].

Clinical cancer drug resistance can be caused by plenty of factors, and deciphering the possible mechanisms will pave the way towards the development of novel precision medicine treatment modalities that are able for overcoming resistance. In the current review, we focus on several important topics in this field, discuss the diverse and dynamic strategies that cancers use to evade drug response, and summarize the current strategies and major obstacles for forestalling cancer drug resistance.

Tumor heterogeneity and evolution

Genetic instability is a major factor in several aspects of cancer pathogenesis such as tumor heterogeneity, which is perhaps the causation of drug resistance that is easiest to conceptualize. High biological heterogeneity exists within a single tumor in individual tumor cells and in cells constituting the tumor microenvironment (TME) (intra-tumor heterogeneity), or within distinct tumors in the same patient or among patients (inter-tumor heterogeneity) [7].

Although cancer is usually caused by the clonal growth of a single founder cell, the expansion of progeny cells after malignant transformation, coupled with a variety of mutational processes that generate spatiotemporal genetic diversity, promotes the emergence of distinct subgroups of cancer cells, leading to an increased and evolved heterogeneity [7]. This so-called “tumor evolution” describes the presence of multiple divergent cancer cell subpopulations in the same tumor or patient, promoting primary/metastatic lesions and cancer relapse as well as drug resistance after therapeutic failure [8]. Selective therapeutic pressure leads to the disappearance of drug-sensitive subpopulations and positive selection of drug-resistant clones, resulting in a decreased cancer heterogeneity and complete change in tumor phenotype [9]. The expansion of resistant subpopulations rapidly reestablishes heterogeneity by acquiring new mutations that provide survival benefits to progeny cells [10].

Differences in TME are another form of tumor heterogeneity contributing cancer drug resistance by preventing immune clearance of malignant cells, hindering drug absorption and stimulating paracrine growth factors to signal cancer cell growth, etc [3, 11]. For example, immunosuppressive TME, so-called “immune desert” (minimal effector immune cell infiltrate), is recognized as a major impediment for immune-mediated anti-tumor effects. This brings about the investigation of a considerable amounts of techniques to turn “immune desert” into “inflamed” tumors (highly infiltrated with immune cells and proinflammatory cytokines) by combining immunotherapy with other anti-cancer agents [1, 11].

Traditionally, cancer cell states are perceived as dichotomous: drug sensitive or resistant. Based on this binary view, the standard regimens are aimed at eradicating tumors as quickly by applying “maximum tolerated dose (MTD)” therapy over multiple cycles, so that drug resistance does not arise [12]. However, owing to the tumor heterogeneity, drug-sensitive and resistant subclones are not mutually exclusive and may coexist and compete with each other for survival within the same tumor. Thus, such aggressive therapeutics not only eliminate the majority of drug-sensitive cells, but also select the minority of drug-resistant subgroups, which eventually becomes the primary clone, leading to treatment failure (Fig. 1) [2, 13].

In fact, competition between drug-sensitive and resistant subgroups might be the only natural force inhibiting the emergence of drug resistance [2]. Therefore, containing tumors at a fixed tolerable level by minimum-necessary dose regimens (“metronomic therapy”) can allow drug-sensitive cells to expand at the expense of resistant ones, and might be more effective in delaying resistance emergence [14]. Another similar strategy is called “treatment holiday”, where a patient’s treatment is stopped for some time to avoid selection for drug-resistant cells (Fig. 1) [15]. However, based on these moderate therapeutics, drug-sensitive cells might acquire de novo mutations and develop adaptive drug resistance [2]. By employing mathematical models, Hansan and colleagues proposed that when patient cannot tolerate any tumor burden, it is advantageous to enforce a swift and sustained aggressive therapeutics. However, when tumors are rapidly mutating, a mild treatment regime is suggested for allowing a certain of drug-sensitive cells to survive and competitively suppress the resistant cells [16].

Genetic and epigenetic determinants

Genetic instability plays a critical role in tumor heterogeneity and evolution, mediating resistance to a range of anti-cancer agents. Goldie JH and colleagues found a positive correlation between chemotherapeutic resistance and the number of spontaneous genetic mutations through mathematical models, which might be utilized in adjusting the dosage or type of administered treatment [17]. As with the tumor heterogeneity, resistant genetic alterations might be pre-existing in a small subset of cancer cells, or generate during treatment [15].

Genetic changes can be divided into driver mutations, which give cancer cells the growth advantage of tumor transformation, and passenger mutations, which do not promote cancer evolvement [18]. Drivers, which are sought after as druggable targets and employed in therapeutic decision making, characterize the molecular characteristics of cancer, and help predict the prognosis of patients [19]. Driver mutations can activate proteins even in the absence of activation signals, making it an escape regulatory control. Drug-resistant alterations, on the other hand, allow proteins to evade inhibition by certain therapeutics, usually by altering the shape or surface of the active site. In addition, alternative mechanisms of drug resistance occur that are independent with qualitative and/or quantitative changes in the driver genes [18, 20].

In addition to genetic mechanisms, accumulating evidence suggests an intimate involvement of the epigenetic machinery in inducing drug resistance [21]. There is evidence that epigenetic regulators may act as promoters or inhibitors in different tumors. Many epigenetic regulators are overexpressed in tumors and contribute to drug resistance by inhibiting apoptosis, inducing autophagy and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), enhancing dryness, and activating a variety of signaling pathways including the PI3K/Akt, Notch, and NF-κB pathways [22]. Epigenetic modifications, represented by DNA methylation, can exert profound effects on the transcriptional network, causing inheritable or non-inheritable phenotypic changes without altering the genetic codes [23].

It has been discovered that a small percentage of cells can enter a “drug-resistant persistence (DTP)” state within a tumor, either through selection of pre-existing inherently refractory subclones or through evolution driven by adaptive transcriptional plasticity [24]. DTP cells, possessing no resistance-associated mutations, can survive without appreciable proliferation after sustained drug exposure [21, 24]. They can also reversibly recover and spawn new progenies after drug withdrawal, or even evolve and propagate in the presence of anti-cancer therapeutics by becoming drug-tolerant expanded persisters (DTEPs) [21]. These DTPs and DTEPs, sharing a remodeled chromatin state that is quite distinct from their drug-sensitive counterparts, may serve as residual reservoirs and survive multiple rounds of therapy in a dormant state during treatment until acquiring more permanent resistant features, assisting them to withstand the onslaught of drug exposure via an epigenetically reprogrammed transcriptome [25].

The genetic and epigenetic resistance-conferring alterations are not mutually exclusive but instead coexist within a given cancer, driving resistance development and therapeutic failure [26]. It is likely that cancers intrinsically sensitive to anti-tumor therapy themselves contain at least one resistant subclone by genetic and/or epigenetic heterogeneity/alteration, whose expansion leads to acquired resistance [3]. Suppressing resistance emergency calls for adopting a multi-drug strategy that targets multiple cancer dependencies. The combination should consider simultaneously targeting a single target with multiple non-overlapping agents, targeting multiple nodes in the same transduction network, targeting distinct targets from bypass pathways, etc [18]. With the vast number of possible combinations, predicting the optimal scheme remains a formidable task. Currently, the development of artificial intelligence (AI) technology and the increased data gathering needed to make reliable predictions for its deep learning, has been made possible by huge advances in computationally intensive capabilities [27]. Such a challenging future perspective can become a reality in the near future.

Among the various mechanisms inducing anti-tumor drug resistance, drug extrusion by the drug efflux pumps represents an important cause of MDR. Cancerous cells can translocate the cytotoxic agents from the inner to the outer leaflet of the cytomembrane in an ATP-dependent manner through the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters [6]. Until now, a total of 50 ABC genes have been identified in human genome, which can be classified into 7 subfamilies (termed from ABCA to ABCG), overexpression of which have been found to be involved in MDR in different types of tumor samples and cells [5, 28].

Multi-drug resistance protein 1 (MDR1; also known as ABCB1 and P-glycoprotein) is the first confirmed ABC transporter, which is expressed at low levels in almost all tissues but over-expressed in MDR cancerous cells [29]. Overexpressed breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP/ABCG2) in malignant cells results in resistance to a wide spectrum of chemotherapeutics, which is reported to be linked with poor prognosis in different cancer types [30]. Many researchers are focusing on the development of chemosensitizers for specifical inhibition of ABC transporters to achieve MDR reversal. Liu K and colleagues found that the overexpression of MDR1 determines the ineffectiveness of epirubicin for treating the diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL), while the utilization of melatonin (a down-regulator of MDR1) can ultimately sensitize DLBCL cells to epirubicin therapy [31].

The large soluble carrier (SLC) is another family of membrane-transporting proteins that determines drug influx, and the inactivation or decreased expression of them can be observed in chemo-resistant cells [32]. Such SLC family members include folate transporters (SLC19A1 and SLC46A1), organic cation transporters (OCT) (SLC22A1-3), organic anion transporters (OAT) (SLC22A6-8), copper transporters (SLC31A), multidrug and toxin extrusion proteins (MATEs) (SLC47A), and so on [6]. Several studies revealed that OCT1 expression is a predictor biomarker of imatinib response in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) patients [33]. Platinum resistance is also reported to be caused by downregulation of copper transporter 1 (CRT1, SLC31A), which was proved to be implicated in uptaking platinum-based antitumor agents, whereas its knockdown led to an 80% reduction in cisplatin uptake [34].

Lysosomes also play a vital role in MDR. Cumulative evidence demonstrated that lysosomes promote the drug resistance of hydrophobic weak base chemotherapeutics through a specific mechanism called lysosomal sequestration [35]. Lysosomal sequestration occurs when hydrophobic weak base drugs become entrapped in lysosomes upon encountering the acidic lysosomal lumen, leading to their increased lysosomal compartmentalization and reduced drug concentrations in target sites [36]. In addition, some ABC transporters can be located on the lysosome membrane, mediating the active transfer of drugs into lysosome, further promoting the active isolation of anti-cancer agents [35]. Studies also suggest lysosomal exocytosis, another process in which lysosomes fuse with the plasma membrane and release their cargo into the extracellular TME, to be involved in the clearance of sequestered drugs and might provide an additional line of cell defense [37]. Ridinger J. and colleagues found that in chemotherapy-resistant neuroblastoma models, the absence and inhibition of histone deacetylases 10 (HDAC10) inhibits doxorubicin efflux through lysosomal exocytosis and promotes doxorubicin accumulation within cells [38].

In light of the lysosomal participation in drug resistance, various anti-tumor strategies have been developed, such as preventing the sequestration in lysosomes by altering the structure and reducing the alkalinity of chemotherapeutic agents [35]; disrupting the acidification of lysosomes and reverse the lysosome isolation of anti-cancer drugs by using well-tolerated lysosome alkalinizers [36]; disrupting lysosome acidification and lysosome sequestration by targeted inhibition of V-ATPase [39]; and lysosomal photodestruction etc.

Cancer stem cells (CSCs)

The CSCs represent minor and distinct populations of cells within a tumor, which own the ability to self-renew and differentiate resemblances to adult tissue stem cells [40]. The CSCs are uniquely capable of seeding new tumors, also being termed as tumor initiating cells or tumor-precursor cells [41]. CSCs were initially identified in Acute Myelocytic Leukemia (AML) and firstly reported by Lapidot et al. in 1994 [42], which have since been found in a variety of solid tumors. However, as for the origin of CSCs, it is not clear whether some malignant cells acquire a CSC phenotype through clonal evolution or CSCs arise in normal stem cells [43].

CSCs are more resistant than non-CSCs to conventional therapies, and are likely to be responsible for drug resistance [44]. CSCs are characterized by some special phenotypes. CD44+/CD24− represents the CSC subset in most solid tumors [45]. This is because CD44 can bind to hyaluronic acid (HA), assisting the attachment of CSCs to the extracellular matrix, and contributing to their migration [46]. Being abundant in the embryonic epithelium, CD133 is also overexpressed in CSCs of many malignancies with strong resistance to anti-cancer agents [47]. The CSCs are MDR and escape various treatments such as radio- and chemotherapy, owing to their enhanced capabilities including overexpression of drug efflux transporters, slower cell cycle kinetics, enhanced DNA damage repair machinery, immune escape, and greater cellular plasticity, etc [43].

The CSC hypothesis has led to some debate that the limited efficacy of conventional anticancer therapies is due to targeting the majority of non-CSCs within a single tumor without eliminating the rare CSC subsets. The surviving CSCs are qualified to serve as the precursors of new tumor masses, ultimately leading to drug resistance and clinical relapse (Fig. 3) [44]. Insights into the CSC hold the promise of yielding novel therapeutic strategies designed to eradicate CSCs, with the possible benefit of achieving extended or permanent clinical remissions.

Novel strategies for the war against cancer drug resistance

Proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC)

PROTAC is a novel strategy for specific degradation of proteins of interest (POI) by hijacking the endogenous ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), which was firstly emerged in 2001 and presents a promising alternative to traditional molecular targeted therapeutic agents [48]. PROTAC molecule consists of three specific components: (1) a POI ligand responsible for specifically recruiting pathogenesis-related proteins (PRPs) involved in cancer development; (2) an E3 ubiquitin ligand responsible for hijacking E3 ubiquitin ligase; and (3) a linker that connects these two functional ligands [48]. Based on this special structure, PROTACs brings POI and E3 Ligase in spatial proximity. Once the “POI: PROTAC: E3” ternary complex is formed, E2 ubiquitin coupling enzyme will transfer ubiquitin to lysine residues on the surface of POIs, which are then recognized by the 26 S proteasome and proteolytically cleaved [48].

In contrast to traditional small-molecule inhibitors (SMIs), PROTAC acts by degrading rather than inhibiting pathogenic POIs in an event-driven manner, which can promote the degradation of multiple rounds of target proteins and assist to prevent the accumulation of drug targets, making it more effective in countering drug resistance due to target overexpression [49]. PROTAC is also less susceptible to the target mutations because they can turn weak inhibitors, agonists, or non-functional binders into potent degraders [49]. Besides, PROTAC can also degrade some proteins that are considered “undruggable”, such as RAS family protiens and transcription factors [50]. Therefore, PROTAC has the advantages of improving selectivity and specificity, which could be utilized in cases of resistant malignancies where traditional inhibitors have failed to be effective. Salami J. and colleagues conducted a head-on comparison of the currently approved androgen receptor (AR) antagonist enzalutamide and its PROTAC derivative ARCC-4, and found that ARCC-4 could solve the resistance barrier of enzalutamide by degradation of AR targeted proteins [51]. Similarly, estrogen receptor (ER) targeted PROTACs offer alternative approaches for treating breast cancer resistant to traditional ER antagonist such as Fulvestrant [49].

Currently, several novel PROTACs for treating drug resistant cancers are in clinical trial. For example, ARV-110/ARV-766, which are potent AR degraders in phase II clinical trial, are evaluated for the treatment of metastatic castration resistant prostate cancer [52, 53]. BGB-16,673, a Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) degrader under phase I investigation, was proved to be a potent bioactive agent for overcoming on-target resistance from BTK inhibitors and presents sustainable long-term tumor regression in lymphoma xenograft models [54]. Although there are still many obstacles and challenges to be overcame, PROTACs have great therapeutic potential with its unique advantages. We anticipate that with novel PROTACs entering clinical testing and many clinical trials ongoing, we are looking forward to witnessing the proof-of-concept data being translated into clinical application soon.

Targeted nanomedicine for cancer therapeutics

Nanomedicine is emerging as an innovative and promising strategy aimed at overcoming MDR of cancer. Nanomedicine modalities have been applied to shape the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamic performance of delivered anti-cancer agents in a variety of ways, such as increasing drug availability at disease sites, prolonging drug circulation time, and impacting the drug’s metabolism pathway, via active/passive or static/dynamic targeting strategies [55–57]. Moreover, nanoparticles (NPs), which are not physically recognized as substrates by ABC transporters, remains an efficient and attractive option for surmounting MDR [56].

Currently, numerous unique NPs, such as liposomes, polymeric NPs, inorganic NPs, and biological NPs (including exosomes, ferroprotein, etc.) have been developed extensively to overcome drug resistance, some of which already made it into preclinical and clinical development. Table 1 lists some representative nano-formulations for overcoming drug resistance currently under clinical investigation. Besides, several formulations have been commercialized, such as liposomal doxorubicin (Doxil®, Caelyx® and Myocet®, etc.), liposomal vincristine (Marqibo®), liposomal daunorubicin (DaunoXome®), albumin-bound paclitaxel (Abraxane®), and polymeric micelles of paclitaxel (Genexol-PM®), etc [56].

Recently, biomimetic nanodrug delivery systems (BNDDS), which are designed and fabricated based on the biomaterials isolated from cytomembranes, platelets, immune cells, and various microbes, have attracted more and more attention [58]. Being natural carriers for proteins and molecules, BNDDS owns low toxicity and immunogenicity, demonstrating favorable biocompatibility and a stronger capability of crossing biological barriers [59]. Pascucci and colleagues study found that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can encapsulate cytotoxic agents through their exosomes, which can be specifically delivered to target cells, resulting in potent tumor suppressing efficacy [60]. Yang and colleagues found that the optimized exosomes of brain endothelial bEND.3 cells can penetrate the Blood Brain Barrier (BBB), resulting in specific delivery of encapsulated agents to human glioblastoma tissues and cells in both in and ex vivo experiments [61].

Despite the fast-track development of BNDDS, there is still a long way to go to fix major bottlenecks in clinical translation. For example, cytomembrane based BNDDS still now faces technical obstacles mainly due to the complex preparation process and relatively low production [59]. Thus, more pioneering research is needed to address the issues in the process of scale-up production and facile preparation. Besides, the repeatability of the manufacturing process and the preservation of functional surface proteins are critical, requiring additional monitoring and control measures.

However, treating cancer is not a one-fit-all scenario because of inter-patient variability. Monitoring and evaluating the degree of tumor drug accumulation can help to predict the therapeutic outcome. This novel concept, which integrates diagnostic and therapeutic functions in the same entity, was termed as theranostics [62]. Being conceived for non-invasively monitoring the evolution of cancer during the course of treatment, nano-theranostic technology would reflect the real-time characteristics of cancer in each patient, through the results of which we can effectively screen patients who are likely to positively respond to the treatment (featured by high accumulation of theranostic NPs) from those need an alternative therapeutic option [63]. The early feedbacks on the therapeutic effects offers a significant advantage permitting a better management of cancer and cancer drug resistance.

Cancer immunotherapy

The advent of immunotherapy has changed the paradigm of cancer treatment. Unlike traditional drugs being employed for directly killing, immunotherapeutic agents are used to eliminate malignancies indirectly via stimulating the activation of immune systems. As described above, malignant tumors can be classified as “inflamed” and “immune deserts”. Unsurprisingly, an inflamed tumor phenotype is associated with better clinical outcomes. However, immunotherapy-induced regulation of an “immune desert” TME can yield similar results, highlighting opportunities for therapeutic intervention [74].

Strategies designed to strengthen a more robust T cell response are at the forefront of cancer immunotherapy. By stimulating immune cells and utilizing the body’s own cancer-specific immune response to overcome the immune-escape of malignant cells, immune cells can continue to be functional in tumor surveillance and elimination [75]. One successful example is chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells directed at CD19, which have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in patients with refractory large B cell lymphoma and B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia [76]. Two other major types of adoptive T cell currently being studied are tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) treatment and T-cell receptor (TCR)-engineered cell therapy [77]. Although adoptive T cell immunotherapy is currently effective in hematologic tumors, its efficacy in solid tumors is not as expected owing to the heterogeneity within cancer cell and external TME [78].

Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) are two well-known immune checkpoints located on surface of T cells, which under normal circumstances can inhibit the over-activation of T cells, preventing autoimmune diseases [79]. However, when encountering a tumor, they will prevent T cells from approaching the tumor, weakening the ability of the immune system to recognize and eradicate cancer cells [79]. By using immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), cytotoxic T lymphocytes can be largely activated, and the anti-tumor immune effects can be reestablished [78]. The anti-CTLA-4 mAb (Ipilimumab) was the first ICI approved by FDA for the treatment of melanoma in 2011. And currently, PD-1/PD-L1 mAbs have been widely employed in lymphoma, melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, kidney cancer, and so on [78].

Another strategy to activate antitumor immune response is to introduce oncolytic viruses (OV) into the TME [80]. OV represents a new class of therapeutic agents that preferentially infect or replicate in tumor cells and exert their anti-tumor efficiency through the induction of tumor cell lysis and systemic anti-tumor immunity [81]. A variety of OVs are currently being explored in preclinical and clinical studies, including herpes viruses, polioviruses, and coxsackie viruses, etc [81]. Talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC), a modified herpes simplex virus, is the first OV approved by FDA for the treatment of unresectable melanoma [82]. T-VEC causes direct lysis of tumor cells and releases granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), promoting antigen presenting cell (APC) recruitment, maturation, and function [82]. ONCOS-102, a novel OV consists of adenovirus engineered to express GM-CSF, showed preliminary antitumor efficacy in clinical trials for treating prostate cancer (NCT03514836), melanoma (NCT03003676), and advanced peritoneal malignancies (NCT02963831) [74, 83].

In that these OVs have co-evolved with humans, they can provide unique ways to stimulate immune attack. However, when they are developed as therapeutic agents, careful attention should be paid to establishing appropriate dosing regimens, pharmacodynamic assays, biosafety concerns, new manufacturing and regulatory pathways, and so on [84].

Monitoring of response and adaptive interventions

Real-time evaluation of drug sensitivity and monitoring of therapeutic response would enable earlier modifications in drug dosage, schedules and therapeutic regimens in the clinic [1]. Conventional way of evaluating therapeutic response is based on measuring changes in tumor volume. Recently, functional imaging during therapeutic intervention has enabled a faster and more-precise assessment of response in some tumor types [85]. For example, using positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET–CT) scan-based functional imaging to assess patients’ response to chemotherapy is clinically available in Hodgkin’s lymphoma [85].

Additionally, growing evidences demonstrate that ctDNA-based evaluation can be a valuable tool for monitoring tumor heterogeneity, evolution and therapeutic drug sensitivity. Real-time ctDNA evaluation might provide a time window for identifying patients who may relapse, providing information on cancer evolution during therapy, thereby facilitating to the identification of the next therapy strategy at an earlier time point, especially if dominant resistance mutations are likely to emerge [86].

The identification of resistance-conferring mutations is paramount for precision care of patients with advanced malignancies. Although our understanding of cancer genetics has grown exponentially during the past two decades, there are still a large number of cases in which genomic analysis fails to identify effective and sensitive therapeutic drugs [87]. Even when targetable genomic alterations are identified, patients do not always respond to the nominated therapy. For these patients, in vitro drug screening, validating the optimal therapeutic strategy in cultured circulating tumor cells (CTCs), established organoids, or patient-derived xenografts (PDX), provides a mechanism to confirm drug sensitivity, predict therapeutic efficacy, and suggest additional therapeutics options [88].

Currently, enrolling patients with advanced cancer — with MDR after progression under second and third-line treatments — in clinical trials is challenging, because these patients are heavily pretreated and do not fulfill the entry criteria for clinical trials. Compassionate request of non-FDA-approved drugs or FDA-approved drugs for another cancer type by “N of 1 study” is time-consuming, costly, and is always not amenable. However, in vitro drug testing provides an effective platform for screening potential sensitive drug combinations, which is beneficial to both physicians and patients [87]. We can anticipate that in the near future, in vitro drug testing might be an effective and important way contributing individualized treatment.

Conclusions, unresolved questions and future directions

The leading cause of treatment failure is cancer drug resistance, which is responsible for more than 90% of cancer-related deaths and should be our most dangerous enemies [89]. The therapeutic response and prognosis of cancer patients largely depends on diverse biological processes that influence the sensitivity of tumor cells to anti-cancer agents. Despite remarkable advances in cancer biology and treatment, this challenge remains pervasive in the protracted war against cancer drug resistance [90].

Multiple determinants of resistance have been identified in previous studies and summarized in this review. Understanding how drug resistance evolves during the course of tumor evolution is essential to design rational therapeutic regimens, determine the timing of therapy deployment, interruption, regimen switch, or dose alteration to tackle the multifaceted problem of cancer drug resistance.

Traditional formulations of chemotherapeutic agents employed for treating cancer owns no specificity and is highly hazardous to normal tissues. In the past decades, nanotherapeutics have transformed the clinical therapeutic concept of cancer from traditional disease-specific therapy (right drug to right disease) to site-specific therapy (right drug to right tumor sites) via a variety of targeting strategies, such as passive/active targeting, static/dynamic targeting [56, 57]. While theranostic technology will certainly revolutionize the therapeutic concept from site-specific to spatiotemporal specific therapy (deliver right drug to right sites at the right time), further improving the therapeutic outcome [62]. However, in spite of the effects that we made, drug resistance still remains a major challenge. Thereby, in vitro drug screening provides an effective platform for the discovery of cancer sensitive therapeutic options for individual patients, truly realizing the concept of individualized treatment (delivery right drug to right patient at the right sites and moment) (Fig. 4).

The advancement of treatment technology promotes the transformation of treatment concept of cancer. With the advent of nanotechnology, theranostics and the establishment of in vitro drug screening, there has been a significant transformation in the concept of cancer treatment from the initial disease-specific treatment with traditional anti-tumor formulations, through site-specific therapy and spatiotemporal therapy, and ultimately to individualized medicine

Unfortunately, with the novel technologies applied in the clinic, cancer cells will still evolve drug resistance by novel mechanisms. Our current strategies for preventing the emergence of resistance or salvaging patients after resistance are still limited. It remains highly likely that we run out of therapeutic options before the tumor runs out of resistance mechanisms. Therefore, apart from the advancement of therapeutic technologies, the revolution of treatment philosophy also holds vital importance.

In the fight against tumor resistance, several important issues hold critical significance (Fig. 5). We have reached an agreement on the first question, acknowledging that both genetic alterations and epigenetic changes collectively play pivotal roles in the development of cancer drug resistance. However, scientists have not yet reached a consensus on some other key aspects. One of the fundamental questions that continues to perplex researchers is the origin of drug-resistant cells. Are these cells the pre-existing clones within the primary tumor, or do they emerge as a result of the acquisition of resistance upon drug exposure? If resistance arises due to drug pressure, are these resistant clones derived from normal drug-sensitive cancer cells that undergo genetic or epigenetic changes, or do they originate from CSCs that differentiate into resistant phenotypes (Fig. 5)? The implications of these unresolved questions and determination of the resistant cell origin are profound, as different answers may guide us towards distinct strategies. Traditionally, we usually recognize that resistance is acquired and resistant cells arise from drug-sensitive cancer cells, and the treatment schedules mainly apply sustained MTD therapy with the expectation to eradicate malignant cells as quickly as possible. However, if resistant cells are derived from CSCs, then therapies aimed at eliminating or inhibiting the self-renewal and differentiation capabilities of CSCs may be necessary. Furthermore, if resistance is primarily intrinsic and arises from pre-existing genetic heterogeneity, it will be difficult for us to eliminate all tumor cells in one go by the above-mentioned strategies. Based on this “pre-existing hypothesis”, the competition between drug sensitive and resistant clones might be the natural force inhibiting the emergence of drug resistance [16]. The drug resistant and sensitive clones are competing with each other in the TME, such delicate interplay is similar to the balance between “yin” (blue) and “yang” (pink) in traditional Chinese culture (the Yin and Yang in crosstalk are mutually inhibitory) (Fig. 6). Thus, in order to start a protracted war against cancer, we should firstly figure out who is the true enemy of us. According to the “acquired resistance hypothesis”, we should regard all tumor cells as our biggest rival, and a MTD therapy must be the best strategy. However, if the resistant subclones pre-exist, it seems prudent to regard the resistant clones as our main adversary. Drawing from the well-known saying “the enemy of my enemy is my friend”, our optimal strategy is no to strive to eliminate all tumor cells indiscriminately based on drug sensitivity. Instead, we should maintain the number of drug-sensitive cells in a certain level, allowing them to compete with drug-resistant clones. This approach resembles the stratagem of “killing a person with a borrowed knife” from the traditional Chinese masterpiece of “Sun Tzu’s Art of War”. According to this hypothesis, the moderate strategies of “metronomic therapy” and “treatment holidays” would be more appropriate. After all, uniting all the forces that can be united is the magic weapon of victory, and tumor cells themselves are no exception. Under the guidance of this medical philosophy, our approach to treating tumors is somewhat similar to managing chronic diseases, with the emphasis on control rather than cure.

In conclusion, the battle against cancer drug resistance necessitates not only advancements in therapeutic technologies but also a paradigm shift in our treatment philosophy. The future of cancer treatment lies in our ability to adapt and evolve our therapeutic approaches, drawing inspiration from both modern science and ancient wisdom. As we continue to unravel the mysteries of cancer resistance, we must remain open to new ideas and innovative strategies, ultimately striving for a balance between controlling the disease and preserving the quality of life for our patients.
