Copper‐Regulated Modulation of the MdbZIP11–MdHAT5 Transcriptional Module Enhances ABA‐Mediated Immunity in Apple
Authors: Hongjia Yu, Zhouzheng Yang, Hanqi Zhou, Chang Geng, Xia Yan, Lili Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTranscription factor‐mediated integration of hormonal signalling is central to plant immunity, yet how such regulatory modules are activated by external signals in perennial fruit crops remains largely unknown. Here, we show that the effector protein SyCD1 from Saccharothrix yanglingensis Hhs.015 enters apple ( Malus domestica ) leaf cells and reduces Cu2+ levels, thereby relieving Cu2+‐associated repression of the basic leucine zipper transcription factor MdbZIP11. This Cu2+ reduction promotes the accumulation and transcriptional activation of MdbZIP11, enabling downstream activation of abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis‐mediated immune responses. Mechanistically, MdbZIP11 physically interacts with the ABA‐related protein kinase MdSnRK2A, and this interaction is associated with phosphorylation of MdbZIP11 and increased transcriptional activity. MdbZIP11 directly binds to the promoter of the HD‐ZIP transcription factor gene MdHAT5, transcriptionally activating its expression; MdHAT5 subsequently functions as a positive regulator of ABA accumulation and disease resistance. Importantly, genetic epistasis analysis confirms that MdHAT5 is indispensable for MdbZIP11‐mediated ABA accumulation and disease resistance. Together, our findings uncover a bZIP–HD‐ZIP transcription factor relay that amplifies ABA signalling to orchestrate immune responses in apple, providing a conceptual framework for hormone‐guided disease resistance in perennial fruit crops.Keywords: ABA signalling, apple ( Malus domestica ), bZIP–HD‐ZIP transcriptional module, copper‐responsive, Valsa mali

Introduction

Apple Valsa canker (AVC), caused by the necrotrophic fungal pathogen Valsa mali (syn. Cytospora mali), is one of the most destructive diseases affecting apple production worldwide (Feng et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2024). The disease is characterized by persistent infection, high recurrence rates and substantial yield losses, posing a serious threat to sustainable apple production (Meng et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2011). Owing to the limited effectiveness of chemical control strategies, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying host resistance to V. mali is essential for identifying key regulatory nodes that can be exploited for durable disease‐resistance breeding in perennial fruit crops (Mine 2024; Aerts et al. 2022).

Basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factors represent a highly conserved regulatory family that plays central roles in stress‐responsive transcriptional reprogramming (Guo et al. 2024; Li et al. 2016). bZIP proteins are well established as key regulators of abscisic acid (ABA) signalling, acting downstream of core ABA signalling components to modulate gene expression under adverse conditions (Srivastava et al. 2017; Tomaž et al. 2022; Meng et al. 2023). In parallel, accumulating evidence indicates that certain bZIP transcription factors are functionally linked to metal ion‐associated processes, including zinc and copper homeostasis, through regulation of metal‐responsive genes and transport pathways (Qing et al. 2024; Lilay et al. 2021). Given that metal ions are integral to redox balance and signal transduction, these observations raise the possibility that bZIP‐mediated transcription integrates multiple physiological cues to fine‐tune stress responses. However, whether bZIP transcription factors coordinate metal‐related regulatory inputs with ABA‐dependent immune signalling during fungal infection remains largely unresolved in apple.

ABA is widely recognized for its central role in abiotic stress adaptation, whereas its contribution to plant–pathogen interactions is highly context dependent and remains mechanistically unresolved in many crop systems. The function of ABA is highly species‐dependent, and it mediates either disease resistance or susceptibility in different crops (Kishor et al. 2022). Depending on the pathogen type, infection stage and host background, ABA can either suppress or potentiate immune activation (Qin et al. 2024; Yop et al. 2023). Increasing evidence indicates that ABA signalling enhances disease resistance in specific pathosystems, yet the transcriptional circuits that link ABA accumulation to immune output during fungal infection are poorly characterized (Qin et al. 2024). Furthermore, pathogen‐induced remodelling of core ABA signalling regulators has been reported to increase ABA sensitivity and amplify immune outputs, enabling plants to more effectively counter pathogen attack (Kong et al. 2015; Huang et al. 2024). Collectively, these findings support the concept that ABA acts as a positive immune regulator under particular pathological conditions, with its effects dependent on the coordinated activity of key signalling nodes. At the molecular level, SNF1‐related protein kinases 2 (SnRK2s) function as central hubs in ABA signalling by modulating the activity of downstream transcription factors, including bZIP proteins, thereby providing a potential interface between hormonal signalling and transcriptional reprogramming (Fujita et al. 2009). Pathogen‐induced perturbation of SnRK2‐mediated signalling has been increasingly recognized as a mechanism to reprogramme ABA responses during immune activation.

In this study, we identify the apple bZIP transcription factor MdbZIP11 as a central regulatory component in SyCD1‐triggered immune responses. We show that MdbZIP11 functions within an ABA‐associated transcriptional module by activating the downstream transcription factor MdHAT5, thereby promoting ABA accumulation and enhancing disease resistance. These findings uncover a regulatory framework linking upstream protein‐mediated immune cues to hormone‐associated transcriptional reprogramming in apple.

Results

SyCD1 Alters Cellular Cu2+ Levels and Cu Responsive Gene Expression in Apple

SyCD1 was previously characterized as a cupredoxin protein that activates immune responses in apple and localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, vesicles and the plasma membrane (Yu et al. 2026). Given that cupredoxin‐related proteins are often associated with copper binding and intracellular copper homeostasis, we next examined whether SyCD1 influences endogenous Cu2+ levels in apple tissues. To examine whether SyCD1 influences Cu2+ levels in vivo, relative Cu2+ levels were quantified using the rhodamine B hydrazide (RBH) method at 0, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60 and 72 h following transient overexpression of EV‐OE or SyCD1‐OE in apple (cultivar Gala 3) leaves. Successful overexpression of SyCD1 was confirmed by DNA‐level detection and immunoblot analysis (Figure S1). Compared with the empty vector (EV) control, SyCD1 expression resulted in a gradual decrease in relative Cu2+ levels over time (Figure 1A), suggesting that SyCD1 negatively affects Cu levels in apple tissues.

To assess whether SyCD1‐associated changes in Cu2+ levels are accompanied by transcriptional responses, stable transgenic apple calli overexpressing SyCD1 were analysed for the expression of Cu‐responsive genes. SyCD1‐overexpressing (SyCD1‐OE) apple calli and corresponding wild‐type (WT) controls were subjected to reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis. SyCD1 overexpression altered the transcript abundance of multiple Cu‐related genes. Specifically, the Cu transporter genes MdCOPT2 and MdCOPT6 were upregulated, whereas MdHMA51, MdYSL3 and MdFSD1 exhibited moderate yet reproducible changes in expression (Figure S2). Notably, among the Cu‐responsive genes affected by SyCD1 overexpression, multiple Cu‐responsive bZIP transcription factors exhibited coordinated transcriptional upregulation. Most bZIP genes showed an overall trend of increased expression, with MdbZIP11 exhibiting the most pronounced induction (Figure 1B). Together, these results demonstrate that SyCD1 alters cellular Cu2+ levels and is associated with coordinated transcriptional responses of Cu‐responsive genes, including the pronounced induction of the bZIP transcription factor MdbZIP11. To determine whether MdbZIP11 expression is responsive to changes in Cu2+ levels, apple leaves transiently expressing SyCD1 were treated with increasing concentrations of Cu2+ (0, 50, 100 and 150 μM), followed by RT‐qPCR analysis. The results showed that MdbZIP11 transcript levels were highest at 0 μM Cu2+ and progressively decreased with increasing Cu2+ concentrations. This indicates that elevated Cu2+ levels suppress MdbZIP11 expression (Figure 1C).

MdbZIP11 Is a Cu‐Responsive Transcriptional Activator Regulated by SyCD1

Subcellular localization analysis demonstrated that MdbZIP11 was exclusively localized to the nucleus (Figure S3), consistent with its predicted function as a transcription factor. To further support its identity as a bona fide bZIP transcription factor, sequence and phylogenetic analyses were performed. MdbZIP11 encodes a protein of 159 amino acids with a predicted molecular weight of 17.78 kDa and contains a conserved bZIP domain (Figure S4A). Secondary structure prediction revealed a prominent α‐helical region characteristic of bZIP transcription factors (Figure S4B). Conserved motif analysis identified the signature sequence SNRESARRSR (Figure S4C,D). Phylogenetic analysis showed that MdbZIP11 clustered closely with bZIP11 homologues from Pyrus × bretschneideri and formed a conserved clade with bZIP family members from Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa , indicating strong evolutionary conservation (Figure S4E).

To evaluate the transcriptional activation activity of MdbZIP11, a GAL4‐based dual‐luciferase (LUC) reporter assay was performed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Co‐expression of BD‐MdbZIP11 with the GAL4‐responsive 5× UAS reporter construct resulted in a significant increase in LUC activity relative to the BD‐EV control (Figure 1D), indicating that MdbZIP11 possesses transcriptional activation activity. Consistently, quantitative measurement using a luciferase activity assay kit further confirmed that LUC activity in MdbZIP11‐expressing samples was significantly higher than that in the control (Figure 1E). In addition, a yeast transactivation assay was performed to further verify the transcriptional activation activity of MdbZIP11. Yeast cells harbouring BD‐MdbZIP11 grew well on selective medium and exhibited blue colouration, whereas no activation was observed in the negative control (Figure S5). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MdbZIP11 possesses strong transcriptional activation activity.

Furthermore, LUC assays were conducted to examine the combined effects of SyCD1 expression and Cu2+ treatment on MdbZIP11‐mediated transcriptional activity. SyCD1 expression markedly enhanced MdbZIP11‐driven LUC activity, whereas Cu2+ treatment strongly suppressed it (Figure 1F,G). Notably, cotreatment with SyCD1 partially alleviated the inhibitory effect of Cu2+ on MdbZIP11 transcriptional activity. Together, these results indicate that SyCD1‐mediated changes in Cu2+ levels can counteract Cu2+‐dependent repression of MdbZIP11, thereby enhancing its transcriptional activity. To further examine whether Cu2+ treatment affects MdbZIP11 protein accumulation, immunoblot analysis was performed using transiently expressed MdbZIP11‐GFP in N. benthamiana leaves. The results showed that Cu2+ treatment caused a slight reduction in MdbZIP11 protein accumulation compared with the untreated control (Figure S6).

MdbZIP11 Positively Modulates Resistance to AVC

To investigate whether MdbZIP11 contributes to the apple response to V. mali infection, transient overexpression and silencing assays were performed, and MdbZIP11 transcript levels were quantified by RT‐qPCR (Figure S7A). Subsequent inoculation assays revealed that transient overexpression of MdbZIP11 reduced lesion areas by 38%, whereas silencing of MdbZIP11 increased lesion areas by 18% compared with the control (Figure 2A–D). Furthermore, stable transgenic apple calli lines overexpressing or silencing MdbZIP11 were generated (Figure S8A). In these lines, MdbZIP11 transcript levels increased by approximately 16‐fold or decreased to 0.4‐fold relative to the control (Figure S8B). Disease evaluation revealed that MdbZIP11‐overexpression (OE) lines exhibited a 34% reduction in lesion areas, whereas MdbZIP11‐RNAi lines showed a 20% increase in lesion areas (Figure 2E,F), further supporting a positive role of MdbZIP11 in apple resistance to V. mali. In addition, the expression levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS)‐related genes, including MdRBOHA, MdRBOHB, MdRBOHD, MdCAT2 and MdCPK2, were significantly higher in plants overexpressing MdbZIP11 than in the control (Figure 2G). Consistently, ROS accumulation was markedly enhanced in apple leaves overexpressing MdbZIP11 compared with control leaves (Figure 2H,I). These findings suggest that MdbZIP11 promotes defence‐associated responses during apple–V. mali interactions. To examine whether MdbZIP11 is required for SyCD1‐mediated disease resistance, MdbZIP11 was transiently silenced in apple leaves concurrently overexpressing SyCD1. Silencing of MdbZIP11 significantly compromised the enhanced resistance conferred by SyCD1 expression (Figure S9), indicating that MdbZIP11 is functionally involved in SyCD1‐mediated defence responses.

MdSnRK2A Interacts With and Enhances the Transcriptional Activity of MdbZIP11

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which MdbZIP11 enhances disease resistance in apple, a yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) screen was performed to identify potential interacting proteins (Table S3). Our analysis identified MdSnRK2A as a candidate interacting protein of MdbZIP11. Their interaction was first confirmed by a one‐to‐one (Y2H) assay (Figure 3A). Co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays further showed that MdbZIP11‐GFP was detected in the immunoprecipitated complex only when co‐expressed with MdSnRK2A‐mCherry in N. benthamiana leaves, but not in the negative controls (Figure 3B). In addition, split‐luciferase complementation imaging (Split‐LCI) assays produced a strong luminescence signal in leaves co‐expressing MdbZIP11 and MdSnRK2A, further confirming their interaction in planta (Figure 3C). In addition, subcellular colocalization analysis showed that MdbZIP11 and MdSnRK2A colocalize in the nucleus (Figure 3D). Analysis based on Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) and Manders colocalization coefficient (MCC) values confirmed the substantial spatial overlap between MdbZIP11 and MdSnRK2A, supporting the localization patterns observed in the confocal images (Table S4).

To further investigate the functional consequences of this interaction, we examined its effect on the transcriptional activity of MdbZIP11 using a LUC reporter system in N. benthamiana leaves. Three combinations were tested: MdSnRK2A‐mCherry/MdbZIP11‐pGreenII BD‐62‐SK/pro5 × UAS‐pGreenII 0800, EV‐mCherry/MdbZIP11‐pGreenII BD‐62‐SK/pro5 × UAS‐pGreenII 0800 and Cu2+/MdSnRK2A‐mCherry/MdbZIP11‐pGreenII BD‐62‐SK/pro5 × UAS‐pGreenII 0800. LUC fluorescence imaging showed that co‐expression of MdSnRK2A with MdbZIP11 resulted in a markedly enhanced luminescence signal compared with the control, whereas Cu2+ treatment substantially reduced the luminescence intensity (Figure 3E). Consistently, quantitative analysis using a luciferase activity assay kit confirmed that MdSnRK2A significantly increased the transactivation activity of MdbZIP11, while this enhancement was strongly attenuated upon Cu2+ application (Figure 3F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MdSnRK2A enhances the transcriptional activity of MdbZIP11, and that this effect is negatively regulated by Cu2+. To explore whether MdSnRK2A‐mediated enhancement of MdbZIP11 activity is associated with post‐translational modification, we performed an in vivo phosphorylation assay. MdbZIP11 was co‐expressed with MdSnRK2A or an empty vector (EV) control, followed by immunoblot detection using a phospho‐specific antibody. MdbZIP11 exhibited clear phosphorylation when co‐expressed with MdSnRK2A (Figure 3G). This result indicates that MdSnRK2A‐associated phosphorylation of MdbZIP11 occurs in vivo, providing a potential mechanistic link between MdSnRK2A interaction and the enhanced transcriptional activity of MdbZIP11.

To elucidate the role of MdSnRK2A in apple resistance to V. mali, we first generated MdSnRK2A‐overexpressing transgenic apple plants and inoculated them with V. mali (Figure S10A). The results showed that, compared with the control plants, MdSnRK2A‐overexpressing plants exhibited significantly reduced lesion areas (Figure S10B–E), indicating that MdSnRK2A positively regulates apple resistance to V. mali. To further investigate whether MdSnRK2A is involved in SyCD1‐mediated immune signalling, we examined the transcriptional level of MdSnRK2A in SyCD1‐overexpressing apple materials using RT‐qPCR. The results showed that SyCD1 did not significantly alter MdSnRK2A transcript abundance (Figure S11). Collectively, these results indicate that MdSnRK2A functions as an independent positive regulator of disease resistance in apple, while its expression is not regulated by SyCD1. Instead, MdSnRK2A likely acts as an auxiliary factor that enhances the transcriptional activity of MdbZIP11, thereby contributing to apple immune responses.

MdbZIP11‐Mediated Resistance Is Dependent on the ABA Pathway

To further explore the functional role of MdbZIP11, DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP‐seq) was performed in apple leaves. High consistency between two independent replicates (MdbZIP11_1 and MdbZIP11_2) confirmed the reliability of the dataset (Figure S12). Peak annotation revealed that approximately 16.7% of the peaks were located in promoter regions, consistent with the functional characteristics of transcription factors (Figure 4A). Motif enrichment analysis further identified a conserved ACGTGC core sequence, consistent with the canonical cis‐elements recognised by bZIP transcription factors (Guo et al. 2024) (Figure 4B). Functional annotation of the putative target genes revealed that they were predominantly enriched in biological processes related to regulation of DNA‐templated transcription and ABA response, as illustrated by GO enrichment analysis (Figure 4C). These results suggest that MdbZIP11 may function as a transcriptional regulator involved in ABA‐associated signalling pathways.

To further determine whether MdbZIP11 influences ABA biosynthesis, the expression of key ABA biosynthetic genes was examined. Overexpression of MdbZIP11 led to a significant upregulation of MdNCED1, MdNCED2 and MdNCED6, whereas silencing of MdbZIP11 resulted in their downregulation (Figure 4D; Figure S7D). Consistent with these transcriptional changes, endogenous ABA levels were markedly increased in MdbZIP11‐overexpressing leaves but significantly reduced in MdbZIP11‐silenced plants compared with the control (Figure 4E), indicating that MdbZIP11 positively regulates ABA accumulation. To assess whether ABA is required for MdbZIP11‐mediated disease resistance, MdbZIP11‐overexpressing plants were treated with fluridone (Flu), an inhibitor of ABA biosynthesis. Flu application substantially increased lesion area upon V. mali infection (Figure 4F,G), suggesting a compromised resistance phenotype. Moreover, Flu treatment markedly suppressed the induction of both defence‐related genes and ABA‐associated genes triggered by MdbZIP11 overexpression (Figure 4H, Figure S13). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MdbZIP11 enhances resistance to V. mali by promoting ABA biosynthesis, and that disruption of ABA accumulation attenuates its resistance function, indicating that the MdbZIP11‐mediated defence response is dependent on the ABA pathway.

MdbZIP11 Activates the Transcription of MdHAT5 Through Binding to Its Promoter

To identify downstream transcriptional targets of MdbZIP11, DAP‐seq data were combined with motif analysis and functional annotation, which highlighted a subset of ABA‐related genes as potential targets. Based on this analysis, four candidate genes—MdHD18, MdCAR2, MdPRP5 and MdHAT5—were selected for further investigation. Transient overexpression of MdbZIP11 in apple leaves led to significant upregulation of all four genes, among which MdHAT5 showed the highest level of induction (Figure 5A). Consistently, LUC reporter assays showed that MdbZIP11 significantly enhanced promoter activity of all four genes, with the strongest activation observed for the MdHAT5 promoter (Figure 5B). Based on its pronounced transcriptional response, MdHAT5, encoding an HD‐ZIP transcription factor, was selected as the primary downstream target of MdbZIP11 (Figure S14).

To determine whether MdbZIP11 directly regulates these genes, yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H), β‐glucuronidase (GUS) staining and LUC assays were performed. The results consistently demonstrated that MdbZIP11 associates with and activates the promoters of all four candidate genes (Figure 5C–E, Figure S15). To further determine the in vivo binding of MdbZIP11 to the MdHAT5 promoter, chromatin immunoprecipitation‐PCR (ChIP‐qPCR) assays were conducted using three regions (F1, F2 and F3) spanning the predicted binding motifs. The results showed that MdbZIP11 was significantly enriched at the F2 region, whereas no obvious enrichment was detected at F1 or F3 (Figure 5F), indicating that F2 represents the primary binding site of MdbZIP11 within the MdHAT5 promoter. To further determine whether MdSnRK2A enhances the transcriptional activation of MdHAT5 mediated by MdbZIP11, LUC reporter assays were performed using the MdHAT5 promoter. Both LUC fluorescence imaging and quantitative LUC enzymatic activity assays showed that co‐expression of MdSnRK2A further increased MdbZIP11‐dependent activation of the MdHAT5 promoter compared with MdbZIP11 alone (Figure S16). These results indicate that MdSnRK2A potentiates MdbZIP11‐driven transcriptional activation of MdHAT5. To further examine the relationship between SyCD1 and MdHAT5, the transcript level of MdHAT5 was analysed in SyCD1‐overexpressing apple tissues. RT‐qPCR analysis showed that MdHAT5 expression was significantly induced upon SyCD1‐overexpression (Figure S17). These results further support the proposed SyCD1‐MdbZIP11‐MdHAT5 regulatory cascade.

MdHAT5 Positively Regulates ABA Accumulation and Resistance to V. mali

To elucidate the role of MdHAT5 in apple resistance to V. mali, MdHAT5‐overexpression (MdHAT5‐OE) and RNA interference (MdHAT5‐RNAi) constructs were transiently introduced into apple leaves via Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation. The efficiency of overexpression and silencing was first validated by RT‐qPCR (Figure S18A). At 24 h post‐inoculation (hpi) with V. mali, MdHAT5‐OE leaves exhibited an approximately 23% reduction in lesion area compared with the control, whereas MdHAT5‐RNAi leaves showed an approximately 19% increase in lesion area (Figure S18B–E), indicating that MdHAT5 positively regulates apple resistance. To further confirm this function, stable transgenic apple calli overexpressing or silencing MdHAT5 were generated. The successful establishment of transgenic lines was verified by RT‐qPCR and DNA‐level analysis (Figure S19). Upon inoculation with V. mali, MdHAT5‐OE calli exhibited an approximately 27% reduction in lesion size compared with the wild type, whereas MdHAT5‐RNAi calli showed an approximately 22% increase in lesion area (Figure 6A,B). These results further support a positive regulatory role of MdHAT5 in apple resistance to V. mali.

Furthermore, 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining showed pronounced brown precipitates in MdHAT5‐OE leaves, indicating enhanced ROS accumulation (Figure 6C,D). Consistently, RT‐qPCR analysis demonstrated that several ROS‐related genes, including MdRBOHA, MdRBOHB and MdRBOHD, were significantly upregulated in MdHAT5‐OE leaves (Figure 6E). Collectively, these results indicate that MdHAT5 enhances ROS accumulation and activates ABA‐associated defence responses, thereby positively regulating apple resistance to V. mali.

MdbZIP11 Requires MdHAT5 to Promote ABA Accumulation and Disease Resistance

To elucidate the functional dependency between MdbZIP11 and MdHAT5, we performed a genetic analysis by transiently overexpressing MdbZIP11 while simultaneously silencing MdHAT5 in apple leaves (Figure S21). The transiently co‐transformed apple leaves were inoculated with the pathogen V. mali. Compared with the control, lesion area increased significantly by approximately 17% (Figure 6F,G), demonstrating that the resistance to V. mali conferred by MdbZIP11 is dependent on MdHAT5. To directly verify the involvement of MdHAT5 in MdbZIP11‐mediated ABA accumulation, endogenous ABA levels were measured in the transiently co‐transformed apple leaves. ABA content was markedly lower than that in leaves transiently overexpressing MdbZIP11 alone (Figures 4E and 6H). Consistently, the combined treatment markedly attenuated the ability of MdbZIP11 to activate ABA pathway‐related genes, indicating that MdbZIP11 regulates ABA signalling in an MdHAT5‐dependent manner (Figure 6I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MdHAT5 is required for MdbZIP11‐mediated ABA accumulation and the consequent enhancement of resistance to V. mali.

Discussion

In apple, effective defence against the canker pathogen V. mali relies on transcriptional networks that coordinate hormone‐mediated immunity (Meng and Zhang 2013). The present study elucidates a critical MdbZIP11‐MdHAT5 transcriptional module in which MdbZIP11 directly binds to the MdHAT5 promoter and activates its transcription (Figure 5A–E). This transcriptional activity is further potentiated through physical interaction with the ABA‐related kinase MdSnRK2A (Figure 3A–E). Functionally, this module serves as a positive regulator of apple resistance to V. mali. Collectively, this bZIP–HD‐ZIP regulatory module between MdbZIP11 and MdHAT5 represents an upstream regulatory layer that contributes to the reinforcement of ABA‐mediated defence signalling within a cellular context shaped by SyCD1‐dependent regulation, thereby promoting a robust immune response upon pathogen challenge.

Beyond transcription factor interactions, our study reveals an additional regulatory layer in which SyCD1 modulates Cu2+ levels to influence MdbZIP11 activity (Figure 1A–G). Copper is an essential yet potentially cytotoxic micronutrient, and fluctuations in intracellular Cu2+ levels are known to exert profound effects on transcriptional regulation in plants (Chen et al. 2022). Notably, several bZIP transcription factors have been implicated in plant responses to micronutrient status and heavy metal stress, suggesting that members of this family can act as regulators in metal‐associated transcriptional networks (Mao et al. 2025; Assunção 2022). In this context, our data indicate that SyCD1 expression leads to a gradual reduction in cellular Cu2+ levels, which is accompanied by a pronounced induction of MdbZIP11 expression. These observations support a model in which Cu2+ acts as a modulatory signal rather than a direct immune trigger, fine‐tuning the activity of specific transcriptional regulators. Importantly, while SyCD1‐mediated Cu2+ reduction alone is insufficient to fully activate immune responses, it appears to relieve Cu2+‐dependent repression of MdbZIP11, thereby creating a permissive cellular environment for downstream ABA‐associated transcriptional reprogramming. This regulatory mode is consistent with emerging evidence that micronutrient homeostasis can indirectly shape immune signalling outputs by constraining or enabling transcription factor function, rather than acting as a primary defence signal.

bZIP transcription factors comprise a large and conserved family in plants and have been implicated in the regulation of various stress responses, including pathogen defence. Previous studies have documented that certain bZIP proteins can act as positive or negative regulators in plant immune responses, integrating signalling pathways and directly influencing expression of defence‐related genes (Noman et al. 2017; Alves et al. 2013). In addition, bZIP factors are known to interface with hormone signalling networks, such as salicylic acid and ABA pathways, which are central to immune regulation (Alves et al. 2014).

SnRK2 kinases are plant‐specific serine/threonine protein kinases that have been established as central components of the ABA signalling network (Lu et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025). They primarily function in ABA responses and abiotic stress signalling by phosphorylating downstream effector proteins and modulating the expression of stress‐responsive genes, including specific transcription factors such as ABFs as well as ion channel regulators (Li et al. 2025; Mao et al. 2020). In the canonical ABA signalling pathway, SnRK2 activation is tightly controlled by ABA receptor‐PP2C complexes, and activated SnRK2s phosphorylate downstream targets to couple hormone perception with transcriptional and physiological responses (Umezawa et al. 2010). In our system, MdSnRK2A enhances the transcriptional activity of MdbZIP11 through physical interaction, indicating that MdSnRK2A functions as a regulatory modulator that potentiates MdbZIP11 activity within the ABA‐associated defence network, rather than acting as a primary immune receptor or a direct resistance effector. Previous studies have established that ABA‐activated SnRK2 kinases can directly phosphorylate bZIP transcription factors, such as AREB/ABFs (Kulik et al. 2011; Umezawa et al. 2013; Xu et al. 2025), and that this post‐translational modification is essential for their transcriptional activity in ABA signalling. In Arabidopsis and rice, SnRK2‐mediated phosphorylation of bZIP proteins has been shown to modulate their stability and regulatory capacity, thereby coupling hormone perception to transcriptional reprogramming (Sirichandra et al. 2010; Kobayashi et al. 2005). Consistent with this conserved regulatory paradigm, our in vivo phosphorylation assays demonstrate that MdbZIP11 undergoes phosphorylation when co‐expressed with MdSnRK2A. While the direct kinase–substrate relationship, the specific phosphorylation sites on MdbZIP11, and the causal link between phosphorylation and enhanced transcriptional activity remain to be determined, these findings suggest that MdSnRK2A‐associated phosphorylation contributes to the modulation of MdbZIP11 function within an ABA‐linked immune regulatory network. Addressing these issues will be important for understanding how ABA signalling modulates transcriptional programmes during immune responses in perennial fruit trees.

Our findings indicate that MdbZIP11 enhances apple resistance to the fungal pathogen V. mali by activating an ABA‐dependent regulatory pathway. Importantly, the ability of MdbZIP11 to induce ABA‐responsive gene expression and promote ABA accumulation is largely dependent on MdHAT5, positioning MdHAT5 as a key downstream component of the MdbZIP11‐mediated ABA regulatory framework. Although MdbZIP11 activates multiple ABA‐related genes, our genetic evidence demonstrates that MdHAT5 is an indispensable component of this regulatory module. Other ABA‐associated targets may function in parallel or contribute redundantly to ABA homeostasis; however, they are insufficient to compensate for the loss of MdHAT5 in MdbZIP11‐induced ABA accumulation and disease resistance, and their precise roles remain to be explored. This genetic dependency establishes a direct mechanistic link between MdbZIP11 activity and ABA homeostasis during pathogen challenge. MdHAT5 belongs to the homeodomain‐leucine zipper (HD‐ZIP) family of transcription factors, which has been extensively implicated in ABA‐mediated abiotic stress responses (Zhang et al. 2022; Liu, Han, et al. 2022). In apple and pear, HD‐ZIP family genes have been shown to modulate water‐use efficiency, stomatal density and ABA‐associated stress responses (Zhao et al. 2021; Liu, Li, et al. 2022; Gong et al. 2019). By functionally linking a bZIP transcription factor to an HD‐ZIP transcription factor, the present study reveals a previously underappreciated transcription factor relay that integrates ABA signalling with disease resistance. This regulatory module expands current understanding of how distinct transcription factor families cooperate to translate hormonal cues into robust immune outputs in perennial fruit trees.

In summary, our study defines a Cu2+‐associated transcriptional regulatory pathway that links SyCD1‐mediated modulation of cellular Cu2+ homeostasis to hormone‐dependent immune regulation in apple (Figure 7). We demonstrate that SyCD1‐driven alterations in Cu2+ levels activate the Cu‐responsive transcription factor MdbZIP11, whose transcriptional activity is further enhanced through interaction with the ABA‐related kinase MdSnRK2A. Activated MdbZIP11 directly induces the expression of the downstream transcription factor MdHAT5, thereby promoting ABA accumulation and enhancing resistance to V. mali. This hierarchical transcription factor cascade reveals how micronutrient homeostasis can be coupled to hormone signalling through coordinated action of distinct transcription factor families.

Working model illustrating a copper‐regulated MdbZIP11–MdHAT5 transcriptional module in apple immunity. SyCD1 is an effector protein derived from Saccharothrix yanglingensis Hhs.015. SyCD1 reduces cellular Cu2+ levels, thereby relieving Cu2+‐mediated repression of the bZIP transcription factor MdbZIP11. Activated MdbZIP11 physically interacts with the abscisic acid (ABA)‐related protein kinase MdSnRK2A, an interaction associated with phosphorylation of MdbZIP11 and enhanced transcriptional activity. MdbZIP11 subsequently binds to the promoter of the HD‐ZIP transcription factor gene MdHAT5, transcriptionally activating its expression. MdbZIP11 and MdHAT5 together function as a coordinated regulatory module to promote ABA accumulation, ultimately enhancing apple resistance to Valsa mali.

Experimental Procedures

Plants, Microbes and Growth Conditions

GL‐3 tissue culture seedlings ( Malus domestica ‘Gala 3’) were maintained on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 0.2 mg/L 6‐benzylaminopurine (6‐BA) and indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA). Apple calli ( M. domestica ‘Orin’) were cultured on MS medium containing 0.4 mg/L 6‐BA and 1.5 mg/L 2,4‐dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4‐D) in the dark at 25°C (Meng et al. 2024). N. benthamiana plants, used as a model species, were grown in a controlled greenhouse at 25°C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod (Wang et al. 2023).

Valsa mali wild‐type strain 03–8 was maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium at 25°C in the dark. Escherichia coli DH5α, E. coli BL21 (DE3) and Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105 were obtained from Shanghai Weidi Biotechnology Co. Ltd. E. coli strains were cultured in Luria Bertani (LB) medium at 37°C with shaking, while A. tumefaciens was grown in LB medium at 28°C with shaking (supplemented with 50 μg/mL rifampicin). Pathogen and plant materials were obtained from the Laboratory of Integrated Management of Plant Disease at the College of Plant Protection, Northwest A&F University, Shaanxi, Yangling, China.

Bioinformatics Analysis

The target gene sequences were retrieved from National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and conserved domains were analysed. Protein three‐dimensional structures were predicted using Alphafold 2 (https://alphafoldserver.com/), and conserved motifs within the protein sequences were identified with ESPript 3 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/cgi‐bin/ESPript.cgi).

RNA Extraction, cDNA Synthesis and qPCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted using a polysaccharide and polyphenol‐rich plant RNA extraction kit (Huayueyang), and RNA concentration was determined with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Subsequently, 1 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qPCR was performed with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a LightCycler 96 system (GenStar), with MdEF1α serving as the internal reference gene. The primers used for each target gene are listed in Table S1. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Genetic Transformation

To generate the recombinant constructs, the coding sequence (CDS) of the target gene and the silencing fragment were cloned into the pK2GW7 and pK7GWIWG2D vectors, respectively. A. tumefaciens cultures carrying the recombinant vectors were first grown in LB medium for 24 h and then transferred into fresh LB medium containing 100 μM acetosyringone (AS) at a ratio of 1:50 (v/v) for an additional 24 h culture. Bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 5–6 min, washed three times with sterile water and resuspended to an OD600 of approximately 0.6. Apple calli subcultured for 15 days were gently fragmented and immersed in the bacterial suspension for 20 min under dark conditions with shaking. After infection, calli were collected using sterile gauze, blotted dry with sterile filter paper and transferred onto antibiotic‐free callus medium for cocultivation in the dark for 24 h. The calli were then transferred onto selective medium containing the appropriate antibiotics and cultured at 25°C in the dark for 1–2 months. Positive transgenic lines were identified by molecular analysis and used for subsequent experiments (Meng et al. 2024).

Subsequent quantification of MdbZIP11 expression levels in the transgenic lines was performed via RT‐qPCR. The primers used in the present study are listed in Table S2. Stable transgenic apple calli constitutively expressing SyCD1 were generated in our previous study and were used here for downstream gene expression analyses (Yu et al. 2026).

Subcellular Localization Analysis

The CDS was cloned into the pBIND vector fused with a GFP tag, and the recombinant constructs were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium‐mediated infiltration. Fluorescence signals were observed 2 days post‐infiltration (dpi) using an FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus). Prior to imaging, 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) was infiltrated 15 min in advance to serve as a nuclear localization marker. Colocalization analysis was performed using Fiji (ImageJ) with the Coloc2 plug‐in. Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected from single optical sections, and background was subtracted prior to analysis. PCC and MCC were calculated using default threshold settings.

DAP‐Seq

The DAP‐seq assay was performed using genomic DNA extracted from leaves of M. domestica ‘Gala 3’. DAP‐seq libraries from two technical replicates and the corresponding input controls were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating paired‐end 150 bp reads in FASTQ format. Raw reads were processed using fastp (Chen et al. 2018) to remove low‐quality sequences and adapters, yielding high‐quality clean reads for downstream analysis. Filtered reads were mapped to the M. domestica ‘Golden Delicious’ reference genome (GCF_002114115.1; NCBI) using the BWA‐MEM algorithm. Peak calling was performed with MACS (Zhang et al. 2008), and peaks from technical replicates were merged using HOMER (Heinz et al. 2010). Peak annotation relative to genomic features was conducted with ChIPseeker (Yu et al. 2015). Given the heterozygous nature of the apple genome, allelic variation may potentially influence read alignment and peak calling in certain genomic regions; however, the use of a high‐quality reference genome and reproducible biological replicates minimizes potential bias in target identification.

Y1H and Y2H Assay

The Y1H assay was performed to examine whether MdbZIP11 binds to the promoter region of MdHAT5. The CDS of MdbZIP11 was cloned into the pGADT7 vector, and the promoter fragment was inserted into the pHIS2 vector. Recombinant constructs (MdbZIP11‐AD and proMdHAT5‐pHIS2) were cotransformed into the Y187 yeast strain. Cotransformations with proMdHAT5‐pHIS2 alone or with empty vector (EV‐AD) plus proMdHAT5‐pHIS2 served as negative controls. Positive colonies were selected, and 3‐amino‐1,2,4‐triazole (3‐AT) was added to suppress self‐activation of the transcription factor. The assay was conducted following the method described by Yu et al. (2020).

For Y2H, the CDS of MdbZIP11 and MdSnRK2A were cloned into the pGADT7 or pGBKT7 vector, respectively. The recombinant plasmids were co‐transformed into the appropriate Y2H yeast strain and initially selected on synthetic dropout (SD) medium lacking leucine and tryptophan (−Leu/−Trp). Positive transformants were subsequently inoculated onto SD (−Leu/−Trp/−His/−Ade) to assess protein–protein interactions. To suppress autoactivation, 3‐AT was added to the selective medium.

Split‐LCI, LUC Reporter Assay, LUC Enzymatic Activity and GUS Staining Assay

The interaction between MdbZIP11 and MdSnRK2A was examined using a split‐LCI complementation assay. A. tumefaciens strains carrying nLCI‐MdbZIP11 and cLCI‐MdSnRK2A were co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves, while combinations of nLCI‐GFP/cLCI‐MdSnRK2A and nLCI‐MdbZIP11/cLCI‐GFP were used as negative controls.

For transcriptional activation assays, the CDS of MdbZIP11 was fused to the GAL4 DNA‐binding domain in the pGreenII 62‐SK vector (BD‐MdbZIP11) and co‐expressed with the GAL4‐responsive 5× UAS‐pGreenII 0800 reporter construct in N. benthamiana leaves. The empty GAL4 DNA‐binding domain vector (BD‐EV) co‐expressed with the 5× UAS reporter served as the negative control. For promoter activation assays, the promoter regions of MdHAT5, MdHD18, MdCAR2 and MdPRP5 were cloned into the pGreenII 0800 vector and co‐infiltrated with the corresponding effector constructs via A. tumefaciens ‐mediated transient expression. After 2 days, LUC enzymatic activity was observed using the PlantView 100 multispectral dynamic fluorescence imaging system (Biolight Biotechnology). Prior to observation, leaves were treated with D‐luciferin (Solarbio) for 10 min. LUC enzymatic activity was further quantified using a Firefly Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Beyotime).

GUS staining of plant tissues was performed using a GUS staining kit (Coolaber) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Each experimental treatment was repeated at least three times.

ChIP‐qPCR Assay

ChIP‐qPCR was performed using the EpiNext CUT&LUNCH Assay Kit (EpigenTek) to assess the in vivo DNA‐binding activity of MdbZIP11. Nuclei were isolated from apple seedlings transiently expressing 35S:GFP (control) or 35S:MdbZIP11‐GFP using a modified Honda buffer, followed by filtration and washing. Isolated nuclei were permeabilized and incubated with anti‐GFP antibody or IgG (negative control). Chromatin was then cleaved, and antibody‐bound DNA–protein complexes were captured using affinity beads. After washing and elution, DNA was purified for qPCR analysis. qPCR was performed using primers targeting three promoter regions (F1, F2 and F3). Enrichment was calculated as fold change over IgG 2Ct_IgG− Ct_IP.

Co‐IP and In Vivo Phosphorylation

For the Co‐IP assays, N. benthamiana leaves were harvested 2 days after agroinfiltration and subjected to total protein extraction. Protein extracts were co‐incubated with mCherry‐Trap A beads (Chromotek) for 2 h at 4°C. After immunoprecipitation, bound proteins were eluted, separated by SDS‐PAGE and analysed by western blotting. The anti‐GFP and anti‐mCherry (Abmart) antibodies were used for western blotting. Ponceau S solution (Solarbio) was used to detect input proteins.

MdbZIP11‐GFP was co‐expressed with MdSnRK2A‐mCherry in N. benthamiana leaves by Agrobacterium‐mediated transient expression. Co‐expression of MdbZIP11‐GFP with EV‐mCherry was used as a negative control. Total proteins were extracted with the addition of phosphatase inhibitors and immunoprecipitated using GFP‐Trap A beads. Phosphorylation of MdbZIP11 was detected by immunoblotting with an anti‐phospho‐Ser/Thr antibody (Phosphosolutions), while anti‐GFP and anti‐mCherry antibodies were used to assess immunoprecipitation efficiency and co‐immunoprecipitated proteins, respectively (anti‐mCherry, 26765–1‐AP, Proteintech; anti‐GFP, 66002–1‐lg, Proteintech).

Pathogen Infection and ROS Content Detection

Valsa mali was subcultured twice on PDA, and 3‐mm mycelial plugs were obtained with a cork borer. Inoculation assays were performed following the method described by Han et al. (2024). After 24 h, lesion areas were quantified using ImageJ, with three biological replicates.

For histochemical assays, apple leaves were collected 72 h after agroinfiltration. ROS accumulation was assessed by immersing leaves in 1 mg/mL DAB solution (pH 3.8) under light for 8 h, followed by destaining in 95% ethanol until colourless. The intensity of DAB staining was then evaluated (Xu et al. 2020), with three independent replicates. Callose deposition was examined by destained leaves in 95% ethanol and incubating them in aniline blue solution in the dark for 12 h. Callose deposits were visualized under UV fluorescence using a microscope, with three independent replicates.

Transient Expression, Pathogen Bioassays and Detection of ABA Content

To investigate gene functions, A. tumefaciens strains harbouring various constructs (EV‐pBIND, EV‐pFGC5941, MdbZIP11‐pFGC5941, MdHAT5‐pFGC5941, MdbZIP11‐pBIND or MdbZIP11‐GFP + MdHAT5‐pFGC5941) were individually resuspended in an infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM MES, 0.2 mM AS) to a final optical density at OD₆₀₀ of 0.6 and infiltrated into 4‐week‐old tissue culture seedlings. For hormonal treatments, leaves of 4‐week‐old Gala 3 seedlings were treated with 100 μM ABA and Flu, followed by sample collection at 12 h post‐treatment. Subsequently, the expression levels of the relevant genes were quantified by RT‐qPCR.

For pathogen inoculation, freshly cultured V. mali was applied to apple leaves using the stab‐inoculation method as described previously (Meng et al. 2024). The lesion area and length were quantified at 24 hpi using ImageJ. For callus assays, 15‐day‐old apple calli were inoculated with V. mali, and disease symptoms were evaluated at 36 hpi (Han et al. 2024). All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.

For ABA content determination, an ABA assay kit (Fankewei) was used, and measurements were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions using a microplate reader.

Determination of Cu2+ Content

SyCD1 and the EV were transiently introduced into apple tissue culture seedlings. Samples were collected 60 h post‐transfection, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into powder. To extract Cu2+, 400 μL of extraction buffer was added to the ground samples. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected as the sample for analysis. A 10 μM working solution was prepared by diluting a 1 mM stock solution of RBH with extraction buffer. One hundred microlitres of the sample was mixed with 100 μL of RBH working solution and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Fluorescence was measured at an excitation wavelength of 554 nm and an emission wavelength of 574 nm using a fluorescence spectrometer. The Cu2+ content was quantified based on a standard curve (Jiao et al. 2018).

For Cu2+ treatment of apple tissue culture seedlings, seedlings were inverted into 50 mL centrifuge tubes containing Cu2+ solution and vacuum infiltrated for 5 min. After treatment, the seedlings were rinsed once with sterile water, gently dried with sterile filter paper, returned to tissue culture bottles and sampled at the indicated time points. For Cu2+ treatment of N. benthamiana leaves, a small wound was made on the abaxial side of the leaf using a sterile needle, followed by infiltration of the Cu2+ solution using a needleless syringe. Treated leaves were collected at the indicated time points for subsequent analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test or Student's t‐test (two‐tailed), as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v. 8.0.2.

Figure S2: Relative expression levels of Cu homeostasis‐related genes of the apple leaves overexpressing EV‐OE and SyCD1‐OE at 72 h. Data are presented as the mean ± SD of three biological replicates. Statistical differences were analysed by Student's t tests (*p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001).

Figure S3: Subcellular localization of MdbZIP11. The MdbZIP11‐GFP fusion protein was transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, and fluorescence signals were visualized by confocal laser scanning microscopy. The nuclei are indicated by DAPI staining, confirming the nuclear localization of MdbZIP11. Scale bar: 20 μm.

Figure S4: Characterization of MdbZIP11. (A) Domain analysis of MdbZIP11. (B) Prediction of MdbZIP11 tertiary structure. (C‐D) Analysis of conserved sites of the MdbZIP11 sequence. (E) Phylogenetic tree of the MdbZIP11 protein sequence constructed using the neighbour‐joining method.

Figure S5: Transcriptional activation activity of MdbZIP11. The coding sequence of MdbZIP11 was fused to the GAL4 DNA‐binding domain (BD) and transformed into yeast cells. Yeast growth on selective media lacking histidine (–His) and adenine (−Ade) indicated transactivation activity.

Figure S6: Cu2+ treatment reduces MdbZIP11 protein accumulation. Immunoblot analysis showed that Cu2+ treatment slightly reduced the accumulation of MdbZIP11 protein compared with the untreated control.

Figure S7: Reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR analysis of MdbZIP11 expression in apple leaves transiently overexpressing or silencing MdbZIP11. (A) Data corresponding to Figure 2A–D. (B) Data corresponding to Figure 4D–E. Data are presented as the mean ± SD from three biological replicates. Statistical differences were determined using Student's t tests (***p < 0.001).

Figure S8: Identification of stable MdbZIP11 overexpression apple calli lines. (A) Identification of MdbZIP11 overexpression (OE), and MdbZIP11 RNA interference (RNAi) apple calli by DNA detection. (B) Relative expression of MdbZIP11 in wild type (WT), MdbZIP11‐OE and MdbZIP11‐RNAi apple calli lines. Data are presented as the mean ± SD from three biological replicates. Statistical differences were determined using Student's t tests (***p < 0.001).

Figure S9: Lesion areas of apple leaves overexpressing EV‐OE/RNAi, SyCD1‐OE and SyCD1‐OE/MdZIP11‐RNAi at 24 h post inoculation (hpi). Statistical significance was determined by one‐way ANOVA followed by least significant difference (LSD) post hoc tests for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05). Scale bars: 10 mm. Data represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least 30 independent samples.

Figure S10: MdSnRK2A positively regulates apple resistance to Valsa mali. MdSnRK2A was overexpressed or silenced in apple leaves. (A) The expression level of MdSnRK2A was determined by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR. (B–E) Lesion areas were quantified using ImageJ at 24 h after V. mali inoculation. Data represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least 30 independent samples. Statistical differences were determined by t tests (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Scale bars: 10 mm.

Figure S11: Expression analysis of MdSnRK2A in apple leaves overexpressing SyCD1. The transcript level of MdSnRK2A was determined by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR in apple leaves overexpressing SyCD1. Data represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. Statistical differences were analysed by two‐tailed Student's t tests (ns, not significant, p > 0.05).

Figure S12: Reproducibility of DAP‐seq peaks across technical replicates.

Figure S13: Expression of abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis‐related genes. Relative transcript levels of ABA pathway genes in apple leaves transiently overexpressing MdbZIP11 following fluridone (Flu) treatment at 24 h post‐inoculation (hpi). Statistical significance was determined by one‐way ANOVA followed by LSD multiple comparison tests. Different letters indicate statistically significant differences at p < 0.05.

Figure S14: Bioinformatic analysis of the conserved domains in the MdHAT5 protein. The conserved domains of MdHAT5 were predicted using tools available on the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Figure S15: MdbZIP11 transactivates the promoters of MdHD18, MdCAR2 and MdPRP5 in Nicotiana benthamiana. (A) Yeast one‐hybrid assays confirming that MdbZIP11 binds to the MdHD18, MdCAR2 and MdPRP5 promoter. (B) GUS staining demonstrating that MdbZIP11 induces MdHD18, MdCAR2 and MdPRP5 promoter activity in N. benthamiana. (C) Dual‐luciferase reporter assay (LUC) showing transcriptional activation of the MdHD18, MdCAR2 and MdPRP5 promoter by MdbZIP11.

Figure S16: MdSnRK2A enhances MdbZIP11‐mediated activation of MdHAT5. (A) Luciferase reporter assay performed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves at 60 h post‐agroinfiltration following co‐expression of MdSnRK2A‐mCherry, MdbZIP11‐pGreenII 62‐SK and proMdHAT5‐pGreenII 0800. (B) Quantification of luciferase activity reflecting the transcriptional activation strength of MdbZIP11 in the presence or absence of MdSnRK2A. Data are presented as the mean ± SD of three biological replicates. Statistical significance was determined using Student's t tests (**p < 0.01).

Figure S17: Overexpression of SyCD1 enhances the expression of MdHAT5. (A) The relative expression level of SyCD1 determined by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR). (B) The relative expression level of MdHAT5 in SyCD1‐overexpressing apple tissues determined by RT‐qPCR. Data are presented as the mean ± SD from three biological replicates (n = 30). Statistical differences were determined by t tests, ***p < 0.001. Scale bars: 10 mm.

Figure S18: MdHAT5 positively regulates apple resistance to Valsa mali. MdHAT5 was overexpressed or silenced in apple leaves.
