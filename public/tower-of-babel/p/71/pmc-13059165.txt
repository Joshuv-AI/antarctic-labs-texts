Genomic characteristics and risk factor analysis of carbapenem-non-susceptible Pseudomonas aeruginosa causing bacteremia in a tertiary hospital (2014–2023)
Authors: Junbin Zhai, Xiaoli Cao, Chang Liu, Jie Zheng, Yan Zhang, Shuo Gao, Xuejing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-non-susceptible Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CnSPA) bloodstream infections (BSIs) are associated with high mortality and limited treatment options, yet long-term genomic–clinical data from China are scarce.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 52 non-duplicate CnSPA isolates causing BSIs from 2014 to 2023 in a tertiary hospital in eastern China. Whole-genome sequencing was used to characterize antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), carbapenem resistance–associated mutations, mobile genetic elements, sequence types (STs), serotypes, genetic relationship, and integron structures. Clinical data were integrated to identify risk factors for mortality and polymicrobial BSIs.ResultsCnSPA isolates showed high resistance to imipenem (98.1%) and multiple β-lactams and fluoroquinolones, while remaining susceptible to colistin. Carbapenemase genes were rare, whereas diverse ARGs, frequent oprD and ampC mutations, and widespread co-localization of ARGs with insertion sequences supported a major role for non-carbapenemase mechanisms and mobile genetic elements in resistance dissemination. Thirty-six distinct STs—including high-risk clones ST235, ST244, ST274, and ST357, heterogeneous serotypes and phylogenetic tree indicated substantial genomic diversity. Integron analysis revealed structurally diverse complete integrons, CALINs, and In0 elements in a subset of genomes. Clinically, mortality was 38.5%; prior carbapenem exposure, organ failure, and prolonged mechanical ventilation were independent predictors of death, while long-term catheterization was associated with polymicrobial BSIs.ConclusionsCnSPA BSIs in this setting are driven by multifactorial resistance mechanisms, high-risk clones, and mobile genetic elements, with modifiable clinical factors strongly linked to poor outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04747-9.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) is a widespread opportunistic pathogen responsible for a range of severe infections, particularly in immunocompromised patients [1]. Bloodstream infections (BSIs) caused by this organism are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [2]. In recent years, the increasing prevalence of carbapenem-non-susceptible Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CnSPA) has further complicated treatment regimens and represents a growing global health concern [3].

Carbapenems remain key therapeutic agents for multidrug-resistant gram-negative infections [4]; However, resistance in CnSPA is multifactorial [5]. Reported mechanisms include OprD porin loss, efflux pump overexpression, chromosomal β-lactamase derepression, and the acquisition of carbapenemase genes through mobile genetic elements [6, 7]. These genomic adaptations not only undermine antimicrobial efficacy but also enhance bacterial persistence and transmissibility.

Clinically, several epidemiological studies have identified risk factors for CnSPA infection—such as prolonged hospitalization, prior antibiotic exposure, invasive procedures, and severe comorbidities [8]. Prior carbapenem use is considered a particularly important driver of resistance due to the selective pressure it imposes. However, there remains a notable research gap: comprehensive genomic–clinical correlation studies focusing specifically on CnSPA BSIs are scarce, especially in China, where data on resistance mechanisms linked to patient-level risk factors remain limited.

To address these limitations, we conducted a ten-year investigation of CnSPA BSI isolates in a tertiary hospital, integrating whole-genome sequencing with detailed clinical data. By characterizing genomic features and identifying clinical determinants—including prior carbapenem exposure—this study aims to clarify the resistance landscape of CnSPA BSIs and provide evidence to guide infection control and antimicrobial stewardship strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was a retrospective observational study conducted at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, a tertiary teaching hospital in eastern China. Clinical and microbiological data of patients with CnSPA BSIs were collected from January 2014 to December 2023. The study integrated genomic analyses of CnSPA isolates with clinical data to investigate resistance mechanisms and patient-level risk factors.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Consecutive, non-duplicate bloodstream isolates (n = 52) of CnSPA collected between 2014 and 2023 were included in this study. Only one isolate per patient per BSI episode was analyzed to avoid overrepresentation. Recurrent isolates obtained from the same patient during the same infection episode were excluded, as were isolates with incomplete sequencing data.

Bacterial Isolation, Identification, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Blood cultures were processed using the BD BACTEC™ FX system (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and positive bottles were subcultured onto blood agar plates (Oxoid, UK) and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Colony morphology and Gram staining were assessed for preliminary identification, while species confirmation was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, bioMérieux, France). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was initially conducted using the VITEK 2.0 automated system and subsequently confirmed by reference microbroth dilution according to CLSI M100 (2023) guidelines [9]. Quality control was performed using Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27,853, following CLSI recommendations. CnSPA was defined as any isolate with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) ≥ 4 µg/mL for imipenem and/or meropenem, in accordance with CLSI (2023) criteria [9].

Genomic extraction, whole genome sequencing and genome assembly

Genomic DNA was extracted from overnight cultures of each P. aeruginosa isolate grown on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar at 37 °C for 18 h using the TianGen Bacterial Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), while DNA integrity was evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

For whole genome sequencing, DNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) with paired-end 150 bp reads, ensuring a minimum sequencing depth of 30× coverage per genome. Raw sequencing reads underwent quality assessment with FastQC (v0.11.9) and trimming using Trimmomatic (v0.39). De novo genome assembly was performed using SPAdes (v3.15.4) with default parameters, and contigs were filtered for a minimum length of 500 bp. Assembly quality was assessed using QUAST (v5.0.2), and the final assembled genomes were submitted to GenBank.

Identification of antibiotic resistance genes, sequence types, and mobile genetic elements

Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were identified using ResFinder 4.1(database updated 2023-09-20) (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/ResFinder/) with a threshold of ≥ 90% identity and ≥ 60% coverage. Sequence types (STs) were determined using MLST 2.0 (v2.0.9, https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/MLST/), with allele profiles retrieved from PubMLST.org (accessed April 2024). Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) were identified using Mobile Element Finder (v1.0.3, Database v1.0.2).

Mutation analysis of carbapenem resistance related genes including oprD, ampC, PbpA and PbpC

Genes associated with carbapenem resistance (oprD, ampC, pbpA, and pbpC) were extracted from assembled genomes using a custom Python script and aligned with the reference strain P. aeruginosa PAO1 (NC_002516.2) using MUSCLE (v3.8.1551) [10]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), insertions, deletions, and frameshift mutations were identified using Snippy (v4.6.0), while their functional effects were predicted using SIFT (Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant) and PolyPhen-2 (Polymorphism Phenotyping v2) [11, 12].

Whole-genome sequencing-based serotyping of P. aeruginosa

Whole-genome sequences of the isolates were analyzed using the O-specific antigen (OSA) prediction method described by Thrane et al. [13]. The P. aeruginosa serotyper (PAst) tool was employed to assign serotypes based on genomic data. WGS data were screened for O-antigen biosynthesis gene clusters, and serotype assignment was conducted based on gene content and sequence homology. Additionally, serotype-specific gene markers were identified using a BLAST-based approach with reference sequences from known O-serotypes.

Phylogenomic analysis

The evolutionary relationships among the 52 P. aeruginosa isolates were inferred using a whole-genome SNP–based phylogenomic pipeline. All genomes were first annotated using Prokka v1.14.6 to generate standardized GFF3 files [14]. Orthologous genes were then identified with OrthoFinder v2.5.5, yielding 3,559 single-copy core genes shared by all isolates [15]. Each core gene was aligned and subsequently concatenated into a supermatrix, from which a SNP-only alignment was extracted using snp-sites v2.5.1. The optimal nucleotide substitution model for phylogenetic inference was determined to be GTR + G based on jModelTest2 v2.1.10. A maximum-likelihood phylogeny was reconstructed using RAxML-NG v1.2.1 under the GTR + G + ASC_LEWIS model, with the ASC_LEWIS correction applied to account for ascertainment bias introduced by SNP-only input data [16, 17]. Branch support was assessed with 500 bootstrap replicates, and branches with bootstrap values < 50 were collapsed to improve interpretability. The resulting tree was visualized using standard phylogenetic visualization tools, with relevant isolate metadata mapped onto the final topology.

Integron analysis

Integron-associated elements in all 52 P. aeruginosa genomes were identified using Integron_Finder v2.0.6 (https://tool2-mml.sjtu.edu.cn/ICEberg3/ICEfinder.php) with default parameters to detect complete integrons, CALINs, and In0 elements. Integron_Finder predictions were subsequently visualized using Gcluster, and final figures were edited and refined in Adobe Illustrator.

Patient inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients included in the study were those with laboratory-confirmed CnSPA BSIs and complete clinical data. Only the first bloodstream isolate per patient episode was included; duplicate or recurrent isolates from the same patient were excluded to avoid sampling bias. Patients with incomplete clinical records were also excluded.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records. The collected data included patient demographics (age, gender), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, malignancy, renal failure, open trauma), prior antibiotic use (especially carbapenems), presence of invasive devices (e.g., central venous catheters, mechanical ventilation), Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission, recent surgeries, duration of hospitalization, and clinical outcomes (mortality, infection resolution). Exposure definitions were as follows: prior antibiotic use was defined as any antibiotic use within 90 days before the onset of bacteremia, and invasive devices were defined as the presence of indwelling catheters or mechanical ventilation for ≥ 7 days.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Nanjing University (Approval No. 2023 − 390) and this study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the study.

Definitions

Primary BSI was defined as a BSI with no identifiable infectious focus outside the bloodstream. Secondary BSI referred to a bloodstream infection arising from a confirmed infection at another anatomical site [18]. Mixed infection was defined as the detection of CnSPA concurrently with other pathogens in blood cultures or in other normally sterile sites.

Statistical analysis

Risk factor analysis was performed exclusively within the CnSPA BSI cohort to identify predictors of adverse clinical outcomes rather than determinants of resistance acquisition. All statistical analyses were conducted within the CnSPA BSIs cohort, as no external control group was included. Risk factor analyses were performed using internal comparator groups, including mortality versus survival, monomicrobial versus polymicrobial infections, and primary versus secondary BSI. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, while continuous variables were analyzed using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate logistic regression models to identify independent risk factors. Multicollinearity among variables was assessed prior to multivariable regression using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and highly correlated variables were excluded. For missing data handling, variables with more than 10% missing values were excluded from regression analyses. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Prognostic analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank tests, and independent predictors of mortality were further assessed using Cox proportional hazards models, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs calculated. A total of 52 CnSPA-BSI patients meeting the inclusion criteria were included in all statistical analyses.

Results

Antimicrobial resistance profiles of 52 CnSPA isolates

Overall, the CnSPA isolates exhibited high levels of drug resistance. Imipenem showed the highest resistance rate, followed by fluoroquinolones. Moderate resistance was observed to several β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations and meropenem. Notably, all isolates remained susceptible to colistin. Distribution of resistance genes and limited carbapenemase presence

Totally, 30 kinds of ARGs were identified, with blaPAO, blaOXA, aph(3’), catB7, and fosA being the most prevalent (Fig. 1). Among these, blaPAO is a recently described class D β-lactamase gene intrinsic to P. aeruginosa. Although its functional characteristics are not yet fully defined, available evidence suggests that blaPAO contributes to baseline β-lactam resistance and may modulate carbapenem susceptibility when acting in concert with other resistance mechanisms, such as porin loss or efflux pump overexpression [19]. In detail, among the 9 β-lactamase, three carbapenemase encoding genes including blaKPC−2, blaVIM−2 and blaPOM−1. To avoid overgeneralization, all blaOXA alleles detected in this study are listed individually as follows: blaOXA−10, blaOXA−14, blaOXA−246, blaOXA−395, blaOXA−396, blaOXA−486, blaOXA−488, blaOXA−494 and blaOXA−50. As noted in previous studies, many of these blaOXA alleles—especially those belonging to the blaOXA−10 family—are narrow-spectrum β-lactamases and do not confer carbapenem resistance (Table 1). It is important to note that blaOXA−50 is an intrinsic gene universally present in P. aeruginosa, and its detection is expected in all isolates. The presence of blaOXA−50 in most isolates is consistent with this intrinsic genetic feature. However, a small number of isolates did not show a positive result for blaOXA−50. We believe that this is most likely due to technical limitations in short-read sequencing and genome assembly, which can lead to issues such as contig fragmentation, incomplete regional coverage, or failure in automated annotation, which may not fully capture partially assembled sequences [20, 21]. These negative results are thus considered assembly artefacts rather than a true absence of the gene. Therefore, it is assumed that all isolates in our study carry blaOXA−50 as part of the intrinsic genetic makeup of P. aeruginosa.

Furthermore, isolates recovered during the early study period (2014–2015) predominantly carried intrinsic or intrinsic-like resistance genes, including fosA, blaPAO, aph(3′)-IIb, and catB7, and were mainly classified as non-MDR or MDR. From 2016 onward, a progressive accumulation of acquired resistance determinants was observed, particularly integron-associated genes (aadA, dfrA, sul1, and qacE), aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, and quinolone resistance–related genes (crpP and aac(6′)-Ib-cr). Notably, high-risk carbapenemase genes, such as blaVIM−2 and blaKPC−2, together with the 16 S rRNA methylase rmtB, emerged in later years and were predominantly associated with XDR phenotypes (Figure S1). Collectively, the clear increasing trend over time indicates progressive accumulation and diversification of ARGs, particularly in later years.

Mutation patterns associated with carbapenem non-susceptibility

Among the 52 CnSPA isolates, no mutations were detected in the oprD gene of three isolates, while one isolate exhibited complete nonsense mutations in the oprD gene. The remaining 48 isolates showed varying degrees of amino acid changes caused by oprD gene mutations (Fig. 2). Additionally, mutations in the ampC gene led to amino acid alterations in 46 isolates (Fig. 3), and mutations in the pbpC gene caused amino acid changes in 43 isolates (Fig. 4). No mutations were detected in the pbpA gene among the 52 CnSPA isolates. Importantly, isolates with disruptive oprD or ampC mutations generally showed higher imipenem/meropenem MICs, demonstrating concordance between genotypic and phenotypic resistance patterns.

Co-localization of ARGs with insertion sequences

Insertional sequences (ISs) are a class of transposons capable of encoding enzymes required for transposition, with short reverse terminal repeats at both ends. During transposition, the insertion sequence copies the target sequence and forms two short forward repeats on either side of the transposon. In this study, 50 of 52 P. aeruginosa strains showed co-localization of resistance genes and IS on the same contig, strongly suggesting the involvement of mobile genetic elements in facilitating horizontal gene transfer (HGT) (Fig. 5). Several transposons—including Tn5563, Tn5501, Tn4371, and Tn6082—were also identified, further supporting genomic mobility as a contributing factor in shaping the resistome.

High genetic diversity and predominant high-risk clones

Totally, 36 distinct STs were identified with ST235, ST244, ST274 and ST357 being the most frequent (Fig. 6). These STs are internationally recognized high-risk lineages and corresponded to broader resistance profiles within our cohort. ST1971, ST606, and ST494 were less common. Other 29 STs were single. In addition, the ST type of 5 strains has not yet been determined (Table 1). A temporal review from 2014 to 2023 did not reveal clear year-specific clustering of sequence types, likely due to the limited number of isolates collected each year.

Clonal complex (CC) structure identified by eBURST analysis

The eBURST analysis of the 52 P. aeruginosa isolates revealed a highly heterogeneous clonal structure, with a variety of clonal complexes (cc1 to cc38) being identified (Fig. 6). This extensive diversity is consistent with the large allelic variation observed across MLST and underscores the non-clonal, recombinogenic population structure of P. aeruginosa. Furthermore, certain complexes, such as, cc6, cc10, cc22 and cc24, were prevalent, suggesting they may be dominant or highly adaptive lineages within this population. Other clonal complexes, including cc1, cc2, and cc3, were less frequent, indicating a more diverse distribution of lineages.

Serotype distribution

Serotyping demonstrated substantial diversity (Table 1) (Fig. 6), with O11 being the most common. Notably, O11 is associated with several globally disseminated high-risk clones [22, 23], aligning with our detection of ST235 and ST244 in this serotype background. O6, O1, and O3 were relatively less (Fig. 5). Other detected serotypes exhibited varying degrees of prevalence (Table 1).

Phylogenetic diversity of P. aeruginosa isolates

Using the core-genome phylogenetic tree of the 52 isolates, we observed an overall genetically diverse population structure with long branches and dispersed placement of many isolates (Fig. 6), indicating that the collection was not dominated by a single outbreak lineage. Nevertheless, several tight phylogenetic clusters with short internal branches were apparent and were concordant with MLST/eBURST-defined clonal complexes (CCs), supporting the presence of limited clonal expansion within an otherwise heterogeneous background. In particular, multiple isolates grouped within CC10 (ST274; O3), CC13 (ST494; O3), CC22 (ST235; O11), CC24 (ST357; O11), CC26 (ST1971; O11), and CC28 (ST606; O13), each forming coherent clades on the phylogeny. Mapping O-antigen types onto the tree further revealed a strong ST/CC–serotype association, with highly consistent O types within individual STs (e.g., ST235–O11, ST357–O11, ST274–O3, ST494–O3, and ST606–O13), while some serotypes (notably O11) occurred across multiple distinct genetic backgrounds, suggesting broader dissemination beyond a single clone. In contrast, the distribution of ARGs did not fully track with core-genome relatedness, and isolates displayed variable ARG burdens even within closely related clades. This pattern, together with the frequent detection of ISs and the sporadic presence of transposons, is consistent with an important contribution of mobile genetic elements and horizontal gene transfer to the observed resistance profiles in addition to clonal spread.

Diversity and structural organization of Integron-Associated elements in P. aeruginosa genomes

Integron analysis of all 52 P. aeruginosa genomes revealed a total of 21 integron-associated elements distributed across 10 genomes. These included 5 complete integrons, 11 CALIN (Clusters of attC sites Lacking Integrase) elements, and 5 In0 elements. For visualization, only integrons containing two or more structural components were displayed (Fig. 7), resulting in the exclusion of four In0-type elements, which consisted solely of an integrase gene and lacked attC sites. Complete integrons were characterized by the presence of an integron integrase (IntI) located adjacent to one or more attC recombination sites, while CALIN elements contained multiple attC sites but lacked a nearby integrase gene. In contrast, In0 elements comprised only an integrase gene without any associated attC sites. The overall distribution of these elements across multiple contigs and isolates highlights the genomic diversity and structural variability of integron-associated mobile genetic elements within the P. aeruginosa population.

Association between molecular typing and clinical outcomes

Overall clinical outcomes varied among patients infected with different STs of P. aeruginosa. Unfavorable outcomes, including in-hospital death or terminal self-discharge, were predominantly observed among patients infected with several high-risk or multidrug-resistant lineages, particularly ST357, ST235, ST1971, ST274, and ST3382. These isolates commonly exhibited extensive antimicrobial resistance phenotypes and carried multiple resistance determinants, including aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, β-lactamase genes, and fluoroquinolone resistance–associated genes. In contrast, clinical improvement or cure was more frequently observed in patients infected with isolates belonging to ST1182, ST244, ST316, ST379, ST260, ST494, ST699, and ST277, despite the presence of carbapenem nonsusceptibility in several of these strains. Notably, isolates carrying major carbapenem resistance determinants, such as blaVIM−2 (ST313) and blaKPC−2 (ST463), were identified in patients with severe disease courses. Taken together, the distribution of clinical outcomes differed across molecular lineages.

Clinical characteristics of patients with CnSPA BSIs

Among the 52 patients, the patient’s ages ranged from 30 to 103 years, with a median age of 67 years. Male patients accounted for 69.2% (36 cases), with most being admitted to the intensive care unit (21.2%) or the geriatric department (17.3%). A majority of patients (92.3%, 48 cases) had hospital stays exceeding 10 days, and the overall prognosis was poor, with 38.5% (20 cases) dying during hospitalization. Comorbidities were common, as 76.9% (40 cases) of the patients had three or more underlying conditions, with hypertension being the most frequent (60.0%), followed by organ failure (45.0%), malignancy (37.5%), diabetes (30.0%), and open trauma (22.5%). Among the 32 patients with secondary BSIs, over half (53.1%) originated from the lungs, while other infection sources included the urinary tract (12.5%), abdominal cavity (12.5%), biliary tract (9.4%), skin (9.4%), and catheters (3.1%). More than 30 patients underwent invasive procedures lasting over seven days, with intravenous catheterization being the most common (59.6%, 31 cases).

Risk factors for mortality and polymicrobial infection

Multivariate Logistic regression analysis showed that history of carbapenem antibiotic exposure (≤ 90 days), organ failure, and mechanical ventilation (≥ 7 days) were independent risk factors for death in CnSPA-BSIs patients (Table 2). Meanwhile, an indwelling catheter (≥ 7 days) was identified as an independent risk factor for BSIs with CnSPA combined with other pathogens (Table 3). No independent risk factors for secondary CnSPA BSIs were found in this study (Table 4).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first decade-long genomic–clinical characterization of CnSPA BSIs in a tertiary hospital in China. By integrating whole-genome sequencing with detailed clinical data, this study provides a comprehensive overview of resistance mechanisms, molecular epidemiology, and patient-level risk factors associated with CnSPA BSIs in high-risk hospitalized populations.

The findings of this study reveal a concerning level of resistance among CnSPA isolates, with widespread β-lactam, fluoroquinolone, and aminoglycoside resistance, as well as the presence of MDR and XDR phenotypes. These resistance trends highlight the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies, rigorous infection control, and global surveillance efforts to curb the spread of CnSPA. Correspondingly, the identification of multiple resistance genes may provide explanation for the resistance.

Of note, only three carbapenemase-encoding genes were identified in our collection, each detected in a single isolate. This observation aligns with previous studies showing that carbapenemase production is not the predominant mechanism of carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa [24, 25]. Consistent with this, other β-lactamase genes were detected only at very low frequencies, further supporting the conclusion that β-lactamase production is not the major driver of carbapenem resistance in this cohort. Although a wide range of blaOXA alleles were identified in our isolates, it is important to emphasize that not all blaOXA variants possess carbapenemase activity. In particular, blaOXA-10 and its related alleles are well-characterized narrow-spectrum class D β-lactamases that hydrolyze penicillins and some narrow-spectrum cephalosporins but have minimal or no ability to degrade carbapenems. Therefore, the presence of blaOXA-10–like genes in our isolates should not be interpreted as a mechanism of carbapenem resistance. Their detection primarily reflects the natural diversity of class D β-lactamases within P. aeruginosa, rather than clinically significant carbapenemase production. In our dataset, the blaOXA family exhibited the greatest allelic diversity, and its widespread presence may reflect evolutionary adaptation to selective pressure from β-lactam antibiotics. The occasional failure to detect blaOXA-50—an intrinsic gene in P. aeruginosa—is most likely attributable to Illumina short-read sequencing artefacts, such as fragmented contigs or incomplete assembly, rather than genuine biological absence. While most blaOXA variants are not directly associated with carbapenem resistance, certain alleles such as blaOXA-50 have been linked to the species’ intrinsic β-lactam resistance profile [26]. Taken together, the limited detection of carbapenemase-encoding genes in CnSPA suggests that non-carbapenemase mechanisms—including efflux pump overexpression, porin mutations, and β-lactamase hyperproduction—likely play a dominant role in mediating carbapenem resistance. In addition, the blaPAO gene, which was detected in the majority of isolates, has recently been described as a class D β-lactamase intrinsic to P. aeruginosa. Although its enzymatic properties and functional contribution to resistance remain under investigation, current evidence indicates that blaPAO may participate in the species’ baseline β-lactam resistance. Given the limited literature available, its clinical significance remains unclear, but its high prevalence in our cohort highlights the need for further functional and epidemiological studies. Most importantly, the observed temporal pattern highlights the dynamic evolution of antimicrobial resistance in P. aeruginosa under sustained hospital selective pressure. Rather than remaining static, resistance gene repertoires progressively expanded and diversified over time, reflecting an increasing contribution of horizontally acquired determinants and mobile genetic elements. This evolutionary trajectory emphasizes the adaptability of P. aeruginosa and underscores the importance of continuous genomic surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship to limit the emergence and spread of highly resistant strains in clinical settings.

The identified mutations in the oprD, ampC, pbpC, and pbpA genes highlight the multifactorial nature of carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa. OprD porin loss is a major contributor to carbapenem non-susceptibility, as it reduces outer membrane permeability, impairing carbapenem uptake and enhancing bacterial survival under sustained β-lactam exposure in hospital settings or chronic infections [27]. AmpC overexpression, driven by mutations like T105A, provides a fitness advantage in environments with frequent β-lactam use, such as ICUs, by conferring resistance to cephalosporins [28]. Mutations in pbpC, particularly A104P, modify penicillin-binding protein function, contributing to β-lactam tolerance by altering cell wall remodeling. Interestingly, pbpA mutations were not significantly observed, suggesting its limited role in this cohort. Together, these findings demonstrate how porin loss, β-lactamase overproduction, and penicillin-binding protein mutations work synergistically to sustain carbapenem resistance. The selection pressures from prolonged carbapenem use, combined with horizontal gene transfer facilitated by insertion sequences, likely promote the emergence and persistence of resistant strains [29].

The identification of 36 distinct STs among the 52 CnSPA isolates underscores the high genetic diversity of these strains. The most prevalent STs-ST235, ST244, ST274, and ST357 are globally recognized MDR clones frequently implicated in hospital outbreaks [30]. Among them, ST235, in particular, has been reported worldwide, often carrying blaVIM and blaKPC [2]. ST235 is recognized as one of the most globally disseminated high-risk clones of P. aeruginosa, frequently associated with MDR and intercontinental spread. In our study, although ST235 was detected, the demographic review of affected patients revealed no history of international travel, foreign origin, or long-distance hospital transfer. These findings suggest that the ST235 isolates identified here likely represent strains circulating locally rather than imported through international transmission routes. Nevertheless, the detection of such internationally prevalent clones highlights the importance of continued genomic surveillance to monitor potential cross-border dissemination and to better understand regional transmission dynamics. ST244 is another well-documented high-risk clone associated with nosocomial infections and diverse resistance mechanisms [31]. Beyond these dominant STs, our study identified 29 unique STs, each represented by a single isolate, along with five undetermined STs. These rare STs may represent local or emerging clones with limited dissemination. Their presence highlights the continuous genetic evolution of P. aeruginosa, driven by mutation accumulation, recombination, and horizontal gene transfer (HGT) [32]. In comparison with previous epidemiological studies, ST235 and ST244 have been widely reported across regions including China, Europe, and North America [33], our data did not indicate that the patients infected with ST235 and ST244 had a history of inter-hospital transfer, international travel, or other relevant epidemiological factors. These findings suggest that these clones may be circulating locally within the hospital or community. while ST1971, ST606, and ST494 have been detected sporadically, suggesting a possible regional expansion of these clones [31]. The high frequency of unique STs may indicate local evolution within our hospital setting, possibly due to antibiotic selection pressure and adaptive responses to environmental conditions. Further analysis on the eBURST-derived CC structure highlights the broad population diversity, limited clonal expansion, and possible parallel evolutionary trajectories of P. aeruginosa circulating in this hospital setting. The presence of multiple CCs represented by single isolates further suggests that many infections may originate from transient, unrelated environmental sources rather than sustained transmission of dominant clones.

Phylogenetic and integron analyses further highlight the genomic plasticity of CnSPA isolates in our cohort. Whole-genome SNP–based phylogenetic reconstruction revealed substantial genetic diversity among the 52 isolates, with clear clustering by ST, but with serotypes widely dispersed across different clades. This diversity reflects the well-recognized evolutionary adaptability of P. aeruginosa and suggests ongoing recombination and microevolution within the hospital environment. Consistent with this, mobile genetic elements—including IS and transposons—were detected throughout the phylogenetic tree, indicating that HGT likely contributes to the dissemination of ARGs across genetically distinct lineages. Furthermore, this interplay between conserved and variable serotype–ST associations reflect the underlying genomic plasticity of P. aeruginosa and suggests that both clonal evolution and horizontal gene transfer contribute to shaping its O-antigen diversity.

Complementing the phylogenetic findings, integron analysis demonstrated additional structural diversity among mobile resistance-associated elements. Although complete integrons were relatively uncommon, the presence of numerous CALIN elements and integrase-only In0 structures indicates that integron remnants are widespread and may serve as genomic hotspots for future cassette acquisition or recombination events. The coexistence of diverse integron configurations—ranging from intact integrase–attC units to fragmented or incomplete structures—highlights the dynamic nature of the P. aeruginosa resistome and supports the notion that integron-associated loci continue to evolve under selective pressure. Taken together, these findings suggest that both lineage-level genomic diversity and structural variability in integron-associated elements play important roles in shaping the resistance landscape of CnSPA.

ISs are key genetic elements promoting HGT, enabling the movement of resistance genes between plasmids, transposons, and chromosomes [33, 34]. In our study, 50 of 52 CnSPA isolates exhibited a high co-location rate (96%) between IS elements and ARGs, suggesting active gene mobilization that facilitates the rapid acquisition and dissemination of resistance determinants within P. aeruginosa populations. This finding aligns with previous studies highlighting the role of IS-mediated recombination in the dissemination of carbapenemase genes like blaVIM, blaIMP, and blaNDM [35]. The strong association between IS elements and β-lactamase genes (blaOXA, blaPAO, blaCARB) suggests that transposition events contribute to increased resistance gene mobility. Additionally, IS elements can activate adjacent resistance genes by introducing strong promoters, leading to increased gene expression and enhanced resistance phenotypes [36].

The observed 96% IS–ARG co-localization rate suggests a potential genomic context that may facilitate inter- and intra-species gene transfer, particularly in ICU settings characterized by high antibiotic pressure and frequent bacterial co-colonization [37]. Importantly, this co-localization should be interpreted as indirect or putative evidence of horizontal gene transfer (HGT) potential, rather than direct proof of active mobilization or transmission. Nonetheless, these findings support the role of P. aeruginosa as a potential reservoir of resistance genes, in which IS-associated genetic architectures may increase the likelihood of resistance gene dissemination under selective pressure. To mitigate the risk posed by such genomic configurations, enhanced infection control strategies, together with genomic surveillance, are warranted to monitor the emergence and spread of IS-associated resistance determinants.

Among the identified serotypes, O11 was the most frequently detected, which is consistent with previous studies indicating that O11 is one of the most predominant serotypes in hospital-acquired P. aeruginosa infections [38]. This serotype is often associated with MDR, biofilm formation, and chronic infections, making it a major concern in nosocomial settings [39]. Similarly, O6, O1, and O3 serotypes were also frequently identified, reflecting their significant presence in clinical infections [40]. Previous reports have suggested that O6 is often linked to antibiotic resistance and increased virulence, while O1 and O3 have been associated with both acute and chronic infections [41]. The relatively high prevalence of these serotypes suggests potential clonal expansion and highlights the need for continuous monitoring to track their epidemiological trends. The detection of multiple O-serotypes in this study suggests that CnSPA exhibits a high degree of serotypic variability, which may contribute to immune evasion, antimicrobial resistance, and adaptation to different host environments. This diversity could be driven by HGT, genetic recombination, or selective pressure from antibiotic use and host immune responses [42].

The observed association between molecular lineages and clinical outcomes suggests that the genetic background of P. aeruginosa may influence disease severity and prognosis. High-risk and hospital-adapted clones are known to accumulate diverse resistance determinants under sustained antimicrobial pressure, which can restrict therapeutic options and complicate infection management [43]. Conversely, heterogeneous outcomes among carbapenem-non-susceptible isolates indicate that antimicrobial resistance alone is insufficient to explain clinical severity, and that additional factors such as bacterial fitness, virulence potential, and host condition are likely to modulate disease progression [44]. The presence of major carbapenem resistance mechanisms may further exacerbate clinical management by delaying effective therapy. Together, these findings highlight the clinical relevance of integrating molecular epidemiology with outcome data to better understand the prognostic implications of P. aeruginosa infections.

In addition, our study predominantly consisted of elderly patients, reflecting the vulnerability of older adults to P. aeruginosa infections due to age-related immune decline, multiple comorbidities, and frequent hospital exposures [45]. The predominance of a male was observed, potentially linked to biological or healthcare utilization differences. Most patients experienced extended hospital stays exceeding 10 days, indicating that prolonged hospitalization is both a risk factor for and a consequence of CnSPA infections. Admission to the ICU underscores the association of these infections with critically ill and vulnerable populations [46]. The overall poor prognosis with a high mortality rate during hospitalization highlights the clinical severity of CnSPA infections, which may be often compounded by delays in effective treatment due to MDR The common comorbidities likely increased susceptibility to infection. The high frequency of secondary BSI highlighted the opportunistic nature of CnSPA in exploiting multiple entry points [47]. The common invasive procedures lasting over seven days emphasized medical devices as significant risk factors and reservoirs for nosocomial infections [48].

Furthermore, the independent risk factors identified in our study align with previous study. As it’s known, mechanical ventilation has been widely reported as a major risk factor for P. aeruginosa BSIs due to its association with ventilator-associated pneumonia and nosocomial sepsis [49]. Shi Q et al. and Yuan Q et al. also identified prolonged mechanical ventilation as a key predictor of mortality in MDR P. aeruginosa BSI patients [39, 50]. The underlying mechanisms include immune suppression from critical illness, microbial aspiration, and biofilm formation in ventilator circuits, all of which increase infection risk. Similarly, organ failure has been consistently linked to poor outcomes in bacterial sepsis. Dong L et al. demonstrated that sepsis-induced multi-organ dysfunction significantly increases mortality in P. aeruginosa infections [51]. Importantly, our study further identified that prior exposure to carbapenem antibiotics (within 90 days) was an independent risk factor for mortality in patients with CnSPA BSIs. This finding directly supports the hypothesis raised in our introduction and corroborates prior studies indicating that previous carbapenem use exerts strong selective pressure, promoting the proliferation of resistant P. aeruginosa strains [52, 53]. In clinical practice, prior exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics like carbapenems not only disrupts the normal flora but also facilitates the selection and expansion of resistant clones, thereby increasing the likelihood of adverse clinical outcomes [45, 50]. The inclusion of this variable in our multivariate analysis reinforces its significance and provides actionable evidence for antimicrobial stewardship programs aimed at minimizing unnecessary carbapenem use to reduce mortality risk. Moreover, in severe infections caused by P. aeruginosa, several clinical guidelines and studies have recommended combination therapy—typically involving two antipseudomonal agents—to enhance therapeutic efficacy and improve outcomes, particularly in high-risk or critically ill patients.

The association of long-term catheterization with polymicrobial BSIs supports the notion that indwelling devices serve as bacterial reservoirs, facilitating biofilm formation and persistent colonization. This finding aligns with Buetti N et al. [54], who found that prolonged catheterization significantly increases the risk of catheterization related BSIs, particularly in ICU patients. Interestingly, our study did not identify independent risk factors for secondary CnSPA BSIs, which contrasts with previous reports suggesting that prior colonization, immunosuppression, and prolonged antibiotic exposure may play key roles [47]. This discrepancy may be due to differences in study design, sample size, or patient demographics, necessitating larger multi-center investigations to further explore secondary infection risk factors. Given these findings, early identification of high-risk patients and aggressive critical care management are crucial. Implementing ventilator stewardship programs, preventing and managing organ failure at an early stage, and adopting strict catheter management protocols can significantly reduce infection risks and improve patient outcomes.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it was conducted in a single tertiary hospital with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings or regions. Second, our molecular analyses focused primarily on selected ARGs and known resistance-associated mutations; therefore, other genetic mechanisms—such as overexpression of efflux pumps or regulatory pathway alterations—were not systematically explored. Third, genomic analyses were based exclusively on Illumina short-read sequencing. While this approach is robust for identifying ARGs and point mutations, it limits the accurate reconstruction of complex genomic structures. In particular, plasmids, integrons, large genomic islands, and repetitive regions cannot be fully resolved, preventing definitive determination of gene localization, cassette organization, and mobile element architecture. Consequently, associations between ARGs and mobile genetic elements should be interpreted as indirect or putative, rather than conclusive evidence of horizontal gene transfer. In addition, potential recombination events and large-scale structural variations may have been underdetected. Future studies incorporating long-read or hybrid sequencing platforms (e.g., Oxford Nanopore or PacBio) will allow complete genome assembly and enable more definitive inference of genomic context, recombination, and horizontal gene transfer dynamics in CnSPA.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the genomic diversity, resistance mechanisms, and clinical risk factors associated with CnSPA BSIs. We found that carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa is driven primarily by oprD, ampC, and pbpC mutations rather than carbapenemase production. The high genetic diversity and widespread presence of IS elements facilitating horizontal gene transfer highlight the urgent need for enhanced genomic surveillance and monitoring of P. aeruginosa in hospital settings. Clinically, the study emphasizes the importance of targeted antimicrobial stewardship to reduce unnecessary carbapenem use, a key risk factor for resistance development. It also identifies prolonged mechanical ventilation, prior carbapenem exposure (≤ 90 days), and organ failure as independent risk factors for mortality, underscoring the need for early identification of high-risk patients and personalized interventions to improve outcomes. Future research should leverage long-read sequencing technologies (e.g., Oxford Nanopore or PacBio) to gain deeper insights into the genomic architecture of P. aeruginosa, particularly regarding mobile genetic elements and the dynamics of resistance determinants. Furthermore, investigating the mechanisms behind serotype variability and the role of integrons in resistance gene acquisition should be prioritized to better understand the pathogen’s evolution and its adaptation to selective pressures in hospital environments.
