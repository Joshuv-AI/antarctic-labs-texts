Cerebrovascular reactivity in migraine: a breath-hold BOLD fMRI study
Authors: Adam S. Sprouse Blum, Richard Watts, Scott G. Hipko, Jay V. Gonyea, Anna E. Senft-Miller, Olivia K. Nop, Julie A. Dumas, Robert E. Shapiro, Benjamin Littenberg, Bruno P. Soares, Lauren A. DaSilva, James B. Allison, Josh E. Furlonge, Jiming Zhang

ABSTRACT

Background and objectives

Migraine as a neurovascular disorder

Migraine is currently understood as a neurovascular disorder [1], acknowledging that changes exist in both systems during attacks. How these systems interact is slowly being elucidated [2]. In the present manuscript, we assess the potential role of cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) in migraine.

What is cerebrovascular reactivity?

Cerebrovascular reactivity is the hemodynamic response of cerebral blood vessels to a vasoactive stimulus. It represents a key measure of vascular reserve and the system’s ability to respond to challenge [3–6]. Common vasoactive stimuli include inhalation of CO2 gas and breath-holding. Either stimulus increases arterial CO2 partial pressure causing endothelial cells to produce nitric oxide leading to vasodilation and increased blood flow [7]. High CVR is often associated with cerebrovascular fitness, though this relationship has been debated [8], whereas low CVR is commonly associated with pathology, including small vessel disease and the presence of white matter hyperintensities [4, 9–11].

Doppler ultrasound has historically been used to measure CVR. A key limitation of Doppler is its low spatial resolution [4, 8, 12], such that assessments of CVR are limited to blood flow changes in large cerebral arteries. More recently, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has been used to assess CVR at higher spatial resolution by measuring changes in blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals in veins and venules in response to a vasoactive stimulus which, when coupled with an anatomical scan, permits assessment of specific brain structures [8,12].

What is known about CVR in migraine?

A systematic review with meta-analysis of studies assessing CVR in participants with migraine interictally versus controls found decreased CVR in the posterior circulation, as measured by Doppler ultrasound, with no differences detected in the anterior circulation. These findings were most evident in participants with episodic migraine and those without aura [7]. Several studies have measured CVR during both the interictal and ictal phases of migraine using Doppler ultrasound. During the ictal phase, one study demonstrated decreased CVR in the bilateral anterior, middle, and posterior cerebral arteries; [13] another showed decreased CVR in the middle cerebral arteries during systole but not diastole; [14] and two studies found no change in CVR [15, 16].

Two studies have applied fMRI to investigate CVR in migraine [17, 18]. In both, CVR was assessed interictally relative to controls without migraine, using inhaled CO2 as the vasoactive stimulus. Neither study found differences in CVR in gray [18] or white [17, 18] matter. To date, no studies have used fMRI to measure CVR ictally. In this study, we measured CVR in participants with migraine during spontaneous attacks, then repeated the scan between attacks when participants were free of all migraine symptoms and compared the two states. This approach enables characterization of dynamic alterations in cerebrovascular function that have not been previously described. We hypothesized that we would identify brain regions with altered CVR ictally, and that these regions would differ between migraine with left- versus right-sided headache. Collectively, these findings address a gap in the existing literature and advance our understanding of vascular physiology in migraine.

Methods

Study design and participant selection

In this observational cohort study, we enrolled adults with migraine between May 2019 and July 2022. Eligible participants met International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) diagnostic criteria [19] for migraine (with or without aura), had experienced migraine for at least one year, did not have daily headache, experienced at least some attacks with unilateral headache, were 18 years of age or older, were eligible for MRI, and were not pregnant. Participants were recruited from patients under care at the academic tertiary headache center at the University of Vermont (UVM) Medical Center and from the public through email lists, online advertisements, and campus postings. All participants provided informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was approved by the UVM Institutional Review Board.

Experimental protocol

Participants were asked to contact the study team at the onset of a migraine attack. Eligible attacks were those in which head pain was restricted to one side of the head, was at least moderately severe on a three-point scale (mild, moderate, severe), and had not been treated with acute pain medications. Participants with eligible attacks came to the UVM MRI Research Center, where attack eligibility was confirmed before undergoing breath-hold fMRI. Participants were also asked whether they experienced migraine aura with the current attack. At the conclusion of the scan, participants were permitted to take their usual acute pain medications.

The breath-hold technique involves a series of breath-holds that raise arterial CO2, resulting in cerebral vasodilation [20]. CVR is inferred by measuring the change in BOLD signal in response to CO2 retention and hypercapnia. All participants who completed an ictal scan were scheduled for a second, interictal scan at a later date. There was no minimum time required between the ictal and interictal scans; however, participants had to be free of all migraine symptoms (e.g., head pain, photophobia, phonophobia, nausea) at the time of the interictal scan. The interictal scan procedures were identical to those used for the ictal scan.

Image acquisition

All scans were acquired on the same Philips 3T Achieva dStream scanner (Best, Netherlands) using a 32-channel head coil. Each participant completed both a 3D sagittal high-resolution (0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 mm3) GRE T1-weighted structural scan (TR/TE/flip angle = 8.8 ms/4.0 ms/8°; inversion time = 950.0 ms) and a breath-hold functional MRI scan (33 slices with 0.4 mm gap; in-plane resolution = 3.75 × 3.75 mm2; slice thickness = 4.0 mm; single-shot EPI gradient echo; axial orientation; TR/TE/flip angle = 2000.0 ms/35.0 ms/90°; fat saturation; 144 volumes). Earplugs and Avotec over-ear headphones (Stuart, FL, USA) were used to reduce acoustic noise during scanning. The headphones also served to limit head motion inside the coil by creating a snug fit. Participants were instructed to remain as still as possible during scanning. A video was displayed on a screen visible to the participant from within the scanner. The video presented a series of breathing instructions in black text on a white background. The instructions guided participants through 6 cycles of breath-holding and recovery. Each cycle consisted of paced breathing (alternating breathing in and out for 3 seconds each) for 18 s, end-expiration breath-holding for 15 s, and recovery (spontaneous breathing with no instructions) for 15 s [20]. Each scan session took under five minutes to complete. The scan session was repeated three times per participant on both the ictal and interictal scanning days.

Image processing and analysis

DICOM files were converted to NIfTI format and organized into brain imaging data structure (BIDS) [21] using HeuDiConv [22]. Preprocessing with fMRIPrep [23] included realignment of functional runs to correct for head motion, co-registration to the high-resolution T1-weighted structural scan, and spatial normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template.

Analyses were completed using the FSL fMRI Expert Analysis Tool (FEAT) [24, 25]. For the first-level analyses, changes in BOLD activity during breath-holding were modeled using a boxcar regressor with an onset of 8 s after the initiation of each breath-hold to account for the lag to hypercapnia, and a duration of 15 s [20]. The default double-gamma hemodynamic response function and temporal filtering were applied. For the second-level analyses, each participant’s three breath-hold runs were combined. For the third-level analyses, the mean change in breath-hold BOLD signal during the ictal versus interictal state was assessed using a general linear model with FSL’s FLAME1 + 2 method, run as a paired analysis with the interictal scan serving as a per-participant control.

Multiple comparisons were addressed using the Analysis of Functional NeuroImages (AFNI) 3dClustSim program [26] to determine the minimum number of contiguous voxels required for a cluster to be considered statistically significant. Spatial smoothness was estimated using 3dFWHMx with the mixed Gaussian–exponential autocorrelation function (ACF) model. The group-averaged ACF parameters (0.76, 4.11, 14.62), derived from all participants, were used as inputs to 3dClustSim to determine the minimum cluster size required for statistical significance. Statistical thresholds were set at a voxel-wise two-sided p < 0.005 (corresponding to a voxel-level z-statistic threshold of 2.81) and a cluster-level α < 0.05. A whole-brain mask was applied. Brain regions were identified using the Harvard-Oxford, cerebellar, and Johns Hopkins University white matter atlases included with FSL.

Results

Nineteen participants completed the study, of whom one with right-sided headache was excluded due to poor image quality. Of the remaining 18 participants, 14 (77.8%) were female, with a mean age of 38.4 ± 13.6 years (range 20.2–60.7 years). Sixteen participants were right-handed, one was left-handed, and one was ambidextrous.

Headache severity immediately prior to the ictal scan was rated as moderate by 14 participants, moderate-to-severe by 2, and severe by 2. Twelve participants were scanned during left-sided headache and 6 during right-sided headache. Five participants reported migraine visual aura (four with left-sided and one with right-sided headache). Four participants were scanned within 4 h of headache onset, seven within 8 h, two within 12 h, four within 24 h, and one within 36 h. Participant characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

The breath-hold paradigm generated a robust BOLD response throughout the brain. Signal dropout was noted in the inferior temporal and inferior frontal lobes, which is common with echo-planar imaging in these regions due to their proximity to air/bone interfaces and associated differences in magnetic susceptibility. Using 3dClustSim with the parameters described above, we determined that a minimum cluster size of 131 contiguous voxels was required for statistical significance.

When comparing the two scans in the full cohort of 18 participants, several brain regions exhibited alterations in CVR (Fig. 1); however, none of these clusters met significance criteria. The largest cluster, comprising 126 voxels in the right splenium/body of the corpus callosum, demonstrated increased CVR. When the cohort was stratified by headache laterality, two voxel clusters – one with decreased and one with increased CVR – met significance criteria in the 6 participants with right-sided headache. The cluster demonstrating decreased CVR was in the left planum temporale/left Heschl’s gyrus. The cluster demonstrating increased CVR was in the right crus I of the cerebellum. No clusters met significance criteria in the 12 participants with left-sided headache, with the largest cluster comprising 28 voxels. Further details are provided in Table 2. Individual results comparing the ictal versus interictal scans are available in the supplementary figure.

Discussion

Main findings

Cerebrovascular reactivity did not differ significantly in the full cohort but did differ in the subgroup of participants with right-sided headache. In this subgroup, CVR was decreased in the left planum temporale/left Heschl’s gyrus and increased in the right crus I of the cerebellum. For both regions, cluster size shrank and the peak voxel z-statistic decreased when participants with left-sided headache were included, suggesting that the addition of these participants attenuated the results.

Prior studies have demonstrated vascular alterations in participants with migraine with right-sided headache, including higher right middle cerebral artery pulsatility and higher basilar artery flow velocity interictally [27] and lower right middle cerebral artery flow velocity ictally [28]. The present findings suggest that lateralized vascular asymmetries in migraine with right-sided headache extend to cerebrovascular reactivity.

The left planum temporale and left Heschl’s gyrus are regions of the temporal lobe classically associated with language and auditory processing [29, 30]. The right crus I is located in the posterolateral portion of the cerebellum. Relatively little is published about either region in migraine. The left planum temporale has been associated with allodynia [31, 32], which was not assessed in this study. The right crus I exhibits increased gray matter volume in individuals with migraine compared to healthy controls [33].

Relationship between CVR and white matter hyperintensities

Migraine is associated with an approximately four-fold increase in the prevalence of white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) [34], which are hyperintense foci seen on T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences believed to result from damage to small blood vessels [35]. Low CVR has been associated with the development of WMHs in migraine [17, 36, 37]. WMHs were not evaluated in the current study; however, in a previous study [38], we identified a relationship between headache laterality and WMH burden. Participants who typically experienced left-sided headache during migraine attacks had more than 50% more WMHs in both cerebral hemispheres than those who typically experienced right-sided headache during attacks – a finding that persisted after adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors. Given prior studies linking low CVR to the presence of WMHs [4, 9-11], we speculate that our finding of lateralized CVR differences may be related to our prior observation of lateralized WMH burden in individuals with migraine.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study was the application of advanced neuroimaging during spontaneous migraine attacks involving unilateral headache, with each participant returning for a repeat scan on a separate day between attacks when free of all migraine symptoms. Additional strengths include that all participants were confirmed to meet ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria for migraine [19], were scanned on the same 3T scanner, experienced at least moderate headache severity during their ictal scan, and had not used acute pharmacological treatment for that attack.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. Participants who completed imaging during a left- or right-sided headache may have also experienced attacks involving bilateral headache or headache on the contralateral side at other times. We did not restrict enrollment to those who always experienced headache on the same side, as doing so would have greatly hampered recruitment. We also did not scan participants with bilateral headache or controls without migraine; therefore, our findings cannot be extrapolated to these populations. The potential contribution of aura to our findings is unknown. Of the 5 participants who reported aura, all reported visual disturbances, some of which likely would have met ICHD-3 criteria (e.g., “arc started in middle and moved up and then ended”) while others likely would not have (e.g. “vision off, foggy/blurry”). No participant reported non-visual aura (e.g., sensory or speech/language). The sample size (N = 18) was small, particularly when stratified by headache laterality, reflecting in part the challenges of completing fMRI during spontaneous migraine attacks, particularly when limiting ictal scans to those involving unilateral headache. Despite this limitation, CVR was shown to differ significantly in the subgroup of participants with right-sided headache. The absence of significant clusters in participants with left-sided headache is notable given the greater statistical power available to detect a significant cluster in this larger subgroup. It is also possible that with a larger cohort of participants with right-sided headache, and therefore greater statistical power, the observed imaging findings might be attenuated. Larger sample sizes may be achieved in future studies by inducing attacks – for example, through intravenous administration of calcitonin gene-related peptide or pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide [39] – though the effects of these vasodilatory peptides on CVR would need to be separately assessed. We did not assess or restrict caffeine consumption prior to MRI, which is relevant given that caffeine has been associated with decreased CVR [40]. Breath-holding effort was also not monitored, such as with CO2 measurement. Given that breath-hold BOLD signal is a composite of vascular responsiveness and task compliance, it is possible that breath-hold performance differed ictally versus interictally, or during left- versus right-sided headache, introducing uncontrolled variance in CVR measures. For some participants, there was a prolonged delay – up to two years – between the ictal and inter-ictal scans following expansion of the study, during which migraine biology, treatment exposure, or vascular health may have changed, leading to state drift. Migraine attack frequency was not assessed, though participants with daily headache were excluded. Finally, the timing of CVR alterations could not be determined from a single scan; a study design in which fMRI is completed daily across a series of attacks, as has been done previously [41], could help clarify this point.

Conclusions

Cerebrovascular reactivity, as measured by breath-hold BOLD fMRI, did not differ significantly between the ictal and interictal states in the full cohort of participants with migraine, but did differ in the subgroup of participants with right-sided headache.
