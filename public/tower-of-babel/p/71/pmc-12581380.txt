Genetic insights of waist-to-height ratio on the risk of steatotic and advanced liver diseases: genome-wide association study and polygenic risk score-based analyses
Authors: Haotian Chen, Zhengye Liu, Hanze Du, Ziqi Wan, Xiaoyin Bai, Jiarui Mi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) is a simple and effective indicator to measure central obesity. However, the genetic basis and clinical implications of WHtR in relation to steatotic and advanced liver diseases remain largely unexplored.MethodsWe performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) in European individuals from the UK Biobank to identify genetic variants associated with WHtR, followed by extensive post-GWAS assessments, including colocalization analysis, transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS), and LD score regression. Logistic and Cox regression, and interaction analysis, were employed to evaluate the impact of WHtR PRS on alcoholic and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (AFLD and NAFLD), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), liver fibrosis, end-stage liver disease (ESLD), and liver cancer.ResultsOur GWAS identified 2,280 significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that were independently associated with WHtR. Colocalization analyses revealed that 55 SNPs influenced both WHtR and gene expression (including VEGFB, EZH1, CCND2 and etc.) in the visceral fat tissue. TWAS confirmed the involvement of these SNPs in visceral fat gene regulation. LD score regression analysis demonstrated that WHtR had significant positive genetic correlations with AFLD (rg = 0.123, p = 4.29 × 10⁻²), NAFLD (rg = 0.178, p = 1.78 × 10⁻⁷), and liver fibrosis (rg = 0.171, p = 2.57 × 10⁻²), yet no significant association was found with hepatocellular carcinoma (rg = 0.015, p = 0.872). Furthermore, the WHtR PRS was associated with increased risk of AFLD (OR = 1.09 [95% CI 1.03–1.13], p = 0.002), NAFLD (OR = 1.10 [95% CI 1.07–1.12], p < 0.001), MASLD (OR = 1.13 [95% CI 1.12–1.14], p < 0.001) and liver fibrosis (OR = 1.11 [95% CI 1.08–1.14], p < 0.001), although there was no statistical significance in relation to ESLD (p = 0.104) and hepatocellular carcinoma (p = 0.108). These findings were further corroborated by Cox regression analysis using WHtR PRS as well as logistic and Cox regression analysis using WHtR measures. The restricted cubic spline regression suggested potential linear relationships of WHtR PRS on the risks of NAFLD, MASLD and liver fibrosis. Notably, Gene-environment interaction analysis indicated that metabolic status, diabetes, and healthy dietary patterns could modulate the genetic liability to a high WHtR to the risk of NAFLD, MASLD, and liver fibrosis.ConclusionsThe study identified WHtR-related genetic variants and their roles in gene regulation in visceral adipose tissue. Individuals predisposed to high WHtR constituted a high-risk group in developing alcoholic liver disease, NAFLD, MASLD and liver fibrosis. Our study highlights the value of WHtR for the risk assessment and personalized disease surveillance in steatotic liver diseases.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00832-8.

Introduction

Liver diseases show a broad diversity in causes and pathophysiology. NAFLD and MASLD are closely associated with metabolic syndrome, obesity, and type 2 diabetes, with insulin resistance as the core pathogenic mechanism [1]. Both NAFLD and MASLD have genetic basis and are linked to adverse intrahepatic and extrahepatic complications [2–5]. Alcoholic liver disease, a prevalent liver condition caused by excessive alcohol consumption, is marked by liver damage. Liver disease progression ranges from simple steatosis to advanced fibrosis. Untreated liver fibrosis can result in cirrhosis and a heightened risk of liver cancer, especially hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and end-stage liver disease (ESLD), denoted by severe and irreversible hepatic dysfunction, typically necessitates liver transplantation [6–8].

The waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), calculated as waist circumference divided by height, is a simple anthropometric measure used to assess central obesity and visceral adipose tissue levels. It has been recognized as a useful tool for evaluating cardiovascular disease risk [9]. Prior research shows that WHtR is more effective than conventional anthropometric measures like body mass index (BMI) in predicting health risks associated with metabolism [10]. Moreover, recent studies indicate that WHtR exhibits superior diagnostic accuracy compared to other common body fat indicators, such as neck circumference and waist-to-hip ratio (WHpR) [11, 12]. Not only in adult, WHtR was also proposed as one of the best physical fitness indicators for predicting NAFLD and metabolic syndrome in children and adolescents [13, 14].

While past cross-sectional and cohort studies have shown WHtR’s predictive value for NAFLD, there is still a lack of evidence on its systematic effects on steatotic and advanced liver diseases. However, as a key measure of central obesity, the genetic basis of WHtR remains not fully understood. Furthermore, the extent to which WHtR and different liver diseases share common genetic variants is still unclear, and the potential genetic correlations and causal relationships between them require further elucidation.

Bridging these knowledge gaps is essential for a comprehensive understanding of central obesity on the development and progression of steatotic and advanced liver diseases.

To address this issue, here, we performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of WHtR in individuals of European ancestry from the UK Biobank. Using multiple post-GWAS approaches, we identified genetic variants associated with gene expression in visceral adipose tissue and explored the genetic correlations and causal links between WHtR and various liver disorders. Polygenic risk score (PRS) combines multiple genetic variants to assess individual genetic risk better than single markers to improve risk prediction, help identify high-risk people, and examines gene-environment interactions. Therefore, we used PRS-based regression models to assess how WHtR affects steatotic and advanced liver diseases in the cohort. Additionally, we analyzed gene - environment interactions to see how lifestyle factors and comorbidities influence the genetic predisposition to elevated WHtR and its impact on the risk of these liver diseases.

Method

Study cohort and GWAS analysis

The UK Biobank is a prospective longitudinal cohort of over 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 in the UK. Genotyping was carried out in two phases using Affymetrix chips [15]. Our analysis included only European descendants and excluded those with sex mismatches, missing genetic data, or heterozygosity outliers. To address population stratification, we used principal component analysis and excluded individuals deviating by over 5 standard deviations in principal components 1 and 2. Participants with a kinship coefficient above 0.0884 were also excluded (https://www.kingrelatedness.com/manual.shtml) [16–18]. We performed GWAS using the “BOLT-LMM” software with a linear mixed model (LMM), adjusting for the first 10 principal components, genotype measurement batch, assessment age, assessment center, and BMI. The Functional Mapping and Annotation of Genome-Wide Association Studies (FUMA) platform annotated independent SNPs.

Disease definitions and covariates assessments

This study focuses on six main liver diseases: alcoholic liver disease, NAFLD, MASLD, liver fibrosis, ESLD, and liver cancer. Except for MASLD, the classifications of the other five diseases are based on ICD-9 and ICD-10 (Supplementary Table 1).MASLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis (fatty liver index [FLI] > 60) plus at least one of following five metabolic characteristics using the baseline measurements: 1, BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 or waist circumference > 94 cm (male) or > 80 cm (female); 2, HbA1c ≥ 5.7%, type 2 diabetes, or treatment for type 2 diabetes; 3, hypertension or antihypertensive medication use; 4, plasma triglycerides ≥ 1.70 mmol/L or lipid-lowering medication use; 5, plasma HDL-C ≤ 1.0 mmol/L (male) or ≤ 1.30 mmol/L (female) or lipid-lowering medication use [19–21]. MASLD diagnosis requires excluding other causes of hepatic steatosis, such as hepatitis B, C, or Wilson’s disease. Since the UK Biobank lacks data on fasting blood glucose levels, the blood glucose levels were not included in the MASLD definition.

Colocalization and transcriptome-wide association study

We used expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data from visceral adipose tissue, sourced from the GTEx V8 database, to explore if WHtR and gene expression in visceral fat share causal variants using colocalization method. For visualization, we generated regional association plots using LocusZoom, focusing on a 1 MB window around the variants, with LD information from the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 dataset. Next, to enhance the accuracy of gene expression predictions by utilizing the biological similarities across tissues and cellular contexts, we integrated multi-tissue data using the joint tissue imputation method. This method was applied to data from the GTEx v8 dataset to construct a gene expression model based on joint tissue imputation [22].

LD score regression and Mendelian randomization

We used the cross-trait LDSC method to assess the genetic correlations between WHtR and NAFLD, liver fibrosis, alcoholic liver diseases, and liver cancer from the FinnGen R10 dataset [23]. This method uses a weighted linear regression to estimate genetic correlation by regressing the product of each disease pair’s Z-scores against the LD scores of genome-wide SNPs. We applied a Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected P-value threshold to determine statistically significant correlations. To explore the causal relationships further, we applied a two-sample MR approach with the inverse-variance weighted method via the “TwoSampleMR” R package. Instrumental variables were clumped at an R² threshold of 0.001 [24]. To estimate the extent to which type 2 diabetes (T2D) mediates the effect of genetically elevated WHtR on liver disease, we performed a two-step MR mediation analysis using “RMediation” package [25]. Summary-level T2D data were obtained from the DIAGRAM consortium [26].

Polygenic risk score and statistical analyses

A polygenic risk score (PRS) was constructed using the leading SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸; r² < 0.001) from our GWAS to estimate the genetic predisposition to WHtR for each individual [27]. To assess the link between WHtR PRS and liver disease risk, we performed logistic regression to calculate odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The WHtR PRS and measurements were normalized via inverse-normal transformation due to extreme values. The covariates included age, sex, BMI, townsend deprivation index (continuous variable), smoking status (never, previous, current), alcohol consumption (infrequent and frequent alcohol consumption), education, ethnicity, albumin levels, urate, baseline comorbidities of type 2 diabetes and hyperlipidemia. Five models were assessed, including raw model (model 1), multi-adjusted model with common covariates including age, sex, BMI, townsend deprivation index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, education, ethnicity and albumin levels (model 2); model2 with further adjustment of urate levels, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia (model 3); and model 3 further adjusted with physical activity (model 4); and model 4 further adjusted with dietary patterns (model 5). We validated our logistic regression results using Cox proportional hazards regression (Cox regression) model in all liver diseases except MASLD (due to the lack of time-to-event information). Linear regression was used to investigate the association between WHtR and various indices indicating liver fat accumulation or liver fibrosis. We used restricted cubic splines to explore the nonlinearity of the association of WHtR PRS on the risk of liver diseases. Gene-environment interactions were analyzed by investigating both multiplicative and additive interactions between WHtR PRS and lifestyle factors regarding liver diseases. The participants were divided into low, middle, and high genetic risk groups according to PRS tertiles. Additive interactions were quantified using relative excess risk due to interaction, attributable proportion due to interaction, and synergy index. Joint associations were analyzed by categorizing individuals stratified by WHtR PRS groups and lifestyle factors. Participants who satisfied less than two of the following four criteria were considered healthy metabolic status: (1) abnormal triglycerides (≥ 1.7 mmol/L) or lipid-lowering medications, (2) increased diastolic blood pressure (≥ 85 mmHg) or systolic blood pressure (≥ 130 mmHg) or anti‐hypertensive drugs, (3) elevated fasting glucose (≥ 5.6 mmol/L) or medications for diabetes (insulin and oral anti‐diabetic) and (4) low high‐density lipid (HDL)cholesterol (1.29 mmol/L for women and 1.04 mmol/L for men) [28]. The cardioprotective diet score, grounded in American Heart Association guidelines and optimized for cardiometabolic health, was derived from food-frequency questionnaire data [29]. Seven food groups were evaluated: fruits (≥ 3 servings/day), vegetables (≥ 3 servings/day), whole grains (≥ 3 servings/day), fish (≥ 2 servings/week), refined grains (≤ 2 servings/day), unprocessed meats (≤ 2 servings/week), and processed meats (≤ 1 serving/week). Each group received one point when the target was met, yielding a 0–7 scale (higher = better adherence). For adequate group sizes, scores were collapsed into five categories: 0–1, 2, 3, 4, and 5–7. For stratified analyses based on BMI, For stratification by BMI, participants were classified as obese/overweight (baseline BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) or lean (baseline BMI < 25 kg/m²). For the comparison of the predictive value of waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) on the risk of liver diseases, we applied the WHR GWAS data from Shungin et al. and constructed the PRS. The regression modelling framework is in consistence with WHtR [30]. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered suggestive significant for all analyses except where specified for GWAS. TWAS used Bonferroni correction. Analyses were conducted using R software version 4.2.3.

Results

GWAS of WHtR

A total of 407,014 European individuals were included in GWAS analyses (Table 1). Our GWAS detected 304 significant genomic loci, comprising 2,280 independent SNPs (not in LD) in autosomal chromosomes (Fig. 1A-C, Supplementary Tables 2–4). Using FUMA annotations, 1,421 genes were successfully mapped (Supplementary Table 5). The most significant SNPs were predominantly located in intergenic and intronic regions, with only 0.5% to 2% residing in untranslated, exon, and downstream regions of protein-coding genes (Supplementary Table 6). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these significant loci are implicated in biological processes including “regulation of steroid hormone biosynthetic process”, “fat cell differentiation”, and “response to hormone” (Supplementary Table 7). Tissue specificity testing showed that visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue are among the organs with the most significant enrichment of genetic variants (Supplementary Table 8).

Colocalization and transcriptome-wide association study

We carried out colocalization analyses between WHtR and visceral adipose tissue eQTLs to identify colocalized SNPs and their associated genes (Supplementary Fig. 1–3, Supplementary Table 9). Significant colocalization was found with the expression of 55 genes, including EBILIN2 (PP.H4 = 1.000), ZBTB7B (PP.H4 = 0.996), EZH1 (PP.H4 = 0.931), ATP13A1 (PP.H4 = 0.931), VEGFB (PP.H4 = 0.905), and SLC39A13 (PP.H4 = 0.889). In the TWAS, 392 genes showed significant associations between their expression levels and WHtR after Bonferroni correction in visceral adipose tissue (p < 3.694 × 10⁻⁶, Supplementary Fig. 4A-B, Supplementary Tables 10–13).

LD score regression and Mendelian randomization

Using LD score regression, we found significantly inverse genetic correlations between WHtR and NAFLD (rg = 0.1777, p = 1.78E-07, fdr = 1.39E-05), liver fibrosis (rg = 0.1705, p = 0.0257, fdr = 0.182), and alcoholic liver disease (rg = 0.1231, p = 4.29E-02, fdr = 0.260), while liver cancer did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2A and B, Supplementary Table 14). MR analyses further suggested potential causal relationships between the genetic predisposition to high WHtR and risk of NAFLD (OR = 4.227, [95% CI 3.016, 5.903], p < 0.001) and liver fibrosis (OR = 2.872 [95% CI 1.268, 6.465], p = 0.011). To quantify the extent to which T2D mediates the causal effect of genetically elevated WHtR on NAFLD and liver fibrosis, we conducted two-step MR mediation analyses. First, WHtR was causally associated with T2D (OR = 2.94, 95% CI 2.41–3.60, p < 0.001). Second, T2D was also causally linked to both NAFLD (OR = 1.65, 95% CI 1.38–1.97, p < 0.001) and liver fibrosis (OR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.14–1.76, p = 0.002). Consequently, 40.3% (95% CI 29.9% − 50.7%) of the total effect of WHtR on NAFLD and 34.5% (95% CI 26.0% − 43.0%) of its effect on liver fibrosis were mediated through T2D.

WHtR and liver disease risks

We conducted logistic regression analyses using WHtR PRS derived from our GWAS in the overall population to further investigate the relationship between genetically predisposed high WHtR PRS and risk of liver diseases (Supplementary Table 15). After full adjustment of confounders, we found that the WHtR PRS was significantly associated with increased risks of AFLD (OR = 1.08 [95% CI 1.03–1.13], p = 0.002), NAFLD (OR = 1.10 [95% CI 1.07–1.12], p < 0.001) and liver fibrosis (OR = 1.11 [95% CI 1.08–1.14], p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). However, no significant associations were observed for ESLD (OR = 1.02 [95% CI 1.00-1.03], p = 0.104) and liver cancer (OR = 1.02 [95% CI 1.00-1.04], p = 0.108). Notably, WHtR PRS was consistently associated with a reduced risk of MASLD defined by FLI (OR = 1.13 [95% CI 1.12–1.14], p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 15). For sensitivity analyses, we confirmed these findings using Cox regression with WHtR PRS and both regression models using WHtR measurements (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6, Supplementary Tables 16–18). Subgroup and meta-analyses in across different ethnic groups and genders also support these findings (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7, Supplementary Tables 19–22). Furthermore, our linear regression results showed that a one-unit increase in the WHtR PRS was linked to a β increase in the FLI (β = 0.56 [95% CI 0.51, 0.61], p < 0.001) and HSI (β = 0.09 [95% CI 0.08, 0.10], p < 0.001), although no consistent significant results were observed using different fibrosis scores: Fib-4 (β = 0.00 [95% CI -0.02, 0.02], p = 0.811), fibrosis index score (β = 0.01 [95% CI 0.00, 0.02], p < 0.001) and Forns index (β = 0.01 [95% CI 0.00, 0.02], p = 0.005). The restricted cubic spline analyses showed no significant non-linear relationship of WHtR PRS on NAFLD (p = 0.331), liver fibrosis (p = 0.450) and MASLD (p = 0.908) with full adjustment (Fig. 4). Next, we analyzed obese/overweight and lean populations respectively to evaluate whether WHtR PRS retains predictive value for steatotic and advanced liver disease across different body composition (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9, Supplementary Tables 23–26). In the obese/overweight group, WHtR PRS recapitulated the associations observed in the overall cohort in both logistic and Cox models. By contrast, in lean participants, WHtR PRS was only significantly associated with MASLD risk. Although the positive trends were consistently observed, the associations with other steatotic and advanced liver disease did not reach statistical significance. Interestingly, the WHtR measurement showed strong non-linear relationships with the risk of alcoholic liver disease, NAFLD, MASLD, liver fibrosis, ESLD and liver cancer (all p < 0.001) with the log ORs rises rapidly when WHtR measures are low, followed by a relatively slow increase as the WHtR increases (Supplementary Fig. 10). To confirm the effect of WHtR PRS in separate populations, we randomly selected 100k and 200k Europeans from UK Biobank and re-ran the WHtR GWAS (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12, Supplementary Tables 27–30). Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the newly-derived PRS in the non-overlapping samples consistently increases risks of NAFLD, MASLD and liver fibrosis. Lastly, our comparative analysis showed that the predictive value of waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) PRS on the risk of liver diseases is smaller than WHtR indicated by the ORs and HRs (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Gene-environment interaction analysis

For gene-environment interaction analyses, we found that diabetes can exacerbate the detrimental effects of WHtR PRS on NAFLD (relative excess risk due to interaction [RERI] = 0.40, [95% CI 0.00, 0.80], attributable proportion due to interaction [AP] = 0.07, [95% CI 0.01, 0.13]), liver fibrosis (RERI = 0.87, [95% CI 0.43, 1.31], AP = 0.16, [95% CI 0.09, 0.24]), ESLD (RERI = 0.32, [95% CI 0.00, 0.63], AP = 0.08, [95% CI 0.00, 0.16]) and liver cancer (RERI = 0.28, [95% CI 0.09, 0.46], AP = 0.11, [95% CI 0.04, 0.18]). Individuals with healthy metabolic status had a stronger protective effect of WHtR PRS against NAFLD (RERI = -0.1, [95% CI -0.16, -0.03], AP = -0.27, [95% CI -0.46, -0.08]), MASLD (RERI = -0.03, [95% CI -0.05, -0.01], AP = -0.17, [95% CI -0.28, -0.06]) and liver fibrosis (RERI = -0.12, [95% CI -0.20, -0.03], AP = -0.30, [95% CI -0.52, -0.08]).

Discussion

Our genome-wide association study (GWAS) of waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) in European individuals from the UK Biobank identified 2,280 significant SNPs associated with WHtR. Further colocalization analyses revealed 55 SNPs that influence both WHtR and gene expression in visceral adipose tissue, with TWAS confirming their role in visceral fat gene regulation. LD score regression and Mendelian randomization analyses demonstrated significant positive genetic correlations and potential causal relationships between WHtR and several liver diseases. The WHtR PRS was associated with increased risks of AFLD, NAFLD, MASLD, and liver fibrosis. Gene-environment interaction analyses indicated that metabolic status and diabetes can modify the genetic liability to high WHtR regarding the risk of NAFLD, MASLD, and liver fibrosis. These findings unveil WHtR’s genetic basis and highlight its clinical implications for liver disease risk assessment.

Numerous candidate genes identified from our GWAS have previously been established as playing a role in adipocyte metabolism and the maintenance of adipose tissue homeostasis. For example, unlike VEGFA, VEGFB is critical for lipid metabolism, influencing lipid deposition and utilization by regulating fatty acid transport [31, 32]. One recent study showed that inhibiting the VEGFB signaling pathway in adipocytes can significantly reduce the visceral fat lipolysis, thereby decreasing intrahepatic lipid deposition [33]. Furthermore, VEGFB significantly regulates adipose tissue development and energy metabolism by dictating white adipose tissue expansion and brown adipose tissue whitening, thereby increasing fat accumulation and reducing energy expenditure [34]. Additionally, by binding to VEGFR1, VEGFB activates the VEGF/VEGFR2 pathway, enhancing capillary density and tissue perfusion. This improves metabolic health and alleviates obesity-related metabolic complications [35]. ZBTB7B, a transcription factor, plays a significant role in the differentiation and metabolic regulation of adipocytes [36]. It can promote the development and heat production of brown and beige fat by interacting with the long non-coding RNA Blnc1 [36]. TBX15 is another key transcription factor for adipocyte metabolism and differentiation [37, 38]. In mice, the absence of Tbx15 significantly impacts adipocyte differentiation and metabolism, particularly in brown and ‘brownish-white’ adipocyte development [39]. Additionally, TBX15 regulate adipocyte metabolism, in particular glycolysis and oxidative metabolism. Studies have shown that subpopulations of adipocytes with high TBX15 expression exhibit higher glycolytic metabolism, while those with low expression demonstrate more oxidative activity [37]. The EZH1, an epigenetic regulator, plays a key role in adipocyte differentiation and lipid metabolism. It modulates adipocyte metabolic functions by regulating lipoprotein-dependent lipid accumulation and may influence lipid metabolism via upregulation of APOE gene expression [40]. ATP13A1, a P5A-ATPase in the endoplasmic reticulum, is crucial for adipocyte metabolism [41]. It inhibits the accumulation and degradation of mis-localized proteins via the ERAD pathway and influences mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation [41]. SLC39A14, a zinc transporter, has an expression in adipose tissue that correlates with inflammatory signals and metabolic changes [42]. Mice lacking SLC39A14 exhibit enlarged adipocytes, elevated cytokine production, and impaired insulin signaling, all linked to adipose tissue inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. The specific functions of many candidate genes in adipose tissue are still not well-understood, but it is possible that they influence adipocyte growth and metabolism by impacting cell proliferation, differentiation, protein synthesis, translocation, and apoptosis.

WHtR is widely recognized for its role in assessing central obesity, insulin resistance (IR), and sarcopenic obesity. It has demonstrated high diagnostic value across different populations and is effective in predicting obesity-related metabolic risks. In children and adolescents, WHtR serves as a reliable tool for detecting obesity and metabolic syndrome across different ethnic and gender groups [43, 44]. In adults, WHtR outperforms BMI and waist circumference in predicting cardiovascular and metabolic risks [45, 46]. In non-diabetic obese individuals, WHtR is an independent predictor of IR, with a higher area under the curve (AUC) than other body measurements like waist circumference (WC) and body mass index (BMI) in predicting IR [47]. For children and adolescents, WHtR is considered a reliable tool for IR screening, showing similar discriminative power to biochemical markers [48]. Moreover, WHtR is associated with sarcopenia, and in elderly patients with chronic lower back pain, higher WHtR is linked to reduced muscle strength and impaired physical function [49]. These results indicate that a higher WHtR can disrupt the metabolism of skeletal muscle and adipose tissue systems, leading to systemic metabolic abnormalities and subsequent liver metabolic dysfunction.

Central obesity is a key risk factor for metabolic diseases like cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome, which are also associated with MASLD development [50, 51]. Previous studies in cross-sectional study design suggested a potential link between WHtR or central obesity and declined liver health, particularly in the context of NAFLD and MASLD. Mechanistically, under IR, increased abdominal fat lipolysis leads to rapid transport of breakdown products into the liver via the portal vein, promoting lipid accumulation. WHtR has shown a positive association with NAFLD in adults with a threshold equal to 0.4 indicating the NAFLD risk. It was suggested that central obesity promote MASLD development through IR, gut-liver axis dysfunction, and inflammatory responses [52]. The interaction between central obesity and genetic variants on key genes like PNPLA3 can further increase liver disease risk [53]. Significant prior research has established WHtR as a superior predictor of NAFLD risk across adult, adolescent, and child populations compared to BMI. However, the molecular and genetic basis connecting WHtR to liver disease have not been thoroughly investigated. Our study delved into the genetic links between WHtR and liver diseases, employing rigorous methodologies including causal inference tests and PRS-based regression models to validate our findings. The LD score regression analysis revealed a positive genetic correlation between WHtR and both NAFLD and liver fibrosis. Complementary two-sample MR analysis further demonstrated a causal relationship: genetically elevated WHtR levels are associated with an increased risk of NAFLD and liver fibrosis. Furthermore, WHtR PRS showed a consistent association with increased risks of various liver diseases, including alcoholic liver disease, NAFLD, MASLD, liver fibrosis, ESLD, and liver cancer after adjusting for notable confounding factors related to steatotic and advanced liver diseases. The RCS regression analysis also supported a linear relationship between WHtR PRS and liver disease risk, indicating a dose-dependent association. These results collectively highlight WHtR PRS as a potentially valuable tool for assessing liver disease risk associated with central obesity.

In obese/overweight individuals, the WHtR PRS robustly predicted overall liver disease, mirroring our main results. In lean participants, the same score remained significantly associated with MASLD but yielded only directionally consistent, non-significant trends for other steatotic or advanced outcomes. This divergence likely reflects (i) the three-fold smaller lean sample size, (ii) under-ascertainment of advanced disease in the community-based UK Biobank, and (iii) the distinct metabolic trajectory of lean MASLD - ranging from an early, FXR-driven “obesity-resistant” phase to later sarcopenic, cachectic progression marked by peripheral lipolysis and shortened telomeres [54, 55]. Accurate stratification therefore demands hospital-based data integrating imaging, laboratory, and comorbidity information; future multi-omic and phenomic profiling across BMI categories will be essential to resolve this heterogeneity and inform precision treatment.

When adjusted for the same covariates used in the WHtR model, the WHR PRS retained significant associations with NAFLD, MASLD, ALD and fibrosis, but effect sizes were consistently smaller than for WHtR, and no links emerged for ESLD or liver cancer. Together, these findings suggest that WHR-based genetic risk is both weaker and less comprehensive than WHtR-based risk, probably because WHR conflates fat distribution with body size and cannot distinguish metabolically distinct gynoid from visceral fat [56, 57].

Effective management of central obesity is crucial for liver disease patients. Maintaining healthy metabolic status and reversing insulin resistance might be key strategies based on recent clinical trials of SGLT2 inhibitor and glucagon-like peptide-1 and glucagon receptor dual agonist on MASLD treatment [58, 59]. Accordingly, we conducted interaction analyses to evaluate the synergistic effect of modifiable risk factors on WHtR genetic predisposition for liver outcomes. Our results indicated that diabetes was associated with a more significant increased risks in alcoholic liver disease, NAFLD, liver fibrosis, ESLD and liver cancer risk among individuals with a higher WHtR PRS. Conversely, a healthier metabolic status can reduce the detrimental effects of a high WHtR PRS on alcoholic liver disease, NAFLD, and liver fibrosis. These findings underscore the importance of lifestyle modifications tailored to an individual’s genetic profile, potentially optimizing the management and prognosis of liver diseases.

Our study has several limitations. First, the GWAS analysis was conducted exclusively in European populations. To enhance the generalizability of our findings, future research should involve more diverse ethnic groups or use trans-ethnic meta-analysis, ideally integrating independent biobanks from different ancestries to cross-validate effect estimates and ensure reproducibility in various ethnic groups. Second, WHtR may be associated with other complex traits. Methods like MTAG could facilitate the identification of more genetic variants across related phenotypes. Third, genetic variants not covered by genotype imputation, especially rare variants in exons or untranslated regions, were not adequately explored. Future studies should consider using whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and exome-wide association studies (ExWAS) to identify novel variants. Fourth, future investigations should also disentangle the potentially independent contributions of distinct adipose depots, such as android versus gynoid, and visceral versus subcutaneous fat, to liver disease risks, as these patterns may modulate risk beyond the information captured by WHtR alone. Lastly, mechanistic studies in vivo models are essential to clarify the underlying mechanisms of gene-environment interactions and visceral fat-liver cross-talks in steatotic and advanced liver development and progression.

Conclusion

Our study provides novel genetic insights into WHtR by identifying associated genetic variants, underscoring its role in liver disease susceptibility. Our findings highlight that individuals with a genetic predisposition to higher WHtR demonstrate elevated risks of AFLD, NAFLD, MASLD and liver fibrosis. The identification of these genetic links enhances our ability to assess liver disease risk and emphasizes the importance of WHtR in the pathogenesis of steatotic and advanced liver diseases. Subsequent research by leveraging these genetic discoveries is warranted to explore targeted therapeutic interventions for obesity-related liver diseases.
