Introduction

Teratomas are germ cell neoplasms arising from the totipotent stem cells, comprising tissues from ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm [1,2]. They commonly occur in midline structures, most frequently in the sacrococcygeal region or the gonads [3]. Primary adrenal gland teratomas are extremely rare in children, with only a few cases reported in the literature [1,4]. The usual presentation includes abdominal mass, distension, and pain, with some cases detected prenatally [3,5]. Preoperative diagnosis of requires a high index of suspicion and imaging, with surgical resection being the primary treatment modality [2].

Herein we discuss a nine-month-old female infant presented with an abdominal mass, following oncological resection, the histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of primary adrenal teratoma. This case report aims to highlight the importance of considering these rare tumors in the differential diagnosis of pediatric patients with adrenal masses. This case report has been written in line with SCARE criteria [6].

Case presentation

A nine-month-old female infant, with no significant prenatal or past medical history, presented with a two-month history of progressively increasing abdominal distention. She had a good appetite, no symptoms suggestive of intestinal obstruction, and no constitutional symptoms. Physical examination revealed a distended abdomen with full right renal angle, and palpable large abdominal mass predominantly in the right upper quadrant, moves with respiration, ballotable, and dull on percussion. Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, renal and liver profiles were normal. Urine for vanillylmandelic acids (VMA), serum alphafetoprotein (AFP), and human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG), were also normal for her age. Abdominal ultrasound identified a large heterogeneous right suprarenal mass with multiple cystic components. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis revealed a well-defined complex mass measuring 12 × 10.5 × 8.5 cm, with fatty hypodense cystic components, calcifications, multiple enhancing septations, and small enhancing soft tissue components. The mass was inseparable from the right adrenal gland, displacing the right kidney posteroinferiorly and exerting mass effect on the right lobe of the liver. It crossed the midline, slightly displacing the aorta and inferior vena cava (IVC) to the left without encasement. There was no abdominal wall infiltration, intraspinal extension, or ascites (Fig. 1).

Selected cuts of enhanced (CT) abdomen and pelvis at level of suprarenal area showing large complex retroperitoneal well defined right adrenal mass with fatty hypodense cystic component, and calcifications, displacing the right kidney posteroinferiorly with mass effect on the liver, crossing the midline and slightly displacing the major abdominal vessels.

The initial diagnosis considered was a right suprarenal neuroblastic tumor. Given the absence of vessel encasement and other risk factors, upfront resection was planned. Intraoperatively, the mass was located in the right retroperitoneum, displacing the right colon, duodenum, IVC, pancreas, and portal triad antero-medially, and attached to the under surface of the liver, pushing it superiorly. The mass was completely excised. Histopathology of the encapsulated right suprarenal mass, weighing 583 g, showed cysts partially lined with skin adnexa, with a wall comprising adipose tissue, glial tissue, choroid, and bone. No immature elements were observed. The patient experienced an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged home 72 h post-surgery. The wound healed well (Fig. 2), and she is undergoing regular follow-up with routine alpha-fetoprotein monitoring.

Discussion

Teratomas are neoplasms of embryonic tissues, classified histologically as mature, immature, teratoma with malignant transformation, and non-endodermal variants [1]. In terms of locations teratomas can be classified into gonadal and extragonadal teratomas. Gonadal teratomas are more common in adults, while extragonadal teratomas frequently occur in the sacrococcygeal, presacral, anterior mediastinum, intracranial, and retroperitoneal regions being the least common [7,8].

Retroperitoneal teratomas exhibit a bimodal incidence peak: in the first six months of life and early adulthood [2]. Primary retroperitoneal teratomas are rare, accounting for <5 % of teratomas in infants. Primary adrenal teratomas in children are exceedingly rare, with few cases reported in the English literature [1,3].

The development of extragonadal teratomas is primarily explained by two main hypotheses. First, during embryogenesis, primordial germ cells migrate along the dorsal root mesentery to the gonadal ridges. Midline extragonadal teratomas are thought to arise from primordial germ cells that migrate abnormally and settle outside the gonadal ridges. In contrast, nonmidline teratomas are believed to originate from pluripotential embryonic cells that abnormally migrated and became embedded within various organs [9].

Teratomas often initially remain asymptomatic due to the ample space within the retroperitoneum, allowing them to grow to a considerable size before clinical presentation [2]. Common symptoms may include abdominal distension, abdominal pain, low back pain, and symptoms of compression of adjacent organs. In some cases, patients may be asymptomatic and the mass is discovered incidentally. Cystic teratomas may rupture, causing sudden abdominal pain, ascites, and peritonitis [1]. Prenatal detection has become more common with advances in ultrasound screening [3,5].

A part from neuroblastomas, primary adrenal tumors are extremely rare in children. Differential diagnoses include Pheochromocytomas, adenomas, adrenocortical carcinomas, and teratomas each with its own unique clinical presentation [10]. Diagnosis relies heavily on imaging, as laboratory examinations are typically normal since these tumors are usually nonfunctional [1]. Imaging modalities like ultrasonography, enhanced CT, and MRI are crucial for diagnosing adrenal tumors, assessing anatomical relationships, detecting vascular invasion, and evaluating resectability [10]. Benign retroperitoneal teratoma on CT imaging typically presents with well-defined margins, internal heterogeneity, areas of fat density, cystic components, and often scattered calcifications [2]. Metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) is an effective tool for diagnosis, and accurate staging of neuroblastoma but unfortunately is not available in our country [10].

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment, aiming for complete tumor removal without damaging adjacent organs, this provides the best chance for tissue diagnosis and cure [2]. A wide skin incision is often necessary for adequate exposure and control of potential bleeding. In cases where total excision is challenging or vital arteries are involved, debulking may be considered. Laparoscopic resection has been successfully performed in some instances [3,11].

Chemotherapy and radiotherapy play minor roles in managing these tumors, given their typically benign nature [1]. Complete resection of benign teratomas generally results in an excellent prognosis. Long-term follow-up with clinical, laboratory, and imaging assessments is essential, especially during the first five years post-resection [2]. Our patient continues to undergo regular follow-up, including monitoring of alpha-fetoprotein levels.

A review of the literature (Table 1) reveals that primary adrenal teratomas in children are exceedingly rare, with few cases documented globally. These cases span various countries, highlighting the international occurrence of this rare tumor. The age at diagnosis ranges from prenatal detection to 8 years. Tumors present with abdominal mass or distension, with incidental findings and other symptoms such as lumbar pain also noted. Tumor sizes vary, with most cases managed surgically. Histopathologically, these tumors are primarily mature mixed or mature cystic teratomas, with a rare report of malignant teratomas. Postoperative complications are infrequent but can include port-side hernia or nephrectomy.

Conclusion

Primary adrenal teratoma is extremely rare in pediatric age group, and present a significant diagnostic challenge therefore it should be considered in the differential diagnosis of pediatric patients with adrenal masses. Our case exemplifies a primary adrenal teratoma in children, aligning with the limited cases reported in the literature. The unpredictable nature of these tumors emphasizes the need for thorough histopathological evaluation and long-term follow-up.
