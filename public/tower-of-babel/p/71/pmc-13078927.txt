Current status and advances in bovine semen production technologies for artificial insemination in Japan
Authors: Yousuke NANIWA, Masashi KINUKAWA, Kyoko UCHIYAMA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial insemination (AI) is the predominant reproductive technology in Japanese cattle breeding, particularly for genetic improvement of dairy and beef cattle. This review outlines the historical development, current status, and recent advances in semen production technologies for AI in Japan and addresses the remaining challenges and future directions. The adoption of AI accelerated after the Act on Improvement and Increased Production of Livestock was implemented in 1950, with frozen semen replacing liquid semen by the mid-1960s. Advances in cryobiology and genomic selection have improved breeding efficiency; however, fertility issues persist. In dairy cattle, conception rates have declined primarily due to high milk yield, negative energy balance, and heat stress. In beef cattle, particularly Japanese Black cattle, conception rates remain stable overall, but subfertile sires still occur despite normal post-thaw semen quality. Studies on sperm motility, acrosomal integrity, and genomic variants have identified a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) marker linked to extreme subfertility in Japanese Black bulls. Japan has developed innovative technologies such as the two-layer semen straw (FCMax), which enhances post-thaw sperm function through in-straw dilution and supplementation. Field trials have suggested potential improvements in conception rates; however, large-scale validation studies are still ongoing. Furthermore, sexed semen technology has been widely adopted in Japan and achieved conception rates comparable to those of conventional semen. Emerging challenges include labor efficiency in large-scale farms, prompting interest in improved thawing protocols or liquid semen alternatives. Future priorities include integrating genomic tools, refining cryobiological techniques, and implementing practical innovations to sustain cattle reproduction in evolving production systems.

Introduction

In Japan, cattle breeding relies predominantly on artificial reproductive technologies, with artificial insemination (AI) and embryo transfer as the primary methods, as natural mating is rarely practiced. As of 2025, approximately 3.7 million cattle are raised in Japan, of which approximately 1.5 million are dairy cows [1]. Among dairy breeds, Holstein cattle account for approximately 99% of the population, whereas Jersey and other cattle breeds account for the remainder. Four Wagyu breeds, Japanese Black, Japanese Brown, Japanese Shorthorn, and Japanese Polled, are officially recognized for beef production; crossbreeding these breeds creates cattle that can also be classified as Wagyu. Among the four breeds, Japanese Black is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 98% of the Wagyu population. Additionally, crossbreeding dairy cows with Wagyu semen (commonly referred to as “Beef-on-Dairy”) [2, 3] has been widely practiced for decades. Based on annual data through 2024, approximately 40% of dairy cows were inseminated with beef-breed semen [4]. In 2025, interim aggregated data indicate that this proportion exceeded 40% and reached a national average of 46.5% in the third quarter, suggesting a further increase for 2025 [4].

Genetic improvement programs for both Holstein and Japanese Black cattle have historically relied on progeny testing to enhance traits, such as milk yield and composition in dairy breeds and carcass weight and meat quality in beef breeds. In recent years, genomic evaluation has been widely adopted, accelerating sire selection and herd improvement [5, 6]. Despite these advances, breed-specific challenges in AI conception rates persist. Progress in genotyping technologies has enabled new approaches to address these issues by improving the semen quality from a genetic perspective. Technological innovations have also contributed to reproductive efficiency. For example, the two-layer straw method, developed and disseminated in Japan, has shown promise in improving conception rates. Furthermore, Japan was among the early adopters of sex-sorted semen, originally developed in the United States, and has promoted its widespread use. The practical use of sexed semen began with in vitro fertilization in 2006 and was extended to AI in 2007 [7].

This review summarizes the current status and challenges of bovine semen production technologies for AI in Japan.

AI in Japan

Details of the global history of AI were not included in this review and can be found in previous studies [8,9,10]. Here, we provide an overview of the development of bovine AI in Japan. The history of livestock AI in Japan evolved under considerable influence from international research. The origin dates back to 1912, when Ishikawa introduced AI techniques to Japan after studying at Iwanoff’s laboratory in Russia [11, 12]. Early research focused primarily on horses and later on cattle, first in dairy cattle in 1928 and then in Wagyu cattle in 1940.

The practical implementation of AI accelerated after the Act on Improvement and Increased Production of Livestock was implemented in 1950, which established an organized AI system and certification of inseminators. Initially, liquid semen was used for insemination; however, the introduction of frozen semen marked a major turning point. In the early 1950s, Polge demonstrated that glycerol was effective for the cryopreservation of sperm in poultry [13, 14] and cattle [15, 16]. In 1952, Nishikawa, recognized as a pioneer of livestock AI in Japan, attended the Second International Congress on Animal Reproduction in Copenhagen, where he listened to Polge and Rowson’s presentation describing successful conception using bovine semen stored at −79°C for 20 weeks. Upon returning to Japan, Nishikawa and his colleagues developed domestic equipment, extenders, and protocols for frozen semen production [17, 18]. Subsequently, research on frozen semen advanced rapidly in 1955, and the results were quickly disseminated across the field, contributing significantly to the widespread adoption of AI.

In 1950, AI utilization rates were 56.1% in dairy cattle and 26.0% in Wagyu; by 1965, both exceeded 90% [11, 12]. Frozen semen, which was developed in the early 1950s, was introduced for practical use in 1965. Initially, its share of inseminations was less than 10% for both dairy and beef cattle, but by 1987 it had reached 99% [18, 19].

The Livestock Improvement Association of Japan (LIAJ) began operating as a frozen semen gene bank in 1965 under its predecessor foundation. In 1971, it was reorganized as the current incorporated association, consolidating regional AI centers. LIAJ was established to produce and distribute frozen semen from superior sires nationwide and to promote genetic improvement through progeny testing. Today, frozen semen is also supplied by organizations such as the Genetics Hokkaido Association, AI centers in each prefecture, and several private companies.

Manufacturing methods of frozen bovine semen

Frozen semen is the predominant form used for AI in Japan. The basic principles of frozen semen production in Japan are similar to those employed worldwide [20, 21]. Materials for semen processing may be domestically produced or imported. This section outlines the key features of the Japanese approach.

Artificial vaginas such as the Nishikawa-type, M-type, and other locally modified designs are commonly used for semen collection. Most facilities use dummy cows for mounting, although a castrated bull or a compatible teaser bull may also be used when necessary. In Japan, semen quality evaluation is regulated under the Act on Improvement and Increased Production of Livestock, which requires assessment of ejaculate volume, color, odor, pH, sperm motility, concentration, viability, and morphological abnormalities. Additional tests may be performed at the discretion of individual facilities.

Extenders used for semen dilution are either facility-specific formulations or those described in the official training textbook published by the Artificial Insemination Association of Japan (AIAJ) [22]. Proprietary extenders are generally based on Tris-citric acid buffer supplemented with egg yolk. Although soy lecithin-based and liposome-based extenders are commercially available in Europe as egg yolk substitutes, they are rarely used for large-scale semen production in Japan. Regarding dilution methods, some facilities have adopted the one-step method; however, the two-step method remains predominant. In the two-step method, semen is first diluted and cooled using an extender without glycerol, followed by the gradual addition of a glycerol-containing extender, which is added in a drip- or stepwise manner. In contrast, the one-step method involves immediate dilution with an extender containing cryoprotectants such as glycerol. Although the two-step method is considered more reliable for maintaining sperm quality, it is labor-intensive and time-consuming. The one-step method is highly efficient, but requires careful management to avoid osmotic shock to sperm cells.

French straws of 0.5 ml and 0.25 ml are widely available [23], although 0.5 ml straws are standard for domestically produced semen. Imported semen often uses 0.25 ml straws. Filling and sealing machines are typically Japanese-made or imported from Europe. European models are widely used internationally, whereas Japanese models are primarily used domestically, despite their similar functional designs. There are also machines developed with unique concepts in Japan, which will be described later. Freezing is performed using programmable freezers, although some facilities still employ conventional freezing equipment. Post-thaw semen evaluation is conducted either by visual assessment of sperm motility under a microscope or, in some facilities, using computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA). Flow cytometry may also be employed to assess sperm viability and acrosomal integrity.

Challenges and countermeasures for conception rates in AI

It has been more than 50 years since AI using frozen bovine semen became widespread in Japan. Nevertheless, issues related to conception rates have persisted, and efforts to improve them remain ongoing. Because these challenges vary by breed, they are discussed separately for dairy and beef cattle.

Dairy cattle

Globally, declining conception rates in dairy cattle are a major concern [24,25,26], and Japan is no exception. LIAJ also compiles results from dairy herd performance tests, including calving intervals and days open. According to data published in 2025, the average calving interval was 403 days in 1976 and 405 days in 1989, increasing to a peak of 438 days in 2011 and then declining to 428 days in 2024 [29]. However, the distribution is highly skewed; in 2024, the mode and median were 362 and 404 days, respectively. This indicates that although most cows have calves at regular intervals, some individuals exhibit extremely prolonged intervals, warranting individual-level investigation.

Several studies have explored factors contributing to reduced fertility. Dochi et al. [30] reported that high-producing cows experience marked postpartum declines in body condition score (BCS), delayed recovery of reproductive function, and an extended interval between first ovulation and estrus. Cows with first ovulation > 21 days postpartum had approximately one month longer of open days open compared to those with ovulation ≤ 21 days, and also showed significantly higher milk yield within 90 days postpartum. Another study on a large commercial farm found that pregnancy probability was associated with previous lactation yield but not with parity or calving season; cows with high or moderate previous milk yield had lower pregnancy rates than those with low yield [31]. Bernabucci et al. [32] suggested an association between heat stress, negative energy balance, and reduced fertility in Italian Holstein cows. In Japan, one study indicated a link between heat stress and negative energy balance [33], and another study suggested genetic correlations between increased milk yield and reduced conception rates have also been suggested [34]. Collectively, these findings suggest that increased milk production and heat stress can lead to maternal energy deficits, which in turn contribute to reduced conception rates in dairy cattle.

Beef cattle

Beef cattle in Japan consist mainly of Japanese Black (Wagyu) and crossbreeds produced by inseminating Holstein cows with Japanese Black semen (beef-on-dairy). According to the AIAJ, the conception rate for Japanese Black cattle in 2022 was 52.0% [28]. Similarly, surveys conducted as part of progeny testing by LIAJ reported an average conception rate of approximately 55% for Japanese Black × Japanese Black inseminations [35]. In contrast, insemination of Holstein cows with Japanese Black semen resulted in a slightly lower conception rate of 50.4%, suggesting that beef-on-dairy fertility may be influenced by female physiological status.

Unlike dairy cattle, the average conception rate in Japanese Black cattle has remained relatively stable over the past 28 years (unpublished data). However, this marker does not consider all cases of low fertility, and the causal gene has not yet been identified. Although WES was used in the initial analysis, whole-genome sequencing will be required for more comprehensive variant detection. Furthermore, because the current reference genome published by the USDA ARS is based on the Hereford breed, mapping Japanese Black sequences may overlook breed-specific regions. Therefore, de novo assembly of the Japanese Black genome is required, combined with advanced sequencing technologies such as long-read platforms. These efforts will enable precise identification of fertility-related variants and establish a system for rapid detection of de novo mutations in the future [38].

Reports from other regions in Japan have also documented the presence of subfertility Japanese Black bulls [39, 40]. Kishida et al. [40] demonstrated that the morphological normality of the sperm acrosome correlated with conception rates following AI. Murase et al. [41] reported an association between sperm motility patterns and sire fertility. When sperm were induced to undergo hyperactivation in vitro, subfertile bulls showed a reduced occurrence of sperm exhibiting asymmetric flagellar beating and circular movement. In addition, another group suggested a relationship between DNA methylation status and conception rates after AI [42]. Whether these findings can be explained by the DNA marker identified by our group remains unclear at present.

As for studies on genetic subfertility in sires outside Japan, Pausch et al. [43] conducted a GWAS in Brown Swiss cattle using SNP haplotypes for semen quality and conception rate as traits. They identified a quantitative trait locus (QTL) on chromosome 6 (BTA6) that was strongly associated with sperm motility, abnormalities, and conception rates. This QTL acts in a recessive manner, with homozygous bulls exhibiting severely impaired phenotypes. Furthermore, the group evaluated genetic correlations between male fertility and economically important traits such as milk yield and body conformation, through large-scale GWAS and meta-analysis and found almost no genetic correlation between these traits [44]. They also collected tissues, including the testes, epididymis, and vas deferens, for comprehensive DNA and RNA analyses and performed whole-genome and transcriptome sequencing to further investigate the causal mechanisms [45, 46]. However, these findings involved genomic regions that were entirely different from those associated with subfertility in Japanese Black cattle. Such findings underscore that fertility problems are breed-specific and may differ even within the same species depending on genetic improvement strategies.

Applied research toward practical improvement of semen quality

Issues related to sperm cryopreservation

Multiple perspectives must be considered to improve AI conception rates. From the perspective of frozen semen production, the critical objective is to preserve or enhance sperm quality after freezing and thawing. Numerous refinements of semen extenders have been proposed worldwide to support bovine sperm function during cryopreservation [47,48,49,50,51]. In general, the core components of extenders include buffers, agents that maintain osmotic pressure and electrolyte balance, energy sources, antibiotics, and cryoprotectants [49]. Many of these components are used for both liquid and frozen semen; however, cryoprotectants such as glycerol are specifically required for freezing.

It is widely recognized that the freeze–thaw process itself exerts profound effects on sperm quality. Among the best-known conceptual models is the two-factor hypothesis proposed by Mazur [52,53,54], which posits that cell survival during cryopreservation is optimized at an intermediate cooling rate determined by (at least) two competing sources of injury: (i) during slow cooling, cells are damaged by solution effects (e.g., high solute concentrations) triggered by extracellular ice formation, whereas (ii) during fast cooling, damage results from intracellular ice formation and its recrystallization during warming. In the case of sperm, particularly in species with minimal cytoplasm, intracellular ice formation has not been observed in some studies on horses [55] and humans [56], leading to the suggestion that osmotic imbalance during thawing may be a predominant source of injury. Beyond these two factors, contemporary literature implicates a suite of interacting mechanisms, including acrosomal damage, oxidative stress driven by reactive oxygen species, alterations in membrane fluidity, reduced metabolic activity, apoptosis-like changes, ferroptosis, DNA fragmentation, and miRNA-mediated responses [50]. Cryobiology in the context of sperm preservation is comprehensively covered in previous reviews [48, 57,58,59,60,61].

From a practical perspective, inorganic salts, such as sodium chloride, are generally avoided in extenders intended for freezing, consistent with earlier observations that egg yolk–saline formulations yield poorer post-thaw motility than egg yolk–citrate buffers [62] and lower survival than egg yolk–citrate solutions in comparative studies by VanDemark et al. [63]. By contrast, under physiological (non-freezing) conditions, electrolytes provide multiple benefits for sperm survival and function. Sodium and potassium are major cations in seminal plasma, and appropriate ionic composition facilitates sperm functionality [64, 65]. For example, in rat sperm, maintenance of the Na+/K+ gradients (Na+ efflux and K+ influx) supports energy metabolism and signaling (pH and Ca2+), thereby sustaining motility [66]. Sperm also perform regulatory volume decrease via efflux through K+ and Cl− channels to lower intracellular osmotic pressure, expelling water and osmolytes to prevent swelling-induced dysfunction [67,68,69]. In bovine sperm, activity of membrane Na+ channels has been implicated in the signaling that promotes capacitation-like functional maturation, suggesting that the normal flow of Na+ ions supplied by NaCl may contribute to pathways required for acquisition of fertilizing ability [70]. Thus, although salts are physiologically advantageous, their levels are minimized or excluded from freezing extenders to mitigate injury associated with cryoprocessing.

Potential toxicity and osmotic stress associated with commonly used cryoprotectants have also been reported [48, 60, 71]. Guthrie et al. [72] examined the osmotic tolerance of bovine sperm in relation to cryoprotectant exposure and observed that the one-time addition of 1 M or 2 M glycerol reduced motility by approximately 25% and 50%, respectively. Moreover, the abrupt removal of glycerol further decreased motility by approximately 31% or 48% relative to post-addition levels, underscoring osmotic stress during both addition and withdrawal. Several studies have leveraged CASA to quantify changes across the processing stages. Using Jersey cattle semen, Sundararaman et al. [73] reported statistically significant declines in average path velocity (VAP) and straight-line velocity (VSL) during the equilibration (glycerol addition) step, with further post-thaw reductions. Using a microfluidic device, Mofadel et al. [74] analyzed rheotaxis. They found that exposure to 7% glycerol and subsequent cooling/freezing significantly decreased VAP and VCL compared to fresh semen or egg yolk-citrate dilution without glycerol. Although not a cryoprotectant, increased medium viscosity alone can impede sperm motion; for example, polyvinylpyrrolidone and methylcellulose solutions reduce swimming speed in a physical model system [75]. Collectively, these findings suggest that cryoprotectant-induced osmotic stress and viscosity-related mechanical resistance jointly contribute to diminished motility.

Species-specific considerations further complicate the choice of cryoprotectants. Glycerol toxicity has been observed in horses, and alternative cryoprotectants have been explored [76]. In human sperm, forward progressive motility declines markedly upon simple mixing with glycerol-containing solutions [77], highlighting that optimal protocols may differ by species and preparation.

In summary, even with state-of-the-art extenders, cryopreservation imposes intrinsic constraints on sperm function. Continued optimization of semen extenders and processing protocols, such as controlled, stepwise cryoprotectant addition and removal, refined cooling/warming rates, tailored viscosity and ionic environments, and the incorporation of antioxidants or membrane-stabilizing agents, is necessary to further mitigate cryoinjury and improve post-thaw fertility outcomes.

Two-layer semen straw

Efforts to improve post-thaw sperm quality have often focused on modifying semen extenders or adding supplements, such as antioxidants. Despite extensive research, only a few additives have shown marginal benefits, and most attempts have yielded inconsistent or negligible improvements [78, 79]. Negative results are rarely published, and in our own experience, several reagents have failed to produce the expected improvements. In some cases, additives may act prematurely during semen processing, accelerating metabolic activity and ultimately reducing sperm function after thawing. Furthermore, as noted earlier, cryoprotectants, such as glycerol, can impair motility by decreasing membrane fluidity. Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that certain functional enhancers should not be present during cryopreservation but should instead interact with sperm only after thawing. This concept led to the development of a “two-layer structure” within the straw, comprising a semen and a diluent layer, containing functional components.

The LIAJ developed and commercialized a two-layer straw, marketed as FCMax, designed to maintain sperm quality after thawing frozen semen. In the initial AI trials, the two-layer straw achieved a 5.9% higher conception rate than the conventional single-layer straws (Fig. 4C); however, the difference was not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.113). Subsequent monitoring revealed similar trends: conception rates in heifers were 46.7% (973/2,082) and 52.7% (481/913) with conventional and two-layer straws, respectively; in cows, conception rates were 32.5% (353/1,085) and 38.9% (394/1,014), respectively, an improvement of more than 6% in both groups (Fig. 4D).

Subsequently, the composition of the diluent layer was modified to include multiple energy sources (modified FCMax). In this experiment, the control group used conventional FCMax, and the test group used modified FCMax. After freezing and thawing, the two layers were mixed and incubated in a water bath at 38°C. After 3 h and 6 h of incubation, sperm motility was significantly higher in the test group (Fig. 4E; paired t-test, P < 0.05). Subsequently, we conducted AI trials on an independent farm. The modified FCMax achieved a 7.7 percentage-point increase over the original FCMax, but the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 4F; Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.066). Continued large-scale monitoring is necessary to confirm whether this technology consistently improves fertility and whether further refinement of the diluent layer may enhance its effectiveness.

To enable large-scale production of this technology, we commissioned a manufacturer to produce a custom straw (SFM straw; Fujihira Industry, Tokyo, Japan) and a dedicated two-layer filling and sealing machine (X-Layer; Fujihira Industry). The straw incorporates a plug-and-powder system, such that the forcing-out load of the straw content is reduced, even with extenders lacking egg yolk, and is compatible with two-layer processing. The machine enables the large-scale production of approximately 3,000 straws per hour, accelerating the dissemination of this technology. As of 2023, 54% of frozen semen produced by LIAJ utilized the two-layer straw method.

The anticipated benefits of the two-layer approach include improved sperm motility through early glycerol dilution (reducing viscosity), functional enhancement via salts, and increased metabolic activity supported by multiple energy substrates. However, the extent to which these factors improve the sperm performance in vivo after insemination remains unclear. Although the two-layer method is not a fundamental solution to cryoinjury based on cryobiological principles, it represents a practical innovation that may broaden the strategies for optimizing thawed sperm. For example, future applications could aim to create conditions closer to the physiological environment, or stimulate sperm activity immediately after thawing.

Sexed semen technology

For a detailed account of the historical development of sexed semen technology in Japan, readers can refer to previous reviews [7, 80]. Here, we briefly outline this context and highlight recent advances.

LIAJ introduced sexed semen based on flow cytometry, which can be used to produce calves of the desired sex with > 90% accuracy using AI. This principle exploits the approximately 4% higher DNA content of X-bearing sperm compared to Y-bearing sperm in cattle [81,82,83]. Sperm DNA is stained with Hoechst 33342, oriented through a specialized nozzle [84], and sorted at high speed [85, 86], allowing the recovery of viable sperms suitable for AI and other assisted reproduction techniques. Commercialization in Japan progressed from field trials (2001–2005) to market launch in 2007. Adoption expanded rapidly; sexed semen increased from 1.4% of distributed dairy semen in 2007 to 31.2% in 2017, and the proportion of female dairy calves increased accordingly.

Initial field data showed lower conception rates with sexed semen than with conventional semen (47.9% vs. 58.7%, respectively), with heifers outperforming cows [7]. Subsequent improvements in equipment, sorting conditions, and handling have increased conception rates to 52.8% in heifers and 40.1% in cows (2012–2016). Reduced fertility in sexed semen has been attributed to sorting-related stresses (laser exposure [83], sheath pressure [87], and dilution [88]), membrane changes resembling capacitation, and diminished oviductal binding.

Recent developments have substantially improved the performance. The application of SexedULTRA technology, which increases sperm numbers from 2.1 million to 4 million per dose (SexedULTRA 4M), has achieved conception rates comparable to those of conventional semen containing 15 million sperm [88]. An analysis of approximately 1.9 million inseminations in Irish dairy cattle by the Irish Cattle Breeding Federation and Teagasc reported an average conception rate of 59% for sexed semen versus 64% for conventional semen, with a 92% relative performance overall. By 2022, the gap narrowed further (60% vs. 63%, 95% relative performance) [89]. Santos et al. [90] reported that conception rates did not significantly differ between sexed and conventional semen when insemination was timed either to estrus detection alerts or according to ovulation synchronization protocols. However, some studies indicate lower conception rates under specific conditions: for example, in beef cattle, SexedULTRA 4M achieved rates similar to conventional semen in females showing estrus at fixed-time AI, but lower rates in those without estrus [91]. In dairy cattle, overall conception rates with SexedULTRA 4M were slightly lower than conventional semen, with notable sire-to-sire variation [92].

The SexedULTRA technology has also been implemented in Japan. Although direct experimental comparisons with conventional semen have not been conducted, a 2023 field survey by LIAJ reported conception rates of 51.4% (647/1,259) for sexed semen and 52.5% (619/1,178) for conventional semen. Similarly, the AIAJ reported conception rates of 47.4% (n = 169,931) for sexed semen and 46.5% (n = 363,551) for conventional semen [28]. The convergence of conception rates in field conditions likely reflects multiple factors, such as improved sperm quality due to technological refinements, widespread familiarity with thawing and handling protocols, and commercial practices favoring the insemination of sexed semen into well-managed, clearly cycling females. These combined effects suggest that sexed semen technology has reached a level of practical reliability comparable to conventional semen in many contexts.

Other challenges in reproductive management

In recent years, Japan’s dairy and beef industries have undergone significant consolidation into large-scale operations. Over the past decade, the number of farms has declined markedly, whereas the average herd size per farm has increased substantially (Livestock Statistics, Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries) [93]. This trend has been driven by policy changes, economic pressure, and technological innovation, with scale expansion pursued to achieve cost reductions through economies of scale. However, large-scale operations also impose serious challenges on labor availability, contributing to chronic workforce shortages [94]. Similar trends in herd expansion have been observed in the United States [95], where strategies for next-generation reproductive efficiency have been discussed [24]. In Japan, structural constraints have made further improvements in reproductive efficiency an urgent priority.

On large farms, AI tasks can become highly concentrated when multiple cows enter estrus simultaneously. Some farms have adopted fixed-time AI protocols to streamline operations. Nevertheless, proper thawing and insemination of frozen semen according to manufacturer recommendations requires considerable time and labor, especially when many cows must be inseminated within a short window.

Although frozen semen offers excellent storage stability and has historically largely replaced liquid semen, the operational burden of thawing raises the question of whether liquid semen warrants reconsideration. Traditionally, liquid semen has been limited by a rapid decline in fertility compared with motility [96], indicating room for improvement in preservation methods. In countries such as New Zealand and Ireland, liquid semen use is widespread, and research continues to optimize storage conditions and conception rates [97,98,99]. Although a wholesale return to liquid semen in Japan is not necessarily anticipated, innovative approaches that enhance convenience and efficiency in insemination practices are essential.

Conclusion

The AI technology has become widely established in both dairy and beef cattle in Japan, contributing substantially to genetic improvement. However, despite these achievements, fertility-related challenges remain both directly and indirectly associated with AI practices. Future research should address these issues from multiple perspectives. From a genetic perspective, priorities include developing DNA marker-based strategies to select highly fertile sires and elucidating the genetic factors underlying subfertility in Japanese Black cattle. From the perspective of semen technology, research should focus on reducing cryoinjury during freezing and thawing, and advancing post-thaw sperm functionality, including approaches such as two-layer straw systems. In addition, innovations that streamline insemination procedures on large-scale farms are essential to meet the growing demand for labor efficiency.
