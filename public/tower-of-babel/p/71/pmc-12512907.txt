Spatiotemporal methylome remodeling during fiber differentiation in Gossypium hirsutum
Authors: Zhipeng Yu, Haijuan Cao, Xiaolian Xiong, Shuhan Wen, Xia Huang, Ying Jin, Junkang Rong, Mingquan Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhole-genome methylome analysis reveals DNA methylation dynamics during upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum) fiber development. DNA methylation levels initially increased and then decreased from -3 days post-anthesis (DPA) to 5 DPA, with a significant rise during the rapid elongation stage (5 DPA to 14 DPA), particularly in CHH methylation levels. Integrated transcriptome analysis links elevated DNA methylation to reduced demethylase gene expression (DME, ROS1, DML3). DNA methylation remodeling modulates fiber development through coordinated regulation of lipid metabolism, DNA replication, phosphatidylinositol signaling system and hormonal signaling pathways. Several key transcription factors, such as TCP14, HD1, HOX3, and MYB25-like, showed a strong correlation with DMRs, suggesting their regulation by DNA methylation. Furthermore, Multi-omics integration posits that DNA methylation may regulate genes related to fiber development, particularly those related to fatty acid biosynthesis and metabolism, ultimately influencing upland cotton fiber development. Analysis of genes highly correlated with changes in differentially methylated regions (DMRs) indicates DNA methylation can modulate the expression of KCS family genes, including KCS13, as well as KCRL1, thereby participating in the fatty acid elongation pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12116-5.Keywords: DNA methylation, Upland cotton, Fiber development, Transcriptome, Demethylase, Fatty acid elongation

Background

Cotton serves as the dominant global source of textile fibers, fulfilling approximately 35% of annual fiber demand worldwide. Furthermore, it is a model crop for studies on plant polyploidization, cell wall synthesis, and cell elongation [1]. The allopolyploid cotton emerged approximately 1–2 million years ago through the combination of diploid cotton Gossypium raimondii (D5) and A0 [2]. Over 95% of cotton fiber produced worldwide comes from upland cotton, which has a high yield and good fiber quality. Cotton fiber development unfolds in five partly overlapping steps: (1) epidermal cells are initiated and begin differentiating from −3 to 3 DPA; (2) fibers undergo rapid elongation during 3–16 DPA; (3) between 16 and 20 DPA the thickening primary wall is extensively remodeled; (4) secondary-wall cellulose is deposited from 20 to 40 DPA; and (5) the fibers mature during the final 40–50 DPA [3].

As an evolutionarily conserved epigenetic regulatory mechanism, DNA methylation orchestrates key plant biological processes spanning morphogenesis, abiotic stress adaptation, and reproductive organ maturation [4]. In plants, DNA methylation exists in both symmetric contexts (CG and CHG) and the asymmetric context CHH. DNA methylation regulation comprises three enzyme-driven processes: de novo modification, pattern maintenance, and active demethylation [5]. CG and CHG methylations are retained through a semi-conservative mechanism involving the MET1 and CMT3 enzymes, while CHH methylation maintenance is governed by CMT2 alongside the enzymes DRM2 via the RNA directed DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway [4]. For cotton fibers, the up-regulation of GhCMT2 may lead to CHH hypermethylation in transposable elements (TE) [6]. The ROS1 subfamily of demethylases exhibits heightened expression during ovule development, and the silencing of ROS1 has a significant inhibitory effect on fiber growth [7, 8].

Cotton fiber development are significantly affected by DNA methylation. Chen used specific 5mC and dot blot assays and found that the m5C content in 5 DPA ovules decreased compared to 0 DPA ovules [9]. Song’s research discovered that during the rapid elongation phase, the CHH methylation level in fibers at 14 DPA was higher than that in 0 DPA ovules. The use of aza-dC to inhibit DNA methylation not only diminishes the initiation of fiber cells but also hinders their elongation [6]. It is believed that the dual-lock feedback system, in which gene activation in ovules correlates with RdDM-dependent CHH methylation, while additional methylation facilitated by CMT2 leads to the repression of transposons and adjacent genes in fibers, represents the mechanism by which DNA methylation regulates fiber development in Gossypium hirsutum [6]. Furthermore, research on the annual development of fibers indicates that DNA methylation could influence the gene expression important to fiber development. Differential expression of three key genes for fiber growth, ERF6, SUR4, and KCS13, may be caused by variations in CHH methylation levels in their promoter region. The demethylase ROS1 regulates fiber development, and its silencing significantly inhibits fiber elongation [8]. In Gossypium barbadense, variations in the genome conformation during the cotton fiber development may be strongly correlated with the CHH DNA methylation. In this process, the euchromatin-to-heterochromatin shift is followed by a steady rise in CHH methylation levels, and the increased DNA methylation in fibers was primarily related to the H3K9me2 pathway's activity and less so to the RdDM pathway [10]. Additionally, it was discovered that DNA methylation has an effect on the efficiency of gene alternative splicing, which regulates the diversity of the cotton fiber's transcriptome [11].

While DNA methylation plays a pivotal role in cotton fiber development, the epigenetically regulated gene networks and phase-specific molecular mechanisms remain poorly delineated. In this study, we created a comprehensive genome-wide DNA methylome that encompasses all the various stages of upland cotton fiber development. The progressive elevation of methylation levels during fiber development was likely attributed to the downregulation of demethylase genes. By systematically analyzing differentially methylated regions (DMRs), integrated multi-omics analysis deciphered epigenetically modulated fiber-related genes, dissected epigenetic-transcriptional crosstalk, and mapped stage-specific regulatory networks. Several key transcription factors that influence fiber development, such as HOX3, HD1, TCP14, and MYB25-like, were found to be associated with DMRs, suggesting potential regulation by DNA methylation. In addition, genes in the fatty acid elongation pathway, such as KCS13 and KCRL1, demonstrated a strong correlation with changes in DNA methylation.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The TM-1 cultivar, cultivated under natural field conditions at Zhejiang A&F University during the 2022 growing season, served as the experimental material. Samples were collected at key fiber development stages: ovules at −3 DPA and 5 DPA, and cotton fibers at 10 DPA. Two biological replicates were collected per time point, resulting in a total of six samples.

Library construction, quality control and NGS sequencing

After evaluating the DNA samples, positive control DNAs were systematically integrated into the existing DNAs, which were then fragmented to sizes between 200 and 400 bp by utilizing the Covaris S220 (Covaris, USA). The subsequent procedure involved ssDNA Bisulfite treatment, ligation of methylation sequencing adapters, size selection, and PCR amplification. The library kit (Accel-NGS Methyl-Seq DNA Library Kit (Swift, USA, Catalog #: 30,096)) was used for Library construction. Subsequently, the quality of the Library was evaluated by Agilent 5400 and sequenced on the Illumina platform (Illumina, USA).

The methylation sequencing data analysis

The methylation sequencing data was filtered using the fastp software (v0.23.2), which produced clean reads [12]. Among these, the WGBS data for 0 DPA ovules and 14 DPA fibers were downloaded from the SRA database (GSE61774). In addition, we generated new WGBS data for − 3, 5, and 10 DPA, each sample was sequenced to approximately 50 × genome depth. The Bismark (v0.23.1) software was used to do short reads alignment [13]. The BatMeth2 software, with the calmeth parameter and settings -c 4 -nC 1, was used to calculate the methylation level of each sample, as well as the methylation levels in different sequence contexts for each sample. To calculate the methylation levels of transposable elements and genes, the methyGff parameter in BatMeth2 software was used with the settings –coverage 4 and -nC 1. The window size for each gene region was set to 2% of the gene region, with a step size of 1% of the gene region. Additionally, the –distance 2000 setting was used to calculate the methylation levels within each gene’s 2000 bp upstream and downstream regions [14].

The methylKit (v1.24.0) package was used to find DMRs between two comparison groups. Parameters were set to -m 4, with sliding window (600 bp) and step size (200 bp). DMRs were identified based on a q-value < 0.05 and meth.diff no less than 20. The genomation (v1.30.0) package was used to annotate the DMRs [15]. Based on the overlap between the DMR and gene regions, DMRs were classified into gene promoter (upstream 2000 bp) region DMRs, exon region DMRs, intron region DMRs, and intergenic region DMRs, in that order of priority. The bedtools with parameter intersect was used to associate DMRs with genes [16]. Combining the transposable elements annotation file, DMRs in the intergenic regions overlapping with transposable elements (TEs) were further annotated as TE region DMRs.

Identification of Transposable elements in the whole genome of G. hirsutum

EDTA (v2.0.1) software was used to identify TEs in the upland cotton genome, employing both de novo and structure-based prediction methods [17]. The insertion times for each full-length LTR were calculated using the parameter -u 3.5e-9. For the LTRs generated by the EDTA software that were unknown and not classified at the superfamily level, we reclassified these unknown LTRs to the superfamily level by using the DeepTE software [18].

Identification of genes regulated by DNA methylation

Based on the classification of genes involved in different methylation pathways, the protein sequences of DNA methyltransferases, demethylases, and RdDM pathway genes from Arabidopsis thaliana were downloaded first. Then these genes were used as query sequences to identify the homologous genes in upland cotton (E-value: 1e-20, sequence similarity > 40%) by using blastp. MAFFT (v7.475) was then used to align the sequences of candidate homologous genes with those of methylation pathway-related genes in Arabidopsis [19]. Subsequently, IQ-TREE (v2.1.2) was employed to construct a phylogenetic tree of these genes, ultimately identifying the genes in upland cotton that belong to the methyltransferase family [20].

Transcriptome sequencing data analysis

This study utilized a total of 13 transcriptome data samples, which were obtained from the National Genomics Data Center (https://db.cngb.org/cnsa/) and the NCBI GEO database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). Among these, the transcriptome data of ovule tissues at −3 DPA, 5 DPA, and 10 DPA fibers were derived from the TM-1 transcriptome (CNP0002656), with three biological replicates for each period [21]. In addition, the ovule tissue at 0 DPA and fiber tissue at 14 DPA were sourced from the TM-1 transcriptome (GSE61774), with two biological replicates for each time point [6].

The HISAT2 was used to align the raw transcriptome sequencing data [22]. The Rsubread (v2.12.3) package was used to calculate the expression levels of transposable elements and genes in each sample [23]. Differential gene expression analysis between each comparison group was performed using Trinity (v2.11.0) software based on DESeq2 parameters [24]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined absolute value of log2(fold change) ≥ 1 and padj < 0.05.

The NGS data visualization

BamCoverage (deepTools) was used to convert the BAM files into BW format files, with data normalization performed using the –normalizeUsing RPKM parameter. The batmeth2_to_bigwig.py (BatMeth2) was used to convert methylation level files into BW format files [14]. Finally, pyGenomeTracks (v3.8) was used to visualize the DMRs and the gene expression levels [25].

Multi-omics analysis of DNA methylation-regulated genes related to the fiber development

To investigate methylation-associated fiber developmental genes, we integrated two distinct datasets: (1) fiber cell specification-related genes (designated as the C3 group) identified through single-cell RNA sequencing analysis [26], and (2) fiber quality-related genes reported in G. hirsutum via transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) [27]. To harmonize these gene sets with the updated G. hirsutum ZJU v2.1 genome, we performed cross-genome alignment. Initially, the longest coding sequences (CDS) of C3 and TWAS-associated genes were extracted from their original HAU v1 genome annotations [28]. These CDS sequences were aligned to the ZJU v2.1 genome using blastn (v2.10.0 +) under strict criteria: ≥ 95% nucleotide identity (-perc_identity 0.95), maximum e-value of 1e-50 (-evalue 1e-50), and ≥ 95% query coverage. Reciprocal alignment analysis was subsequently employed to establish unambiguous cross-version correspondence between HAU v1 and ZJU v2.1 gene models. By retaining all gene pairs meeting alignment thresholds, we finally identified 7,817 C3 genes and 1,693 TWAS-associated genes in ZJU v2.1.

Correlation analysis between DMRs and DEGs

Using 0 DPA as a reference, the methylation levels of DMRs in the gene body and promoter regions at (−3, 5, 10, 14) DPA were calculated. Stage-specific Pearson correlations quantified DMR methylation levels and co-expressed DEG profiles.

RT-PCR valadation

Stage-stratified ovule (−3 to 5 DPA) and fiber (10–14 DPA) samples were collected in triplicate. Total RNA was isolated using the FastPure Universal Plant Kit (Vazyme RC411), followed by gDNA removal and reverse transcription with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme R223-01). qPCR amplification employed Taq Pro SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme Q712-02), with stage-specific gene expression quantified via 2^-ΔΔCt analysis using primers detailed in Table S9.

Results

Features of methylome during cotton fiber development

To study real-time methylation dynamics during cotton fiber development, we constructed a detailed DNA methylation map at single-base resolution for the allotetraploid cotton (G. hirsutum TM-1) across key fiber development stages, including initiation and differentiation (−3 DPA, 0 DPA) and rapid elongation (5 DPA, 10 DPA, 14 DPA). The short reads mapping efficiency varies between 61 and 76%, and the chromosomal coverage of all sample data is between 93 and 96%. The total level of methylation was found to be 21.0868% to 29.7301% and the corresponding methylation rates for CG, CHG, and CHH are from 80.389% to 83.9185%, 62.8947% to 68.809% and 5.9387% to 14.9371% (Supplementary Table S1). Unlike transposable elements (TEs), coding genes along the chromosomes typically exhibit lower levels of DNA methylation. (Fig. 1A-B).

Methylation levels during fiber development rose from −3 DPA to 0 DPA, peaking at 0 DPA, then declined between 0 and 5 DPA. During rapid fiber elongation, methylation levels gradually increased from 5 to 14 DPA, showing a consistent trend in both biological samples. Notably, CHH methylation levels significantly increased at 10, 14 DPA fibers, particularly at 14 DPA, compared to CG and CHG contexts (Fig. 1C). An interesting finding revealed that CHH methylation level varies in the regulatory regions of TEs, as well as the 2,000 bp regulatory regions flanking genes, align with overall genomic methylation dynamics during cotton fiber development (Fig. 1D, E). This suggests that CHH methylation in TE regions and adjacent gene regulatory regions is closely linked to cotton fiber development stages, underscoring its potentially important role in fiber development regulation (Fig. 1D-E).

Down regulation of DNA demethylase genes corresponding to the increased DNA methylation during fiber development

We identified 8 DNA demethylase, 11 de novo DNA methyltransferase, and 65 maintenance DNA methyltransferase genes in the G. hirsutum genome using BLASTP (E-value < 1e-20). Our findings show no significant increase in the transcription levels of de novo methyltransferase or maintenance methylase during cotton fiber development. (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Fig. S3). In G. hirsutum, we identified eight homologous DNA demethylase-related genes, including DME (GH_D01G1377, GH_A01G1303), ROS1 (GH_A04G0840, GH_D04G1160, GH_D09G1532, GH_A09G1535), and DML3 (GH_D05G0850, GH_A05G0856). Concurrent with an increase in DNA methylation, several demethylase genes exhibited significant downregulation (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S1). The expression trends were verified by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Heatmap of demethylase genes’ expression during fiber development

The expression of genes like DRM2 (GH_A05G3768, GH_A09G0315, GH_D04G0578, GH_D09G0322) and AGO4 (GH_A05G1026, GH_A07G1809, GH_A08G2245, GH_D05G1013, GH_D07G1849, GH_D08G2249, GH_D09G1005) that are responsible the RDdM pathway were down regulated in the rapid elongation stage of fiber development (Supplementary Fig. S2). Our findings suggest that elevated DNA methylation during fiber development likely arises from suppressed demethylation-related gene expression rather than RdDM pathway activity.

Identification of DMRs during fiber development

We categorized the DMRs into four comparison groups (−3 DPA, 5 DPA, 10 DPA, 14 DPA versus 0 DPA) to further investigate the variations in DNA methylation during fiber development. Across these groups, we identified 68,375, 84,886, 73,464, and 16,624 hypomethylated DMRs (hypo-DMRs), and 53,233, 54,717, 65,940, and 38,047 hypermethylated DMRs (hyper-DMRs), respectively (Fig. 3A). Among them, the At subgenome had more DMRs than the Dt (Supplementary Fig. S4A-B). In all stages of fiber growth within the CG and CHG contexts, hypo-DMRs outnumbered hyper-DMRs (Fig. 3A). However, during the rapid elongation phase, CHH-DMRs Shifted significantly, with hyper-DMRs markedly more common than hypo-DMRs at 10 DPA and 14 DPA (Fig. 3A).

Analysis of DMR characteristics at each period of fiber development. A Number of differentially methylated regions (DMRs) at each fiber‑development stage relative to 0 DPA (control). B Methylation levels of DMRs at each period of fiber development

Genomic distribution analysis of DMRs during fiber development revealed 13.5–18.3% localized to promoters and 5.8–22.9% to gene bodies (exons and introns) across developmental stages (Fig. S5). Pronounced enrichment was observed in 5ʹ and 3ʹ flanking regions relative to gene bodies, with minimal overlap detected at transcription start sites (TSS) (Fig. 3B). This pattern indicates that during fiber development, the regulatory sequences flanking genes are more likely to undergo differential methylation, while TSSs are less susceptible to changes in methylation.

Identification of the fiber development related genes associated with DNA methylation

Initially we analyzed differentially methylated regions (DMRs) across the following comparative groups: 0 DPA vs. −3 DPA, 5 DPA vs. 0 DPA, 10 DPA vs. 5 DPA, and 14 DPA vs. 10 DPA. We identified 53,233, 84,886, 26,399, and 57,239 hypo-DMRs, and 68,375, 54,717, 44,112, and 74,255 hyper-DMRs in these groups (Supplement Table S2). Similarly, transcriptome analysis revealed 23,800, 24,980, 5,950, and 11,886 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across the four comparison groups (Supplementary Table S3).

Differential methylation analysis identified 348, 401, 154, and 487 differentially methylated genes (DMGs), along with 370, 386, 78, and 555 genes harboring differentially methylated promoters (DMPs), in the −3 DPA vs 0 DPA, 0 DPA vs 5 DPA, 5 DPA vs 10 DPA, and 10 DPA vs 14 DPA comparisons, respectively (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table S4 and S5). During fiber cell differentiation, 515 protein-coding genes showed synchronized DNA methylation remodeling and expression reprogramming, suggesting stage-specific epigenetic regulation. KEGG analysis indicated significant enrichment of these genes in pathways associated with oxidative phosphorylation, RNA polymerase, ribosome biogenesis, photosynthesis, peroxisome function, fatty acid biosynthesis, and carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms (Fig. 4B). Additionally, we identified signal transduction-related genes, including hormone-responsive transcripts such as Auxin-responsive protein (GH_A07G1658), Auxin response factor (GH_A01G1096), Auxin efflux carrier family protein (GH_A07G0831), and Ethylene-responsive transcription factor (GH_A01G0804). Several transcription factors implicated in cotton fiber development were identified, including the WRKY (GH_A08G1141) and bHLH transcription factor (GH_A05G1508).

Considering the overall trend in methylation level changes during fiber development, an increase from −3 DPA to 0 DPA, a decrease from 0 to 5 DPA, and a subsequent increase from 5 to 14 DPA, we focused on hyper-DMRs at 0 DPA, hypo-DMRs at 5 DPA, hyper-DMRs at 10 DPA, and hyper-DMRs at 14 DPA. Our investigation emphasized DMRs located in promoter regions and gene bodies, while also exploring their related DEGs. At 0 and 5DPA, DMR-linked DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways like inositol phosphate metabolism, phosphatidylinositol signaling, glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor biosynthesis, and fatty acid metabolism, highlighting the critical role of cell membrane dynamics and phosphatidylinositol signaling in cotton fiber development, which are regulated by DNA methylation. (Fig. 4C-D). At 10 DPA, DEGs linked to hyper-DMRs were notably enriched in pathways including amino and nucleotide sugar metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and steroid biosynthesis, including brassinosteroid biosynthesis (Fig. 4E). At 14 DPA, hyper-DMR-associated DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways like inositol phosphate metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, steroid biosynthesis (including brassinosteroid biosynthesis), MAPK signaling, as well as energy generation pathways related to pyruvate and TCA cycle metabolism (Fig. 4F). Importantly, within the plant hormone signal transduction pathway, we identified 25 significantly down-regulated genes containing hyper-DMRs, which may affect the biosynthesis of plant hormones such as ethylene and brassinosteroids (Supplementary Table S6).

DNA methylation is dynamically associated with key transcription factors governing cotton fiber development. GhTCP14 (GH_A11G0331), a key auxin-associated transcription factor, exhibited 5 DPA-specific upregulation coupled with promoter hyper-DMRs. (Fig. 5A). Similarly, GhHD1 (GH_A06G1710), essential for fiber initiation, exhibited a significant increase in expression at 0 DPA, with hyper-DMRs present in the exon regions of its gene body (Fig. 5B). Additionally, GhMYB25-like (GH_D12G1824), a key transcription factor for fiber initiation, demonstrated a significant rise in expression at 0 DPA, alongside hypo-DMRs in its promoter region (Fig. 5C). GhHOX3 (GH_D12G2952), known to promote fiber elongation during upland cotton fiber development, also showed a significant increase in expression at 0 DPA, with a CG context hyper-DMR identified in its gene body (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Table S7).

Integrated multi-omics analysis of DNA methylation-regulated genes in cotton fiber development

Previous studies utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have demonstrated that the C3 cell cluster potentially plays a critical role in regulating fiber cell initiation [26]. Developmental stage-specific analysis revealed conserved CHH methylation in C3 gene-proximal regulatory regions (2,000 bp), mirroring genome-wide patterns (Fig. 6A), implicating methylation regulation of fiber development. During the fiber initiation stage (−3 DPA to 0 DPA), we identified 1,230 differentially expressed C3 genes (912 upregulated and 318 downregulated) with high expression levels, which overlapped with 2,491 DMRs (1,397 hyper-DMRs and 1,094 hypo-DMRs) in their body and promoter regions. Complex heatmap analysis revealed a preferential distribution of DMRs, with promoter regions exhibiting a higher abundance compared to exonic and intronic regions (Fig. 6B). C3 gene-associated DMRs showed KEGG enrichment in ribosomal, fatty acid/amino acid metabolic, and lipid barrier biosynthetic pathways (Fig. 6C). We subsequently analyzed the impact of DNA methylation on fiber quality genes. In a prior study, You et al. (2023) identified fiber quality-related genes in G. hirsutum through transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) [27]. We identified 629 fiber quality-associated genes with developmentally regulated DMRs in their promoter and gene body regions, showing KEGG enrichment in fatty acid and linoleic acid metabolism (Fig. 6D).

Methylation-correlated gene networks during fiber development

DMR-DEG pairs exhibiting an absolute Pearson correlation coefficient (absolute Pearson’s r > 0.8) between methylation and expression levels across developmental stages were identified, yielding 13,137 significant associations. We propose that these highly correlated genes are consistently influenced by methylation changes across all stages of fiber development. KEGG analysis revealed these genes enriched in pathways such as nucleocytoplasmic transport, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and fatty acid elongation. Notably, the fatty acid elongation pathway is critical for regulating fiber development (Fig. 7A). Within this pathway, we identified 13 genes, including eight from the KCS (3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase) family. Among these, KCRL1 (GH_A03G1518, annotated as KCR1) and KCS13 (GH_D02G1843, annotated as KCS6) [29, 30], known to promote fiber elongation, exhibited a high correlation between their expression and the methylation levels of their promoter region DMRs, underscoring their potential key roles in fiber development (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Table S8, Supplementary Fig. S6).

Discussion

WGBS-based methylome profiling during G. hirsutum fiber development reveals early-stage hypermethylation in ovules (−3 to 0 DPA), suggesting DNA methylation's critical regulatory role. After early fiber initiation, methylation levels significantly decrease (Fig. 1C), in agreement with the dot blot assay findings reported by Chen et al. (2022). We observed that CHH methylation significantly increases during fiber elongation compared to the initiation stage. This phenomenon has also been documented in other species, including G. barbadense during fiber development, Pyrus bretschneideri and Citrus sinensis during maturation, and Arachis hypogaea L. and Glycine max during seed development [10, 31–34]. Therefore, we speculate that the rise in CHH methylation levels may represent a conserved trend in plant growth and development, potentially playing a critical role in the fiber development of G. hirsutum.

Emerging evidence suggests that genome-wide DNA methylation changes during cotton fiber development may be driven by diverse regulatory mechanisms. Previous research by Song et al. (2015) indicated that the heightened DNA methylation levels in G. hirsutum fibers could be linked to the upregulation of GhCMT2 and the downregulation of HISTONE H1, which enhances CHH site methylation in transposable elements.[6]. In sea island cotton, the rise in DNA methylation during fiber development is largely regulated by H3K9me2-dependent mechanism [10]. In G. hirsutum fiber development, increased DNA methylation correlates negatively with demethylase gene expression and appears primarily due to reduced expression of these genes. Supported by the earlier findings, silencing the ROS1 gene has been shown to significantly inhibit fiber growth through increased DNA methylation levels, thereby highlighting the essential function of demethylase genes in fiber development [8]. Similarly, in other plants and crops, the increase in DNA methylation during seed maturation in A. hypogaea is attributed to reduced expression of demethylase genes, such as ROS1 and DML [33]. In C. sinensis, this phenomenon is associated with decreased expression of DNA demethylase genes, including CsDME, CsDML1, CsDML3, and CsDML4 [32] During the maturation of P. bretschneideri fruit, the persistent increase in DNA methylation levels is connected to a decrease in expression of PbDME1.4, PbDME1.5, PbROS1.1, PbROS1.2, and PbROS1.3 [31]. In S. lycopersicum, knockout of the SlDML2 gene resulted in genome-wide methylation increases that inhibited genes responsible for fruit ripening, ultimately disrupting proper ripening [35]. Collectively, these studies indicate that demethylase genes are pivotal in the regulation of DNA methylation during the growth and development of plants.

Our study revealed complex dynamics in the relationship between DNA methylation and gene expression. While DNA methylation is commonly regarded as a marker of transcriptional repression, hypermethylation can sometimes promote gene transcription [36]. For example, in S. lycopersicum, hypermethylation activates numerous genes that inhibit fruit ripening, a mechanism also observed in the maturation of C. sinensis fruits [32, 35]. In our study on G. hirsutum fiber development, we found that the expression levels of thousands of genes correlated with the methylation levels of their associated DMRs (Supplementary Table. S7). Notably, DNA methylation patterns are associated with the expression of key transcription factors in cotton fiber development, showing a positive correlation with TCP14, HD1, and HOX3, and a negative correlation with GhMYB25-like (Fig. 5A-D). Specifically, GhTCP14, which promotes fiber elongation by activating auxin response genes such as IAA3 and AUXIN1, showed hyper-DMR and increased expression at 5 DPA, suggesting that promoter methylation enhances its expression [37]. GhHD1, known to initiate fiber development, exhibited hyper-DMR and elevated expression at 0 DPA, indicating that exon methylation may boost its transcription [38]. Conversely, silencing GhMYB25-like results in fiberless seeds; at 0 DPA, its promoter displayed hypo-DMR with increased expression, indicating negative regulation by methylation [39]. Additionally, GhHOX3, critical for fiber elongation, demonstrated hyper-DMR with significantly increased expression at 0 DPA, suggesting that increased methylation enhances its expression and impacts fiber development [40].

Multi-omics analysis has revealed that genes associated with fatty acid synthesis and metabolism are highly sensitive to regulation by methylation (Fig. 6C, Fig. 6D). Furthermore, an investigation into genes exhibiting an absolute correlation coefficient greater than 0.8 between their expression and DMR methylation levels throughout fiber development identified significant enrichment in the fatty acid elongation pathway, as shown by KEGG analysis (Fig. 7A). Previous studies have highlighted that the biosynthesis and elongation of fatty acids are critical biochemical pathways that are upregulated during the elongation of cotton fiber cells [41]. Our results imply that the process of DNA methylation might affect fiber development by influencing the expression levels of genes related to fatty acid elongation, particularly eight KCS family genes that were regulated by DMRs. The enzyme known as 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase (KCS) is responsible for catalyzing the initial production of β-ketoacyl-CoA, which is a crucial step in the biosynthesis of very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) [42]. Meanwhile, 3-ketoacyl-CoA reductase (KCR) facilitates the conversion of 3-ketoacyl-CoA to 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA [43]. KCS13 and KCRL1 genes have been demonstrated to promote fiber elongation [29, 30]. In Our study, at 10 DPA, a hypo-DMR in the CHG context within the promoter region may negatively regulate KCS13, while a hyper-DMR in the CHH context at 14 DPA may positively regulate KCRL1 (Fig. 7B). Prior research on upland cotton fiber development has indicated that changes in CHH methylation may affect KCS13 expression [8]. Similarly, studies on G. barbadense fiber development found that a hypo-DMR at 10 DPA correlates with increased expression of KCS family genes such as KCS6 [10]. Taken together, these collective insights imply that changes in DNA methylation during fiber development play a significant role in the regulation of KCS family gene expression, thereby contributing to fiber development. While this study reveals correlative relationships, the mechanistic basis linking DNA methylation to KCS gene regulation or transcription factor activity remains unestablished. Future work will employ targeted demethylase editing/overexpression to systematically determine methylation’s causal role in fiber development pathways and associated phenotypic outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, this study elucidates the remodeling role of DNA methylation in the development of upland cotton fibers (Fig. 8). The “DNA methylation remodeling” refers to the dynamic changes in methylation levels (particularly the significant increase in CHH context during fiber elongation) and their potential functional consequences on gene expression networks, including key pathways such as fatty acid elongation, hormone signaling, and transcriptional regulation of fiber-related TFs.

A proposed model of DNA methylation affects genes and pathways in fiber development of G. hirsutum

Our results suggest that elevated DNA methylation levels may stem from reduced expression of demethylase genes, including DME, ROS1, and DML3. Furthermore, the expression of key transcription factors such as TCP14, HD1, HOX3, and MYB25-like is correlated with DNA methylation patterns, hinting at a potential regulatory relationship. By integrating TWAS and single-cell sequencing data, the research highlights that DNA methylation may regulate genes associated with fiber development, particularly those involved in fatty acid biosynthesis and metabolism. These findings offer new insights into the regulatory mechanisms of DNA methylation in upland cotton fiber development.
