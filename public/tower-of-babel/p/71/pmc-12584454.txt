Association between breastfeeding and autism spectrum condition in Saudi Arabia: a case-control study
Authors: Modia Batterjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutism spectrum condition (ASC) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent difficulties in social communication and repetitive behaviors. Emerging evidence suggests that early nutrition, particularly reduced breastfeeding exposure, may be associated with an increased risk of ASC. However, this evidence is limited, especially in non-Western populations.MethodsWe conducted an unmatched case-control study in Saudi Arabia to investigate the association between breastfeeding practices and ASC. Data were collected through an online questionnaire from mothers of children with and without ASC. Cases were children with a confirmed ASC diagnosis, and controls were neurotypical children without a diagnosis of ASC. Breastfeeding exposure was categorized according to the World Health Organization definitions (from highest to lowest exposure) as follows: exclusive, predominant, partial, or no breastfeeding. Logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsBetween 1 October 2024 and 25 January 2025, a total of 283 participants (126 cases and 157 controls) were recruited. A dose-response relationship was observed, where decreased breastfeeding exposure was associated with progressively higher odds of ASC (OR for trend: 1.58; 95% CI 1.24, 2.01). In univariable analyses, using exclusive breastfeeding as the reference category, partial breastfeeding was associated with increased odds of ASC (OR: 2.49; 95% CI 1.40, 4.42). Similarly, children who were not breastfed had significantly higher chance of ASC than the reference category (OR: 3.46; 95% CI 1.47, 8.13). The strength of these associations was attenuated after multivariable adjustment but remained statistically significant (OR: 2.28; 95% CI 1.22, 4.25 for partial versus exclusive breastfeeding and OR: 2.86; 95% CI 1.12, 7.26 for no breastfeeding versus exclusive breastfeeding).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that reduced breastfeeding exposure is associated with increased odds of ASC. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously, considering the inherent limitation of case-control studies and the potential of reverse causality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13006-025-00777-7.Keywords: Autism, Breastfeeding, Risk, Nutrition, Infant

Background

Autism spectrum condition (ASC) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by impairments in social communication, and the manifestation of restricted interests and repetitive behaviours [1, 2]. The clinical presentation of ASC can vary significantly among individuals, ranging from mild to severe, and can be influenced by parental support and early interventions [1, 2]. Findings from longitudinal studies suggest that the severity of ASC may also show time trends within individuals, with a significant proportion experiencing a decrease in severity over time [3].

ASC is recognized as a multifactorial condition with multiple genetic variants and environmental factors potentially associated with its development. For example, a meta-epidemiological analysis of 28 systematic reviews and meta-analyses identified 12 genetic variations strongly associated with ASC [4]. Among non-genetic factors, advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years), chronic and gestational hypertension, overweight before pregnancy, pre-eclampsia, and antidepressant use during pregnancy have shown robust associations with an increased risk of ASC [5]. A few observational studies suggest that insufficient breastfeeding may also contribute to the development of ASC. However, the evidence remains inconclusive [6] due to the limited number of studies available [7].

Recent global estimates indicate that more than 62 million people may live with ASC [8]. The global age-standardized prevalence of autism is notably higher in males, with approximately 1,065 males with ASC per 100,000, compared to 508 females with ASC per 100,000 [8]. Alongside this, the economic burden of ASC is substantial, with the lifetime cost of care for a person with ASC and intellectual disability in the US or UK being estimated around 2.2–2.4 million USD [9]. Of note, the incidence and prevalence of ASC in Saudi Arabia may be lower compared to global estimates [10]. While several reasons could explain this discrepancy, it presents an opportunity to better understand the unique contributing factors within the region, particularly regarding early-life nutrition, including breastfeeding practices [11]. To this end, we conducted a case-control study in Saudi Arabia to investigate the potential association between breastfeeding practices and the ASC development. By focusing on this specific environmental factor, this study aims to contribute valuable insights into the role of early-life nutrition in the development of ASC, with particular attention to cultural and regional practices in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study design

This was an investigator-initiated, unmatched, case-control study. Participants were recruited from 10 centers (Additional file 1). We screened cases and controls through a review of medical records and reports, and direct referrals made by administrators and physicians. Mothers were then sent a link to complete the online survey, which was administered via the Google Forms platform. Electronic responses were anonymized using randomly generated unique identifiers. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the institutional review board of the Ethics Committee at the Saudi German Hospital (H-06-KM-103). All participants provided electronic consent and were informed that they could discontinue their participation in the study at any time. Participation was voluntary and no financial incentives were offered. We reported results following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines [12].

Setting

The setting encompassed both urban and rural areas in the Makkah province, the third largest province in Saudi Arabia. Mothers of children with ASC or neurotypical children were eligible to participate, provided that they had internet access for data collection. Online questionnaires were developed and piloted in Arabic and were accessible from 1 October 2024 to 25 January 2025. Purposive and snowball sampling approaches were used for recruitment by reviewing medical records at Albidayah Breastfeeding and Women’s Health Awareness Non-profit Organization and the Saudi German Hospital, as well as through referrals from already enrolled participants. Mothers of cases and controls were invited via text messages and provided with online study information before completing the questionnaire upon consent.

Participants

To be eligible for inclusion, participants had to be at least 2 years old when their mothers completed the online questionnaire. There were no restrictions regarding the age of mothers, ethnicity/race of participants, or any other socio-clinical demographic variables. When feasible, a case was defined as a child with a confirmed diagnosis of ASC according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-V) criteria, as verified through available medical records. To identify cases, we also incorporated maternal self-reports. Specifically, children from mothers who responded “Yes” to the online questionnaire item “Are you the mother of a child with a formal diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder?” were classified as cases. A control was defined as a neurotypical child without a history of learning and psychiatric abnormalities. When feasible, controls were systematically screened for potential undiagnosed ASC through a comprehensive review of health records and medical reports. However, we also identified controls via maternal self-reports. Children from mothers who responded “No” to the online questionnaire item “Are you the mother of a child with a formal diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder?” were classified as controls. In families with children diagnosed with ASC, mothers were instructed to provide clinical and behavioral information specific to the child with ASC. Conversely, in families comprising exclusively neurotypical children, mothers were asked to report on their youngest neurotypical child to minimize memory bias.

Variables

After obtaining consent, mothers responded to a structured questionnaire to gather information on demographic characteristics, infant nutrition, breastfeeding history, socioeconomic status, and family history of ASC. Family income was recorded in Saudi riyals (SAR) per year and categorized as low (less than 75,000 SAR/year), lower-middle (75,000 to 125,000 SAR/year), upper-middle (125,001 to 200,000 SAR/year), or high (more than 200,000 SAR/year). We also collected data on smoking status during pregnancy and key clinical factors potentially associated with ASC in the literature [1, 5], such as arterial hypertension, pregnancy-related hypertension, autoimmune disease history, use of paracetamol (acetaminophen), preterm birth, neonatal intensive care unit admission, and the type of delivery (vaginal or cesarean). Mothers were asked to self-classify their body weight status during pregnancy into one of four categories: underweight (body mass index [BMI] < 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (BMI: 18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (BMI: 25.0–29.9 kg/m²), or obese (BMI: ≥30.0 kg/m²).

We defined exclusive breastfeeding according to the World Health Organization criteria when “the infant has received only breast milk from his/her mother or a wet nurse, or expressed breast milk, and no other liquids or solids, except drops or syrups consisting of vitamins, mineral supplements or medicines” [13]. Additional file 1 provides the English version of the questionnaire used to capture breastfeeding exposure. Predominant breastfeeding was defined as the infant’s predominant source of nourishment has been breast milk (including expressed breast milk or from a wet nurse as the predominant source of nourishment). In addition, the infant may also have received liquids (such as water and water-based drinks, or fruit juice), or local ritual fluids, including drops or syrups containing vitamins, mineral supplements, or medicines [14]. Partial breastfeeding was also defined according to the WHO criteria as “a situation where the baby is receiving some breastfeeds but is also being given other food or food-based fluids, such as formula situ milk or weaning foods. Sometimes referred to as mixed feeding.” No breastfeeding was defined as infant’s main and only source of nourishment was through artificial formula milk, a breast milk substitute.

Data sources/measurement

Data for the study were collected via an online structured questionnaire completed by mothers. Therefore, the data collection procedure was identical for both cases and controls.

Study size

We determined the sample size to give the study 80% power to detect an association if it truly exists. We assumed an unmatched case-control design and used previous studies performed in Saudi Arabia, showing that the proportion of non-breastfed children in the control group would be approximately 30% [15]. Besides, considering a recent dose-response meta-analysis [6], we predicted that non-breastfed children would be associated with a two-fold increase in the odds of autism compared to children who had any type of breastfeeding (complete or partial). We calculated the required sample size for a two-sample proportions test, assuming a two-sided alpha of 5%. Based on the parameters and assumptions above, a total of 282 participants (141 cases and 141 controls) would be required to achieve 80% power. The final sample size was increased to 314 participants (157 cases and 157 controls) to account for a potential 10% rate of missing data or exclusions.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation, SD). Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). To test for between-group differences in sociodemographic variables, we used Pearson’s chi-square tests for categorical variables. For continuous variables with a normal distribution, we applied unpaired Student’s t-tests. To assess the association between relevant variables and the odds of ASC, we used unconditional logistic regression models. First, we fitted univariable models to examine the association between ASC and each clinical predictor individually. Next, we fitted a multivariable logistic regression model including all clinical predictors. We also developed a more parsimonious model incorporating both sociodemographic and clinical variables. Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) were used to inform the multivariable model, and the minimal sufficient adjustment set was identified to estimate the direct effect of breastfeeding exposure on ASC [16]. Associations were summarized as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). We did not apply correction for multiple testing due to the exploratory nature of our investigation. Two-sided P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All calculations were performed in Stata (version 18, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Overall, 327 mothers were invited to participate. Figure 1 outlines the study selection process. A total of 283 participants were included in the study: 126 cases and 157 controls.

Description of the study population

The overall mean (SD) age of mothers at the time of their child’s birth was 29.7 (5.9) years, while the corresponding mean (SD) age for fathers was 34.7 (7.1) years. Cases and controls were similar regarding parents’ age, residential setting, maternal marital status, maternal BMI, and maternal working status during pregnancy. However, the two groups differed substantially in other sociodemographic characteristics. For example, case participants were more likely to come from families with lower education levels, lower incomes, and be of Asian descent. Association between breastfeeding and autism spectrum condition

Table 2 presents the univariable and multivariable analyses examining the associations between categories of breastfeeding and autism spectrum condition. Overall, cases were more likely to have been partially breastfed or not breastfed at all. For instance, a markedly higher proportion of cases (19 of 126, 15.1%) had not been breastfed, compared to controls (12 of 157, 7.6%), resulting in a crude OR of 3.46 (95% CI 1.47, 8.13). Univariable analysis further revealed a significant association between partial breastfeeding and ASC (OR: 2.49; 95% CI 1.40, 4.42). A linear trend analysis demonstrated a dose-response relationship, indicating that categories with decreased breastfeeding exposure were associated with significantly increased odds of ASC (crude OR for trend: 1.58; 95% CI 1.24, 2.01).

The multivariable linear trend test further corroborated a dose-response association, with categories of reduced breastfeeding exposure still associated with an increased chance of ASC (OR for trend: 1.52; 95% CI 1.13, 2.03).

Other associations with autism spectrum disorder

Our results showed strong associations between male sex (OR: 4.55; 95% CI 2.61, 7.94) and family history of ASC (OR: 12.1; 95% CI 3.54, 41.2). We also observed suggestive associations between the number of previous siblings and lower odds of ASC in subsequent children. The odds of ASC were significantly lower in families with 1 prior child (OR: 0.43; 95% CI 0.23, 0.80), 2 prior children (OR: 0.42; 95% CI 0.20, 0.87) or 3 prior children (OR: 0.33; 95% CI 0.15, 0.75) (Table 2).

Discussion

Principal findings

In this unmatched, case-control study investigating 126 cases and 157 controls from Saudi Arabia, reduced exposure to breastfeeding was significantly associated with increased odds of autism spectrum condition. We found suggestive evidence for a dose-response relationship, in which decreasing levels of breastfeeding exposure were associated with progressively higher odds of ASC. The results remained robust across different adjustment levels for potential residual confounding. Altogether, our findings corroborate the growing body of evidence supporting reduced breastfeeding as a risk factor for the development of autism spectrum condition, and can potentially inform new (and reinforce existing) targeted breastfeeding practices and ASC prevention strategies in Saudi Arabia and globally.

Comparison with previous studies

To our knowledge, this study is the largest case-control study investigating the association between breastfeeding exposure and the odds of ASC in a Saudi population. Aloufi et al. (2022) conducted a community-based case-control study in Saudi Arabia during the COVID-19 pandemic (August-September, 2021) [17]. The study used a convenience sampling approach, enrolling 102 children with ASC from various regions of Saudi Arabia and 100 matched neurotypical children. The authors reported that children with ASC were significantly more likely to have never been breastfed than their neurotypical counterparts (34.2% vs. 22%). Our findings are consistent with those of Aloufi et al. [17] but extend their observations by demonstrating a more evident dose-response relationship between reduced breastfeeding exposure and increased odds of ASC in Saudi Arabia.

The association between breastfeeding exposure and ASC has been assessed in other populations worldwide. For instance, Ghozy et al. (2020) performed a meta-analysis of observational studies, incorporating data from seven investigations from the United States, India, Turkey, Japan, Indonesia, and Oman [6]. When combined, the evidence revealed that children who were never breastfed were associated with 2.4 times higher odds of developing ASC than those who were exposed to some breastfeeding (OR: 2.38; 95% CI 1.75, 3.33) [6]. Our findings align closely with these estimates. Although our findings are not definitive and require further investigation, they contribute to the growing body of evidence suggesting a consistent association between breastfeeding exposure and ASC across different cultural, geographical, and socioeconomic contexts [6, 7, 18].

It is important to highlight that our results do not imply causation. The temporality of events cannot be established in a case-control study design such as ours. An alternative explanation is that reduced breastfeeding exposure is not the cause of ASC, but rather a consequence of ASC. For example, some children who are later diagnosed with ASC already exhibit early developmental or behavioral difficulties [19, 20]. These early difficulties may include challenges with breastfeeding, such as problems with latching and other feeding-related behaviors that affect breastfeeding negatively [21]. Thus, reduced breastfeeding may not represent a causal factor in the development of ASC, but rather an early marker or manifestation of underlying neurodevelopmental differences. Prospective cohort studies following well-characterized children from birth, with detailed monitoring of feeding and neurodevelopmental indices, will be essential to clarify the directionality and potential causality of our observed associations.

Our findings also align with existing literature on non-breastfeeding risk factors for ASC, confirming previous associations between male sex, family history of ASC, and maternal history of ASC with increased odds of ASC [22, 23]. For example, recently, Sandin et al. (2024), in a large cohort study of Swedish children [24], reported that 8128 of 538,283 males and 4098 of 509,366 females were diagnosed with ASC, yielding a crude odds ratio of 1.89 (95% CI 1.82, 1.96). The study also found significant differences in heritability (87% for males versus 75.7% for females), highlighting the role of genetic and sex effects in ASC [24]. Our crude odds ratio of 4.55 (95% CI 2.61, 7.94) for males versus females closely corroborates these findings. Interestingly, our analysis revealed suggestive associations between higher family income and maternal education with lower odds of ASC. The relationship between family income and maternal education has been a subject of considerable debate, particularly in the context of underdiagnosis and reduced access to health care among socioeconomically disadvantaged families [25]. However, our findings align very well with those reported in studies from China [26], Japan [27], and Sweden [28], for instance, reinforcing the evidence that lower socioeconomic status could be a risk factor for ASC.

Limitations

Our investigation has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, we employed online questionnaires, which could lead to selection bias, potentially favoring participants with higher socioeconomic status [29]. Second, we asked mothers to recall past events, which could have occurred either recently or further back in time, depending on when the child was born. Although we demonstrated that the mean age of mothers at birth was very similar between cases and controls, recall bias remains an inherent limitation of retrospective studies such as ours [30]. Third, our study was conducted in Saudi Arabia only, limiting its generalizability to other populations. Fourth, we were unable to confirm the diagnosis of ASC for all case participants in our study. In our investigation, case classification was based on a combination of medical record reviews, referrals from administrators and physicians, and parental self-reports, all of which are susceptible to information bias and misclassification. However, case participants were recruited from specialized centers, including government-funded nonprofit institutions and schools that require medical documentation and formal ASC diagnoses for enrollment. These institutions have strict screening processes and do not serve individuals without verified diagnoses, reducing our risk of misclassification. For control participants, data were primarily collected via self-report. Given the anonymous nature of the questionnaire, we were unable to confirm that it was completed by the mothers of the intended control individuals, whose neurotypical status had been previously confirmed through our medical record reviews. As prevalence of ASC in the general population is very low, any resulting misclassification would likely be minimal. Although speculative, if present, such misclassification is expected to bias association estimates toward the null and diminish statistical power [31], suggesting that our results could represent conservative estimates of the true underlying association. Fifth, our study was designed to detect large association effects. Consequently, its statistical power to identify small to moderate associations was limited. As a result, we cannot rule out potential associations with ASC, such as those between use of paracetamol (acetaminophen) during pregnancy [32] or small-for-gestational-age [33], which have been previously documented in the literature, but are still in debate [34]. Similarly, our study did not assess the association between breastfeeding exposure and the severity of clinical presentation in children with ACS. For example, recent findings from the ELENA cohort suggest that the relationship between breastfeeding exposure and the onset or severity of ASC remains inconclusive [35]. Further research is needed to clarify this association.

Conclusion

In Saudi populations, reduced breastfeeding exposure was associated with increased odds of autism spectrum condition. Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting exclusive breastfeeding as a public health priority. Future research using large, internationally diverse cohorts with validated ASC diagnostic criteria is warranted to examine this association.
