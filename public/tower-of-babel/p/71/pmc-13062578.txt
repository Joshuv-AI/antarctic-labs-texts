A rare case of caval foramen hernia involving the small intestines and hepatic lobes in a dog
Authors: Yejin JUN, Juhyang PARK, Sang-Kwon LEE, Kija LEE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 6-year-old intact male French Bulldog presented with a 5-day history of vomiting. Radiographs revealed a round soft tissue mass in the right caudal thoracic cavity with tubular structures suggestive of small intestinal herniation through the diaphragm. Ultrasonography confirmed herniation of fluid-filled small intestinal loops and hepatic lobes positioned cranial to the diaphragm. Computed tomography (CT) revealed herniation of the involved organs through a dilated caval foramen and secondary caudal vena cava compression, consistent with Budd–Chiari-like syndrome resulting from venous outflow obstruction. Laparotomy confirmed strangulation of herniated organs, necessitating partial hepatectomy and segmental enterectomy. This case highlights the potential for small intestinal involvement in caval foramen hernia and underscores the diagnostic value of CT in assessing associated vascular compromise.

Caval foramen hernia (CFH) is an uncommon and poorly characterized form of diaphragmatic hernia that has rarely been reported in human and veterinary medicine [7, 8, 11]. It is generally regarded as a congenital variant of diaphragmatic hernia; however, its precise embryological mechanism remains unclear. Several hypotheses have been proposed; these include excessive migration of hepatic cords through the septum transversum during embryonic development, delayed differentiation of hepatocellular precursor cells during the formation of the intrahepatic caudal vena cava (CVC), and developmental anomalies in the structural formation of the septum transversum [14].

As only 12 cases of CFH have been reported in small animals (10 dogs and two cats), the prevalence of CFH among diaphragmatic hernias has not been clearly established [8, 9, 11, 14,15,16]. However, congenital diaphragmatic hernias account for only 5%–10% of all diaphragmatic hernias. Moreover, peritoneopericardial diaphragmatic hernia has been more frequently reported than other congenital diaphragmatic hernias [2, 12]. Therefore, CFH can be considered an exceptionally rare condition. All published CFH reports describe herniation of at least one hepatic lobe, with one feline case also involving the gallbladder and omentum [14]. Among these cases, all but a single feline case were diagnosed using computed tomography (CT). Thus, CT is the primary and most reliable imaging modality for identifying CFH. To the authors’ knowledge, small intestinal herniation through the caval foramen has not been previously reported. The concurrent involvement of the caudate hepatic lobe, an unreported hepatic lobe in CFH, further distinguishes this case from prior reports.

A 6-year-old intact male French Bulldog weighing 6.4 kg was initially evaluated for a 5-day history of postprandial vomiting, lethargy, and hyporexia. No history of trauma or other triggering events was noted. Blood examination revealed marked elevations in the levels of liver enzymes, including alanine aminotransferase (669 U/L; reference range [RR]: 17–78 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (228 U/L; RR: 17–44 U/L), and total bilirubin (1.4 mg/dL; RR: 0.1–0.5 mg/dL). Systemic inflammation was also noted, characterized by neutrophilia (17.36 K/μL; RR: 2.9–11.6 K/μL), increased C-reactive protein levels (158 mg/L; RR: 0–9 mg/L), and hyperglobulinemia (5 g/dL; RR: 1.6–3.7 g/dL). No left shift or toxic changes were observed in the neutrophils.

Thoracic and abdominal radiographs of the dog were acquired (EVS 4343A, DRTECH, Seoul, Korea). Subsequent thoracic and abdominal CT images were acquired using a 16-slice CT scanner (Alexion, Canon Medical Systems). The patient was positioned in sternal recumbency on the CT table under general anesthesia induced with propofol and maintained with isoflurane. The technical parameters of CT were as follows: 120 kV tube voltage, 100 mA tube current, 2 mm slice thickness, and 0.75 sec rotation time. Contrast studies were then performed through the intravenous administration of iohexol (600 mg/kg; Omnipaque 300, GE Healthcare, Cork, Ireland) for 7 sec using an autoinjector (Medrad®, Bayer HealthCare LLC, Whippany, NJ, USA). Dual-phase postcontrast CT images were acquired 40 and 90 sec after injection. CT revealed a 2-cm dilation of the caval foramen, through which the right lateral hepatic lobe and the caudate hepatic lobe, along with multiple jejunal and ileal segments and their associated mesentery and vessels, had herniated into the thoracic cavity. The hernia primarily involved the caudate lobe and a small portion of the right lateral lobe. The herniated hepatic parenchyma showed markedly reduced and heterogeneous enhancement (Hounsfield unit range: 21–69), indicative of vascular compromise and possible necrosis (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the vessels and parenchyma of the non-herniated hepatic lobes exhibited normal contrast enhancement patterns. The herniated intestinal segments exhibited mild, diffuse dilation with fluid accumulation; however, no remarkable mural abnormalities were observed. Moreover, there were no apparent vascular abnormalities suggestive of torsion within the herniated organs. The CVC was severely compressed at the level of the foramen (Fig. 2B, 2C), accompanied by secondary findings, including ascites, pancreatic edema, and mild dilation of portal veins.

Emergency laparotomy was performed immediately after the CT examination. A large amount of serous peritoneal effusion was observed. A diaphragmatic defect at the caval foramen was identified by tracing the herniated hepatic lobes and small intestinal loops extending into the thoracic cavity. No distinct hernia sac was identified; herniated organs passed directly through the caval foramen. An initial attempt was made to reduce the herniated small intestine by gentle caudal traction; however, reduction was not possible due to the large volume of the herniated organs relative to the hernia opening. To facilitate safe reduction and avoid excessive traction, the caval foramen was carefully incised. The herniated small intestine was then gently drawn caudally (Fig. 3AFig. 3.Intraoperative findings. (B) The herniated hepatic lobe and (C) a segment of small intestine after reduction appear dark, congested, and reddish–purple in color, suggestive of strangulation.), followed by reduction of the herniated hepatic lobes into the abdominal cavity. During reduction, particular care was taken to minimize tension on the abdominal organs passing through the hernia ring, especially considering potential adhesion-related traction injury, although the herniated organs within the thoracic cavity were not directly visualized. The reduced tissues, particularly the caudate hepatic lobe and jejunal segments, appeared dark, congested, and reddish–purple, consistent with strangulation (Fig. 3B, 3C). These findings indicated severely compromised tissue viability. Therefore, devitalized jejunal segments were resected, followed by end-to-end anastomosis of the remaining viable intestine using simple interrupted sutures with 4–0 polyglyconate. Subsequently, necrotic hepatic tissue was resected. The caval foramen was then partially closed using simple interrupted sutures with 3–0 polydioxanone, leaving sufficient space (approximately 5–6 mm) to allow passage of the caudal vena cava without compression. During suturing, sutures were placed superficially under direct visualization of the caudal vena cava to minimize the risk of vascular injury and inadvertent narrowing. After partial closure of the caval foramen, the caudal vena cava was grossly evaluated, and no evidence of venous congestion or luminal compression was observed. Abdominal lavage was performed, and before closure of the abdominal wall, a closed active suction drain (Barovac®, Sewoon Medical, Seoul, Korea) was placed in the abdominal cavity through a separate stab incision.

Postoperative thoracic radiographs (Fig. 1C, 1D) confirmed successful reduction of the herniated organs and appropriate positioning of the diaphragmatic repair. Postoperatively, cefazolin (22 mg/kg IV q12 hr) was administered as routine surgical prophylaxis. Because intestinal resection and anastomosis were performed and the resected intestinal segment was severely compromised, additional short-term antimicrobial coverage with marbofloxacin (2.75 mg/kg IV q24 hr) and metronidazole (15 mg/kg IV q12 hr) was prescribed for 5 days to address the risk of enteric contamination and bacterial translocation. Omeprazole (1 mg/kg IV q12 hr) and maropitant (1 mg/kg SC q24 hr) were also administered. On the first postoperative day, the patient experienced a single episode of hematemesis and intermittent hematochezia, which was resolved within 4 days without further complications. The abdominal drain was removed on postoperative day 3, with no recurrence of effusion. Blood examination on postoperative day 5 revealed complete resolution of the previously elevated liver enzyme and total bilirubin levels. The patient remained clinically stable without recurrence of gastrointestinal or abdominal signs during a follow-up period of 2 months.

Given its unique anatomical location, CFH is one of the least frequently documented congenital diaphragmatic defects in small animals. Only 10 cases of CFH have been reported in dogs [8, 11, 15, 16]. Among these, herniation of the right lateral hepatic lobe was identified in seven cases, making it the most commonly affected lobe [8, 11]. The right medial lobe was involved in three cases [8, 15, 16]. Two feline cases have also been reported; the left medial and left lateral hepatic lobes were affected in one case, while the other case involved a broader range of structures, including the cranial portion of the right lateral lobe, right medial lobe, and quadrate lobe, in addition to the entire left medial lobe, gallbladder, and omentum [9, 14]. In dogs, the predominance of right-sided hepatic lobe herniation is likely explained by the anatomical position of the caval foramen, which lies slightly to the right of the midline and adjacent to the right lateral and right medial lobes [8]. However, this anatomical explanation does not fully account for feline presentations, in which both left-sided and extensive multiorgan herniations have been documented, suggesting greater variability in herniation patterns among cats. Notably, neither the caudate lobe nor the small intestine has been previously described as a herniated structure in CFH. However, in the present case, the right lateral lobe, caudate lobe, and small intestine—structures anatomically distant from the caval foramen in dogs—were uniquely herniated. Therefore, when evaluating diaphragmatic hernias with intestinal displacement into the thoracic cavity, clinicians should consider CFH as a differential diagnosis in addition to traumatic rupture.

In patients with CFH, even a small volume of herniated tissue may cause significant compression of the CVC [8]. In the present case, severe compression of the CVC at the level of the caval foramen led to impaired hepatic venous outflow, accompanied by ascites, elevated liver enzyme levels, and CT evidence of postsinusoidal hypertension; all these findings raised suspicion of Budd–Chiari-like syndrome (BCLS). This mechanism is analogous to the inferior vena cava obstruction type described in Ludwig’s classification of Budd–Chiari syndrome in human medicine [5]. BCLS is characterized by impaired hepatic venous outflow due to obstructive lesions of the hepatic veins, hepatic venules, CVC, or right atrium. It is typically associated with secondary portal hypertension and ascites [1]. In veterinary medicine, BCLS associated with diaphragmatic hernias has rarely been reported. Only a single canine case has been reported in the literature. In that case, a congenital hiatal hernia displaced a hepatic lobe, resulting in CVC distortion and subsequent BCLS [1]. Furthermore, in a study of seven dogs with CFH, one dog exhibited severe CVC compression and hepatic venous congestion caused by a small herniated hepatic lobe [8]. Taken together, the clinical findings in the present case suggest that the hepatocellular injury and necrosis were more consistent with local ischemia caused by strangulation at the hernia orifice, rather than being solely attributable to a BCLS. In contrast, marked compression of the CVC by the herniated liver likely resulted in hepatic venous outflow obstruction, which can reasonably be interpreted as a BCLS and provides a plausible explanation for the presence of ascites. Accordingly, the pathophysiology of this case is best interpreted as a dual mechanism involving both strangulation-related ischemia of the herniated organs and secondary hepatic venous outflow obstruction.

CT played a pivotal role in the overall diagnosis and clinical decision-making in the present case. In prior reports on CFH, most cases have been diagnosed using CT, with the exception of a single feline case that was identified during surgical exploration [8, 9, 11, 14,15,16]. In patients with hernia, accurate evaluation of the presence and severity of CVC is essential. In the present case, CT provided a comprehensive visualization of the diaphragmatic defect, delineated the exact organs that were herniated, and most importantly, enabled the direct assessment of severe CVC compression. Moreover, contrast-enhanced CT allowed the evaluation of tissue perfusion by revealing poorly enhanced hepatic regions indicative of vascular compromise and possible necrosis. This information was valuable in preoperative planning, as it provided objective evidence supporting the need for hepatic lobectomy. However, despite preserved enhancement of the herniated intestinal segments on CT, intraoperative findings revealed severely compromised intestinal tissue requiring resection, highlighting the limitations of CT in predicting intestinal viability.

Herniated hepatic lobes often exhibit congestion, which can result in venous outflow obstruction, biliary compression, and jaundice [9]. In a previous study involving traumatic diaphragmatic herniorrhaphy, concurrent procedures were performed in six out of 17 cats and 28 out of 79 dogs; however, hepatic lobectomy was necessary in only one case [10]. This indicates that hepatic lobectomy is rarely required because most herniated hepatic lobes remain viable once normal circulation is restored [11]. Ultimately, the decision to perform hepatic lobectomy or intestinal resection versus simple reduction should be based on intraoperative assessment of tissue viability, including color, peristaltic activity, and mesenteric arterial pulsation [6]. In the present case, intraoperative findings of markedly dark–red, nonviable hepatic and intestinal segments justified resection. Although extensive resection poses inherent risks, retention of necrotic tissue may result in more severe postoperative complications, such as leakage or anastomotic dehiscence [3].

CFH is considered a congenital diaphragmatic defect, with patients often remaining asymptomatic or exhibiting vague, chronic signs; earlier recognition tends to occur in more overt cases and at a younger age [13]. Nevertheless, herniation may still develop or worsen later in life due to trauma or transient increases in intra-abdominal pressure [13]. In contrast to this typical clinical course, the present patient experienced acute vomiting only 5 days before admission, despite being 6 years old. This presentation may reflect previously unnoticed mild signs or a recent unrecognized event, such as trauma or a transient surge in abdominal pressure, which precipitated herniation. Because the present patient was a brachycephalic breed, the potential contribution of increased negative intrathoracic pressure associated with brachycephalic airway obstructive syndrome was considered [4]. However, no abnormal respiratory sounds or other clinical signs suggestive of this condition were observed. Therefore, brachycephalic airway obstructive syndrome was not considered a major predisposing factor in this case. Notably, vomiting resolved immediately after surgery, consistent with prior reports describing the improvement in gastrointestinal signs following CFH repair, further supporting herniation as a contributor to the clinical signs [8]. However, because vomiting and dyspnea are also reported in dogs with herniation of only the hepatic lobe, a direct causal relationship between intestinal herniation and vomiting cannot be confidently established [8, 15].

This case report has several limitations. First, histopathological examination of the resected hepatic and intestinal tissues was not performed; therefore, objective determination of tissue viability was not possible. Consequently, whether the observed lesions represented severe congestion or true necrosis caused by strangulation remains unclear. Intraoperative assessment of organ viability relied exclusively on the gross judgment of the operating surgeon. Second, the etiology of the ascites was not fully characterized. Although the intraoperative appearance of the effusion was serous and the fluid gradually resolved without recurrence, neither cytological nor biochemical analyses were conducted. Given the concurrent imaging findings of CVC compression, mechanical obstruction leading to postsinusoidal portal hypertension was considered the most plausible explanation. Nevertheless, this interpretation should be regarded as tentative, and the precise mechanism underlying the development and resolution of ascites in this patient cannot be definitively established.

In conclusion, this case expands the known spectrum of organs that may herniate through the caval foramen in CFH and further documents that venous outflow obstruction resulting from CFH can lead to BCLS. CT is instrumental in detecting CFH and identifying compression of the CVC, thereby emphasizing the need for rapid surgical intervention to prevent ongoing venous obstruction and consequent hepatic dysfunction.
