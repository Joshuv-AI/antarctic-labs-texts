Cost-Effectiveness of Nirsevimab and Clesrovimab in Preventing Respiratory Syncytial Virus Lower Respiratory Tract Disease in Infants in the United States: A Modeling Study
Authors: Benjamin Yarnoff, Matthieu Beuvelet, Samira Soudani, Maureen P. Neary, Erin N. Hodges, Ayman Chit, Maribel Tribaldos, Veronica Gabriel, Robert Musci, Jeroen Geurtsen, Leonard R. Krilov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the leading cause of infant hospitalizations in the United States, with a significant seasonal impact on healthcare resources. Long-acting monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), nirsevimab and clesrovimab, provide protection against RSV-related lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD); however, no head-to-head comparisons are available to inform clinical decision-making.MethodsWe developed a cost-effectiveness analysis framework to perform an indirect comparison of nirsevimab and clesrovimab for RSV prevention, consistent with CDC guidance for pre-season catch-up and in-season immunization for infants born outside and during the RSV season, respectively. Pharmacokinetic data were used to model the duration of cumulative efficacy over time. A 150-day duration was evaluated for nirsevimab, while three scenarios were assessed for clesrovimab (100-, 120-, and 150-day durations). A cost–utility analysis evaluated health outcomes, healthcare resource use, and associated costs.ResultsThe modeled prediction suggests nirsevimab demonstrated greater clinical benefit and more favorable economic outcomes compared to clesrovimab across all protection duration scenarios. Nirsevimab use consistently resulted in reduced cases of RSV medically attended (MA)-LRTDs and therefore direct and indirect costs associated with RSV management. From a cost-effectiveness perspective, nirsevimab was dominant (i.e., more effective and less costly) compared to clesrovimab in all analyzed scenarios. Additional scenario analyses leveraging trial data differentiated by infant gestational age for both nirsevimab and clesrovimab and alternative RSV seasonality demonstrated that model conclusions remain stable. These analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings, with nirsevimab consistently offering greater value.ConclusionsOur findings predict that nirsevimab is economically dominant over clesrovimab while providing greater clinical protection against RSV-related disease in these scenarios. These results are robust to sensitivity analyses with nirsevimab consistently estimated as reducing healthcare resource utilization and total costs compared to clesrovimab across assumptions related to clesrovimab duration of cumulative efficacy as well as variation in key model parameters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01311-w.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the leading cause of infant hospitalization in the United States, with a significant seasonal impact on healthcare resources and clinical burden.While both nirsevimab and clesrovimab are long-acting monoclonal antibodies approved for RSV prevention in infants in the US, there are key pharmacokinetic differences between the two products. As no comparative randomized clinical trials, observational studies, or modeling studies exist at this time to guide healthcare decision-making, we caution against the notion of a class effect between the two products (i.e., assuming that the two products offer the same level of protection).This study provides the first comparative modeling study to estimate the incremental cost–utility of nirsevimab and clesrovimab for RSV prevention in infants, highlighting the implications of the uncertainty around the duration of protection and cumulative efficacy for clesrovimab. What was learned from the study? Nirsevimab was estimated to provide greater clinical benefit and more favorable economic outcomes compared to clesrovimab, preventing significant numbers of RSV-related health events while also reducing total direct and indirect costs. Thus, nirsevimab was consistently estimated to dominate (i.e., more effective and less costly) compared to clesrovimab.These findings provide healthcare decision-makers with evidence-based insights to potentially help optimize RSV prevention strategies and reduce the clinical and economic burden of RSV in the US infant population.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) remains a significant seasonal health threat, particularly affecting infants and young children by causing lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) most commonly in the form of pneumonia and bronchiolitis [1, 2]. In the United States (US), RSV is the leading cause of infant hospitalizations annually [3, 4], with viral circulation typically beginning in the fall, peaking during the winter months, and subsiding in the spring [5, 6]. The burden of RSV is especially pronounced in infants under 12 months of age, where it contributes significantly to healthcare resource utilization and costs [1, 2, 7, 8].

Historically, prevention of severe RSV in infants has been limited to palivizumab, a monoclonal antibody (mAb) with a half-life of 20 days, which is administered monthly throughout the RSV season [9–11]. This intervention was recommended only for infants most at increased risk of severe RSV disease, specifically infants born prematurely (< 29 weeks gestational age [wGA]) or those with hemodynamically significant congenital heart disease (CHD) or chronic lung disease of prematurity (CLDP) [9, 12]. However, this approach only offers protection to approximately 2% of the infant population, leaving the vast majority of infants unprotected. This approach left a substantial gap in protection, as epidemiological data consistently demonstrate that over 90% of infants hospitalized with RSV and over 93% of infants experiencing outpatient visits related to RSV LRTDs are otherwise healthy and born at full term [1, 7, 9, 13]. This limitation, coupled with the logistical challenges of monthly administration, highlights the need for more comprehensive and accessible preventive strategies.

The RSV prophylaxis landscape transformed dramatically in 2023 with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of both a maternal RSV vaccine [14] and nirsevimab [10, 15]. Maternal vaccination against RSV is recommended for pregnant persons 32–36 weeks of gestation during September to January to offer protection to infants born during the RSV season [16]. Nirsevimab is a long-acting RSV-neutralizing mAb, exhibits a half-life of 71 days, and offers protection to infants for the full length of the RSV season with a single dose [10, 17, 18], reducing the administrative burden of RSV prevention compared to monthly dosing of palivizumab. In the US, nirsevimab is recommended for all infants born during or entering their first RSV season (aged < 8 months) and for children 8–19 months old who are at increased risk for severe RSV disease entering their second RSV season [19, 20]. Clinical trials demonstrated robust efficacy, with significant reductions in medically attended (MA) RSV-related lower respiratory tract infections, including hospitalizations [17, 18, 21, 22]. Subsequent real-world evidence has further validated these findings, supporting the role of nirsevimab in comprehensive RSV prevention [23, 24]. Current recommendations indicate that either maternal RSVpreF vaccination or nirsevimab administration is sufficient for RSV prevention in most infants, with both interventions generally not needed for the same infant [16]. These new prevention strategies allowed a paradigm shift in the protection of infants, expanding access to the broad infant population [15].

The RSV prevention strategy in the US continues to evolve with the development of clesrovimab, a second mAb, which received US FDA approval in June 2025 to help protect infants against RSV LRTD in their first RSV season [25, 26]. Recent analysis by the FDA of the clinical trial data, including time-to-first-event analysis, provides important insights into the durability of protection offered by available RSV preventive options. The FDA Integrated Report for clesrovimab revealed limitations in assessing its duration of protection, as the majority of events (78%) occurred within the first 90 days of follow-up due to a trial design with continued enrollment ending before the estimated peak of the RSV season [27]. This design may not fully reflect the implications of the shorter half-life of clesrovimab (44 days [26] versus 71 days for nirsevimab [15]) on efficacy, particularly for infants weighing ≥ 5 kg, where Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves show similar case accumulation between groups after 45 days post-administration. In contrast, the clinical trials for nirsevimab enrolled participants before the start of the RSV season, allowing assessment across a full season [17, 18, 28]. The KM curves for nirsevimab demonstrate consistent efficacy throughout the full length of the RSV season with clear separation between treatment and placebo groups throughout the follow-up period, and this sustained protection has been further validated in real-world effectiveness studies [17, 18, 22, 29, 30]. Understanding any differences in durability of protection between the two mAbs is particularly important for infants born before the start of the RSV season, who require protection throughout the entire season (potentially as long as 5–6 months, depending on virus circulation). Significant evidence gaps persist regarding the comparative effectiveness of nirsevimab versus clesrovimab, impeding a comprehensive assessment of the options for protecting infants against RSV disease.

Understanding the duration of protection provided by extended half-life mAbs against RSV is crucial for optimizing their clinical application. The persistence of passive immunity through antibodies is influenced by several factors, including dosing and persistence over time, distribution in tissues and serum, half-life extensions, and neutralizing potency [31, 32]. Understanding the pharmacokinetics (PK) of antibodies can serve to inform efficacy extrapolation against RSV disease, though no correlate of protection for RSV has been established to date [33, 34]. Importantly, clinical trial design and the timing of RSV circulation can also significantly impact the evaluation of efficacy for seasonal diseases such as RSV. Studies employing extended enrollment into the RSV season (i.e., clesrovimab) versus those restricting enrollment to the start of the RSV season (i.e., nirsevimab) may be impacted differently by antibody waning. Participants enrolled in the middle of the season will have a limited risk of disease later in the follow-up period when RSV is no longer circulating. Understanding these product and data differences is important to accurately determine the protective window provided by each product and thus optimize dosing strategies for RSV prevention.

A related modeling analysis by several of the same authors previously evaluated the public health impact of RSV prevention strategies for infants in the US, comparing nirsevimab, clesrovimab, and RSVpreF maternal vaccination in terms of MA RSV outcomes and total costs relative to the pre-2023 standard of practice [35]. That analysis was designed to assess population-level disease burden and did not evaluate incremental cost-effectiveness or cost–utility outcomes. The present study is distinct in that it focuses on incremental cost-utility, including quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), to indirectly compare nirsevimab and clesrovimab, which are two monoclonal antibodies indicated for the same infant population and administered under comparable clinical pathways [15, 26]. Maternal RSVpreF vaccination was not included because it targets a different population and timing of intervention, making direct cost–utility comparison methodologically inappropriate.

In this study, we employ a PK modeling approach to evaluate the duration of cumulative efficacy and protection provided by mAbs against RSV MA LRTDs. Our methodology builds on previous work [36], which developed and validated a model-based meta-analysis framework for predicting RSV efficacy based on serum neutralizing antibody titers that demonstrated that antibody concentration profiles could be effectively used to predict clinical protection against RSV disease. Building on this foundation, we utilize comparative antibody serum concentration analyses to estimate the duration of cumulative efficacy and protection for different mAbs to inform a cost-effectiveness analysis. This methodology allows for a standardized comparison of efficacy over time, which is critical for evaluating the relative clinical and economic value of different RSV preventive strategies.

Under this context, our study employs a previously published decision-analytic model [13] to evaluate the outcomes and cost-effectiveness of both nirsevimab and clesrovimab compared to the historical (pre-2023) standard of practice of palivizumab alone for prevention of RSV-LRTD in the US infant population. This analysis incorporates available clinical trial data, real-world evidence, and economic considerations to assess the RSV-related clinical and economic burden associated with each of these prophylactic interventions based on a differential duration of cumulative efficacy and protection estimated through PK modeling for comparing the waning of serum mAb concentrations. This study aimed to assess the impact of clinically relevant differences in nirsevimab and clesrovimab on public health outcomes and the reduction of total RSV burden.

Methods

This study used an economic model developed in Microsoft Excel®. The model has been previously described in detail [13, 35] so here we provide a high-level overview and describe in detail only those elements that have been updated for this analysis, primarily the modeling of efficacy, specifically to model uncertainty in the efficacy data for clesrovimab. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Model Overview

Model Structure

The model presented here is a static decision-analytic model that tracks the entire US infant cohort for one RSV season, compares two immunization strategies, and aggregates the associated RSV-related health outcomes and costs. The US infant cohort is accordingly split into three distinct populations according to differential individual risks of LRTDs and other RSV-related health events as previously described [13] (Supplemental Section S1). Figure S1 illustrates the model structure implemented for this analysis [13]. The model analysis examined one RSV season but also incorporated the lifelong impact of premature deaths during the season.

The model is designed to follow three key dimensions, as shown in Fig. 1. The first dimension is the definition of the RSV season and infant age at the start of the defined season. Infants enter the model in monthly birth cohorts where the first cohort corresponds to infants born in the month immediately after the end of the preceding RSV season. The second dimension is the distribution of RSV cases over the year. As RSV prevention offers a window of protection limited in time, delineation of the distribution of RSV cases throughout the year can inform when protection against infection is most needed for infants. Finally, the third dimension is the rate of RSV-related healthcare resource use by age in months. Intervention Strategies

The analysis involved a pairwise comparison of two implementation strategies, the strategy of interest and the comparative strategy. The comparative strategy was specified as a palivizumab-only strategy (i.e., monthly administration of palivizumab during the RSV season for palivizumab-eligible infants only with a maximum of five doses), as per pre-2023 AAP recommendations [9], and no prophylaxis for the remaining infant populations. The strategy of interest was specified as one of two immunization strategies using an extended half-life mAb: immunization with nirsevimab or clesrovimab for the entire birth cohort, with infants born before the RSV season receiving one dose of nirsevimab or clesrovimab at the start of the RSV season (i.e., beginning of October) and those born within the RSV season receiving one dose at birth following current ACIP guidance [37].

The impact of these modeled interventions was then indirectly compared, leveraging the pre-2023 AAP recommendation scenario as an anchor between the two long-acting mAb strategies. We also present results here for the direct comparison of strategies leveraging nirsevimab or clesrovimab.

Prevention Efficacy

Efficacy of prophylaxis was defined as the reduction of RSV LRTD in either the inpatient or outpatient setting. Efficacy characteristics for clesrovimab were informed by outcomes from the Phase 2b/3 CLEVER trial [38] and US prescribing information [26], while effectiveness characteristics for nirsevimab were informed by US-specific real-world analysis [23] due to its methodological alignment and relevance to the US context. Specifically, enrollment and the definition of hospitalization events were more similar between these studies than the endpoint definitions outlined in the nirsevimab clinical trials [17, 18, 22] (Table 1). Additionally, a US-based CDC study offers the most contextually appropriate and reliable source for modelling impact within the US healthcare setting. For this analysis, it is important to distinguish between cumulative efficacy and protection over time (i.e., durability of efficacy). Cumulative efficacy refers to the overall reduction in disease risk observed in an immunized population compared with an unimmunized population over a predefined period, whereas protection over time refers to how long the preventive intervention continues to provide protection and may include how that protection wanes over time [39].

Mean Cumulative Efficacy

Nirsevimab

The mean cumulative efficacy of nirsevimab was sourced from a prospective population-based surveillance study estimating RSV disease burden and nirsevimab effectiveness in young children, based on The New Vaccine Surveillance Network (NVSN) multi-pathogen enhanced surveillance network led by the CDC to assess pediatric viral respiratory and gastrointestinal infections and vaccine impact in children [40] in the US [23]. Findings from this study represent recent real-world data related to nirsevimab effectiveness (Table 1). Similar efficacy was assumed across the entire US birth cohort.

Under the scenario analysis leveraging stratified trial data by infant subpopulation, efficacy in the palivizumab eligible infant population was informed by the pooled trial analyses and assumed non-inferior with palivizumab [10], the phase 2b trial for the preterm infant population, and the MELODY phase 3 trial for the term infant population [22].

Clesrovimab

The mean cumulative efficacy of clesrovimab was sourced from the primary and secondary endpoints of the phase 2b/3 study [38] evaluating efficacy in preterm and term infants as presented in the clesrovimab USPI [26] (Table 1). As was done for nirsevimab, efficacy was assumed to be similar across the entire US birth cohort. We leveraged data directly from the phase 2b/3 trial for clesrovimab, given this was the only evidence available on the efficacy of clesrovimab at the time of analysis.

Under the scenario analysis leveraging stratified trial data by infant subpopulation, efficacy in the palivizumab eligible infant population is informed by the phase 3 SMART trial [41] and similarly assumed non-inferior with palivizumab as well as stratified results from the phase 2b-3 CLEVER trial for both the preterm and term infant populations [38].

Time Period of Cumulative Efficacy

Nirsevimab

The period of the mean cumulative efficacy of nirsevimab was derived from clinical trials, which consistently demonstrated efficacy to be maintained over 150 days [17, 18], with high efficacy throughout the RSV season based on enrollment ending prior to RSV circulation. This sustained efficacy of nirsevimab through at least 150 days has been further demonstrated outside of controlled clinical studies in the US real-world setting as well as other global real-world evidence studies [29, 30, 43].

Clesrovimab

Per the Phase 2b/3 study protocol [38, 44], enrollment began during the 4 weeks prior to the estimated onset of the RSV season and ended before the estimated peak of the RSV season (approximately 17-week duration in temperate regions during a standard season). Given that the participant enrollment in the CLEVER trial continued to the peak of the RSV season, we attempted to better assess the period over which efficacy was measured for clesrovimab. To accomplish this, we leveraged and compared published pharmacokinetic data for clesrovimab [26, 45, 46] with data from other RSV preventive monoclonal antibodies currently licensed (i.e., palivizumab and nirsevimab). Recommendation on the number of doses needed to provide protection to infants throughout the RSV season has previously been based on antibody serum concentration, half-life (Table S1), and demonstration of duration of protection in randomized control trials (RCTs) [9].

We compared antibody serum concentration decay over time for both nirsevimab and clesrovimab, using a standard one-compartment model with first-order elimination to characterize the serum concentration–time profiles.

Even though no formal correlate of protection for RSV has been established, we assumed that no efficacy remains once the antibody concentration falls below the detection limit (reported to be 0.5 µg/ml for both products). Time to reach the detection limit was estimated through this modeling for both clesrovimab (354 days) and nirsevimab (567 days) in order to inform the time at which efficacy drops to zero, highlighting the quantitative differences in half-life between the two mAbs (Figure S2).

Accordingly, we employed an empirical approach to estimate the minimum protective serum antibody concentration for clesrovimab. The analysis was based on the demonstrated clinical protection against RSV disease conferred by nirsevimab for at least 150 days post-administration, which is the length of a typical RSV season in a temperate climate. The serum concentration of nirsevimab at day 150 (29.4 μg/ml) was utilized as the minimal threshold associated with clinical protection (Fig. S3).

We then calculated the time required for clesrovimab serum concentration to decline to the reference threshold established for nirsevimab (29.4 μg/ml at day 150). This approach conservatively assumes equal potency between the mAbs and thus that similar antibody concentrations result in equivalent efficacy [36].

Based on the comparative antibody serum concentration analyses between nirsevimab and clesrovimab, we assumed a rounded duration of 100 days as the lower-bound for the time point reflecting the mean cumulative efficacy estimates to inform the sigmoid-based efficacy curve for clesrovimab. Economic Evaluation

The model was utilized to assess the cost-effectiveness and public health impact of each of the mAbs from a societal perspective over one RSV season. The societal perspective aligns with ACIP recommendations for economic analysis and includes caregiver productivity costs, QALY losses, and lifetime productivity losses due to premature death, in addition to direct medical costs and QALY losses. The model assumed price parity ($500 per dose based on midpoint between the public and private list prices [49]) and equal coverage for each of the products (82% in the palivizumab-eligible population [50]; 80.4% equally in the preterm and term infant populations [51]). Using the same level of coverage across comparators ensures that cost-effectiveness comparisons do not conflate differences in coverage with differences in efficacy and price. Outcomes assessed included medically attended RSV cases, healthcare resource utilization, QALYs, and ICERs.

Given that no head-to-head clinical data exist comparing the mAbs nirsevimab and clesrovimab, the analyses primarily considered an indirect comparison leveraging the palivizumab-only strategy as an anchor between the two alternative mAb-based strategies. However, results are also presented for a direct comparison between the nirsevimab-based and clesrovimab-based all-infant immunization strategies based on naïve comparison using separate data sources with respect to comparative efficacy.

Sensitivity Analysis

Structural and parameter uncertainty in the model was investigated using two sensitivity analyses.

A one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis (DSA) was performed on all major model inputs by systematically varying individual parameters from the base case to determine the main drivers of the model results. Variances around the base-case were obtained from the 95% CIs derived from the source material or a ± 20% variation was assumed to determine the lower- and upper-bound values.

A probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was performed to account for statistical uncertainties of multiple key parameters. In the PSA, all parameters with uncertainty were varied simultaneously, sampling various input parameters from the appropriate probability distributions.

Recognizing that annual variation in RSV circulation timing may influence immunization impact, we conducted two seasonality scenario analyses in addition to the base case. In these scenarios, RSV activity was assumed to peak 1 month earlier or 1 month later than in the base case, allowing evaluation of how shifts in season timing affect clinical and economic outcomes for nirsevimab and clesrovimab strategies compared with the pre-2023 SoP.

Results

Burden and Costs

Compared to the palivizumab-only strategy, both nirsevimab and clesrovimab demonstrated substantial reductions in RSV-related healthcare utilization over one RSV season, though with varying effectiveness.

All-infant protection with nirsevimab was estimated to provide the most comprehensive protection, reducing RSV LRTDs by 70% (364,202 fewer cases) (Table 3). This included decreases of 73% in hospitalizations (32,404), 69% in emergency room (ER) visits (88,647), 69% in primary care (PC) visits (243,103), and 68% in RSV deaths (50) (Fig. 3). While total direct and indirect costs were estimated to increase by $2.8 million, direct healthcare expenditures declined substantially such that hospitalization costs fell by 72% ($955.3 million), ER visit costs by 69% ($60.4 million), and PC visit costs by 69% ($24.1 million) (Fig. 4). QALYs and Cost-Effectiveness

In comparison to the palivizumab-only strategy, all-infant protection with nirsevimab was estimated to result in 7017 QALYs saved, which reflects an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of $411/QALY saved in the all-infant population (Table 3). Under all-infant protection with clesrovimab (100-day scenario), 3361 QALYs were saved, reflecting an ICER of $113,148/QALY saved while the clesrovimab 120-day scenario was estimated to save 4123 QALYs, equating to an ICER of $64,510/QALY saved. Under clesrovimab (150-day scenario), 5041 QALYs were estimated to be saved with an ICER of $28,237/QALY saved.

In comparison to all-infant protection with nirsevimab, all-infant protection with clesrovimab (100-day, 120-day, and 150-day) was estimated to result in additional QALY loss of 3656, 2894, and 1976, respectively (Table 5). Thus, in addition to estimated increases in total costs across the clesrovimab scenarios, clesrovimab was consistently dominated by nirsevimab. This indicated that nirsevimab was more cost-effective compared to clesrovimab across all assumptions related to the clesrovimab duration of cumulative efficacy.

Number Needed to Immunize

With the introduction of nirsevimab, the estimated NNI to prevent one RSV MA LRTD in any setting was 9 in the entire birth cohort. This compares to an estimated NNI to prevent one RSV MA LRTD in any setting of 17, 14, and 12 for clesrovimab under the 100-day, 120-day, and 150-day scenarios, respectively (Table 3). The estimated NNI to prevent one RSV LRTD hospitalization was 91, 123, 109, 97 for the nirsevimab, clesrovimab (100-day), clesrovimab (120-day), and clesrovimab (150-day) scenarios, respectively.

Sensitivity Analyses

In the DSA, the key drivers of incremental costs for the nirsevimab scenario were the risk of all RSV LRTDs in the term infant population, the nirsevimab net price, the distribution of RSV infections by calendar month, and the efficacy curve for nirsevimab (i.e., the parameters specifying the sigmoid decay function for efficacy over time; Fig. S5a). The key drivers of incremental costs for both the 100-day and 120-day clesrovimab scenarios were the clesrovimab net price, the risk of all RSV LRTDs in the term infant population, the efficacy curve for clesrovimab, and the distribution of RSV infections by calendar month (Fig. S5b, c). For the clesrovimab 150-day scenario, the key drivers of incremental costs were the risk of all RSV LRTDs in the term infant population, the clesrovimab net price, the distribution of RSV infections by calendar month, and the efficacy curve for clesrovimab (Fig. S5d).

Based on the results of 1000 PSA simulations for the nirsevimab scenario, the mean incremental costs increased by $4.8 million with 6861 QALYs saved, resulting in a mean ICER of $689/QALY saved (Fig. S6a), where in 49.2% of the simulations nirsevimab was estimated to be dominant over the pre-2023 SoP (i.e., occur in Quadrant IV of the scatterplot; Fig. S6b). For the clesrovimab scenarios, the mean incremental costs were estimated to increase by $376.7–$144.6 million with 3352–4985 QALYs saved, resulting in a mean ICER of $112,372–$29,009/QALY saved (Fig. S6c, e, g) and 13.6–36.5% of simulations resulting in dominance over the pre-2023 SoP (Fig. S6d, f, h) under the 100-day and 150-day scenarios, respectively.

In scenario analyses evaluating alternative RSV seasonality assumptions, projected outcomes of the analyses were shown to be sensitive to the shifting of RSV circulation 1 month earlier or later relative to the base case. When RSV circulation was shifted 1 month earlier, both nirsevimab and clesrovimab strategies prevented additional RSV cases and saved additional QALYs as a percent change from the pre-2023 SoP strategy compared to the base case (Table S9). However, incremental total costs moved in opposite direction: for nirsevimab, total incremental costs rose slightly (from $2.9 to $29.3 million), while for clesrovimab they decreased (from $142–$380 to $124–$287 million across the three clesrovimab scenarios). As a result, the ICER for nirsevimab increased from $411 to $4285 per QALY saved, while ICERs for clesrovimab improved slightly, ranging from $23,297 to $69,670 per QALY saved in the early-peak scenario.

These directional changes reflect differences in product cumulative efficacy duration and waning patterns relative to RSV circulation. Given that prophylaxis costs remain fixed, small shifts in the number and timing of prevented high-cost inpatient events disproportionately influence total costs. The protection window for nirsevimab overlaps slightly less with the earlier RSV peak, reducing inpatient event and cost avoidance, whereas the estimated clesrovimab efficacy curve under all modeled scenarios aligns more directly with the shifted season.

Delaying RSV circulation by 1 month yielded fewer cases averted, higher incremental costs, and less favorable ICERs for each product (Table S9).

Additionally, direct comparisons showed that nirsevimab continued to dominate clesrovimab under all seasonality scenarios, though differences in outcomes narrowed modestly under the early peak and widened with delayed RSV circulation (Table S13). These findings underscore that the alignment between product duration of cumulative efficacy and RSV seasonality critically affects projected benefits and cost-effectiveness.

Under the RCT-based efficacy scenario, all-infant protection with nirsevimab was projected to decrease total RSV MA-LRTD cases by 60% (70% in the base case), including 61% fewer hospitalizations (73% in the base case), 60% fewer ER visits (69% in the base case), and 58% fewer primary care visits (69%) compared to the pre-2023 SoP (Table S14). This translated to an estimated reduction in QALYs lost of 6031 (7017 in the base case) and an increase in total costs of $206 million ($2.8 million in the base case), with an ICER of $34,147 per QALY saved ($411 in the base case). The number needed to immunize to prevent one RSV LRTD and one hospitalization was estimated at ten (nine in the base case) and 109 (91 in the base case), respectively.

In comparison, protection with clesrovimab under the 100-, 120-, and 150-day duration scenarios resulted in 27–44% fewer RSV MA-LRTD (33–50% in the base case) and 51–65% fewer hospitalizations (54–69% in the base case), with corresponding QALYs saved of 2790–4453 (3361–5041 in base case) compared to the pre-2023 SoP (Table S14). Although hospitalization and outpatient visit costs declined under all clesrovimab scenarios, total costs increased by $240–$480 million relative to the pre-2023 SoP ($142–$380 million in the base case). The ICERs for clesrovimab versus the pre-2023 SoP were estimated at $172,171, $102,093, and $54,034 per QALY saved ($113,148, $64,510, and $28,237 in the base case) for the 100-, 120-, and 150-day assumptions, respectively. Results by month of birth are presented in Table S15.

Direct comparison between the two mAbs demonstrated that nirsevimab was consistently estimated to outperform clesrovimab across all duration scenarios, averting 40–82% more RSV MA-LRTDs and achieving greater reductions in all healthcare settings (Table S16). While clesrovimab incurred 7–12% higher total costs, it also resulted in 38–79% higher QALY loss estimates, indicating that nirsevimab dominated clesrovimab in terms of cost-effectiveness across the three scenarios related to clesrovimab’s duration of cumulative efficacy.

Discussion

The comparative analysis of nirsevimab and the palivizumab-only strategy demonstrated substantial clinical and economic benefits, with the all-infant nirsevimab strategy preventing an additional 364,204 RSV MA cases. This translated into significant reductions in healthcare utilization, including decreases in PC visits (69%), hospitalizations (73%), and ER visits (69%), culminating in direct healthcare cost savings of $955.3 million (72% reduction).

Our model predicts varying degrees of effectiveness for clesrovimab across the different scenarios related to duration of cumulative efficacy when compared to the palivizumab-only strategy. At 100, 120, and 150 days, the all-infant clesrovimab strategy prevented 173,276, 213,427, and 261,358 RSV MA cases, respectively. The corresponding reductions in healthcare utilization ranged from 30 to 48% for ER visits, 32–48% for PC visits, and 54–69% for hospitalizations, with associated healthcare cost savings of $790–970 million (55–67% reduction). Notably, clesrovimab showed substantially lower levels of healthcare resource use aversion for infants born OoS (28–47% reduction in RSV MA cases) compared to nirsevimab (68% reduction), highlighting the considerable impact that the duration of cumulative efficacy and protection for clesrovimab may have on this infant population who are born OoS and receive administration in October when compared with that of nirsevimab.

Direct comparison between nirsevimab and clesrovimab consistently revealed more favorable outcomes for nirsevimab across all scenarios. Nirsevimab prevented an additional 102,849–190,928 RSV MA cases, depending on the alternative assumptions about the time period for the efficacy of clesrovimab. The reduction in healthcare utilization was consistently higher for nirsevimab, with advantages in hospitalizations (16–70%), ER visits (69–128%), and PC visits (68–123%). While the majority of direct cost savings are attributed to averted inpatient events, our analysis demonstrates that the greatest volume of prevented RSV cases and QALY gains occur in the outpatient setting. These outpatient benefits represent substantial clinical value through reduced disease burden and improved quality of life, as well as economic advantages through decreased caregiver productivity losses and prevention of potential disease progression—benefits that extend beyond the direct healthcare costs captured in our primary analysis. These clinical benefits, combined with greater cost savings, establish the economic dominance of nirsevimab over clesrovimab across all analyzed scenarios. In a price sensitivity analysis, the estimated price reduction required for clesrovimab to match the cost-effectiveness of nirsevimab ($411/QALY saved) ranged from 21 to 5%, depending on the assumed duration of cumulative efficacy (100 to 150 days).

Results from the RCT-based scenario analysis confirmed the robustness of the base case findings. When efficacy inputs for both nirsevimab and clesrovimab were derived exclusively from their respective clinical trials and stratified by infant subpopulation, nirsevimab consistently demonstrated greater reductions in RSV-associated disease burden and healthcare utilization, as well as more favorable cost-effectiveness outcomes. These findings were directionally consistent with those from the base-case analysis, reinforcing the stability of conclusions across evidence sources. The selection of the NVSN-based real-world evidence [23] to inform nirsevimab efficacy was appropriate given its methodological and contextual alignment with the clesrovimab trial design, particularly regarding infant enrollment timing, hospitalization definitions, and relevance to the US population. Together, these results suggest that the comparative benefits of nirsevimab are robust to variations in evidence type and analytic assumptions.

A recently reported cost-effectiveness analysis of clesrovimab versus nirsevimab used a post-hoc analysis of trial data, in an attempt to align case definitions for LRTI endpoints across agents within respective clinical trials, in order to employ an RCT versus RCT cost-effectiveness evaluation [75]. Despite these efforts, notable discrepancies were observed. Specifically, a clinical review showed that the endpoint case definitions for LRTI did not align well across these studies, with different signs/symptoms of LRTI collected (Table S17) and there was also exclusion of a considerable proportion of cases meeting the primary endpoint definition in the post hoc analyses. From a clinical perspective, these differences likely reflect underlying variations in trial design (e.g., possible differences in original case report forms used to collect signs/symptoms to determine the case definitions, etc.) and differences in criteria used to define disease severity criteria. Relative to severity, the nirsevimab trial required the presence of at least one sign of LRTI together with at least one indicator of severity, whereas the clesrovimab trial defined a primary endpoint based on the presence of cough plus a sign of LRTI (e.g., rales), without necessitating evidence of severe disease. These clinically relevant differences in case definitions may partially account for observed differences in efficacy and may limit robustness with respect to the use of these post-hoc LRTI endpoints in a comparative analysis.

The clinical profile of nirsevimab is supported by a robust and growing body of evidence from both randomized controlled trials and real-world effectiveness studies. The phase 2b [17] and MELODY [18, 22] trials demonstrated high efficacy against RSV-associated MA LRTDs, inclusive of hospitalizations (70.1% and 74.5%, respectively) in healthy late preterm and term infants [17, 18]. This protection has been consistently validated in real-world settings across multiple countries and healthcare systems [23, 29, 30]. Most notably, the recently published BEAR (BEYFORTUS™ [Nirsevimab] Effectiveness Against Medically-Attended RSV Events in Infants) study conducted across 259 medical clinics and 21 hospitals in Northern California during the 2023–2024 RSV season demonstrated high effectiveness, with a 98.0% reduction in RSV-associated hospitalizations among infants who received nirsevimab compared to those who did not. This study, which included 31,900 infants, provides compelling evidence of the effectiveness of nirsevimab in a real-world US setting and confirms the durability of protection throughout the entire RSV season. The consistency between clinical trial efficacy and real-world effectiveness data for nirsevimab across diverse populations and settings provides a high degree of certainty regarding its protective benefits and duration of protection, which is particularly important for healthcare decision-makers implementing RSV immunization programs.

The molecular targeting mechanisms of nirsevimab and clesrovimab represent distinct approaches to RSV neutralization, with nirsevimab specifically binding to the prefusion-specific antigenic site Ø, while clesrovimab targets antigenic site IV that is present in both pre- and post-fusion conformations of the F protein [36]. The structural and functional characteristics of antigenic site Ø make it the most potent neutralizing epitope, as it is strategically located at the apex of the prefusion trimer and directly interferes with viral fusion machinery [76].

While both nirsevimab and clesrovimab have received single-dose recommendations for full RSV season protection [20, 48], the distinct pharmacokinetic profiles between the two products warrant careful consideration. The marked difference in half-life characteristics (71 days for nirsevimab [15] versus 44 days for clesrovimab[26]) raises important questions about sustained protection throughout the 150-day RSV season. Recent availability of the FDA’s time-to-first event analysis of the clesrovimab efficacy trial has not fully resolved questions regarding the sustained protection of clesrovimab through the length of the RSV season [26, 27, 38].

Of particular note, the FDA analysis revealed overlapping KM curves for the ≥ 6 to < 9 months and ≥ 9-months subgroups, attributed primarily to lower placebo group incidence rates in these older age cohorts [27]. These findings, derived from post hoc subgroup analyses with limited sample sizes and event numbers, were not powered to detect subgroup differences and should be interpreted as exploratory. This, together with the similar case accumulation among infants ≥ 5 kg, creates uncertainty regarding protection levels for infants born before or at the beginning of the RSV season. In contrast, nirsevimab, with its weight-banded dosing strategy, has demonstrated consistent and sustained protection across infant age and weight subgroups. These results are particularly relevant for preventative strategies, as infants born before or at the start of the RSV season, who account for half of RSV hospitalizations, require adequate protection through the full 5-month period due to continuous exposure risk.

The combination of limited long-term efficacy and shorter half-life suggests that the current single-dose recommendation for clesrovimab requires additional supportive evidence to confirm consistent protection throughout the full 150-day RSV season in temperate climates. These factors collectively present a scientific rationale for enhanced monitoring and potential reassessment of the single-dose recommendation timing relative to RSV season onset.

Our efficacy estimates and protection duration analysis for clesrovimab are derived from modeling the relationship between antibody decay data [26, 45] and temporal efficacy patterns [26, 38]. To address uncertainties in protection duration, we implemented a scientifically grounded approach using three scenarios: 100-day, 120-day, and 150-day protection durations for mean cumulative efficacy. The 100-day lower-bound scenario is supported by pharmacokinetic evidence showing that clesrovimab reaches comparable antibody levels at day 96 to those demonstrated by nirsevimab at day 150, when protection has been clinically validated. This conservative assumption aligns with available biological and mechanistic evidence, particularly considering the shorter half-life of clesrovimab (44 days versus 71 days for nirsevimab).

Our analysis framework extends to include 120-day and 150-day scenarios, providing a comprehensive sensitivity analysis spanning from pharmacokinetically-supported estimates to regulatory-claimed durations. The 120-day intermediate scenario serves as a bridge between the pharmacokinetic-based estimate and the 150-day duration reported in the clesrovimab EtR framework and USPI [26, 48]. This structured analytical approach synthesizes available clinical evidence and biological plausibility while acknowledging current regulatory positions. The resulting framework enables robust evaluation of economic and clinical implications across varying protection durations—a critical consideration given the documented shorter half-life and site IV targeting mechanism of clesrovimab compared to nirsevimab. Notably, nirsevimab substantially outperforms clesrovimab under all three scenarios.

While our study aligns with the previous Hutton et al. (2024) study [53] in utilizing mathematical modeling based on sigmoid decay functions for time-based efficacy estimates, our methodology incorporates several key differences. Rather than employing fixed efficacy periods, we implemented detailed pharmacokinetic modeling with explicit concentration thresholds, enabling more precise protection duration estimates. Furthermore, we used a known nirsevimab concentration that correlates with season-long protection to model the duration of mean cumulative efficacy of clesrovimab, in contrast to the reliance on assumed efficacy decline patterns without concentration-based validation [53]. Our approach extends the analytical framework by facilitating direct comparisons between different mAbs through standardized pharmacokinetic parameters. However, we acknowledge inherent limitations in both methodologies: our assumption that similar antibody concentrations yield equivalent efficacy across different mAbs may oversimplify the biological reality, while the approach taken in the Hutton et al. study [53] may not fully capture product-specific protection dynamics. Both approaches are constrained by the current lack of established correlates of protection.

There are inherent limitations associated with the analyses performed and presented in this study. In the absence of data on comparative potency, we assumed similar potency between nirsevimab and clesrovimab in the base case, which may be a conservative analytical approach that may overstate the impact of clesrovimab. The assessment of protection duration against RSV for mAbs faces significant methodological challenges when comparing different products. Valid neutralizing antibody comparisons require testing within identical assay systems—a standardization currently lacking across products. Technical constraints further complicate this analysis, including potential interference from endogenous infant-generated antibodies, transplacental acquired maternal antibodies, inconsistencies across laboratory methodologies, and the inability to directly compare products evaluated in different systems. While serum concentration analysis provides a more standardized approach for comparing pharmacokinetic properties, this methodology has inherent limitations in directly measuring functional activity. Our modeling approach assumes that similar antibody concentrations yield equivalent efficacy across different mAbs, which may oversimplify the biological reality. Both our approach and that taken in the Hutton et al. study [53] are constrained by the current lack of established correlates of protection. The time-to-first event analysis of the clesrovimab efficacy trial has limitations, with the majority of cases (78%) accumulating in the first 90 days of follow-up, restricting conclusions about the duration of protection or waning efficacy. Additionally, findings from post hoc subgroup analyses had limited sample sizes and event numbers, were not powered to detect subgroup differences, and should be interpreted as exploratory. A further limitation is the absence of robust real-world evidence for clesrovimab, which precluded development of a real-world evidence-based base case for both products. Consequently, while nirsevimab outcomes were informed by real-world data, clesrovimab results relied solely on clinical trial evidence, which may not fully reflect effectiveness or utilization patterns under routine conditions. Further, no direct head-to-head comparison of efficacy was available at the time of this study, so the interventions were compared indirectly. This is subject to limitations related to differences in study design, population, and timing. An additional limitation relevant to the RCT-based scenario analysis is that there are some differences in the composition of the endpoint definitions for RSV MA-LRTI relative to symptoms and severity as defined and implemented in the key clinical trials for nirsevimab and clesrovimab. As detailed in Table S17, RSV MA-LRTI in the nirsevimab trials required evidence of more severe disease than the corresponding RSV MALRI-related endpoints in the clesrovimab CLEVER trial [38, 77]. Given that outpatient-managed RSV MA-LRTI events represent a substantial proportion of total RSV cases and are a key driver of costs, particularly in the term infant population, these definitional differences introduce some uncertainty into comparative estimates of both clinical impact and cost-effectiveness. In the absence of direct head-to-head data, public health decision-makers still require a basis for treatment decisions, and modeling studies like this can offer informative insights by synthesizing the most current and methodologically relevant evidence. Several alternative scenarios for efficacy data and duration of efficacy were examined to provide ranges of uncertainty around estimates. Importantly, it is not possible to determine the direction or magnitude of potential bias that would result if a common RSV MA-LRTI definition were applied or available across trials, as reclassification could plausibly increase or decrease estimated efficacy for either intervention. Additionally, this analysis is based on historical data in the US and so may not be generalizable to future years or other countries. Due to data availability, the analyses also did not consider differentiated prophylaxis coverage by birth timing, although the prevalence of coverage differing by infant birth month is likely to exist in real-world practice. Maternal immunization was not included as a comparator in this cost-effectiveness analysis due to its distinct target population and timing of intervention, which differ fundamentally from infant immunization strategies such as nirsevimab and clesrovimab. Evaluating interventions within a consistent population framework is critical in health economic modeling, as cost-effectiveness outcomes are highly sensitive to variation in disease burden, risk profiles, and healthcare utilization across populations. While maternal immunization represents a potentially valuable public health strategy, its economic impact should be assessed through separate analyses designed to reflect its unique implementation context. In addition, mixed strategies combining maternal immunization with selective monoclonal antibody use represent an alternative approach that could be explored in future research. However, such strategies are beyond the scope of this cost-effectiveness analysis. Lastly, although the analyses presented were performed from a societal perspective, there remain additional endpoints not captured in this analysis which are important for societal considerations (e.g., transportation costs, follow-up care costs and QALY implications, etc.).

Differences between products are important to consider when implementing a protection program. Nirsevimab and clesrovimab have several important clinical differences. Based on our modeling of publicly available data, concerns emerge regarding the ability of clesrovimab to provide adequate protection for infants born before the RSV season, potentially creating vulnerability during peak RSV circulation periods. This finding aligns with the emphasis by ACIP on October administration of RSV immunizations for infants born outside the RSV season, underscoring the critical importance of optimal timing for protection initiation. These conclusions highlight the need for careful consideration of administration timing to ensure effective protection throughout the entire RSV season.

Conclusion

In this modeling analysis, nirsevimab was consistently estimated to provide greater clinical benefit and more favorable cost–utility outcomes compared to clesrovimab across a range of assumptions related to duration of cumulative efficacy or clinical trial endpoints leveraged. While uncertainty remains due to the absence of head-to-head comparative data and differences in clinical endpoint definitions across trials, particularly for RSV MA-LRI-related outcomes, results were robust across multiple sensitivity and scenario analyses. These findings suggest that, among RSV monoclonal antibodies indicated for the same infant population, nirsevimab may offer greater value from a societal perspective. Future comparative real-world studies conducted across similar settings and RSV seasons will be critical to further inform decision-making as additional evidence becomes available.
