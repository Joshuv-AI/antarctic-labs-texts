Three-dimensional choroidal parameter differences in myopic anisometropia
Authors: Qiujian Zhu, Xiaoying Xing, Lie Ma, You Yuan, E Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo quantitatively compare 3D choroidal parameters between fellow eyes in individuals with varying degrees of myopic anisometropia and evaluate their correlations with interocular differences in spherical equivalent (△SE) and axial length (△AL).MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study enrolled 102 participants (204 eyes) categorized into low (△SE < 2.0D), moderate (2.0D ≤ △SE < 3.0D), and high (△SE ≥ 3.0D) anisometropia groups. Choroidal parameters, including choroidal vascular volume (CVV), choroidal stroma volume (CSV), and 3D choroidal vascularity index (3D-CVI), were measured using swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT). Retinal vessel density (VD) and regional variations were analyzed.ResultsCVV and CSV were significantly lower in more myopic eyes across all groups (P < 0.05), with the greatest reductions observed in the high anisometropia group. Nasal regions exhibited the strongest correlations between △CVV/△CSV and △SE/△AL (coefficients up to 0.403, P < 0.001). No interocular differences in 3D-CVI were detected (P > 0.05). Retinal VD in the high anisometropia group was elevated in more myopic eyes (P < 0.05), suggesting compensatory microvascular changes.ConclusionThree-dimensional choroidal metrics, particularly CVV and CSV, reflect myopic severity in anisometropia, with nasal choroid demonstrating heightened vulnerability to axial elongation. These findings highlight the potential of volumetric choroidal imaging for evaluating myopic structural changes and underscore the need for further longitudinal studies to elucidate causal mechanisms.Keywords: Myopic anisometropia, Choroidal vascular volume, Choroidal stroma volume, Three-dimensional choroidal vascularity index, Swept-source OCT

Introduction

Myopia refers to a refractive status with a spherical equivalent (SE) of ≤ − 0.50 diopter (D), which is the most common visual disorder worldwide and a major public health concern [1]. Myopia progress is usually caused by the axial length elongation, which is often accompanied by thinning and stretching of the sclera, choroid, and retina, increasing the risk of irreversible vision-threatening impairment such as choroidal neovascularization, retinal and choroidal atrophy, posterior scleral staphyloma, lacquer crack formation [2–5]. Due to the potentially significant economic and societal burdens these complications may impose, research into the progression and control of myopia is paramount importance and urgency [6, 7].

However, the exact etiology and pathogenesis of myopia remain unclear. Several theories have been proposed to explain the onset and development of the myopia, such as defocus theory and scleral remodeling theory, but none of them is flawless [8–11]. Among the majority of hypotheses, alterations in ocular blood perfusion, encompassing changes in blood flow within the retina and choroid, are closely associated with the onset and progression of myopia. Notably, the choroid always plays a key role. The choroid is a vascular layer located between the sclera and Bruch’s membrane, which could be divided into choroidal capillary plexus, Sattler’s medium vascular layer, and Haller’s large vessel layer from the inside out [12, 13]. In 1995, Wallman et al. [14] demonstrate that retinal defocus modulates choroidal thickness to compensate for imposed refractive errors and these changes were associated with changes in eye growth by the chicken model. Since then, a large number of studies have begun to investigate the relationship between choroid and myopia. More recently, it has been suggested that the choroid regulates scleral extracellular matrix remodeling by changing microenvironmental oxygen and nutrient supply during the development of myopia [15, 16].

On the other hand, evaluation of the choroid in vivo is always a challenge due to its physical inaccessibility. Recently, the rapid advances in optical coherence tomography (OCT) technology have enabled quantitative measurement of the choroid, which provides a new insight into the study of myopia in humans. Some easily available choroidal parameters, such as choroidal thickness (ChT) and luminal area (LA), have been used to reveal the relationship between choroid and myopia in humans [17–21]. Moreover, choroidal vascularity index (CVI), referring to the ratio of vascular area to total choroidal area, has also been applied in several studies on myopic eyes [12, 22]. However, some researchers contend that, given its three-dimensional structure, the choroid's characteristics cannot be completely captured by two-dimensional parameters [23, 24]. Volumetric data such as choroidal vascular volume (CVV), choroidal stroma volume (CSV) and three-dimensional choroidal vascularity index (3D-CVI) are more reliable indicators for evaluating choroidal changes.

Besides, given the diversity of choroidal morphology, many factors may have impact on it, such as gender, age, race, environment and genes, etc., which brings certain difficulties to the study of choroid [25–27]. Myopic anisometropia, possessing the same genetic background and analogous environmental exposures, is an ideal model for studying the characteristics of myopia. Previous studies have mainly used 2D parameters to assess choroidal changes in myopia, which may not fully reflect its 3D structural complexity—especially in anisometropia. This study quantifies 3D choroidal parameters (CVV, CSV, and 3D-CVI) in fellow eyes with varying degrees of myopic anisometropia. Using a paired-eye design, we aim to explore their relationship with axial and refractive differences. Current study is the first time, to the best of our knowledge, to investigate the three-dimensional features of the choroid between two eyes of people with various severity of myopic anisometropia.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This retrospective, cross-sectional study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the Lixiang Eye Hospital of Soochow University and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant. All subjects were enrolled from the Implantable Collamer Lens surgery candidates in Lixiang Eye Hospital between July 1, 2023, and June 30, 2024. All patients underwent SS-OCT as part of the institutional preoperative protocol. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 45 years, (2) spherical equivalent (SE) of both eyes < − 0.5 D and the difference in SE (△SE) between two eyes ≥ 1.0 D, (3) best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) better than 20/25, (4) astigmatism ≤ 2.0 D, and (4) intraocular pressure (IOP) < 21 mmHg. No participants had undergone any intraocular surgery. Participants with a history of retinal disease, uveitis, glaucoma or systemic disease were excluded.

Ophthalmic examination

All participants underwent a complete ophthalmic evaluation, including standard comprehensive optometry for SE and BCVA (ARK-510A; NIDEK, Gamagori, Japan); slit lamp examination; IOP (Tonemeterx-10; Canon, Tokyo, Japan); dilated fundus examination by a three-mirror lens; white to white diameter (WTW), K1, K2 and anterior chamber depth (ACD) (Pentacam; Oculus, Germany); axial length (AL) and lens thickness (LT) (ZEISS IOLMaster 700; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA). Based on the △SE of two eyes, the participants were further divided into three groups: low anisometropia (LA) group, △SE < 2.0 D; moderate anisometropia (MA) group, 2.0 D ≤ △SE < 3.0D; high anisometropia (HA) group, △SE ≥ 3.0 D. All image acquisitions and parameter measurements were performed by masked evaluators who were unaware of the refractive status of the participants to minimize observer bias.

Swept-Source OCT/OCTA (SS-OCT/OCTA) image acquisition

All participants were imaged using an UWF SS-OCTA device (BM400K BMizar; TowardPi Medical Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). With the combination of long wavelength (1060 nm) full range swept source and 100 kHz A-scan rate, the device has capability to acquire as deep as 6 mm scan depth, scan range of 12 mm × 12 mm and the largest field of view of 68 × 68°, which providing a transverse resolution of 10 μm and in-depth resolution (optical) of 3.8 μm in tissue. To minimize the magnification error, the AL was inputted into the SS-OCT system prior to the scan. The SS-OCT system then internally adjusted the scan area in accordance with the variations in magnification resulting from differing ALs among individual eyes, thereby enabling more precise comparisons across subjects. If the image quality is lower than 9, another scan will be conducted until a satisfactory image is obtained; if a satisfactory image cannot be obtained, the subject will be excluded.

All the examinations were performed by the same experienced ophthalmologist between 2:00 PM and 4:00 PM and at a constant illumination level (30 lx) to reduce the influence of daily rhythms and light exposure on the choroid [28, 29]. Three days prior to the examination, subjects were not allowed to quote drinks containing alcohol and caffeine or to smoke cigarettes, to avoid potential effects [30–32].

Image analysis

All data segmentation and image analysis processes were performed automatically by the built-in software of the instrument. To further ensure segmentation accuracy, an experienced doctor conducted a reassessment of the boundaries, and manually segmented adjustments would be performed if necessary, utilizing the built-in software. Then the choroidal Sattler’s and Haller’s layers (CSHL, defines as 29 μm below the Bruch’s membrane to the choroid–sclera interface) were generated automatically, subsequently, the CVV, CSV and 3D-CVI (the ratio of CVV to total CSHL volume, which reflects the volumetric vessel density of CSHL) were calculated automatically by the built-in software too. Besides, the vessel density (VD, the ratio of the area occupied by blood vessels, excluding the major trunks, to the total area) of superficial capillary plexus (SCP, from the internal limiting membrane to 9 μm above the inner plexiform layer) and deep capillary plexus (DCP, from 9 μm above the IPL interface to 9 μm below the outer plexiform layer) was obtained automatically too. The similar details were described previously [23, 33, 34].

According to the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grids (6 × 6 mm), the macular zone was divided into three distinct eccentricities: the fovea (ranging from 0 to 0.5 mm), the parafovea (spanning from 0.5 to 1.5 mm), and the perifovea (extending from 1.5 to 3 mm). Each eccentricity was further divided into four quadrants: temporal, superior, nasal and inferior (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., New York, NY, USA) was used to perform statistical analyses. The numerical data were expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) if the parameters had a normal distribution through the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, otherwise expressed as median and quartile. The paired t-test was used to assess the differences in retinal and choroidal parameters between the fellow eyes. The one-way analysis of variance (One-Way ANOVA) was used to compare differences among groups and the Bonferroni test was used for post hoc analyses. Pearson’s correlations were used to study the association between the choroid parameters and the △SE and △AL. Two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 110 initial participants, we excluded eight due to poor OCT image quality. Two cases (case 1, SE = −10.5D, AL = 29.81 mm; case 2, SE = −12.25D, AL = 29.90 mm) need to manually re-segment by doctor in nasal region. Finally, a total of 204 eyes (102 subjects; 25 males and 77 females) were enrolled. All participants exhibited compound myopic anisometropia. Astigmatism subtypes included: with-the-rule (WTR, 85.3%), against-the-rule (ATR, 7.8%), and oblique (6.9%). The baseline characteristics of the three groups were detailed displayed in Table 1. The K1s of more myopic eyes in LA group were slightly lower than MA group (P = 0.037). The SEs of less myopic eyes in HA group were significantly less than the other two groups (P = 0.005), while the ALs were shorter (P = 0.012). There were no significant differences in IOP, ACD, WTW, K1, LT, and age among the three groups (all P > 0.05).

Figure 2 shows the binocular differences in choroidal parameters among the three groups of subjects. HA exhibited the greatest △ChT in most regions except parafoveal temporal (all P < 0.05, Fig. 2A). The △CVV were significantly higher in the HA group compared to the LA group in foveal, parafoveal superior, parafoveal nasal, perifoveal superior and perifoveal nasal, and were also higher than that in the MA group in parafoveal nasal and perifoveal nasal regions (P = 0.033, 0.004, < 0.001, 0.036, 0.049, < 0.001, 0.027, 0.031, Fig. 2B). Additionally, across all nine regions, the △CSV were significantly higher in HA in comparison to the other two groups (all P < 0.05; Fig. 2D). It is worth noting that no statistically significant differences in △3D-CVI were observed among the three groups in any regions (all P > 0.05, Fig. 2C).

Table 4 illustrates the Pearson’s correlation of the three-dimension choroid parameter differences with △SE and △AL in nine regions. △CSV was significantly positively correlated with △SE and negatively correlated with △AL in all regions (all P < 0.05). △CVV showed significant positive correlation with △SE in six regions excep parafoveal temporal, perifoveal temporal and perifoveal inferior, while negative correlated with △AL except perifoveal temporal and perifoveal inferior regions (all P < 0.05). However, △3D-CVI was not significantly associated with △SE and △AL in any region (all P > 0.05). Intriguingly, for both △CVV and △CSV, the nasal regions always showed the strongest correlation coefficients with SE compared to other regions (coefficient = 0.331, 0.350, 0.396, 0.403).

Discussion

In this study, regardless of the degree of anisometropia, the CVV and CSV in more myopic eyes were lower than those in less myopic eyes; however, there was no significant difference in 3D-CVI. The degree of reduction in the HA group was higher than that in the LA and MA groups. Furthermore, for both △CVV and △CSV, most of the regions were closely correlated with interocular difference in △AL and △SE, and the nasal regions showed the strongest correlation. To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to investigate the differences of three-dimension choroidal parameters in subjects with different degrees of myopic anisometropia. Besides, we also find higher retinal vessel density of more myopic eyes in HA group. These findings may offer a new perspective for further understanding the pathophysiology of myopia.

The consistent reduction in CVV and CSV in more myopic eyes across all groups underscores the pivotal role of choroidal vascular and stromal atrophy in myopia progression. This observation aligns with prior studies reporting choroidal thinning in myopia but highlights the need for volumetric analyses to capture the full extent of structural changes [17, 23, 24, 35, 36]. The pronounced reduction in CVV and CSV in the HA group further supports the hypothesis that severe anisometropia exacerbates choroidal degeneration, potentially due to prolonged mechanical stretching and hypoxia-driven remodeling [10, 15]. Notably, the absence of significant differences in 3D-CVI between fellow eyes suggests that while both vascular and stromal components diminish proportionally in myopic eyes, the volumetric density of choroidal vasculature remains relatively stable. The relationship between 3D-CVI and myopia severity remains controversial. Liu et al. [23]demonstrated that both CVV and CSV decrease with increasing myopia severity, but the reduction in CSV appears more pronounced than that in CVV, leading to a progressive elevation of 3D-CVI with myopia progression. However, contradictory findings were documented in a study by Liu and his colleagues, which revealed lower 3D-CVI values in high myopia compared to low-to-moderate myopia [24]. We hypothesize that these conflicting results may be attributed to population heterogeneity across studies, given that demographic variables including age, height, and weight were known confounders of 3D-CVI measurements [37–39]. Notably, this study employed a paired-eye comparative design within the same subjects, effectively controlling for most confounding factors through intra-individual comparisons. This methodological approach significantly enhances the reliability of our findings.

The strong correlations between △CVV/△CSV and interocular differences in △SE/△AL, particularly in nasal regions, suggest localized susceptibility of the choroid to axial elongation. The nasal quadrant, being anatomically adjacent to the optic nerve and biomechanically distinct, may experience greater tensile stress during eye growth, leading to accelerated vascular and stromal loss [24]. This regional vulnerability aligns with earlier reports of nasal choroidal thinning in high myopia [18, 23]. Although 3D-CVI remained unchanged between fellow eyes and did not correlate with ΔAL or ΔSE (Table 4), CVV and CSV were consistently reduced in the more myopic eye and showed strong correlations with both ΔAL and ΔSE. This suggests that absolute choroidal volume loss, rather than volumetric vascular density, better reflects myopic severity in anisometropia. Instead, absolute reductions in choroidal volume may better correlate with disease severity, emphasizing the importance of three-dimensional metrics in clinical assessments.

The elevated deep capillary plexus density observed in more myopic eyes of the HA group introduces an intriguing paradox. While choroidal perfusion declines, retinal microvascular compensation might transiently counteract hypoxia in myopia. Similar compensatory mechanisms have been proposed in our previous research, which found that the deep capillary plexus density in individuals with high myopia and super-high myopia was significantly higher than in the control group and low-to-moderate myopia groups [40]. The inner segments of photoreceptors, the outer plexiform layer, and the deep portion of the inner plexiform layer constitute the primary oxygen-consuming regions of the retina [41]. A reduction in overall ocular blood flow may lead to a prioritized blood supply to these regions covered by the deep retinal vascular network. On the other hand, this phenomenon might also explain why myopia, particularly high myopia, serves as a protective factor against diabetic retinopathy [42, 43].

This study has limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences between choroidal changes and myopia progression. Further longitudinal studies, particularly cohort studies, are required to establish temporal relationships. Additionally, all participants were aged 18 years or older, having passed the peak period of myopia progression (typically 6–12 years). Whether these findings generalize to pediatric populations undergoing active axial elongation should be validated in future studies. Furthermore, most participants were ICL surgery candidates with high myopia, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to populations with moderate/low myopia or hyperopic anisometropia. Future studies incorporating broader refractive error spectra are warranted to validate our observations. Moreover, given the large number of statistical comparisons performed, some of the observed ‘significant’ findings may simply represent false-positive results arising by chance. Consequently, these particular analyses should be regarded as exploratory rather than conclusive. Besides, although this study included a relatively large sample compared to similar investigations, a formal power analysis was not conducted prior to enrollment. This may limit the generalizability of the findings and should be addressed in future prospective studies. Finally, although our sample size was relatively large compared to similar studies, it remains limited for detecting nuanced associations and further studies should enroll more subjects.

Conclusion

This study shows that 3D choroidal volumetric parameters—CVV and CSV—were significantly reduced in more myopic eyes across all degrees of anisometropia. These reductions correlated strongly with interocular differences in spherical equivalent and axial length, especially in nasal regions. The findings suggest that CVV and CSV may serve as sensitive biomarkers for myopia severity. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm their role in tracking progression.
