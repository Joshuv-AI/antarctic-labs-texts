Binational comparison of the association between socioeconomic status and metabolic syndrome: A nationally representative study in South Korea and the United States
Authors: Yoon Lee, Ye Won Kim, Juyeong Kim, André Hajek, Yusheng Li, Jiyoung Hwang, Selin Woo, Dong Keon Yon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDespite the rising global burden of metabolic syndrome (MetS), evidence on its association with socioeconomic status (SES) remains limited. We aimed to compare associations between SES and MetS in South Korea and the US.MethodsWe conducted an analysis using data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES, 2007–2020, n = 73,023) and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 2007–2020, n = 24,198), targeting adults aged ≥20 years. MetS was defined based on the criteria of the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III. SES was categorized according to income and educational level. The association between SES and MetS was assessed using multivariable weighted logistic regression, yielding adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsAge-standardized MetS prevalence was higher in South Korea (25.8% [95% CI, 25.1, 26.6]) than in the US (20.4% [18.4, 22.3]) in 2017–2020. In contrast, the aOR for MetS (lowest vs. highest SES group) was higher in the US than in South Korea (1.73 [1.49, 2.02] vs. 1.38 [1.27, 1.50]).ConclusionsOur findings support country-specific strategies, with South Korea prioritizing early MetS prevention and the US reducing access barriers and optimizing health-care spending.Keywords: KNHANES, NHANES, Metabolic syndrome, Socioeconomic status

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a growing public health concern due to its increasing global prevalence (Neeland et al., 2024), including in South Korea and the US (Kwon and Song, 2022; Liang et al., 2023). In response to this trend, numerous studies in both countries have investigated the prevalence of MetS across different income and educational levels (Liang et al., 2023; Park et al., 2022; Kim and Kim, 2022; Li et al., 2023). These studies have consistently shown that individuals with lower income and educational attainment exhibit higher prevalence of MetS, providing valuable insights for targeted public health policies (Liang et al., 2023; Park et al., 2022).

Although these important policy implications are noted, prior research has been scarce and has faced several limitations. Previous studies did not utilize long-term data and were constrained by small sample sizes, undermining the robustness and generalizability of their findings (Kwon and Song, 2022; Liang et al., 2023; Park et al., 2022). Moreover, to our knowledge, few studies have systematically assessed the MetS prevalence using an integrated framework that jointly considers income and education, two fundamental dimensions of socioeconomic status (SES). This cross-classified approach can provide policymakers with nuanced and integrated guidance for developing SES-specific intervention strategies (Abdalla et al., 2025a).

To address these gaps and provide a more integrated perspective, we utilized nationally representative datasets from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), collected between 2007 and 2020. Furthermore, we constructed SES quartiles based on the cross-classified income and educational levels, each dichotomized as high or low. In this study, we aimed to compare adjusted odds of MetS and its diagnostic components across these SES quartiles to identify SES-based differences in MetS burden between South Korea and the US.

Methods

Data source and study population

This study utilized nationally representative data from the KNHANES and the NHANES. For KNHANES, all survey years from 2007 through 2020 were included. For NHANES, data from 2007 through March 2020 were analyzed, as data collection was suspended thereafter due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Both surveys employed multistage, stratified, probability-based cluster sampling designs to obtain nationally representative samples (Yoon et al., 2023). A total of 113,091 individuals from KNHANES and 66,148 from NHANES were initially identified. After excluding individuals younger than 20 years, pregnant adults, and participants with missing data. The final analytic sample consisted of 73,023 participants from South Korea and 24,198 participants from the US (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study population of adults in South Korea and the US from 2007 to 2020.* KNHANES analyses included data from five survey cycles: 2007–2009, 2010–2012, 2013–2015, 2016–2018, and 2019–2020. ** NHANES analyses included data from seven survey cycles: 2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016, 2017–2018, and 2019–March 2020 (partial due to COVID-19).

The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency for KNHANES (2007-02CON-04-P through 2018-01-03-2C-A), and by the Ethics Review Board of the US National Center for Health Statistics for NHANES (Protocol #2005–06, #2011–17, and #2018–01; effective through October 26, 2017, and thereafter). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation, and both datasets provide public access to de-identified data. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Ascertainment of metabolic syndrome

MetS was defined according to the criteria established by the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III, as updated by the American Heart Association (Rhee et al., 2022). The definition was based on five clinical indicators—waist circumference, fasting plasma glucose, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL—C), triglycerides, and blood pressure—and MetS was classified as present when three or more of these criteria were met. In this study, MetS was treated as a categorical variable. Waist circumference thresholds were set at greater than 90 cm for male and 85 cm for female in South Korea (Rhee et al., 2022), and greater than 102 cm for male and 88 cm for female in the US (Chen et al., 2025). Fasting plasma glucose of 100 mg/dL or higher, HDL-C levels below 40 mg/dL in male or below 50 mg/dL in female, triglyceride levels of 150 mg/dL or higher, and systolic or diastolic blood pressure of 130/85 mmHg or higher were considered abnormal according to these guidelines (Rhee et al., 2022).

Definition of income and education groups

Household income and education level were combined to create a four-level categorical variable in both KNHANES and NHANES. Household income was assessed differently in the two surveys. In KNHANES, we used the household income quintile variable provided in the dataset, which is based on monthly equivalized household income calculated as total monthly household income divided by the square root of household size. In NHANES, household income was measured as the ratio of family income to the federal poverty threshold, which represents relative income adjusted for household size and composition. To enable cross-country comparison, income levels were harmonized into two categories: high income (top 20%) and low income (bottom 80%). In NHANES, this corresponded to participants with a ratio of family income to poverty ≥5.0 (top 20%) or < 5.0 (bottom 80%) (Abdalla et al., 2025b). Education level was based on self-reported education in each survey. Because KNHANES and NHANES used different categorical systems, education levels were harmonized into two groups—high school or less and college or higher—to enable cross-country comparison. Based on the combination of income and education, participants were grouped into one of four categories: (group one: top 20% income and college or higher; group two: bottom 80% income and college or higher; group three: top 20% income and high school or less; and group four: bottom 80% income and high school or less).

Covariates

Referring to prior population-based studies (You et al., 2025a; You et al., 2025b), covariates included sex (male and female), age group (20–39, 40–59, and ≥ 60 years), region of residence (urban and rural; KNHANES only), race/ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, and other race/ethnicity; NHANES only), body mass index (BMI) group, smoking status (current smoker, ex-smoker, and non-smoker), alcohol consumption status (current drinker, ex-drinker, and non-drinker), marital status (married or living with a partner, divorced or separated, and never married), and sleep duration (<7, 7–9, and ≥ 9 h/day). BMI was classified using region-specific criteria: the Asia-Pacific guidelines for South Korea (underweight, <18.5 kg/m2; normal weight, 18.5–22.9 kg/m2; overweight, 23.0–24.9 kg/m2; obese, ≥25.0 kg/m2) (Yim et al., 2025), and the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for the US (underweight, <18.5 kg/m2; normal weight18.5–24.9 kg/m2; overweight, 25.0–29.9 kg/m2; obese, ≥30.0 kg/m2) (Akyea et al., 2021). Smoking status was defined as follows: non-smokers had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime (Quach et al., 2025). Among those who had smoked 100 or more cigarettes, current smokers were those who currently smoked, and ex-smokers were those who did not. Alcohol consumption status was defined as follows: non-drinkers had consumed fewer than 12 alcoholic drinks in their lifetime. Among those who had consumed 12 or more drinks, current drinkers had consumed at least 12 drinks in the past year, and ex-drinkers had not (Tian et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

To allow for cross-national comparisons, KNHANES and NHANES were analyzed separately. Descriptive analyses were conducted to compare demographic and health-related characteristics across four groups defined by income-education combinations. Using survey design–adjusted complex sampling procedures, we evaluated age-standardized trends in the prevalence of metabolic syndrome and its five components, and estimated beta coefficients representing absolute changes over the survey period using linear regression models (Hong et al., 2024). Weighted multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine associations between SES and MetS, adjusting for sex, age, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status, sleep duration, region of residence (KNHANES only), and race/ethnicity (NHANES only). Results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and associations were considered statistically significant when the 95% CI did not include 1.00. A sensitivity analysis was performed to test the robustness of the results by SES indicators, with household income and education level analyzed separately. Based on previous studies, household income was categorized into quintiles and education level was classified into three groups: under high school, high school, and college or higher education (Zare et al., 2022; You et al., 2024). Following previous literature, appropriate strata, cluster, and weight variables were applied to account for the complex sampling designs and ensure nationally representative estimates (You et al., 2025c; Lee et al., 2024). For each country, sampling weights provided by the survey organizers were used and total weights were calculated by incorporating both weight and survey period. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) with two-sided tests.

Results

Population characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of individuals with MetS in South Korea (KNHANES, 2007–2020, n = 73,023) and the US (NHANES, 2007–2020, n = 24,198). In South Korea, females comprised 56.5% of the sample, and 31.7% of females in group four, compared with males (20.2%). While in the US, females accounted for 50.7% of the sample, and 19.9% of females were in group four, similar to males (20.8%). Adults aged ≥60 years constituted 32.6% of the sample in South Korea and 32.0% in the US. In both countries, group four was the most prevalent SES category (South Korea, 52.0%; the US, 40.7%), followed by group two (South Korea, 26.6%; the US, 40.2%).

Trends in metabolic syndrome and its components

Table 2 shows the age-standardized prevalence of MetS components in South Korea and the US by year group. Waist circumference had consistently higher prevalence in the US (2017–2020: South Korea, 32.6% [95% CI, 31.7, 33.4]; the US, 59.6% [57.1, 62.1]). The prevalence of elevated triglycerides in South Korea was more than twice that observed in the US (2017–2020: South Korea, 29.7% [95% CI, 28.9, 30.5]; the US, 9.4% [8.2, 10.6]). Meanwhile, the prevalence of elevated fasting glucose increased more substantially in South Korea (β, 0.27 [95% CI, 0.24, 0.29]) than in the US (β, 0.07 [0.04, 0.10]), while the low HDL-C prevalence declined more significantly in South Korea compared to the US.

Overall, the prevalence of MetS was consistently higher in South Korea than in the US across all periods (Table 2). Although the prevalence of MetS increased in both countries across the study period, the upward trend was more significant in South Korea (β, 0.08 [95% CI, 0.06, 0.11]) than in the US (β, 0.03 [0.00, 0.05]). In additional analysis of crude prevalence trends, the trajectories of MetS and its components closely mirrored those observed in the age-standardized prevalence, with the beta coefficients likewise showing comparable estimates (Table S1).

Adjusted odds ratios for metabolic syndrome and its components

Table 3 presents aORs for MetS and its components across SES group in South Korea and the US. In these analyses, SES is the exposure, with SES group one (lowest) serving as the reference category. The overall multivariable logistic regression model was adjusted for sex, age, BMI group, smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status, sleep duration, region of residence (KNHANES only), and race/ethnicity (NHANES only). Across all components of MetS, excluding high blood pressure in NHANES, group four exhibited significantly higher odds than group one. The most pronounced component-level associations for group four were high blood pressure in South Korea (aOR, 1.39 [95% CI, 1.29, 1.50]) and low HDL-C in the US (aOR 1.73 [1.50, 1.99]). Notably, except for high blood pressure, the aORs for group four were consistently higher in the US than in South Korea; the largest between-country difference occurred for low HDL-C (South Korea, 1.30 [95% CI, 1.22, 1.40] vs. the US, 1.73 [1.50, 1.99]).The aORs for MetS were generally higher in the US than in South Korea, with the most pronounced difference observed in group four (the US, 1.73 [95% CI, 1.49, 2.02] vs. South Korea, 1.38 [1.27, 1.50]).

Discussion

Across all periods, the age-standardized prevalence of MetS was higher in South Korea than in the US, with a more pronounced upward trend observed in South Korea. In contrast, using group one as the reference, aORs for MetS and most of its components in SES group four were higher in United States than in South Korea. In both countries, individuals in group four had higher adjusted odds of MetS and all its components compared with those in group one. Socioeconomic gradients were apparent, with lower income (vs. the highest quintile) and less than a high school education (vs. college or higher) each associated with higher adjusted odds of MetS. These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted public health strategies that address socioeconomic disparities in metabolic health.

Although previous studies have treated income and education as individual covariates, this study provides meaningful insights by using a joint income-education stratification to assess MetS burden. Our research indicates that higher SES levels are associated with a lower prevalence of MetS in both countries. This finding aligns with previous studies, which have also presented an inverse relationship between SES and MetS prevalence (Kwon and Song, 2022; Liang et al., 2023; Park et al., 2022; Kim and Kim, 2022; Li et al., 2023; Hyeon Cho et al., 2021). However, prior research have been limited by statistically insignificant results (Park et al., 2022; Kim and Kim, 2022), the absence of recent data (Liang et al., 2023; Park et al., 2022; Kim and Kim, 2022; Li et al., 2023; Hyeon Cho et al., 2021), and smaller sample sizes with shorter study periods, such as n = 24,564 (2017–2020) (Kwon and Song, 2022), n = 8183 (2011–2018) (Liang et al., 2023), n = 6995 (2016–2018) (Park et al., 2022), n = 1545 (2015) (Kim and Kim, 2022), and n = 14,490 (2016–2018) (Hyeon Cho et al., 2021). Our study addresses these limitations by incorporating extensive datasets from both South Korea (n = 73,023) and the US (n = 24,198) between 2007 and 2020. Notably, the only previous study comparing the prevalence of chronic diseases between South Korea and the US reported a higher age-standardized prevalence of MetS in the US (An et al., 2022). This result contrasts with our findings, which indicate a higher age-standardized prevalence of MetS in South Korea. The discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the study population and study period between the prior study (adults aged 40–69 years, 2003–2014) and our analysis (adults aged ≥20 years, 2007–2020). Moreover, whereas the comparator study applied direct age standardization using the WHO world standard population, we performed age standardization within our analytic dataset. To our knowledge, no prior study has directly compared the association between combined income and education stratification and MetS in both countries. Therefore, our study provides novel insights that may inform the development of more targeted public health policies aimed at mitigating MetS within socioeconomic groups.

Previous studies have shown that low income and education are associated with MetS, possibly due to limited health literacy, poor dietary quality, and inadequate nutrition (Berete et al., 2024; Carrillo-Alvarez et al., 2025). Moreover, individuals in lower-SES groups were more likely to exhibit metabolic abnormalities even at the same BMI (Wang et al., 2023). This finding aligns with the higher prevalence of metabolically unhealthy obesity, defined as having more than two metabolic abnormality, among lower SES groups (Wang et al., 2023). As presented in our study, the prevalence of MetS is higher in South Korea, with a more rapid increase observed between 2007 and 2020. This may be partly explained by the fact that Asians tend to have 3–5% more body fat than White individuals at the same BMI, with greater visceral fat accumulation (Singleton et al., 2021). Moreover, South Korea has undergone lifestyle shifts including increased sedentary behavior, greater fat intake, and more stress-related overeating, which contribute to metabolic dysfunction (Chae et al., 2021). In the US, the higher odds of MetS among low- versus high-SES may reflect shortcomings in the US health system. Despite spending approximately twice as much on healthcare as other high-income countries, the US exhibits markedly increasing rates of avoidable mortality (Papanicolas et al., 2025). In addition, a lower share of the population with health insurance, and the highest per-capita expenditures on prescription drugs among the ten high-income comparator nations (Papanicolas et al., 2018).

Findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, while KNHANES and NHANES represent national populations, differences in sampling methods may lead to under- or overrepresentation of certain subgroups. Second, both surveys are susceptible to information and measurement bias due to self-reporting methods (Akinbami et al., 2022). Third, KNHANES releases data annually, while NHANES provides integrated datasets biennially, potentially delaying the capture of rapid societal changes, such as pandemics. To mitigate these inherent limitations, we performed multivariable weighted logistic regression, accounting for complex survey design. Fourth, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference between SES and MetS, although prior studies support the validity of these associations. Fifth, a considerable number of participants were excluded due to missing data. Although this exclusion may have affected the national representativeness of the analytic sample, the use of survey weights and standardized sampling procedures is expected to have minimized potential selection bias. Sixth, because several outcomes had a prevalence greater than 10%, aORs may slightly overestimate the strength of association compared with prevalence ratios (PRs). Although log-binomial regression could directly estimate PRs, convergence issues were encountered in some models; therefore, logistic regression was applied to ensure model stability. Lastly, because the two surveys used different assessment methods for lifestyle factors—important determinants of MetS—were not included in the analyses. Instead, we incorporated the maximum set of variables that were available and comparable across both national datasets, including those previously reported to be associated with MetS.

Despite these limitations, our study presents several strengths. This is the first analysis to examine the associations between combined income and education levels and MetS using nationally representative data from KNHANES and NHANES. In contrast to previous studies, our joint stratification approach offers a more refined analysis of socioeconomic disparities. The large sample sizes and extended study period further strengthen the robustness of our findings. When identical waist circumference cutoffs for MetS were applied to the entire population, the overall prevalence trends were preserved but absolute values differed; therefore, country-specific cutoffs were applied to enhance methodological rigor and validity (Fig. S1). This study provides valuable cross-national evidence to inform more equitable public health policies.

Based on our results, we propose policy recommendations tailored to South Korea and the US. Given the higher and more rapidly increasing age-standardized prevalence of MetS in South Korea, along with higher visceral fat at equivalent BMI, public health agencies should prioritize early prevention. The Ministry of Education and local governments should collaborate to promote physical activity and implement structured nutrition education in schools (Singhal et al., 2021). Governments should provide subsidies and issue national guidelines to support these initiatives in schools.

In the US, compared with group one, SES group four had higher adjusted odds of MetS, and these odds were higher than those in South Korea. Accordingly, federal and state public health agencies should reduce access barriers for low-SES populations. This includes expanding enrollment outreach and navigation services for the uninsured and lowering out-of-pocket prescription costs (Papanicolas et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2024). In addition, given the limited effectiveness of the current system, federal and state governments should allocate health-care expenditures more efficiently (Papanicolas et al., 2018). Investments should be evidence-informed and practice-oriented, drawing on comparative lessons from other countries' health systems.

In both countries, individuals in SES group four exhibited higher adjusted odds for all MetS components relative to those in group one. These findings underscore the need for SES-tailored policies aligned with WHO Sustainable Development Goal 3.4 to reduce premature deaths from non-communicable diseases by one-third by 2030 (Lancet, 2018). Taken together, this study offers policymakers actionable guidance to design equitable, country-specific strategies that address socioeconomic disparities in metabolic health.

Conclusion

Although the prevalence of MetS was consistently higher and increased more rapidly in South Korea, socioeconomic disparities in the adjusted odds of MetS were greater in the US. In both countries, individuals in SES group four exhibited higher adjusted odds for all MetS components relative to those in group one. These findings offer timely insights to support the development of targeted and equitable public health policies to mitigate MetS across diverse socioeconomic populations.
