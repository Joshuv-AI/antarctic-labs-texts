Ectomesenchyme contributes to epidermal stem cell formation through mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition
Authors: Asaka Miura, Yuki Kobayashi, Yoshikazu Hirose, Tomoyuki Azuma, Takashi Shimbo, Yuya Ouchi, Tomomi Kitayama, Ryoma Yamamoto, Eiichi Takaki, Sho Yamazaki, Machika Kawamura, Aino Sasabuchi, Kotaro Saga, Sachiko Iseki, Yasufumi Kaneda, Manabu Fujimoto, Katsuto Tamai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutaneous development is initiated by crosstalk between non-neural ectodermal epithelium and underlying mesenchymal cells. Recent studies have shown that some mesenchymal cells contribute to keratinocyte regeneration in injured skin. However, whether mesenchymal cells contribute to keratinocyte formation in physiologically normal skin remains unclear. Here we show, using lineage tracing, single-cell transcriptome and epigenome analyses of mouse skin, that the interfollicular epidermis consists largely of mesenchymal-lineage cells. Further lineage-tracing and live-imaging analysis of mouse embryos indicate that these mesenchymal-lineage epidermal progenitor cells arise from ectomesenchyme and contribute to the surface ectoderm through a mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition-like process between embryonic days 8.5 and 9.5. We also establish human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived ectomesenchyme and demonstrate that it generates p63-expressing keratinocytes. These data reveal a previously unappreciated contribution of ectomesenchyme to cutaneous development and provide insight into skin diseases involving epidermal mosaicism.Subject terms: Skin stem cells, Cell lineage, Embryology, Mesenchymal stem cells

Introduction

Cutaneous development is triggered by crosstalk between non-neural ectodermal epithelial cells and the underlying mesenchymal cells during early embryo development1–3. Mesenchymal cells stimulate monolayered ectodermal epithelial cells to induce stratification and appendage formation1. Subsequently, these mesenchymal cells develop fibroblasts that generate the dermal matrix to maintain epidermal and dermal homeostasis, and contribute to tissue repair and regeneration after skin injury3.

Recent studies have highlighted the epithelial transition potential of mesenchymal cells. Dermal fibroblasts can be directly converted into epidermal keratinocytes by transduction of key transcription factors, including DNP63A, TFAP2A, GRHL2 and MYC in vivo4. In addition, we have demonstrated that bone marrow mesenchymal cells expressing platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (Pdgfra) can migrate to necrotic skin grafts, where they regenerate necrotic epithelial keratinocytes5. More recent studies further showed that Pdgfra-positive bone marrow mesenchymal cells can transition into keratinocyte tumour cells6. Together, these findings suggest that some mesenchymal cell populations in the skin and bone marrow harbour epithelial potential under appropriate microenvironmental conditions. However, the specific role of mesenchymal cells in keratinocyte development under normal physiological conditions remain unclear.

Among mesenchymal populations, ectomesenchyme is of particular interest because it arises from ectodermal epithelium and occupies a developmental territory including the non-neural ectoderm, the source of epidermal keratinocytes3. Over the past two decades, studies have shown that cranial ectomesenchyme delaminating from the cranial neural fold gives rise to multiple craniofacial mesenchymal derivatives7–10. Moreover, the first wave of ectomesenchymal delamination originates from the non-neural domain of the cranial neural fold7,11. Together, these observations raise the possibility that ectomesenchymal populations also contribute to epidermal keratinocyte formation. However, this possibility has not been directly addressed.

Here, we show whether ectomesenchymal cell populations contribute to epidermal keratinocyte development in mice. Genetic lineage tracing, single-cell RNA-sequencing and single-cell ATAC-sequencing analyses, together with whole-embryo culture and live imaging, reveal that ectomesenchyme-derived cells enter the surface ectoderm through a mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition-like process during early embryogenesis and give rise to epidermal stem cells. We further establish a human induced pluripotent stem cells-derived cranial ectomesenchyme model and demonstrate that these cells generate p63-expressing keratinocytes in vitro. These findings identify ectomesenchyme as a previously unrecognized source of epidermal stem cells and reveal a new route of skin development.

Results

Mouse epidermal Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes initiate in the dorsal skin

To evaluate the contribution of mesenchymal cells to epidermal keratinocyte development in mice, we used Pdgfra, an established marker of mesenchymal cells12,13. We examined adult (9-week-old) PdgfraEGFP; PdgfraCre; ROSAtdTomato mice, in which Pdgfra-expressing and Pdgfra-lineage cells are marked by EGFP and tdTomato fluorescence, respectively. In adult head skin, keratinocytes were in predominantly tdTomato-positive Pdgfra-lineage cells that lacked Pdgfra-EGFP expression (Fig. 1a). Similarly, Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes were also observed in the back, abdomen, tail, and hands. Notably, some showed continuous columnar arrangement extending from single basal keratinocytes to the upper stratum corneum, consistent with stem cell function (Fig. 1b). To exclude non-specific leakage of tdTomato gene, we examined Ai9 mice (B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm9(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J) and detected no epidermal tdTomato fluorescence in the absence of the Pdgfra-Cre line (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). We also confirmed Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes in the epidermis of PdgfraCre; ROSAEYFP mice (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

To further characterize these cells, we subjected whole cells from adult PdgfraEGFP; PdgfraCre; ROSAtdTomato mice to single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq). Cell clustering analysis identified 16 different cell clusters with distinct expression profiles (Fig. 1c, d and Supplementary Fig. 2a), which were annotated using previously reported skin markers14–17 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Keratinocytes were distributed across six clusters (cluster 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7). Clusters 1 and 7 co-expressed Integrin alpha 6 (Itga6) and Thy1, markers of interfollicular epidermis (IFE) basal layer cells, including stem cells and undifferentiated progenitors (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Cluster 7 uniquely expressed Mki67 and Cdk1, consistent with transient-amplifying cells (TA cells). Krt1 and Krtdap expressed in differentiated keratinocytes (cluster 2), Fst and Sostdc1 expressed in outer bulge (cluster 3), Cd34 and Lgr5 expressed in lower bulge (cluster 4), and the infundibulum marker Cst6 expressed in upper hair follicle (HF) (cluster 5) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Our analysis revealed that the Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes were enriched in IFE (clusters 1 and 2) and were a minor population in follicular bulge keratinocytes (clusters 3 and 4) (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Quantitatively, Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes constituted more than 70% of IFE but less than 30% of follicular bulge (Fig. 1e). This predominance was evident in head and back skin and was significantly less in abdominal skin (Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Fig. 2a), indicating a dorsal-dominant specificity for Pdgfra-lineage IFE keratinocytes.

Thy1 and Itga6 double-positive keratinocytes were recently identified as slow-cycling stem cells in the mouse IFE18. We found that Thy1+/Itga6+ cells in IFE basal (cluster 1) consisted primarily of Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2c). To quantify their contribution, we sorted Thy1+/Itga6+ cells from PdgfraEGFP−, CD31−, CD34−, and CD45− populations to exclude endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and haematopoietic cells from mice of different ages. Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes accounted for approximately 40% of sorted Thy1+/Itga6+ cells in neonatal head and back skin, increasing to approximately 80% at 8-week-old and nearly 100% at 100-week-old (Supplementary Figs. 2d and 17). These data indicate that Pdgfra-lineage IFE stem cells progressively predominate IFE maintenance during postnatal life.

Pdgfra-lineage epidermal cells were significantly less frequent in abdominal skin compared to that in the head and back, increasing from approximately 20% in newborn mice to 40% at 8-week-old and 80% at 100-week-old (Supplementary Figs. 2d and 17). Collectively, these data suggest that Pdgfra-lineage epidermal cells development begins earlier in the dorsal skin and later extends, subsequently progresses into the ventral skin during embryonic development.

Next, we examined the appearance of Pdgfra-lineage epidermal cells during fetal and neonatal skin development. In newborn mice and at embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5), Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes were present in the stratified epidermis of the head, back, abdomen, and mandible (Supplementary Fig. 2e). At E14.5, when epidermal stratification begins19,20, Pdgfra-lineage cells were detected in the head and back epidermis, but not in the abdomen (Supplementary Fig. 2e), indicating that Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes first appear in the dorsal skin and later in the abdominal region.

Pdgfra-lineage IFE stem cells predominantly self-renew in the head skin

Unlike adult mice, neonatal mice exhibit different distributions of Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes between head and trunk skin. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that Pdgfra-lineage epidermal cells accounted for 13.89, 8.09 and 8.39% of EpCAM-positive epidermal cells in the head, back, and abdomen, respectively (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 18). To investigate the head-predominant enrichment of Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes, we analysed scRNA-seq data from neonatal head skin and identified three subclusters expressing the IFE stem cell marker Thy1, but not the follicular bulge stem cell marker Lgr5 (Fig. 2b–d). These Thy1-positive subclusters were predominantly composed of Pdgfra-lineage epidermal cells (Fig. 2e–h and Supplementary Fig. 19), indicating a major contribution of Pdgfra-lineage cells to the development of Thy1-positive IFE stem cells13 in the head skin. Thy1-expressing keratinocytes also expressed Tlx1, a transcription factor required for splenocyte development21,22 (Fig. 2d). Gene Ontology analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between Tlx1− (c2) and Thy1+/Tlx1+ (c6) IFE basal keratinocytes showed enrichment of terms related to epithelial cell proliferation and response to wounding in Tlx1− keratinocytes, consistent with slow-cycling stem cell features of Thy1+/Tlx1+ Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes in vivo (Fig. 2i)18. To confirm the stem cell properties of these cells, we sorted fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and subjected them to a colony-forming unit (CFU) assay. Pdgfra-lineage epidermal cells generated significantly larger colonies than non-Pdgfra-lineage epidermal cells in neonatal head skin, indicating greater self-renewal activity of Thy1+/Tlx1+ Pdgfra-lineage keratinocyte stem cells in vitro. Although non-Pdgfra-lineage keratinocyte colonies were smaller (Fig. 2j, k), their colony-forming efficiency across serial passages was comparable to that of Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes (Fig. 2l, m), suggesting the presence of a distinct stem cell population within non-Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes.

To further characterize Pdgfra-lineage Thy1+/Tlx1+ keratinocytes, we performed scATAC-seq of neonatal head skin. UMAP analysis identified six clusters based on chromatin accessibility, corresponding to keratinocytes (Krt5 and Lgr5; cluster 1, 3), fibroblasts (Vim; cluster 2), Schwann cells (Cryab; cluster 4), melanocytes (Sox10; cluster 5), and immune cells (Ptprc; cluster 6) (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Keratinocyte cluster 3 (c3) was predominantly composed of Pdgfra-lineage cells and exhibited increased chromatin accessibility at Thy1, Tlx1, and Kazald1, consistent with the transcriptomic signature of Pdgfra-lineage Thy1+/Tlx1+ keratinocytes (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d and Fig. 2d; c6_IFE basal). This cluster also exhibited increased chromatin accessibility at Trp63 and Col17a1 (Supplementary Fig. 3d), both highly expressed in epidermal stem cells23–25, and enrichment motifs for Pbx3, Tgif1, Tgif2 and Meis1, regulators of cell proliferation and migration26–29. Motif for Ap2-alpha and Trp63, both critical for epidermal development, were also enriched (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

Subclustering of c3 identified a unique keratinocyte subpopulation with relatively high accessibility at Tfap2a, Trp63 and Tlx1 loci. This subpoplulation also showed increased chromatin accessibility at Pdgfra, Vim and Fzd7, suggesting retention of a chromatin state consistent with transition from the Pdgfra-expressing mesenchymal cells (Supplementary Fig. 3f). We next subclustered fibroblasts (c2) to search for transitional states towards keratinocytes and identified one subcluster with relatively high chromatin accessibility at Cdh1 (Supplementary Fig. 3g). This fibroblast subpopulation also showed elevated chromatin accessibility at Trp63, Tfap2a and Grhl2, previously shown to induce direct reprograming of dermal fibroblasts to epidermal keratinocytes when these genes were transduced together with Myc in vivo4. Taken together, these findings support the presence of epigenetically regulated transitional states associated with the emergence of Pdgfra-lineage epidermal keratinocytes.

Pdgfra-lineage epidermal progenitor cells emerge by E9.5 from mesenchymal cells

To investigate the developmental timing of Pdgfra-lineage epidermal progenitor cells, we used PdgfraEGFP/CreERT2; ROSAtdTomato mice to trace the Pdgfra-expressing cells following tamoxifen (TAM) administration at different embryonic stages. Pregnant mice were administered TAM intraperitoneally at E7.5, E8.5, E9.5 and E10.5, and the presence of Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes was assessed in neonatal skin after birth. Flow cytometry analysis showed that TAM administration at E7.5 (E7.5-TAM) and E8.5-TAM resulted in robust labeling of Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes in neonatal skin, whereas labeling was markedly reduced after E9.5-TAM. Immunohistochemical analysis likewise confirmed labeling of a subset of epidermal keratinocytes after E7.5- and E8.5-TAM, whereas E9.5- and E10.5-TAM did not label these cells. Together, these data indicate that Pdgfra-expressing progenitors that give rise to Pdgfra-lineage epidermal cells emerge within a restricted developmental window centered around E8.5–9.5 (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 20).

To visualise the appearance of the Pdgfra-lineage cells in the surface ectoderm after E8.5, we labelled Pdgfra-expressing cells by E7.5-TAM and traced their descendants at E8.5, E9.5 and E14.5. Embryos were subjected to tissue clearing and three-dimensional confocal imaging. At E8.5, E7.5-TAM labelled all mesenchymal cells directly beneath the surface ectoderm, but not cells within the surface ectoderm itself (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 4a). By E9.5, Pdgfra-lineage cells began to appear in the surface ectoderm at the head and the back, but not in the abdomen (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 4b). By E14.5, the E7.5-TAM-labelled Pdgfra-lineage cells were widely detected in the head and back, whereas a small number of the cells were observed in the abdomen. (Fig. 3f). To exclude aberrant Pdgfra expression in the surface ectoderm, we carefully examined E8.5 and E9.5 PdgfraEGFP embryos and detected no EGFP-positive cells in the non-neural ectodermal epithelium of the head or trunk regions (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Consistently, RNA scope at E8.25 detected no Pdgfra mRNA expression in the non-neural ectoderm (Supplementary Fig. 5b). These data suggest that Pdgfra-lineage cells appearing in the surface ectoderm by E9.5 arise from the underlying mesenchymal cells, if not exclusive.

Asymmetrical cell division of mesenchymal cells develops Pdgfra-lineage epidermal progenitor cells

To determine whether Pdgfra-expressing mesenchymal cells directly give rise to Pdgfra-lineage epithelial cells in the non-neural ectoderm, we established a culture system of whole-embryo dissected from the pregnant PdgfraEGFP; PdgfraCre; ROSAtdTomato mice at E9.5 and examined behaviour of Pdgfra-expressing (EGFP+) mesenchymal cells residing just beneath the non-neural ectoderm by two-photon live imaging (Fig. 4a). The border between the ectoderm and the underlying mesenchyme was defined by the Pdgfra promoter-driven histone H2B-EGFP expression together with Hoechst33342 nuclear staining (Fig. 4b). The live imaging showed that the EGFP+/tdTomato+ mesenchymal cells aligned vertically beneath the surface ectoderm in the trunk gradually elongated into the ectodermal epithelium and ultimately divided to generate the EGFP−/tdTomato+ epithelial cells in the non-neural ectoderm (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 6a and Supplementary Movie 1). Similar behaviour, in which the EGFP+/tdTomato+ mesenchymal cells gave rise to EGFP-/Ecad+ epithelial cells, was also observed in the head region (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 6b). To define the developmental timing of mesenchymal cells that give rise to ectodermal epithelial cells, we used PdgfraEGFP/CreERT2; ROSAtdTomato mice and labelled Pdgfra-expressing mesenchymal cells by E7.5- TAM administration. Live imaging of cultured E9.5 embryos confirmed the presence of E7.5-TAM-labelled Pdgfra-lineage cells in the surface ectoderm in the trunk area (Supplementary Fig. 6c), and these cells subsequently developed into stratified Krt5-expressing epidermal keratinocytes by E14.5 (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Together with the TAM-chase data, these findings indicate that mesenchymal cells residing directly beneath the ectodermal epithelium at E8.5 give rise to epidermal keratinocyte progenitors via cell divisions crossing the ectoderm–mesoderm boundary by E9.5.

Ectomesenchyme contributes to epidermal cell formation in the skin

To investigate the developmental origin of mesenchymal cells that give rise to epidermal keratinocyte progenitors, we traced the Myelin protein zero (P0)-lineage cells in P0Cre; ROSAtdTomato mice and Paired-related homeobox 1 (Prx1)-lineage cells in Prx1Cre; ROSAtdTomato mice (Fig. 5a). P0 and Prx1 mark ectoderm-derived neural crest cells (NCCs)30 and mesoderm-derived mesenchyme in the limb bud31, respectively. Adult epidermis was predominantly composed of P0-lineage cells, but not Prx1-lineage cells (Fig. 5a). Consistently, Prx1-Cre labelled neither the non-neural ectoderm nor the underlying mesenchyme at E8.5 but labelled some Pdgfra-expressing cranial mesenchymal cells at E9.5 (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). To further assess mesodermal contribution, we used the Mesp1-Cre to label early mesodermal cells at embryonic stages32. Mesp1-lineage cells did not contribute to Krt5-expressing epidermal keratinocytes in neonatal mouse skin (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). As an internal control, a small number of CD45-expressing Mesp1-lineage cells were observed in the epidermis (Supplementary Fig. 8c), consistent with a mesodermal contribution to hematopoietic lineages such as Langerhans cells. These findings indicate that mesodermal mesenchyme dose not measurably contribute to the Pdgfra-lineage keratinocyte population.

Since Wnt1 is another established marker of NCCs32–34, we traced Wnt1-lineage cells in newborn PdgfraEGFP; Wnt1Cre2; ROSAtdTomato mice. Only the head epidermis was predominantly composed of Wnt1-lineage cells, indicating that Wnt1-lineage contribution to epidermal development is largely restricted to the head skin in newborn and adult mice (Fig. 5b–d and Supplementary Fig. 21). Because Pdgfra-lineage epidermal cells arose from ectomesenchymal cells directly beneath the ectodermal epithelium at E8.5 (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 6a–d), we examined the hindbrain cranial neural fold at E8.25, when cranial ectomesenchyme emerges7,11, in Wnt1- and P0-lineage tracing mice (Fig. 5e–g). A small number of Wnt1-lineage NCCs delaminated from the neural domain, but not from the non-neural (Ecad+) domain of the hindbrain cranial neural fold at E8.25 (Fig. 5f). By contrast, many Wnt1-lineage-negative but P0-lineage-positive cranial ectomesenchymal cells delaminated from the non-neural domain (Fig. 5g). Wnt1-lineage cranial NCCs and Wnt1-lineage-negative cranial ectomesenchymal cells, both of which expressed the delamination-associated factor Sox98,35, were predominantly observed in the midbrain and hindbrain, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9). No Pdgfra-expressing cells were detected in the non-neural ectoderm (Krt8+) between E8.25 and E9.5 in the head and trunk regions (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 5a), indicating that Pdgfra expression was restricted to delaminated mesenchymal cells during this period. Notably, P0-lineage, Pdgfra-expressing mesenchymal cells were detected in the head, back, and abdominal skin of newborn mice, accounting for approximately 20–60% of the populations, and this population increased to more than 90% in adult skin (Supplementary Figs. 10a and 22). By contrast, Wnt1-lineage fibroblasts were detected only in the head skin, accounting for approximately 40% of the fibroblast population in both newborn and adult mice (Supplementary Figs. 10b and 22).

To assess whether delaminated ectomesenchyme contributes to epidermal keratinocyte development in the head and trunk skin, we examined Pax7-Cre traced keratinocytes in Pax7CreERT2; ROSAtdTomato mice, as Pax7-Cre has been reported to trace ectomesenchymal lineage cells9. Both Pdgfra-lineage cells and E7.5-TAM-labelled Pax7-lineage cells exhibited a characteristic linear, segmental arrangement in the back skin of newborn and E18.5 mice, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). In addition, E7.5-TAM-labelled Pax7-lineage cells gave rise to Krt5-expressing keratinocytes in both head and trunk (back) skin (Supplementary Fig. 11c). Together, these data suggest that delaminated ectomesenchymal populations contribute to epidermal keratinocyte development in both head and trunk skin, although a direct contribution of Wnt1- and/or P0-expressing non-neural ectodermal cells between E8.5 and 9.5 cannot be excluded.

Induction of cranial ectomesenchyme from hiPSCs

To evaluate our in vivo findings, we developed a strategy to induce ectomesenchyme from hiPSCs that enables investigation of early embryonic developmental events. hiPSCs were cultured on laminin 511 under feeder-free conditions in StemFit culture medium for 10 days (−10 to 0 d in Fig. 6a–c) to obtain undifferentiated hiPSC colonies, and then switched to differentiation medium (D-med) for the next 10 days (0 to 10 d in Fig. 6b, c) to induce neural plate border (NPB) cells flanked by neural and non-neural ectodermal cells36. This timing is equivalent to mouse embryonic stage E8.25–E8.5 prior to cranial neural tube closure (Fig. 6a).

To confirm 10 d as the onset of cranial ectomesenchymal development in this system, we harvested cells over a 14-day of D-med culture (0–14 d) and analysed their gene expression at each time-point by qPCR. PAX7, an NPB marker, increased at 4 d, remained elevated until 10 d, and then abruptly decreased9,37,38 (Supplementary Fig. 12a). In contrast, the ectomesenchyme markers TFAP2A and NCAD (N-cadherin) sharply increased at 10 d and remained elevated thereafter. The undifferentiated iPSC marker OCT4 and NANOG gradually decreased, transiently increased at 10 d, and were then maintained at low levels. Among epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers, SNAI2 increased at 10 d but decreased at 11 d (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Together, these data supported 10 day as the critical time point at which hiPSC-derived NPB cells transition into non-neural cranial ectomesenchyme (iEMC)39.

At 10 d, two distinct cellular structures formed in the central and peripheral zones (Fig. 6c). To identify the cell lineages in these zones, we performed scRNA-seq, qPCR analyses and immunostaining (Fig. 6d, e and Supplementary Figs. 12b–e and 13a–d). Integrated clustering of undifferentiated hiPSCs and differentiated cells revealed that cells in the central and peripheral zones had typical gene expression profiles for neural and non-neural ectoderm, respectively8,40 (Supplementary Fig. 12b–e). No clusters indicative of mesodermal or endodermal fates was identified, confirming the exclusive induction of cranial ectodermal cells. The central zone expressed neural ectoderm markers PAX6, NCAD and SOX1, whereas the peripheral zone exclusively expressed the non-neural ectoderm markers DNP63, TFAP2A and ECAD (E-cadherin).

At 10 d, clustering analysis further separated peripheral cells into two clusters, Peri1 and Peri2 (Fig. 6d). Peri1 expressed NPB-specific genes, including PAX3, PAX7, DLX5, MSX1 and MSX238,41, whereas Peri2 expressed mesenchymal genes such as COL1A1, COL1A2, THY1 and TWIST1 (Fig. 6e). Notably, Peri2 showed significantly higher expression of pluripotent stem cell transcription factors OCT4, NANOG and other different stem cell markers, suggesting stem cell activation in this cluster42,43. The central zone cluster lacked NPB-specific genes and was particularly fated to neural ectoderm (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Figs. 12b, c and 13a–c). These data suggest that Peri1 and Peri2 represent distinct differentiation states corresponding to NPB cells and delaminated cranial ectomesenchyme, respectively. We also observed that part of the cells in the periphery expressing an ectomesenchyme/EMT marker, NCAD (Supplementary Fig. 13d). These NCAD+ cells were presumably the iEMC progenitors. Additionally, the peripheral zone contains some satellite cells from the central zone, as confirmed by PAX6 immunostaining (Supplementary Fig. 13a, c), and neural makers were also expressed in the periphery (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Importantly, these satellites did not express ECAD (Supplementary Fig. 13c). In brief, these peripherals did not affect subsequent iEMC induction because the center lacked iEMC markers (P75 (NGFR) and ECAD) (Supplementary Figs. 12f and 23).

Previous studies demonstrated that cranial ectomesenchyme develops from the non-neural domain of the NPB at E8.25–8.5, similar to ECAD-expressing mesenchymal cells7,11. In addition, hiPSC-derived cranial ectomesenchyme via NCCs has been characterised as P75-positive and ECAD-negative (P75+/ECAD−) cells40. Based on these reports, we used P75 and ECAD expression to identify hiPSC-derived NCCs (iNCC) (P75+/ECAD−) and non-neural ectomesenchyme (P75+/ECAD+). Prior to induction, we confirmed that P75+/ECAD+ cells were uniquely present in the periphery, but not in the center, of hiPSC-derived cell cultures (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 12f). Because transcriptomic analysis revealed that P75+ cells were predominant in Peri2 (Fig. 6e), we carefully removed the central zone at 10 d and then cultured only dissociated peripheral cells in ectomesenchyme-inducing medium (Ecto-med) for an additional 4 d until 14 d (Fig. 6f, g).

Immunostaining of peripheral cells at 14 d revealed SOX9 nuclear staining in both ECAD+ and ECAD- cells (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 14a, b). Consistent with the immunostaining pattern in the hindbrain region of the E8.25 mouse embryo (Fig. 5f), SOX9 intensity was significantly higher in ECAD− cells compare to that of ECAD+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b), delineating SOX9high/ECAD− cells and SOX9low/ECAD+ cells as iNCC and pre-migratory ectomesenchyme (prem.iEMC), respectively.

FACS-sorted peripheral cells at 14 d revealed that iEMC fraction (P75+/ECAD+) consisted of both integrin beta 4 (ITGB4)-positive and -negative fractions. In directed differentiation of ectomesenchyme from hiPSCs, ECAD-positive iEMC were mainly induced together with ECAD-negative NCC (Supplementary Figs. 14c, d and 23). Because ITGB4 is expressed in epidermal keratinocytes, we hypothesised that P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4− cells represented canonical iEMC for epidermal keratinocyte differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 14c, d). To test this, we FACS-sorted iNCC fraction (P75+/ECAD− cells) and prem.iEMC fraction (p75+/ECAD+/ITGB4− cells) from peripheral cells cultured for 14 d and performed qPCR analysis. The P75+/ECAD− cell population particularly expressed the NCC-marker genes FOXD3 and SOX10, and showed higher SOX9 expression than P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4− cells (Fig. 6h), further supporting their identity as iNCC37,40. By contrast, P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4− cells exclusively expressed the non-neural ectodermal fate specification genes ECAD, GRHL3 and KLF5 compared with iNCC (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 15a)44. Of note, cranial neural plate border markers (PAX7, FGF8, and MSX1) were expressed significantly more in prem.iEMC than P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4+ cells.

To evaluate the functionality of P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4− cells, we cultured FACS-sorted P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4− cells and transferred them to ectomesenchyme maturation until 21 d. At 35 d, gene expression profiles of prem.iEMC and iEMC were compared by qPCR (Fig. 6i). We observed significantly reduced expression of the ectodermal/epithelial genes P75, ECAD and TFAP2A and increase expression of mesenchymal genes PDGFRA, NCAD and TWIST1, indicating EMT of P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4− cells to mature iEMC (Supplementary Fig. 15a). In addition, the morphology of prem.iEMC, P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4+, and iEMC differed clearly under ectomesenchyme-inducing culture conditions (Supplementary Fig. 15b).

Keratinocyte-generating potential of iEMC

Finally, we evaluated the ability of iEMC to transition into keratinocytes. Upon maturation, iEMC exhibited a fibroblastic morphology (Fig. 7a) and expressed the mesenchymal markers PDGFRA and SNAI2 (Fig. 7b). CFU assays of iEMC indicated a CFE of 1.46 ± 0.66% (Fig. 7c). To evaluate the mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition (MET) activity of iEMC during keratinocyte generation, mature iEMC were seeded on 3T3-J2 feeder cells in keratinocyte-growth medium (Fig. 7d). After 10–14 d, iEMC formed epithelial colonies expressing keratinocyte markers including keratin 5 (KRT5), keratin 14 (KRT14), ECAD and P63 (Fig. 7e). We also confirmed KRT5, KRT14, deltaN(DN)-P63 and COL17A1 expressions in iEMC-derived epithelial colonies, but not in iEMC themselves (Fig. 7f). These data support the MET potential of iEMC to generate keratinocytes. We also investigated the MET potential of iNCC (P75+/ECAD−). iNCC expressed neural markers but lacked keratinocyte markers (Supplementary Fig. 16a, b), indicating minimal, if any, MET potential. The CFE of P75−/ECAD+ surface ectodermal cells (iSEC) was significantly lower (0.47 ± 0.28%) that of iEMC (17.42 ± 4.50%) (Supplementary Figs. 14a and 16c, d). Altogether, these data strongly suggest that cranial ectomesenchyme has greater potential than neural crest cells and surface ectoderm to generate epidermal keratinocyte stem cells.

Discussion

The concept of ectodermal mesenchyme, referred to as “mesectoderm,” was first introduced in 189310,45. Studies over the last decades identified two ectoderm-derived mesenchymal populations delaminating from the cranial neural fold: an initial wave arising from the E-cadherin-positive non-neural domain around E8.5, followed by a second wave arising from the N-cadherin-positive neural domain just before neural tube closure7,8,10. Although cranial ectomesenchyme/NCCs have been extensively studied26,42,46,47, their specific roles in skin development remain poorly defined.

In this study, TAM-labelled Pdgfra-lineage, ectodermal-lineage, and mesodermal-lineage tracing, together with scRNA-seq/scATAC-seq analyses and live imaging of cultured mouse embryos, suggest that cranial and trunk ectomesenchymal cells located directly beneath the non-neural ectodermal epithelium at E8.5 contribute to epidermal keratinocyte development through a MET-like process within a restricted developmental window of approximately E8.5–9.5. Two-photon live imaging visualized the asymmetric division of ectomesenchyme across the ectoderm–mesenchyme boundary in cultured E9.5 embryos, producing mesenchymal and epithelial daughter cells with distinct chromatin states marked by Pdgfra and Cdh1 gene, respectively. These findings suggest that surface ectodermal cues recruit mesenchymal cells and trigger asymmetric cell division. We also established a hiPSC-based culture system to generate ectoderm-derived mesenchymal cells (iEMC) and demonstrated their keratinocyte-generating potential.

Pdgfra-lineage and non-Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes largely overlapped across transcriptomic clusters, except for a Thy1-positive stem cell-like population that was predominantly enriched within the Pdgfra-lineage keratinocyte compartment and exhibited significantly greater self-renewal capacity in vitro than its non-Pdgfra-lineage counterpart. A previous study likewise identified Thy1-positive cells as slow-cycling epidermal stem cells in the mouse skin18. These findings suggest that Pdgfra-lineage and non-Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes may serve broadly similar functions under physiological conditions, while Pdgfra-lineage cells may make a greater contribution to epidermal maintenance or regeneration. This interpretation is consistent with our finding that the proportion of Pdgfra-lineage cells increased postnatally from approximately 40% to nearly 100% among interfollicular basal keratinocyte population in both head and back skin. Correctively, these data suggest that an initially small ectomesenchyme-derived epidermal progenitor fraction subsequently expands within the epidermal compartment. Although we did not detect ongoing Pdgfra-expressing cells in the non-neural ectoderm or developing epidermis, a direct epithelial contribution to the Pdgfra-lineage keratinocyte population, independent of MET, cannot be fully excluded. Likewise, although our data argue against a substantial ongoing ectomesenchymal contribution after E9.5, a more precise assessment of any continued ectomesenchymal contribution to keratinocyte development in embryonic and neonatal skin will require further investigation. In this context, scATAC-seq analysis of neonatal mouse head skin identified a small but distinct keratinocyte subcluster with relatively high chromatin accessibility at the Pdgfra locus, suggesting that limited MET activity may persist, at least in neonatal head skin. This cluster also showed relatively increased chromatin accessibility at the Fzd7 locus, which encodes a receptor involved in canonical Wnt signaling20. Given the established role of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in epidermal keratinocyte development48, the Fzd7/Wnt axis may represent a candidate cue promoting the induction of Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes from ectomesenchyme. Further studies will be needed to define the molecular and cellular interactions between ectomesenchyme and surface ectoderm that govern this process.

The regional distribution of lineage-labelled keratinocytes further suggests that epidermal development is more heterogeneous than previously appreciated. In the head skin, epidermal keratinocytes uniquely include a substantial contribution from Wnt1-lineage cells, which account for approximately 60% of keratinocytes in both newborn and adult mice. In aged mice, the Pdgfra-lineage keratinocyte population occupies more than 90% of basal keratinocytes, indicating substantial overlap between Wnt1-lineage and Pdgfra-lineage populations. Taken together with the absence of obvious Pdgfra-expressing cells in the ectodermal epithelium before and after birth, these findings are consistent with the possibility that Wnt1-lineage ectomesenchymal populations contribute to epidermal keratinocyte development in the head skin, although a direct contribution from Wnt1-expressing non-neural ectodermal cells cannot be fully excluded.

In the trunk skin, by contrast, Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes were not labelled by the Wnt1-Cre. Previous studies have shown that, around E8.25, Wnt1-lineage cranial neural crest cells (CNCCs) predominantly delaminate from the midbrain, whereas P0-lineage cranial ectomesenchyme delaminates from the hindbrain46. Consistent with this, our embryonic analyses showed that P0-lineage, but not Wnt1-lineage, ectomesenchymal cells emerge from the non-neural domain of the hindbrain neural fold and reside immediately beneath the non-neural ectodermal epithelium at E8.25, supporting their contribution to trunk keratinocyte development. Mesp1-Cre lineage tracing further argued against a mesodermal contribution to keratinocytes development, supporting ectomesenchymal contribution in the trunk skin. Nevertheless, the precise developmental origins of P0-lineage keratinocytes are not fully resolved, and these lineage relationships should be interpreted with caution.

E7.5-TAM-labelled Pax7-Cre-lineage cells also give rise to K5-expressing epidermal keratinocytes in both head and trunk skin. Both Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes and E7.5-TAM-labelled Pax7-lineage keratinocytes exhibit similar characteristic linear distribution pattern, generating segmentally arranged keratinocytes in trunk skin. Because Pax7 is an early NPB and premigratory neural crest marker associated with ectomesenchymal derivatives9, the similar distribution patterns of E7.5-TAM-labelled Pax7-lineage keratinocytes and Pdgfra-lineage keratinocytes are consistent with a contribution of ectomesenchymal lineages to keratinocyte development. The segmental pattern is reminiscent of Blaschko’s line49,50, which has long been interpreted as reflecting clonal expansion of developing keratinocyte progenitors in human skin. Mutations in KRT1 or KRT10 in these cells can drive pathological clonal expansion along Blaschko’s line, as in linear epidermal nevi. Although our data do not establish a direct developmental origin for this pattern, they raise the possibility that ectomesenchymal lineage-related keratinocyte populations contribute to its formation. Future clonal tracing of ectomesenchymal populations will be important for testing this idea quantitatively.

Previously, we reported that HMGB1released from necrotic cells mobilizes bone marrow–derived Pdgfra-expressing cells, which contribute to epidermal regeneration in necrotic skin5. We also described the physiological circulation of Pdgfra-lineage mesenchymal cells in foetal mice with transcriptional signatures reminiscent of neural crest cell51. Other studies identified rare populations of P0- and Wnt1-lineage ectodermal mesenchymal stem cells in bone marrow, co-expressing Pdgfra and Sca152. These Pdgfra/Sca1 double-positive fibroblasts are found in multiple tissues and are thought to contribute to tissue homeostasis53. Notably, subcutaneous Pdgfra/Sca1 double-positive fibroblasts exhibit bipotent potential to regenerate both dermal fibroblasts and epidermal keratinocytes following severe skin injury54. Collectively, these data support a role for Pdgfra-expressing mesenchymal cells, possibly including ectomesenchyme-derived populations, in postnatal tissue maintenance and homeostasis.

Finally, we established a platform for evaluating epidermal keratinocyte generation using iEMC. This system mimics early developmental events around neural tube closure and generates two ectoderm-derived mesenchymal populations identified by p75 and E-cadherin expression. Using this system, we showed that iEMC can differentiate into keratinocyte-like cells. Our rationale for using p75 as a selection marker was twofold. It has been used to isolate multipotent human mesenchymal stem/stromal cell populations55, and it is also associated with neural crest identity, making it a reasonable marker for cells emerging from the neural plate border during delamination56. In addition, the initial wave of ectomesenchyme arising from the non-neural domain of the cranial neural fold has been reported to retain E-cadherin expression. However, definitive confirmation will require tracing ectomesenchyme development using more specific markers. Identification of such markers will help clarify the mechanisms by which ectomesenchyme contributes to epidermal stem cell generation.

Although the precise mechanisms by which cranial ectomesenchyme gives rise to epidermal stem cells remain incomprehensively defined, iEMC provide a promising model system for investigating the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying human skin development, homeostasis and regeneration.

Tamoxifen injection and sample collection from newborn mice

Tamoxifen (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was dissolved in sunflower seed oil (Merck) to a concentration of 20 mg/mL. Pregnant females were injected intraperitoneally with tamoxifen at a dosage of 70 µg/g body weight. In cases of overdue births, pups were delivered by caesarean section.

Immunostaining of mouse tissues

Collected skin tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C overnight and then incubated with 30% sucrose solution. After sufficient sucrose replacement, the samples were embedded in O.C.T. compound (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan). Cryosections (8 µm) were prepared on glass slides (Matsunami Glass, Osaka, Japan). Samples were blocked with blocking buffer (3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.3% TritonTM×-100 (Merck) in PBS) at 20–25 °C and then incubated with primary antibodies: Krt5 (1:500, Poly9059; BioLegend, San Diego, CA), GFP (EYFP) (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), CD45 (1:100, 30-F11; BioLegend), Krt8 (1:100, 1E8; BioLegend), SOX9 (1:1000; Merck) or CD324 (E-cadherin, conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647) (1:100, DECMA-1; BioLegend) at 4 °C or 20–25 °C overnight. After washing three times with PBS, the samples were labelled for 1–2 h at 20–25 °C with secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 594 (1:500, anti-rabbit; Thermo Fisher Science, Waltham, MA), Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, anti-goat; Thermo Fisher Science), Alexa Fluor 594 (1:500, anti-rat; Thermo Fisher Science or Alexa Fluor 647 (1:500, anti-chicken; Abcam). Following washing with PBS three times, nuclei were labelled with Hoechest33342 (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and mounted in ProLong Gold antifade mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Confocal images were acquired using a LSM880/LSM980 confocal microscope with Airyscan (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Animal tissue-clearing and immunostaining

After fixing the embryo with 4% PFA at 20–25 °C for 15 min, tissue clearing was conducted using CUBIC-L (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) and CUBIC-R + (M) (Tokyo Chemical Industry) reagents as previously described66,67. Sox9 (1:100; Merck) and CD324 (E-cadherin) conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 (1:100, DECMA-1; BioLegend) were applied and incubated at 37 °C for 48–72 h. After washing with PBS, the samples were incubated with secondary antibody. Nuclei were labelled with DAPI (1:1000; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Confocal images were acquired using an Ti2-E confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). 3D image reconstruction was performed using Imaris 9.1.2 (Oxford Instruments, Oxfordshire, United Kingdom).

Cell preparation from mouse tissues

For whole-layer preparation, the skin cell suspension was prepared based on a protocol with a few modifications68. To harvest adult skin samples, hair was removed using an electronic hair clipper, followed by hair removal cream (Epilat, Kracie, Tokyo, Japan), and then subcutaneous tissue was carefully scraped off with forceps. Liberase Mix consisting of RPMI1640 medium (NACALAI TESQUE, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 300 µg/mL LiberaseTM (Merck), 1U DNaseI (Takara, Shiga, Japan), and 5% foetal bovine serum (FBS), was used for the skin digestion. The minced skin tissue was incubated twice with 4 mL Liberase Mix in a 37 °C water bath and subsequently incubated with 2–4 mL TryPLE Select (1×) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 1U DNaseI. After 10 min of incubation at 37 °C, the sample was pipetted thoroughly in RPMI1640 supplemented with 5% FBS and filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer. Following centrifugation at 300 × g for 5–10 min, the remaining pellet was suspended in 200 µL MEMα (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 2% FBS. For newborn single-cell RNA-seq, epidermal cells and dermal fibroblasts were collected separately. The skin was soaked at 4 °C overnight in a solution containing 2000 PU/mL DISPASEII (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan). Subsequently, the epidermis and dermis were separated and washed with MEMα (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 2% FBS. Each tissue was minced and then incubated with 2–3 mL of TryPLE Select (1×) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 1U DNaseI. After incubation in a 37 °C water bath for 30–40 min, the cell supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5–10 min. The remained pellet was then suspended in 200 µL of MEMα supplemented with 2% FBS.

Flow cytometry analysis of cells isolated from mouse tissues

After blocking non-specific antibody binding with purified anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (S17011E; BioLegend) for 5 min at 4 °C, the cells were stained with the following fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C: CD326 (EpCAM)-Alexa Fluor 647 (G8.8, BioLegend), CD45-BV421 (30-F11; BioLegend), CD34-BV421 (MEC14.7; BioLegend), CD31-BV421 (390; BioLegend), ITGA6-APC/Cy7 (GoH3; BioLegend), CD90.2 (THY1)-APC (53-2.1; BioLegend), Alexa Fluor 647 Rat IgG2a, κ isotype control (BioLegend), BV421 Rat IgG2a, κ isotype control (BioLegend), BV421 Rat IgG2b, κ isotype control (BioLegend), APC/Cy7 Rat IgG2a, κ isotype control (BioLegend), or APC Rat IgG2a, κ isotype control (BioLegend). Dead cells were identified by staining with SYTOXTM Blue Dead Cell Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a BD FACS Aria III instrument (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and data were analysed using the FlowJo Software program (BD Biosciences). The gating strategies for all flow cytometry experiments are shown in Supplementary Figs. 17–22 .

PCR

Targets were amplified by performing at 94 °C for 2 min and 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 1 min. Extension was performed at 72 °C for 1 min, followed by final extension at 72 °C for 2 min. PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on 1.8% agarose gels. Fragments with apparent sizes were 408 bp (Cre) and 324 bp (internal control).

Whole-embryo culture and live imaging

PdgfraEGFP; PdgfraCre; ROSAtdTomato and PdgfraEGFP/CreERT2; ROSAtdTomato mice were used for whole embryo culture and live imaging. The whole embryo was cultured according to the previous report with some modification69,70. For basal medium, we used DMEM/Ham’s F-12 with L-Gln, Sodium Pyruvate and HEPES, without Phenol Red, liquid (DMEM/F-12; Thermo Fisher Scientific). E9.5 embryos were dissected from the uteri of PdgfraEGFP; PdgfraCre; ROSAtdTomato and PdgfraEGFP/CreERT2; ROSAtdTomato mice in DMEM/F-12 preheated at 37 °C. After dissection, embryos were cultured in the culture media containing 50% DMEM/F-12 and 50% mouse serum, collected from C57BL/6JJcl by using capiject CJ-2AS (Terumo Japan, Tokyo, Japan). For nuclei staining, embryos were incubated in the culture media containing Hoechst33342 (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 30–60 min before live imaging. Then, embryos were transferred to 35 mmφ glass-bottomed culture dish (Matsunami Glass) with freshly prepared culture media and observed using an inverted two-photon excitation microscope (A1R-MP, Nikon) with a × 25 silicone-immersion objective lens. The microscope stage was kept at 34–36 °C. Multiphoton images were acquired at every 15 min with 900-nm laser excitation for Hoechst33342 and GFP and 1040-nm laser excitation for tdTomato. 3D image reconstruction and data analysis were performed using Imaris 9.1.2 (Oxford instruments).

Cell lines and culture conditions

The human iPS line 201B7 was provided by the RIKEN Bio Resource Center (Tsukuba, Japan)71. The cells were cultured in StemFit medium (Ajinomoto, Tokyo, Japan) on plates coated with Laminin-511E8 (0.5 μg/cm2) (Laminin-511E8 (iMatrix-511 silk): Nippi, Tokyo, Japan). Cells were passaged every seven days, and the medium was changed every 2–3 d72. All experimental procedures involving recombinant DNA were approved by the Recombinant DNA Committee of The University of Osaka and were performed according to institutional guidelines. Mouse 3T3-J2 cells (Kerafast, Boston, MA) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM: Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% bovine calf serum (CS: Cytiba, Tokyo, Japan). For feeder cells in the colony-forming unit assay, 3T3-J2 cells were treated with 8 μg/mL mitomycin C (FUJIFILM Wako) in DMEM containing 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Differentiation of hiPSCs into ectodermal cells

The hiPSC differentiation protocol is depicted in Fig. 5a. Cells were seeded onto Laminin-511E8-coated plates at a density of 100–150 cells/cm2 and cultured in StemFit medium for 10 d. To induce cranial ectodermal cells, the StemFit medium was replaced with differentiation medium (D-med) formulated with Glasgow-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% Knockout Serum Replacement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mM L-Alanyl-L-glutamine solution (NACALAI TESQUE), 55 μM monothioglycerol (FUJIFILM Wako), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (NACALAI TESQUE).

Induction of pre-migratory ectomesenchyme from cranial ectoderm

After 10 d, two distinct zones formed: a center neural ectoderm and periphery non-neural ectoderm. The center was manually removed using a micropipette tip under a microscope. The peripheral cells were dissociated using Accutase (NACALAI TESQUE) for 30 min at 37 °C. The dissociated cells were seeded onto Laminin-511E8-coated plates and cultured in ectomesenchyme-inducing medium (Ecto-med) consisting of DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 2% B27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mM L-Alanyl-L-glutamine solution, 3 μM CHIR99021 (FUJIFILM Wako), 10 μM Y-27632 (Tocris, Bristol, UK), 0.05% BSA (FUJIFILM Wako), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2/5% O2 for four days73,74.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting for hiPSCs

Periphery cells were dissociated with Accutase for 30 min at 37 °C, and the harvested cells were filtered with a 40 μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences). The cells were then stained using antibodies against CD271 (P75) (BioLegend), CD324 (ECAD) (BioLegend), and CD104 (ITGB4) (BioLegend) for 45 min on ice. Stained cells were washed twice with cold 2% FBS in PBS and subsequently sorted using a FACS Aria III instrument (BD Biosciences). Data acquisition was performed using BD FACS Diva Software (BD Biosciences), and data analysis was conducted using the FlowJo Software program (BD Biosciences). The gating strategies for all flow cytometry experiments are shown in Supplementary Fig. 23.

Differentiation of pre-migratory ectomesenchyme into ectomesenchyme

Sorted pre-migratory ectomesenchyme (P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4−) were cultured in ectomesenchyme medium on Laminin-511E8-coated plates. For the initial passage, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in 12-well plates coated with Biocoat Collagen I (Corning, Corning, NY). After culturing one or two passages on Biocoat, the ectomesenchyme (iEMC) were prepared for the further experiments.

Differentiation of epithelial cells from pre-migratory ectomesenchyme

Sorted human iEMC (P75+/ECAD+/ITGB4−) and iNCC (P75+/ECAD−) were seeded on Laminin-511E8-coated plates. Subsequently, the cells were cultured in CnT-07 medium (CELLnTEC Advanced Cell Systems, Bern, Switzerland) supplemented with 10 μM SB431542 (FUJIFILM Wako) and 10 μM Y-27632 for 5–14 d.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells in 12-well plates were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min at 20–25 °C and then washed three times with Tris-buffered saline (TBS; Takara) for 5 min each. The samples were incubated for 1 h in TBS containing 5% donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA) and 0.3% Triton ×-100 (FUJIFILM Wako). They were then probed overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against P63 (1:200, 4A4; Abcam), KRT5 (1:200, Poly19055; BioLegend), KRT14 (1:1000, Poly19053; BioLegend), SNAI2 (1:100; Abcam), PDGFRA (1:100, D13C6; Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA), ECAD (1:200, G-10; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), P75 (1:1000; Merck), SOX9 (1:1000; Merck), TUBB3 (1:1000; Abcam), NCAD (1:200, Abcam), Nestin (1:200, SP103; Abcam), Peripherin (1:200; Abcam), or ZEB1 (1:200, H-3; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), PAX6 (1:300, PRB-278P, BioLegend) in TBS containing 1% donkey serum and 0.3% Triton ×-100. Subsequently, the samples were labelled for 1 h at 20–25 °C with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 and 568 (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cells were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000; Dojindo) to visualise the nuclei, and imaged using a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan).

qRT-PCR

Differentiated hiPSCs were harvested at specific time points and lysed using QIAzol reagent (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands). Total RNA was extracted from the lysates, and reverse transcription was performed using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix for qRT-PCR (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). The synthesised cDNA was subsequently amplified on a StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using TaqMan Fast Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The targets were amplified using TaqMan MGB probe assays against TaqMan probes (Supplementary Table 1). Thermocycling conditions for the TaqMan reactions consisted of an initial cycle at 95 °C for 20 s, followed by 45 cycles at 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s.

Colony-forming unit assay for mouse and human samples

To evaluate the self-renewal capacity of MSCs, iEMC was seeded at a density of 2000–4000 cells/well in 6-well plates coated with Biocoat Collagen I (Corning). They were cultured in a 1:1 mixture of mesenchymal stem cell growth medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) and mesenchymal stem cell growth medium 2 (PromoCell, Heidelberg, Germany) for 7–10 d. Colonies were then stained using the Diff-Quick staining kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For the keratinocyte colony formation assays in mouse and human, cells were seeded onto Mitomycin-C-treated 3T3-J2 feeder cells at a density of 200–500 cells/well (mouse) or 400–4000 cells/well (human) in 12-well plates. The mouse cells were cultured in keratinocyte medium (KCM) composed of a 3:1 mix of DMEM without glutamine and Nutrient Mixture F-12 Ham (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.4 μg/mL hydrocortisone (Merck), 2 nM 3,3,5-triiodo-l-thyronine sodium salt (Merck), 1 nM cholera toxin (Merck), 2 mM L-Alanyl-L-glutamine solution, 10 μg/mL insulin (Merck), 10 μM Y-27632, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (NACALAI TESQUE) containing 5 ng/mL mouse recombinant EGF (BioLegend) and 1 μM RepSox (Selleck Biotechnology, Kanagawa, Japan) for 5–7 d. The human cells were cultured in KCM supplemented with 5 ng/mL human recombinant EGF (Peprotech, Cranbury, NJ) for 10–12 d. After culture, the colonies were fixed with 10% formaldehyde neutral buffer solution for at least 2 h at 20–25 °C and then stained with 2% Rhodamine B solution (FUJIFILM Wako) for 2 h. Colonies were counted under a microscope, and the colony-forming efficiency (CFE) was calculated as the number of colonies per number of seeded cells × 100.

Plate sorting-based single-cell RNA-seq in mice

To ensure accurate sorting for single-cell RNA-seq, dead cells were identified by staining with SYTOX™ Blue Dead Cell Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific) immediately before sorting. Target cells were sorted using a BD FACS Aria III instrument (BD Biosciences). The single-cell RNA-seq protocol followed previously described reports with minor modifications75–77. Briefly, 1 ng of amplified cDNA was used for titration, and libraries were sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 platform. The read length was set to 20 (read 1) + 8 (i7) + 8 (i5) + 51 (read 2) bases.

Droplet-based single-cell RNA-seq for hiPS cells

Following collection, cells were washed with PBS supplemented with 0.04% BSA and subsequently resuspended at the appropriate concentration. Libraries were prepared using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Kit v3.1 (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 platform. The read length was set to 28 (read 1) + 8 (i7) + 0 (i5) + 91 (read 2) bases.

Bioinformatics

Sequence outputs were demultiplexed using bcl2fastq78 and cell ranger mkfastq79 for plate-sorting and droplet-based single-cell RNA-sequencing, respectively. For bcl2fastq, the following options were used: –use-bases-mask Y20, I8, I8, Y50n, –no-lane-splitting, –minimum-trimmed-read-length 20, and –mask-short-adapter-reads. Demultiplexed fastq files were aligned to the mm10 or GRCh38 genome using the STAR aligner80 and cellRanger count for plate-sorting and droplet-based single-cell RNA-sequencing, respectively. STAR was performed with –soloType CB_UMI_Simple, –soloFeatures Gene, and –soloUMI filtering, and MultiGeneUMI options and the soloCBwhitelistSTARsolo output matrix were merged and analysed. CellRanger analysis was performed using the default options. The aligned count data were analysed using the Seurat package81. Marker genes for each cell cluster were identified using the Seurat FindAllMarkers function with the default options. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the Seurat FindMarkers function with the option test.use = MAST. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed and visualised using clusterProfiler82,83 and enrichplot84. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), gene expression, and other sequenced data were visualised using the Plotly package85 and ggplot286.

Plate sorting-based single-cell ATAC-sequencing

A plate-based single-cell ATAC-seq library was generated using a modified OMNI-ATAC protocol87. In total, approximately 100,000 cells were prepared from each sample. Cells were collected into 1.5 ml tubes and resuspended in 100 μL Lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Tween-20, 0.1% IGEPAL®CA-630 (Merck), and 0.01% Digitonin (NACALAI TESQUE)). The suspension was then placed on ice for 3 min to extract the nuclei. Next, 1 mL ATAC-RSB/T (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, and 0.1% Tween-20) was added to the cell suspension, mixed by inversion, and centrifuged. The nuclear pellet was washed and resuspended in 100 μL Transposition mix (50 μL 2× TD buffer (Illumina, San Diego, CA), 1 μL 1% Digitonin, 1 μL 10% Tween-20, 33 μL 1 × PBS, 5 μL TDE1 (Illumina), and 10 μL nuclease-free water) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to perform nuclear tagmentation. Tagmented nuclei were sorted using a FACS Aria III (BD Biosciences) into a 384-well plate, where each well contained 2 μL of a primer set (10 μM each) with unique barcode sequences. In each well, 2 μL NEBNext Hi-Fi 2× PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, MA) was added, and each single nuclei library was amplified using the following conditions: 72 °C for 5 min; 98 °C for 30 s; 20 cycles of amplification at 98 °C for 10 s, 63 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. After pooling the libraries, purification and size selection were performed using Ampure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN) to maintain fragment sizes in the range of 200–600 bp using the following procedure: The library was first treated with a 0.5× volume of beads to remove larger fragments. Additional beads were added to a final volume of 1× to remove smaller fragments. Library quality control was performed using a 4200 TapeStation (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). The library was sequenced on the NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina). The read length was set as 39 (read 1) + 6 (i7) + 6 (i5) + 39 (read 2) bases.

Single-cell ATAC-sequencing data analysis

FASTQ files for single-cell ATAC sequencing data were processed using TrimGalore with the following parameters: –paired, –trim1, –length 30q 0, and –nextera. Next, data were aligned using bowtie2 with the following parameters: -X 2000, –no-mixed, and –no-discordant. The SnapATAC pipeline88 was used to analyse the single-cell ATAC sequencing data. To create a snap file, each BAM file was merged and sorted by read name using samtools89. The snap file was generated using Snaptools (https://github.com/r3fang/SnapTools) with the following parameters: genome name = mm10, –min-mapq = 30, –min-flen = 0, –max-flen = 2000, –keep-chrm = False, –keep-single = False, –keep-secondary = False, –overwrite = True, and –min-cov = 100. Further analyses followed methods outlined in a previous report90. Peak calling for each cluster was performed using the runMACS function in the SnapATAC pipeline, with the following parameters: –nomodel, qval 5e-2, B, and keep-dup all. Union peaks were created using bedtools merging functions with default settings and a cell-by-peak count matrix was created in these peak regions using snaptools. The gene accessibility score was calculated using the createGmatFromMat function in the SnapATAC pipeline and normalised using the scaleCountMatrix function with the following parameters: cov = Matrix::rowSums(bmat) + 1, mat = “gmat”, and method = “RPM”. The normalised values were then smoothed using the runMagic function with a parameter step size = 3. UMAP results were clustered using hclust (method = “ward.D2”) and similar clusters were merged manually. Differentially accessible peaks (DARs) in each cluster were analysed using the findDAR function (method = ”BH”, FDR < 0.05, and logFC > 0). The transcription factor motif was enriched in each DARs set identified using Homer (Top 2000 DARs, -size 500 -mask).

RNA in situ hybridization

RNA in situ hybridization for Pdgfra expression was performed using an RNA scope Fluorescent Multiplex Assay kit (ACD Bio-Techne, Newark, CA, USA) and Pdgfra probe (480661-C3; ACD Bio-Techne). Mouse embryos at E8.5 were fixed in 1% PFA at 4 °C for 15 min, incubated with 30% sucrose solution and the placed in O.C.T. compound (Sakura Finetek). Samples were sectioned at 10 µm and digested with H2O2 and protease III. After hybridization with target probes, the tissue sections were amplified and labelled with fluorophores. Nuclei were labelled with DAPI (1:1000, Dojindo) and slides were imaged using Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss).

Statistical analyses

Data were represented as the mean ± standard error (SE) or standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance analyses were performed via two-tailed student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the JMP Pro ver.17 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) or Prism ver.7 software (GraphPad, Boston, MA). Differences were considered significant at P values below 0.05.
