Identification of Poor Prognosis-Associated Fibroblast Subpopulation Signature Genes Utilizing the Scissor Algorithm to Classify Colorectal Cancer Subtypes and Evaluate the Immune Landscape
Authors: Meng Wang, Jianhui Gu, Jie Zhang, Xing Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsColorectal cancer (CRC) shows high heterogeneity. Conventional bulk transcriptome-based classification methods fail to capture the complex tumor microenvironment of CRC, limiting precision therapy advances. The Scissor algorithm integrates single-cell/bulk transcriptomic data with clinical information to identify prognosis-linked cell subpopulations, offering new insights into tumor heterogeneity resolution.MethodsWe integrated bulk RNA-seq data, single-cell RNA-seq data, and clinical information from The Cancer Genome Atlas and Gene Expression Omnibus databases. The Scissor algorithm was employed to screen fibroblast subpopulations strongly associated with prognosis. Multidimensional analyses, including signature gene identification, functional enrichment analysis, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)-based subtyping, survival analysis, immune landscape assessment, cell–cell communication, mutational profiling, and drug sensitivity prediction, were conducted.ResultsWe identified Scissor+ fibroblast subpopulations significantly correlated with prognosis, whose signature genes were associated with pro-metastatic pathways such as extracellular matrix remodeling and transforming growth factor-beta signaling. GSVA scoring stratified samples into Scissor_high and Scissor_low subtypes, with the former being associated with worse survival outcomes, immunosuppressive microenvironment features (including Treg and M2 macrophage enrichment), and stronger immune evasion tendencies. Cell communication analysis revealed that the Scissor_high subtype strongly interacted with many cell types, with remarkable enrichment in the ANNEXIN, SPP1, and NIF signaling pathways. Drug sensitivity predictions suggested that patients with this cell subtype may respond better to specific anticancer agents (e.g., AZD3759, Erlotinib, Gefitinib).ConclusionsThis study was the first to identify Scissor+ fibroblast subpopulations markedly associated with poor prognosis in CRC, revealing their ability to activate pro-metastatic pathways and immune-activated but functionally exhausted characteristics. Moreover, their predictive value in CRC therapy was revealed. The study provided new perspectives for CRC prognosis evaluation and personalized immune-targeted combination therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent malignancy globally, with approximately 50% of patients developing liver metastases during disease progression, resulting in a 5-year survival rate below 20%.1-3 Although molecular typing based on genomic and bulk transcriptome data (such as consensus molecular subtypes, CMS) to some extent reveals the molecular heterogeneity of CRC,4 this method still has difficulty comprehensively reflecting the complex heterogeneity between and within tumors. Moreover, there are many limitations in clinical applications such as patient stratification, treatment response prediction, and efficacy monitoring.5 Therefore, deeper characterization of heterogeneous features in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of CRC is crucial for advancing mechanistic research and precision therapy.

In recent years, fibroblasts within the TME—particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs)—have garnered extensive attention. CAFs not only play pivotal roles in tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression but are also implicated in CRC liver metastasis.6,7 CAFs remodel the extracellular matrix (ECM) through the secretion of collagen, chemokine, and growth factor to facilitate cancer cell migration and adhesion. They also regulate micrometastasis lesion formation and progression via complex signaling networks with immune and cancer cells.8-10 Furthermore, multiple studies have further emphasized the potential value of CAFs in clinical prognosis and treatment response. For instance, Ma et al.11 discovered that multiple CAF subgroups (such as myCAFs, tCAFs, iCAFs, etc.) were significantly enriched in the CMS4 subtype, and constructed the CAF Prognostic Risk Gene model to predict patient prognosis and chemotherapy resistance based on this. Yu et al.12 classified CRC based on CAF differentiation trajectory genes into C1 and C2 subtypes, where C2 exhibited more marked CAF characteristics, stronger immune evasion, and worse prognosis, and established the HCAFRS prognostic marker. Wang et al.13 proposed the CAFGs scoring system, which also consistently predicted adverse clinical outcomes in multiple cohorts and was associated with the CMS4 subtype, lymphatic invasion, and poor response to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Additionally, Yin et al.14 screened out CAF marker genes closely related to prognosis through large-scale bioinformatics analysis. A review study15 systematically summarized the cell origin, markers, and functional duality of CAFs, highlighting their high complexity and heterogeneity. These studies collectively indicate that the functions and clinical significance of CAFs in CRC are highly complex and exhibit pronounced heterogeneity, which also highlights the urgent need to utilize more refined technical means to deeply analyze their subgroup characteristics and clinical correlations.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology provides a powerful tool for addressing this issue in depth. For instance, Wang et al.16 utilized single-cell technology to reveal CAF heterogeneity across CRC metabolic subtypes. Zeng et al.17 identified a liver metastasis-driving malignant cell population that interacts with fibroblasts via COL1A1-CD44/SDC4 and LAMA4-CD44 signaling axes to promote metastatic lesion formation. Nevertheless, current single-cell studies face challenges including limited sample sizes, incomplete clinical phenotype information, and inconsistent cell annotation methods, hindering direct correlations between specific cell subpopulations and patient survival outcomes.18 To address these limitations, researchers developed the Scissor algorithm, which innovatively integrates single-cell expression, bulk RNA-seq data, and clinical phenotypes to identify disease-relevant cell subpopulations.16 Scissor has been successfully applied across multiple cancers—including breast cancer,19 hepatocellular carcinoma,20 and head-neck squamous cell carcinoma.21 In esophageal cancer, the Scissor algorithm identified Scissorhi and Scissorlow dendritic cell subtypes and constructed a Scissor+ dendritic cell prognostic model for survival and immune response prediction.22 In CRC, Scissor is also used to integrate scRNA-seq data, bulk transcriptome data, and prognostic information. Through this method, researchers identified cell populations associated with a favorable prognosis. Further analysis revealed that in highly differentiated fibroblasts, the key biomarker ADAMDEC1 was highly expressed.23 These findings provide new directions for prognosis assessment and targeted therapy in CRC.

This study collected scRNA-seq data, bulk transcriptome data, and patient survival information related to CRC from public databases, such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). By applying the Scissor algorithm, we identified the Scissor+/– fibroblast subpopulations that were noticeably associated with prognosis. Subsequently, through differential expression analysis, pathway enrichment analysis, and the construction of an immune interaction network, we systematically analyzed the molecular characteristics of the metastasis-promoting Scissor subtype. This study sought to identify potential metastasis-promoting markers and provide a theoretical basis and target support for combined immunotherapy targeting the tumor immune microenvironment. The overall design and technical route are detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Data acquisition and preprocessing

We obtained transcriptomic expression profiles and corresponding clinical information (including age, sex, and tumor stage) for people with CRC (colon adenocarcinoma and rectal adenocarcinoma) from TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The dataset included 600 samples, comprising 549 tumor samples and 51 normal samples. To enhance the robustness of the findings, we downloaded the expression dataset GSE39582 from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) for independent validation of key results, which contained 585 CRC samples. Additionally, we acquired scRNA-seq data (GSE166555) from GEO, consisting of single-cell expression profiles from tumor tissues of 13 CRC patients, which were subsequently used for TME heterogeneity and cell communication analyses.

2. scRNA-seq data preprocessing and cell type annotation

We performed normalization and cell type annotation on the scRNA-seq dataset GSE166555 from GEO to investigate cellular heterogeneity in CRC tumor tissues. First, we created a single-cell data object using the CreateSeuratObject function from R package “Seurat,”24 with parameters set to min.cells=3 and min.features=200 (default values were used for other parameters). We then calculated the proportion of mitochondrial genes and filtered cells with mitochondrial gene expression ≤50%, gene counts (nFeature_RNA) between 200 and 5,000, and transcript counts (nCount_RNA) between 200 and 25,000 to ensure data quality. The single-cell expression matrix was normalized, screened for highly variable genes, and normalized by applying NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, and ScaleData functions from package “Seurat.” To eliminate batch effects between samples, we applied function RunHarmony from package “Harmony.”25 In light of dimensionality reduction, utilizing the top 25 principal components from Harmony, we constructed an SNN graph via FindNeighbors, followed by cell clustering through FindClusters, and visualization analysis with RunUMAP. R package “clustree”26 was used to visualize resolution selection trends, with a final resolution of 0.6 chosen for stable cluster identification. Cell type annotation was performed using published markers,27 the CellMarker database (http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker/index.jsp), and R package “SingleR.”28 Marker gene expression was visualized using bubble plots to demonstrate annotation results.

3. Identification of fibroblast subpopulations

Fibroblast subpopulations were extracted from the annotated dataset and reprocessed using the NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, and ScaleData functions in package “Seurat,” with 2,000 highly variable genes selected. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were repeated, and package “clustree” was used to evaluate clustering stability across resolutions, with a resolution of 0.4 selected for stable cluster identification.

4. Identification of Scissor+ fibroblasts

Scissor (https://github.com/hyochoi/SCISSOR) is an analytical tool published in 2021 and later converted to the R package29,30 in 2022 for identifying phenotype-associated RNA transcript structural variations. Following established methods,30 we integrated single-cell expression, bulk expression matrices, and clinical phenotype data (survival status and time) from CRC patients by employing the R package “Scissor”30 to identify prognosis-associated fibroblast subpopulations. The specific process involves constructing a cell similarity network based on single-cell data, calculating the correlation matrix between single-cell and bulk expression matrices, and establishing a regression model with phenotypic data, thereby identifying cells related to prognosis. Based on the regression coefficients of the model, the cells are classified into three categories: Scissor+ (positively correlated with poor prognosis), Scissor– (negatively correlated with poor prognosis), and Background (with no significant correlation with prognosis).

5. Signature gene extraction and functional enrichment for Scissor+ fibroblasts

To systematically identify signature genes of Scissor+ fibroblast subpopulations, in light of single-cell transcriptome data, we performed differential expression analysis between Scissor+ fibroblasts and other fibroblast subpopulations using the FindMarkers function in package “Seurat.” The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed with thresholds set to min.pct=0.01 (genes expressed in at least 1% of Scissor+ cells), avg_log2FC (fold change)>0.585, and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value (p_val_adj)<0.05 to obtain the final Scissor+ fibroblast signature gene set. Additionally, we conducted Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses on the signature gene set to elucidate the functional characteristics of Scissor+ fibroblasts at the single-cell level. All enrichment results were visualized using the “enrichplot”31 and “GOplot”32 R packages.

6. GSVA-based subtyping and survival analysis

In both TCGA and GSE39582 datasets, we implemented Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) using R package “GSVA”33 to calculate enrichment scores for Scissor+ fibroblast signature genes in normalized expression data. Samples were divided into Scissor_high and Scissor_low subtypes based on median enrichment scores. Survival analysis was conducted using R package “survminer,”34 with Kaplan-Meier curves generated to evaluate the association between Scissor+ fibroblast signatures and prognosis.

7. Cell communication analysis in single-cell subpopulations

For scRNA-seq data, we first performed pseudobulk processing using R package “presto”35 to improve signal-to-noise ratio and analytical stability. Based on the expression levels of Scissor+ fibroblast signature genes, we calculated gene set enrichment scores for each cell subpopulation utilizing GSVA and stratified them into Scissor_high and Scissor_low groups according to median enrichment scores. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to compare Scissor enrichment score differences between these two groups, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Furthermore, we systematically analyzed intercellular communication patterns between groups using R package “CellChat”26 combined with the CellPhoneDB database, generating cell–cell interaction networks and comparing the number and strength of interactions between groups. Additionally, we evaluated differences in information flow in signaling pathways between groups based on cell communication results. Wilcoxon tests were used to identify marked changes in pathway activity, revealing pathways specifically enriched in two groups, thereby exploring the potential regulatory role of Scissor+ fibroblast features in cell communication.

8. Functional enrichment analysis of Scissor-associated genes

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying Scissor-based stratification, we performed differential expression analysis between Scissor_high and Scissor_low subtypes in both TCGA and GSE39582 datasets. In the TCGA cohort, we implemented nonparametric tests using the wilcox.test function from R package “stats,”36 while for GSE39582, we employed differential analysis via R package “limma.”37 The screening threshold was set at false discovery rate <0.05 and |log2(FC)| >0.585 to identify biologically relevant differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Intersecting DEGs between the two datasets were subjected to KEGG and GO functional enrichment analyses to uncover potential biological pathways and functional characteristics.

9. Association between clinical features and Scissor scores

To assess relationships between Scissor scores and clinical characteristics, we collected and organized clinical information, including TNM tumor stage, age, and sex. Scissor scores were derived from output values of the previously constructed Scissor model. We evaluated score differences across clinical stratification utilizing Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (for two-group comparisons) and Kruskal-Wallis tests (for three or more groups).

10. Immune microenvironment scoring and immune escape analysis

In TCGA and GSE39582 datasets, we calculated ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore for each sample using the ESTIMATE algorithm to assess relative proportions of immune and non-immune components in the TME. Additionally, we performed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis using R package “SVA”33 on an immune gene set comprising 782 genes to predict abundances of 28 tumor-infiltrating immune cells (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecule and immune checkpoint gene expression levels were extracted and compared by applying normalized transcriptomic matrices. Immune escape potential was quantified via the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm to evaluate probable immunotherapy response and escape tendencies. Inter-subtype differences were assessed via Wilcoxon rank-sum and Fisher test.

11. Tumor mutation analysis

CRC single-nucleotide variation data were obtained from TCGA and analyzed by adopting R package “maftools”38 for visualization and statistical comparison. We compared mutational profiles (including mutation type distributions and gene mutation frequencies) between Scissor_high and Scissor_low subtypes, with particular focus on the top 20 most frequently mutated genes. Mutation types (e.g., frame shift insertions, missense mutations) were categorized and compared between groups.

12. Drug sensitivity prediction analysis

To evaluate potential differences in drug responsiveness between Scissor_high and Scissor_low subtypes, we performed drug sensitivity predictions on TCGA samples using the R package “oncoPredict.”39 We employed trained models based on Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer database transcriptomic profiles and drug IC50 (half maximal inhibitory concentration) values to predict patient-specific drug sensitivities. Expression matrices were first normalized, and the calcPhenotype function was used to predict IC50 values for each subtype. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to compare predicted IC50 differences between subtypes across multiple drugs to identify subtype-specific sensitive agents, with significance set at p<0.0001.

RESULTS

1. Single-cell clustering and annotation identify key cellular subpopulations in CRC

To systematically analyze the cellular composition and immune microenvironment characteristics of CRC tissues, we performed stringent quality control on scRNA-seq data from 13 CRC patients in dataset GSE166555, selecting 2,000 highly variable genes for subsequent analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2A). To eliminate batch effects, we integrated the normalized data using the Harmony algorithm and constructed an SNN graph according to the top 25 principal components. The cells in different samples were evenly distributed in UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) dimensionality reduction, indicating successful removal of batch effects (Fig. 1A). Subsequent clustering analysis at a resolution of 0.6 identified 20 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 2B). By comparing marker gene expression levels, we annotated these cell clusters into seven major cell types: B cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells, monocytes, and T cells (Fig. 1C and D). Further stacked bar plots grouped by patients demonstrated significant immune heterogeneity among CRC samples, with particularly prominent differences in fibroblast infiltration (Fig. 1E). Given the critical part of fibroblasts in regulating the immune microenvironment and promoting tumor progression,40,41 we focused on them as the primary research subject in subsequent analyses.

2. Identification of Scissor+ fibroblasts

We extracted the fibroblast-related single-cell dataset and implemented re-analysis including normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering. Grounded in clustering results at a resolution of 0.4, we ultimately obtained 10 stable cell clusters (Cluster0-Cluster9) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 2C). Subsequently, based on the Scissor analysis and the cell annotation results, these 10 cell clusters were classified into three categories: Scissor+, Scissor–, and Background (Fig. 2B). Among them, the Scissor+ cells had positive regression coefficients in the model, suggesting that their expression had a positive contribution to adverse survival outcomes and might have tumor-promoting or progression-promoting functions. Examination of cell type distribution across clusters revealed that all clusters contained certain proportions of Scissor+ cells, demonstrating considerable heterogeneity. Among them, Cluster0, Cluster2, Cluster3, Cluster7 and Cluster9 (containing only Scissor+ and Background cells) showed relatively higher proportions of Scissor+ cells (Fig. 2C). The balloon plot further quantified the absolute numbers of different cell types across clusters (Fig. 2D), again confirming the dominant presence of Scissor+ cells in multiple clusters, particularly concentrated in Cluster0 (n=105) and Cluster2 (n=137). Given the remarkable association of Scissor+ fibroblasts with poor prognosis and their high proportions in multiple cell clusters, they may be of great significance in the progression of CRC. Thus, we focused on Scissor+ fibroblasts in follow-up analyses to investigate their molecular characteristics and functional pathways in depth.

3. Acquisition of signature genes for Scissor+ fibroblasts

To systematically identify signature genes of Scissor+ fibroblast subpopulations, we performed differential expression analysis between this cell population and other fibroblast subpopulations. Utilizing the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a gene expression proportion threshold of min.pct=0.01, we identified DEGs with mean log2FC >0.585 and adjusted p<0.05. A total of 93 signature genes for Scissor+ fibroblasts were obtained (Fig. 3A). Enrichment analysis revealed that these signature genes were mainly involved in KEGG-related signaling pathways such as vascular smooth muscle contraction, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, and MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 3B). Additionally, these genes were also enriched in GO terms such as metal ion stress response (e.g., copper ions), platelet aggregation, responses of cells to zinc and copper ions, and actin binding (Fig. 3C). To further investigate the impact of these signature genes on prognosis, we calculated the GSVA scores of these genes in the TCGA dataset and the GSE39582 dataset. According to the median score, the samples were divided into two subtypes: Scissor_high and Scissor_low. Survival curve analysis demonstrated that compared with the Scissor_low subtype, the prognosis of the Scissor_high subtype was significantly worse (p<0.05) (Fig. 3D and E). Additionally, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis results indicated that Scissor could serve as an independent prognostic factor, and its prognostic value was not affected by clinical characteristics such as sex, age, TNM stage, and tumor stage (p<0.05) (Fig. 3F and G).

4. Cell communication between the Scissor_high and Scissor_low groups

The previous analysis showed that the Scissor+ fibroblast characteristic gene highly expressed group (Scissor_high subtype) was associated with poor prognosis. To explore the potential regulatory role of these Scissor_high cells in the TME, especially whether they affect other cell functions by altering the cell communication network, we analyzed in the scRNA-seq dataset GSE166555. Specifically, based on the classification methods of the TCGA dataset and the GSE39582 dataset, we performed enrichment analysis of the Scissor+ fibroblast signature gene set employing the “GSVA” method, and divided the cell population into the Scissor_high group and the Scissor_low group according to the median of the enrichment score (Fig. 4A). The Scissor_high group showed appreciably higher Scissor enrichment scores (p<0.01) (Fig. 4B), validating the grouping approach. Subsequent systematic analysis of intergroup differences in cell communication revealed potential mechanisms underlying CRC microenvironment remodeling. Single-cell level communication analysis demonstrated that the Scissor_high group exhibited both greater numbers of cellular interactions and stronger interaction intensities (Fig. 4C and D). Specifically, the interactions among fibroblasts, macrophages, endothelial cells, monocytes, and epithelial cells in the Scissor_high group were remarkably enhanced. Particularly, the self-interactions among fibroblasts and the interactions between monocytes and endothelial cells, as well as between monocytes and macrophages, were the most notable (Fig. 4E and F). Furthermore, a further analysis of the differences in the information flow of signaling pathways between the two groups revealed that cells in the Scissor_high group were significantly enriched in signaling pathways such as ANNEXIN, SPP1, and NIF, while those in the Scissor_low group were enriched in the GALECTIN pathway (Fig. 4G). These results suggest that these signaling pathways may play a crucial regulatory role in different Scissor characteristic groups.

5. Differential gene expression analysis and functional enrichment between Scissor subtypes

To explore molecular mechanism differences between two subtypes, we conducted differential expression analyses in both TCGA and GSE39582 datasets. Results exhibited 2,767 DEGs in TCGA and 507 DEGs in GSE39582, with 460 overlapping genes (Fig. 5A). GO enrichment analysis of these 460 common genes revealed their predominant involvement in biological processes including regulation of vasculature development, regulation of leukocyte migration, regulation of angiogenesis, myeloid leukocyte migration, and leukocyte migration. In terms of cellular component, these genes were associated with extracellular structure organization, platelet alpha granules, and fibrillar collagen trimers. Regarding molecular function, they participated in integrin binding, growth factor binding, ECM binding, and collagen binding (Fig. 5B). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated significant enrichment of genes in multiple pathways, including proteoglycans in cancer, protein digestion and absorption, ECM-receptor interaction, cytoskeleton in muscle cells, and complement and coagulation cascades (Fig. 5C).

6. Association between clinical features and Scissor scores

Analysis of clinical feature correlations showed significant associations of Scissor scores with T, N, and tumor stages (p<0.05), but not with age, sex, or M stage (p>0.05) (Fig. 6). Specifically, patients with deeper local tumor invasion (higher T stage), more lymph node metastases (higher N stage), and advanced tumor stages (III-IV) generally exhibited higher Scissor scores, which demonstrated that Scissor scores may correlate with tumor biological aggressiveness.

7. Distinct immune microenvironment characteristics and clinical correlations of Scissor subtypes

To further characterize the TME features of Scissor subtypes, we conducted systematic evaluations of TCGA and GSE39582 datasets. Compared to Scissor_low, the Scissor_high subtype exhibited higher ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore (p<0.0001) (Fig. 7A and B), indicating a more complex and active immune microenvironment. Subsequent quantitative analysis of immune cell infiltration and functional activity based on single-sample gene set enrichment analysis revealed significant upregulation of all examined immune cells and functional pathways, including macrophages, Tregs, check-point, and Parainflammation, in Scissor_high subtype (p<0.05) (Fig. 7C). Further analysis showed elevated expression of HLA molecules (HLA-E, HLA-F, HLA-G) and immune checkpoint molecules (PDCD1, CTLA4, LAG3, TIGIT) in Scissor_high subtype (p<0.05) (Fig. 7D and E), suggesting a state of heightened immune activation potentially coupled with immunosuppression. Additionally, Scissor_high displayed higher TIDE scores (p<0.05) (Fig. 7F), indicating probably stronger immune escape capability.

8. Comparative analysis of mutational signatures between Scissor subtypes

Comparative analysis of mutational profiles revealed highly similar mutation spectra between Scissor_high and Scissor_low. Both subtypes shared identical predominant mutation types, with APC, TP53, and TTN ranking as the top three genes with the highest mutation frequency (Fig. 8A-D). Notable differences included slightly higher overall mutation rates in Scissor_high, while Scissor_low showed higher mutation proportions in the most frequently mutated gene APC, with remarkably more frameshift insertions. Furthermore, no significant difference was observed in the tumor mutation burden (TMB) between the two subtypes (p>0.05) (Fig. 8E). These results suggest that although the overall mutation background of the two subtypes is similar and there is no significant difference in TMB, minor differences in key driver genes (e.g., APC) and specific mutation types (e.g., frameshift insertions) may have certain effects on the activity of signaling pathways or the biological behavior of tumors in each subtype. However, these speculations still require further experimental verification.

9. Differential drug sensitivity analysis between Scissor subtypes

To identify potential subtype-specific therapeutic agents, we performed drug sensitivity prediction analysis in the TCGA cohort leveraging oncoPredict. Scissor_low showed higher predicted sensitivity to drugs like BMS.7548072171, AZD80551059, AZD13321463, Dasatinib_1079, JQ12172, AZ9601250, and WZ40031614. Conversely, Scissor_high exhibited greater sensitivity to Erlotinib 1168, TAF154961732, Gefitinib_1010, Ulixertinib1908, and AZD37591915 (p<0.0001) (Fig. 9, Supplementary Table 1).

DISCUSSION

CRC represents one of the most metastatic and lethal malignancies, with its heterogeneity manifesting across multiple dimensions including genome, transcriptome, epigenetics, TME, and spatial architecture. This multi-layered heterogeneity significantly impedes the advancement of precision therapy and the accuracy of treatment response prediction.42-45 Fibroblasts, particularly CAFs, as critical components of the TME, play indispensable roles in CRC progression and immune evasion.46,47 Previous studies have revealed the heterogeneity of fibroblasts in their functional states and classified them into various subtypes. Nevertheless, research predominantly based on single-omics data has seldom directly linked specific CAF subtypes to patient prognosis. Notably absent was functional subtyping identification derived from the combined analysis of bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq. Therefore, in this study, employing the Scissor algorithm, we identified the prognostic-related Scissor+ fibroblast populations from the integrated single-cell and bulk data. We further systematically explored their molecular characteristics, immune landscape, and potential therapeutic targets, providing new insights for the subtype classification and precise treatment of CRC.

We first performed high-quality integration and detailed annotation of the single-cell dataset GSE166555, identifying multiple major cell types including fibroblasts. Further analysis revealed striking heterogeneity in immune cell infiltration among different CRC samples, with fibroblasts showing the most prominent variations, highlighting their pivotal role in the CRC TME. Fibroblasts, especially CAFs, participate in and accelerate tumorigenesis through mechanisms such as promoting immunosuppression, ECM remodeling, and angiogenesis.48-50 Thus, in-depth investigation of fibroblast mechanisms holds substantial research significance.

At the single-cell level, we successfully identified prognosis-associated fibroblast subpopulations leveraging the Scissor, including Scissor+ cells positively correlated with poor prognosis and Scissor– cells negatively correlated with favorable outcomes. Further analysis demonstrated that Scissor+ cells predominated in multiple fibroblast clusters, suggesting their possibly broader biological functions in the TME. Scissor+ fibroblast signature genes exhibited distinct transcriptional profiles compared to other fibroblast subpopulations, primarily enriched in pathways related to actin regulation, MAPK signaling, and smooth muscle contraction, which were all closely associated with cell migration, contraction, and signal transduction.51,52 These findings indicate this subpopulation may possess stronger tumor-promoting capabilities. These functional characteristics align well with previously reported “myofibroblastic CAFs,” which have been proven to play essential roles in promoting tumor cell invasion and angiogenesis.6,53,54 Additionally, we observed that the Scissor+ subpopulation exhibited stress response capabilities to metal ions (e.g., copper), potentially related to oxidative stress regulation within tumors,55 providing new directions for further functional investigations. In summary, Scissor+ fibroblasts not only showed remarkable association with poor prognosis but also possessed typical pro-tumor molecular characteristics and microenvironment regulatory potential, representing a vital subpopulation with critical research and intervention value during tumor progression.

At the sample level, in light of Scissor+ fibroblast signature gene expression profiles, we identified two Scissor subtypes via GSVA scores. Patients with the Scissor_high subtype, which has a high Scissor score, have significantly worse survival prognosis, possibly due to the complex and immunosuppressive microenvironment they are in. Specifically, the Scissor_high subtype is higher than the Scissor_low subtype in multiple aspects such as the ESTIMATE immune score, immune cell infiltration, and immune function pathway activity. Moreover, the Scissor_high subtype is also accompanied by higher levels of HLA class molecules and immune checkpoint molecules, such as PDCD1 (PD-1), CTLA4, LAG3, and TIGIT. This phenomenon suggests that the Scissor_high subtype may be in an immune phenotype similar to immune exhaustion. As observed in various solid tumors, the “inflamed but exhausted” immune phenotype (although there are cytotoxic immune cells, their functions are dysregulated, thus leading to tumor progression)56 is also present. Furthermore, the high TIDE scores discovered in Scissor_high further corroborate this observation. Therefore, we speculate that the Scissor_high subtype may have a special immune phenotype characterized by high immune activation but immune evasion, and may have a limited response to immune checkpoint inhibitors; however, this speculation still requires further experimental verification.

Although the Scissor_high and Scissor_low subtypes have highly similar overall mutation profiles and TMB, there are some discrepancies in the mutation frequencies of certain key genes (such as APC) and certain structural variations (such as frameshift insertions) between the two subtypes, suggesting that there may be functional differences in specific driving mechanisms between the two subtypes. The Scissor_low subtype shows a higher mutation frequency in the key driver gene APC, and the number of frameshift insertion mutations is significantly greater. The APC gene plays an important tumor suppressor role in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Its inactivation leads to the accumulation of β-catenin in the nucleus and abnormal activation of oncogenic gene expression.57 Frameshift insertion mutations usually result in protein truncation or loss of function.58 Therefore, these specific types of mutations, rather than the overall TMB, may explain the biological differences between the two subtypes. Future studies should further integrate functional experiments and multi-omics analyses to more comprehensively reveal the downstream effects of these mutations. Additionally, drug sensitivity analysis revealed differential responses to molecular targeted agents between Scissor subtypes. For instance, the Scissor_low subtype may be more sensitive in prediction for inhibitors targeting the IGF1R pathway (such as BMS-754807),59 while the Scissor_high subtype is more susceptible to inhibitors related to the EGFR-TKI pathways (such as AZD3759, Erlotinib, Gefitinib, etc.).60,61 This Scissor signature-based drug screening strategy may provide a foundation for future personalized precision treatment.

In conclusion, we employed the Scissor algorithm to identify prognosis-associated Scissor+ fibroblast subpopulations for the first time in CRC, systematically revealing their unique transcriptional features, immune landscape, and potential drug sensitivities. These findings not only deepened our understanding of TME complexity but also provide new perspectives and promising targets for CRC subtyping and personalized treatment strategies. However, despite significant progress, this study has several limitations. While we have inferred the biological functions of Scissor+ cells based on expression profile data, and analyzed their immune characteristics and communication patterns at the cellular and sample levels, the relevant findings still lack prospective experimental validation and in-depth mechanism explanations. Future research can combine in vitro and in vivo experiments, spatial omics and multi-omics data, and further confirm their functions and systematically elucidate the specific mechanisms in a larger multicenter cohort. Although this study conducted in-depth analyses of existing single-cell data, due to sample size and platform differences, the results may have certain selection bias, and the cross-platform generalizability is not clear. Subsequently, by expanding the sample size, conducting multi-platform comparisons, and integrating analyses, the reliability and universality of the results can be enhanced. Moreover, the Scissor algorithm demonstrated good recognition ability in this study, its performance may be biased due to the data set structure. In the future, cross-validation with multiple computational methods can be combined to further improve the robustness and reliability of the analysis.
