Hemoglobin receptor redundancy in Staphylococcus aureus: molecular flexibility as a determinant of divergent hemophore activity☆
Authors: Valeria Buoli Comani, Omar De Bei, Francesca Pancrazi, Marcos Gragera, Giulia Paris, Marialaura Marchetti, Barbara Campanini, Luca Ronda, Ben F. Luisi, Serena Faggiano, Anna Rita Bizzarri, Stefano Bettati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractLocal flexibility in the heme-binding domains of IsdH bound to hemoglobin (Hb) enables a structure–activity hypothesis whereby IsdH, being more flexible, initiates heme extraction concurrently with Hb binding, but exhibits reduced efficiency in completing the process. In contrast, the homologous domain of IsdB, which is more structurally stable when bound to Hb, displays a delayed onset of heme extraction but proceeds with higher efficiency once initiated. These findings suggest that IsdH and IsdB have evolved to function optimally under different growth conditions or within distinct host niches.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is a common human-associated bacterium that can shift from being commensal to becoming a dangerous pathogen (Bröker et al., 2014, Wertheim et al., 2005) by exploiting a broad array of virulence factors (Foster and Geoghegan, 2015). An important aspect contributing to S. aureus pathogenicity is its ability to acquire iron, an essential nutrient whose availability is tightly restricted by host nutritional immunity. To circumvent this restriction, the bacterium relies on the Iron-regulated surface determinant (Isd) system, a multi-protein machinery that captures hemoglobin (Hb), extracts heme, and releases iron for bacterial use (Grigg et al., 2010, Marchetti et al., 2020).

The Isd system includes the isofunctional, cell wall–anchored hemophores IsdB and IsdH, which play a pivotal role in the initial capture of heme from extracellular Hb, specifically from its oxidized form metHb (Maresso and Schneewind, 2006). From a drug discovery perspective, the inhibition of both IsdB and IsdH interaction with Hb holds potential for the development of therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting heme acquisition by the pathogen and thus inducing iron starvation (Cozzi et al., 2024). IsdB and IsdH are modular proteins composed respectively of two and three NEAr-iron Transporter (NEAT, N) domains connected by linker regions. NEATs are responsible for both Hb binding (IsdHN1, IsdHN2 and IsdBN1) and heme removal (IsdHN3 and IsdBN2)(Ellis-Guardiola et al., 2020, Pilpa et al., 2009).

Although the role of hemophores in iron acquisition is well established, the reason for the apparent redundancy of IsdB and IsdH remains unclear. A key difference may lie in their dissimilar ability to interact with the Hb:haptoglobin (Hb:Hp) complex, a physiological assembly formed to limit oxidative damage caused by free Hb. Both hemophores can bind this complex, but only IsdH retains partial heme extraction ability (Mikkelsen et al., 2020), while IsdB is fully inhibited (Bowden et al., 2018). Structural studies on the ternary complex (i.e. hemophore bound to Hb:Hp) suggest that this inhibition stems from Hb stabilization within the complex, rather than direct interference by Hp (Mikkelsen et al., 2020).

A detailed functional and structural comparison between IsdB and IsdH is essential to support the development of inhibitors with potential antimicrobial activity. The Hb binding and heme extraction mechanism of IsdB has been progressively clarified. Early studies (Gianquinto et al., 2019, Zhu et al., 2014, Zhu et al., 2008) converge toward a mechanistically consistent model of heme acquisition by IsdB, integrating quantitative kinetic characterization, identification of structurally and functionally critical residues, and definition of a sequential heme transfer mechanism. In parallel, structural investigations employing truncated constructs and mutant variants (Bowden et al., 2018, Bowden et al., 2014, Gaudin et al., 2011) provided key structural insights into both the binding interface with Hb and the heme coordination by the hemophore. More recently, integration of high-resolution structural data with time-resolved biochemical and biophysical approaches (De Bei et al., 2025, De Bei et al., 2022) has enabled the establishment of a comprehensive kinetic model describing the entire heme extraction pathway, from Hb recognition to heme transfer. Conversely, despite IsdH has been more extensively studied over the years, the experimental conditions and the reported functional data exhibit greater variability (Dryla et al., 2007, Kumar et al., 2011, Pilpa et al., 2009, Sjodt et al., 2016, Sjodt et al., 2018, Spirig et al., 2013, Valenciano-Bellido et al., 2022). Most in vitro studies on IsdH exploit a truncated version of the protein lacking the first NEAT domain (IsdHN2N3), as it was proved not to be essential for Hb binding and heme extraction (Dickson et al., 2015, Spirig et al., 2013). This truncated form closely resembles IsdB structurally and functionally, allowing direct comparisons. Recently, we resolved the structures of the IsdB:Hb complexes before and after heme extraction using cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), enabling the analysis of the wild-type hemophore bound to Hb (De Bei et al., 2022). To overcome limitations of previous crystallographic studies − where Hb autoxidation was unavoidable due to long crystallization times (Bowden et al., 2018) − carboxyhemoglobin (HbCO) was used. In this form, hemic iron remains reduced, its extraction from Hb is hampered and autoxidation is markedly slowed compared to oxyhemoglobin (oxyHb) (Gianquinto et al., 2019, Sjodt et al., 2018). This strategy enabled the stabilization and the structural characterization of the preextraction complex. In the case of IsdH, however, all available preextraction complex structures were obtained using mutant hemophores incapable of extracting heme (Protein Data Bank [PDB] ID: 4XS0 (Dickson et al., 2015), 4IJ2 (Dickson et al., 2014), 5VMM (Bowden et al., 2018), 6TB2 (Mikkelsen et al., 2020).

To better understand the different structural basis of heme acquisition by S. aureus hemophores, we used cryo-EM to characterize the preextraction complex formed between Hb and the minimal functional construct IsdHN2N3. The experimental setup, based on the most widely adopted IsdH construct and conditions directly comparable to those used for IsdB (De Bei et al., 2022), enabled a direct structural and functional comparison. Cryo-EM revealed multiple stoichiometries and conformational states of the IsdHN2N3:Hb complex, with the predominant species consisting of two IsdH molecules bound to a Hb dimer (2IsdH:Hbdim) prior to heme removal. In the context of the 2IsdH:Hbdim complex, a key finding was the pronounced flexibility within the IsdH NEAT3 domain. This structural feature was characterized using a combination of classical biochemical techniques – such as spectroscopy and chromatography – and more advanced approaches including time-resolved analyses and nanoscale quantification of intermolecular forces. Complementary methodologies revealed that IsdH engages Hb in a promiscuous and transient manner, without stabilizing a defined complex or inducing detectable structural stress on the host protein, and extracts heme following a biphasic process. These results, when compared to the functional data reported for IsdB, provide insight into the divergent roles of hemophores in S. aureus iron acquisition and identify shared structural features that could be exploited for the simultaneous and selective inhibition of both hemophores.

Results and Discussion

Cryo-EM structure of the preextraction complex IsdHN2N3:HbCO

To investigate the interactions underlying IsdH binding to Hb, we analyzed by single-particle cryo-EM the complex formed by wild-type IsdHN2N3 and HbCO mixed at a 1:1 ratio (Fig. 1 and Table S1), a stoichiometry determined from preliminary size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) analysis (Fig. S1). This approach enabled us to capture the hemophore-Hb interaction prior to heme extraction, which cannot occur from HbCO. Most of the particles identified in the collected cryo-EM dataset depicted the preextraction complex as a Hb dimer bound by two IsdHN2N3 (2IsdH:Hbdim assembly, Fig. 1), matching the SEC-based stoichiometry and capturing a conformation not previously resolved. Indeed, tetramer dissociation of hemophore-bound Hb has only been reported in (i) the crystallographic structure of IsdH:Hb:Hp complex (PDB ID 6TB2 (Mikkelsen et al., 2020), where this process is driven by Hp, or (ii) the IsdB:metHb cryo-EM structure (PDB ID: 7PCF (De Bei et al., 2022)), in which heme extraction already occurred. Given the intrinsic propensity of Hb to dissociate into dimers upon dilution following release into the plasma from red blood cells (RBCs) (Hargrove et al., 1997) and the additional promotion of this process by IsdH binding (Fig. S1), the captured assembly offered a framework close to physiological conditions for investigating the early stages of heme acquisition.Fig. 1IsdHN2N3:HbCO complexes cryo-EM maps. The 2IsdH:Hbdim complex is notably small, yet the high-quality cryo-EM data enabled reconstruction of two distinct quaternary arrangements with identical stoichiometry, both reaching comparable overall resolutions of 4.0 Å and 3.6 Å. The key difference between the two conformations lies in the linker and NEAT3 regions of IsdH, which exhibit weak or missing density and reduced local resolution across distinct regions of the complex in each map (Fig. 1). In the first conformation, hereafter referred to as the 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim complex (Fig. 1A), the region corresponding to linker and NEAT3 domains of IsdH bound to the Hb α-subunit is poorly resolved. In contrast, clear density is observed for the NEAT2 domain of the same molecule and for the entire IsdH bound to the Hb β-subunit. The second conformation, hereafter referred to as the 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim complex (Fig. 1B), displays a mirrored pattern of flexibility, with poor resolution affecting the linker and NEAT3 regions of the IsdH molecule bound to the Hb β-subunit.

Beyond the 2IsdH:Hbdim complexes, 3D classification revealed additional oligomeric species of higher stoichiometries (Fig. S2). The map of one of these complexes yielded highresolution (3.1 Å), allowing for the generation and refinement of an atomic model (Fig. S3). While this complex does not recapitulate the spatial constraints at the bacterial surface (details in Fig. S3), it was nonetheless useful to assess the interactions between IsdHN2N3 and Hb in the preextraction complex (Table S2).

Structural flexibility of the 2IsdHN2N3:Hbdim complex

Regions of weak density and reduced local resolution frequently occur in cryo-EM data due to underlying local flexibility. To gain further insight into the conformational dynamics of IsdHN2N3 within the 2IsdH:Hbdim complexes, we performed 3D variability analysis (3DVA) using cryoSPARC (Punjani and Fleet, 2021). 3DVA revealed two principal components of motion for each complex: components 0 and 4 for 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim (c0 and c4), and components 0 and 1 for 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim (c0 and c1). By inspecting intermediate volumes along each eigenvector, we identified common patterns of NEAT3 domain motion in the IsdH molecules bound to both α- and β-chains. To represent these dynamics, we used an x–y plane cutting through the complex (Fig. 2A), where the Hb α- and β-subunits lie to the left and right, respectively, and the two IsdH molecules sit to the sides of the Hb dimer. Within this reference frame, the conformational changes described by 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim c4 and 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim c0 represent “in–out” motions of the NEAT3 domain approaching or detaching from the Hb dimer within the x–y plane (Buoli Comani et al., 2025). Since particles along each conformational component are distributed according to a Gaussian curve, we refer to the resulting maps as “left” and “right”, indicating those derived from particles at the respective extreme tails of the component. For example, the refined maps for 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim c0 are named 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim c0-left and 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim c0-right. The structural template of 2IsdH:Hbdim complex was generated using AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024), then a series of models was generated using Namdinator for flexible fitting within the maps reconstructed with particle subsets from the tails of the Gaussian (Fig. S4A-L). Since only the four maps corresponding to the 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim complex reached high resolution, models were refined within these specific maps (Fig. S4I-L and Table S3). The resulting models were superimposed, revealing no significant differences in the protein regions resolved within these maps.

In addition to analysing particles from the tails of the Gaussian distributions, representative PDB models were obtained for the intermediate volumes extracted from the 3DVA exploiting Tempy-REFF (Beton et al., 2024). Given that the observed flexibility involves very small regions of IsdHN2N3 – approaching the resolution limits of the technique – we compared the intermediate models with all high-resolution IsdH structures available in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) and confirmed the robustness of our analysis (Fig. S5). The result contrasts with earlier reports that pointed to a hinge region between NEAT2 and the N-terminal portion of the linker as the main site of flexibility. Sjodt et al. (Sjodt et al., 2016) using NMR on IsdH in solution, described marked mobility in this hinge region, while observing only minor dynamics near the linker-NEAT3 junction. Further insight into IsdH dynamics came from a report combining NMR with restrained molecular dynamics simulations (Ellis-Guardiola et al., 2020). This study, while informative, relied on a recombinant form of Hb that does not dissociate into dimers, a condition that may influence the interaction landscape and constrain the formation of the final IsdH stoichiometry, determined here as 1:1, whereas NMR experiments have shown spectral variations extending up to a twofold molar excess of Hb. Building on this, a subsequent study conducted unrestrained MD simulations of the IsdH:Hb complex, again highlighting hinge flexibility as the dominant feature (Clayton et al., 2022). In contrast, our cryo-EM analysis – performed on IsdHN2N3 bound to native Hb dimers – depicts a different scenario. In the bound state, the hinge region appears considerably stabilized, while conformational variability shifts toward the NEAT3 domain and C-terminal region of the linker. Interestingly, this region had already been noted as modestly flexible (Sjodt et al., 2016), though it received less emphasis. Our findings suggest that the binding to a Hb dimer (the oligomeric state that is mainly present at the level of the infection site) reshapes the conformational landscape of IsdH, attenuating motions at the hinge region but increasing dynamics specifically at the linker-NEAT3 junction. Indeed, it is highly plausible that this structural switch reflects distinct functional requirements of the Hb receptor at different stages of interaction. When IsdH is free on the bacterial surface (as shown in (Sjodt et al., 2016)) or during the early steps of engagement with Hb (as reported in (Ellis-Guardiola et al., 2020)), the hinge region likely remains flexible, allowing NEAT2 to “chase” Hb by exploring a wider conformational space. Once the complex with dimeric Hb is fully formed, however, this flexibility appears to shift toward the linker–NEAT3 junction, where it may support the proper translation of the heme cofactor from Hb to IsdH.

Structural differences between IsdH and IsdB underlie their differential interaction with Hb

Our cryo-EM analysis provided experimental evidence of a significant difference between the preextraction complexes formed by IsdHN2N3 and IsdBN1N2 with Hb. Indeed, compared to the IsdB:HbCO complex (De Bei et al., 2022), which consists of two IsdBN1N2 molecules bound to the β-subunits of an Hb tetramer, the 2IsdH:Hbdim complex shows Hb in a dimeric state, the hemophore binding to both Hb chains, and substantial flexibility of the heme-binding domain (NEAT3) of IsdHN2N3. To effectively acquire heme from Hb, IsdH and IsdB depend on forming a stable complex with the human protein, which then enables their heme-binding domains to extract the heme. Therefore, we first investigated whether structural divergence influences the binding affinity of the two hemophores for oxyHb, the physiological form resistant to heme extraction, by adapting a previously developed immunoassay (Cozzi et al., 2024). When immobilized on a 96-well plate by exploiting the StrepTag-II at their C-termini, both hemophores exhibited very similar affinities for oxyHb, with dissociation constants (KDs) of 38.5 ± 7.1 nM for IsdHN2N3 (Fig. 3B) and 43.2 ± 2.5 nM for IsdBN1N2 (Fig. 3C). These KD values were in good agreement with previously reported data (Table S4). To further dissect the specific role of the heme-binding domain in Hb interaction, we then assessed the affinity for Hb of IsdHN2L and IsdBN1L, hemophore constructs lacking this domain (Fig. 3B and C). The affinity of IsdHN2L for oxyHb was comparable to that of the IsdHN2N3 construct (KD = 31.1 ± 5.3 nM). In contrast, the IsdBN1L variant showed a 17-fold increase in KD compared to the wild-type hemophore​ (KD​ = 753.0 ± 64.2 nM). This difference suggests that while for IsdB the heme-binding domain is crucial for its interaction with Hb, the heme-binding domain of IsdH is not strictly necessary for its initial binding to Hb.

Since we demonstrated that IsdH affinity for Hb is predominantly mediated by its Hb-binding domain, we then investigated the interaction of the IsdHN2L construct with the separated α- and β-chains of Hb by chromatographic analysis, with IsdBN1L included for comparison (Fig. 3D-K). This approach follows the strategy used by Kumar et al. (Kumar et al., 2011) to study the selectivity of isolated IsdH domains (NEAT1 and NEAT2). Our results show that IsdHN2L can form a complex with both α- and β-chains of Hb chains. Specifically, in the presence of this IsdH variant, the chromatographic elution profile displays a single peak both at 280 nm, where the polypeptide chains absorb, and at 415 nm, a heme spectral signature. This co-elution confirms the formation of a stable complex between IsdH and either the α- or β-subunits of Hb. In contrast, the same experiment with IsdBN1L revealed selective binding to Hb β-chains, in full agreement with previous reports (De Bei et al., 2022). This evidence is supported by the chromatographic profiles showing that when IsdBN1L is in the presence of β-chains, a single peak corresponding to the complex is observed, analogously to the IsdHN2L:β-chains interaction. Conversely, the mixture of IsdBN1L with α-chains results in the elution of two distinct peaks, corresponding to the unbound individual components, indicating no complex formation.

Our results suggest that, unlike IsdB, IsdH evolved a highly stable NEAT2-Hb interaction that may compensate for the intrinsic flexibility of its NEAT3 domain, potentially at the expense of chain specificity, resulting in a more promiscuous binding to Hb.

The distinct dynamic properties of the hemophores’ heme-binding domains have functional implication in the heme extraction process

To explore whether the distinct dynamic properties of the heme-binding domains in IsdH (NEAT3) and IsdB (NEAT2) influence the heme extraction process, we performed a time-resolved spectroscopic analysis under the same experimental conditions previously used for IsdB (De Bei et al., 2025). Heme transfer from metHb, the physiological substrate of IsdH, was monitored on the millisecond timescale using a stopped-flow apparatus. Full visible spectra (350–700 nm) were acquired with a photodiode array detector (PDA) to track IsdH heme extraction, exploiting the characteristic absorbance changes of the cofactor upon transfer. This spectroscopic signal has been widely used to kinetically characterize the heme extraction process (Muryoi et al., 2008, Pilpa et al., 2009). Unlike the conventional approach of monitoring absorbance changes at a single wavelength (typically 406 nm, corresponding to the heme Soret peak), we applied singular value decomposition (SVD) to the entire time-resolved spectral dataset acquired via PDA. This analysis revealed two main components accounting for 83 % and 13 % of the total spectral variation (Fig. 4A and S6A). Of notice, the first SVD component can be directly associated with the heme extraction event, as it closely matches the well-established heme binding signature, characterized by an increase in absorbance at 380 nm and a decrease at 406 nm. The kinetic trace of this component allowed us to follow temporally the heme transfer process.

In parallel with the PDA signal, time-resolved measurements of IsdH intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence upon mixing with Hb were acquired (Fig. 4B). This approach builds on our recent findings with IsdB, where monitoring intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence proved effective for tracking hemophore-Hb binding over time (De Bei et al., 2025).

To extract apparent kinetic constants integrating information from both PDA and fluorescence signals, a global fitting was performed on the kinetic traces of the two SVD components (Fig. 4A) and the fluorescence signal (Fig. 4B). This approach allowed the determination of rate constants for the time-coupled processes described by the distinct spectroscopic readouts. The kinetic analysis of heme extraction presented here provides insight across a broad timescale, ranging from tens of milliseconds to several minutes, by combining the evolution of the first SVD component with the spectrophotometric kinetic trace. For IsdH, an initial fast kinetic phase is evident (described by observed k3), followed by a slower process (captured by k6 from the global fitting and rate k2 from the cuvette-based assay). Complementary information was obtained from time-resolved tryptophan fluorescence, which tracks protein–protein binding. In this case, the fluorescence transition is complete within 1 s, captured by the first three rates of the global fit, and remains unchanged thereafter, eliminating the need for additional cuvette-based fluorescence assays. Together, these data suggest that the initial extraction step is kinetically coupled to complex formation, while a second, slower process occurs after the complex is fully assembled. In contrast, previous results on IsdB (De Bei et al., 2025) showed that heme extraction only begins after complex formation is complete, followed by rapid transfer. As proposed in our previous study (De Bei et al., 2025), IsdB requires a kinetic activation step. The current findings indicate that IsdH does not follow the same mechanism, a difference that may be linked to the greater flexibility of the NEAT3 domain observed in IsdH, which is not present in IsdB. Instead, IsdH appears capable of initiating heme extraction immediately upon binding to Hb; however, this property may come at the expenses of the overall efficiency of the process, potentially limiting the full utilization of the heme available.

Limited Hb autoxidation by IsdH reflects the dynamic nature of its heme-binding domain

In addition to its role in heme extraction, IsdHN2N3 is also proposed to promote the autoxidation of oxyHb to metHb, a mechanism previously demonstrated for IsdB (De Bei et al., 2022). This mechanism would be physiologically relevant as S. aureus infections release oxyHb, which is resistant to direct heme acquisition. Accelerating Hb autoxidation would increase the local concentration of metHb, thereby enhancing the iron acquisition process. The ability of IsdHN2N3 to increase the rate of oxyHb autoxidation was evaluated under the same experimental conditions as for IsdBN1N2 (PBS buffer, pH 7.4, at 37 °C). The results demonstrated that IsdHN2N3 does enhance the oxyHb autoxidation rate (Fig. 5A), but not as effectively as IsdBN1N2. To further elucidate the specific role of the heme-binding domains in increasing the autoxidation rate, we monitored Hb autoxidation in the presence of IsdHN2L and IsdBN1L. In both cases, the oxidation rate did not significantly differ from the control without hemophores (Fig. 5A), indicating that the effect observed for IsdHN2N3 and IsdBN1N2 is mediated by their heme-binding domains IsdHN3 and IsdBN2. IsdHN2N3 binding to metHb is not stabilized by a catch bond-like interaction

In certain infection scenarios, such as sepsis, S. aureus is thought to directly encounter Hb within the host's bloodstream. For effective iron acquisition, the bacterium must interact strongly enough with Hb to overcome the mechanical forces exerted by blood flow. We recently demonstrated that IsdB exhibits a remarkable catch bond-like mechanism (Botti et al., 2024). This unique phenomenon arises because the strength of the interaction between IsdB and its ligand (metHb, specifically) increases proportionally to the applied mechanical forces. We propose that this catch bond-like behavior — originally studied in the context of IsdB-mediated bacterial adhesion (Mathelié-Guinlet et al., 2020, Miajlovic et al., 2010, Paiva et al., 2025, Pietrocola et al., 2020, Zapotoczna et al., 2013) —also plays a crucial role in helping the bacterial protein withstand shear stress, when hemoglobin or the bacterium is moving within a fluid. This mechanism likely provides the hemophore:Hb complex with enough time to complete efficient heme extraction. In our prior publication, we found that a key determinant for this catch bond-like behavior in IsdB is the cis–trans isomerization of Pro173, which precisely positions Lys172 to stabilize IsdB Loop2, a critical structure for high-affinity Hb binding (Pancrazi et al., 2025).

Interestingly, in IsdH the corresponding proline residue (Pro374) is conserved in a cis configuration, but Glu373 occupies the position analogous to Lys172 in IsdB. Based on our previous findings, we hypothesized that this residue in IsdH would not be able to establish the necessary interactions with Loop2, thus precluding the characteristic catchbond-like behavior. The detailed analysis of the large number of IsdHN2N3 structures that we obtained here with cryo-EM revealed that in none of the observed conformations Glu373 interacts with Loop2 (Fig. S7), further reinforcing the assumption that we made based on published crystallographic structures.

To experimentally confirm the absence of catch bond-like behavior of the IsdH:Hb complex, we investigated the interaction between IsdHN2N3 and metHb by Atomic Force Spectroscopy (AFS), following the same procedure as in previous studies on IsdBN1N2 (or IsdBP173A) and Hb (Botti et al., 2024, Pancrazi et al., 2025).

We measured the most probable unbinding force (F*) as a function of the logarithm of the loading rate (Fig. 5B) and found a linear trend across the analyzed range (2–200 nN/s), in agreement with the Bell-Evans model (Bell, 1978, Evans, 2001). This behavior, characteristic of slip bonds, indicates that increasing the applied force facilitates unbinding, unlike catch bonds where force stabilizes the interaction (Marshall et al., 2003, Thomas, 2008). From fitting the unbinding forces across a range of loading rates to the Bell–Evans model, we extracted a dissociation rate constant (koff) of 3 ± 2 × 10−4 s−1 and a distance to the transition state (xβ) in line with typical values for non-covalent biomolecular interactions (Bizzarri and Cannistraro, 2010). Even more importantly, both parameters agree with those previously reported for the IsdBN1N2:Hb complex (Botti et al., 2024), supporting a shared energy landscape for these NEAT domain-mediated interactions with Hb. However, in contrast to IsdB, which exhibits pronounced catch bond-like behavior under mechanical load, IsdHN2N3 showed no force-dependent strengthening. This absence highlights a fundamental mechanistic difference between the two receptors, despite their similar static binding properties. The divergence likely reflects distinct functional roles within the Isd system and indicates that force-enhanced binding is not a general feature of NEAT-containing Hb receptors. These findings also support the notion that specific structural elements in IsdB − particularly Pro173 and Lys172 − are critical for its unique mechanical regulation.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive structural and biochemical characterization of the S. aureus hemophore IsdH, under experimental conditions directly comparable to those previously reported for IsdB (Botti et al., 2024, De Bei et al., 2025, De Bei et al., 2022, Gianquinto et al., 2019). Although both receptors mediate heme acquisition from host Hb, the reason for their coexistence and functional redundancy has been unclear. Our experimental approach reveals structural and functional differences between the two hemophores.

Global kinetic analysis of absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy data suggests that, in the IsdH-Hb interaction, the heme extraction is tightly coupled to the initial binding of Hb, occurring as a synchronous event rather than as a delayed, downstream process. This mechanism contrasts with that of IsdB, for which heme extraction only initiates after full complex formation and requires a distinct kinetic activation step (De Bei et al., 2025). While the prompt and non-selective engagement observed for IsdH may allow for rapid heme acquisition upon contact with Hb, it appears to come at the cost of reduced efficiency over longer timescales, potentially limiting the complete utilization of the heme pool.

This functional distinction is further supported by our structural observations. Upon binding to Hb, the NEAT3 domain of IsdH exhibits pronounced flexibility – a feature not detected in the structurally homologous domain of IsdB. This mobility likely weakens the interaction with the heme-binding site, contributing to the reduced ability of IsdH to promote Hb autoxidation, unlike the more rigid IsdB. At the same time, the structural plasticity of IsdH appears to redistribute interactions determining binding affinity across its domains: even if NEAT3 is highly dynamic or disengaged, the NEAT2–linker region maintains a strong and stable interaction with Hb, ensuring overall complex stabilization.

Altogether, these findings support a model in which IsdB has evolved a catch bond-like mechanism to stabilize prolonged interactions with free Hb in the bloodstream, enabling sustained heme extraction. In contrast, IsdH appears to have adapted alternative strategies that favor rapid heme acquisition during transient encounters with Hb or from structurally constrained complexes, such as Hb:Hp.

Furthermore, our structural and functional observations provide insight to support rational drug design aimed at simultaneously targeting IsdB and IsdH. By disrupting their interaction with Hb, inhibitors could effectively starve the pathogen of iron, offering a promising antibacterial strategy. In this scenario, the lack of Hb chain selectivity observed for IsdH, combined with the established preference of IsdB for β-chains, highlights this Hb subunit as a shared and accessible target surface for dual inhibition. Moreover, our structural data reveal a conformational arrangement of the NEAT2–linker region that differs from previous molecular dynamics simulations (Clayton et al., 2022, Ellis-Guardiola et al., 2020, Sjodt et al., 2016) yet not inconsistent with their predictions. Instead, our findings likely capture an alternative stage in the conformational landscape of IsdH, associated with complex formation between two IsdH molecules and a Hb dimer. This interaction appears to stabilize the hinge region previously described as flexible, resulting in an architecture that closely resembles the NEAT1–linker organization observed in IsdB when bound to Hb.This finding highlights an extended and accessible structural element that could be exploited for dual-inhibition strategies.

Materials and methods

Cloning, protein expression and purification

The genomic sequence of S. aureus IsdHN2N3, corresponding to IsdH NEAT2 and NEAT3 linked together (UniProt entry Q8NW39, residues 326–660), was cloned into the pASK-IBA3plus vector (IBA Lifesciences), resulting in the expression of IsdHN2N3 with a C-terminal Strep-tag® II. E. coli RP523 (Li et al., 1988) cells were transformed with pASK-IBA3plus::IsdHN2N3 and growth was carried out in TB medium under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 8 h. The culture was then induced with 0.2 µg/mL anhydrotetracycline (IBA Lifesciences) and allowed to express the protein at 20 °C for 20 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in buffer W (100 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) containing 1 mg/mL lysozyme and a protease inhibitor mix. The cells were lysed via sonication, and the resulting debris was cleared by centrifugation. The protein was purified using a pre-equilibrated column packed with StrepTactin®XT resin (IBA Lifesciences) and eluted with 50 mM biotin in Buffer W. Since IsdHN2N3 partially acquires heme during overexpression, the amount of holo-IsdHN2N3 in the preparation was estimated by calculating the heme concentration (ε401 = 81,200 M−1 cm−1) using the Pyridine Hemochromogen Assay (Barr and Guo, 2015) and resulted lower than 3 % of the total amount of the protein.

The expression and purification protocol of S. aureus IsdB was adapted from Gianquinto et al. (Gianquinto et al., 2019). IsdBN1N2 (UniProt entry Q8NX66, residues 125–485) was codon-optimized for E. coli, cloned into the pASK-IBA3-plus vector, and expressed in E. coli BL21 cells. Protein expression was induced with 0.2 μg/mL anhydrotetracycline at 20 °C for 20 h. After cell lysis, the protein was purified using Strep-Tactin®XT affinity resin, followed by SEC in buffer W. The final yield exceeded 100 mg/L with a purity > 95 %. Protein concentration was determined using an extinction coefficient of ε280 nm = 47,790 M−1 cm−1. The holo-IsdB content was estimated to be < 3 % based on heme absorbance at 405 nm (ε405 nm = 90,500 M−1 cm−1) (Gianquinto et al., 2019).

IsdHN2L (UniProt entry Q8NW39, residues 326–543) and IsdBN1L (UniProt entry Q8NX66, residues 125–340) constructs were produced through deletion of the heme binding domain via PCR. After separation, CMB was removed by treating the subunits with a 50-fold molar excess of cysteine relative to the number of thiol groups to be restored. Finally, the protein solutions were bufferexchanged into the Hb storage buffer.

Cryo-EM specimen preparation and data collection

Based on SEC experiments, IsdHN2N3 was mixed with HbCO in a 1:1 stoichiometric ratio (one IsdH molecule per Hb chain) to achieve a final concentration of 6 g/L. The samples were prepared in a 10 mM HEPES buffer with 1 mM EDTA (pH 7.2) to minimize background electron density during cryo-EM data collection. The zwitterionic detergent 3-([3-cholamidopropyl]dimethylammonio)-2-hydroxy-1-propanesulfonate (CHAPSO) was added to a final concentration of 8 mM immediately before plunge freezing to prevent the particles from adopting a preferred orientation in the vitreous ice. A total of 3 μL of the complex solutions was deposited onto glow-discharged 300-mesh R1.2/1.3 QUANTIFOIL® grids (Quantifoil Micro Tools GmbH), blotted for 3 s, and rapidly frozen in liquid ethane. The grids were processed using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with the sample chamber maintained at 4 °C and 99 % humidity.

IsdHN2N3:HbCO sample was imaged in a Titan Krios cryo-electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 300 kV, with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector (Diamond Light Scource facility, Oxford) at the calibrated magnification of × 130,000, yielding a pixel size of 0.645 Å. The dataset resulted in a total of 10,000 micrographs, with a defocus range of −1.5/-3.3.

Cryo-EM image processing

The single-particle analysis of the IsdHN2N3:HbCO complex was carried out using RELION (Scheres, 2012). Following the SPA workflow in RELION-5.0, the micrographs were pre-processed (beam-induced motion correction and CTF estimation). Micrographs with low resolution (rlnCtfMaxResolution > 5.5 Å) and high astigmatism (rlnCtfAstigmatism > 300) were excluded. The TOPAZ neural network (Bepler et al., 2019) was employed to auto-pick 3 million particles, which were subsequently extracted. An initial round of 2D classification allowed us to exclude poor-quality particles. Finally, iterative steps of 2D and 3D classification (with and without alignment) led to the separation of four distinct subsets of particles, each containing more than 100,000 particles, describing different conformational states of the IsdHN2N3:HbCO complex (Fig. S2).

Cryo-EM flexibility analysis

Flexibility analysis was carried out within Scipion framework (de la Rosa-Trevín et al., 2016). Particles from 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim (90,561) and 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim (170,956) complexes were subjected to 3D Variability Analysis (3DVA) in cryoSPARC (Punjani and Fleet, 2021), with a solvent mask covering Hb and IsdH molecules. A total of 5 components were solved, considering only up to 5 Å resolution. Conformational changes captured by each component were assessed by running 3D Variability Display job in simple mode and examining the intermediate volumes generated along each eigenvector. Next, conformational changes observed in 3DVA were validated with reconstructions made from subsets of particles obtained with 3D Variability Display in intermediate mode. Namely, subsets were made based on their position along a given component in a total of 10 non-overlapping, equally spaced subsets for both 2IsdH:Hbdim complexes. Since particles followed a Gaussian distribution along each component, subsets from the left and right tails of the distribution were subjected to a local refinement job in order to obtain 3D reconstructions of the most distinct conformations detected in each component. CryoSPARC-generated intermediate reconstruction for each subset was used as initial volume for local refinements. The resulting maps are referred to as “left” and “right” in the main text.

ZART (Herreros et al., 2023) and Dynamight (Schwab et al., 2024) flexible refinement protocols were applied to the 3IsdH(α2β):Hbtet complex to increase the local resolution of flexible regions and facilitate model building and interpretation in those areas. Calculation of local resolution was performed using MonoRes (Vilas et al., 2018). Initial per-particle Zernike coefficients necessary for flexible reconstruction with ZART algorithm were calculated with “flexible align – Zernike3D protocol” (Herreros et al., 2023), with Zernike and Harmonical degree values of 3 and 2, respectively, learning rate of 10-5 for batchsize 8 for 20 epochs. Resulting particles with Zernike coefficients were subjected to ZART reconstruction. The output volume served as a reference in a new iteration of the flexible align – Zernike3D protocol, with the aim of refining the Zernike coefficients. This cycle of coefficient estimation and ZART reconstruction was iterated for five times until a final 3D reconstruction was obtained.

Dynamight was run after a 3D auto-refine protocol using a solvent mask and 3500 gaussians and initial map threshold of 0.006. Inverse-deformation estimation and deformed backprojection steps were executed afterwards to obtain deformation-corrected half maps, which were used as input for a Postprocessing job to calculate Gold-Standard FSC.Cryo-EM Model building.

The atomic model of the IsdH:HbCO 3IsdH(α2β):Hbtet complex was prepared by manually adjusting and refining an initial structure predicted with AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024) The cryo-EM map was sharpened using DeepEMhancer (Sanchez-Garcia et al., 2021), either locally in Scipion (de la Rosa-Trevín et al., 2016) or via the COSMIC2 cloud platform (Cianfrocco et al., 2017). The model was further optimized through multiple iterative cycles of real-space refinement in Phenix (Afonine et al., 2018), applying secondary structure and Ramachandran restraints, followed by manual rebuilding in Coot (Emsley et al., 2010).

To analyse the contact network between IsdH and Hb in the 3IsdH(α2β):Hbtet complex, we used the RING tool (Del Conte et al., 2024) via the online server (https://ring.biocomputingup.it/). The obtained output, which includes both polar and non-polar interactions, was further processed and summarized in Table S3 to visualize the contact patterns based on the Hb residues involved. Taking advantage of the high sequence homology, homologous contacts between IsdH bound to α-chains and those bound to β-chains were grouped for comparative analysis.

Model building was performed on the maps obtained from the 3D refinement of the tail distributions (components 0 and 4 for the 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim complex, and components 0 and 1 for the 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim complex) generated by 3DVA). A generic model of the 2IsdH:Hbdim complex was initially built based on the previously refined 3IsdH(α2β):Hbtet complex. This model was then used as the input for flexible fitting with Namdinator (Kidmose et al., 2019) on each of the 3D-refined maps. In order to optimize the fitting, the Namdinator procedure was run twice per map: first using the generic 2IsdH:Hbdim model as input and then repeating the fitting with the output from the first run. For the maps corresponding to the 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim complex, resolution was insufficient to allow further refinement beyond flexible fitting, and the resulting models were considered final (Fig. S4A-D). In contrast, the maps corresponding to the 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim complex exhibited sufficient resolution to proceed with additional refinement steps.

Atomic models corresponding to the 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim complex (Fig. S4I-L), derived from maps obtained by isolating the tails of the Gaussian distributions defined by CryoSPARC 3DVA (Punjani and Fleet, 2021) for components c0 and c4, were prepared following the same protocol described for the 3IsdH(α2β):Hbtet complex. In this case, the starting models were not based on AlphaFold predictions, but instead, those obtained through flexible fitting using Namdinator (Fig. S4E-H) were used.

Due to poorly resolved or missing density in certain regions across all maps, atomic models for both 3IsdH(α2β):Hbtet and 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim complexes were omitted in those areas.

3DVA also enabled the reconstruction of intermediate volumes describing the structural flexibility of the 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim and 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim complexes along components 0 and 4, and 0 and 1, respectively. To perform model fitting into each intermediate volume in an automated, high-throughput manner – thus avoiding the need for submitting individual Namdinator jobs – TEMPy-REFF (Beton et al., 2024) was used via command-line execution. The selected protocol involved rigid-body refinement of the models previously obtained by flexible fitting into the left-tail volumes (i.e., models 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim c0-left, 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim c4-left, 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim c0-left, and 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim c1-left; see Fig. S4A,C,E,G), using the “fitting-density” mode of the software. Rigid bodies for refinement were first extracted from the initial models using RIBFIND (Malhotra et al., 2023).

Evaluation of conformational variability

Conformational dynamics across conformational landscape identified by 3DVA were investigated using a custom Python script that leveraged the ProDy library (Zhang et al., 2021). The series of rigid-body refined PDB files parsed using prody.parsePDB() to load the atomic coordinates, was analyzed to extract C-alpha (CA) atom coordinates from each frame. A mean CA structure was subsequently derived by averaging the atomic coordinates over the entire ensemble. To quantify local flexibility, the Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) was calculated for each CA atom. The calculated RMSF values were then assigned as B-factors to the corresponding atoms in the averaged CA structure. For visualization and interpretation of these dynamic properties, the mean CA structure, annotated with RMSF values as B-factors, was exported in PDB format using prody.writePDB(). A PyMOL script was generated to render this structure. The thickness of the protein backbone was scaled according to the B-factor values, with thicker regions indicating higher flexibility. Additionally, a blue-white-red color spectrum was mapped onto the B-factors, providing a visual gradient from low (blue) to high (red) atomic fluctuations.

To identify similarities between the conformational states revealed by 3DVA and known IsdH structures from the literature (PDB IDs: 4IJ2 (Dickson et al., 2014), 4XS0 (Dickson et al., 2015), and 6TB2(Mikkelsen et al., 2020), assumed to represent stable states), as well as stable conformations observed in this study (IsdH bound to β1- and α2-chains in the 3IsdH(α2β):Hbtet complex, to α-chains in the 2IsdH(αβ*):Hbdim complex, or to β-chains in the 2IsdH(α*β):Hbdim complex), a root mean square deviation (RMSD)-based analysis was performed. Hemophore structures from models refined by rigid-body fitting with TEMPy-REFF (considered “flexible” in this analysis) were extracted and compared to all IsdH chains from the models assumed to be stable. Structures were aligned based on the NEAT2-linker region, identified as the most stable portion in the deformation analysis (Fig. 2B-C). RMSD values between aligned models were then calculated, keeping IsdH conformations bound to α-chains separate from those bound to β-chains. Results were summarized in a correlation matrix, where each flexible–stable IsdH pair was color-coded: dark blue for low RMSD values (high similarity), and yellow for high RMSD values (low similarity).

Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSorbent assay (ELISA)

The affinity of IsdHN2N3, IsdHN2L, IsdBN1N2 and IsdBN1L were measured exploiting an optimized immunoassay, as previously described in Cozzi et al. (Cozzi et al., 2024). Briefly, hemophores were immobilized onto Strep-Tactin®XT coated microplates (IBA Lifesciences) exploiting the protein C-terminal StrepTag®II. A solution of the hemophores is incubated for 1 h in a microplate at the final concentration of 400 nM in 200 µL/well of a 2 mM EDTA, 140 mM NaCl, and 25 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.6, solution (binding buffer). Hb is then added at concentrations ranging from 0.5 nM to 50 µM in a final volume of 100 μL of binding buffer per well and incubated for 1 h. IsdB/H:Hb complexes were detected by 1 h incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-coupled anti-Hb polyclonal antibodies (Abcam) at 1:10,000 dilution. Each step is anticipated by a quick wash using washing buffer (binding buffer supplemented with 0.05 % Tween-20). Bound Hb was quantified by measuring the catalytic conversion of 100 µL/well of a 3,3′,5,5′- tetramethylbenzidine (TMB, Acros Organics) solution, and the reaction was developed for 1 min. Subsequently, 100 µL/well of 1 N H2SO4 were applied to stop the reaction. Absorption of the HRP reaction product was recorded at 450 nm with a microplate reader (Dynamica Halo LED 96), and background values from TMB solutions were subtracted from each reading. All the steps before the plate reading were carried out at 4 °C. Size exclusion chromatography

SEC was conducted at 25 °C on an Agilent 1260 HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) connected to either a AdvanceBio SEC column (300 Å, 2.7 μm, 7.8 × 300 mm) or a Superdex S200 increase 3.2/300 column, an Agilent 1260 Infinity II WR Diode Array Detector, and an Agilent 1260 Infinity II Refractive Index Detector. The column was equilibrated with PBS buffer and operated at a constant flow rate (1 mL/min for AdvanceBio column or 0.07 mL/min for Superdex S200 column). Samples containing either oxyHb, separated Hb chains in the oxygenated form, IsdHN2N3, IsdHN2L and IsdBN1L, or the complexes between Hb and tested hemophores were injected at a concentration of approximately 1 g/L (see Fig. 3D-K and S1 legends for exact concentrations). Data analysis was performed using Agilent GPC/SEC software to estimate the oligomeric state of the protein samples. The molecular weight standards used for column calibration were ferritin (440 kDa), alcohol dehydrogenase (140 kDa), conalbumin (75 kDa), and myoglobin (17 kDa).

Stopped-Flow time-resolved absorbance and fluorescence Kinetics analysis

Time-resolved absorbance and fluorescence measurements were carried out using an SX18 stopped-flow apparatus (Applied Photophysics) equipped with a 150 W xenon light source having a dead time of ∼ 1.5 ms. The system was operated either with a photomultiplier tube for bandpass-filtered fluorescence detection or with a Direct Coupled Photodiode Array Detector for rapid multi-wavelength absorbance acquisition. Experiments were conducted at 20 °C in buffer W (pH 8.0) using equimolar concentrations (40 μM) of the hemophore and metHb. Fluorescence measurements were performed by exciting the samples at 298 nm and detecting emission at 90° through a 320 nm long-pass filter; each fluorescence trace consisted of 10,000 points recorded over 300 s. In parallel, absorbance measurements were acquired as 1,000 logarithmically spaced spectra from 320 to 700 nm within the same time window. Absorbance data were processed using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to identify the main kinetic components, followed by global fitting in MATLAB (lsqmultinonlin function) using a six-exponential model (Eq. 2):yx=ae-r1t+be-r2t+ce-r3t+de-r4t+fe-r5t+ge-r6t+y0In this equation, the amplitudes (a–g) were allowed to vary across datasets, while the rate constants (k1–k6) were globally shared. All data analysis procedures were performed using custom MATLAB scripts.

Uv–vis spectroscopy

Heme extraction experiments were conducted using a Cary 4000 spectrophotometer (Agilent) to measure absorbance in the UV–visible range. Spectra were recorded between 350 and 700 nm, with heme-transfer rates specifically monitored at 406 nm. The cell holder's temperature was kept constant at 20 °C using a circulating water bath. Heme transfer from metHb to either IsdHN2N3 or IsdBN1N2 was monitored by tracking the decrease in absorbance at 406 nm, following the method described by Gianquinto et al. (Gianquinto et al., 2019). Briefly, 3 µM metHb (on Hb chain basis) was pre-incubated for 3 min at 20 °C in buffer W. After equilibration, the heme transfer reaction was initiated by adding either IsdHN2N3 or IsdBN1N2 to a final concentration of 15 µM (5-fold molar excess). Kinetic traces of absorbance at 406 nm were fitted to the following bi-exponential equation (Eq. 3) using Matlab fitting toolbox (https://www.mathworks.com/products/curvefitting.html):y=a-k1t+b-k2t+y0where y is the absorbance at 406 nm, t is time (s), a and b are the amplitudes of the fast and slow kinetic phases, k1 and k2 their corresponding rate constants, and yo is the baseline offset.

Hb autoxidation

The autoxidation of oxyHb was assessed using UV–Vis spectroscopy in PBS buffer (pH 7.4) at 37 °C with a Cary 4000 spectrophotometer (Agilent). Spectra were recorded between 310 and 750 nm every 30 min over a total period of 330 min. The autoxidation of Hb was analyzed in the absence or presence of IsdHN2N3. In all conditions, both Hb (on a heme basis) and the tested hemophores were used at a final concentration of 7 μM. The data collected at each time point were analyzed by fitting them to a linear combination of reference spectra as reported by De Bei et al. (De Bei et al., 2022). The reference spectra for IsdHN2N3, oxyHb, metHb, and the IsdHN2N3:metHb complex were acquired under the same conditions as the autoxidation experiment. Fitting was performed in MATLAB using the FMINCON function () to minimize the χ2 function defined as (Eq. 4):χ2=experimentalspectrum-∑pari∗referencespectrum(i)2The par in Eq. 4 are the coefficients of each i reference spectrum used. Autoxidation of oxyHb was quantified by plotting the coefficient associated with the metHb spectrum as a function of time. In samples containing IsdHN2N3, we monitored the coefficient of the IsdHN2N3:metHb complex instead, based on the assumption that IsdHN2N3 rapidly extracts heme upon Hb oxidation.

The same fitting protocol was applied to assess the autoxidation of oxyHb in the presence of IsdHN2L or IsdBN1L. In this case, the reference spectra of IsdHN2N3 were replaced with those of IsdHN2L or IsdBN1L, respectively. Since these truncated variants lack the heme-binding domain and are unable to extract heme, the extent of Hb autoxidation was quantified using the metHb coefficient.

Atomic force spectroscopy (AFS)

IsdHN2N3 and metHb molecules were covalently immobilized onto AFM cantilevers and glass substrates, respectively, following previously established protocols (Botti et al., 2024, Pancrazi et al., 2025). Silicon nitride AFM tips (cantilever D, SNL-10; Bruker Corporation, Massachusetts, USA) with a nominal spring constant of 0.06 N/m were first treated with 2 % (v/v) 3-mercaptopropyl-trimethoxysilane (MPTMS; Sigma-Aldrich) in toluene (99.5 %; Sigma-Aldrich) for surface thiol activation. Tips were then incubated for 3 h in 1 mM N-hydroxysuccinimide–polyethylene glycol–maleimide (NHS-PEG-MAL, 3.4 kDa, hereafter PEG; Iris Biotech) in DMSO (99.9 %; Sigma-Aldrich). Functionalization with IsdHN2N3 was achieved by overnight incubation at 4 °C with 25 μL of 5 μM protein in Buffer W, promoting covalent coupling between NHS esters of the PEG and lysine side chains on the protein surface. Aldehyde-functionalized glass slides (1 cm2; PolyAn GmbH, Germany) were incubated in an air-tight container with 50 μL of 10 μM metHb in Buffer W for 4 h at 25 °C to allow covalent attachment via Schiff base formation between aldehyde groups and protein amino groups. After rinsing with buffer and Milli-Q water, residual reactive groups on both tips and substrates were passivated by 30 min incubation with 1 M ethanolamine hydrochloride (pH 8.5) in Milli-Q water (GE Healthcare). Measurements were immediately conducted after the glass slides functionalization, conversely, IsdH-functionalized-tips were wet stored in buffer W at 4 °C in between experiments.

AFS experiments were conducted at room temperature using a Nanoscope IIIa/Multimode AFM (Veeco Instruments, New York, USA) in fluid (Buffer W saturated with N2 to preserve metHb oxidation state). Force curves were collected by approaching the IsdHN2N3 functionalized tip to metHb-charged substrate and then retracting it. The approaching phase was stopped upon reaching a preset maximum contact force value of 0.7 nN. A ramp size of 150 nm and an encounter time of 100 ms were set up. The approach was fixed at a constant velocity of 50 nm/s, while the retraction velocity was varied from 50 to 4200 nm/s, leading to different loading rates (Fig. S8A-E), defined as dF/dt and given by the product of the cantilever retraction velocity (v) and the spring constant of the system, ksyst, accounting for the effect of the molecules tied to the tip, and is calculated from the slope of the retraction curve immediately before the unbinding event (Hutter and Bechhoefer, 1993). The unbinding force F was calculated by multiplying the cantilever deflection by its effective spring constant (keff), experimentally evaluated by the procedure outlined in Kienberger et al. (Kienberger et al., 2006). Curves characterized, during the retraction phase, by sharp peaks with starting and ending points at zero deflection line, and by a nonlinear curved shape before the jump-off, which was related to the stretching features of the PEG linker, were selected as related to specific unbinding events according to the procedure reported in (Bizzarri and Cannistraro, 2011, Liu and Naismith, 2008). For each loading rate, a thousand force curves were acquired. Each experiment was carried out by using the same functionalized tip and experiments were conducted in triplicates.

To assess the force dependence of bond dissociation, the most probable unbinding force (F*) was plotted against the natural logarithm of the loading rate. This analysis follows the Bell-Evans theory (Bell, 1978, Evans, 2001), which describes how the application of an external force lowers the energy barrier for dissociation, thereby accelerating bond rupture. According to this model, F* and the loading rate (r) are related through the following expression (Eq. 5):F∗=kBTxβlnrxβkoffkBTwhere kB is the Boltzmann constant, T the absolute temperature, koff the dissociation rate at zero force, and xβ the width of the energy barrier along the force direction.

Blocking experiments (Fig. S8 Panel F-G) were carried out by collecting force curves at the retraction velocity of 350 nm/s with the IsdHN2N3-functionalized tip, which had been previously incubated with a Hb solution at 10 μM, against the Hb-substrate in Buffer W at 25 °C.
