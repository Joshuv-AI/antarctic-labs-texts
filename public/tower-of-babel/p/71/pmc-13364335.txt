Two Lungs, One Pleural Space, Managing Buffalo Chest Syndrome with Bilateral Drainage Following Computed Tomography-guided Lung Biopsy, A Case Report
Authors: Parth Gulati, Angus Chew, Kenneth K. Lau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Buffalo chest syndrome is a rare condition describing simultaneous bilateral pneumothoraces due to pleuropleural communication, with fewer than 50 reported cases. We describe a 70-year-old with prior esophagectomy/thoracotomy, who developed symptomatic bilateral pneumothoraces immediately after computed tomography-guided biopsy of a single lung lesion. This prompted insertion of bilateral 14-French pleural drains, achieving immediate clinical stability and full radiological resolution within 24 hours, compared with 2 to 5 days reported with single-drain management in existing literature. This single case highlights the potential role of early bilateral pleural drainage in buffalo chest syndrome, suggesting the potential of more rapid clinical and radiological resolution compared with previously reported single-drain approaches in symptomatic patients.

Introduction

Buffalo chest syndrome is a rare phenomenon describing bilateral pneumothoraces due to pleuropleural communication, allowing air to equalize across pleural cavities. First described in bison anatomy and rarely reported in humans, with fewer than 50 cases in existing literature, most following cardiothoracic surgery such as esophagectomies, cardiac bypasses, or trauma [1]. Lung biopsy-triggered cases are exceptionally uncommon, and most symptomatic cases reported often resolved with a single chest tube drainage over 2-5 days [2, 3]. Management debates persist, particularly whether bilateral chest tubes achieve faster clinical and radiologic resolution [4].

This case report describes a 70-year-old male who underwent a CT-guided lung biopsy for a suspicious left lower lobe lung lesion complicated by symptomatic bilateral pneumothoraces that achieved rapid resolution with 14-French (14F) pleural drains bilaterally within 24 hours. It highlights the need for procedural radiologists to recognize this unusual phenomenon in patients with prior thoracic procedures, which may require prompt bilateral pleural drainage.

Case Report

A 70-year-old Caucasian male with a previous history of Ivor-Lewis procedure for esophageal cancer more than 20 years prior and wedge resection of right lower lobe lung adenocarcinoma about 10 years prior presented for a CT-guided biopsy of a recently discovered suspicious spiculated 15 mm × 13 mm × 15 mm left lower lobe lung lesion. There was no apparent connection between pleural cavities, pleural effusion, or pneumothorax on the pre-procedural CT chest. The patient was placed prone on the CT table for the biopsy procedure. An Argon 17-gauge, 6.8 cm coaxial introducer needle was inserted into the left lower lobe lung lesion. The needle traversed the left posterior pleura only once, and the contralateral chest was not punctured (Figure 1). An Argon BioPince Ultra 18-gauge, 10 cm biopsy needle was then used to obtain the core specimen. The immediate post-biopsy CT demonstrated rapid development of bilateral pneumothoraces with an apparent air-distended communication between the two pleural cavities posterior to the stomach pull-through (Figure 2). The patient became very dyspneic with worsening chest pain. Bilateral intercostal 14F, 29 cm Cook Wayne pneumothorax drains were promptly inserted and connected to an underwater seal (Figure 3). The patient experienced immediate relief of symptoms upon drain insertions and was admitted overnight for monitoring.

Repeat chest x-ray at 24 hours post procedure confirmed resolution of both pneumothoraces. Both drains were clamped at 28 hours post-procedure and removed shortly after. The patient was discharged on Day 3 following complete clinical and radiological resolution (Figure 4).

Discussion

Buffalo chest phenomenon has been hypothesized to result from interpleural communication due to damage to pleural folds at junction lines in the setting of previous major mediastinal surgery [5]. It is plausible that our patient’s pleuropleural communication was established at the time of his Ivor-Lewis procedure over two decades ago and was subsequently unmasked by the left lower lobe biopsy air leak despite untraversed right pleura.

Existing literature reveals that most buffalo chest phenomena have been treated either with conservative treatment or single chest drain tube insertion, in which both clinical and radiological resolution were commonly achieved within 2-5 days, which therefore required the duration of hospital inpatient stay, monitoring, and thus resources [2, 3]. While Salman et al. [4] first reported simultaneous bilateral pneumothorax post-biopsy requiring bilateral 16F drains (with discharge on Day 1 and drains removed post-discharge at 3 weeks), our patient demonstrates even faster resolution with bilateral chest drain and was discharged at 48 hours post-biopsy. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported buffalo chest case managed with primary bilateral chest tube insertion at initial diagnosis on the immediate post-biopsy CT, achieving radiologic resolution at 24 hours versus 2–5 days with single-drain approaches in prior literature. It is important to recognize that the optimal management strategy remains a point of clinical debate, as an approach is required that balances between procedural urgency and the principle of avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures. Bilateral pleural drainage may be warranted in patients who present with severe symptoms, hemodynamic instability, or rapid clinical deterioration – indicators of potential tension physiology that can occur rapidly when both pleural spaces are compromised. Conversely, unilateral drainage may be sufficient in selected stable patients where the interpleural communication is narrow or non-obstructive, allowing for air to effectively equilibrate and be evacuated from a single tube. Each additional chest drain increases the risk of associated complications such as neurovascular injury, localised infection, and procedure-associated pain [6]. Therefore, clinicians must carefully weigh the clinical evidence against the morbidity associated with additional tube insertions.

This case report reinforces several teaching points. Firstly, the risk of bilateral pneumothoraces should be explained to patients with a previous history of thoracic procedures prior to CT-guided chest procedures. Secondly, immediate post-biopsy CT must include assessment of the contralateral hemithorax in patients with a history of thoracic surgery, as subtle bilateral pneumothoraces can progress rapidly. Moreover, we hypothesize from our case that a high suspicion for buffalo chest phenomenon may warrant early bilateral drainage over a conservative or unilateral approach in symptomatic patients to avert tension physiology and expedite recovery times. The findings of our case report require validation through larger series; however, the imaging timeline and rapid bilateral drain response provide compelling procedural evidence in symptomatic patients.

Patient Perspective

The patient described sudden post-biopsy dyspnea that was relieved immediately by drains. The patient was counseled on future risks; phone review at one week confirmed no recurrence. The patient provided verbal consent for the case report.
