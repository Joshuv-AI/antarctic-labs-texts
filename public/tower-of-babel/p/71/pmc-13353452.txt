Regarding: “Development and validation of a self-management efficacy questionnaire for patients with Fabry disease in China”
Authors: Dan Zhao, Wei Zhou
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

We read with great interest the study by Cao et al., which presents the development and validation of the first self-management efficacy questionnaire (F-SEQ) for patients with Fabry disease (FD) in China [1]. The authors have conducted a rigorous psychometric evaluation, resulting in a tool with excellent structural validity and reliability. The identification of the ‘Emotional and Social Management Confidence’ (ESMC) dimension and its significant correlation with patient-reported outcomes (PROs), such as reduced bodily pain, provides a strong empirical rationale for integrating psychosocial assessment into holistic FD management [1]. This instrument represents a pivotal advancement in PROs measures for this rare disease.

In the spirit of scholarly dialogue, we offer reflections to enrich the clinical translation and future optimization of this excellent tool, specifically regarding its application in the paediatric cohort included in the study. The study included individuals aged ≤18 years, accounting for 4.5% (n = 24) of the validation sample, with guardians permitted to assist in understanding the items [1]. Notably, FD is an inborn error of metabolism with early-onset manifestations in childhood. Neuropathic pain, gastrointestinal symptoms and corneal opacities may emerge as early as 2–4 years of age, with progressive multisystem involvement adversely affecting quality of life [2]. Timely diagnosis and intervention are critical to avert progressive organ damage. Thus, paediatric patients warrant particular clinical and research attention. This further raises important methodological considerations for paediatric assessment. Reliance on proxy reporting or assisted completion can introduce bias, as parents’ perceptions of a child’s self-efficacy may be influenced by their own caregiver burden and not fully align with the child’s experience [3]. This challenge of ensuring authentic, developmentally appropriate assessment is central to paediatric PROM development. A recent multi-stakeholder Delphi study aimed at creating paediatric patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) underscored the necessity of treating children as active subjects in their care, advocating for tools designed to capture their direct perspectives rather than relying solely on parental proxy [4]. The consensus from that process strongly recommended developing age-stratified instruments (e.g. 4–7 years, 8–13 years) and integrating visual elements to support comprehension and engagement for younger children [4].

Given these established principles, the inclusion of paediatric participants in the F-SEQ validation, while commendable, highlights a critical gap and an immediate research opportunity. The current adult-oriented tool, even with guardian assistance, may not validly capture the unique self-efficacy perceptions of children and adolescents with FD, whose cognitive understanding, disease experience and reporting capacities differ fundamentally from adults. This is particularly relevant as clinical research and management in adolescent FD increasingly utilizes PROs to monitor treatment response and quality of life [5].

Therefore, to fully realize the potential of the F-SEQ across the lifespan of FD, we propose that the logical and necessary next step is the development and validation of a parallel, paediatric-adapted version (p-F-SEQ). This endeavour should be guided by the child-centred framework emphasized in paediatric measurement science. The precedent for using children-specific PROs in FD research, such as the Fabry-specific Paediatric Health and Pain Questionnaire (FPHPQ) employed in recent clinical trials, confirms both the feasibility and clinical utility of such tailored instruments for younger populations [5]. Specifically, the p-F-SEQ should be developed through a dedicated process involving cognitive interviews with paediatric patients, employing simplified language and utilizing age-appropriate response formats. The successful translation and validation of tools like the Visual CARE Measure, which uses pictorial Likert scales to assess children’s perceptions of clinical empathy, demonstrate the feasibility, acceptability and strong psychometric performance of such visual adaptations for children as young as 7 years [3]. Future research could explore embedding a gamified, interactive p-F-SEQ within a digital health platform, transforming assessment from a passive task into an engaging experience.

In summary, the F-SEQ is a seminal and methodologically sound contribution to FD care. We commend the authors for including paediatric participants, which rightly spotlights the need for dedicated tools in this population. We strongly encourage the research team to build upon this excellent work by pursuing the development of a paediatric-specific version. By integrating principles of child-centred design, evidence from validated visual paediatric measures, and insights from targeted paediatric FD research and PRO utilization, such a tool would ensure that the self-efficacy and unique care experiences of children and adolescents living with FD are accurately captured. This will be essential for guiding developmentally appropriate, personalized interventions and ultimately improving long-term outcomes across all ages.
