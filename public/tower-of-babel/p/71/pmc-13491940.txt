Training status, needs, and factors associated with prior participation in phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training among cardiopulmonary rehabilitation nurses: a cross-sectional study with an exploratory nomogram analysis
Authors: Rui Liu, Hui Ji, Xiang Zhou, Xiaoyue Yang, Min Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhase II cardiac rehabilitation is essential for patient recovery, and nurses play a central role in implementing exercise prescriptions. However, training systems for this competency remain underdeveloped in China. This study examined the training status and self-reported training needs of nurses working in cardiopulmonary rehabilitation settings, and explored factors associated with prior formal participation in phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training. An exploratory nomogram was further developed as a preliminary tool for characterizing differences in prior formal participationMethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from September to October 2024 among 281 nurses from cardiopulmonary rehabilitation centers in eight hospitals across Guangxi, Yunnan, and Sichuan using purposive sampling. Data were collected through a self-developed electronic questionnaire covering participant characteristics, training status, and training needs. Prior formal participation in phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training was used as the outcome variable. A theory-informed analytical strategy that combined a DAG, univariable logistic regression, and multivariable logistic regression was used to identify associated factors and construct an exploratory nomogram. Internal validation was performed using a 7:3 training-validation split, and model performance was assessed in terms of discrimination and calibration for describing differences in prior formal participation.ResultsAlthough respondents reported high awareness of phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training and strong institutional support, prior formal participation remained insufficient. Existing programs were largely theory-based, with limited practical components and incomplete alignment with clinical needs. Nurses reported substantial demand for further training and generally preferred small-group, blended, and practice-oriented formats. In the multivariable model, age, city, sex, professional title, and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation experience were retained as factors associated with prior formal participation. The nomogram showed moderate discrimination in the training set (AUC=0.77, 95% CI: 0.71–0.84) and acceptable discrimination in the validation set (AUC=0.71, 95% CI: 0.60–0.83), with acceptable calibration in both sets (H-L P = 0.548 and 0.665, respectively).ConclusionNurses working in cardiopulmonary rehabilitation settings reported clear unmet training needs related to phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training. The exploratory nomogram showed moderate discrimination and acceptable calibration for estimating prior formal participation and may help characterize subgroup differences in prior formal participation. Introduction

Cardiovascular and respiratory diseases remain major causes of mortality and disability worldwide and substantially impair quality of life and long-term prognosis [1,2]. Advances in rehabilitation science have underscored the importance of integrated cardiopulmonary rehabilitation, which addresses the functional interdependence of the cardiovascular and respiratory systems [3]. This approach emphasizes multidisciplinary, individualized management and has been associated with improved functional capacity, better patient-reported quality of life, and fewer hospital readmissions [4,5]. Phase II cardiac rehabilitation centers on structured exercise training and is particularly important during recovery [6,7]. Evidence indicates that individualized exercise prescriptions based on FITT-VP principles can safely restore physical function, reduce risk factors, and facilitate reintegration into daily and social life [8].

Nurses are indispensable throughout this process. As frontline providers, they implement and monitor rehabilitation plans, deliver health education, manage clinical risks, and provide long-term follow-up [9]. In cardiac rehabilitation, nurses play a central role in multidisciplinary care through exercise guidance, symptom monitoring, behavioral support, and continuity of care. As cardiac rehabilitation has been shown to improve both physical and mental quality of life, nurses are essential to supporting patient-centered recovery [10]. Therefore, the effectiveness and safety of phase II exercise prescription depend largely on nurses’ competence in theoretical knowledge, standardized practice, and risk management [11]. In China, however, phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training for nurses remains insufficient, with substantial variation in training content, instructional models, and resulting competencies [12]. In the regions surveyed in this study, where cardiopulmonary rehabilitation centers are still developing, rehabilitation nursing is often delivered by general ward nurses who work across both cardiac and pulmonary settings. Without systematic training, standardized protocols, or dedicated staffing, these nurses may face difficulty conducting precise assessments, implementing exercise prescriptions effectively, and providing adequate patient support [13].

Against this background, it is important to clarify the training status and training needs related to phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training among nurses working in cardiopulmonary rehabilitation settings [14]. In addition, examining factors associated with prior formal participation may help identify groups with more limited access to structured training and thereby inform more targeted educational planning [15]. The ADDIE model provides a useful instructional design framework comprising analysis, design, development, implementation, and evaluation [16]. Guided by the analysis phase of the ADDIE framework, this study aimed to: (1) describe the training status; (2) assess nurses’ training needs and preferences; and (3) explore factors associated with prior formal participation and develop an exploratory nomogram that might offer a preliminary reference for optimizing future training coverage.

Methods

Study design and setting

A multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted from September to October 2024 in cardiopulmonary rehabilitation centers of eight hospitals affiliated with a regional medical alliance across Guangxi, Yunnan, and Sichuan provinces in southwestern China. These hospitals were purposively selected to represent a range of socioeconomic and clinical settings. Each center functioned as a designated regional cardiopulmonary rehabilitation facility with a multidisciplinary rehabilitation team that included nursing staff. All selected sites had also demonstrated a prior commitment to collaborative research and training within the alliance framework. This purposive sampling approach provided access to relatively harmonized clinical practices and enabled coordinated data collection, thereby supporting both the present investigation and the potential future implementation of research-based training programs.

Questionnaire development and validation

A research team of seven specialists in cardiopulmonary rehabilitation and nursing was established to oversee study design, implementation, and quality control. Details of team composition and task allocation are provided in Additional file 1. We conducted a comprehensive literature review across CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, Sinomed, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library; the detailed search strategy is presented in Additional file 2. Based on this review, we summarized the content and formats of existing phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training programs and used those findings to develop the initial draft questionnaire [17]. Because the questionnaire was designed to collect study-specific information on training status and training needs rather than to measure a single latent construct, content validity evaluation was considered more appropriate than conventional scale-based psychometric testing. To strengthen content validity, the draft questionnaire underwent structured expert review by a specialist panel, and all items demonstrated an item-level content validity index (I-CVI) greater than 0.78. Further details are provided in Additional file 3.

In August 2024, a pilot survey was administered to 30 randomly selected eligible nurses from the alliance. The sample included 6 male and 24 female nurses, with a mean age of 36.41 years. All participants reported that the questionnaire was understandable and provided suggestions for refinement. After team review, two items were deleted and one item was revised, yielding the final questionnaire. The final questionnaire comprised 40 items across three sections: general information, training status, and training needs. The general information section collected demographic and professional characteristics, including age, sex, education level, professional title, nursing experience, and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation experience. The training status section assessed prior formal participation, format, duration, content, and perceived relevance. The training needs section examined preferences for future training with respect to format, scale, duration, instructional methods, and expected outcomes.

The primary outcome for model development was prior formal participation in phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training, assessed as a binary variable (trained vs. untrained). Formal training was defined as organized teaching with predefined content, schedule, and delivery arrangements, regardless of when or where it had been completed before the survey. In the absence of a mature and objective indicator for prioritizing future training allocation, prior formal participation was treated as a pragmatic proxy for previous access to structured training opportunities. Under this framework, the exploratory model was intended to characterize variation in prior structured training status across subgroups rather than to predict competence, training effectiveness, or absolute future training needs.

Other training-related items, including training needs and perceived relevance, were analyzed descriptively only. Dichotomous items were coded as 0/1, single-choice items were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and multiple-choice items were summarized as the number and proportion of respondents selecting each option. No composite score was calculated because the questionnaire was designed to describe distinct training characteristics and needs rather than to generate scale totals. The questionnaire incorporated logic checks to improve data integrity, and multiple-choice items were used to capture variation in training content and teaching methods. The final questionnaire is provided in Additional file 4.

Participants and sampling

Figure 1 illustrates the participant flow. The sample size was calculated using Kendall’s method, considering 5–10 times the number of questionnaire items and accounting for a 20.00% non-response rate, resulting in a minimum required sample of 270 [18]. Eligible participants met all of the following criteria: (1) registered nurse or above, with at least two years of clinical experience in cardiac or pulmonary rehabilitation; (2) possession of a valid national nurse practice registration certificate; and (3) ability to understand the study and provide voluntary written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) inability to work during the study period because of maternity leave, sick leave, advanced training, or similar reasons; and (2) voluntary withdrawal or discontinuation during the study period.

Participant flow diagram

An electronic questionnaire was administered through the Wenjuanxing platform. The research team worked closely with affiliated hospitals and obtained ethical approval before data collection. Questionnaire links, QR codes, and the inclusion and exclusion criteria were provided to designated local coordinators together with detailed explanations of study objectives, confidentiality safeguards, and informed consent procedures. These coordinators then assisted with screening eligible participants. Owing to practical constraints, 300 nurses were invited to participate. Of these, 298 questionnaires were submitted, corresponding to a response rate of 99.33%. After screening, 17 invalid questionnaires were excluded, leaving 281 valid responses and a valid response rate of 93.66%.

Quality control

The survey could be completed without time or geographic restrictions, but each IP address was limited to a single submission to prevent duplicate responses. Standardized instructions were provided to promote accurate understanding of the questionnaire. After data collection, duplicate and invalid questionnaires were excluded according to prespecified screening rules, including repeated submissions from the same IP address, substantial logical inconsistencies between related items, obviously uniform or patterned responses suggestive of non-engaged completion, and missing key eligibility or outcome items [19]. Only questionnaires that passed these checks were retained for analysis. To reduce potential selection and information bias, all sites followed uniform eligibility criteria, recruitment procedures, and survey administration protocols; in addition, pilot testing, expert review, built-in logic checks, and prespecified data-screening rules were applied to enhance data consistency and reliability.

Statistics

Data were entered independently by two researchers, and all statistical analyses were performed using Zstats, R version 4.5.1, and SPSS. The final analytical dataset contained complete data for all analyzed variables, thus no imputation was applied. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons were performed using the t-test; non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. To improve interpretability, continuous and ordinal variables were categorized where appropriate. Age was grouped as < 30, 30–40, and > 40 years, and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation experience as < 10 and ≥ 10 years to reflect career stage and experience thresholds. Other experience-related variables, including nursing experience and patient volume, were categorized similarly to improve consistency and interpretability.

A theory-informed modelling strategy was adopted to explore factors associated with prior formal participation in phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training. Before univariable and multivariable analyses, a DAG was developed on the basis of prior literature, clinical knowledge, and preliminary findings to identify candidate variables and clarify plausible dependency structures. Univariable logistic regression was then performed to examine the association between each candidate variable and prior formal participation, using a threshold of P < 0.20 to reduce the risk of excluding variables with potential clinical or contextual relevance [20]. Variables considered clinically relevant a priori according to the DAG, together with those meeting the univariable screening criterion, were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model.

For construction of the exploratory nomogram, the dataset was randomly divided into training and validation sets at a ratio of 7:3. Model adequacy was assessed with reference to the observed event proportion, the number of candidate variables, and the EPV ratio, with EPV ≥ 10 considered indicative of acceptable model stability [21]. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination and calibration using ROC curves, Calibration Curves, the H-L test, and classification indices including Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, PPV, NPV. DCA was conducted as a supplementary analysis [22,23]. All final inferential analyses employed two-sided tests at α = 0.05. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Additional file 5 presents the training status of phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training. Among the 281 nurses, 162 (57.65%) had previously received formal training, whereas 119 (42.35%) had not. Although most respondents reported institutional emphasis on training (92.17%) and active attention to developments in the field (65.12%), overall participation remained limited: 23.84% had attended one session, 12.46% had attended two sessions, and 21.35% had attended three or more sessions, whereas only 15.30% had received training within the previous 3 months. Participation was driven mainly by professional skill development (92.88%), career advancement (87.90%), and job requirements (81.49%). Existing training opportunities were concentrated at the departmental (46.62%) and hospital-based (39.15%) levels, with relatively few opportunities at the municipal (12.46%) or provincial (9.96%) levels. The most common instructional method was theoretical teaching (54.09%), whereas case-based learning (32.03%), simulation exercises (25.27%), and interactive workshops (17.79%) were less common. Training was generally brief, with 51.96% lasting < 10 h. Offline intensive teaching was the most common theoretical format (32.03%), while blended online-offline delivery accounted for only 10.32%. In practical training, passive video observation was more common than case analysis, simulation without equipment, or hands-on practice with equipment. Assessment was also limited, with only 38.79% and 40.21% of respondents reporting theoretical and practical assessments, respectively, and only 48.40% indicating that the training met their actual needs. Overall, the current training landscape was characterized by a strong theoretical emphasis, limited practical depth, and incomplete alignment with nurses’ needs.

Additional file 6 summarizes nurses’ training needs related to phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training. Demand for further training was high: 249 of 281 nurses (88.61%) reported a need for additional training, and 274 (97.51%) supported development of a standardized in-hospital training program. Preferred organizational formats were departmental training (76.51%) and hospital-based training (70.46%), and more than half of respondents favored small-group training with fewer than 20 participants (54.80%). Respondents also preferred multidisciplinary teaching teams involving clinical specialist nurses, rehabilitation therapists, industry experts, and clinical physicians. For theoretical instruction, online delivery was strongly preferred (77.58%), whereas for practical training, clinical instruction (60.14%) and simulated scenarios (31.32%) were favored. Most nurses preferred relatively short training periods, although more than one-third expected practical training lasting at least two weeks. Expected outcomes extended beyond basic competence and included mastery of theoretical knowledge (90.75%), acquisition of operational skills (88.97%), clarification of roles and responsibilities (87.54%), and improvement in rehabilitation practice, health education, communication, critical thinking, evidence translation, and research capacity. Taken together, these findings indicate a strong preference for a compact, practice-oriented, and competency-based training program that combines flexible theoretical delivery with clinically supervised skills training. Given the observed variation in prior formal participation, we further explored factors associated with this outcome and constructed an exploratory nomogram. Participants were randomly divided into a training set (n = 196) and a validation set (n = 85) at a ratio of 7:3. According to the DAG-informed analytical strategy, age, city, sex, educational level, position status, professional title, nursing experience, specialist nurse, patient volume, and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation experience were prespecified as candidate variables of prior formal participation (Additional file 7). Univariable logistic regression was performed in the training set using prior formal participation as the outcome and these 10 variables as candidate variables (Table 2). Variables meeting the screening criterion of P < 0.20, including age, city, sex, educational level, professional title, nursing experience, and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation experience, showed no concerning multicollinearity on VIF assessment (Additional file 8) and were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Based on the multivariable analysis, age, city, sex, professional title, and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation experience were retained in the exploratory nomogram (Table 3). In the full sample, 162 of 281 nurses had previously participated in prior formal training (57.65%); the training set contained 113 positive events, corresponding to an EPV of 11.3 for the 10 candidate variables and 22.6 for the final 5- variable model.

A nomogram was then constructed from the final multivariable logistic regression model, with prior formal participation as the outcome and age, city, sex, professional title, and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation experience as variables included in the model (Fig. 2). Each variable was assigned a corresponding point value, and the total score was used to estimate the probability of prior formal participation. The model was intended primarily to describe differences in prior formal participation across subgroups rather than to support direct educational decision-making. For example, a nurse aged 30–40 years (35 points), from Yunnan Province (45 points), male (40 points), holding a senior professional title (30 points), and with ≥ 10 years of cardiopulmonary rehabilitation experience (25 points) would have a total score of 175 points, corresponding to an estimated 65.00% probability of prior formal participation.

Nomogram model of influencing factors

ROC analysis showed moderate discrimination in the training set (AUC = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.71–0.84) and acceptable discrimination in the validation set (AUC = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.60–0.83). Although the AUC was lower in the validation set, the decrease was limited, suggesting acceptable internal stability of the model (Fig. 3). Calibration curves were close to the ideal line, and the H-L test indicated acceptable calibration in both the training and validation sets (P = 0.548 and P = 0.665, respectively; Fig. 4). Supplementary DCA is provided in Additional file 9 and was not used as a primary basis for model interpretation. A summary of model performance in both datasets is provided in Table 4. Overall, the primary discrimination and calibration metrics suggested acceptable internal performance across the two datasets when the common cutoff value of 0.55 was applied.

Discussion

This study examined the training status of phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training, nurses’ training needs, and factors associated with prior formal participation in Southwest China. Three main findings emerged. First, despite generally strong institutional emphasis on training, prior formal participation remained limited. Second, training status was still dominated by theoretical teaching, with insufficient practical depth and incomplete alignment with clinical needs. Third, nurses reported training needs and preferred flexible, small-group, and practice-oriented formats. In addition, the exploratory nomogram showed moderate discrimination and acceptable calibration, and may serve as a preliminary descriptive tool for characterizing subgroup differences in prior formal participation. Participants in this study were relatively young and well educated, but many held junior professional titles and had limited rehabilitation-related experience. This workforce profile suggests that current phase II cardiac rehabilitation nursing teams may have a reasonable educational foundation while still lacking the specialty experience needed to support precise and individualized rehabilitation practice. Similar patterns have been reported in other emerging specialist nursing fields, where educational attainment develops more rapidly than clinical depth and role maturity [24]. Future training therefore needs not only to address core competencies in phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription, but also to support progressive professional development across different career stages.

The findings also point to a clear mismatch between training status and actual clinical needs. Although most nurses reported strong organizational attention to training, prior formal participation remained limited. Training status was concentrated mainly at the departmental and hospital levels, whereas opportunities at the municipal or provincial levels were relatively scarce. In addition, training content remained largely theory-oriented, practical components were less common, training duration was often short, and assessment was not implemented consistently. Together, these features suggest that current programs do not yet provide a sufficiently complete pathway from knowledge acquisition to skills development, performance assessment, and feedback. Similar concerns have been reported in previous studies of specialist nurse education in China, which have highlighted uneven access to training resources, limited practical teaching, and the need for more systematic program design [25,26,27]. The gaps identified in this study are also broadly consistent with international literature that positions nursing as a core element of rehabilitation and emphasizes the importance of structured, competency-based education for rehabilitation practice [28].

At the same time, nurses in this study expressed strong and highly practice-oriented training needs. Most respondents supported the establishment of a standardized in-hospital training program and favored delivery at the departmental or hospital level. They also preferred small-group learning, flexible theoretical instruction, and clinically supervised practical training. These preferences suggest that future programs should place greater emphasis on feasibility, workplace integration, and the translation of knowledge into practice. Small-group teaching may be particularly well suited to exercise prescription training because it supports hands-on guidance, interaction, and timely feedback. Likewise, delivering theoretical content online may improve flexibility, whereas clinical instruction and scenario-based simulation may better meet the practical demands of skills acquisition [29,30]. Taken together, these findings support development of a blended, competency-based training model that combines accessible theoretical learning with structured practical training [31].

The regional practice context also help explain these findings. In many hospitals in Southwest China, cardiopulmonary rehabilitation services are still evolving and are often organized in an integrated manner, with nurses working across both cardiac and pulmonary rehabilitation settings. In such contexts, nurses may need to manage a broader range of rehabilitation tasks without the support of highly specialized staffing structures or standardized training pathways. This may increase demand for training that is clinically practical, role-oriented, and adaptable to mixed cardiopulmonary scenarios. It also suggests that training programs designed for settings with fully separated cardiac and pulmonary rehabilitation pathways may not be directly transferable to this regional context. Future program design should therefore take local service organization, staffing patterns, and the actual scope of nursing responsibilities into account. It does not identify causal determinants of participation, nor can it be used to infer which nurses will necessarily benefit most from future training.

Several retained variables may offer useful insights for future workforce planning. Age and professional title were associated with prior formal participation, suggesting that access to or engagement in training may vary across career stages [32] Senior nurses may have had more opportunities, stronger institutional support, or clearer professional expectations related to prior formal participation. Conversely, this pattern may indicate that junior nurses need more structured entry-level support if equitable training participation is to be achieved [33] City-level differences likewise suggest that regional disparities in training resources, institutional implementation capacity, or professional development opportunities may influence participation [34]. The association observed for sex should be interpreted cautiously, but it may reflect differences in role allocation, work arrangements, or access to training that warrant further investigation [33,35]. Professional title level was also associated with prior formal participation. One might speculate that connecting training to career progression could potentially support the leadership development of senior nurses while offering advancement pathways for junior nurses, thereby perhaps facilitating greater engagement [33,35,36]. Cardiopulmonary rehabilitation experience was also retained in the final model, which may indicate that greater specialty exposure is associated with a higher probability of prior formal participation [37]. The nomogram in this study should be interpreted in light of its outcome definition. Because the model takes prior formal participation in phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training as its outcome, rather than competence, proficiency, or actual future educational need, it is not intended to determine which nurses are most in need of training in an absolute sense, nor does it identify causal determinants of prior formal participation. Instead, it is better understood as an exploratory tool for characterizing variation in prior formal participation among nurses working in cardiopulmonary rehabilitation settings. In contexts where standardized indicators of training priority are not yet available, prior formal participation may serve as a pragmatic proxy for previous access to structured training opportunities. From this perspective, subgroups with a lower estimated probability of prior formal participation may reflect populations with comparatively limited historical training access, and the nomogram may therefore provide a preliminary reference for future program planning, particularly in relation to training outreach, participation optimization, and identification of groups that may warrant greater institutional attention.

Taken together, these findings suggest that future training strategies should be more targeted, with attention to regional context, professional seniority, and specialty exposure. Medical institutions should consider establishing standardized, competency-based training programs that better integrate theoretical learning, practical training, assessment, and feedback. A tiered continuing education framework and blended delivery formats may further improve accessibility, flexibility, and practice relevance. In addition, nurses should be more clearly recognized as key implementers of exercise prescriptions within cardiopulmonary rehabilitation services, with stronger role definition, staffing support, and supervision.

Limitations

The use of a non-probability sample from eight hospitals across three provinces, together with the absence of explicit modeling of center effects because of the limited number of hospitals and small sample sizes in some centers, may limit generalizability to other regions and medical settings in China. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow evaluation of training effectiveness, and the use of self-reported data may have introduced recall bias, social desirability bias, and misclassification. Although a DAG was used to guide variable selection, it was based on prior knowledge and clinical judgment and may not have captured all relevant relationships. During model development, a liberal threshold of P < 0.20 was used to retain potentially relevant variables. Although this approach may have allowed inclusion of variables with weaker statistical associations, multicollinearity was assessed using VIF, and model interpretation was based on overall performance and the combined contribution of variables. In addition, the questionnaire was designed for descriptive data collection rather than as a standardized scale, and its broader psychometric properties were not formally assessed. Because the outcome represented prior formal training participation rather than a clinical or directly actionable endpoint, the DCA findings were descriptive only. Finally, the nomogram underwent internal validation only, and the absence of external validation further limits its generalizability and broader applicability. Future studies should include larger and more diverse samples, explicitly address center-level heterogeneity where feasible, perform external validation, and incorporate standardized measurement tools and prospective educational outcomes.

Conclusion

This study found that phase II cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription training among nurses working in cardiopulmonary rehabilitation settings remains insufficient in both participation and practical depth, despite strong demand for further education and broad support for a standardized in-hospital training program. The exploratory nomogram showed moderate discrimination and acceptable internal calibration for estimating prior formal participation, but because it reflects prior formal participation rather than competence, actual training needs, or training effectiveness, it should be viewed only as a preliminary tool for describing subgroup differences in training status. Larger studies with external validation and more meaningful prospective outcomes are needed before broader application.
