Morphological and physiological responses of Pistacia rootstocks to salinity stress and commercial microbial formulation
Authors: İzzet Açar, Kamil Sarpkaya, Islem Abid, Shahid Farooq, Ziya Yıldız
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Salinity is a major constraint to pistachio establishment in arid and semi-arid regions. The use of plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) may enhance the salinity tolerance of different pistachio rootstocks. This study assessed the morphological and physiological responses of seven pistachio rootstocks [Pistacia khinjuk (type A and B), P. atlantica, P. mutica, P. terebinthus, and P. vera (Siirt and Kırmızı cultivars)] to salinity and a commercial microbial formulation (Lifebac S Plus - Chlorobium limcola, C. vibrioforme, Nitrosomonas spp, Nitrobacter spp, Pseudomonas vulgaris with a cumulative concentration of 1 × 106 cfu/ml). Two-year-old seedlings of all rootstocks were subjected to 0, 100, and 200 mM NaCl with or without Lifebac S Plus (hereafter PGPR) inoculation at 15, 30, and 45 days after initiation of salinity treatments. Increasing salinity significantly reduced biomass production, leaf development, relative water content, chlorophyll index, stomatal conductance, and the K⁺/Na⁺ ratio, while increased Na⁺ accumulation. The PGPR caused genotype-dependent responses, which were evident under moderate salinity (100 mM NaCl). Inoculation partially maintained shoot and root growth, tissue hydration, stomatal activity, and ionic balance in P. atlantica, P. mutica, and Siirt rootstocks under moderate salinity. These advantages were limited in non-saline conditions, and PGPR proved unable to mitigate severe stress (200 mM NaCl). Principal component analysis revealed clear differentiation of treatments along a salt-induced gradient characterized by Na⁺ accumulation and biomass reduction, with PGPR-treated plants at moderate salinity exhibiting improved physiological and ionic conditions. Salinity was the primary factor influencing performance, whereas certain PGPR-rootstock combinations improved tolerance mainly under moderate stress. These results highlight the importance of combining salt-tolerant rootstocks (P. atlantica, P. mutica, and Siirt) with appropriate microbial biostimulants to enhance pistachio establishment under saline conditions. The observed benefits mediated by PGPR should be interpreted in the context of the specific commercial microbial formulation used in this study, and broader generalizations to other PGPR products or strains require further confirmation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08568-w.

Introduction

Pistachio (Pistacia vera L.) is a significant nut crop globally, with a remarkable economic value, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions where few alternative crops can thrive [1, 2]. It has become a critical horticultural crop in Türkiye [3], generating significant income [4]. A total of 388,865 tons of pistachios were produced in Türkiye during 2024 [5]. Pistachio cultivation is concentrated in the South-eastern Anatolia (SEA) region of Türkiye [6]. The region is generally arid, and pistachio is treated as a traditional crop in the region [7]. Pistachio cultivation has been dramatically increasing in the SEA region after the development of a large irrigation project named GAP (Southeast Anatolia Project) [8]. Nevertheless, pistachio production in SEA is threatened by climate change. Recent forecasts indicate an increasing drought and salinity in the SEA by 2050, which would significantly reduce pistachio production [9]. Hence, innovative adaptation strategies are needed for sustainable pistachio production in SEA.

The Pistacia genus comprises 11 different species globally; nevertheless, P. vera L. is the only one grown commercially [10]. The other species serve as rootstocks for the scions of P. vera [11]. Apart from serving as rootstocks, these species possess intrinsic ecological significance in preserving agrobiodiversity [12]. Several Pistacia species are native to the Turkish flora, of which P. terebinthus has the widest distribution, followed by P. khinjuk Stock and P. atlantica Desf. Although P. mutica is mostly distributed in Iran, it has limited distribution in restricted areas of Türkiye [13–15]. The abundance of Pistacia germplasm offers genetic resources for rootstock development to enhance tolerance to salinity and drought [12, 16].

Salinity is among the primary abiotic stresses that cause production losses in a wide range of crops [17]. Soil salinity imposes two major physiological constraints on plants, namely osmotic stress and ion-dependent toxicity [18]. Osmotic stress prevents plants from absorbing water from the soil solution, and ion-dependent toxicity disrupts specific physiological processes [19]. Osmotic stress develops immediately after exposure to salinity, which decreases the water potential of the soil solution, thus preventing water uptake [20]. On the other hand, ion-dependent toxicity develops over longer periods due to the accumulation of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in shoot tissues, leading to premature senescence and plant mortality. Excessive accumulation of salts disturbs ion homeostasis by increasing Na⁺ and Cl⁻ concentrations while suppressing uptake of essential nutrients, including K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺ [21]. This imbalance leads to secondary effects, including inhibited photosynthesis, stomatal closure, oxidative stress via the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and reduced nutrient transfer to developing tissues [22].

Although pistachio is regarded as salt-tolerant compared to many other fruit trees [23, 24]; the tolerance level significantly varies among species [25–27]. Various studies have reported significant differences in salinity tolerance among rootstocks [24–26, 28]. Akbari et al. [29] reported that ion homeostasis, osmotic potential regulation, and physiological responses significantly differ among pistachio rootstocks in saline environments. Similarly, Heydari et al. [30] noted that interspecific hybrids of P. integerrima exhibited varying levels of salt tolerance, with some hybrids exhibiting better development relative to commercial rootstocks. Commercial rootstock often exhibits less salt tolerance than wild Pistacia species, which limits orchard growth in marginal saline soils [16, 31]. Recent breeding initiatives in Iran and California have mitigated this limitation by creating interspecific hybrids (e.g., UCB-1 from P. atlantica × P. integerrima; Arota from P. atlantica × P. integerrima) exhibiting enhanced salinity and drought resilience [32]. Despite these advancements, a systematic comparative assessment of several Pistacia rootstocks under regulated salt stress remains scarce in the literature.

Conventional approaches to improving salinity tolerance (e.g., soil amendments, leaching, and chemical treatments) are often expensive and ecologically unsustainable in resource-limited areas [33]. Consequently, biological approaches using microbial inoculants have attracted increased interest [34]. Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) are free-living soil bacteria that inhabit plant roots and promote plant development through various mechanisms, particularly under stressful environmental conditions [35, 36]. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of PGPR in alleviating drought and salinity stress in various crops, including wheat [37], maize [38], cotton [39], and rice [40]. These improvements are attributed to osmolyte biosynthesis, regulation of ion homeostasis, stimulation of antioxidant enzyme activity, production of exopolysaccharides (EPS), and emission of volatile organic compounds (VOC) [41, 42]. Specific PGPR genera consistently demonstrate success in salt-stressed environments, including Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Arthrobacter, Streptomyces, and Klebsiella, which significantly influence biomass accumulation, chlorophyll concentration, and leaf hydration status [43]. Some studies have shown that inoculating rhizobacteria improves the development of salt-stressed pistachio seedlings [44, 45]. Nonetheless, empirical confirmation of PGPR performance at the field level and comprehensive comparisons across pistachio rootstocks remain scarce.

This study investigated the physiological and morphological responses of seven Pistacia rootstocks to salinity and examined the role of PGPR inoculation in mitigating its adverse effects. It was hypothesized that Pistacia species exhibit significant variation in salinity tolerance. It was further hypothesized that commercial PGPR applications would improve salt tolerance by modulating growth performance, ion homeostasis, and physiological traits. The results will help identify rootstocks with greater salinity tolerance, thereby improving and sustaining pistachio productivity in saline environments.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and plant material

This study was conducted in the greenhouse of the Department of Horticulture, Faculty of Agriculture, Harran University, Türkiye (37° 10’ 17’’ N and 39° 00’ 25’’ E, 505 m). Stratified (50 days) seeds of Pistacia species treated with 250 ppm gibberellic acid were planted in 8-liter plastic pots. Each pot was filled with 5 kg of air-dried soil collected from the experimental areas of Harran University, Türkiye, yielding an approximate bulk density of 0.62 g cm⁻³, typical for pot-based greenhouse experiments. The collected soil was analyzed for physical and chemical properties before the initiation of the experiment. Representative samples were taken from the experimental soil prior to sowing for physical and chemical analysis. The samples were air-dried, sieved to pass through a 2 mm mesh, and analyzed. Particle size distribution was determined using the hydrometer technique [46] after dispersion with 40 g L⁻¹ sodium hexametaphosphate. The soil pH was determined in a 1:2.5 soil-to-deionized water suspension using a calibrated glass electrode pH meter (WTW InoLab pH 720, Germany) in accordance with ISO 10390 (2005). The suspension was equilibrated for 30 min with intermittent stirring, then measured at 25 °C.

Electrical conductivity (EC) was measured in a 1:5 soil-to-deionized water extract using a conductivity meter with automated temperature compensation (WTW Cond 720, Germany) [47]. The EC values were normalized to 25 °C and shown in dS m⁻¹. Organic matter was quantified using the Walkley–Black dichromate oxidation technique [48], and results were expressed as percentages. Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) content was determined using a Scheibler calcimeter [49] by measuring the CO₂ released after treatment with 1 N HCl and translating it to a percentage of CaCO₃ equivalent.

The total nitrogen (N) concentration was quantified using high-temperature dry combustion utilizing the LECO TruSpec CN Analyzer (LECO Corp., St. Joseph, MI, USA) [50]. Plant-available phosphorus (P) was extracted by the Olsen technique [51] with 0.5 M NaHCO₃ (pH 8.5) at a soil-to-extractant ratio of 1:20. Suspensions were stirred for 30 min, filtered, and then evaluated calorimetrically at 880 nm using the molybdenum blue–ascorbic acid technique on a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan).

Exchangeable cations were extracted using 1 M ammonium acetate (pH 7.0) at a soil-to-extractant ratio of 1:10 [52]. Exchangeable potassium (K) from the NH₄OAc extract was quantified directly by ICP-OES as above [53]. Exchangeable magnesium (Mg) from the NH₄OAc extract was quantified by ICP-OES, as described for K. The DTPA-extractable iron (Fe), copper (Cu), and manganese (Mn) were determined by following Lindsay and Norvell [54]. Ten grams of soil were extracted using 20 mL of a solution comprising 0.005 M DTPA, 0.01 M CaCl₂, and 0.1 M triethanolamine (pH 7.3) for a duration of 2 h at 25 °C. The suspension was filtered, and micronutrients were measured using ICP-OES. The soil properties are presented in Table 1.

The soil had a clay texture, including 50.14% clay, 30.45% silt, and 19.21% sand (Table 1). The soil exhibited a slightly alkaline pH (7.8) and low electrical conductivity (0.21 dS m⁻¹), indicating non-saline conditions. The organic matter content was low at 1.58%, and calcium carbonate was 8.63%, indicating a highly calcareous soil. The total nitrogen concentration was 0.45%, available potassium was 181.2 mg kg⁻¹, and available phosphorus was low at 4.45 mg kg⁻¹. The magnesium content reached 301.2 mg kg⁻¹, whereas the levels of micronutrients were comparatively low to moderate, including Fe (3.23 mg kg⁻¹), Cu (0.61 mg kg⁻¹), and Mn (0.71 mg kg⁻¹). The soil was characterized as a non-saline, calcareous clay with low organic matter and limited available phosphorus, making it suitable for assessing the effects of salinity and PGPR on plant performance.

Seedlings of the seven Pistacia rootstocks were planted in a non-saline environment during the 1st year for uniform establishment. Baseline measurements of growth and trunk diameter were taken at the end of 1st year. Salinity treatments were imposed in the 2nd year by slowly adding NaCl solutions (0, 100, and 200 mM) to prevent osmotic shock. The PGPR treatments were initiated at 15, 30, and 45 days after salinity treatments. Pistacia species exhibited variation in plant growth and trunk diameter during the 1st year (Table 2). Two-year-old seedlings of 7 different rootstocks belonging to 5 Pistacia species, i.e., P. khinjuk Stocks. [Bigger size seed (P. khinjuk-A) and a smaller seed size (P. khinjuk-B) types], P. atlantica L., P. mutica L., P. terebinthus L., and P. vera L. (Siirt and Kırmızı cultivars) were used as plant materials. The seedlings were obtained from the Pistachio germplasm collection available at Harran University, Sanliurfa, Türkiye. The species were identified by Prof. Dr. Bekir Erol Ak, Department of Horticulture, Harran University, Sanliurfa, Türkiye.

The greenhouse was maintained at day/night temperatures of 31/24°C, an average relative humidity of 75%, and a light/dark cycle of 14/10 h, with a photosynthetic photon flux density of 450–550 µmol m− 2 s− 1. The HOBO MX1101 Temp/RH Data Logger installed in the greenhouse was used to record temperature and relative humidity. The instrument measures temperature every 4 h. The values presented are average.

Treatments

Sodium chloride (NaCl) was used to induce salinity concentrations of 0, 100, and 200 mM. A commercial Plant Growth Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPR) product [Lifebac S Plus™ (Yeditepe Saglik Hizmetleri Co.) containing Chlorobium limcola, C. vibrioforme, Nitrosomonas spp, Nitrobacter spp, Pseudomonas vulgaris with the concentration of 1 × 106 cfu/ml] was purchased from the local market and used in the study. The microbial strains were in a dormant, stable state, a prevalent formulation method for commercial microbial biostimulants and biofertilizers. Bacteria are preserved in a low-metabolic, or inactive, physiological state during storage, achieved through restricted food availability, osmotic stabilization, or dehydration-based preservation methods. The lack of an external energy source in the formulation does not negate bacterial viability. When applied to soil or the rhizosphere, where moisture, nutrients, and root exudates are available, microorganisms become metabolically active and demonstrate their functional benefits. This formulation method is often used for agricultural microbial products, ensuring both shelf stability and biological efficacy upon application. Root colonization was not tested in the current study, which is a limitation; future studies should test actual colonization. The treatments used in the study are summarized in Table 3. The recommended fertilizers for pistachios were used uniformly across all treatments to minimize bias.

Inoculation

Each pot contained a single seedling, and each pot was considered a replicate. Seedlings with two complete leaves were irrigated with NaCl solutions at different concentrations starting in April, using the same water volume (500 ml) twice a week for 90 days. The irrigation volume (500 mL per pot) was determined in preliminary trials before the experiment to identify the minimum water volume needed to fully wet the soil profile and maintain adequate soil moisture for seedling growth, while allowing limited drainage through the pot drainage holes. This volume was subsequently applied uniformly across all treatments to ensure consistent water availability and to isolate the effects of salinity from those of water supply. The control seedlings received normal water without salinity. Similarly, all pots were irrigated with normal water before the initiation of salinity treatments. Lifebac S Plus (PGPR hereafter) was applied three times at 15-day intervals after the initiation of salinity treatments. Soil salinity was monitored using the saturated paste extract method (ECe). Target soil EC values were approximately 6 dS m⁻¹ for the 100 mM treatment and 12 dS m⁻¹ for the 200 mM treatment. Once these EC levels were reached, subsequent irrigations were applied with non-saline water as needed to maintain EC within the intended range and to prevent excessive salt accumulation. The plants were uprooted 90 days after treatment initiation [55]. The plants were divided into shoots and roots to measure morphological and physiological traits [56].

The experiment was conducted using a factorial design, with rootstocks as the main factor, salinity levels as a subfactor, and PGPR inoculation as a sub-subfactor. All treatments had three replications.

Morphological traits

The number of leaves, plant height (cm), trunk diameter (mm), and dry weights of stem and roots (g) were recorded to assess morphological responses to salt stress and PGPR inoculation. The number of leaves per plant was counted and averaged. The heights of the plants in all replications were measured with a meter rod, and the lengths from the base to the tip were averaged. A digital caliper was used to measure trunk diameters. Stems and roots were washed, gently blotted dry on paper towels, and dried for 48 h at 70 °C to determine dry weight.

Physiological traits

Leaf samples were collected from each replication and soaked in distilled water for 24 h to determine the turgid weight (TW). Afterward, they were blotted dry on paper towels and weighed. Here, FW = fresh weight, DW = dry weight, and TW = turgid weight.

Three mature leaves were randomly selected from each treatment to measure chlorophyll index (SPAD values) by using the Chlorophyll Content Meter (Opti-science, CCM-200 Plus, USA). Stomatal conductance (Gs) was recorded by measuring the stomatal conductivity values of the leaves using a leaf porometer (SC-1 Leaf Porometer, Decagon, Pullman, WA). Three fully expanded leaves per plant were measured, with three readings per leaf taken between 9:00 and 10:00 a.m. every 15 days. Measurements were taken on the upper and lower leaf surfaces under ambient greenhouse conditions. Values are expressed as mmol H₂O m⁻² s⁻¹.

Ionic contents

Dried leaf samples were ground to a fine powder after determination of dry weight, and 100 mg subsamples were digested in 2 ml concentrated HNO₃. The K⁺ and Na⁺ were quantified by flame photometry (Jenway PFP7, UK). The Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ were determined by ICP-OES (Perkin Elmer Optima 7000 DV, US). Similarly, K⁺/Na⁺ ratios were calculated for each sample to assess ion homeostasis.

Rootstock performance index

A rootstock performance index (RPI) was computed for each combination of rootstock, salinity, and PGPR using treatment mean values to combine growth and ionic balance responses into a single parameter. Initially, key traits indicative of plant performance under salinity were identified, including shoot dry weight (Shoot DW), K/Na ratio, and Na concentration. where x represents the observed value, µ denotes the general mean, and σ signifies the standard deviation of the characteristic.

The RPI was then calculated by using Eq. 3.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{RPI}=\mathrm{z}(\text{Shoot DW})+\mathrm{z}\mathrm{K/Na}-\mathrm{z}(\mathrm{Na})$$\end{document}

Higher RPI values indicate superior overall performance, with increased shoot biomass, improved ionic balance (higher K/Na), and reduced Na accumulation. Negative RPI values indicate poorer performance under salt stress. RPI was used as a descriptive, integrative index to compare rootstock performance across salinity levels and was not subjected to separate statistical testing.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was designed as a factorial experiment with rootstock (R), salinity (S), and PGPR (inoculation vs. non-inoculation) as fixed factors, encompassing all two-way and three-way interactions (R×S, R×PGPR, S×PGPR, R×S×PGPR). Replication (Rep) was treated as a random effect to account for variance among replicates. A mixed-effects model was applied to each response variable using a complete factorial design (Eq. 4).4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Y}\sim\mathrm{R}\times\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{PGPR}+(1\mid\mathrm{Rep})$$\end{document}

Model terms were assessed using likelihood-ratio χ² tests, comparing layered models while systematically excluding one word at a time from the entire model. The resultant χ² statistics and p-values were documented for R, S, PGPR, and their interactions. When a phrase was significant, the interpretation focused on that term and the most significant interaction.

Given the significant interactions, treatment effects were analyzed using simple-effects comparisons rather than general main-effect assertions. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were calculated for the relevant factor combinations (usually PGPR within each R × S, or R within each S × PGPR) and analyzed using pairwise comparisons. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Tukey correction for each set. Significance was indicated on plots using **ns, *, **, *** (according to p-values).

Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were computed for all traits using the psych package in R to assess relationships among growth, ion homeostasis, and physiological responses. A correlation heatmap was created using the ‘corrplot’ package to illustrate the strength and direction of correlations.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to describe joint variance across traits and to illustrate treatment differences. PCA was conducted on numeric trait matrices after mean-centering and scaling to unit variance to eliminate unit effects across characteristics such as biomass, ions, and physiological variables. All statistical analyses were conducted in R statistical environment [58]. The data has been provided as supplementary material.

The K/Na ratio demonstrated a significant positive correlation with biomass parameters, including DW (r = 0.70), shoot FW (r = 0.70), and root FW (r = 0.56). It exhibited a strong and positive correlation with the stomatal conductance of the lower leaf surface (SC L; r ∼ 0.52) and the upper surface (SC U; r = 0.46). These correlations indicate that rootstocks capable of maintaining a higher K/Na ratio under saline environments also preserve stomatal function and accumulate more biomass. Conversely, Na concentration exhibited a significant and negative correlation with key performance indicators. For instance, it showed a negative correlation with shoot DW, shoot FW, and root FW (-0.78 to -0.62), as well as with K/Na (r = -0.75), SC L (r = -0.57), Mg (r = 0.56), and Ca (r = -0.51). This pattern clearly indicates that Na accumulation adversely affected growth, cation absorption, and stomatal conductance, consistent with the typical mechanisms of salt injury.

All rootstocks had positive RPI values in non-saline environments (0 mM NaCl), indicating effective ionic control that improved shoot biomass in relation to Na accumulation. The PGPR inoculation caused genotype-specific responses. Notable improvements in RPI were observed in Kırmızı, P. khinjuk-A, P. khinjuk-B, and P. terebinthus, indicating improved K/Na homeostasis and growth stimulation in the presence of PGPR. Conversely, P. atlantica, P. mutica, and Siirt exhibited reduced RPI after PGPR inoculation, suggesting that benefits were not generally applicable under optimal conditions. Among non-inoculated plants, Siirt and P. mutica exhibited the highest RPI, whereas inoculated P. khinjuk-A showed the most significant relative enhancement (Fig. 7).

At moderate salinity (100 mM NaCl), RPI values significantly decreased, reaching zero or negative across all rootstocks, indicating a disturbance in ionic balance and reduced shoot function. PGPR inoculation did not effectively mitigate the effects of salinity. Inoculated P. atlantica, P. khinjuk-B, P. mutica, and P. terebinthus exhibited more negative RPI values, suggesting a limited protective potential. Partial stability of RPI was observed in P. khinjuk-A and Siirt, indicating a modest improvement in salt-tolerance mechanisms in some genotypes (Fig. 7).

Under high salinity (200 mM NaCl), RPI values declined significantly across all rootstocks, indicating pronounced Na toxicity and a substantial reduction in shoot dry weight. The effects of PGPR were genotype-specific but typically inadequate to enhance performance. Marginally reduced RPI values in inoculated P. atlantica, P. khinjuk-A, and Siirt indicate a little gain in tolerance, whereas further declines in Kırmızı, P. khinjuk-B, and P. terebinthus show a decrease in PGPR effectiveness under severe salinity conditions (Fig. 7).

Salinity was identified as the principal factor influencing RPI, resulting in a distinct progressive decrease from 0 to 200 mM NaCl across all genotypes. PGPR inoculation enhanced performance only under non-saline conditions and provided only marginal, genotype-dependent alleviation under salt stress, underscoring the importance of rootstock selection in maintaining ionic equilibrium and productivity in saline conditions.

Discussion

Salinity was the dominant driver of variation across all growth, physiological, and ionic traits, while rootstock identity strongly modulated the magnitude of these responses. The significant interactions (R×S, R×PGPR, S×PGPR, and R×S×PGPR) indicate that both salt damage and the response to the commercial microbial formulation were context dependent, so treatment effects should be interpreted within each rootstock × salinity combination. Overall, increasing NaCl from 0 to 200 mM imposed a clear stress gradient characterized by reduced shoot and root biomass, fewer and smaller leaves, lower relative water content (RWC), reduced chlorophyll index, and impaired stomatal conductance (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4), accompanied by marked Na⁺ accumulation and a decline in the K⁺/Na⁺ ratio (Fig. 5). These coordinated changes match the canonical sequence of salinity injury in woody perennials, where osmotic stress restricts growth and gas exchange early, followed by ion toxicity and nutrient imbalance as Na⁺ accumulates in photosynthetically active tissues.

Rootstocks differed substantially in their ability to maintain hydration, gas exchange, and ionic homeostasis under salt stress, highlighting the value of exploiting Pistacia genetic diversity for salt-prone regions. In the present greenhouse conditions, tolerant responses were associated with comparatively higher RWC and chlorophyll index under salinity (Fig. 3), less severe reductions in stomatal conductance (Fig. 4), lower Na⁺ accumulation, and a higher K⁺/Na⁺ ratio (Fig. 5). The tolerance aligns with prior studies indicating extensive genotypic variability in salt tolerance among Pistacia species [26, 29, 59, 60]. High tolerance ability could be linked to several synchronized physiological systems [61–63]. Recent studies have suggested that osmotic adjustment by suitable solute accumulation is a more important characteristic than growth in differentiating salt-tolerant Pistacia clones from salt-sensitive ones [28, 64]. Tolerant rootstocks can hold osmolytes such as proline, soluble sugars, and glycine betaine, which allows them to sustain cellular hydration and protect cellular structures under osmotic stress [65, 66]. Extensive dehydration was recorded for P. vera-Siirt (RWC 30.8% at 200 mM), which indicates poor ability of osmotic adjustment, consistent with previous reports of considerable variability in salt tolerance across commercial pistachio cultivars [64, 65] [29].

Salinity exerted the most significant influence on ion-homeostasis (Na, K, Ca, Mg, and K/Na ratio). The R × S and S × PGPR interactions were significant for most morphological traits. This indicates that ionic homeostasis is modulated by genotype-specific and PGPR-mediated pathways in response to salt stress. The rootstocks’ varied responses indicate their differing abilities to exclude Na+ and compartmentalize tissues. The results indicated notable shifts in ion homeostasis with salinity, including significant increases in Na levels and a concurrent decrease in the K/Na ratio, particularly at elevated salinity. The disturbance of ionic equilibrium is a well-established fundamental mechanism of salt toxicity [67]. Recent studies have suggested that the K/Na ratio is more sensitive to salinity stress than individual ion concentrations and serves as a reliable measure of plant salt tolerance [68, 69]. The inverse relationship between the K/Na ratio and physiological parameters indicates that maintaining optimal ion homeostasis is essential for supporting metabolic activity in saline conditions. Different responses of rootstocks reflect their varying capacities to exclude Na and compartmentalize tissues. Retention of Na in roots and stems serves as a vital tolerance mechanism in woody perennials such as pistachio, reducing the accumulation of toxic ions in photosynthate-active tissues [70, 71]. Zhang et al. [72] demonstrated that salt-tolerant pistachio rootstocks use both vacuolar Na sequestration in root cortical cells and enhanced contraction of apoplastic barriers to inhibit Na transport to the shoots. Elevated Na/Ca and Na/K ratios under salinity stress indicate competitive inhibition of essential nutrient uptake, a secondary effect that worsens salt toxicity. Calcium and potassium are vital for maintaining membrane integrity, enzyme functionality, and osmotic equilibrium. Earlier studies have indicated that Na/Ca ratios exhibit a higher sensitivity than Na/K ratios. This indicates that calcium homeostasis may be severely disturbed under high salinity [73, 74].

Stomatal conductance was reduced across all treatments, particularly at elevated salt levels. Consequently, stomatal closure is a critical initial response to salt stress. The strong positive correlation between the K/Na ratio and both upper leaf surface stomatal conductance (r = 0.46) and lower leaf surface stomatal conductance (r = 0.52) indicates that maintaining an optimal ionic balance is essential for retaining stomatal function under saline conditions. Salt-tolerant genotypes frequently exhibit sensitive stomata that close rapidly during the initial phase of stress, followed by a slight recovery after acclimatization. Conversely, salt-sensitive genotypes often exhibit delayed stomatal closure, resulting in increased Na+ accumulation and greater damage [75]. The observed differences in the current study can be linked to the genetic makeup of the rootstocks. The retention of higher chlorophyll levels in some rootstocks under moderate salinity with PGPR inoculation indicates that certain genotypes may maintain photosynthetic pigment stability despite ionic stress. The reduction in chlorophyll content under higher salinity is attributed to many factors, including the detrimental effects of Na + and Cl- on chloroplast enzymes, oxidative damage from reactive oxygen species, and mineral deficiency [76, 77].

The integrative rootstock performance index (RPI), which combines shoot biomass and ionic balance, indicates that any PGPR benefits under salinity were modest and inconsistent. RPI declined sharply with increasing salinity across all genotypes (Fig. 7), confirming that NaCl severity was the primary constraint on overall performance. Importantly, at 100 mM NaCl, inoculation did not systematically increase RPI and, in some combinations, coincided with more negative RPI values (Fig. 7). At 200 mM NaCl, RPI values were strongly negative across rootstocks and inoculation provided, at best, marginal improvements in a limited subset of genotypes (Fig. 7). These outcomes suggest that the capacity of this commercial formulation to mitigate salinity is constrained once osmotic and ionic stress exceed a critical threshold, and they emphasize that inoculation cannot substitute for inherent rootstock tolerance under severe salinity.

The multivariate PCA further clarified the dominant stress axes and the secondary role of inoculation. The first principal component largely represented the salinity-induced shift from a “high performance” state (higher biomass, longer roots, higher K⁺/Na⁺, and higher Mg²⁺) toward a “stress” state characterized by Na⁺ accumulation and reduced growth (Fig. 8; Table 5). PGPR-treated samples tended to shift toward more favorable positions, primarily under moderate salinity, whereas the separation between inoculated and non-inoculated plants diminished under severe salinity (Fig. 8). This aligns with the univariate results and supports the general conclusion that microbial mitigation is most likely detectable when stress is sufficient to activate beneficial pathways, but not so severe that plant function is irreversibly constrained.

Mechanistically, the observed PGPR-linked improvements in ionic balance and leaf physiology under moderate salinity are consistent with several established bacterial traits, including stimulation of root growth and nutrient acquisition, modulation of stress ethylene via ACC deaminase activity, production of exopolysaccharides that improve rhizosphere water retention, and induction of antioxidant defenses. Nevertheless, the present study evaluated a commercial consortium and did not quantify rhizosphere colonization, microbial persistence, or specific functional traits in planta. Therefore, mechanistic explanations should be considered as plausible interpretations rather than direct evidence. Future works that combine phenotyping with measurements of colonization, root ion fluxes, osmolytes, and antioxidant enzyme activity would be particularly valuable for identifying which processes are most responsive in each rootstock–microbe combination. The use of three replicates per treatment should be considered a limitation of the study. Three replications are common in controlled pot studies; this may limit statistical power to detect higher-order interactions, especially in a three-factor factorial design. Consequently, complex variations in treatments may not have been fully recorded. Subsequent studies using higher replication or multi-environment validation could strengthen the robustness of the observed interaction patterns and verify the consistency of rootstock-specific responses to salt stress.

From an applied perspective, the results support a “rootstock-first” strategy for establishing pistachios in saline environments, with microbial inoculation considered a complementary tool that may yield incremental gains under moderate stress. Because several traits (including Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, leaf area, and stem structural traits) showed limited or non-significant direct effects of PGPRs at the population level (Table 4), selecting tolerant genotypes that inherently maintain hydration, gas exchange, and K⁺/Na⁺ homeostasis remains essential. Field validation is needed, as greenhouse conditions cannot fully represent fluctuating salinity, heterogeneous soils, irrigation regimes and resident microbial communities. Longer-term trials using grafted scion–rootstock combinations, alongside soil and plant ion monitoring, will be crucial to translate these controlled findings into robust recommendations for pistachio production in salt-affected regions.

Conclusion

Salinity stress significantly reduced growth, physiological stability, and overall performance in pistachio rootstocks, indicating that elevated salinity is the primary limitation on early seedling development. Nevertheless, the extent of decline varied among genotypes, suggesting distinct rootstock-dependent tolerance. Pistacia mutica and P. atlantica consistently demonstrated superior growth capacity, ionic balance, and RPI values under saline conditions, making them the most promising candidates for stress-prone environments, whereas more sensitive rootstocks experienced significant declines in RPI, particularly at 200 mM NaCl. PGPR inoculation offered only little and uneven alleviation of salinity damage and failed to counteract severe osmotic and ionic stress, underscoring that inherent genetic resistance is the primary factor influencing performance. The findings indicate that effective pistachio establishment in saline agroecosystems is predominantly reliant on the selection of tolerant rootstocks, particularly P. mutica and P. atlantica, while PGPR may function under moderate stress. Field-scale validation and comprehensive physiological characterization of tolerant genotypes are required to ensure sustainable pistachio production. The beneficial effects of PGPR in this study are limited to the specific commercial microbial formulation used; therefore, the applicability of the results to other strains or products should be approached with caution until further research is conducted.
