Background

The path to becoming a physician is lengthy and demanding, often coinciding with a period of peak reproductive potential. For medical trainees, particularly women, the rigorous structure and demands of graduate medical education contribute to delayed childbearing, higher rates of infertility, and increased obstetrical complications [1]. Prior studies have consistently shown that female physicians, particularly those in surgical specialties, face infertility at rates two to three times higher than the general population. Many subsequently require assisted reproductive technologies to conceive [1, 2]. Despite growing attention to trainee wellbeing, little is known about institutional factors shaping access to fertility benefits for residents and fellows [3–5]. The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) emphasizes institutional responsibility for trainee wellness, yet fertility benefit policies remain unexamined. With women comprising ~ 50% of trainees, equitable reproductive support is essential for retention and career sustainability.

Several states have enacted fertility insurance mandates aimed at improving access to and cost coverage of infertility evaluation and treatment. However, the implementation and impact of these mandates remain variable. Furthermore, residents and fellows may not fall under the same benefit structures as faculty or staff. Employer-sponsored insurance plans, particularly those governed by the ERISA law, are not subject to state mandates, creating a critical loophole through which fertility benefits may be denied despite legislative protections [6]. For resident physicians who often rely on institution-sponsored or ERISA-regulated health plans, this regulatory gap can limit access to timely reproductive care and future planning.

We evaluated fertility benefits for physician trainees across major U.S. medical schools and examined the influence of state insurance mandates. Our analysis reveals that training in a state with a fertility coverage mandate increases the availability of some fertility benefits but that inclusive coverage, particularly for in-vitro fertilization services (IVF), is severely limited. By highlighting systemic gaps in fertility access, this work contributes to the growing literature on equity, physician wellbeing, and structural determinants in medical education.

Methods

To characterize the landscape of fertility benefits for trainees, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis and reviewed publicly available benefit data from GME programs located at the top 50 U.S. medical schools. Although we identified programs using the U.S. News & World Report 2024 data, coverage and health benefits information was collected from affiliated GME-sponsoring institutions, typically the academic medical center or hospital system that employs resident physicians. The study aimed to evaluate the association between state-level fertility insurance mandates and the provision of fertility coverage benefits to physician trainees inclusive of all specialties and subspecialties. Two independent investigators reviewed publicly available information between March and April 2025. Each institution's GME website and affiliated human resources or benefits webpages were examined for explicit mention of fertility-related healthcare benefits. Where available, institutional benefit handbooks or summary plan descriptions were also reviewed.

A chi-square test revealed a statistically significant association between state mandate status and the availability of fertility benefits to trainees (χ2 = 8.12, p = 0.004). Institutions located in states with fertility insurance mandates were significantly more likely to offer fertility benefits than those in non-mandated states (78.1% vs. 33.3%).

What the data reveal

Our findings demonstrate that state insurance mandates significantly shape fertility benefit availability in graduate medical education, with institutions in mandated states more than twice as likely to offer fertility coverage compared to those in non-mandated states (78.1% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.004). While mandates increase access, they do not guarantee it. Furthermore, the scope and extent of fertility care remain lacking. This underscores the limits of state-level policy alone in ensuring equitable reproductive healthcare coverage during medical training. For trainees navigating their peak reproductive years, these inconsistencies represent a critical structural barrier to wellness, reproductive autonomy, and career planning. Perhaps most strikingly, only one program (3.2%) provided explicit coverage for fertility preservation, the very service most beneficial for a population training through its peak reproductive years.

A major structural constraint arises from the Employee Retirement Income Security Act, or ERISA, a federal law that governs many employer-sponsored health insurance plans at academic medical centers and hospital systems [6]. Under ERISA, self-insured employer health plans are exempt from state insurance mandates, including those that require coverage for fertility treatments. This regulatory loophole undercuts the intent of state mandates and permits substantial variability in coverage within mandated states. Mandated states may also reflect broader political and economic contexts that are more supportive of reproductive health benefits, which could partially explain higher coverage frequency even when mandates are not fully applied [8].

We also observed wide variation in scope and clarity of benefits, including requirements for an infertility diagnosis, lifetime dollar caps, and ambiguous descriptions of “fertility services”. These features may delay or limit access, particularly for trainees who wish to pursue oocyte preservation or early evaluation. These data suggest that even when existing mandates are applied, they largely address diagnostic workup and lower-acuity interventions rather than the fertility preservation services most relevant to trainees who may need to defer family building. This represents a fundamental misalignment between policy intent and trainee needs. Our findings build on prior work documenting delayed childbearing, high infertility rates, and adverse pregnancy outcomes among physicians, and on data showing that many trainees fear infertility and strongly support fertility preservation education (1–4). With women now comprising nearly half of surgical residents and the majority of medical school matriculants [9], the need to address fertility-related challenges during medical training is more urgent than ever.

These structural barriers have direct implications for the physician workforce. Delays in family building have been linked to concerns about negative perceptions from colleagues, disruption of training, and limited institutional support [2, 10]. Many physicians ultimately require assisted reproductive technologies (ART) to conceive, yet they still face systemic barriers such as demanding schedules, limited access, and high out-of-pocket costs [1, 5]. The average resident salary was $64,000 in 2021, whereas a single IVF cycle can cost $15,000 to $20,000, and even upwards of $30,000 if a donor egg is needed [11]. For trainees without institutional coverage, the decision to pursue fertility treatment may therefore compete directly with basic financial security.

The lack of robust fertility benefits also intersects with persistent gender disparities in medicine. Despite the rising number of women physicians and surgeons, women remain underrepresented in leadership positions. According to the 2024 data from the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC), women represent 27% of U.S. medical school deans and only 25% of department chairs [12]. Although reasons for this gender gap are likely complex, many report foregoing advancement opportunities due to parenting and caregiving demands [1]. In this context, fertility coverage is not only a wellness intervention but also a workforce equity strategy.

A call to action

Our study highlights the need for both policy reform and institutional accountability to ensure equitable access to fertility-related care in GME. Institutions can commit to this support, even within ERISA constraints. They can do so by committing to comprehensive fertility benefits, curating their own fertility benefits package, or partnering with third-party insurance groups or fertility benefit companies, such as Carrot Fertility or Progyny. GME should publish transparent benefit descriptions online and integrate fertility education into resident orientation and wellness curricula, which would further normalize family-building conversations and help residents understand their options early in training. Improving fertility coverage has the potential not only to enhance trainee health but also to reduce attrition and help support a sustainable, diverse physician workforce.

Conclusion

GME programs must lead fertility benefit reform through comprehensive coverage, policy advocacy, education, and transparent communication. These structural changes are essential for advancing reproductive equity, trainee retention, and workforce development, and to supporting the next generation of physicians in both their professional and reproductive lives.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations. First, it uses a cross-sectional design and cannot establish causality between state mandates and benefit availability. Second, data were derived from publicly available institutional websites and documents, which may not capture the coverage nuances or most current status of fertility benefits. Third, we analyzed benefits at 50 U.S. GME programs located at academic institutions identified by the U.S. News and World Report 2024, which may not represent the greater landscape of nationwide GME programs. Although these programs train a substantial portion of the physician workforce, future studies should broaden the scope beyond top-ranked institutions. It is also worth noting that institutional funding levels may vary across GME-sponsoring institutions. Larger academic medical centers with greater financial resources may be better positioned to offer comprehensive fertility benefits regardless of state mandate status, which may limit generalizability of these findings to community-based or smaller residency programs.
