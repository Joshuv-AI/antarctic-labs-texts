Arthroscopically Assisted Anatomic Repair of a Medial Gastrocnemius Proximal Tendon Avulsion
Authors: Benjiman J. Wilebski, Luke V. Tollefson, Dustin R. Lee, Matthew T. Rasmussen, Robert F. LaPrade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The medial gastrocnemius tendon's (MGT) primary role is to perform plantarflexion of the foot. It attaches to the gastrocnemius tubercle, one of the primary medial femoral osseous landmarks. Injury to the musculotendinous junction of the MGT often is referred to in the literature as “tennis leg” syndrome because of the classic acute onset of pain while participating in such sports. Tendon avulsions of the medial gastrocnemius occur less frequently, and documentation is limited as injuries typically occur at the musculotendinous junction. Conservative treatment typically is recommended for MGT avulsion injuries unless patient function continues to be limited, and then surgical intervention is considered. This technique describes an anatomic repair of a proximal avulsion tear of the medial gastrocnemius tendon in the setting of a chronic injury.

The medial gastrocnemius tendon (MGT) functions as one of the primary plantar flexors of the foot. The tendon attaches slightly proximal and posterior to the gastrocnemius tubercle, which is referenced as one of the main medial bony prominences of the knee.1 Tears of the MGT often occur at the musculotendinous junction and are referred to as “tennis leg” syndrome, whereas avulsion fractures are less frequent.2 The mechanism of injury has been described in case reports to occur primarily with forced foot dorsiflexion during knee extension in acute settings, whereas chronic presentations have limited documentation.3, 4, 5 Medial gastrocnemius proximal tendon avulsions typically are managed conservatively, and surgical intervention is generally recommended if strength and function are not regained.6,7 Limited repair techniques including screw or suture fixation are reported in the literature.2,5 This Technical Note details an anatomic repair of a MGT proximal avulsion in the context of a chronic injury.

Patient Evaluation

Patient evaluation begins with a thorough clinical examination to check for tenderness to palpation of the MGT and gastrocnemius tubercle, knee range-of-motion differences, strength deficits, and stability of the knee. Radiographic imaging should include lower extremity long-standing views to assess alignment bilaterally and magnetic resonance imaging to assess for any MGT or possible meniscal pathology (Fig 1).Fig 1Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of a sagittal view of the left knee. This MRI scan demonstrates a chronic proximal medial gastrocnemius tendon avulsion with thickening of the tendon (blue arrow).

Anesthesia and Positioning

The patient is positioned in the supine position on the operating table and induced under general anesthesia. A knee examination is performed to validate the clinical examination findings. A well-padded high thigh tourniquet is placed on the surgical leg. The surgical leg is placed in a leg holder (Mizho OSI, Union City, CA) and the nonsurgical leg into an abduction stirrup (Birkova Product LLC, Gothenburg, NE). For infection prophylaxis, 2 g of perioperative cefazolin is administered if no allergy is documented.

Dissection continues to expose the posterior oblique ligament and carried further to dissect and identify the semimembranosus tendon (Fig 3). Tendon Preparation

To fixate the avulsed tendon back to bone, 2 double-loaded suture anchors are placed at the anatomic MGT attachment (Fig 6). Before whipstitching the MGT, the thickened, scarred tendon should be removed to provide adequate tendon tissue quality for future fixation. One limb of each stitch is then whipstitched down and up the tendon, avoiding the capsule, while the other limb is used as the post (Fig 7). To evaluate whether the whipstitched tendon will reach the anatomic footprint, the sutures are tensioned with foot dorsiflexion and cycling of knee flexion and extension. This assesses whether the MGT can be fixated at the anatomic footprint and if the knee should be positioned in extension postoperatively to prevent overtightening of the repaired MGT and suture pullout.Fig 6Medial gastrocnemius tendon (MGT) femoral suture anchor placement in a MGT anatomic repair for the left knee with the patient in the supine position. Arthroscopy

Arthroscopy is initiated with the creation of medial and lateral portals. The 30° camera is inserted and visualization of the compartments presented with normal articular cartilage. Arthroscopy is performed to confirm there is no intra-articular pathology and address any possible intra-articular pathology that is present.

Next, the camera is inserted through the femoral notch via a transnotch view to visualize the posteromedial aspect of the knee. The MGT appears taut and is reduced to the anatomic footprint while pulling tension on the previously applied sutures (Fig 8). Fluid extravasation is also eliminated from this technique as any posteromedial capsule violations are eliminated as the suture tension secures the MGT to the capsule before fixation. The fluid within the knee joint is then evacuated.Fig 8Arthroscopic evaluation from the anterolateral portal during an anatomic medial gastrocnemius tendon (MGT) repair for the left knee with the patient in the supine position. Fixation

With the knee flexed at 20°, the sutures are then tied for a solid fixation and repair (Fig 9). While extending the knee, the MGT repair tightens 10° short of full extension. Postoperative Rehabilitation

Patients are non−weight-bearing on the surgical limb for 6 weeks. Knee extension is limited to 20° short of full knee extension for the first 2 weeks with slow increases in knee extension as the patient can tolerate. It is critical that the patient is compliant with their postoperative range of motion exercises to prevent the risk of loss of knee extension or arthrofibrosis. At 6 weeks, patients may slowly progress to activities such as stationary bikes and ankle stretching.

Discussion

In cases in which MGT tears do not respond to conservative treatment and functional outcomes are less than optimal, repair of the MGT should be considered in active patients that cannot return to sport or activity. MGT avulsions are a relatively rare occurrence described to occur with significant forces of dorsiflexion with knee extension.5,8,9 A reported surgical technique describes the fixation of a proximal MGT avulsion with running lock stitches and suture anchors to fixate the remnant to the tendon, although the chronicity of the injury was not reported.2 Although there are no reported outcome studies currently published on surgical fixation of proximal MGT avulsion, multiple case reports have described excellent functional outcomes.2,5,7 In their study, Prakash et al.10 evaluated retrospectively 100 consecutive cases of calf injuries. They demonstrated that greater grades of connective tissue injury (intramuscular tendon, aponeurosis, epimysium) required longer times to return to play and also proposed an associated magnetic resonance imaging grading system for severity of injury and return to play.10 There are limited case reports describing MGT avulsions that require surgical intervention.
