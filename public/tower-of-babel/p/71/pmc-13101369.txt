Giant renal hilar angiomyolipoma with Mayo level II venous tumor thrombus: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: WeiZhou Pan, YinYin Li, ChengLong Fan, PengNan Hu, Hua Mi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present the case of a 54-year-old woman with an incidentally discovered left renal hilar mass. Imaging examination revealed a large, heterogeneous mass of fat density in the left renal pelvis, accompanied by a tumor thrombus extending into the renal vein and inferior vena cava (IVC), consistent with a Mayo level II thrombus—a finding reported in fewer than 50 cases worldwide. A preoperative diagnosis of renal angiomyolipoma (RAML) was established. Despite the patient’s strong preference for nephron-sparing surgery (NSS), intraoperative findings of extensive hilar invasion and calyceal involvement, along with significant hemorrhage, necessitated conversion to radical left nephrectomy with IVC thrombectomy. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of classic RAML, as indicated by positive immunohistochemical staining for Human Melanoma Black 45 (HMB45), Melanoma Antigen Recognized by T-cells 1 (Melan-A), and Smooth Muscle Actin (SMA), and negative staining for Cytokeratins (CK), S-100 Protein (S-100), and Desmin. The tumor thrombus was derived from the RAML. The patient recovered well postoperatively, with no recurrence or metastasis observed at the 8-month follow-up. This case underscores the surgical challenges of large hilar RAMLs with venous extension and highlights the importance of preoperative planning and intraoperative adaptability.Keywords: Renal angiomyolipoma, Venous tumor thrombus, Mayo classification, Nephrectomy, Case report

Introduction

Renal angiomyolipoma (RAML) is a benign mesenchymal tumor characterized by varying proportions of blood vessels, smooth muscle, and adipose tissue. It is the most common benign renal neoplasm, with an estimated incidence of 0.1% to 0.3% [1]. A rare but serious complication of RAML is its extension into the renal vein or inferior vena cava (IVC), which can result in the formation of a tumor thrombus. A literature review identified fewer than 50 documented cases of RAML with IVC tumor thrombus from 1982 to 2013 [2]. The presence of a venous thrombus arising from a benign tumor poses significant therapeutic challenges, including the risk of pulmonary embolism, increased surgical complexity, and the need to balance nephron preservation with oncological safety. This article presents the diagnosis and management of an asymptomatic, giant left-sided hilar RAML with a Mayo level II IVC tumor thrombus. Additionally, we review the relevant literature since 2020 to discuss perioperative strategies and the challenges associated with this complex condition.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old woman was referred to our urology department due to a left renal hilar mass that was incidentally discovered during a health examination conducted four months earlier. She was asymptomatic, with no reported flank pain, hematuria, or fever. The physical examination yielded unremarkable findings, and there was no family history of tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) with three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction revealed a heterogeneous left renal hilar mass measuring 7.6 × 5.4 × 6.4 cm, predominantly composed of fat (Hounsfield units ≈ -80) and containing tortuous vascular structures. Linear fat-density lesions were observed within the left renal vein and IVC (Fig. 1A-D). Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of a well-circumscribed hilar mass, which appeared hyperintense on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), isointense on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), and exhibited signal dropout on fat-suppressed sequences. Non-enhancing abnormal signal foci were identified within the IVC and left renal vein (Fig. 1E-F). The preoperative diagnosis was left RAML with a Mayo level II IVC tumor thrombus.

The patient exhibited a strong preference for nephron-sparing surgery (NSS). The patient was in good general preoperative condition with no underlying comorbidities. Preoperative evaluations indicated the presence of a benign tumor, laboratory tests revealed normal renal function and coagulation parameters, with a hemoglobin level of 134 g/L, and no imaging evidence of bleeding. Technetium-99 m diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA) renal dynamic imaging was performed to evaluate the functional status of both kidneys. The R.E.N.A.L. Nephrometry Score (R.E.N.A.L.-NS) affirmed the feasibility of NSS. The planned procedure involved IVC thrombectomy combined with a left partial nephrectomy. During the operation, the IVC was carefully dissected and controlled. A large, well-circumscribed, soft, yellow mass measuring approximately 5.0 × 6.0 × 9.0 cm was identified at the renal hilum. Intraoperative ultrasound confirmed the extension of the thrombus below the first hepatic porta. Vascular control was achieved using a sequential three-stage clamping technique: first, the distal IVC was secured with atraumatic vascular tape; second, the right renal vein was clamped; and finally, the proximal IVC was controlled infrahepatically. The IVC was incised adjacent to the renal vein orifice, allowing for the complete extraction of the tumor thrombus. The venotomy was closed with a running 4 − 0 Prolene suture after confirming the absence of residual thrombus and irrigating with heparinized saline. Clamps were released sequentially. Intraoperatively, the clamping of the main renal artery was considered; however, due to the extensive involvement of the renal hilum by the tumor, the main renal artery was closely adhered to the tumor mass, which made safe clamping difficult without risking damage to the vessel wall. Therefore, segmental artery clamping was attempted first. During the attempted partial nephrectomy, it was discovered that the tumor extensively involved the hilum and collecting system, leading to uncontrollable hemorrhage (estimated blood loss: 3,400 mL) due to injury to a segmental artery. Consequently, the procedure was converted to a radical left nephrectomy. The total operative time was 208 min. The patient received 11.5 units of packed red blood cells and 700 mL of autologous blood transfusion. Postoperatively, the patient was returned to the ward in stable condition. Hemoglobin levels gradually declined to a nadir of 76 g/L on Postoperative Day (POD) 8, without any clinical signs of active bleeding. CT imaging revealed minor postoperative fluid collections (Fig. 2A), attributed to chronic consumptive processes and hemodilution. Drains were removed on POD 6 (renal fossa) and POD 10 (peri-IVC). The patient was discharged on POD 11 with a hemoglobin level of 87 g/L.

Gross examination revealed a gray-yellow, soft mass measuring 8.5 × 5.5 × 4.9 cm located in the renal hilum, accompanied by an elongated tumor thrombus (Fig. 3A). Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of classic RAML (Fig. 3B), demonstrating positive immunohistochemical staining for HMB45, Melan-A, SMA (Fig. 3C-D), while exhibiting negative staining for CK, S-100, and Desmin. The Ki-67 Antigen (Ki-67) proliferation index was approximately 1% in hotspot areas (Fig. 3F), and surgical margins were confirmed to be tumor-free. Histopathological examination of the tumor thrombus confirmed that it was composed of the same components as the primary tumor, consistent with RAML thrombus without malignant transformation. Imaging conducted at the 8-month follow-up revealed no signs of recurrence or metastasis (Fig. 2B-D).

Discussion

RAML is a common renal mesenchymal tumor composed of blood vessels, smooth muscle, and adipose tissue. It occurs sporadically (80%) or in association with TSC (20%). TSC-associated RAMLs are frequently bilateral and multifocal [3]. Typically, RAMLs, primarily found in middle-aged women, are benign and asymptomatic. However, larger tumors (> 4 cm) may present with flank pain, a palpable abdominal mass, or gross hematuria. The most serious complication associated with RAMLs is spontaneous tumor rupture and hemorrhage [4, 5]. Although generally indolent, RAMLs can exhibit aggressive local growth, including venous invasion, which necessitates careful management [6]. This paradox may be attributed to the absence of anatomical barriers surrounding the renal hilum and the tumor’s high vascularity [7, 8]. The low Ki-67 index (1%) observed in this case implies an indolent biological nature, suggesting that the aggressive local growth may be influenced more by anatomical factors than by malignant transformation. This observation exemplifies the paradox between the pathological benignity and the clinical aggressiveness of RAML. Notably, several studies have reported a higher incidence of venous tumor thrombus in right-sided RAMLs, likely due to anatomical advantages such as a shorter and more direct renal vein [1, 2, 8]. The 2022 WHO classification distinguishes classic RAML from the potentially malignant epithelioid subtype (EAML) [9]. Our case, confirmed as classic sporadic RAML, is particularly notable for its large left-sided origin, absence of symptoms, and extensive Mayo level II thrombus, underscoring that biological factors beyond tumor size alone may influence thrombus formation.

Preoperative imaging is crucial in surgical planning. Conventional imaging modalities, including ultrasonography, CT, and MRI, each have unique advantages. While CT is particularly effective in identifying fat components, MRI offers superior soft-tissue characterization and thrombus delineation [10]. The use of three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction, as demonstrated in this case, provides invaluable anatomical guidance for complex surgical procedures [11], which is a practice we strongly advocate. The preoperative imaging findings in this case were highly indicative of RAML, though differential diagnoses such as renal cell carcinoma with tumor thrombus, liposarcoma, and solitary fibrous tumor were also considered. The definitive diagnosis of RAML is established through the immunohistochemical gold standard, which necessitates the co-expression of melanocytic markers (HMB45, Melan-A) and myogenic markers (SMA), alongside the absence of epithelial differentiation (CK) [12]. Immunohistochemical positivity is characterized by diffuse or focal staining of the cytoplasm or cell membrane in tumor cells, with a positive cell rate exceeding 1%. In this case, the postoperative immunohistochemistry met these criteria, thereby providing conclusive evidence for classic RAML. In addition, schwannoma is a potential radiological mimic of RAML. Renal schwannoma, a rare benign Schwann cell neoplasm, manifests as a heterogeneous renal hilar mass on imaging but lacks fat components on CT and MRI. Immunohistochemical staining is positive for S-100 and negative for HMB45 and Melan-A, which is different from the positive HMB45, Melan-A and negative S-100 in our case [13].

Since the initial report of RAML with IVC thrombus by Kutcher et al. [14], approximately 13 additional cases documented in English have been published on PubMed between 2020 and 2025. As summarized in Table 1, the mean age of these patients was 45.61 years (range: 14–72), with a significant female predominance (12 women, 92.31%; 1 man, 7.69%). Tumors were more frequently located on the right side (8 cases, 61.54%) compared to the left (4 cases, 30.77%), with one case presenting bilaterally (7.69%). Most patients (9 cases, 69.23%) were symptomatic at the time of diagnosis, while only 2 patients (15.38%) had tuberous sclerosis complex. The majority of patients underwent nephrectomy and thrombectomy, resulting in generally favorable outcomes. Our case aligns with the literature’s observation of female predominance and complements the clinical spectrum of this rare condition by presenting a left-sided tumor, thereby contributing to the limited data on left-sided RAML with venous thrombus. Similar to most reported cases, our patient underwent radical nephrectomy and thrombectomy, resulting in a favorable short-term outcome. This consistency in treatment approach and outcomes further validates the clinical feasibility of surgical resection for such complex cases.

The management of RAML has expanded beyond observation and now includes selective arterial embolization (SAE), nephron-sparing surgery (NSS), radical nephrectomy, and medical therapy with mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors. The management of this complex case involved two critical decisions. First, surgical excision with thrombectomy was preferred over angioembolization due to the presence of a Mayo Level II tumor thrombus. Although embolization addresses arterial hemorrhage, it does not eliminate the friable thrombus itself, which presents an ongoing risk of catastrophic pulmonary embolism [15]. Prophylactic IVC filter placement was considered but ultimately deemed unnecessary by our multidisciplinary team. This decision was informed by the stable, fat-rich composition of the thrombus and its infrahepatic (Mayo II) level, indicating a lower embologenic potential. Furthermore, definitive surgical removal would eliminate the source of the embolism, thereby avoiding the risks associated with filter placement. Additionally, while mTOR inhibitors such as sirolimus have been reported to reduce TSC-related RAML size by 38–95% and facilitate partial nephrectomy [16], their efficacy in giant RAMLs with high-level thrombus remains undefined. Second, the intraoperative conversion from planned partial nephrectomy to radical nephrectomy was a necessary decision dictated by patient safety. Preoperatively, the R.E.N.A.L. -NS was applied to objectively assess the anatomical complexity of the tumor and the feasibility of NSS. Renal mass abuts main renal vasculature and, therefore, is designated as hilar with suffix “h”. The R.E.N.A.L. -NS = 3 + 1 + 2 + a+3 h=9ah, classifying the lesion as of intermediate complexity [17]. Considering this classification, the patient’s strong preference for renal preservation, and the preoperative diagnosis of a benign RAML, NSS was initially planned. However, intraoperative exploration revealed extensive tumor invasion of the renal hilum, obliterating regular anatomical planes. During dissection, injury to a segmental artery encased and distorted by the tumor resulted in life-threatening hemorrhage (approximately 3,400 mL). Continuing with NSS under these circumstances would have unjustifiably increased the risk of mortality. The conversion to radical nephrectomy was essential to achieve hemodynamic stability and save the patient’s life, a decision supported by literature indicating high technical and complication risks associated with NSS in hilar tumors greater than 4 cm [18].

Long-term surveillance is warranted due to the tumor’s aggressive features despite its benign pathology. The low Ki-67 index (1%) suggests an indolent biology [19]. However, considering the tumor size and the presence of a venous thrombus, our follow-up period was relatively short and was insufficient to assess long-term outcomes. Based on the 2024 ESMO guidelines [10], we have formulated a long-term follow-up plan for the patient: abdominal imaging and renal function tests every 6 months for the first 2 years, and annual evaluation thereafter to monitor contralateral kidney function and potential recurrence. Key monitoring points include contralateral kidney morphology and function, potential RAML recurrence, and late complications of venous thrombectomy. Modern targeted therapy options also guide our follow-up strategy. As mTOR activation is a key driver of angiomyolipoma, its inhibitors, such as everolimus, can achieve significant tumor reduction [20]. Therefore, everolimus represents a viable non-surgical option should new lesions emerge in the future, reflecting a personalized, mechanism-based approach to long-term management. Based on the treatment experience from this case and a review of relevant literatures, we propose the following perioperative management recommendations: 1) conduct thorough thrombus imaging and 3D modeling if feasible to clarify tumor-vessel adhesion; 2) adopt preoperative selective renal artery embolization for hypervascular hilar tumors to reduce intraoperative bleeding risk; 3) prepare comprehensive vascular control measures for tumors closely adhering to the main renal vessels; 4) maintain readiness to convert to radical nephrectomy in cases of uncontrolled bleeding or invasion; 5)minimize manipulation of veins during thrombectomy to avoid vascular injury; and 6) implement long-term surveillance due to recurrence risks.

Conclusion

Giant hilar RAMLs with venous tumor thrombus present significant surgical challenges. A meticulous preoperative evaluation, which includes advanced imaging and assessment of thrombus levels, is essential. While nephron-sparing surgery should be considered when feasible, surgeons must be prepared to convert to radical nephrectomy in cases of uncontrolled hemorrhage or critical involvement of surrounding structures. A multidisciplinary approach, individualized surgical planning, and long-term follow-up are crucial for achieving successful outcomes in these complex cases.
