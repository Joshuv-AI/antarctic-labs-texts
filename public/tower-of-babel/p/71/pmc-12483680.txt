Identification of priority practice areas for evidence-based care in congenital heart Disease: An exploratory Survey of non-medical healthcare professionals affiliated with the Italian Society of Pediatric Cardiology
Authors: Arianna Magon, Serena Francesca Flocco, Samantha Scolari, Gianluca Conte, Cristina Arrigoni, Greta Ghizzardi, Giovanna Campioni, Raluca-Bianca Nedesca, Enrico Gambirasi, Floriana Di Iorio, Rossella Orlando, Mariassunta Castaldi, Chiara Gatti, Paola Claudia Fazio, Mariella Rubin, Cinzia Pirti, Stefania Baratta, Assunta Carandente, Salvatore Angileri, Rosario Caruso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introductions Identifying priority areas of nursing practice for individuals with congenital heart disease (CHD) is essential to address evolving care needs and support professional development. However, standardized criteria to guide such prioritization remain limited.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted between April and September 2024 using an online survey distributed to all nurse members of the Italian Society of Pediatric Cardiology and Congenital Heart Diseases (SICPED). Sixteen CHD-related practice areas and five prioritization criteria were defined through literature review and expert consultation. Participants rated each area on a 5-point Likert scale according to the five criteria.ResultsA total of 78 nurses completed the survey. Among the criteria, the absence of clinical guidelines (mean = 4.04 ± 0.98) and the required level of professional competence (mean = 3.95 ± 1.24) received the highest scores. Mixed ANOVA revealed significant variability in scores across practice areas and criteria (F(48) = 2.385, p < .001). Four areas emerged as priority domains across multiple criteria: psychosocial and emotional support for individuals with CHD and their families, management of neonatal opioid withdrawal, palliative and end-of-life care, and the transition of adolescents with CHD to adult services.ConclusionsThis study provides a structured foundation for identifying nursing practice priorities in the care of individuals with CHD. These findings may guide the development of interdisciplinary strategies, clinical guidelines, and professional training initiatives within communities of practice.

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) comprises a heterogeneous group of structural malformations affecting the heart and/or great vessels, present from birth [1]. Globally, CHD affects approximately 9 per 1000 live births [1], with a slightly lower prevalence of 7 per 1000 in Italy [2]. Advances in diagnostic and interventional strategies have significantly improved survival, with over 90 % of individuals with CHD now reaching adulthood [3,4]. By 2030, it is estimated that 11 % of this population will be over 60 years of age [3]. This demographic shift has led to increasingly complex care needs and demands new diagnostic, surgical, interventional, and supportive strategies that challenge the sustainability of health and social care systems [5].

The recognition of CHD as a chronic, lifelong condition has prompted growing attention to underexplored practice areas in both pediatric and adult care [5,6]. Among these, mental health has emerged as a critical concern due to the high prevalence of psychosocial disorders, such as anxiety and depression, affecting both individuals with CHD and their families. These conditions are frequently underdiagnosed or diagnosed late [5,7,8]. Access to psychoeducational interventions and mental health services also remains limited [5]. Similarly, the prevalence and management of comorbidities, such as heart failure, diabetes, and hypertension, and their impact on health outcomes represent a key area for the prevention and management of multisystemic conditions [6,9]. Accordingly, identifying and exploring priority practice areas has become essential to meeting the evolving needs of the population with CHD and guiding both clinical practice and professional development.

In Italy, the establishment of scientific and technical-professional societies for healthcare professions (Law No. 24/2017) has emphasized the importance of adherence to clinical guidelines (CGs) as a core component of professional responsibility. These guidelines, developed by the societies themselves, serve as essential tools for clinical governance and evidence-based care. However, the development of CGs or best-practice recommendations requires a preliminary assessment of context-specific priorities. To support this process, the Italian National Institute of 10.13039/100018696Health (Istituto Superiore di Sanità, 10.13039/501100004008ISS) has introduced prioritization criteria for identifying areas most in need of guideline development [10]. These criteria include inequities in access, unwarranted variation in care, and the overall burden of the health problem. Despite these frameworks, no structured evaluation of CHD practice areas has yet been conducted using such criteria, which poses a significant barrier to producing and implementing CHD-specific guidelines tailored to the Italian context.

Practice areas are specialized domains of care that require an integrated, multiprofessional, and multidisciplinary approach [11]. The complexity of chronic conditions, including CHD, often necessitates coordinated health and social services involving a wide range of professional roles [12]. Recent evidence supports the value of integrated and transdisciplinary care models in cardiovascular medicine, demonstrating benefits such as improved personalization and continuity of care, reduced hospital admissions, and greater system efficiency [12].

Within the Italian healthcare landscape, the Italian Society of Pediatric Cardiology and Congenital Heart Diseases (SICPED) is the only scientific society specifically dedicated to the clinical and professional advancement of CHD care. Among its organizational structures, the affiliate group comprises non-medical healthcare professionals (i.e., nursing and allied health professions) who actively contribute to scientific initiatives, professional development, and the implementation of evidence-informed, integrated care aligned with SICPED's mission. This group functions as a community of practice, offering a distinct perspective within SICPED's broader multiprofessional dialogue. At the European level, the role of non-medical professionals in cardiovascular care is similarly recognized. The Association of Cardiovascular Nursing and Allied Professions (ACNAP), a branch of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), represents nurses and allied health professionals from 62 countries and 80 national societies worldwide [13].

In light of these developments, non-medical healthcare professionals play a critical role across the continuum of cardiovascular care, including prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation [13]. Their involvement spans from pediatric to adult care and touches nearly every aspect of service delivery for people with CHD and their families [14]. Despite their central role, no prior effort has been made to systematically map or prioritize CHD-related practice areas from the perspective of nursing and allied health professionals within SICPED. Generating such a mapping is pivotal for guiding national strategies for professional development and evidence-based practice, as well as contributing to broader international discussions on optimizing CHD care models. Accordingly, this study aimed to identify and prioritize CHD-related practice areas from the perspective of nursing professionals affiliated with SICPED, who represent 92.5 % of the society's non-medical membership.

Methods

Design

A cross-sectional observational design with convenience sampling was adopted. Data were collected between April and September 2024 using an ad hoc questionnaire administered to all affiliate members of the SICPED.

Questionnaire development and definition of practice areas

To define the relevant practice areas, understood as specialized domains of care related to CHD, we adopted a multi-step approach.

First, we conducted a targeted narrative review of international and European clinical guidelines, consensus statements, and scientific publications published between 2018 and 2024, including recommendations from the ESC Working Group on Adult Congenital Heart Disease (ACHD) [15] and the American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) [16]. Second, the research team carried out a thematic analysis of this material to identify recurrent domains of practice. Third, the resulting list of candidate practice areas was reviewed and refined by the research team and two external experts to ensure clinical relevance and contextual applicability. This process led to the identification of 16 distinct CHD-related practice areas, presented in Table 1 along with their primary bibliographic sources and derivation. To account for potential gaps, an open-ended “Other practice area” option was included at the end of the questionnaire. In addition, the five prioritization criteria were adapted from the framework proposed by the Italian National Institute of Health (ISS) for the identification of guideline development priorities [10] and tailored to the context of CHD nursing care. Finally, the questionnaire was subjected to an informal assessment of face validity through an open-ended evaluation by a group of 20 senior, experienced SICPED members, whose feedback confirmed the clarity, comprehensiveness, and relevance of the items. Prioritization criteria and evaluation of practice areas

The evaluation of practice areas was based on five risk-related criteria adapted from the framework of the Italian National Institute of Health (ISS) for guideline development prioritization [10] and tailored to the context of CHD nursing care. The prioritization criteria assessed in this study included: (C1) lack of national guidelines or recommendations; (C2) quality of institutional protocols and procedures; (C3) unjustified variability in clinical practice; (C4) disparities in care processes and health outcomes; and (C5) level of specialized or advanced professional competence.

Each of the 16 practice areas was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Not important”) to 5 (“Essential/Very important”). Higher scores reflected greater criticality, meaning that the area was perceived as insufficiently supported by guidelines or protocols, subject to heterogeneous care approaches, associated with inequities in access or outcomes, or dependent on advanced professional expertise. In this study, priority practice areas were therefore defined as those that received consistently higher mean scores across one or more criteria, indicating the presence of significant unmet needs or system-level vulnerabilities.

Data collection

Data were collected through an online survey (e-survey) developed and administered using the SurveyMonkey platform, which also served as the data repository. A unique web link to the e-survey was distributed via the mailing list of SICPED-affiliated members. The initial invitation was sent on May 22, 2024, followed by a reminder on June 11, 2024. The e-survey consisted of three sections: an introductory section that presented the purpose of the study and requested informed consent; a second section that gathered sociodemographic and professional data; and a third section focused on evaluating the identified practice areas. To ensure proper response recording, participants were required to confirm submission upon completing the questionnaire. The full version of the e-survey is available in Supplementary File 1. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were provided. Although anonymity was guaranteed, each access to the link was associated with an automatically generated IP address to ensure only one response per device. The study followed the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) to ensure high-quality methodological reporting; see Supplementary File 2 [29]. In total, the invitation was distributed to 97 nurses affiliated with SICPED, of whom 78 completed the survey, corresponding to a response rate of 80 %.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the SICPED Affiliate Members’ Coordinating Council on March 22, 2024, and subsequently endorsed by the SICPED Scientific Committee. Data collection and management were conducted in accordance with Italian privacy legislation (Law No. 101/2018), ensuring participant anonymity and use of data strictly for research purposes. Additionally, the data collection platform (SurveyMonkey) complies with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR 679/2016).

Statistical analysis

For descriptive statistics, means and standard deviations (±SD) were calculated for continuous variables, while absolute frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. Differences in mean scores across prioritization criteria were assessed using between-subjects ANOVA. To compare mean scores for each practice area across the five evaluation criteria, a within-subjects ANOVA was conducted. Additionally, a mixed ANOVA was used to evaluate whether variations in mean practice area scores were associated with prioritization criteria. In this model, the within-subjects factor was represented by the five prioritization criteria (C1–C5), each of which was rated for all practice areas, while the between-subjects factor corresponded to the practice areas. This approach allowed us to test whether mean scores differed across criteria, across practice areas, and whether the effect of criteria varied by practice area (interaction effect). When overall ANOVA tests were statistically significant, Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons were performed to identify specific differences between levels. The analysis required two dataset structures: a long format for the between-subjects analysis and a wide format for the within-subjects analysis.

A significance level of 0.05 was adopted for all tests. Only e-survey responses with more than 40 % completion were included in the analysis. This threshold was selected to ensure that partially completed questionnaires contributed sufficient information while excluding highly incomplete responses that could distort results. Pairwise deletion was applied to handle remaining missing data, as this approach allowed for maximal retention of available responses across analyses [30]. Although pragmatic and consistent with common practices in survey-based research [31], we acknowledge that this strategy may lead to variable sample sizes across tests and may affect internal consistency and generalisability if data were not missing completely at random. Missing data were handled using a pairwise deletion approach. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

Results

Respondent characteristics

Approximately 80 % of the nurses affiliated with the SICPED subgroup participated in the survey (n = 78). However, only 38 % of respondents disclosed their institutional affiliation. Among those who did, the majority were from Policlinico S. Orsola Malpighi in Bologna (n = 6; 20 %), IRCCS Policlinico San Donato in Milan (n = 5; 17 %), and the Gabriele Monasterio Tuscan Foundation – Ospedale G. Pasquinucci in Massa-Carrara (n = 5; 17 %).

The average time required to complete the e-survey was 7 min. Full response rates (100 %) were obtained for all evaluation items.

To enable a comprehensive stratification of item scores, considering all significant post-hoc interactions, the overall mean score per criterion was used as a cut-off to identify priority practice areas. For interpretive clarity, criterion-specific mean scores were rounded to the nearest whole number. Accordingly, for Criterion C1 (cut-off M ≥ 4), the following items had significantly higher mean scores than others: items 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, and 8, as compared to items 5, 10, 13, 15, and 16 (see Table 2).

For Criterion C2 (quality of institutional protocols and procedures), no statistically significant differences were observed in the mean scores across practice areas (F(10.168) = 1.533, p = .121, η2 = 0.020). Descriptively, the overall mean score for this criterion (M = 3.52 ± 0.08) suggests a consistently moderate rating across all items, indicating that the perceived quality of protocols and procedures was relatively uniform among the practice areas assessed (see Table 2).

In contrast, for Criterion C3 (unjustified variability in clinical practice), Item 2, i.e., palliative and end-of-life care for individuals with CHD, received the highest mean score (3.06 ± 1.27), with statistically significant differences among practice areas (F (10.203) = 5.176, p < .001, η2 = 0.067). Using the overall mean score for C3 (M ≥ 3) as a threshold, Items 2, 3, 7, 8, and 16 were rated significantly higher than Items 5, 11, 12, 13, 14, and 15 (see Table 2).

For Criterion C4 (disparities in care processes and outcomes), Item 8, i.e., management of psychosocial and emotional disorders in patients with CHD and their families, was rated the highest (2.94 ± 1.15), with significant variability across practice areas (F (10.088) = 5.009, p < .001, η2 = 0.065). Based on the overall mean threshold for C4 (M ≥ 3), only Item 8 scored significantly higher compared to Items 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 10, 14, 15, and 16 (see Table 2).

For Criterion C5 (level of specialized or advanced professional competence), Item 2 again emerged with the highest score (4.16 ± 1.20), demonstrating statistically significant differences (F (7.498) = 4.431, p < .001, η2 = 0.058). When comparing items against the overall mean score for C5 (M ≥ 3), Items 2, 7, and 8 were rated significantly higher than Items 5, 13, and 15 (see Table 2).

Focusing on the practice areas identified as significantly critical for at least one prioritization criterion, highlighted in red in Fig. 2, four key priority areas emerged. These include Item 8 (management of psychosocial and emotional disorders in patients with CHD and their families), Item 7 (management of neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome in newborns with CHD), Item 2 (palliative and end-of-life care for individuals with CHD), and Item 3 (transition care for adolescents with CHD and their families).

Notably, Item 8 was flagged as a priority area across multiple domains of concern: lack of national clinical guidelines (4.18 ± 0.91), unjustified variability in clinical practice (2.99 ± 1.31), disparities in care processes (2.94 ± 1.15), and limited availability of advanced or specialized training among healthcare professionals (4.17 ± 1.06). Items 7 and 2 were also considered high priority, primarily due to gaps in national guidelines (4.19 ± 0.88 for Item 7; 4.34 ± 0.83 for Item 2), variability in clinical practice (2.99 ± 1.31 for Item 7; 3.06 ± 1.27 for Item 2), and insufficient specialist training (4.06 ± 1.06 for Item 7; 4.16 ± 1.20 for Item 2). Lastly, Item 3 emerged as a priority mainly due to the absence of national guidelines (4.30 ± 0.85) and variability in care delivery (3.03 ± 1.15).

Discussion

This study identified priority practice areas in the care of individuals with CHD, as perceived by non-medical healthcare professionals affiliated with SICPED. Among the prioritization criteria, the lack of clinical guidelines and the variability in clinical practice emerged as key barriers to the consistent application of evidence-based practices, the adoption of integrated care pathways, and the standardization of care models. Indeed, the Italian National Guidelines System does not currently provide finalized national guidelines for any area of CHD management [32]. In this regard, this study provides an objective and structured contribution to identifying priority care domains that require further development to address the complexity of CHD-related health needs better by systematically mapping perceived unmet needs. Scientific societies play a crucial role in elevating the contribution of non-medical healthcare professionals across these domains, particularly as part of strategies aimed at strengthening the sustainability of the Italian national healthcare system through integrated models of care and professional recognition [12,33].

Psychosocial care and support (Item 8) emerged as a top-priority area, marked by the absence of national guidelines (4.18 ± 0.91), significant variability in care (2.99 ± 1.31), inequities in care processes (2.94 ± 1.15), and limited specialized training among professionals (4.17 ± 1.06). Literature confirms that 19–43 % of adults with CHD experience anxiety or depression, and 30–80 % of parents report emotional distress [7]. These issues are strongly associated with reduced quality of life, harmful lifestyle behaviors, and increased cardiovascular risk [7,8]. Furthermore, out-of-pocket expenses for psychosocial services in Italy remain substantial, averaging 61.7 % of total costs [34]. In light of this, the 10.13039/100000968American Heart Association recommends the integration of psychosocial support into CHD care programs, underscoring the need for accessible mental health services and holistic care [17]. Nurses are central to this effort, particularly in screening and follow-up interventions (nurse-led psychological support), and in fostering interdisciplinary links with mental health services [35].

The need for improved palliative and end-of-life care (Item 2) is amplified by rising survival rates and the aging population with CHD. International guidelines recommend the use of advance care planning (ACP) in individuals with complex or advanced CHD [17,18], as ACP has been shown to reduce hospitalizations and support informed care decisions [18]. However, people with CHD are 64 % more likely to die in hospital than the general population (41 %) [36]. This may be attributed to insufficient professional training in palliative care and delayed discussions about prognosis and care preferences. Nurses and cardiologists are the most frequently identified reference figures in initiating these conversations [18]. Strengthening nurses’ competencies in palliative care through continuous education is essential to enabling earlier ACP implementation [37].

Clinically, the management of neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) in infants with CHD (Item 7) was identified as a priority area. Inadequate weaning from analgesia/sedation in postoperative infants with CHD could prolong ICU stays and increase iatrogenic complications [25]. International surveys reveal significant variability in analgesia protocols across hospitals [25,38], despite the shared use of standardized assessment tools. Developing evidence-based practice recommendations for weaning protocols is crucial to improving outcomes. Nurses play a pivotal role in nurse-driven analgesia and sedation protocols, which have been associated with reduced hospital stays and lower opioid/benzodiazepine use [39]. Moreover, they are instrumental in symptom management and parental education for home-based tapering after early discharge [[38], [39], [40]].

Taken together, these four domains mirror international priority-setting efforts in CHD nursing. For example, in the United Kingdom (UK), psychosocial care and transitional pathways have been repeatedly highlighted in strategic frameworks for congenital heart disease services [ref]. Similarly, United States (US) guidelines and nursing position papers stress the integration of palliative care and psychosocial support into CHDcare [17,18], while European consensus documents emphasise the critical role of specialist nurses in coordinating transition programs and ensuring equitable access to services [20]. These parallels indicate that the unmet needs identified in the Italian context resonate with broader international trends, underscoring the global relevance of advancing nursing competencies and structured care models in CHD.

Transition care for adolescents with CHD (Item 3) was also deemed a high-priority area, mainly due to the absence of guidelines and clinical variability. Although recent randomized controlled trials have supported the effectiveness of transition programs, leading to Class I, Level A recommendations [1,19], national implementation remains inconsistent. These programs improve adolescents' readiness, health literacy, and satisfaction, while reducing delays in adult care transfer [19,20]. The role of the Transition Coordinator, typically a nurse with advanced competencies, is central to delivering educational interventions and coordinating care [20,41]. In Italy, however, integrated care models supporting transitional care are still in place [42]. Future efforts should aim to identify contextual barriers and facilitators, alongside developing monitoring indicators that can be shared nationally. While transition has primarily focused on adolescence, a lifespan approach is needed to address the evolving needs of individuals with CHD [43].

In Europe, the 2020 ESC Guidelines for the management of stress in adults with CHD state that the integration of psychosocial support and structured transition is an essential component of high-quality care [1,21]. Complementarily, the recent global consensus statement on transition to adulthood explicitly identifies transition as a Class I, Level A recommendation, underscoring its universal relevance [19,20]. Palliative and advance care planning are likewise emphasized in European position papers jointly issued by ESC, ACNAP, and EAPC [18,37]. At the same time, psychosocial outcomes and interventions are increasingly recognized in the AHA statements as key determinants of long-term health in CHD populations [6,7]. Moreover, health policy frameworks such as the AHA's “2030 Future” agenda highlight the critical role of nursing leadership in addressing unmet needs through coordinated models of care [5]. These parallels demonstrate that the Italian priorities identified in this survey are not isolated findings, but rather align with broader international agendas, reinforcing the need for globally coherent strategies to strengthen nursing competencies and service delivery in CHD.

In particular, our findings reinforce the directions outlined in recent 2023–2025 ESC/ACNAP position papers and AHA scientific statements, which prioritize transition care, psychosocial integration, and palliative preparedness as core components of contemporary CHD nursing practice [[5], [6], [7],18,19,37]. Beyond reinforcing current European and American priorities, our results may also inform future international consensus-building efforts. Organisations such as the International Society for Adult Congenital Heart Disease (ISACHD) and ACNAP are increasingly working to define ACHD nursing competencies across domains, including transition, palliative integration, and psychosocial support [20]. In this context, this study contributes by providing insights into identifying unmet needs and practice gaps that could be leveraged in these international frameworks to promote harmonised, evidence-based competencies and more coherent service delivery worldwide.

Although the focus of this study was on high-priority areas, several domains were identified as relatively less critical based on their average scores. These include: care during donation and transplantation (Item 1); surgical wound management (Item 9); care for patients with CHD and comorbidities (Item 11); and care of women with CHD (Item 12). These areas may benefit from existing care pathways and well-established nursing roles, or they may have received high scores in only one prioritization domain. For instance, Italy's transplant network is robust, with clearly defined nursing roles in procurement and waitlist management [44], though the absence of CHD-specific transplant guidelines remains a gap. Similarly, the wound care domain benefits from strong evidence and advanced nursing competencies. The relatively lower prioritization of comorbidity and women's health may reflect the current limitations of nursing scope in these areas, although variability in practice still warrants attention.

Even though our study was conducted within the Italian healthcare context, the findings may carry broader relevance. Variations in nursing workforce structures, educational systems, and access to psychosocial or mental health support across countries may influence how such priorities are implemented [45]. Nonetheless, core priority areas identified here, including the importance of transitional care, structured psychosocial support, and integration of palliative approaches, are strongly echoed in international frameworks and consensus statements [6,7,[18], [19], [20]]. This alignment indicates that the priorities highlighted in our study resonate with wider global challenges in nursing care for individuals with CHD and may inform policy and practice across diverse healthcare systems, boosting an international debate in this regard.

Limitations

This study has several limitations, primarily related to the data collection methodology. A self-report evaluation system was used, which may have introduced social desirability bias, potentially influencing participants’ responses based on their training and professional experience. This could have affected both the assessment and interpretation of prioritization criteria.

Regarding the study population, the survey included only nurses, as they represent the largest group of non-medical healthcare professionals affiliated with SICPED. While this focus was consistent with the study's aim to consolidate a professional perspective within a specific community of practice, future investigations should incorporate the views of a broader range of stakeholders, including other healthcare professionals and patient representatives. In this study, while patients were not directly surveyed, preliminary feedback and discussion of the findings were shared with a patient expert and a clinical psychologist from the Italian Association of Congenital Heart Disease Patients and Families (AICCA).

Additionally, no detailed sociodemographic data were collected from respondents, aside from their institution and region of practice. This limits the ability to stratify responses by potentially relevant characteristics such as years of experience, educational background, or clinical setting. Moreover, although the response rate was high, selection bias cannot be excluded. For example, nurses with a stronger interest in areas such as psychosocial care may have been more likely to participate.

Another limitation concerns the validation of the questionnaire. Although face validity was established through open-ended feedback from 20 senior SICPED members, no formal psychometric testing (e.g., reliability analysis, construct validity) was performed. Thus, the instrument should be regarded as exploratory, designed to capture practice priorities rather than as a validated measurement scale. Moreover, the methodological choices related to missing data handling (40 % completion threshold and pairwise deletion) may have introduced variability across analyses and could limit both internal consistency and generalisability, particularly if the data were not missing completely at random.

Finally, the single-profession representation of the respondent pool, although intentional and aligned with the study's objective to capture the nursing perspective within the non-medical healthcare community, constrains the generalisability of the findings. These results should therefore be interpreted as a first step in developing a shared framework that will serve as a platform for future interdisciplinary dialogue. The evaluation of prioritization criteria for developing practice recommendations must ultimately draw on the collective perspectives of diverse professional groups. In this regard, the present study should be viewed as a preparatory effort, intended to stimulate future multi-professional and multidisciplinary engagement through the development of collaborative communities of practice.

Conclusions

This study enabled the identification of priority practice areas that warrant further scientific and professional dialogue aimed at developing or adapting clinical best practice recommendations for the care and management of individuals with CHD. The findings represent an initial phase in this broader conversation and can serve as a foundation for establishing strategic directions in research, clinical practice, and professional education. In doing so, this work contributes to the recognition and enhancement of the role of non-medical healthcare professionals in CHD care. In this context, the establishment of interdisciplinary and multi-professional communities of practice around each identified priority area emerges as a strategic approach to addressing the evolving health needs of individuals with CHD. Moreover, such communities can foster collaborative engagement and dialogue with other scientific societies, ultimately supporting more integrated and evidence-based care models.
