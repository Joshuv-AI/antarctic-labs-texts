Eating healthy within the boundaries of one planet: case study for the Netherlands
Authors: Corné van Dooren, Judith Groen, Carolina Carrillo Diaz, Lana Liem, Lisanne de Weert, Alessandra C. Grasso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe current Dutch diet fails to meet existing nutritional guidelines and exceeds the environmental boundaries as defined by the EAT-Lancet Commission. The purpose of this study is to model planet-based diets for the Netherlands for 2030 and 2050 that are in line with national dietary recommendations and within the planetary boundaries, as input for future Food-System Based Dietary Guidelines.MethodsFuture Dutch planet-based diets were modelled using mathematical optimisation (Optimeal 3.0) with the most recent 2019–2021 food consumption survey for males and females (18–50 years) and the environmental impact database for Dutch foods (2024). Environmental impacts were adjusted to align with the planetary boundaries and incorporate forecasted improvements. Constraints were applied on nutrients and environmental impacts (greenhouse gas emissions in kg CO2eq/y, blue water use in m3/y, cropland use in hectares, phosphorus application and surplus nitrogen in kg/y). The ReCiPe 2016 endpoint indicator method was applied to calculate biodiversity loss related to diets (extinctions/ million species year).ResultsThe optimised diets resulted in a significant reduction in meat, eggs, fats/oils, potatoes/tubers, and sugar/confectionery, and a significant increase in legumes, nuts/seeds, vegetables, fish, and meat replacers. In the optimised diets, 64% of total protein is plant-based substantial higher than the 43% in the current diet. These dietary changes led to a 65% lower impact on species richness loss and a diet within five planetary boundaries.ConclusionA healthy and sustainable diet within the boundaries of one planet is possible, if we manage to reduce food waste, shift to more sustainable production methods, and change our diets. This study shows the urgent need for policymakers to take big steps in the five years leading up to 2030. The results of this study could contribute to more sustainable dietary guidelines and eating habits in the Netherlands and could be an example for other countries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01273-9.

Background

Nature is our foundation – not only for our health, but also for our food supply. Unfortunately, nature is in a global crisis as biodiversity continues to decline at an alarming rate [1]. The overconsumption in High Income Countries and the population growth, is stretching Earth beyond the limits of its ecological capacity [2]. A food systems transformation in the current way industrialized countries produce and consume food is fundamental for solving crises related to the climate, biodiversity, health, and justice. Unsustainable food systems are currently the number one threat to nature and human health: it is responsible for 90% of tropical deforestation, 70% of freshwater withdrawals [3], and 27% of greenhouse gas emissions (GHGE) [4] and a threat to 86% of species at risk of extinction [1, 3]. To achieve a liveable future where everyone has access to healthy food and humanity lives in harmony with nature, it is necessary to change eating habits [5]. Scientific studies show that eating healthy and in balance with nature offers great benefits [2, 5]: A global shift towards healthy, sustainable diets could improve human health as well as food security, in part due to the positive effect this could have on the global climate. Other benefits include more robust supply chains, a reduced risk of new pandemics, and less food waste [6].

Food systems rely on healthy soil, insect pollination, and clean drinking water. Using the most accurate methods available, scientists have estimated the Earth’s planetary boundaries (PBs) [2], i.e. the ecological limits mankind cannot exceed. If humanity manages to stay within these limits, it will keep the Earth healthy and liveable for present and future generations. Several of the limits have already been transgressed and the global food system is a major driver to this [7, 8]. To live within the PB, we need to halve the global food system footprint in the areas of GHGE, nitrogen, phosphorus and material footprint by 2030 at the latest [9]. Additionally substantial reductions are required in use of fertilisers such as nitrogen and phosphate. Many countries, including the Netherlands, have set sustainability targets for GHGE and biodiversity footprint reductions [10]. These targets are based on the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), the Paris Climate Agreement, and the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework and must be met by 2030 or 2050.

National food-based dietary guidelines play an important role in helping consumers switch to a healthier and more sustainable diet [11]. In the Netherlands, the Health Council’s Dutch dietary guidelines 2015 [12] exist, which together with the Dietary Reference Values, were turned into an educational model called the Wheel of Five (Wo5) by the Netherlands Nutrition Centre [13]. In the 2016 optimisation of the Wo5, sustainability was considered by putting a maximum constraint on animal products that contribute significantly to climate change, such as meat, and on aquatic biodiversity loss through restriction to fish consumption. If the Dutch population adheres to the recommendations, it could lead to a slight reduction in the environmental impact of the Dutch diet (13% less GHGEs for adult men) [14]. However, a review of a large number of official food-based dietary guidelines shows that most of these national recommendations exceed the PB [15] and are therefore not yet sustainable enough. There is a growing consensus for the need of a food systems approach to address the complexity of food system transformation. It is a positive opportunity to collaborate on FBDGs that are more appropriate for the 21st century and ambitious enough to meet the environmental challenges at hand [11]. Both the Health Council of the Netherlands and the Netherlands Nutrition Centre are currently exploring ways to prioritize sustainability in their dietary guidelines. International official bodies consider it necessary for future dietary guidelines to further integrate sustainability [16, 17]. A recent study clarified the most impactful approaches to incorporating environmental sustainability and PBs into dietary guidelines. The optimisation and food system approach, used in this study, are in line with their recommendations [11]. The goal of this study is to model planet-based diets for the Netherlands for 2030 and 2050 that are in line with national dietary recommendations and within the PBs. A food system based approach for future dietary guidelines, is a type of intervention that can lead to the fulfilment of desired policy goals, including SDGs and PBs [11].

Methods

Input data

The reference diet was based on the current Dutch population diet, derived from the most recent Dutch National Food Consumption Survey 2019–2021 using two 24-hour recalls of food intake. This study focused on adults aged 18–50 years, including men (n = 318) and women (n = 284). Pregnant and lactating women were excluded. Foods were grouped into 18 main and 135 sub food groups according to the GloboDiet classification [18]. Food products were further classified into 21 sub food groups according to the EAT-Lancet planetary health diet, as well as whether they are included or excluded in the Wo5. All food products were linked to the Dutch Food Composition Database (NEVO) 2021 version 7.1 by NEVO code [19]. An average diet was derived by first averaging food products reported during the two recalls for each participant for men and women aged 18–50 years, respectively, then averaging food products across all participants per gender.

Products representing more than 1% of current consumption of food group (in total calorie intake and/or total intake in grams) were included in the optimisation, plus products considered as relevant for future diets (i.e. meat and dairy alternatives) [20]. The quantity of the food items contributing less than 1% (in g/d) were proportionally distributed across the remaining food items in the respective food groups to maintain the composition of the current diet. Food products similar in type were further combined. The total number of products in the diet of adults aged 18-50y was reduced from 825 to 354 food products, of which environmental impact data were available.

Food products in the reference diet were linked to environmental impact data of foods taken from an intermediate version of the Blonk Food Database (June 2023). Environmental impact data represent the average environmental impact of foods consumed by the Dutch population, accounting for Dutch market mixes, energy grids, and food loss and waste statistics. This database contained life cycle inventories (LCI) data for approximately 400 food products commonly consumed in the Netherlands from farm to fork. The basis of the methodology and assumptions from cradle to farm and processing gate are described in Agri-Footprint 6 methodology report (Version 2) [21, 22]. Methods and assumptions made for post-farm stages can found in previous reports [23, 24]. In short, it was created in compliance with the ISO14040 and 14,044 LCA guidelines and, where applicable, aligned with the Product Environmental Footprint Category Rules [25, 26]. Food waste is considered at the consumer stage [2]. Despite the original PBs from the EAT-Lancet Commission, we considered that knowing the total (i.e. farm to fork) impact of consumed food was worth being analysed, since that indicator reflects better the actual impact of diets in the planet. Thus, both impacts up to processing and up to consumption are reported. The impacts shown for each food group reflect production impacts, because we only included impacts from production in the optimisations. Additionally, the category “to consumption” is shown separately, as it represents the aggregate impacts across all food groups beyond production (e.g. post-farm stages), rather than an additional food group. The Blonk Food Database covers 87% of all foods consumed. Proxies were identified (13% of all foods consumed) for foods and beverages for which data were not available, based on similarities in types of food, production systems, and ingredient composition by expert judgement.

Defining planetary boundaries for the Dutch diet

The full optimisation methodology is described in detail in the supplementary materials [27, 28].

Aligning EAT lancet PBs with impact assessment methodology

The environmental constraints that are used to find the optimal Dutch diet are based on the cradle-to-processing gate per capita impacts for five PBs: greenhouse gas emissions (GHGE), cropland use, freshwater use, and nitrogen and phosphorus application. Furthermore, a progressively stringent constraint was placed on the sixth PB, biodiversity loss, starting from the current impact. Based on publications by the Stockholm Resilience Centre [8], and EAT-Lancet Commission [2], PBs were translated to per world citizen for 2030 and 2050 (See Table 1) for six environmental impacts: greenhouse gas emissions, blue water use, cropland use, phosphorus application, surplus nitrogen (nitrogen application) and biodiversity loss. The PBs were calculated based on the predicted world’s population size in 2030 and 2050 by the UN world population prospects in 2019 [29] (Table 1), assuming equal contribution for everyone, regardless of age, country, or caloric needs.

As the PB indicators differ from the environmental midpoint impact categories calculated by the publicly available impact assessment methods (e.g. ReCiPe 2016, EF 3.0), there was a need to develop impact assessment methods aligned with the scope of the PBs for this analysis. We adjusted the environmental impacts in two ways: (1) to align with the PBs and (2) to consider projected improvements in production and reductions in waste for 2030 and 2050.

We made several assumptions and decisions to align the indicators to the EAT-Lancet PBs. EAT-Lancet assumed the food system will be fossil-free by 2050. We do not assume this is realistic in the agricultural sector, so we still included some fossil fuel and synthetic fertilizer use in our calculations: about 0.8–1.0 kg of CO₂, because of the high uncertainty of the path towards reducing the energy sector’s reliance on fossil fuels. This is in line with the IPCC 2013 (AR5) characterisation factors that we applied, including the effects of climate carbon feedback [27, 31].

The environmental impact data were adjusted to incorporate expected improvements in 2030 and 2050 as described in the report “The Menu of Tomorrow” (in Dutch) [27, 32] and demonstrated in Broekema et al. 2022 [33]. Projected changes that were taken into account include less carbon intensity of electricity used by supermarkets and consumers, improved farming techniques (livestock and cultivation), less input of N-fertilizer, food processing efficiency to increases in the use of green energy, innovations in packaging, and a reduction of food waste (factors ranged from a 11 to 26% decrease in environmental impact per kg). We assume that land use change is zero in the future: deforestation and reverse nature loss is halted by 2030 in line with the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022).

Calculating biodiversity loss

The EAT-Lancet target for biodiversity loss is expressed in extinction per million species*year and is based on background rate of 1 E/MSY (extinction without humans, which is estimated from fossil records) (See [34]). The impact on biodiversity (or: biodiversity loss) used in this study is calculated using the ReCiPe 2016 [35] endpoint indicator of damage to ecosystems, which shows the environmental impact on a higher aggregation level compared to a midpoint indicator, and includes a combination of impact pathways. It measures the species richness loss (potentially disappeared fraction of species) over space and time, and it differentiates damages to three distinct types of ecosystems: freshwater, terrestrial, and marine. The ReCiPe 2016 biodiversity endpoint is measured the damage to ecosystems due to global warming, water use, freshwater ecotoxicity, freshwater eutrophication, tropospheric ozone formation, terrestrial ecotoxicity, terrestrial acidification, marine eutrophication, marine ecotoxicity, and land use. The temporality of the effect of these different pressures varies: For example, land use leads to species disappearance during the occupation and relaxation time, but species diversity can recover after this. Climate change has a longer-lasting effect, due to increased extinction rates as the effect of greenhouse gas emissions. Integrating the biodiversity impact over time (100y), allows diverse types of pressures to be expressed in a common unit. This way, the ReCiPe impact assessment method calculates total number of species lost*year, based on the potentially disappeared fraction and global species densities in terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems. The unit of species lost*year reflects an integrated temporary effect and does not directly relate to (permanent) extinctions [36]. For this reason, a direct link cannot be made between the EAT Lancet target (see Table 1) and the biodiversity results from this study. Instead, for the final optimisation, we used a stepwise reduction approach meaning that we used the current impact of the reference diet on biodiversity loss (species lost*year) as a starting point to further reduce the number of species loss. From that point the largest reduction possible within acceptability constraints is found. Although the ReCiPe 2016 method is a most usable and complete biodiversity method in LCA-context, drawbacks include poor consideration of impact on marine species and poor consideration of spatial variability of impact and species density [37]. Other direct effects on biodiversity such as competitive effects of invasive species, but also positive effects such as nature-inclusive agriculture or agroecology measures are not yet accounted for, mainly due to knowledge gaps on the exact impacts of these modes of production [38].

Constraints used in optimisation

Planetary boundaries

EAT-Lancet only considered the environmental impact up to and including production, while additional impacts occur post-farm gate. To align with EAT-Lancet, the environmental constraints were applied to environmental impacts for food production, while environmental impacts from cradle to grave were also quantified. The PBs for GHGE, nitrogen and phosphorus application, water use, and cropland use, as outlined in Table 1, were applied as constraints for 2030 and 2050.

Nutritional requirements

As nutritional constrains, we determined nutritional needs (nutritional constraints based on dietary reference values) based on the guidelines issued by the Health Council and the Nutrition Centre, as well as on feedback from the latter [39]. These minimum or maximum quantities, based on dietary reference values (DRVs) and FBDGs for Dutch males and females aged 18-50y, are the constraints that are used for the optimisation process. Acceptability

Acceptability constraints of 33–150% of the current consumption level for a product group were established. This range takes into account the asymmetric distribution of food group intake, similar to what was done in Broekema et al. [33]. Those constraints were not applied to legumes, vegetables, fruits, and alcoholic beverages, as nutritional constraints took priority and were otherwise not met.

Diet optimisation

In this study, we not only looked for ways to reduce GHGE and land use (LU), like most other studies [40], but we also explored solutions that could address five different indicators simultaneously, without exceeding the PBs. All boundaries with increased or high risk to be transgressed, were included: nitrogen flows, phosphate flows, LU, climate change, and freshwater use (for irrigation, so-called blue water) [7]; quantitative limits (constraints) were set for all indicators except biodiversity loss.

The model we used was Optimeal®3.0 [41], a tool that applies mathematical optimisation techniques to simultaneously reduce the environmental impact of the diet and meet all nutritional and dietary requirements (Table 2) while staying as close to the current diet as possible. The objective function is to find a diet as similar as possible to the reference diet while satisfying the set of optimisation constraints. The optimisation diet is found by minimizing the deviation function subject to all constraints (Tables 1 and 2). In words, the deviation function sums up the square change, in grams, of the consumption of each food item available, and the optimisation goal is to minimize the deviation [28].

As the method for calculating biodiversity loss is still under development and contains several uncertainties and the unit of biodiversity assessment in LCA does not match exactly with the unit of the planetary boundary, we did not have a hard limit for this [42]. Biodiversity loss is expressed as an integrated temporary effect in this study, while the PB is expressed as (permanent) extinctions (see section Calculating biodiversity loss). Because a direct link cannot be made between the PB and the biodiversity results from this study, we took a stepwise approach and applied a progressive reduction on biodiversity loss starting from the current impact on biodiversity loss, while applying nutritional constraints and a maximum constraint on the other environmental indicators equal to the PB. The diet with the lowest possible impact on biodiversity loss, while remaining it culturally acceptable and nutritional adequate, was identified and the resulting diet’s effects on the remaining five PBs was quantified (as balance point). Number of changes in the diet served as a proxy for cultural acceptability. Before this balance point the changes in the diet are more effective in reducing biodiversity loss. A further reduction in biodiversity loss requires proportionally more changes to the diet, making it less acceptable for the average consumer [28].

The current diets for men and women were optimised to create four diets for the future (men 2030, men 2050, women 2030 and women 2050) using Optimeal® 3.0. The optimal diets meet national nutrient requirements, national recommendations on specific food groups, such as fruit, vegetables and legumes, acceptability constraints, 33–150% of the current food group consumption, as well as environmental targets based on the PBs (Table 1). Quadratic programming is used to find the closest solution to the starting diet, resulting in a diet which complies to all nutritional and environmental constraints with the least changes to the starting diet. More information on the optimisation algorithm is provided in the Optimeal® documentation [43].

We compared outcomes with the current diet, the Wo5 [13], and EAT-Lancet diet [2, 44]. In calculating and modelling the diet, we tried to align the outcomes with Dutch food culture. Results

A healthy and sustainable diet within five planetary boundaries

This study compares the Dutch population’s current diet and four optimised diets of the future (men 2030, men 2050, women 2030 and women 2050, all adult 20-50y). Table 3 summarizes the results. For all optimised diets, there is a sharp decrease in meat consumption: from 111 to 4 g/day for men, and from 67 to 19 g/day for women. The total quantity of dairy (including substitutes) remains mostly unchanged, but there is a shift from animal to plant protein; the share of milk substitutes increases from 5.4% in the current consumption to 8.9% (males) and 9.3% (females). In addition, cheese intake decreased 30 g/day. At the same time meat substitutes going from 5 to 8 g/day to 47–53 g and dairy substitutes from 15 to 20 g/day to 27–34 g. The quantity of fruits and vegetables increases for all optimised diets. The four diets led to a slight decrease in the proportion of bread, cereal products and potatoes for women, and a slight increase for men. For both groups, consumption of these products remains below the intake recommended by the Wo5. The quantity of oil and fat is, and remains, small in both the current and optimised diets. Product groups outside the Wo5, such as soups, sauces, sweets, biscuits and snacks, are lower, but still have a place in the diet (together accounting for 104–122 g/day), due to the imposed 33% lower limit for each product group, except for meat. This limit could not be imposed for meat, as the PBs would not be met. Since there are few major differences between 2030 and 2050, this paper will focus on the results for 2050 from this point forward.

Comparison with current Wheel of Five and EAT-Lancet recommendations

The proposed diet for 2050 is compared to the current Wo5 recommendations and the EAT-Lancet’s global diet (Table 3). The optimisations include minimum constraints for fish, fruits (including nuts), vegetables, legumes, dairy (incl. replacers), and whole grains: all of those are met, except for dairy within the women subgroup (291 instead of 300 ml). Because the fruit group constraint includes nuts, the fruit consumption separate is below 200 g: m 170 & w 186 g). Interestingly, the optimisation shows that eating lots of fruits and vegetables is not the solution: the intake of these products is slightly lower in the optimal diets, compared to the current dietary guidelines and the EAT-Lancet diet, but more than the current intake. Meat consumption is lower than the currently recommended maximum of 500 g/week. The same holds for cheese: for men, there is little room for cheese in the 2050 diet, and for women the proposed amount of cheese is half of what is currently recommended. Liquid dairy (including plant-based substitutes) and vegetables, nuts, and legumes provide sufficient calcium. Seafood, eggs, and liquid dairy remain on the menu, although the amount of dairy will be 84 ml/day smaller for women compared to the current recommendations, while the amount of seafood (fish and shellfish) will be 7 to 9 g/day larger. Only sustainable caught seafood is included and endangered species are excluded from the diet.

The current consumption of nuts and legumes need to increase in the optimised diet, although less pronounced than suggested by EAT-Lancet – the current recommendations from the Wo5 can remain in place (a weekly portion of legumes and a handful of nuts daily). There is a slight decrease in the amount of cereal products compared to the current recommendations, but a shift towards more wholegrain products – also due to the lower number of calories in our proposed diet – and potato consumption is close to the level recommended by EAT-Lancet. Oils and fats are used sparingly, as nuts, seeds and seafood provide most of the essential fatty acids and vitamins needed. Whereas EAT-Lancet does not consider drinks, our diet includes two litres of non-alcoholic drinks per day and zero litres of alcoholic beverages, in line with the Wo5 (1.5–2 L of fluids).

Animal-based foods in a plant-based diet

Variety is reflected in the choice of 15 types of fruit and 30 types of vegetables offered by the diet of the future. Processed products, such as French fries, chocolate, margarine, and soft drinks, are not eliminated, but the focus is on fresh, local products and imported foods with a low environmental impact: broccoli, carrots, peas, lettuce, onions and tomatoes that are mostly grown local and local fruits such as apples, pears, and strawberries, supplemented with fruits imported by boat (oversee) or truck (Europe). The optimised diet proposes 64% plant-based protein for women and 69% for men by 2030, and 64% for women and 74% for men by 2050. This means that in the future two-thirds of Dutch protein intake should come from plants. This is equal to about 24 g animal protein per person per day. By 2050, the amount of meat should be reduced to between 0.5 and 1.5 weekly servings to keep food consumption and the planet healthy, consisting mainly of pork (15–130 g/week). When it comes to seafood, the optimal diets propose 1.5 weekly servings (147–161 g/week), which is slightly higher than the current recommendation. Total protein consumption remains stable, falling 4% for men and rising 7% for women. According this results, a fully vegetarian or vegan diet is not necessary to stay within PBs.

The model calculated solutions that include a small amount of pork, but no beef or chicken. This finding does not consider a food system perspective, as continuous consumption of dairy and eggs would relate to production (and thus consumption) of cattle and poultry meat. This dependency is examined. Dairy cows are required to provide for the recommended intake of cow’s milk, and those cows yield beef: about 11 g/day for every 300 ml of liquid dairy (264 to 350 ml/day). Similarly, eggs require chickens, which also provide meat: almost 2 g/day for every two eggs per week (14 g/day) [45]. If these amounts are factored in, there is a shift in the diet from pork to beef and chicken, as well as a small overall increase in the amount of meat: 101 g/week for men and 153 g for women, or 1 to 1.5 servings per week. While environmental impact increases, PBs are not exceeded. This is because of a relatively low environmental impact of ‘spent dairy cows’ and ‘old laying hens.’

Environmental impact reduced by 43 to 83%

The proposed diet for the Netherlands would lead to a 43–83% reduction in GHGE, cropland use, phosphate application, and nitrogen surplus. Men’s blue water use would be reduced by 43%; for women, there would be a minor increase (+ 7%) (Fig. 1), but the total amount used would remain well within the PBs (Fig. 2).

Discussion

At the outset, it was not a given that a diet meeting food, energy, and nutrient constraints would even be possible within the PBs. Fortunately, the results show that this is mathematically feasible. The optimised planet-based diet, with about two thirds of total protein being derived from plants, resembles EAT-Lancet’s recommendation, which states that plants should account for approximately 63% of protein intake. Another recent study by Wageningen University & Research, based on LU and GHGEs in a circular food system, also arrives at 63% plant-based protein for Europe [46]. At present, 57% of the protein consumed by the Dutch population comes from animals and 43% from plants [20]. If consumers follow the current Wo5 recommendations with a maximum of 500 g meat per week, about half of their protein will be plant-based. When eating according to the Wo5, but replacing all meat by legumes, nuts, and egg, the proportion of plant-based protein increases to about 60% [47]. Several recommendations issued to the government propose a target of 60% plant-based protein for 2030 [47]. In short, while a 60% target for plant-based protein will not be enough to stay within the PBs, an 80 or 100% target (i.e. vegan) is not required when considering improvements in efficiency and stop of land conversion.

The reduction in environmental impact with 43 to 83% reductions across all environmental indicators is greater than expected and would be a significant step towards healthy food production and consumption within PBs. Earlier calculations based on options available within the current Wo5 showed that making more conscious choices can reduce the GHGEs of our diet by a third (by eating vegetarian or making sustainable choices within each product group), up to a maximum of 50% (if you do both) [14]. Similar studies for Belgium and UK found that switching to a more sustainable diet would reduce GHGEs by 52% and 36% respectively [48, 49]. An international WWF study into the effects of a planet-based diet estimated a 30% decrease in GHGEs, 41% less LU, and 46% less biodiversity loss [50]. We did not set any dietary scenario in advance – for instance, we did not assume that everyone would switch to a vegetarian or Mediterranean diet. Instead, we used optimisation based on a mathematical model (quadratic programming), which means that the model itself arrived at the best possible solution by considering certain constraints in terms of nutrition, PBs, and acceptance [51].

Meat, dairy, cereal products, drinks (coffee, tea, soft drinks), vegetables, fruits, nuts, and olives contributes the most to biodiversity loss in the Dutch diet (Fig. 3) [42]. That is why the optimal diet recommendations for fruits and vegetables remain at around 200 g per person per day, as prescribed by current guidelines. This contrasts with the higher quantities – 600 g of vegetables and 300 g of fruit – recommended by EAT-Lancet. To reduce biodiversity loss, it is key to reduce consumption of meat and dairy, and to drink water instead of coffee, tea, and fruit juices.

WWF introduced the concept of planet-based diets. These diets will not only ensure that everyone on the planet has healthy and nutritious food, but they will also curb the negative impact of the food system by encouraging a shift from exploiting the Earth to restoring the balance of nature, which will ultimately benefit humankind as well [50].

This study emphasizes some strength and weaknesses. It is a strength that the optimisation approach applied in this study leads to greater environmental gains while limiting solutions with low consumer acceptability, such as proposing that everyone switch to a full vegan diet [51] or a diet without any sweets, coffee, and soft drinks. Despite this, there will still have to be substantial changes in some of the product groups to stay within PBs. The biggest change is the reduced consumption of meat. The proposed increase in the consumption of plant-based meat and dairy substitutes is in line with current Dutch trends. There are also other shifts within product groups, for example from white to wholemeal bread.

At the end of 2025 -after we performed this research- the EAT-Lancet commission updated the Planetary Health Diet and PBs [30]. The recommended quantities of products groups only changed slightly compared to 2019 (i.e. whole grains from 232 to 210 g/d, red meat from 14 to 15 g/d), but our outcomes stayed within their new ranges. The new PBs where tightened for Blue water use, N surplus and P application and they introduced a new indicator for Biodiversity loss. Nevertheless, the diets stay within the new PBs except for P application, which was already the hardest to achieve.

This study was conducted based on the latest food consumption survey and environmental impact data and using advanced calculation methods. It was also the first known study for the Netherlands to have adapted the ReCiPe impact assessment method to align the environmental impact indicators to EAT-Lancet PBs scope. However, it also had several limitations. For example, we only considered 354 products, selecting those that make up 1% or more of our menu by weight, as well as some well-known plant-based alternatives. We conducted isocaloric optimisations, using the current energy intake obtained from the Dutch National Food Consumption Survey, in which energy consumption (kcal) is underreported – in reality, consumers are likely to eat more, meaning their actual energy intake is higher and consequently their entire daily menu also has a bigger impact. In the calculation of protein needs, the quality of protein is not considered. We do not expect that this is a major issue, because in the most sustainable diets protein adequacy remains sufficient [52]. The Dutch Health Council has determined that the effect of the protein transition on protein quality will not affect the majority of the Dutch population or only to a limited extent [53]. The fact that EAT-Lancet is not clear about how it calculated the PBs provided another challenge and weakness. Methods to calculate biodiversity loss are new and developing, and contain large margins of uncertainty, and it is challenging to align the ReCiPe endpoint indicator, damage to ecosystems, to the PB for biodiversity loss. Factors like marine biodiversity are therefore not yet sufficiently considered. Despite the limitations of the used method, this research is a relevant step in identifying the biodiversity impact of diets, and as an extension of footprint analyses on mid-point pressures like carbon emissions and land-use. This study is limited to the Netherlands, which is not representative for the entire world, i.e. it is a country with high nitrogen overload and a high livestock density, including animal welfare issues. The local nitrogen surplus boundary is already transgressed, linked to the high dairy production [54]. This is not considered in the study, where the PBs are recalculated as a global ‘fair share’ daily personal budget. If it would be considered that the PB shall not be transgressed in any location, then dairy consumption in the Netherlands would probably be much lower in the optimised diet.

Products that are linked in the food system, such as beef and milk, are not linked in this model, i.e. recommending eggs, but no chicken. We increased circularity aspects in a second run of the model through minimum constraints on meat from laying hens and dairy cows. Another point to note is that this study only looked at adults between the ages of 18 and 50 years. Different recommended amounts may be required for children and senior citizens, who are not included in this paper.

In this study we made several assumptions build on assumptions about PBs, future efficiencies and population sizes and equal distribution of consumption, comparing global (EAT-Lancet) and local data (Wo5). Due to uncertainties in indicators, the exact quantities mentioned in this study should not be taken too strictly – the proportions and overall trends in consumption are more important. The study provides guidelines on how sustainability can be considered in future FBDGs without compromising on health, as recommended by FAO (2024) [55].

Although the case study in this paper is about the Netherlands, the methodology and approach could be applied to other countries where consumption and impact data are available. It demonstrates how the EAT Lancet framework provides boundaries that are globally applicable with a high potential of local adaptation and scalability [2]. Our food system approach can be considered path-breaking in that it has the potential to be a disruptive innovation by considering health and the environment equally and creating major changes by setting the food system on an entirely new pathway for dietary guidelines that differ from past norms [11]. The case presented here implies some change in direction for many food-related policy actors. Today, the ambition to feed all people healthily is not enough. We also must consider how to produce this food sustainably. We see this as a positive opportunity to collaborate on FBDGs that are more appropriate for the 21st century and ambitious enough to meet the environmental challenges at hand. European national FBDGs committees are highly inspired by examples from other countries how they incorporate sustainability [11]. This case is additional to and in line with other recent studies that uses optimisation to provide national guidelines for healthy sustainable diets with national data, for example Germany [56], European Union [46], and USA [57]. Their results are also diets that limit meat consumption and highlight grains, legumes, and nuts as protein sources. This is relevant, because most official FBDGs recommendations exceed the PBs [15] and are therefore not yet sustainable enough.

This approach can help prioritize competing demands, as it demonstrates priority on the critical boundaries of phosphate application, climate change, and biodiversity loss. This study is also -together with many others on sustainable diets- an input for the Dutch national committees of the Health Council and Nutrition Centre that in 2025 and 2026 are working on the update of their FBDGs. The results can also inspire NGOs and businesses such as supermarkets, caterers, and restaurants to apply the planetary health diet in the local context making it culturally acceptable and close to the current Dutch diet. It is a hopeful message to consumers that an adapted diet meeting food, energy, and nutrient constraints is possible within the PBs.

Our conclusion is that a healthy and sustainable diet within the boundaries of one planet is possible, if we manage to reduce food waste and shift to more sustainable production methods, in addition to changing our diets. All three of these steps are vital to a coherent food and agriculture policy. This study shows the urgent need for policymakers to take big steps in the next five years leading up to 2030. The results of this study could contribute to more sustainable dietary guidelines and eating habits in the Netherlands and could be an example for other countries.
