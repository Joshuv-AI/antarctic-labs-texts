A case of panophthalmitis with orbital infection of polymicrobial origin
Authors: Xiaoyang He, Maoling Luo, Jianchuan Ran, Honghai He, Yonghong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfectious endophthalmitis following penetrating ocular trauma represents an acute and often blinding disease with a generally poor prognosis. The causative pathogens involved are diverse, among which bacterial organisms are the most prevalent. In contrast, infections caused by fungi of the order Mucorales are rare, constituting acute and fulminant opportunistic mycoses. Background

Traumatic infectious endophthalmitis after penetrating injuries has a prevalence ranging from 4% to 16% and progresses rapidly, representing a clinically acute blinding ophthalmic disease [1, 2]. Bacteria are the most common infectious agents, accounting for 80% to 90% of cases, with gram-positive cocci comprising approximately 83% of these, including Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus aureus, and various Streptococcus species. Gram-negative bacilli, primarily Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterobacter, are also significant contributors [3].

In contrast, endophthalmitis resulting from fungal infections is relatively rare, occurring in 4% to 14% of cases [4], and typically has a slower onset. Among fungal infections, those caused by Mucorales are particularly lethal and can lead to invasive infections [5]. The mortality rate of mucormycosis varies significantly based on the underlying disease and the site of infection, typically ranging from 40% to 80% [6]. A review of Mucorales infections in China from January 2001 to July 2020 indicated an overall mortality rate of 37.2%. Among these infections, disseminated mucormycosis exhibited the highest mortality rate at 63.9%, followed by rhino-orbito-cerebral mucormycosis at 54.4% [7]. The clinical presentation typically includes acute vision loss, ocular pain, and eyelid edema with rapid progression. Infections may manifest as localized endophthalmitis or evolve into disseminated disease with fatal outcomes [6]. Early and targeted treatment is essential for the survival of patients with mucormycosis [8–10].

To the best of our knowledge, no previous case of traumatic endophthalmitis caused by the concomitant infection with Rhizopus arrhizus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterococcus faecalis has been reported in the literature, underscoring the exceptional rarity of this condition. Notably, retrospective studies demonstrate significantly higher detection rates of Rhizopus infections among diabetic populations, suggesting diabetes mellitus as a critical predisposing factor for such polymicrobial infections [11].

We present a case of diabetes mellitus complicated by traumatic endophthalmitis after a penetrating injury. The patient’s initial symptom was acute vision loss, which was found to be caused by a co-infection with Rhizopus arrhizus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterococcus faecalis. Through comprehensive literature review, we summarize the clinical characteristics, diagnostic-therapeutic approaches, and potential pathogenic mechanisms of this condition.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old male patient was admitted to the hospital with pain and progressive loss of vision in the right eye that had persisted for 5 days. This was accompanied by a significant amount of foul-smelling, purulent discharge resulting from a penetrating injury to the temporal side of the right eyeball. The injury occurred due to slag penetrating the right eye while using a chainsaw to cut timber 10 days prior to the onset of symptoms, which progressed rapidly. Upon examination, the right eye exhibited marked swelling of the upper eyelid, moderate conjunctival edema and congestion. Physical examination of the right eye revealed significant upper eyelid swelling with pseudomembrane formation on the upper palpebral conjunctiva. The bulbar conjunctiva showed moderate edema and congestion (++). A laceration was observed in the temporal region of the bulbar conjunctiva, accompanied by scleral lysis, necrosis, and loss of corneal and intraocular structures (Fig. 1a). The patient has a history of diabetes for 5 years.

Laboratory investigations on admission revealed poorly controlled diabetes, with a glycated hemoglobin level of 11.7% and random blood glucose of 16.5 mmol/L. The patient also had significant systemic inflammation, indicated by an elevated C-reactive protein level of 54.67 mg/L and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 87 mm/h, along with mild hypokalemia (K + 3.14 mmol/L). The complete blood count and other biochemical parameters are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Computed tomography (CT) images (Fig. 3a and b) revealed structural damage to the right eyeball and its accessory structures, accompanied by partial bone resorption of the medial orbital wall.

On the day of admission, debridement and drainage of the infected ocular tissue were performed on the patient’s right eye; the procedure involved resection of the residual scleral structures (Fig. 1b). Gram staining of tissue specimens obtained during the debridement procedure revealed the presence of broad septate fungal hyphae, gram-positive cocci, and gram-negative bacilli (requiring further culture for identification; Fig. 2a and b). For glycemic control, therapy included subcutaneous injections of insulin aspart (8 U before breakfast, 8 U before lunch, and 12 U before dinner) and insulin glargine (15 U at bedtime), along with oral metformin hydrochloride extended-release tablets at a dosage of 0.5 g twice daily. Additionally, ceftazidime at a dosage of 1 g every 8 h and topical levofloxacin eye drops were prescribed as preventive anti-infective treatment.

On day 3 of admission, fungal cultures of tissue specimens revealed the presence of grayish-white, cotton-wool-like Rhizopus spp. fungi (Fig. 2c and d). The cultures exhibited solitary, slender sporangia and the presence of pseudoroots. Identification of the fungus as Rhizopus arrhizus was confirmed through targeted DNA sequencing. Concurrently, bacterial cultures isolated two pathogenic bacteria, which were identified by mass spectrometry as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterococcus faecalis. The drug sensitivity of Rhizopus arrhizus was assessed using the micro-broth dilution method, revealing that the lowest inhibitory concentration of amphotericin B against Rhizopus arrhizus was 0.25 µg/mL. Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterococcus faecalis demonstrated susceptibility to piperacillin-tazobactam and ampicillin, respectively. The specific drug sensitivity results are presented in Table 1.

On day 4 of admission, intravenous liposomal amphotericin B(L-AmB) was initiated at a starting dose of 5 mg/day as part of the antifungal therapy. The dosage was subsequently increased by 5 mg/day, reaching a maintenance dose of 40 mg/day. Additionally, ceftazidime was replaced with piperacillin/tazobactam sodium at a dosage of 4.5 g every 8 h to ensure adequate coverage against Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterococcus faecalis in the anti-infective regimen.

On the 10th day of admission, the edema of the right eyelid and conjunctiva gradually subsided, and the foul-smelling ocular discharge was significantly reduced. Given the highly invasive angioneurological nature of Rhizopus arrhizus, a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination was performed (Fig. 3c and d), which revealed structural destruction involving the retrobulbar fat, medial and lateral rectus muscles, and the optic nerve. During the right orbital exploration procedure, the orbital contents—including orbital fat, extraocular muscles, residual sclera, and the intraorbital segment of the optic nerve—were systematically removed. This was followed by orbital septum repair, fascial tissue flap reconstruction, conjunctival sac reshaping, and placement of negative pressure drainage. The intraoperative bleeding was 15 mL, and postoperative care included the application of tobramycin dexamethasone ophthalmic ointment to the conjunctival sac. Pathological sections of the intraoperative tissue specimen stained with hematoxylin and eosin stain (HE stain) indicated acute suppurative inflammation with granuloma formation. Additionally, broad septate-free fungal hyphae were detected (Fig. 2e and f), and Gram’s stain revealed the presence of Gram-positive cocci, while culture identified Enterococcus faecalis.

On the 15th and 21st days of admission, the smear and culture of the drainage fluid sent for examination yielded negative results. By day 28 of admission, the patient’s recovery had improved significantly (Fig. 1c and d), and he was subsequently discharged from the hospital. During the hospitalization period, L-AmB injections were administered at a maintenance dose of 40 mg/day. This treatment was accompanied by several episodes of critically low serum potassium levels, necessitating a combination of oral and intravenous potassium supplementation. Notably, there were no observed abnormalities in liver or renal function.

Forty days of treatment were completed after discharge, utilizing natamycin eye drops in conjunction with oral esaconazole capsules. An MRI was performed at the 40-day follow-up (Fig. 3e and f), revealing significant resorption of the ocular infection lesions, with no cranial abnormalities observed. The fasting blood glucose level was 6.34 mmol/L, and the glycosylated hemoglobin was 6.3%. The clinical treatment of this patient throughout the treatment cycle is detailed in Fig. 4.

Discussion

Mucorales are widely distributed in the air, moldy food, and soil. It is generally rare to cause infections in immunocompetent patients, of which the most commonly reported is Rhizopus arrhizus [12]. In this case report, we have documented an intraocular infection associated with the concomitant presence of Rhizopus arrhizus. The patient presented with a history of ocular trauma and a clear route of infection. Additionally, the patient had a history of diabetes and poor blood glucose control, which are known risk factors for infection. The results of smear staining, culture, pathological examination, protein spectrum analysis, and sequencing corroborated one another. Considering the clinical characteristics of the patient, it was evident that the primary infectious pathogen was Rhizopus arrhizus, with Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterococcus faecalis also identified as contributors to the infection. Following two surgical interventions, the patient received treatment with L-AmB in combination with antibiotics, and blood glucose levels were actively managed. The patient exhibited a favorable outcome.

In clinical practice, Mucorales infections predominantly occur in immunocompromised patients, including those with diabetes, neutropenia, tumors, and individuals receiving immunosuppressive therapy [13]. Additionally, infections resulting from penetrating injury warrant special attention [14, 15]. Mucormycosis is frequently misdiagnosed due to its low culture positivity rate, only 33% of patients with mucormycosis can be diagnosed through tissue culture [12, 16]. The direct smear method for observing drift-like, segregation-free mycelium is crucial for the diagnosis of mucormycosis [17]. Additionally, fluorescent staining of fungi in tissue specimens is widely employed due to its high sensitivity, specificity, and rapidity [12]. Mucormycosis is known for its rapid progression and high mortality rate. An international guideline developed by the European Confederation of Medical Mycology (ECMM) and the Mycoses Study Group (MSG), based on global expert consensus and available evidence, strongly recommends L-AmB as the cornerstone and preferred first-line agent for the treatment of invasive mucormycosis, with earlier initiation of therapy providing greater patient benefit [6]. Data indicate that patients who initiate treatment within six days exhibit a survival rate of 76–81%, whereas a delay exceeding twelve days results in a survival rate reduction to 36–42% [6]. In addition to systemic antifungal therapy, early surgical interventions—including debridement and surgical excision of the pathogenic foci—strict glycemic control, and optimization of glucocorticoid use can significantly enhance the prognosis for patients with mucormycosis and reduce mortality.

The current study, being a single-case report, is inherently limited in the generalizability of its findings. While multimodal diagnostic approaches allowed for cross-verification of the pathogens, the underlying microbial interactions in this co-infection remain unexplored. The integrated treatment strategy—combining surgical intervention, broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, and strict glycemic control—precludes assessment of each component’s individual contribution to the successful outcome. Moreover, the relatively short follow-up duration limits evaluation of long-term prognosis and potential disease recurrence, highlighting the need for validation through larger cohort studies with extended observation periods.

Conclusions

This case report describes the successful management of a severe ocular mixed infection in a diabetic patient, involving Rhizopus arrhizus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterococcus faecalis. Successful outcomes relied on three critical factors: rapid blood glucose control, early and precise pathogen identification (including drug susceptibility testing), and timely surgical intervention. This case underscores the critical importance of a multidisciplinary approach—combining aggressive surgical intervention and targeted antimicrobial therapy—in preventing life-threatening complications, including intracranial infection and sepsis, in managing such critical infections.
