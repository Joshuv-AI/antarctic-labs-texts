Nasal respiration modulates pain perception via the olfactory Bulb–Anterior cingulate cortex circuit
Authors: Ruixue Cheng, Yuxue Wang, Chunyan Dou, Song Gu, Weixiang Tang, Chen Chen, Ke Chen, Shuai Cui, Weiwei Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBreath regulation has been shown to reduce pain and anxiety-like emotions in humans, but the underlying neural mechanisms are still unclear. Here, we demonstrate that nasal respiratory sensory deprivation (Triton X-100) reduces the respiration-entrained rhythm (RR) transmitted from the olfactory bulb (OB) to the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), ultimately exerting an antinociceptive effect in a mouse model of neuropathic pain. Specifically, optogenetics, in vivo fiber photometry, viral tracing, and immunofluorescence staining revealed that nasal respiratory sensory deprivation activates GABAergic projections from the OB to the ACC and inhibits glutamatergic (Glu) neurons in the ACC. Chemogenetic activation or inhibition of the OB–ACCGlu pathway leads to nociceptive hypersensitivity or exerts antinociceptive effects, respectively. Similarly, the activation or inhibition of ACC-projecting OBGABA neurons reduces neuropathic pain or induces nociceptive sensitization. Taken together, our findings offer new insights into therapeutic approaches for pain relief through nasal breathing.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Neuroscience, Sensory neuroscience

Introduction

Breath modulation has emerged as a clinically significant intervention for pain management. Some studies have shown that modifying breathing patterns can alleviate pain perception,1,2,3 while also demonstrating clinical benefits in managing various conditions, including anxiety, hypertension, asthma, and other conditions.4 Despite these well-documented clinical effects, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear.

Respiratory movements can generate a regular, frequency-consistent neural rhythm, known as respiration-entrained rhythm (RR).5 RR is thought to be generated by the mechanical stimulation of olfactory sensory neurons in the nasal epithelium by gases during respiratory movements, which are then transmitted to the OB and its downstream brain regions.6 This RR persists when the odor disappears. In addition to processing olfactory information, the olfactory bulb also receives information about airflow that is not related to olfaction.7,8,9 In addition, RR has been detected in areas such as the whisker barrel cortex,10 medial prefrontal cortex,6,11 and hippocampus in rodents.12,13,14,15 When mice are subjected to nasal occlusion, tracheal intubation,10,12,14 or olfactory bulb resection,10,11,16 these oscillations are attenuated or absent. These findings suggest that the RR is modulated by respiration patterns, thus potentially serving as a mechanism for interregional neural synchronization and telematic communication between brain regions.17,18,19

Our previous studies have revealed that RR is also present in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), which is an important pain-related brain region. Many studies have demonstrated that increased neural activity in the ACC occurs in rodents and human patients with chronic pain.20,21,22 It has also been observed that nasal respiratory sensory deprivation induced by intraperitoneal injections of methimazole can reduce olfactory afferents in its downstream brain region known as the prelimbic prefrontal cortex (plPFC), thereby resulting in a decreased coherence between the OB and plPFC local field potentials (LFPs) and prolonged freezing time in mice.6 Furthermore, nasal respiratory sensory deprivation induced by nasal rinsing with zinc sulfate has been observed to attenuate pain perception in mice.23 These results suggest that the respiratory modulation of the pain threshold may be related to synchronized respiration-entrained oscillations in the OB and ACC; however, the involvement of specific neuron types and neural circuits in this antinociceptive effect is still unknown.

Using a nasal respiratory sensory deprivation model, we investigated the influence of the olfactory afferent system on neural activity in the ACC and pain perception via viral tracing, optogenetics, chemical genetics, in vivo fiber photometry, and c-Fos staining. On the basis of our findings, we propose that the reduction in pain induced by nasal airway sensory deprivation is mediated by the OBGABA–ACCGlu circuit.

Results

Olfactory epithelial injury reduces pain and impairs respiration-entrained rhythm in pain model mice

To investigate the effects of disrupting olfactory inputs on pain perception, we induced chronic constriction injury (CCI) of the right hindlimbs of C57BL/6J mice to establish a chronic neuropathic pain model, and Triton X-100 (1% saline, 70 μL/nasal side, 3 times per day for 2 days) was used to damage mouse olfactory epithelium on days 3 and 4 after CCI to establish a nasal sensory deprivation model.24 Nasal tissues were collected on days 7, 14, and 21 after CCI (Figures 1A and 1B), and immunofluorescence staining with an anti-olfactory marker protein (OMP) antibody was performed. The results revealed that the olfactory epithelium of the mice in the Triton X-100 group was thinner than that of the mice in the saline group on days 7, 14, and 21 after CCI, and the number of olfactory sensory neurons was significantly reduced (Figures 1C, S1A, and S1C).

Moreover, thermocouples were placed in the nasal cavity of the mice to record respiratory signals, and electrodes were placed in the brain regions of the OB and ACC to record LFPs (Figures 1D, S1B, and S1D). LFPs and respiratory signals in the OB and ACC were recorded at 7, 14, and 21 days after CCI. The peak power of RR (1–4 Hz) in the OB and ACC was decreased in the Triton X-100 group compared with the saline group at 7, 14, and 21 days after CCI (Figures 1E–1H, S1E, S1F, S1H, and S1I). Furthermore, the coherence of RR (1–4 Hz) between the OB and ACC was also significantly lower in the Triton X-100 group than that in the saline group (Figures 1I, 1J, S1G, and S1J). In addition, the results of a pain behavior test revealed that the mechanical pain thresholds were increased (p < 0.05) and that the thermal pain latency was longer (p < 0.05) in the CCI model mice in the Triton X-100 group than in the mice in the saline group on days 7, 14, and 21 (Figures 1K, 1L, S2C, and S2D). However, the application of Triton X-100 alone did not significantly affect the mechanical or thermal pain thresholds in the hind paws of the mice (Figures S2A and S2B), nor did it significantly affect the sensory mechanical pain thresholds in the faces of the mice (Figure S2E).

Additionally, we performed EPM and OFT behavioral tests on day 7. The results showed that the Triton X-100 group traveled a shorter total distance, spent less time in the center and open arm, and entered the open arm fewer times than did the saline group (p < 0.05). There was no significant difference observed in the CCI mice (p > 0.05). (Figures S3A and S3B). These results suggest that olfactory epithelial injury may lead to anxiety-depression-like behaviors in mice. However, in mice with neuropathic pain, olfactory epithelial injury does not exacerbate this negative mood further.

Triton X-100 damage to the mouse olfactory epithelium decreases anterior cingulate cortex neuronal activity in pain model mice

To further verify whether the activity of ACC neurons is altered in response to Triton X-100 exposure, we labeled nonspecific neurons within the ACC by infusing hsyn-Gcamp6s into the left ACC of C57BL/6J mice. CCI of the right paws of the mice was induced during 14 days of virus expression, and subthreshold von Frey filaments were applied to the right subtalar paws of the mice on day 7 after CCI surgery. Moreover fiberoptic recordings were performed (a 1.8 g von Frey filament was applied to the mouse paw) (Figures 2A–2C). The fluorescence intensity of GCaMP6-expressing neurons in the saline + CCI group was greater than that in the sham-operated group after the administration of a subthreshold mechanical stimulus. However, this increase in fluorescence intensity was reversed by nasal respiration sensory deprivation (Figures 2D–2F).

Anterior cingulate cortexGlu neurons receive inputs from olfactory bulbGABA neurons

As olfactory epithelial injury attenuates nociceptive sensitivity in mice,23 we sought to identify olfactory-related OB–ACC neural circuits that may be involved in the nasal respiratory sensory deprivation modulation of pain perception. First, we injected paracrine rAAV-DIO-hChR2-mCherry into one side of the OB of GAD2-Cre mice. Positive axons projecting from the OBGABA could be observed in the ipsilateral ACC brain region (Figures S4A and S4B). We then delivered scAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPREs viruses into the OB and a DIO-mCherry virus into the ACC (Figure 3A). After 3 weeks of virus expression, abundant mCherry fluorescence signals were visible in the ACC (Figure 3B). Notably, the costaining results revealed that mCherry+ neurons were co-localized with glutamate (90.54%) (Figures 3C and 3D). However, the types of neurons in the OB that projected to the ACC were unclear; therefore, we injected the retrogradely traced retroAAV-hSyn-CRE virus into the ACC and the CRE-dependent rAAV-DIO-mCherry virus into the OB (Figure 3E). Abundant mCherry fluorescence signals could be observed in the OB (Figure 3F), and the costaining results demonstrated that mCherry+ neurons were co-localized with GABAergic neurons (86%) (Figures 3G and 3H). Together, these results suggest that ACCGlu neurons receive signaling inputs from OBGABA neurons.

To further validate the anatomical connectivity of OBGABA neurons with ACCGlu neurons, the rAAV-DIO-hChR2-mCherry virus was injected into the left OB of GAD2-Cre mice; moreover, the rAAV-CaMKIIa-Gcamp6m virus was injected into the ipsilateral ACC, and ceramic inserts were implanted in the OB and ACC (Figures 3I and 3J). Triton X-100 increases olfactory bulbGABA neuronal activity and decreases anterior cingulate cortexGlu neuronal activity

On the basis of the above results, Triton X-100 induces a decrease in neuronal activity in the ACC, but whether this effect is mediated by OB projections to ACCGlu neurons is not clear. Therefore, we delivered scAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPREs into the OB of C57BL/6J mice and CRE-dependent glutamatergic neuron-specific labeled calcium indicators (rAAV-DIO-CaMKIIa-Gcamp6s) into the ipsilateral ACC. CCI surgery was performed on the right paws of the mice after 14 days of virus expression. On day 7 after CCI surgery, subthreshold von Frey filaments were applied to the right subtalar paws of the mice, and fiber-optic recordings were performed (Figures 4A and 4B). The results revealed that the calcium signal of ACCGlu neurons in the saline + CCI group was higher than that in the sham-operated (p < 0.05) and Triton X-100 + CCI (p < 0.05) groups (Figures 4C–4F). Furthermore, c-Fos (an early gene marker representing neuronal activity) costaining revealed a significantly lower proportion of activated glutamatergic neurons in the ACC in the Triton X-100 + CCI group than in the saline + CCI group (p < 0.05) (Figures 4G and 4H), which was consistent with the results of the fiber-optic recordings.

The olfactory bulbGABA–anterior cingulate cortexGlu circuit mediates Triton X-100 induced antinociception

There is substantial evidence that ACCGlu activation leads to nociceptive sensitization and that ACCGlu inhibition has an antinociceptive effect on the CCI pain model mice.25 To determine whether the OBGABA–ACCGlu circuit plays a role in pain perception, we delivered scAAV-hSyn-CRE virus into the bilateral OB of mice, and rAAV-CaMKIIa-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry was delivered into the bilateral ACC. The mice in the experimental group were sequentially intraperitoneally injected with the ligand CNO (control: saline) on days 19, 20, and 21 of viral expression to activate the OB–ACCGlu neural circuit (Figures 5A and 5B), and pain behavior experiments were performed on day 21. Compared with those in the saline group, the mechanical pain threshold and thermal pain latency of the mice in the experimental group were significantly lower (both p < 0.05) (Figures 5C and 5D). In addition, the retro-rAAV-hSyn-CRE virus was injected into the bilateral ACC, and the rAAV-VGAT1-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry virus was injected into the bilateral OB (Figure 5E). After 14 days of viral expression, CCI surgery was performed. CNO was intraperitoneally injected on days 5, 6, and 7 after CCI to inhibit the OBGABA-ACC circuit. Pain behavioral assays were conducted on day 7. The mechanical threshold and thermal pain latency were also lower in the CCI model mice in the experimental group than in those in the saline group (both p < 0.05) (Figures 5F and 5G); moreover, both the mechanical and thermal pain thresholds were restored 3 days after the CNO injections were terminated.

To mimic the antinociceptive effects of nasal respiratory sensory deprivation, we employed chemogenetic approaches to modulate the OBGABA–ACCGlu circuit. The scAAV-hSyn-CRE virus was injected into the bilateral OB, and the rAAV-CaMKIIa-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry virus was delivered into the bilateral ACC (Figure 5H). The experimental group was intraperitoneally injected with CNO for 3 consecutive days (following the same procedures performed in the OBGABA–ACC circuit inhibition experiment) to inhibit the OB–ACCGlu neural circuit. The results revealed that the mechanical pain threshold and thermal pain latency were increased in CCI model mice in the experimental group compared with those in the saline group (both p < 0.05) (Figures 5I and 5J). Afterward, the retro-rAAV-hSyn-CRE virus was injected into the bilateral ACC, the rAAV-VGAT1-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry virus was delivered into the bilateral OB, and the experimental group was intraperitoneally injected with CNO for 3 consecutive days (following the same procedures performed in the OBGABA–ACC circuit inhibition experiment) to activate the OBGABA–ACC neural circuit (Figure 5K). Both the mechanical pain threshold and thermal pain latency were greater in the CCI model mice in the experimental group than in those in the saline group (both p < 0.05) (Figures 5L and 5M), and this antinociceptive effect was abolished within 3 days following CNO withdrawal.

Inhibition of olfactory bulbGABA neurons reverses the antinociceptive effect of Triton X-100

To identify the role of OBGABA neurons in alleviating the chronic neuropathic pain caused by Triton X-100 exposure, we inhibited OBGABA neurons by using a chemical genetic technique. The rAAV-VGAT1-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry virus was bilaterally delivered into the OB of each mouse. After 14 days of viral expression, CCI surgery was performed, and Triton X-100 was administered on postoperative days 3 and 4. CNO was intraperitoneally injected on days 5–7 after CCI (control: saline) (Figures 6A and 6B), and a pain behavior test was performed. Compared with saline treatment, the chemogenetic inhibition of OBGABA neurons resulted in decreased mechanical and thermal pain thresholds in CCI model mice (both p < 0.05) (Figures 6C and 6D).

After 14 days of viral expression, electrodes were implanted into the OB and ACC of the mice, and temperature-sensitive electrodes were placed in the nasal cavity. The mice were then allowed to recover from surgery for 7 days. Discussion

Pain relief through respiratory modulation has been widely demonstrated in clinical studies. Some studies have also shown that nasal respiratory sensory deprivation can lower the pain threshold in mice, although the underlying neural mechanisms underlying this effect have not been fully explored. In the present study, we used Triton X-100 to establish a model of nasal respiratory sensory deprivation and investigated the mechanisms by which nasal respiration regulates pain perception. The results identified a previously undescribed OBGABA–ACCGlu neural circuit that mediates the antinociceptive effects of Triton X-100-induced nasal respiratory sensory deprivation. We observed that Triton X-100-induced injury to the olfactory epithelium resulted in a reduction in RR (1–4 Hz) power within the OB and ACC, along with a decreased OB–ACC coherence and elevated pain thresholds in CCI model mice. Moreover, Triton X-100 increased GABAergic neuronal activity in the OB while decreasing glutamatergic neuronal activity in the ACC. Furthermore, activation or inhibition of the OB–ACCGlu neural circuit resulted in either nociceptive sensitization or antinociceptive effects. In addition, the inhibition of OBGABA neurons resulted in an increase in the peak power of RR (1–4 Hz) and improved the coherence of the OB and ACC, suggesting that OBGABA neurons play a critical role in the generation of RR.

The OB is the primary center of the olfactory pathway and receives afferent fibers from the olfactory nerves. Its neuronal layers form complex synapses,27 with mitral and tufted cells serving as the main projection neurons.28,29 These cells receive direct excitatory inputs from OSNs and inhibitory inputs from granule cells.28 Studies have demonstrated that preventing nasal airflow over the olfactory epithelium disrupts rhythmic discharges in mitral and tufted cells and attenuates the lateral inhibition of mitral cells. The OB also receives input from several brain regions, including the anterior olfactory nucleus, pyriform cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus, to modulate olfactory processing during different behavioral states, as well as to modulate odor perception,30 learning, and memory.31,32,33,34 The silencing of GABAergic outputs to the OB impairs fine odor discrimination.35 Although most studies have focused on olfactory perception, our results demonstrate that the OB also plays a role in pain modulation, which has not yet been unexplored.

Previous studies have shown that injury to the olfactory epithelium of mice with zinc sulfate reduces nociceptive sensitivity.23 In the present study, we found that Triton X-100 causes localized injury to the nasal mucosa, which increases pain thresholds in CCI model mice in a similar manner. Additionally, we used Triton X-100 to damage the olfactory epithelia of mice in open-field and elevated cross-maze experiments. The results demonstrated that Triton X-100 may induce anxiety and depression-like behaviors, which is consistent with previous results.23 However, this olfactory injury did not further exacerbate these negative behavioral phenotypes in neuropathic pain model mice. Although the relationship between pain and mood remains controversial, our results effectively exclude the possibility that the Triton-induced elevation of pain thresholds is primarily mediated by anxiety- and depression-like behavioral states. However, reducing the mechanical stimulation of OSNs in the olfactory epithelium weakened RR in the OB, which in turn reduced the RR afferent to the ACC. Low-frequency oscillations serve as long-range communication carriers, and the decrease in neural synchrony between the OB and ACC may explain why Triton X-100 decreased pain perception. Additionally, we observed a link between OBGABA and glutamate in the ACC. Thus, nasal respiratory sensory deprivation may relieve pain by activating GABAergic neurons in the OB, which inhibits glutamatergic activity in the ACC, thereby ultimately altering RR. Some studies have shown that the olfactory bulbs were examined using immunohistochemistry to detect potential damage caused by Triton X-100. Immunoreactivity for Iba-1 (a marker for microglia/macrophages36) in the olfactory nerve fiber and glomerulus layers was significantly enhanced in exposed mice by day 7 when compared with normal controls.24 The apical dendrites of mitral cells extend into the olfactory glomeruli and form glutamatergic synapses with the axon terminals of olfactory receptor neurons (ORNs).37 When cells in the glomerular layer of the olfactory bulb are excited, neurons in the mitral cell layer are usually also directly excited. The released glutamatergic neurons then excite the granule cell layer,38 which is predominantly GABAergic, consistent with our results. Furthermore, the role of GABAergic neurons (as the main inhibitory neurons in the granule cell layer39) in RR regulation remains unclear.

Respiration has been shown to transmit rhythmic neural activity to brain regions beyond the olfactory system, including the whisker barrel cortex, medial prefrontal cortex, ACC, and hippocampus in rodents.5 These oscillations were attenuated or eliminated when the mice were subjected to nasal airflow block or inhibition of the OB. RR in these regions is modulated by respiration and contributes to communication between distant brain areas.40 In addition to its role in olfactory processing, RR is involved in emotional and cognitive regulation.41,42 Therefore, studying RR production and regulation in the OB is essential for understanding its role in distant brain network activity. In our study, we inhibited GABAergic neurons in the OB via a chemogenetic technique, which led to an increased peak power of RR (1–4 Hz) in the OB and ACC and increased the coherence of the OB and ACC. However, the chemogenetic activation of GABAergic neurons in the OB did not significantly affect the peak power of the RR (1–4 Hz) or OB-ACC coherence in these brain regions. The possible reasons for these findings are as follows: First, the LFP presentation is the result of the pooled neuronal activity in the region. Chemical genetic activation of OBGABA neurons may influence the neural network activity in the region, as well as the activity of other neurons, such as those in the olfactory glomerular and mitral cell layers.26 Second, RR is thought to be generated by the mechanical stimulation of olfactory sensory neurons in the nasal epithelium by gas during respiratory movements. Although airflow input to the mouse olfactory epithelium is present, if the effect of gas input on RR generation outweighs the effects produced by modulating GABAergic neurons, the activation of OBGABA neurons may not appear to significantly differ in power from RR. Further studies are required to investigate this phenomenon.

The ACC is an important brain region related to pain. It is activated during both acute and chronic pain and shows sustained hyperactivation.43,44 The ACC is closely associated with pain-related negative emotions, such as unpleasantness, fear, and anxiety.45,46,47 In animal models of chronic pain, ACC-related anxiety and depressive behaviors have been reported to be driven by sustained hyperactivity in the ACC. The ACC modulates aversive pain behaviors by projecting to the nucleus ambiguus (NAc) and the ventral tegmental area (VTA).48 In addition to receiving inputs from the thalamus, noxious stimuli are also transmitted to the ACC via the amygdala, and there is a connection between the central nucleus of the amygdala and the lateral parabrachial nucleus, which contains oprm1 neurons encoding the μ-opioid receptor gene that is involved in the regulation of respiration and pain.49 The ACC also receives nociceptive inputs from the cortex and other pain-related brain regions, such as the primary sensory cortex (S1),50,51 the insular cortex, and the periaqueductal gray (PAG)52,53 in the middle insular aqueduct. These findings suggest a potential association between pain and various aspects of feeding,54 sensory processing, and emotional experience.55 In addition, peripheral nerve injury-induced spinal cord vulnerability contributes to chronic neuropathic pain development. ACC inhibition has been observed to reduce spinal spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) in spared nerve injury (SNI) model rats,56 thus suggesting that the suppression of the ACC–spinal cord pathway alleviates neuropathic pain in rodents. The observed effects of olfactory epithelial injury on ACC activity may be mediated through descending spinal pathways, thereby potentially reducing spinal synaptic transmission and consequently attenuating pain. However, this mechanism requires further investigation. Our experimental results suggest that the activation of OB projections to ACCGlu neurons also leads to nociceptive sensitization and that the inhibition of this circuit attenuates the pain threshold in CCI model mice. Notably, the activity of glutamatergic neurons within the ACC leads to the output of different behavioral outcomes.25,54,55,57 Importantly, GABAergic neurons in the OB that are connected to the ACC are involved in pain modulation. Specifically, the chemogenetic inhibition of OB GABA neuronal inputs to this loop resulted in decreased pain thresholds in CCI mice, whereas the activation of this loop produced an antinociceptive effect.

Taken together, Nasal respiratory sensory deprivation attenuates pain perception in CCI model mice, which may be mediated by the OBGABA–ACCGlu neural circuit through a decreased coherence of RR between the OB and ACC. These findings suggest that the olfactory epithelium is involved in the modulation of pain perception in mice and that respiratory movements may modulate pain perception by modulating the OBGABA–ACCGlu circuit, which in turn alters the neural rhythms of OB afferents to the ACC.

Limitations of the study

However, several limitations of this study need to be further explored. First, the mitral cell layer of the OB consists primarily of glutamatergic projection neurons that receive input from OSNs, while reciprocal input occurs between the various layers of the OB. The role of olfactory bulb projection neurons in regulating pain perception within this circuit and their function in regulating RR remain unclear. Second, the AON is part of the primary olfactory cortex and is one of the first cortical stations to receive olfactory information after it leaves the olfactory bulb. The GCL receives direct centrifugal projections from structures such as the AON, anterior piriform cortex (APC), and horizontal diagonal band of Broca (HDB). Whether the AON functions as an intermediate brain region and whether an internal circuit within the ACC participates in these projections remains to be investigated. Third, the specific mechanisms by which Triton X-100 modulates granule cell activity through olfactory sensory neurons and interferes with olfactory function, as well as the molecular pathways that regulate anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in mice, remain unclear. Finally, the reason why the activation of GABAergic neurons in the olfactory bulb does not affect the peak power of RR remains unclear.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experimental animals

Male 8–12-week-old C57BL/6J mice, weighing 22–28 g were purchased from Jiangsu Jicui Pharmachem Biotechnology Company (Nanjing, China), and GAD2-Cre mice were purchased from Shulaibao Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). The offspring of GAD2-Cre mice crossed with female C57BL/6J mice presented no obvious physiological or behavioral abnormalities. The mice were housed 5 per cage at a comfortable temperature (22°C–24°C) and 50–60% humidity on a 12-hour day/night cycle, with free access to food and water. Laboratory animal handling and husbandry practices were strictly followed. The mice were acclimatized to the laboratory environment for at least 1 week before the start of the experiment. The animal protocol for this experiment was approved by the Standing Committee on Animals of Anhui Medical University (Protocol No. LLSC20232174).

Modeling neuropathic pain

The mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane for chronic constriction injury (CCI) surgery. Specifically, an approximately 1.5 cm incision was made in the skin of the right thigh, and the muscles of the middle thigh were gently separated to expose the sciatic nerve. An approximately 5 mm section of the trunk of the sciatic nerve before branching was isolated. Then, it was loosely ligated four times with a nonabsorbable 5-0 suture so that the toes slightly twitched, with an interval of 1 mm between each ligature. At the end of surgery, the skin was stitched and disinfected with iodophor. The mice were placed on a heating pad to maintain body temperature during surgery and were returned to their home cage when they regained consciousness after surgery. The mice in the sham-operated group underwent the same procedure except for sciatic nerve ligation.

Establishment of a nasal respiratory sensory deprivation model

Triton X-100 was diluted to 1% with saline, and the solution (70 μl on each side) was instilled into the bilateral nasal cavities of the mice in the experimental group using a microsyringe three times a day for two days. The heads of the mice were gently shaken so that sufficient liquid flowed into the nasal cavity. The solution was administered slowly to avoid causing aspiration pneumonia, which could lead to death. In the control group, an equal volume of saline was administered.

Method details

Measurement of nociceptive thresholds in mice

Von frey test

The mice were habituated to metal grids in Plexiglas cages 1 hour per day for 3 days. After 30 min of acclimatization prior to pain measurement and after the mice were quiet, the paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) of each mouse was assessed using an electromechanical von Frey hypersensitivity tester; the filament forces (in grams) were chosen based on the range of pain thresholds of the mice, and the filament was applied from the smallest force to the largest force. The tip of each polypropylene filament was applied perpendicularly to the central region of the right hind paw, and the pressure was gradually increased. The value displayed by the pain meter when the mouse withdrew its hind paw or licked its foot was recorded. Facial sensory withdrawal thresholds in mice were assessed using an electromechanical von Frey hypersensitivity tester by placing the tip of a polypropylene filament perpendicular to the whisker pad area of the mouse's right test face and then gradually increasing the pressure. The minimum force that triggered flinching, face-scratching, or avoidance responses was recorded. The measurement was repeated three times, with a 5-minute interval between each measurement, and the average of the three measurements was used for statistical analysis.

Hargreaves test

Paw withdrawal latency was measured using a Hargreaves nociceptometer (IITC Life Science Inc., USA). The mice were placed on a thermally conductive glass plate (temperature 34°C) in a Plexiglas cage containing a thermal stimulator and acclimated to the glass plate 1 h per day for 3 days. The cutoff time was 25 s to prevent damage to the plantar tissues of the mice. The mice were placed in the test environment 30 min before testing for acclimatization. The removable thermal stimulator was then positioned on the center of the mouse’s right hind paw, and a button was pressed to initiate the test. When a mouse exhibited an avoidance reflex, such as paw withdrawal or foot licking, the response time was recorded as the PWL. Each mouse was tested at least three times, with a 5-minute interval between measurements, and the average threshold was calculated.

Behavioral procedures

For all behavioral tests, mice were moved to the testing room on the day of the examination to allow them at least one hour to acclimate to the indoor environment, during which they had unrestricted access to food and water. To reduce the animals' anxiety, the room was illuminated with soft, dim lighting. The same mouse was only used once in the same experiment.

Open-field test (OFT)

The mice were tested in an open field to measure their general locomotor activity and anxiety behavior. Under dim light, each mouse was gently placed in an open box (50 × 50 × 50 cm), which was invisibly divided into nine small squares. The mice were placed in the same area at the start of the experiment and allowed to move freely for five minutes. Their movements were recorded using AnyMaze software (Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL, USA), and the following parameters were measured: total distance moved and time spent in the center.

Elevated plus-maze test (EPM)

The EPM was used to test for anxiety-related behavior in mice. After the open field test, the mice were placed in the center of an elevated maze. Their heads and upper limbs were randomly placed in the open arms. Then, the mice were allowed to move freely on the elevated platform for 5 minutes. Their behavior was evaluated using AnyMaze software (Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL, USA), and the following parameters were recorded: number of entries into the open arms and time spent in them.

Immunohistochemistry, imaging and image analysis

The mice were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg kg-1) and slowly perfused transcardially with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 7.4) and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution. The mouse brains were removed on ice, placed in 4% PFA solution at 4°C overnight, and then dehydrated in 15% and 30% sucrose solutions to isotonicity. The frozen brains were sliced into 10 um or 40 um coronal sections at −20°C by using a cryostat (Leica, Germany). The sections were washed once with PBS for 10 min. After washing, the slides were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min and then washed three times with PBS for 5 min each, after which the washed slides were immersed in citrate antigen repair buffer and placed in a thermostatic water bath at 95°C for 10 min. Then, the slides were removed and rewarmed at room temperature for 1 hour. The slides were then naturally air-dried until the surface of the brain slices was slightly wet, and a drop of 5% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100 PBS solution was added to the slides for permeabilization and blocking for 1 hour. Finally, the brain slices were transferred to PBS containing 5% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100 and anti-glutamate (1:200, Sigma, US) , anti-GABA (1:200, Sigma, US) or anti-c-Fos (1:200, Santa Cruz, US) primary antibodies , after which they were incubated at 4 °C overnight. After primary antibody incubation, the brain slices were rewarmed at room temperature for 1 h, and then washed 4–5 times with PBS (10 min each). They were then incubated for 90 min in the dark at room temperature in PBS containing 5% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100, and Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:500, Abcam, UK) , Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:500, Abcam, UK) , or Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:500, Abcam, UK). After incubation, the slices were washed 4–5 times with PBS (10 min each) and finally mounted with an antifade reagent containing DAPI.

The nasal tissues were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, after which they were incubated with 2% BSA in PBS at 37°C for 1 hour. After blocking, they were incubated with primary antibody (anti-OMP antibody, 1:200, Abcam, UK) in PBS containing 2% BSA followed by Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:200, Abcam, UK) in PBS containing 2% BSA for 50 min at 37°C in the dark. The remaining steps were the same as those for the brain tissue immunofluorescence staining protocol. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM880 (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) or fluorescence microscope (cat. no. IX71; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and processed using ZEN software (Carl Zeiss) and a panoramic histocellular quantitative analysis system (TG, Austria). Further analyses, including cell counting and colocalization analysis, were performed in a blinded manner using ImageJ software (Fiji edition, NIH).

Stereotaxic virus injections

The mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (20 mg kg-1, intraperitoneal injection) and secured in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). A heating pad was used to maintain body temperature. A median scalp incision was made to expose the cranial surface, and the length of the incision was determined according to the surgical site. The virus was backfilled into a drawn glass microinjector, which was connected to a microinjection pump (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). Owing to the different titers and expression abilities of the viruses, the injection volume ranged from 200–300 nL, and the drip rate was 35 nL min-1. After injection, the glass microinjector was left in place for 10 min and then slowly withdrawn to allow sufficient diffusion of the virus. The coordinates for OB injection were 4.5 mm anteroposterior (AP), ±0.8 mm mediolateral (ML), and −1.0 mm dorsoventral (DV), those for ACC injection were 1.98 mm AP, ±0.35 mm ML, and −1.7 mm DV, and those for nasal administration were 11.0 mm AP and −0.5 mm ML.

For anterograde tracing of neurocircuits, scAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPREs (250 nL) were injected into the left OB of C57BL/6J mice, and the CRE-dependent virus rAAV-EF1a-DIO-mCherry (250 nL) was injected into the ipsilateral ACC. rAAV-EF1a-DIO-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry (250 nL) virus was injected into the left OB region of GAD2-Cre mice. For retrograde tracing, retro-rAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPRE-hGH polyA (250 nL) was injected into the left ACC, and the CRE-dependent virus rAAV-EF1a-DIO-mCherry (250 nL) was injected into the ipsilateral OB.

For the fiber-optic photometry experiments, the nonneuronal calcium indicator rAAV-hSyn-GCaMp6s-WPRE-hGH polyA (250 nL) was injected into the left ACC of the mice; scAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPREs (250 nL) was injected into the left OB; the CRE-dependent glutamatergic neuron-specific calcium indicator rAAV-CaMKIIa-DIO-GCaMp6s-WPRE-hGH polyA (250 nL) was injected into the ipsilateral ACC; CCI of the right hind limb of each mouse was induced after two weeks of viral expression; and fiber-optic recordings were made after three weeks of viral expression.

For the optogenetic experiments, rAAV-EF1a-DIO-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry (250 nL) virus was injected into the left OB region of GAD2-Cre mice, and rAAV-CaMKIIa-GCaMP6m (250 nL) virus was injected into the ipsilateral ACC region. The viruses were allowed to express proteins for three weeks to carry out optogenetic manipulations and fiber-optic recording.

For the chemogenetic experiments, scAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPREs (250 nL) were injected into the bilateral OB of each mouse, and rAAV-CaMKIIa-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (250 nL) was injected into the bilateral ACC to inhibit the OB-ACCGlu circuit. For activation of OB–ACCGlu circuits, scAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPREs were injected into the bilateral OB of each mouse, and rAAV-CaMKIIa-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (250 nL) was injected into the bilateral ACC. To inhibit OBGABA-ACC circuits, retro-rAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPRE-hGH polyA (250 nL) was injected into the bilateral ACC of each mouse, and rAAV-VGAT1-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (250 nL) was injected into the bilateral OB. To activate the OBGABA-ACC circuits, retro-rAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPRE-hGH polyA (250 nL) was injected into the bilateral ACC of each mouse, and rAAV-VGAT1-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (250 nL) was injected into the bilateral OB. To inhibit OBGABA neurons, rAAV-VGAT1-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (300 nL) was injected into the bilateral OB of each mouse. The CCI model was established after 14 days of virus expression. The experimental group received continuous intraperitoneal injections of CNO (2.5 mg kg-1) twice daily at 6-hour intervals on days 5, 6 and 7 after CCI or on days 19, 20 and 21 of viral expression. In the control group, an equal volume of saline was injected intraperitoneally.

For LFP recording, rAAV-VGAT1-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (300 nL) and rAAV-VGAT1-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (300 nL) were injected into the bilateral OB of each mouse, and the viruses were allowed to express for 14 days. Electrodes were implanted in the left OB and ACC, and LFPs were recorded on day 21 before CNO injection, 30 min after intraperitoneal injection of CNO, and 6 h after the effect of CNO was abolished.

In vivo fiber photometry recording

After 3 weeks of virus expression, the mice were acclimated to a test chamber for 3 days for PWT testing or optogenetic recording. The mice were then anesthetized with isoflurane, and fiber optic patch cords were connected to the implanted ceramic inserts (200 μm OD, 0.37 NA, Thinkertech, Nanjing, China), and the mice were returned to their home cages for at least 30 min. After the mice were acclimated, a subthreshold (1.8 g) von Frey filament was applied to the right paw using an electromechanical supersensitizer (the threshold was chosen on the basis of the results of the preliminary pain measurements); the stimulus was applied five times at 5-minute intervals. Fiber photometry was performed, and fluorescence signals were captured in real time. LED light (480 nm, Lumileds, Shanghai, China; 405 nm, Lumileds, Shanghai, China) was reflected by a dichroic mirror (Edmund Optics, Barrington, United States) and focused through an objective lens (20X, NA0.4, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The laser power was adjusted to 40–70 μW to minimize bleaching. The fluorescence signals were bandpass filtered (87753, Edmund Optics, Barrington, United States) and detected with a photomultiplier tube (H10721, Hamamatsu, Shizuoka, Japan). An amplifier was used to convert the photomultiplier tube current output to voltage, which was further filtered through a low-pass filter (35 Hz cut-off; Thinkertech, Nanjing, China). Finally, the recorded signals were imported into MATLAB (R2022b, MA, USA) for analysis. The change in fluorescence intensity (ΔF/F) was calculated as ΔF/F (%) = (F(duration) − F(baseline))/F(baseline) × 100%, and the means were used for statistical analysis. The signals obtained 2 s before von Frey filament application and 5 s before light stimulation were defined as the baseline signals.

Optogenetic and chemogenetic manipulation

Optogenetic and chemogenetic manipulations were performed after 3 weeks of virus expression. The mice were acclimated to the test room 1 hour per day for 3 days prior to testing. The mice were then anesthetized with isoflurane, an external fiber optical fiber was used to connect the optogenetic light source to an implant in the head of the mouse (200 μm OD, 0.37 NA, Thinker Tech), and the mice were returned to their home cage to acclimate for at least 30 min. Pulsed optogenetic activation experiments (473 nm, 3 Hz, 5–8 mW, 5 ms pulses) were performed by delivering a laser (Class IIIB, RWD Life Science) through the fiber optic insert.

For chemogenetic manipulation, the chemical ligand CNO (CNO, Enzo, USA) was injected intraperitoneally into mice anesthetized with isoflurane. Behavioral tests were then performed at least 30 min later. For controls, an equal volume of saline was injected intraperitoneally. After all the behavioral tests were completed, the mice were sacrificed to verify the viral injection site and the optic nerve fiber site. Data from mice whose target brain regions were missing were excluded from our analysis.

Multichannel electrophysiology recording

In vivo recordings were performed by using multichannel electrodes. The electrode wires consisted of a nickel–titanium alloy. After inserting the headstage into the mouse brain, the animals were returned to their home cages and acclimated for 30 minutes. To minimize noise and 50 Hz industrial interference, an external grounding wire was connected to a nearby metal object, and unnecessary instruments were disconnected. LFP signals were collected using a sampling frequency of 1 kHz and continuous sampling. The signals were transmitted to a computer through multi-stage amplification and digital-to-analog conversion and collected through the OmniPlex server and Plexon in vivo multichannel for online sorting and referencing. LFPs were recorded during resting and awake states and analyzed for 30 seconds per sample. For further processing, data were filtered by using NeuroExplore software with a Gaussian kernel (width = 3) and a 50% window overlap. The frequency range was restricted to 0–12 Hz for visualization.

To record respiratory signals, we implanted a temperature-sensitive electrode into one of the mouse's nasal cavities. Specifically, we first drilled a small hole above the nasal cavity using a cranial drill bit, then gently inserted the thermocouple tip into the nasal cavity (typically to a depth of 1–2 mm), ensuring the sensing site was fully exposed to airflow. The thermocouple detected temperature fluctuations in the nasal airflow and converted them into electrical signals representing respiratory activity. The corresponding statistical methods are detailed in the Statistical analysis section.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All the groups of mice were randomly divided into groups for pain threshold experiments, olfactory epithelial injury model establishment, fiber optic recordings, optogenetic experiments, and chemogenetic experiments. Graphing and data analysis were performed using Origin 2024 b, NeuroExplorer 5x64, GraphPad Prism 8, MATLAB R2022b and ImageJ. The Shapiro-Wilk tests was used to determine whether the data conformed to a normal distribution. Statistical analyses of data from both groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney test; statistical analyses of pain thresholds were performed with two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni's multiple comparison post hoc test; and statistical analyses of LFP power spectral density (PSD) and coherence data were performed with Unpaired two-sample t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, Friedman test, and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc test. Behavioral data were analyzed using Welch's ANOVA or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. All the data are presented as the means ± standard errors. Significant differences in statistical results were defined as follows: when there was no significant difference, p > 0.05; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
