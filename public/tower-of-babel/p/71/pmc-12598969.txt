Introduction

Palliative care and end-of-life care in the intensive care unit (ICU) are crucial in improving critical patients’ quality of life and supporting their families in the medical decision-making process.1,5 Quality indicators (QIs) are essential tools to evaluate various aspects of care, including service accessibility, communication between medical personnel and families, pain and symptom management, and psychosocial and spiritual aspects of patients.6,8 This systematic review aims to fill this evidence gap by identifying and analysing the characteristics of QIs used in palliative and end-of-life care for critically ill ICU patients.

Although various models of palliative care have been developed,9,12 no universal consensus exists on the most effective for assessing indicators for assessing end-of-life care quality in the ICU. Differences in health systems, cultural values and policy frameworks across countries further influence the implementation of these indicators. International efforts, such as the worldwide end-of-life practice for patients in intensive care units consensus and the Delphi study by Zegers et al,13 14 have attempted to define core domains—including symptom management, communication, spiritual support and shared decision-making—and establish key indicators emphasising patient-centredness, interdisciplinary collaboration and timely recognition of dying. These initiatives provide a foundation for evaluating and comparing QIs across healthcare systems but highlight the ongoing need for context-sensitive and comprehensive assessment frameworks, particularly relevant for low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).

Several studies have highlighted the importance of QIs in improving palliative and end-of-life care in ICUs. These indicators are generally categorised into three types based on the Donabedian model: structure, process and outcome. However, most existing QIs disproportionately focus on processes, while outcome-based and spiritual care indicators are often underrepresented.15 16 Additionally, many lack psychometric validation and show inconsistencies across healthcare contexts. These limitations hinder the widespread adoption and comparability of QIs. Therefore, a systematic review is warranted to consolidate existing evidence, assess methodological robustness and identify gaps, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Palliative care services in Indonesia exemplify the challenges commonly faced by LMICs. In Indonesia, such services in ICUs remain limited and are generally available only in referral hospitals, as regulated by the Minister of Health Regulation No. HK.01.07/MENKES/2180/2023.17 Palliative care includes early identification, comprehensive symptom assessment, pain management and psychosocial and spiritual support delivered through a multidisciplinary approach.18,25 However, its implementation is hindered by limited funding, a shortage of trained healthcare personnel and unequal distribution of facilities. These challenges are further compounded by Indonesia’s lower-middle-income status, with a Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita of about US$ 4876 in 2024.26 Limited healthcare budgets and inadequate insurance coverage for palliative care further restrict patient access, particularly in remote areas.

Although this review adopts a global perspective, particular attention is given to LMICs—including Indonesia—as they face similar systemic barriers and a disproportionate burden of critical illness. An estimated 80% of individuals with life-threatening conditions live in LMICs, and many deaths from ICU-related conditions such as sepsis occur in these regions.27 Therefore, identifying feasible and context-sensitive QIs is essential to strengthen palliative and end-of-life care in resource-limited settings.

This study aims to identify and analyse the characteristics of palliative care and end-of-life care QIs applied in the care of critical patients in the ICU. Using a systematic approach, this study is expected to provide more precise insights into the most relevant and effective indicators. The results of this study are expected to contribute to the development of better palliative care standards in the ICU, as well as become the basis for more targeted health policies in improving the quality of life of critical patients.

This review builds on previous works that have proposed or piloted QIs for palliative care in the ICU. While these studies made significant contributions, they were often limited in scope—either focusing on single-country settings, specific conditions such as cardiovascular or surgical patients, or lacking structured indicators appraisal frameworks. In contrast, our review systematically synthesises global evidence using both the Donabedian model of quality (DMQ)28 to classify indicators and the appraisal of indicators through research and evaluation (AIRE) tool to assess methodological rigour.29 Additionally, we aim to include studies up to March 2025, capturing the most recent advancements in this evolving field. This broader and more structured approach is expected to generate a comprehensive and context-sensitive set of indicators relevant to resource-limited settings like LMICs.

While previous studies have provided valuable contributions to the development of QIs in ICU-based palliative care—often within specific contexts such as cardiovascular or surgical ICUs and primarily in high-income countries12 16 30—this review expands the scope conceptually and methodologically. It incorporates literature published up to March 2025 and applies the Donabedian Model to classify indicators into structure, process and outcome domains. Moreover, it critically appraises the methodological rigour of the indicators using the AIRE tool. This review also addresses several persistent gaps in the literature, including the underrepresentation of outcome-based, spiritual and emotional care indicators; the lack of psychometric validation; limited stakeholder involvement and challenges adapting existing indicators to LMIC settings. By integrating these dimensions, this review aims to generate a comprehensive, context-sensitive synthesis that supports developing and implementing relevant QIs, particularly in resource-constrained environments such as Indonesia.

Methods and analysis

This systematic review uses the Systematic Review, which follows the Preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis (PRISMA) 2020 Guidelines.31 This study states that the Preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis protocols (PRISMA-P) checklist was used in the writing of the protocol.32 A comprehensive literature search will be conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar. Studies published up to 30 March 2025, will be included. The quality of indicators will be assessed using the AIRE tool,29 and the characteristics of QIs will be analysed using the DMQ.33 34

Studies will be included if they focus on QIs for palliative and end-of-life care in adult ICU settings. Studies focusing on paediatric or neonatal ICU (NICU) populations, or those not explicitly examining QIs, will be excluded.

The primary outcome is the identification and classification of palliative and end-of-life care QIs in adult ICU settings. Secondary outcomes include the evaluation of these indicators based on structure, process and outcome domains. Data synthesis will consist of thematic analysis to identify common patterns across studies.

This research aims to answer the following question: what are the characteristics of palliative and end-of-life care QIs in critical patients in the ICU based on structure, process and outcome approaches? This systematic review aims to identify and analyse the characteristics of QIs in palliative and end-of-life care for critically ill ICU patients.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria for this systematic review were determined using the population, intervention, comparison and outcome (PICO) framework to ensure a focused and comprehensive analysis. The population includes critically ill adult patients receiving palliative and end-of-life care in adult ICUs and healthcare professionals such as physicians, nurses and palliative care specialists involved in their care. The intervention focuses on studies that identify, assess and evaluate QIs for palliative and end-of-life care, particularly those categorised under the DMQ,28 35 which includes structure, process and outcome indicators. This systematic review does not require a direct comparison group but will analyse variations in QI characteristics across different studies, healthcare settings and regions. The outcome of interest is identifying and classifying palliative and end-of-life care QIs in adult ICU settings, highlighting frequently discussed domains such as staff capacity building, ethical considerations, legal frameworks and spiritual approaches.

This review excludes studies that focus on general palliative care outside the ICU, do not specifically examine QIs, or involve paediatric and NICU populations. Additionally, studies consisting of conference abstracts, opinion pieces, letters to editors, unpublished dissertations and those relying solely on secondary data without primary research on QIs are also excluded. These criteria ensure that only high-quality, relevant studies are included in this systematic review, allowing for a rigorous evaluation of palliative and end-of-life care QIs in adult ICU settings (table 1).

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search will be conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, ProQuest, EBSCOhost, Scopus, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect and BASE. To include unpublished materials and grey literature, an additional search will be performed using Google Scholar, employing the exact keywords as those used in other database searches. Following the guidelines of Haddaway et al, the Google Scholar search will focus on the first 300 most relevant articles related to this research topic.36 This approach ensures comprehensive literature coverage and guarantees the inclusion of all pertinent sources of this systematic review.

Search strategy

Two researchers will independently conduct literature searches and screen articles using predefined keywords. The literature search strategy will follow the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) 2015 guidelines37 to ensure accuracy and reliability. The search strings were independently peer-reviewed by both researchers based on the PRESS checklist to enhance the comprehensiveness and precision of the queries. The checklist includes the following components: (1) Translation of the research question (PICO), (2) Use of Boolean and proximity operators (AND, OR, NOT), (3) Application of subject headings (eg, Medical Subject Headings), (4) Inclusion of free-text terms, (5) Consideration of spelling, grammar and line structure and (6) Identification of potential search limitations (eg, Title/Abstract filtering).

In PubMed and MEDLINE, the search terms are based on the PICO framework, where the population includes critically ill patients in ICU settings. Intervention and control are not restricted, and the outcome focuses on QIs related to palliative and end-of-life care. To identify relevant studies, a combination of MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to palliative care, end-of-life care, QIs and intensive care is used. These terms are systematically combined using the Boolean operator ‘OR’ within each category and ‘AND’ between categories to refine the search strategy.

A full draft search strategy for the PubMed/MEDLINE database is presented below, following PRISMA-P (point 10):

(‘Critical Care’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Critical Care Outcomes’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Critical Illness’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Intensive Care Units’(Title/Abstract)) AND (‘Quality Indicators, healthcare’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Health Status Indicators’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Index, Health Status’(Title/Abstract)) AND (‘Death’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Terminal Care’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Palliative Care’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Supportive Care’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Cares, Supportive’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Care, Supportive’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Palliative Treatments’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Treatment, Palliative’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Treatments, Palliative’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Palliative Therapy’(Title/Abstract) OR ‘Therapy, Palliative’(Title/Abstract)).

Filters applied: humans, Adults (19+)—last search conducted on 30 March 2025. Selection process

The first phase of the article selection process involves a literature search conducted using PubMed, followed by searches in additional databases (ProQuest, EBSCOhost, Scopus, Google Scholar, Science Direct and BASE) based on each database’s specific criteria and restrictions. After a comprehensive literature search across all databases, the retrieved articles will be uploaded into Rayyan management software for processing, allowing for efficient organisation, screening and selection of relevant studies. A duplication check will be conducted to identify and remove redundant entries. Once duplicates are eliminated, the first author will screen the titles, abstracts and language based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

In the second phase, articles deemed potentially relevant will undergo a thorough full-text review to determine their eligibility for inclusion. The selection process will follow the pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure consistency with the research objectives. The first, second and third authors will independently screen the full-text articles and assess their quality using The AIRE tools.29 The AIRE framework consists of four key domains: purpose, relevance and organisational context. It assesses whether the purpose of each indicator is clearly defined and relevant to the research objectives. It also examines the organisational context in which the indicator is applied and ensures alignment with the quality domains in healthcare. Stakeholder involvement evaluates the extent to which relevant stakeholders, including researchers, clinicians and policymakers, have been involved in the development and validation of the indicators, ensuring that multiple perspectives are considered in the systematic review process. Scientific evidence examines whether the indicators are supported by robust scientific evidence, including an evaluation of the systematic methods used to gather evidence, the presence of evidence-based guidelines and the critical appraisal of supporting literature. Additional Evidence, Formulation and Usage focuses on the technical formulation and practical application of the indicators, assessing the clarity of numerator and denominator definitions, the validity and reliability of the indicators and the feasibility of data collection and interpretation.

If an article does not meet the inclusion criteria, it will be excluded, with the reasons for exclusion documented in the systematic review. Multiple screening and extraction techniques will be employed to ensure accuracy, using Rayyan management software to facilitate a structured and transparent selection process. In cases where discrepancies arise between reviewers, they will be resolved through discussion. If a consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will provide an independent assessment.

This systematic review will include studies published in all languages that meet the inclusion criteria. No restrictions will be set on the starting publication year as long as the database can retrieve the articles, ensuring that all relevant studies are considered until 30 March 2025.

Critical appraisal

The article was conducted using the AIRE tool to systematically assess the methodology used in determining QIs in research. This approach ensures a structured and objective evaluation of the study’s quality and reliability.29 In this study, the strength of the evidence will be assessed using the AIRE tools.

The AIRE tool consists of 20 items categorised into four key domains: (1) Purpose, Relevance and Organisational Context, (2) Stakeholder Engagement, (3) Scientific Evidence and (4) Additional Evidence, Formulation and Usage. Each item is rated using a four-point Likert scale, where (1) Represents ‘Strongly Disagree’ or ‘No Information Available’, (2) Indicates ‘Disagree/Unsure’ if Criteria Are Met, (3) Denotes ‘Agree’ and (4) Signifies ‘Strongly Agree’. This scale allows for a standardised assessment of QI eligibility based on predefined criteria.

The total score for each domain in the AIRE tool is then converted into a percentage (0% to 100%) to reflect the overall evaluation across all reviewers. All discrepancies in AIRE scoring between reviewers will be addressed through discussion. If the difference exceeds one point on the 4-point Likert scale for any individual item (ranging from 1=strongly disagree to 4=strongly agree), and consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted to arbitrate the final score. The discussion will be conducted via synchronous meetings (video call or in-person) to allow real-time clarification. If consensus is not achieved during the initial discussion, a follow-up meeting will be arranged, and final arbitration will be conducted by the third reviewer.32 38 39 A higher percentage score indicates better methodological quality and a lower risk of bias, ensuring a more reliable and valid appraisal of the indicators used in the study (see online supplemental file 2).

Data extraction

The data extraction process for this systematic review follows a structured approach to ensure consistency and accuracy in capturing relevant information related to palliative care and end-of-life QIs in critically ill patients in the ICU. The extraction tools are adapted according to the following frameworks: characteristic study, PRISMA, Donabedian Model and Domain AIRE (onlinesupplemental files 2 3). The study description includes key elements such as methodology, method, phenomena of interest, setting, geographical and cultural context, participants, data analysis and conclusions. Data will be extracted systematically by identifying themes and subthemes that address the research objectives, such as types of palliative care interventions, QIs in end-of-life care, factors influencing decision-making and the impact of interventions before and after implementation. Quotations or illustrative examples will be incorporated to support the thematic analysis of extracted data.

The credibility of each finding will be assessed and classified as strong, credible or unsupported. If necessary, the authors of the included studies will be contacted to obtain missing or online supplemental information. If there is no response within 2 weeks, the studies will be reported in the PRISMA chart but will not be included in the final systematic review analysis. Articles that meet the inclusion criteria but lack supporting data will be categorised as unsupported. Every study included in the review will undergo data extraction along with relevant visual representations, and each finding will be assigned a plausibility rating. The process of developing thematic categories requires a meticulous and iterative analysis of the collected data. Furthermore, synthesis findings will be expressed as indicator statements.

Any disagreements among the reviewers will be resolved through open discussion or, if necessary, with the assistance of a third reviewer. When required, the authors of the publications will be contacted to obtain missing or additional information.

Data items

The authors identified the characteristics of QI-related studies using the DMQ, which encompasses structure, processes and treatment outcomes.28 Structure refers to the physical facilities and healthcare personnel available to meet patient needs. The process involves the actions taken in response to a patient’s condition, and the outcome represents the changes observed in a patient’s health following treatment.

In this study, we identified the characteristics of the selected articles, including title/abstract, author, year, country of origin, study design, respondents, research methods and research findings. These characteristics were further used for data extraction. The authors extracted data on bibliographic information, QI objectives, study design, psychometric measures, study population, study setting and the structure-process-outcome framework, along with QI outcomes. Additionally, QI characteristics were assessed based on bibliographic data, construct definitions and the theoretical approaches applied in QI development.

Data extraction followed a structured approach, considering key elements such as domain, indicator, measurement, indicator definition, numerator, denominator, unit of analysis, target sample, potential exclusions, data sources, data collection methods and primary support for measurement. If specific details regarding the characteristics of an indicator were unavailable in the article, they were categorised as ‘unknown’.

Synthesis methods

The collected study data has been extracted in a qualitative format. Although a meta-analysis cannot be conducted for this review, we will present a narrative synthesis focusing on the quantity and quality of the findings in the study. QI development research findings must be synthesised using the DMQ.34 The data synthesis process, employing the DMQ Model, includes selecting input, process and output in the development of health indicators within articles. This process is guided by the inclusion criteria established by the AIRE guidelines.29 These serve as the primary framework for developing palliative care QIs in our ICU. Indicators are designed for each finding, incorporating three key components: input, process and outcome. Each finding is categorised based on consensus among researchers involved in the systematic review.

This approach integrates various pieces of information to formulate statements that reflect the consolidated findings. The collected findings are compiled and categorised based on their semantic similarities. Identified indicators are then combined to derive comprehensive conclusions, forming the foundation for evidence-based practice. If text synthesis is not feasible, research findings are presented in a narrative format. In qualitative research, data collection relies on accuracy and organisation to facilitate practical data synthesis. Only articles that meet standards of clarity and credibility are included in the synthesis process.

Quality assessment

The methodology of the AIRE guidelines: in the AIRE guidelines, several aspects are assessed when developing treatment QIs. First, the relevance of the indicator is key, namely whether the indicator measures essential elements that have a direct impact on the quality of care and patient outcomes. In addition, scientific validity is also highly concerned, namely the extent to which indicators are based on strong and reliable scientific evidence. Practicality or feasibility is also assessed, ensuring that the data needed can be collected quickly and applied without burdening health workers. Indicators should also be clearly interpretable, providing insights that are easy to understand and actionable. Reliability and reproducibility are also considerations to ensure consistent results across multiple contexts. Finally, indicators should have follow-up elements, which means they can drive tangible changes in clinical practice and improve the quality of care.

Additionally, results will be shared with policymakers and healthcare providers to inform them of the development of better palliative care standards in ICU settings.
