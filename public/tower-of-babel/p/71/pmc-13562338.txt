Association of the red blood cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio with incident depression and depressive symptom trajectories in older adults
Authors: Zong Jiang, Xin Cai, Fang Tang, Zuqiao Zhao, HuiJiao Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ratio of red blood cell distribution width to albumin (RAR) is a systemic blood-based marker associated with adverse health outcomes. Although cross-sectional studies have suggested a link between RAR and depression, its longitudinal association with the incidence and long-term course of late-life depression has not yet been established. We aimed to address this knowledge gap using data from a large cohort of older adults.MethodsThis longitudinal cohort study used data from the Health and Retirement Study (2016–2022). The analysis of incident depression included 6,151 U.S. adults aged ≥ 60 years who were free of depression at baseline. Multivariable Cox regression was used to assess the association, and restricted cubic splines were applied to examine the dose-response relationship. In a subcohort of 5,588 participants, group-based trajectory modeling was used to identify depressive symptom trajectories, and their association with baseline RAR was examined using multinomial logistic regression.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 6 years, 1,287 participants developed incident depression. In the fully adjusted model, each 1-unit increase in baseline RAR was associated with a 19% higher risk of incident depression (HR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.06–1.33). Compared with the lowest quartile, the highest RAR quartile was associated with a higher risk of incident depression (HR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.01–1.42). The dose-response analysis indicated a linear relationship (P for non-linearity = 0.961). Four distinct symptom trajectories were identified: “Non-depressed” (28.11%), “Low-stable” (40.89%), “Moderate-progressive” (25.42%), and “High-progressive” (5.58%). Compared with the “Non-depressed” group, higher baseline RAR was associated with greater odds of membership in the “High-progressive” (OR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.11–1.55) and “Moderate-progressive” (OR = 1.36; 95% CI: 1.07–1.73) groups.ConclusionIn this large, nationally representative cohort of older adults, higher baseline RAR was longitudinally associated with an increased risk of incident depression and a subsequent long-term trajectory of worsening depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00406-026-02311-0.Keywords: Depression, Red blood cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio, Older adults, Trajectories, Cohort study

Introduction

Late-life depression represents a substantial and growing public health challenge, with clinical and subclinical depressive symptoms affecting up to one-third of adults aged over 60 years [1]. Globally, depression ranks as the third leading cause of disease burden and is projected by the World Health Organization to become the leading contributor by 2030, partly because of increasing prevalence in aging populations [2, 3]. Moreover, its detection and management are often complicated by heterogeneous clinical presentations and an unpredictable disease course, frequently leaving the condition untreated in clinical practice [2, 4]. Prospective evidence has shown that depression is strongly associated with a range of subsequent adverse physical outcomes, which may compromise healthspan and functional independence in older adults. Specifically, depression has been associated with accelerated multimorbidity, a 1.39- to 1.65-fold higher risk of cardiovascular disease, a 2.09-fold increased risk of dementia, and a 4.0-fold increase in the odds of debilitating falls [5–8]. This increased morbidity may also impose a substantial economic burden, as studies have shown that comorbid depression can double total healthcare expenditure [9, 10]. Despite the considerable burden of late-life depression, methods for identifying older adults at elevated risk before symptom onset remain limited, highlighting the need to investigate novel and readily available risk markers.

Red blood cell distribution width (RDW), an indicator of erythrocyte volume heterogeneity, reflects underlying pathophysiological processes, including impaired erythropoiesis, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation [11, 12]. Elevated RDW is an established predictor of adverse outcomes across numerous conditions, including cardiovascular and kidney diseases [13]. Serum albumin, the most abundant circulating protein, serves as a key marker of nutritional status and systemic inflammation and also has antioxidant properties [14]. Low serum albumin is also a well-documented independent predictor of morbidity and mortality in various conditions [15–17]. Because these biomarkers capture distinct but complementary aspects of systemic health, the ratio of RDW to albumin (RAR) has been increasingly investigated in recent studies. Emerging evidence suggests that RAR may serve as an integrated index with prognostic value [18, 19]. Large-scale prospective studies have found that higher RAR is associated with an increased risk of mortality in the general population [14]. Furthermore, the prognostic relevance of RAR has been demonstrated across a broad spectrum of clinical conditions, including acute critical illnesses such as sepsis and pancreatitis, as well as chronic cardiometabolic diseases and cognitive dysfunction [13, 18, 20–23].

Emerging evidence suggests that chronic low-grade inflammation and impaired nutritional status may contribute to the development of late-life depression [24, 25]. Meta-analytic data have confirmed a robust association between clinically relevant inflammation and depression [26], while nutritional frailty has been established as a significant risk factor for the onset of depressive symptoms [27]. Biological processes related to inflammation and nutrition have prompted investigation into relevant biomarkers, among which serum albumin has been shown to be inversely associated with depression [28]. Given the limited clinical utility of individual biomarkers, research has increasingly focused on composite indices such as RAR. Multiple cross-sectional analyses in large, nationally representative cohorts have reported a consistent positive association between RAR and the prevalence of depression [29–31]. The prognostic relevance of RAR has also been suggested, as elevated levels have been associated with a higher risk of mortality [32]. However, the available evidence remains limited by several factors, including reliance on cross-sectional designs that preclude establishing temporal relationships and a focus on general adult populations with limited attention to older cohorts. Current literature is also constrained by a lack of long-term follow-up. Longitudinal research has identified heterogeneous trajectories of symptom severity in late-life depression, including stable-low symptoms, gradual worsening, and persistent severity [33, 34]. Furthermore, these trajectories have been associated with elevated risks of adverse health outcomes, including dementia and mortality [35]. Despite their clinical relevance, no definitive biomarker has been validated for the subsequent long-term course of depressive symptoms.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to examine the longitudinal association between baseline RAR and the risk of incident depression in a large, nationally representative cohort of older adults. We also examined the association between baseline RAR and distinct long-term trajectories of depressive symptoms. Clarifying these relationships may help evaluate the potential value of RAR as a readily available marker related to depression risk and symptom progression in older adults.

Methods

Study population

We used data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a nationally representative longitudinal panel study that examines the complex interplay of health, social, and economic factors among U.S. adults aged over 50 years. The HRS uses a multistage area probability sampling design and conducts biennial follow-up interviews. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Michigan [36].

The present analysis integrated data from the 2016 h core survey and the accompanying 2016 Venous Blood Study (VBS). The detailed participant selection process is shown in Fig. 1. The initial cohort included all 20,912 participants from the 2016 h wave. We then excluded 14,761 individuals based on the following criteria: age under 60 years (n = 7,648), depression at baseline (n = 1,492), missing baseline information on depressive symptoms (n = 819), or missing RAR data (n = 4,802). Consequently, 6,151 participants were included in the longitudinal analysis of incident depression. For the depressive symptom trajectory analysis, an additional 563 individuals with fewer than two waves of depressive symptom data were excluded, yielding a final sample of 5,588 participants for trajectory modeling.

Flowchart of participant selection

Assessment of RAR

Biochemical data for this study were derived from venous blood samples collected in 2016 as part of the VBS. Samples were collected during home visits by trained phlebotomists and transported overnight to the Advanced Research and Diagnostic Laboratory at the University of Minnesota for centralized analysis. Serum albumin was quantified using the bromocresol purple method on a Roche Cobas 6000 Chemistry Analyzer. RDW was measured from a complete blood count performed on EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood using a Sysmex XE-2100 instrument. The final RAR value was calculated as RDW (%) divided by serum albumin (g/dL) [13, 32].

Assessment of depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) at baseline in 2016 and during follow-up in 2018, 2020, and 2022. A score of 3 or higher on the 8-point scale was used to indicate the presence of depression [37, 38]. Participants with depression at baseline were excluded from the analysis. Among the included participants, incident depression was defined as the first follow-up assessment at which this criterion was met. Follow-up time was calculated from the baseline interview, and participants were censored at their last available interview if lost to follow-up or at the final 2022 assessment if they remained free of depression.

Covariates

All potential confounding variables were assessed at baseline, with information primarily obtained from the HRS core interview. Covariates included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, clinical information, and biochemical measures. Sociodemographic characteristics included age (continuous), sex, education level (categorized as less than upper secondary, upper secondary and vocational, or tertiary), and marital status (married or other). Lifestyle factors included self-reported smoking status, alcohol consumption, and moderate physical activity, all dichotomized as no/yes. Clinical information included physician-diagnosed histories of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and stroke. A panel of biochemical measures from the VBS was also analyzed, including vitamin D, C-reactive protein (CRP), fasting glucose, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides. All biomarkers, along with age and body mass index (BMI), were included as continuous variables in the analysis (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were presented across quartiles of RAR. Continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation, SD) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). To compare characteristics across RAR quartiles, we used analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Before multivariable modeling, multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). No significant collinearity was detected, with all VIF values less than 5.

To examine the association between baseline RAR and the risk of incident depression, we used Cox regression models to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). RAR was analyzed both as a continuous variable and by quartiles, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference group. For continuous analyses, estimates were expressed per 1-unit increase in the calculated RAR. A test for linear trend was also performed by modeling the quartiles as a continuous term. We constructed three models: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, education level, and marital status; and Model 3 was further adjusted for lifestyle factors, clinical information, and biochemical measures. The potential non-linear dose-response relationship was examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots. We also conducted subgroup analyses to assess potential effect modification by including interaction terms in the fully adjusted model.

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was applied to identify distinct longitudinal trajectories of CES-D scores, using the 2016, 2018, 2020, and 2022 waves as the time scale [39]. The analysis was restricted to participants with at least two CES-D measurements, and the GBTM model included all available scores for each individual. CES-D scores were modeled using a censored normal distribution, and polynomial terms up to the cubic order were evaluated to determine the optimal shape of each trajectory. To select the final number of groups, models with one to six trajectories were fitted and compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), entropy, average posterior probability (AvePP), class size, model stability, and clinical interpretability. The final model was selected by balancing model fit, classification quality, parsimony, and clinical interpretability, with AvePP values greater than 0.70 for all groups and no trajectory group comprising less than 5% of the sample. Although the 3-class model showed acceptable classification quality, the 4-class model provided a more clinically informative separation of “Moderate-progressive” and “High-progressive” symptom patterns while maintaining acceptable entropy, AvePP values, and class sizes. Models with five or six classes showed lower AIC/BIC values but generated empty classes and unavailable posterior probabilities for some classes. Therefore, the 4-class model was retained as the final trajectory solution. Subsequently, multinomial logistic regression was used to examine the association between baseline RAR and membership in the identified trajectory groups, using the same adjustment strategy as in the Cox regression analyses.

The robustness of the findings was evaluated using sensitivity analyses tailored to each outcome. For incident depression, we first performed three analyses to assess the influence of reverse causality and missing data: a lag analysis excluding incident events that occurred within the first two years of follow-up, a complete case analysis among participants with no missing data on any covariates, and multiple imputation with chained equations to address missing covariate data. We further conducted two additional analyses to examine whether the observed associations could be explained by broader chronic disease burden or health/social vulnerability. In the first analysis, the individual chronic disease indicators in the fully adjusted model were replaced with an 8-condition comorbidity burden score, defined as the total number of baseline chronic conditions, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, stroke, heart disease, cancer, lung disease, and arthritis. In the second analysis, the fully adjusted model was further adjusted for baseline activities of daily living (ADL) limitation, cognitive score, household net wealth, and social isolation, with definitions provided in the Supplementary Methods. For depressive symptom trajectories, these latter two sensitivity analyses were repeated using multinomial logistic regression models.

No formal correction for multiple testing was applied. The primary analyses were prespecified, whereas subgroup and sensitivity analyses were considered exploratory or supportive. For all statistical tests, significance was determined using a two-sided P value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of participants according to RAR quartiles are presented in Table 1, including 6,151 participants in the longitudinal analysis. Significant differences were observed across RAR quartiles for most demographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables. Compared with participants in the lowest RAR quartile (Q1), those in the highest quartile (Q4) were older on average (mean age, 73.75 vs. 69.41 years), included a higher proportion of females (59.69% vs. 51.67%), and had a lower proportion of tertiary education (43.24% vs. 59.11%). Participants in Q4 also had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, including hypertension (72.17% vs. 56.65%), diabetes mellitus (33.29% vs. 22.86%), and stroke (11.05% vs. 5.44%). Moreover, their biomarker profile was characterized by higher BMI, CRP, and fasting glucose levels, along with lower levels of vitamin D and HDL-C. Notably, no significant difference in the prevalence of dyslipidemia was observed across RAR quartiles (P = 0.786).

Association between RAR and depression

During a median follow-up of 6 years, 1,287 individuals developed incident depression (4.78% per year). A significant positive association was observed between baseline RAR and the risk of incident depression (Table 2). In Model 3, each 1-unit increase in RAR was associated with a 19% higher risk of incident depression (HR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.06–1.33). When RAR was analyzed by quartiles, participants in Q4 had a 20% higher risk of developing depression than those in Q1 (HR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.01–1.42). In addition, a significant linear trend was observed across quartiles (P = 0.001).

Associations between RAR and trajectories of depressive symptoms

Based on the modeling of CES-D scores from 2016 to 2022, a four-group trajectory model was identified as the optimal fit, and the model selection statistics are presented in Table S4. The four distinct trajectories were labeled as “Non-depressed” (28.11%), “Low-stable” (40.89%), “Moderate-progressive” (25.42%), and “High-progressive” (5.58%) (Fig. 4). Baseline characteristics differed significantly across the four trajectory groups (Table S5). Compared with the “Non-depressed” group, participants in the “Moderate-progressive” and “High-progressive” groups had higher mean baseline RAR values, as well as a greater burden of adverse health and social factors.

Trajectories of depressive symptoms from 2016 to 2022

In the fully adjusted multinomial logistic regression analysis, higher baseline RAR was significantly associated with greater odds of belonging to trajectories characterized by worsening symptoms (Table 3). Specifically, each 1-unit increase in RAR was associated with 31% higher odds of membership in the “High-progressive” group (OR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.11–1.55) and 36% higher odds of membership in the “Moderate-progressive” group (OR = 1.36; 95% CI: 1.07–1.73), relative to the “Non-depressed” group.

Sensitivity analysis

The robustness of the primary association between RAR and incident depression was supported by several sensitivity analyses. To account for potential reverse causality, events occurring within the first two years of follow-up were excluded. In this analysis, the association per 1-unit increase in RAR remained significant (HR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.03–1.43) (Table S1). The results were also materially unchanged among participants with no missing data on any covariates (HR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.07–1.34) (Table S2). Consistent results were observed after accounting for overall chronic disease burden using the 8-condition comorbidity burden score, with each 1-unit increase in RAR remaining associated with incident depression (HR = 1.18; 95% CI: 1.06–1.32; P = 0.003), and the highest RAR quartile showing an elevated risk compared with the lowest quartile (HR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.01–1.41; P = 0.038; P for trend = 0.001). Moreover, after further adjustment for ADL limitation, cognitive score, household net wealth, and social isolation, the association persisted for continuous RAR (HR = 1.14; 95% CI: 1.03–1.26; P = 0.012), with similar results observed for the highest versus lowest RAR quartile (HR = 1.18; 95% CI: 1.01–1.39; P = 0.045; P for trend = 0.038). Finally, when multiple imputation with chained equations was applied to address missing data, the findings remained consistent with the primary analysis (HR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.05–1.29) (Table S3).

Similar robustness was observed for the trajectory analyses (Table S6). After accounting for the 8-condition comorbidity burden score, higher RAR remained associated with higher odds of membership in the “Moderate-progressive” trajectory (OR = 1.33; 95% CI: 1.05–1.68; P = 0.016) and the “High-progressive” trajectory (OR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.04–1.45; P = 0.015), whereas no significant association was observed for the “Low-stable” trajectory. After further adjustment for ADL limitation, cognitive score, household net wealth, and social isolation, the associations remained significant for the “Moderate-progressive” trajectory (OR = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.01–1.67; P = 0.042) and the “High-progressive” trajectory (OR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.06–1.46; P = 0.007), but not for the “Low-stable” trajectory.

Discussion

In this large, nationally representative cohort of older adults, each 1-unit increase in baseline RAR was associated with a 19% higher risk of incident depression over a median follow-up of 6.0 years (HR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.06–1.33). Furthermore, higher baseline RAR was associated with greater odds of membership in trajectories characterized by worsening symptoms, including the “Moderate-progressive” (OR = 1.36; 95% CI: 1.07–1.73) and “High-progressive” (OR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.11–1.55) trajectories. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the longitudinal association of RAR with both incident depression and long-term depressive symptom trajectories specifically in older adults. Although the associations were consistent across analyses, their magnitude was modest. Therefore, RAR should be interpreted as a supplementary, non-specific blood-based marker rather than as an isolated clinical indicator, and its potential relevance may be better understood in combination with other markers or risk factors.

The longitudinal association between RAR and incident depression observed in our study (HR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.06–1.33) was similar in magnitude to findings from previous cross-sectional studies. Regarding RAR levels, Liu et al. reported an OR of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.18–1.51) for prevalent depression, while Zhou et al. reported an OR of 1.22 (95% CI: 1.05–1.41) in a study with a similar design [29, 31]. In contrast to these cross-sectional studies, our longitudinal design clarifies the temporal sequence by linking baseline RAR measurements in a depression-free cohort to the subsequent development of depression. Our focus on adults aged over 60 years also provides new evidence relevant to this high-risk population, as previous studies were based mainly on general adult populations [30, 31]. Another novel aspect of our study is the use of trajectory analysis, which extends beyond predicting disease onset to identify factors associated with the longitudinal course of depressive symptoms. By providing longitudinal evidence in an older population and examining symptom trajectories, our findings offer a more dynamic perspective on the relationship between RAR and late-life depression.

Our findings showing an association between RAR and late-life depression are consistent with the established role of RAR as a general indicator of adverse health outcomes. Extensive studies have linked elevated RAR levels to an increased risk of mortality, highlighting its relevance as a marker of systemic frailty in large general populations [14, 40]. In clinical settings, its prognostic value has also been demonstrated in patients with severe acute conditions such as sepsis and respiratory failure, as well as in those with chronic illnesses, including cardiometabolic diseases [13, 41–43]. The evidence further extends to chronic inflammatory conditions, such as asthma, and to low cognitive performance commonly observed in older adults [18, 44]. RAR should not be interpreted as a depression-specific biomarker. Rather, it is a composite hematological-nutritional index that may reflect several non-specific systemic processes, including chronic low-grade inflammation, impaired nutritional status, oxidative stress, and chronic disease burden. This interpretation is consistent with prior evidence linking RAR to diverse adverse health outcomes across general and clinical populations. In the present study, the association between RAR and late-life depression remained evident after adjustment for BMI, CRP, metabolic biomarkers, and major chronic diseases. Additional sensitivity analyses using an 8-condition comorbidity burden score and further adjustment for ADL limitation, cognitive function, household wealth, and social isolation also yielded consistent results. By linking baseline RAR to both incident depression and longitudinal symptom trajectories, our study extends previous cross-sectional evidence and suggests that these non-specific systemic processes may be relevant to the development and progression of late-life depression.

RAR may provide a more comprehensive assessment of systemic vulnerability than either component alone, and several interconnected biological pathways may underlie the observed association [23, 45]. Chronic low-grade inflammation appears to be a central mechanism and has been strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of depression [46]. Elevated RDW may serve as a peripheral indicator of this inflammatory process [11, 47]. Systemic inflammatory cytokines can disrupt erythropoiesis by shortening red blood cell survival and reducing cellular deformability, thereby increasing heterogeneity in erythrocyte size and elevating RDW levels [48]. In parallel, low serum albumin contributes to a higher RAR and plays a dual role in inflammatory conditions. It is a negative acute-phase reactant during inflammation [49, 50] and an indicator of malnutrition, which may reflect inadequate protein intake and has been associated with depression risk [28, 51]. Nutritional deficiency may lead to structural and functional brain changes, impair emotional regulation, and contribute to depression [52]. The systemic state of inflammation and malnutrition is also linked to heightened oxidative stress. Reduced albumin levels may weaken antioxidant capacity by impairing the neutralization of free radicals [53, 54], thereby contributing to oxidative neuronal injury in depression [46, 55]. These systemic disturbances may affect the central nervous system and promote neuroinflammatory processes, such as sustained microglial activation, which is considered a key factor in the pathogenesis of depressive symptoms [56]. An elevated RAR may therefore serve as an integrated but non-specific marker of chronic inflammation, impaired nutrition, oxidative stress, and chronic disease burden. Although these processes are not specific to depression, they are biologically relevant to late-life depression and may help explain the observed associations with incident depression and worsening symptom trajectories.

Baseline characteristics of participants were presented across quartiles of RAR. Continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation, SD) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). To compare characteristics across RAR quartiles, we used analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Before multivariable modeling, multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). No significant collinearity was detected, with all VIF values less than 5.

The use of data from a large, nationally representative cohort strengthens the findings of this study. The consistency of the observed associations across examined subgroups and their stability in multiple sensitivity analyses further support the robustness of our results. However, the findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, causal inferences cannot be made because of the observational study design, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors may persist. Second, RAR was measured only once at baseline, which precluded analyses of dynamic changes in RAR in relation to depressive symptom trajectories. In addition, the available biomarker panel was limited, preventing a more comprehensive assessment of inflammatory, nutritional, oxidative stress-related, and hematological status. Regarding depression assessment, depressive symptoms were measured using the CES-D screening scale rather than a formal clinical interview, which may have introduced some misclassification. Selection bias may also have occurred because participants with missing data were excluded. Finally, the study was conducted exclusively among U.S. adults, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to younger individuals or populations in other countries. Future longitudinal studies should validate these findings in other populations, particularly across a wider age range, ideally using structured clinical interviews to diagnose depression. Future studies should also incorporate repeated measurements of RAR and broader biomarker panels to examine associations between changes in RAR and the long-term course of depressive symptoms.

Conclusion

In this large, nationally representative cohort of older adults, a higher baseline RAR was longitudinally associated with an increased risk of incident depression and a more adverse long-term course of depressive symptoms. These findings suggest that RAR may serve as a readily available, non-specific blood-based marker related to late-life depression risk and symptom progression.
