Joint associations of multiple healthy lifestyles with the risk of cardiovascular disease: a prospective cohort study of UK Biobank
Authors: Zewen Yang, Yujie Zhao, Jia You, Cheryl Carcel, Yuzhu Li, Shitong Xiang, Jujiao Kang, Wei Zhang, Zeyu Li, Yongwei Zhang, Lijun Wang, Pengfei Xing, Pengfei Yang, Jianfeng Feng, Jianmin Liu, Yu Zhou, Wei Cheng, Yang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major contributor to disability and the leading cause of global mortality. This study aims to perform a population-based prospective cohort study to examine the combined impact of multiple lifestyle factors on CVD risk and examine the differences in the relationships across sociodemographic groups. We used data from the UK Biobank. Exposures include seven lifestyle behaviors (smoking, physical activity, alcohol consumption, diet, sleep duration, sedentary behavior, and social connection) and combined multiple behaviors. The lifestyle score was subsequently categorized as favorable (5 to 7 healthy lifestyle factors), intermediate (2 to 4 healthy lifestyle factors), and unfavorable (0 to 1 healthy lifestyle factor) lifestyle classes. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for incident CVD and its subtypes. This study showed a significant association of favorable lifestyle with incident CVD (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.54-0.63), myocardial infarction (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.54-0.64), and ischemic stroke (HR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.48-0.65). Similarly, there was a significant association of intermediate lifestyle with incident CVD (HR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.64-0.75), myocardial infarction (HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.64-0.77), and ischemic stroke (HR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.57-0.77). The protection effect of lifestyle was more pronounced in the midlife group, females, and those individuals with high Townsend deprivation index levels. Adherence to a broad range of healthy lifestyle factors was associated with a significantly lower risk of CVD and subtypes. Lifestyle modification through multifactorial approaches should be prioritized for preventing and delaying onset of CVD.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), primarily ischemic heart disease (IHD) and stroke, remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide and represent a major contributor to global disability. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019, there were 523 million prevalent cases of CVD, 34.4 million years lived with disability, and 18.6 million CVD-related deaths in 2019, placing a substantial burden on global public health [1, 2]. Urgent action is required to implement existing cost-effective policies aimed at reducing the incidence of these diseases.

Previous studies have identified several modifiable lifestyle factors—including excessive alcohol consumption, poor diet, physical inactivity, smoking, inadequate sleep, sedentary behavior, and social isolation—as important contributors to CVD risk [3–6]. Managing these behaviors has been shown to significantly reduce the risk of cardiovascular events [3–6]. However, most studies to date have focused on individual factors, and the cumulative impact of multiple lifestyle behaviors on CVD risk remains inadequately explored. Few studies in the UK have investigated whether a comprehensive set of healthy lifestyle behaviors can mitigate CVD risk [7, 8]. Understanding the combined effect of these behaviors carries substantial public health significance and may inform strategies for large-scale prevention.

The UK Biobank is one of the largest population-based cohorts of middle-aged and older adults [9]. This extensive resource, which includes detailed health data and long-term follow-up, provides a unique opportunity to investigate the relationship between lifestyle behaviors and CVD in this population. In this study, we conducted a prospective cohort analysis to examine the long-term associations of multiple modifiable lifestyle factors with CVD and its subtypes, including myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke. Additionally, we explored whether these associations varied across sociodemographic subgroups.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a prospective, population-based cohort that enrolled over 500 000 participants aged 37–73 years at one of 22 assessment centers across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010 [10]. Ethical approval was obtained from the National Information Governance Board for Health and Social Care and the National Health Service North West Multi-center Research Ethics Committee (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/learn-more-about-uk-biobank/about-us/ethics). All participants provided electronic informed consent at the baseline assessment.

In this study, CVD outcomes were defined as the earliest recorded event of fatal or non-fatal myocardial infarction (International Classification of Diseases [ICD]-10 codes I21, I22, I23, I24, I25) or fatal or non-fatal ischemic stroke (ICD-10 codes G45, I63, I64), identified through hospital episode statistics, primary care data, or death registries, excluding events identified solely through self-reported data [11]. Participants with prior CVD diagnoses from surgical or vascular records before the baseline assessment were also excluded. The list of codes used to define the CVD phenotype is provided in Supplementary Table S7. The flowchart detailing sample selection for the survival analyses is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Lifestyle factors and healthy lifestyle score

In this study, we constructed a healthy lifestyle score based on seven well-established behavioral factors: smoking, physical activity, alcohol consumption, diet, sleep duration, sedentary behavior, and social connection, all of which were assessed at baseline using a standardized touchscreen questionnaire [10]. Participants self-reported sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, environmental exposures, and medical history via the device. Further details are available at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/refer.cgi? id=10024712. Comprehensive details regarding the assessment of each lifestyle factor are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Participants received 1 point for meeting the criteria for each healthy lifestyle factor, based on national or international guidelines. Never smoking was considered the healthy category [10], while current or former smoking was considered unhealthy. Regular physical activity was defined according to the American Heart Association guidelines as engaging in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity per week—or an equivalent combination—or participating in moderate activity on at least 5 days per week or vigorous activity at least once per week [12]. Moderate alcohol consumption was defined as never drinking or consuming 0–14 g/day for women and 0–28 g/day for men, according to the U.S. Dietary Guidelines for Americans [13]. A healthy diet was defined as the consumption of at least 4 out of 7 recommended food groups, following dietary priorities for cardiometabolic health [14]. In accordance with National Sleep Foundation recommendations and previous research [15], a sleep duration of 7–9 hours per night was considered healthy. Consistent with World Health Organization guidelines and prior literature [10], high levels of sedentary behavior have been associated with adverse health outcomes. Screen-based sedentary time was estimated as the sum of self-reported daily hours spent watching television and using a computer (excluding computer use for work); a total of 0–4 hours was categorized as low-to-moderate and considered healthy [10].

Social connection was evaluated using a social isolation index [11]. This index was calculated based on three components: (1) living alone (1 point), (2) low frequency of contact with friends/family (1 point for reporting visits about once a month or less, or no social ties), and (3) non-participation in any leisure or social activities (1 point) [16]. Individuals scoring 0 were considered least isolated, 1 moderately isolated, and 2–3 most isolated; those classified as least or moderately isolated were considered to have frequent social connection [10, 16]..The individual scores were summed to generate a total lifestyle score ranging from 0 to 7, with higher scores indicating greater adherence to a healthy lifestyle [13]. Participants were then categorized into three groups: favorable (5–7 healthy lifestyle factors), intermediate (2–4), and unfavorable (0–1), for use in subsequent analyses.

Covariate selection

Covariate data were collected at baseline. All statistical models were adjusted for baseline age (Field ID 21022), sex (Field ID 31), assessment center (Field ID 54), Townsend Deprivation Index (Field ID 189), an area-based measure of socioeconomic status, body mass index (BMI; Field ID 21001), ethnicity (Field ID 21000; categorized as White, Mixed, Asian, Black, Chinese, or other ethnic groups), educational attainment (Field ID 6138; categorized as college degree, A-level, O-level, CSE, NVQ, professional qualification, or other), and home area population density (Field ID 20118; classified as urban or rural, including village, town, or isolated dwelling). Participants were further categorized based on the presence of hypertension (Category ID 100011), defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg; hyperlipidemia (ICD-10 code E78); and diabetes (ICD-10 codes E10–E14). These conditions, which are closely associated with CVD, were also included as additional covariates in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were summarized as means with standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Participants with missing covariate data were excluded from the analysis. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to examine the associations of individual lifestyle factors, lifestyle categories, and composite lifestyle scores with incident CVD. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using complete-case analysis. Participants were followed from baseline (2006–2010) until the first occurrence of CVD diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up (July 19, 2022), whichever occurred first. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals and was found to be satisfied [17]. In sensitivity analyses, stratified models were run by baseline age (<60 and ≥60 years), sex (male/female), Townsend deprivation index (quantiles 1, 2–4, and 5), and home area population density (urban vs. rural). Interaction effects were evaluated by including cross-product terms between lifestyle category and each covariate in the regression models. All statistical analyses were conducted using the survival package in R (version 4.2.1). A two-sided P values of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive analysis

A total of 363 945 participants with complete data were included in the final analysis. The mean age at baseline was 56.35 years (SD: 8.03), and 170 288 participants (46.79%) were male. The mean lifestyle score (range: 0–7) was 4.83 (SD: 1.34), with 1.03% categorized as having an unfavorable lifestyle (score 0–1), 36.70% as intermediate (score 2–4), and 62.27% as favorable (score 5–7). Over a median follow-up of 13.12 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 12.35–13.96), 32 554 participants developed CVD, including 25 156 myocardial infarctions and 9120 ischemic strokes. Single healthy lifestyle and CVDs

Table 2 presents the associations between individual lifestyle factors and the risk of CVDs. Moderate alcohol consumption, adherence to a healthy diet, regular physical activity, never smoking, adequate sleep duration, low-to-moderate sedentary behavior, and frequent social connections were each independently associated with a significantly lower risk of CVD. The estimated risk reductions for these factors ranged from approximately 5% to 15%, as detailed in Table 2. These findings highlight the substantial and independent contributions of multiple modifiable lifestyle behaviors to the primary prevention of CVD.

Combined behavioral lifestyles and CVDs

When low-risk lifestyle factors were considered in combination, the risk of CVDs decreased progressively across lifestyle categories and lifestyle score levels. Compared with participants in the unfavorable lifestyle category, those in the intermediate lifestyle category had significantly lower adjusted HRs: 0.69 (95% CI: 0.64–0.75) for CVD, 0.70 (95% CI: 0.64–0.77) for myocardial infarction, and 0.58 (95% CI: 0.54–0.64) for ischemic stroke. Participants in the favorable lifestyle category exhibited even greater risk reductions, with adjusted HRs of 0.58 (95% CI: 0.54–0.63) for CVD, 0.58 (95% CI: 0.54–0.64) for myocardial infarction, and 0.56 (95% CI: 0.48–0.65) for ischemic stroke (see Supplementary Table S4).When lifestyle was evaluated as a composite score, a clear inverse dose–response relationship was observed. Compared to individuals with a lifestyle score of 0–1, those with the maximum score of 7 had adjusted HRs of 0.53 (95% CI: 0.48–0.58) for CVD, 0.52 (95% CI: 0.47–0.58) for myocardial infarction, and 0.53 (95% CI: 0.45–0.63) for ischemic stroke (see Fig. 1).

Sensitivity and stratified analyses

We conducted stratified analyses to assess whether sociodemographic factors modified the association between lifestyle and the risk of CVD. Using the unfavorable lifestyle group as the reference, a favorable lifestyle was associated with a reduced risk of CVD across all four sociodemographic subgroups.

The protective effect of lifestyle was more pronounced among middle-aged participants than among older adults (P for interaction < .0001), in females than in males (P for interaction < .0001), and with increasing levels of socioeconomic deprivation as measured by the Townsend Deprivation Index (P for interaction = .0019). However, no significant interaction was observed between home area population density and lifestyle category (P for interaction = .2776) (see Fig. 2). Similar stratified patterns were observed for myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke (see Supplementary Tables S5 and S6).

Discussion

While individual lifestyle factors have been linked to cardiovascular risk, the combined impact of multiple unhealthy behaviors on CVDs in the UK remains insufficiently understood. In this prospective cohort study, we investigated the long-term associations between multiple modifiable lifestyle behaviors and the risk of CVD, using data from a large, population-based cohort in the UK. To our knowledge, this is the first study to simultaneously assess seven behavioral factors—including non-smoking, physical activity, moderate alcohol consumption, a healthy diet, adequate sleep duration, low sedentary behavior, and social connection—and their joint effects on cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes. A composite healthy lifestyle score incorporating these factors was strongly associated with a graded reduction in the incidence of CVD and its major subtypes, such as myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke. These associations remained consistent across subpopulations with varying baseline comorbidities. These findings are consistent with prior studies and meta-analyses reporting that adherence to an overall healthy lifestyle is associated with 58% and 62% reductions in CVD mortality and incidence, respectively [1]. Furthermore, individuals who adopted the most favorable lifestyle profiles demonstrated 55–71% lower risks of fatal or total stroke, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, peripheral artery disease, coronary heart disease, and heart failure [18]. These associations have been observed across diverse populations, including variations by country, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status [1]. Life expectancy was also found to increase by 7.4–18.5 years among individuals with greater adherence to low-risk lifestyle behaviors in cohorts from North America, Europe, and China [19–22]. Although several studies have evaluated the role of individual lifestyle behaviors in relation to CVD and cardiometabolic mortality, there is limited evidence on the cumulative effects of multiple lifestyle factors in the UK context [3–5]. Exposure to clustered risk behaviors may partially explain the co-occurrence of chronic diseases and suggest shared etiologic pathways [23–25]. Similar patterns have been reported in studies from the USA and Asia [26–28]. To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms linking combined healthy behaviors with CVD risk and mortality, additional high-quality cohort studies with long-term follow-up are warranted. From a public health and clinical perspective, these results underscore the substantial benefit of adhering to multiple healthy behaviors regardless of socioeconomic background. Public health initiatives should prioritize the promotion of favorable lifestyle patterns, including smoking cessation, reduced alcohol intake, healthy eating, adequate sleep, regular physical activity, and social engagement. In clinical practice, greater attention should be given to identifying and supporting individuals with fewer healthy behaviors, who may be at higher risk. These findings support the development of personalized preventive strategies and population-level interventions targeting modifiable behaviors. Moreover, stratification based on a lifestyle score may enhance risk prediction, allowing healthcare systems to allocate resources more efficiently to high-risk individuals [29, 30]. This highlights the utility of lifestyle profiling as a preventive and health-promoting strategy rather than a treatment endpoint. This study has several limitations. First, lifestyle information was self-reported at baseline and not updated over time, which may introduce measurement error. Repeated assessments of lifestyle behaviors would improve exposure classification. Second, the lifestyle score assigned equal weight to each component, although their relative contributions to CVD risk may differ. A weighted scoring system based on effect size may offer more precise estimates. Third, only a limited set of lifestyle factors was included; other environmental or psychosocial variables may also influence CVD risk. Fourth, baseline covariates were used for adjustment, potentially underestimating associations due to unmeasured changes over time. Fifth, sedentary behavior was self-reported and excluded occupational computer use, possibly underestimating total sedentary time, especially among individuals in sedentary occupations. Lastly, given the observational nature of this study, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and causal inference remains limited. Well-designed interventional studies are needed to further validate these associations.

Conclusions

In a large cohort of more than 360 000 adult participants from the UK, favorable lifestyle habits were strongly inversely associated with incidents of CVD and subtypes. Adherence to a broad range of healthy lifestyle factors was associated with a significantly lower risk of CVDs among participants in the UK. Lifestyle modification through multifactorial approaches should be a priority for preventing and delaying the onset of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases in adults.
