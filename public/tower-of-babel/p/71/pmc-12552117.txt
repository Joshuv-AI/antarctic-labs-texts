Microclimate governs the morphology of sediment flows on Mars
Authors: Jacob B Adler, Frances Rivera-Hernández, Sharissa Thompson, Petr Brož, Matthew E Sylvest, Zoe S Emerland, Manish Patel, Mark Fox-Powell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLandforms created by flowing water with sediment have left deposits on the surface of Mars, allowing study of the ancient environment. These features could provide constraints on surface water activity and past habitability. However, only a few lab studies have investigated the appearance and behavior of sediment-rich flows at relevant Mars surface conditions. We conducted experiments in a Mars environment chamber to understand the rheology and deposit morphology of mud under atmospheric pressures from 5 to 1000 mbar and surface temperatures between 248 and 297 K. We found that sediment flows in the Noachian era, when most aqueous activity occurred, could behave similarly to Earth analogs, but only under certain climate conditions. However, in the Hesperian and Amazonian periods, the dominant physical regime changed due to global atmospheric loss. Sediment flows during these eras would not have been similar to Earth analogs, and would have been dominated by freezing, evaporative cooling, and boiling depending on the microclimate (local pressure and temperature). Thus, regional climate and compositional context are important factors for interpreting satellite remote sensing images of these features on Mars. The results suggest we may be able to discover the paleo-atmospheric pressure record on Mars by analyzing sediment flow morphology.Subject terms: Geomorphology, Hydrology, Inner planets

Introduction

Interpreting the history of sediment transport on Mars is essential to our understanding of the planet’s aqueous history and habitability of past surface environments. This importance has been stressed by many Mars science missions including the two current NASA rover missions (MSL Curiosity and Mars 2020 Perseverance) and planned ESA Rosalind Franklin rover all exploring landing sites with sedimentary features identified in remote sensing datasets1–3. Despite this importance, there has been very little work to date investigating how sediment flows behave at Mars surface conditions4–7.

Sedimentary deposits are ubiquitous on Mars, indicating a complex history of aqueous activity spanning the past ~4 Ga (Fig. 1)8–10. The sediment in these features varies compositionally, but most deposits range from largely basaltic in origin to various alteration products11–15. Some commonly proposed aqueous processes that could lead to sediments transport over the surface include glacial melting, precipitation, fluvial transport in rivers and gullies, groundwater table interaction with regolith, and sedimentary volcanism8,16–21.

The surface environment across Mars today ranges annually from 1–14 mbar and 248–297 K. This is vastly different from the environment under which terrestrial analog sediment-water mixtures flow (roughly 1000 mbar and 260–320 K). While the atmosphere of Mars is currently thin (~5–7 mbar on average), the atmosphere was likely much thicker in the past as evidenced by landforms indicative of flowing water. Estimates from models could put the martian atmospheric pressure in the early Noachian up to 100 or 1000 mbar (e.g., refs. 8, 9, 22), but these numbers are poorly constrained (e.g., refs. 23, 24). Large oscillations in the planet’s obliquity over Myr timescales also cause large swings in the atmospheric pressure as polar ice melts and migrates25. Still, the general trend is that Mars lost most of its atmospheric pressure by the Hesperian, and while climate may shift dramatically as Mars’ axis moves, the pressure may at most increase by 2–10x its current value, and will never return to the more hospitable paleoclimates of the Noachian26. Thus, past Mars with higher atmospheric pressures would likely have a more Earth-like range of local climates, and these environments would vary largely with elevation and latitude. Still, key questions remain: Can we use Earth analogs of sediment flow deposits as a basis for studying Mars examples? And how would the formation process vary over time and across the planet’s surface?

In this study, we conducted 79 sediment flow experiments using The Large Dirty Mars Chamber in the HVI & SPE Laboratories at The Open University (Supplementary Data Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2) under a variety of Mars surface conditions. We controlled pressure, temperature, and composition to investigate how rheology and deposit morphology would change during the Noachian (4.1–3.7 Ga), Hesperian (3.7–3.0 Ga), and Amazonian (3.0 Ga–present) eras. This collection of laboratory analog experiments illustrates that the behavior of these sediment flow processes on Mars has varied significantly over time, and that local conditions and context are key considerations for interpretation of remote sensing images.

Results

Flow process varies across Amazonian Mars (3.0 Ga to present)

In laboratory sediment flow experiments at low atmospheric pressures, we found that boiling and freezing are the most dominant processes that govern morphology of the flows. By logging pressure and temperature of the water-sediment mixtures, we experimentally measured a portion of the phase diagram (vaporization curve and triple point) for experiments and found similar results to the phase diagram of pure water (Supplementary Fig. 3)27. Combining this measurement of the phase diagram with Mars Climate Database models of present-day surface conditions (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5), we determined where on Mars the surface conditions are conducive to sediment-rich flows (or Mars “mud”) freezing, boiling, or flowing in a liquid state today (Fig. 2).

Not shown: At 5:00 (predawn low surface temperatures), freezing dominates for all of Mars in all seasons (solid purple maps).

Figure 2 shows that on a given afternoon in northern Spring, Summer, Fall, or Winter (Ls = 0 °, 90 °, 180 °, or 270 °, respectively), surface sediment-rich flows in certain regions would experience boiling (yellow), while others would freeze (purple) or experience neither process (teal). Thus, despite what appears to be a narrow range of surface pressures on Mars today (1–14 mbar), the resultant morphologies across the surface could be vastly different.

To illustrate the importance of this point, consider the following hypothetical example of three astronauts performing a simultaneous experiment. If astronaut 1 at a research station near the Perseverance landing site in Jezero crater poured water on the regolith-covered surface at 13:00 local time on a summer day, the mixture would flow similarly to the same scenario on Earth. However, 100 km due west of the crater at the same latitude of 18.5° N, astronaut 2 would witness vigorously boiling mud. At the same time, astronaut 3 in the Isidis basin 100 km due east of the crater would witness a small heaping pile of rapidly freezing mud.

Our results illustrate that throughout much of the Amazonian there is no common set of physics that can apply to all sediment flow features. Instead, changes in phase due to local conditions would drive the rheology and range of expected morphologies. In many regions, especially at poleward latitudes, sediment-rich flows would freeze. Near the mid-latitudes, most sediment-rich flows would experience boiling, while some would experience freezing, and some neither. This places great importance on evaluating the climatic context of Amazonian-aged features of interest. For example, there are very few regions on modern Mars where we would expect mud flows to behave like terrestrial liquid flows (Fig. 2, teal). The most likely locations to find terrestrial-like behavior would be in the low northern latitudes (teal band from ~0°–35° N) during northern summer afternoons.

Some smaller regions and times of day or year could support flow processes not dominated by boiling or freezing. For example, at the global to regional scale we find some regions that would support flow in the afternoon (Fig. 2, teal). Additional regions that would support flow exist at smaller scales due to microclimate variability. For example, factors that could be modeled or observed at higher resolution in situ or from orbit like surface albedo, emissivity, insolation, shadowing, slope, and slope direction would all influence the surface temperature. Such models often find that within a single cell of a global climate model output the surface temperatures could vary by several degrees (e.g., ref. 28). In the Amazonian, a few degrees Celsius is enough to change the dominant flow process and resulting morphology (e.g., Supplementary Figs. 3 and 5).

However, there is no location on Mars today that would support terrestrial-like flow late at night or in the pre-dawn hours. The four other global phase maps produced for the Amazonian at 5:00 local time are entirely in the solid phase (purple), illustrating that freezing processes during flow would govern depositional morphology throughout much of the day and night (discussed in the section “Freezing governs morphology in low-pressure climes with “cold” surfaces”).

Conditions were more conducive to terrestrial flow during the Noachian and Hesperian

In the Noachian and Hesperian, these phase maps are different as we expect higher pressures (Supplementary Fig. 6). There is still no consensus, however, on whether temperatures were necessarily much warmer. But by raising the atmospheric pressure and keeping all surface temperature values the same (Supplementary Fig. 5), we found an increase in areas that would support either terrestrial-like flows or freezing. Raising the distribution of surface condition values eliminated the possibility of boiling, leaving only two phase options during periods of higher atmospheric pressure.

Boiling governs morphology in low-pressure climes with “warm” surfaces

As Mars lost much of its atmosphere in the late Noachian and Hesperian, the surface pressure dropped, which would cause a change in appearance of sediment-rich flow deposits. One trend recorded over a series of ten experiments over an ambient temperature surface (~294 K) was a decrease in deposit length at lower pressures. Figure 3 shows this trend for a bulk regolith simulant (MGS-1)29 and for our control simulant (Basalt-1). Both simulants are representative of the mineralogical composition of martian regolith—MGS-1 is a commercially available simulant that matches the bulk mineralogy measured by the curiosity rover at the Rocknest site, and Basalt-1 is a custom simulant containing only the top four ingredients in MGS-1 (see the section “Laboratory experiments”). At Earth pressures, flows created roughly straight single-lobed deposits. At these higher pressures, deposits were relatively longer and narrower. As pressure was decreased, simulating a transition from the Noachian (~100–1000 mbar) to the Amazonian (<20 mbar), deposit run out lengths became shorter and slightly wider. At pressures around 14–25 mbar, morphology transitioned as boiling became active. At pressures below this transition, deposits were complex and multi-lobate.

Top: MGS-1 Mars bulk regolith simulant. Bottom: Basalt-1 regolith simulant. Right angle rulers are 8 cm on each internal side.

In these experiments simulating low-pressure conditions on Mars (Patm < 20 mbar) over a warm surface (~289–297 K), the effects of boiling were profound (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 7). At these low pressures, the water content in the mixture (both while in the mud reservoir and during the flow) bubbled vigorously. Bubbles expanded within the mixture and would burst upon contact with the fluid surface (Fig. 4A), often leaving behind transient craters. For experiments with higher viscosities, burst bubbles were more likely to leave craters in the final deposit, and to eject more mud to their surroundings.

Boiling: Common processes and deposit morphologies in warm bed experiments.

A Intense boiling along the perimeter and a large bubble about to burst during flow. B Intense erosion near the start of the flow, lowering the deposit below prior ground level. C Levee along flow margin. D Multi-lobate flow from frequent avulsion. E Ruffled surface texture. F Positive relief cone ring from subsurface boiling.

As the water content of the sediment flow contacted dry sand grains on the test bed, intense boiling and outgassing of water vapor would occur. This caused grains to sputter, and the intense outgassing beneath the flow caused it to levitate and skid downslope (like a hovercraft) sometimes reaching the end of the test bed. Thus, we observed the Leidenfrost effect on a modern warm Mars surface, which has been observed in prior Mars experi6,30,31. This outgassing continued for several minutes after flow stopped along the deposit perimeter. This created sand levees by eroding and spreading loose sand along the perimeter (Fig. 4C, E yellow arrows). Outgassing was thus an erosional process which affected the loose sand of the substrate and created a “moat”, and constructional process as it created positive relief sand levees beyond the moat.

Another characteristic unique to boiling experiments was enhanced avulsion. Many of the flows split or avulsed to form new lobes. The position of the avulsion was highly variable, ranging from the first contact point with the sand upslope to further downflow at distal breakout points. Some experiments produced deposits with over a dozen thinner finger-like lobes (e.g., Fig. 4D), in strong contrast to the terrestrial and cold bed experiments which mostly created a single lobe (see Fig. 3 deposits at 5 mbar compared to 20 mbar).

For sediment mixtures with high water contents, flows were increasingly erosive. For example, Fig. 4B shows an experiment where the majority of the sand bed was eroded near the initiation of flow, and the upstream portion of the deposit is thus lower than the surrounding terrain. This was a common trend in experiments with intense boiling. The distal portion of these deposits often remained near surface level, but with small perimeter levees.

In several early experiments the mud poured from the reservoir did not travel above the sand surface as desired but instead into the surface immediately and poured into the bottom of the test bed (Fig. 4F). For these experiments this was due to the lack of the plastic half-pipe to translate flows horizontally over the surface, which was introduced at experiment 33. Nonetheless, in these experiments without surface flow we found that boiling still dominated the morphologic expression of the deposit. Many of these experiments left mud deposits in a sand covered rounded shape with a digitate or ruffled perimeter and a circumscribed sand levee ring created from vigorous subsurface boiling.

Freezing governs morphology in low-pressure climes with “cold” surfaces

In experiments simulating low-pressure conditions on Mars (Patm < 20 mbar) over a cold surface (246–261 K), freezing and evaporative cooling strongly influenced the final morphology (Fig. 5). In these cold bed experiments, we did not observe water loss of the mud due to vigorous boiling and outgassing. We also did not observe water loss into the surrounding sand. Since the cold sand was frozen overnight and likely contained some small water content, it formed a rigid surface for the mud to flow on. We observed freezing first along the perimeters of mud flows as they traveled downslope (Supplementary Fig. 8). We deduce that freezing also began underneath flows due to conduction with the frozen surface as we found afterwards a liquid interior in some experiments and frozen crust including the underside. Most flow deposits in this regime eventually froze solid after about 5–10 min in the chamber.

Mud flows on a simulated cold Amazonian surface did not avulse as frequently as over looser, warmer sand. This is partly to be expected because (1) the surface was more rigid and less erodible and (2) there was a lack of boiling to assist the flow in overcoming obstacles. Most flows in cold conditions were singular lobes of roughly uniform width (e.g., Fig. 5I). These deposits had roughly uniform thickness and were entirely above the surface of the bed.

We found that freezing morphologies also included warping. In some experiments, the entire deposit started to warp and lift away from the surface as it froze (Fig. 5C, H). In one experiment (Exp. 24), the deposit bent upon freezing, creating a large arc above the sand bed. Separation of water ice from basalt sediment commonly occurred in basalt muds but not in other clay or salt compositions (Fig. 5C). This is likely due to the lack of cohesion in pure basalt–water mixtures, whereas clay and ion content created mud flows that had stronger cohesive properties. We also observed ice lensing (Fig. 5G), as frozen water preferentially grew and attracted more water to freeze.

The formation of freezing crusts during flow also led to similarities to pahoehoe lava flows on Earth. For example, flows exhibited inflation as new liquid mud moved internally and lifted the frozen crust. Breakouts of liquid mud emerged from crusted lobes and continued advancement downslope. These processes were similar to those reported for a different mud composition5,6.

“Earth-like” morphologies are possible at mid to high pressures

To simulate flows in the Hesperian (~3.7–3.0 Ga) and Noachian (~4.1–3.7 Ga), we varied the atmospheric pressure in large increments from 20 to 1000 mbar. We roughly consider pressures of around 100 mbar and higher as a conservative estimate for the Noachian era, and intermediate pressures between 20 and 100 mbar representative of the Hesperian (or other Amazonian aged transient periods of local high pressure due to climate events, seasons, and/or obliquity).

We found that above 25 mbar, sediment flows are far more “Earth-like”. This is consistent with our expectation based on the vaporization curve of water (Supplementary Fig. 3). The changes we found from 25 mbar to 1000 mbar were very subtle at the scale of our experiments. For example, as pressures were increased to 1000 mbar, Basalt-1 and MGS-1 flows gradually became longer and thinner (Fig. 3).

Another way in which we account for the variation in conditions from the Noachian to Amazonian is accounting for a likely change in sediment flow composition from more clay-rich and salt-poor to more clay-poor and salt-rich. During the Noachian, surface water activity was more abundant and active weathering produced clays. While salts could form in the Noachian, the widespread presence of water likely diluted brines. In the Amazonian, Mars became much colder and drier and limited surface water favored the concentration of salts in localized deposits. Clay formation in the Amazonian was minimal due to lower weathering rates.

To test effects of clays, we spiked control basalt simulants with varying clay type and concentration. Of the clay minerals found to date in mostly Noachian terrains on Mars, two categories stand out as being both widespread and likely to produce opposing end-member effects: swelling clays (e.g., smectites including montmorillonite, bentonite, nontronite, and saponite) and non-swelling clays (e.g., kaolinite or illite). We tested the effects of bentonite and kaolinite on two of the control basalt simulants (Basalt-1 and Basalt-GT). We found the flows with clays were less likely to form multiple lobes or avulse, and appeared to have increased uniformity in grain size and thickness likely due to added cohesion. There were clear differences in rheologic properties even with small concentrations of clays. For example, with only 5–10 g (~1–2 wt%) of either of the clay compositions added to ~600 g of basaltic mud, there were large differences in runout distance, flow color, and uniformity (flow width and grain size distribution)32.

In warm bed clay experiments, we observed that the added cohesion kept the water content within the flow from seeping into the surrounding sand. This reduced the amount of sand sputtered by boiling along the perimeter and the amount of sand incorporated into the deposit. It also contributed to a more plug-like flow, which traveled further downslope.

In experiments spiked with varying types of salts (MgSO4·7H2O, anhydrous MgSO4, anhydrous Na2SO4, and NaCl), we found no major morphologic changes at the tested conditions. One should expect the salts to suppress the freezing point and raise the boiling point as in one recent work7, but our methods did not capture differing morphologic trends at the experiment scale.

Determining the paleo-atmospheric pressure from morphology and context

Taken together, laboratory observations of the behavior of sediment-rich water flows under varying Mars climate conditions and extrapolation of where and when on Mars to expect these processes provides context to interpreting features on Mars. Future remote sensing observations of sediment flow features on Mars could search for distinct morphologies that could be clues to the climatic conditions during formation. For example, signs of boiling and therefore a low paleo-atmospheric pressure with warmer surface temperatures include enhanced erosion, perimeter moats or levees, downslope skidding, craters from outgassing, and enhanced avulsion. Morphologies indicative of freezing do not necessarily require a low atmospheric pressure, merely a cold enough surface environment and insufficient salts incorporated in the flow. Signs of freezing include rounded lobes, pahoehoe-like textures, ice lensing, warping, or crust formation. Thus, a critical component of interpreting flow features of unknown origin in Mars satellite images is consideration of the likely environmental conditions.

Methods

Laboratory experiments

In this study we investigated the effects of composition and sediment:water ratio on laboratory analog martian flows. We conducted 79 experiments using The Large Dirty Mars Chamber in the HVI & SPE Laboratories at The Open University (Supplementary Fig. 2). The Mars chamber is a cylindrical adaptable environmental simulation chamber that has previously been used for a wide range of Mars experiments (including for studies of mud-volcanism, surface runoff, brine solutions, and CO2 sublimation at Mars atmospheric conditions4–6. The chamber measures 1.8 m long and 0.9 m wide, and has adaptable ports used for feedthroughs and viewports. Pressure is automatically controllable between 0.8 mbar and 1000 mbar. The chamber is ideal for these types of sediment flow experiments as it is large enough to house an inclined test bed (10°) measuring 0.4 m × 0.9 m, pressure and temperature sensors, and cameras from multiple viewing angles. The precise control on pressure inside the chamber makes it possible to investigate the effects important to this work. We used the same chamber, test bed, tools, equipment, photogrammetry targets, and other assets as in previous work5,6 for consistency and relevant comparison to prior experiments.

To create each sediment flow experiment, we used a metal test bed filled with dry sand and fixed photogrammetry targets (Supplementary Fig. 2). The bed was placed in the chamber at the same 10 ° incline for all experiments and with cameras capturing video from three angles (top, front, and side). We first mixed our customized dry regolith simulant with DI (de-ionized) water at specific water:sediment ratios (ranging from approximately 30:70 to 80:20 by weight) and transferred the final mixture into the reservoir in the Mars chamber. The mixtures ranged in volume from 500-850 grams. For each experiment, the reservoir in the chamber was a glass jar blender attached to a manual pour lever. The blender speed was operated by a fine control power dial (Veriac) outside the chamber so that the mud could remain thoroughly mixed up to the time of pouring. Three thermocouples were used to log the temperature of mud in the reservoir, the test bed, and chamber throughout each experiment. A pressure sensor inside the chamber was attached to the pressure regulator and logged the pressure in the chamber throughout each experiment. After loading and sealing the chamber, the chamber vacuum pumps were set to pump out atmosphere and then maintain a specific pressure (e.g., 5, 20, 100 mbar). When the desired pressure was reached, the mud was poured at an approximately volumetrically steady rate onto the surface of the inclined bed to flow over the surface. We added a plastic half-pipe for experiments 33–79 to better channelize the initial pour and translate flows horizontally over the surface.

After completing each experimental run and re-pressurization of the chamber, we removed the test bed with flow and photographed key features. After imaging the intact flow, we created cross-sectional cuts at key locations to observe the internal structure. We documented the location of cuts and acquired macro photos of the cross section to characterize the internal structure.

The experiments were performed over four weeks in 2022 and 2024. Three different basaltic regolith simulants were tested to provide a control set of experiments at varying pressures, temperatures, and viscosities with no added clays or salts. The three basalt types accounted for a total of 37 out of 79 experiments (19 Basalt-1, 10 Basalt-GT, and 8 Basalt-GG as labeled in Supplementary Data Table 1). The three types of Basaltic regolith came from different sources and had a different grain size distribution. Two were custom made for this project (Basalt-1 and Basalt-GT) and one was commercially available (Basalt-GG). Basalt-1 was a custom ordered simulant created by Exolith labs for this project. Basalt-1 was the top four constituents (plagioclase, glass-rich basalt, pyroxene, and olivine) in their commercially available Mars Global Simulant (MGS-1). The same relative proportions of the four components were used as in MGS-1, but all minor constituents and alteration products were held out. Basalt-GG is a commercially available basaltic dust powder from Gaia Green Organics from a basalt source in British Columbia, Canada. Products with a date of 2024-06 were found to be of high purity when examined in a petrographic microscope and vis-NIR reflectance spectrometer. Basalt-GT was made at Georgia Institute of Technology from massive Colombia river flood basalt samples. We used a rock crusher, motorized disc mill and series of sieves in the PLANETAS lab at Georgia Tech to create a basaltic powder of unimodal grain size distribution (~5–250 µm). Of all three basaltic controls, we expect Basalt-GT to be the most reliably representative of martian basaltic regolith.

To create the “mud” we mixed basaltic regolith with DI water. We varied several parameters including pressure (to simulate both flows on Earth and from early-Noachian Mars to present-day Amazonian Mars), temperature (to simulate flows throughout history and also at different locations on the planet), and viscosity (by controlling the sediment:water ratio).

We also performed 13 experiments by using the commercially available Mars Global Simulant (MGS-1) from Exolith Labs. MGS-1 contains several additional minor components as it was designed to match the Curiosity chemical analysis of soils at Rocknest (Cannon et al., 2019).

To test the effects of added clays, we conducted 23 experiments where a Basaltic control was spiked with various clays. We tested a swelling bentonite clay from the Rokle bentonite formation by adding to Basalt-1 in 7 experiments. This was the same composition used in refs. 6,5. In 5 experiments, we included a commercial sodium bentonite powder, which was 99.9% montmorillonite added to Basalt-1. In 6 experiments, we tested a non-swelling kaolinite clay powder (Thiele B80 Kaolinite powder) added to Basalt-1. In 5 additional experiments, we added the bentonite powder containing sodium montmorillonite to Basalt-GT. And in 5 experiments, we added the kaolinite powder from Thiele Kaolin Co. in Georgia to Basalt-GT.

To measure the effect of relevant Mars salts on the flows, we conducted four warm bed experiments and one cold bed experiment with Basalt-GT simulant as the base composition. The concentration of each salt was a constant 10% brine solution (30 g salt dissolved in 300 g water). The exception to this was experiment 75 for which we added 61.43 g MgSO4·7H2O (the equivalent 30 g anhydrous MgSO4) to keep the brine at the same 10% salt concentration. The brines were each mixed with 300 g dry Basalt-GT simulant to form the mud. A total of 5 experiments were conducted with added salts (anhydrous sodium sulfate Na2SO4; table salt NaCl; magnesium sulfate MgSO4·7H2O; and anhydrous magnesium sulfate MgSO4).

As reported in prior works, this experimental setup creates a close laboratory analog environment to the surface of Mars5,6. The length scale of sediment flows is smaller than those observed on Mars from remote sensing (meters to kilometers in length), but the thickness of laboratory flows could be on the same order (centimeters) for water-dominated mixtures propagating over shallow slopes (see, ref. 5 Fig. 1a, b). Thus, the processes observed and effects on morphology would be analogous for these smaller volume laboratory flows. Our methods are not focused on meters-thick clay-dominated flows (see, ref. 5 Fig. 1c), in which the type of flow (laminar vs. turbulent33) and viscosity4 have significant roles.

The effects of gravity (at Earth’s magnitude of 9.8 m/s2 or 3.7 m/s2 on Mars) are not entirely straightforward. When mud moves over a warm surface, lower gravity may allow chunks of material to remain aloft for longer, effectively prolonging the levitation phase and enabling the mud to spread further laterally and downslope. However, over a cold surface, the freezing process we expect is largely independent of gravity, yet greatly affects the flow’s motion. Moreover, gravity would have little effect on the hydrostatic pressure required to fracture the ice-mud crust, which allows the mud to be extruded forming new breakouts. Thus, we expect the effects of gravity to be minor relative to other reported factors.

Comparison with Mars surface condition maps

We used the Mars Climate Database (MCD) v6.1 to obtain surface pressure and surface temperature numerical maps of Mars at two times per day (5:00 and 13:00) and four times per year (Ls = 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270°) to capture the range of potential pressure-temperature (PT) conditions (https://www-mars.lmd.jussieu.fr/mcd_python/)34,35. We merged the data from the eight pressure maps with the respective eight temperature maps so each map pixel would have both a pressure and temperature value. These times were chosen because 5:00 is roughly the coldest time of day on Mars (pre-dawn), and 13:00 is roughly the warmest time of day (early afternoon). Each global map was 48 × 64 pixels, resulting in 3072 PT values for each of 8 maps. To create the density heatmap of potential Mars surface conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5), we plotted all 24,576 PT values. We then assigned each of the 24,576 PT values a unique phase (solid, liquid, gas) depending on its position in the phase diagram of water (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 5). We made a small alteration to the phase diagram shape of water to include our experimental phase diagram along the small portion of the vaporization curve between 4 and 20 mbar (average of all basaltic muds brown dashed line in Supplementary Fig. 3). We then plotted all phase points back in their map orientation. We show the four afternoon maps for the Amazonian in Fig. 2. The Noachian maps were similarly created by multiplying the pressure map values by a factor of 100x (Supplementary Fig. 6).
