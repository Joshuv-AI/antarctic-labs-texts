SynClear: A one-step synchronous clearing and labeling strategy for multiscale 3D brain mapping
Authors: Xiao Xiao, Canyu Ma, Yashuo Wang, Jiazhu Zhu, Jiajia Chen, Simei Li, Yongjie Wang, Wei Gong, Ke Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-resolution three-dimensional imaging is essential for resolving the multiscale organization of biological tissues. However, conventional workflows treat tissue clearing and molecular labeling as separate steps, leading to a kinetic mismatch between reagent transport and probe binding that limits imaging depth, labeling uniformity, and throughput. Here, we introduce SynClear, a one-step strategy that synchronizes nuclear labeling with tissue clearing by embedding fluorescent probes within a chemically engineered clearing medium. This integrated formulation enables rapid and uniform labeling across millimeter-scale samples while preserving endogenous fluorescence and remaining compatible with multiplexed immunostaining. We demonstrate the general applicability of SynClear across diverse tissue types, including mouse brain, peripheral organs, and post-mortem human cortex. In mouse brain sections, SynClear supports accurate 3D atlas registration and quantitative mapping of cytoarchitecture. In glioblastoma models, it resolves pathological features across scales, from tumor boundaries to immune microenvironments. In human cortex, it enables laminar-resolved structural analysis and neuronal subtype mapping. By coupling labeling and clearing within a single chemical framework, SynClear provides a robust and scalable platform for volumetric tissue imaging, with potential applications in both basic neuroscience and translational pathology.Keywords: Tissue clearing, 3D imaging, Spatial biology, Brain mapping, Volumetric imaging

Introduction

Deciphering the multiscale organization of the brain requires imaging strategies that preserve spatial continuity from cellular to tissue-level architecture [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Optical tissue clearing has emerged as a powerful approach for deep three-dimensional imaging by reducing light scattering and refractive index mismatch [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Existing clearing methods can be broadly categorized into solvent-based [[11], [12], [13], [14]], aqueous-based [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]], and hydrogel-based systems [[20], [21], [22]], each offering distinct advantages in transparency, structural preservation, and molecular compatibility. The physical and chemical principles underlying these approaches, particularly light-tissue interactions and mechanisms for enhancing photon penetration, have been extensively established [[23], [24], [25]], and the rapid development of clearing technologies has been comprehensively reviewed in recent years [[26], [27], [28]].

Despite these advances, optical transparency alone does not ensure biologically informative imaging of complex tissues. Molecular labeling, particularly nuclear labeling, is essential for defining cytoarchitectural landmarks, quantifying cellular populations, and enabling spatial registration to anatomical atlases [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. In most existing workflows, labeling is performed separately from clearing, either before or after the clearing process [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. This separation introduces several fundamental limitations [[38], [39], [40], [41]]. First, probe transport into dense tissues is slow and often incomplete, resulting in shallow penetration and heterogeneous labeling. Second, prolonged processing and repeated solution exchanges increase the risk of tissue deformation and signal loss. Third, chemical incompatibilities between labeling reagents and clearing media can compromise fluorescence stability. Together, these constraints limit the achievable depth, uniformity, and reproducibility of high-resolution 3D imaging.

Here, we introduce SynClear, a one-step strategy that integrates nuclear labeling directly into a chemically engineered clearing system. By exploiting the ability of clearing agents to increase tissue permeability, SynClear combines tissue clearing with probe delivery within a single chemical environment, thereby synchronizing the clearing and labelling processes—which were previously carried out at different times. This integration enables rapid and uniform nuclear labeling in millimeter-scale tissue sections while preserving endogenous fluorescence and maintaining compatibility with immunostaining. We demonstrate the general applicability of SynClear across multiple biological contexts. In thick mouse brain sections, SynClear supports accurate 3D spatial registration to standard brain atlases. In glioblastoma models, it captures pathological features across scales, from tissue architecture to immune microenvironments. In post-mortem human cortex, it enables laminar-resolved analysis of cytoarchitecture and neuronal subtype distribution at subcellular resolution.

Results

Development of SynClear for synchronous clearing and labeling

To mitigate the limitations of conventional sequential clearing and labeling workflows, including limited probe penetration, prolonged processing time, and non-uniform labeling, we developed SynClear, a one-step strategy that integrates nuclear labeling directly into the tissue clearing process (Fig. 1a). The core principle lies in utilizing the hyperhydration capacity derived from two NH2 groups in the chemical reagent, which can dissolve dense fiber proteins and disturb their hydrogen-bonding networks [41,42], thereby allowing both the clearing agent and nuclear probe to penetrate the tissue simultaneously (Fig. 1b). Instead of relying on passive post-clearing staining, SynClear leverages the inherent diffusivity of the clearing process to co-deliver nuclear probes deep into tissues, enabling synchronized clearing and labeling within a simplified workflow (Fig. 1c).

We systematically optimized the SynClear formulation by evaluating the contributions of buffering agents, hyperhydration reagents, and refractive index-matching compounds to both optical transparency and nuclear labeling performance. The final SynClear formulation combines PBS for buffering stability, urea for enhancing permeability through disruption of hydrogen-bonded protein networks, and a mixture of D-sorbitol and iohexol for refractive index matching and reduced scattering (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1a–b). This composition supports efficient clearing and stable signal retention across a range of nuclear probes (DAPI, SYTO, PI, TO-PRO-1), achieving uniform signal intensity and depth coverage in 500-μm mouse brain and 200-μm human brain sections within 30 min (Fig. 1e and f, Supplementary Fig. 1c–f). Beyond the 500-μm mouse and 200-μm human brain sections described above, SynClear also enables efficient clearing of thicker tissue samples, maintaining uniform signal intensity and depth coverage in mouse and human brain slabs up to 1 mm (Supplementary Figure 2).

We further assessed SynClear in Thy1-GFP mouse brain slices to evaluate its optical homogeneity across tissue depth. Results showed consistent transparency and nuclear labeling throughout the z-axis (Fig. 1g–i, Supplementary Fig. 3a–f). In larger coronal brain sections (10 × 6.5 × 0.5 mm3), SynClear achieved widespread nuclear labeling across anatomically distinct regions while maintaining structural integrity (Fig. 1j and k). Importantly, cellular and subcellular structures were preserved with sufficient resolution to support downstream spatial and quantitative analysis (Fig. 1l).

Universal compatibility of SynClear across labeling strategies and tissue types

To evaluate the compatibility of SynClear with multiple labeling strategies, we first applied it to Thy1-GFP mouse brain sections. Following treatment, we observed strong and uniform nuclear labeling with TO-PRO-3 (pink) throughout the tissue, establishing a reliable 3D reference framework for spatial analysis (Fig. 2a). Importantly, GFP fluorescence was well preserved, allowing high-resolution visualization of neuronal morphology and axonal projections across diverse brain regions, including the cortex, hippocampus, striatum, and amygdala (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 4). Longitudinal imaging confirmed the temporal stability of GFP signals, with minimal fluorescence quenching over three days of continuous reagent exposure (Fig. 2c and d).

We next assessed the compatibility of SynClear with immunofluorescence staining, a widely used method for detecting specific proteins and cellular phenotypes. SynClear-treated brain tissues exhibited robust co-labeling of nuclear signals with a variety of antibodies (Fig. 2e), key connectivity structures components (white: MBP for myelin, blue: NFL for axons), vasculature (red: CD31 for endothelial cells), cell-type markers (yellow: GFAP for astrocytes, orange: Iba1 for microglia), and subcellular structures (purple: Synaptophysin for synaptic vesicle). Moreover, SynClear supported labeling with neuron-specific markers, including NeuN (blue: pan-neuronal), CHAT (purple: choline acetyltransferase), and major inhibitory neuron subtypes such as CR (yellow: Calretinin), PV (orange: Parvalbumin), and NPY (pink: Neuropeptide Y) (Fig. 2f), demonstrating its utility in mapping fine cellular identities in intact tissues.

To explore broader tissue applicability, we applied SynClear to murine visceral organs with distinct anatomical and biochemical properties. Across kidney, liver, spleen, and heart, SynClear achieved simultaneous clearing and nuclear labeling, resulting in uniform transparency and preserved nuclear morphology (Fig. 2g–o). In kidneys, both glomerular structures and cortex–medulla zonation were clearly defined (Fig. 2g–I, Supplementary Fig. 5a–d). In liver, spleen, and heart tissues, major architectural features—including central veins, lymphoid follicles, and myocardial fibers—were readily resolved (Fig. 2j–o, Supplementary Fig. 5e–h). These findings support the generalizability of SynClear across tissue types with variable density and structure, while maintaining consistent labeling fidelity and imaging quality. This robust performance across multiple tissue types enables SynClear to support high-throughput volumetric analysis of complex 3D disease models. For instance, multiple samples can be rapidly processed and imaged, allowing efficient quantification of three-dimensional morphometric features and significantly reducing experimental turnaround compared to conventional multi-day protocols (Supplementary Figure 6).

Precise atlas registration in thick brain sections

Spatial alignment to brain atlases is fundamental for interpreting the relationship between structure and function in neuroanatomy. A commonly used approach involves manually identifying anatomical features in brain sections by referencing standardized atlases. SynClear enhances this process by enabling simultaneous tissue clearing and nuclear labeling, thereby establishing a dense and evenly distributed 3D nuclear reference framework suitable for precise atlas registration in thick tissue sections.

In thick coronal brain slices near Bregma +2.3 mm, we constructed a three-dimensional coordinate system using SynClear-labeled nuclei (red) and NeuN-positive neurons (cyan) (Fig. 3a). Maximum intensity projections (MIP) revealed region-specific differences in nuclear density, which facilitated boundary delineation of major brain regions such as the cortex and striatum. High-magnification 3D reconstructions of hippocampal subregions showed that the layered architecture in CA regions and compact granule cell organization in the dentate gyrus (DG) provided clear morphological cues for precise subregional annotation (Fig. 3b).

The multimodal compatibility of SynClear further enabled structural comparisons across multiple cortical areas within a unified spatial framework. At Bregma −0.6 mm, the caudate putamen (CPu) displayed lower vascular density than the lateral septal intermediate nucleus (LSI) (Fig. 3c–e), which may explain differential infarction patterns observed in clinical stroke cases. In sections near Bregma −0.1 mm, CPu nerve fibers (NFL-positive) exhibited highly ordered radial bundles that support rapid sensorimotor signal transmission, whereas fibers in the septofimbrial nucleus (SFi) formed intersecting architectures, consistent with their role in mediating cognitive flexibility across hemispheres (Fig. 3f–h). In addition, GFAP-positive astrocytes localized preferentially to region boundaries, such as the interface between the superficial gray layer (SuG) and optic layer (Op), forming morphological barriers that may regulate interregional signaling and exchange (Fig. 3i–k).

To further illustrate the analytical potential of SynClear-enabled spatial registration, we quantified inhibitory microcircuits surrounding Bregma −1.9 mm (Fig. 3l–n). Three-dimensional cell counting revealed that parvalbumin-positive (Pv+) inhibitory neurons were more abundant in cortical areas than in hippocampal regions. In the cortex, Pv+ neuron density reached 9694 ± 668.4 cells/mm3 (11.8 ± 2.1% of total cells), whereas the lowest densities were observed in the corpus callosum (333.3 ± 220.5 cells/mm3, 0.3 ± 0.2%) (Fig. 3o and p, Supplementary Fig. 7). These results suggest a region-specific balance between inhibition and plasticity: higher cortical Pv + neuron density may support rapid, precise control of neuronal activity during cognition, while lower hippocampal density may allow for greater network adaptability during memory formation.

Multi-scale 3D dissection of GBM-associated pathological remodeling

Glioblastoma (GBM) is characterized by its aggressive infiltration, which disrupts native brain architecture and promotes malignant remodeling of the surrounding microenvironment [43,44]. To better understand the multiscale pathological mechanisms underlying GBM progression [45,46], we performed high-resolution 3D analysis of tissue structural disruption and immune phenotypic transitions using SynClear-enabled imaging.

At the macroscopic level, panoramic imaging of coronal tumor sections revealed extensive tissue destruction on the GBM-affected side, including complete disintegration of key anatomical landmarks such as the forceps minor of the corpus callosum (fmi) and the caudate putamen (CPu) (Fig. 4a). Within the tumor core, boundaries between normal and abnormal tissue became diffuse, and adjacent regions exhibited reactive astrocytosis, as evidenced by GFAP + astrocyte proliferation (Fig. 4b). Quantitatively, GBM tissue showed a marked increase in nuclear density compared to non-tumor regions (2.44 ± 0.85 × 105 vs 1.07 ± 0.31 × 105 cells/μm3, n = 6 regions, p < 0.01), representing a 2.28-fold increase (Fig. 4c and d, Supplementary Fig. 8). In parallel, average nuclear volume increased by 35.6% (231.5 ± 33.5 μm3 in GBM vs 170.8 ± 15.3 μm3 in brain, p < 0.05, Fig. 4e), while the relative proportion of GFAP + astrocytes decreased significantly (from 10.05 ± 2.18% to 1.95 ± 0.91%, p < 0.001, Fig. 4f), suggesting space-occupying tumor cell proliferation dilutes native glial components.

At the cellular level, immune landscape remodeling was observed in microglial morphology and distribution. In healthy tissue, Iba1+ microglia exhibited small somas and highly branched processes characteristic of a resting state (Fig. 4g). In contrast, GBM-associated microglia adopted an amoeboid morphology with reduced branching (Fig. 4h). Quantitative analysis revealed a 194% increase in microglial density (5.64 ± 0.75 × 104 vs 1.92 ± 0.34 × 104 cells/μm3, p < 0.001, Fig. 4i), and their proportion among total cells rose significantly (29.21 ± 2.0% vs 17.12 ± 2.5%, p < 0.001, Fig. 4j). Morphologically, GBM microglia showed a 27.7% increase in cell body volume (1.57 ± 0.17 × 103 μm3 vs 1.23 ± 0.18 × 103 μm3, p < 0.01, Fig. 4k, Supplementary Fig. 9) and an almost 50% decrease in surface area (1.36 ± 0.34 × 103 μm2 vs 2.70 ± 0.47 × 103 μm2, p < 0.001, Fig. 4l). Sholl analysis further demonstrated substantial reduction in branching complexity: the number of branch intersections decreased by 68%, peak branching shifted toward the soma, and maximum branch length shortened from 54 μm to 21 μm (Fig. 4m).

These 3D morphological changes are consistent with a transition from surveillance to reactive states, implicating a loss of immune homeostasis and the emergence of a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. Importantly, these 3D morphological changes are not fully captured by conventional two-dimensional histology, due to partial sampling inherent to section-based analyses (Supplementary Figure 10).

Moreover, SynClear facilitates precise delineation of clinical GBM boundaries. From normal tissue to GBM-infiltrated and tumor-core regions, nuclear density progressively increases, nuclear volume decreases, and cellular heterogeneity shows marked variation (Supplementary Figure 11). These continuous, quantitative 3D phenotypic measurements enable more comprehensive and accurate assessment of tumor aggressiveness than traditional 2D approaches.

3D mapping of human cortical laminar organization

The six-layered structure of the human cerebral cortex underlies many higher cognitive functions and is defined by region-specific cytoarchitecture. By synchronizing tissue clearing and nuclear labeling, SynClear enables high-resolution visualization of laminar organization in thick human brain sections, establishing a reliable spatial reference for quantitative 3D analysis (Supplementary Figure 12).

In frontal lobe tissues triple-labeled with α-SMA, CD31, and PI, we observed clear demarcation between gray and white matter based on vascular and nuclear features (Fig. 5a). Gray matter exhibited dense capillary networks, whereas white matter showed more aligned, sparsely branching vasculature consistent with axonal fiber orientation. The distribution pattern of PI-labeled nuclei across cortical depth supported gradient-based laminar segmentation (Fig. 5b–e). Quantitative measurements revealed that vascular diameters decreased progressively from superficial to deeper layers: arteries (labeled by α-SMA) tapered from 25.83 ± 5.19 μm in L1 to 20.38 ± 2.33 μm in L6 (Fig. 5f), while vasculature (labeled by CD31) declined from 12.14 ± 1.39 μm to 8.52 ± 0.98 μm (Fig. 5g). In contrast, vascular diameters increased again in white matter. Layer-specific differences were also observed in nuclear density. Cell numbers increased from L1 (6.36 ± 0.44 × 104 cells/mm3) to L3 (16.62 ± 1.17 × 104 cells/mm3), reaching a maximum in the white matter (22.98 ± 0.70 × 104 cells/mm3) (Fig. 5h), highlighting regional variation in cell packing.

SynClear's resolution allowed subtype-level characterization of neuronal populations. To quantitatively characterize the laminar organization, we analyzed the distribution of NeuN-positive mature neurons relative to total cells (PI-labeled) across cortical depths. In the sampled human frontal cortex, layer II exhibited the highest proportion of NeuN + neurons (36.96 ± 0.24% of total cells), while layer I contained the lowest proportion (8.17 ± 0.15%) (Fig. 5i–k). This laminar-specific distribution pattern is clearly visualized in the three-dimensional reconstructions and reflects the distinct cellular composition of each layer. Inhibitory Pv + interneurons were more enriched in deeper layers, peaking in L4 (8.04 ± 1.38%) and comprising less than 1% in L2 (Fig. 5l–n). Conversely, CR + interneurons were more prevalent in superficial layers, with the highest density in L2 (4.96 ± 1.20%) and minimal presence in L5 (Fig. 5o–q). Notably, the varying patterns across layers in different samples arose from the distinct distribution of molecular markers within the unified PI-based framework. SynClear's multiplex compatibility allowed simultaneous imaging of these markers, revealing intrinsic heterogeneity without compromising spatial accuracy.

Finally, SynClear-enabled imaging revealed microstructural interactions between glial populations in situ. Specifically, contacts between microglia and astrocytes were resolved in 3D (Fig. 5r and s), suggesting potential cellular interfaces involved in maintaining cortical homeostasis and coordinating immune responses within the central nervous system.

Discussion

This study introduces SynClear, a strategy that integrates tissue clearing and nuclear labeling within a single chemical system to overcome limitations of conventional sequential workflows. The core principle lies in utilizing the strong hyperhydration ability generated by the two NH2 groups in urea [41,42], which dissolves dense fibrin and disrupts its hydrogen-bond network, thereby enabling the clearing agent and nuclear probe to penetrate the tissue simultaneously. By embedding fluorescent probes directly into the clearing medium, SynClear couples probe transport with tissue permeabilization, thereby synchronizing processes that are otherwise temporally decoupled. This integration improves labeling uniformity, reduces processing time, and supports compatibility with multimodal imaging, providing a practical framework for consistent volumetric tissue analysis.

The performance of SynClear arises from a chemically coordinated formulation. Through systematic optimization, we identified complementary roles for buffering, hyperhydration, and refractive index matching components [[47], [48], [49], [50]]. The final formulation consists of PBS, urea, D-sorbitol, and iohexol, which together enhance tissue permeability and optical homogeneity while maintaining probe stability. This balance enables uniform labeling and clearing of 500-μm-thick brain sections within 30 min, while preserving endogenous fluorescence and compatibility with immunostaining. More broadly, these results demonstrate that coordinated chemical environments can regulate transport and labeling processes in thick tissues and address a key limitation of diffusion-limited approaches [[26], [27], [28]]. Compared to conventional two-dimensional histology, SynClear reduces potential biases arising from partial sampling and sectioning, providing more accurate volumetric morphological information.

Beyond workflow efficiency, SynClear establishes a dense and uniform nuclear reference framework for three-dimensional spatial analysis. In the mouse brain, this framework enables accurate atlas registration and region-resolved quantification of cytoarchitecture. In glioblastoma models, SynClear captures structural disruption and immune remodeling across scales, linking tissue-level architecture with cellular phenotypes. In human cortex, it enables laminar-resolved analysis of cytoarchitecture and vascular organization and supports quantitative investigation of layer-specific structure. In addition, SynClear facilitates high-throughput volumetric analysis for preclinical pharmacodynamic studies and enables three-dimensional assessment of clinical specimens, providing quantitative insights that are difficult to achieve with conventional 2D approaches. Together, these results demonstrate that SynClear provides a general platform for multiscale tissue mapping.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the current implementation is optimized for millimeter-thick tissue sections rather than whole-organ clearing. As sample size increases, reagent transport and optical homogeneity become limiting, which reduces performance in larger specimens. Second, the present system has been validated primarily for nuclear labeling, and integration with more complex labeling strategies such as whole-mount immunostaining or FISH remains to be established. Third, downstream analysis currently relies on standard image-processing tools, and dedicated pipelines for large-scale quantitative analysis will be required to fully leverage the generated datasets. In addition, while SynClear currently does not support seamless multi-section stitching for larger volumes, future improvements in volumetric registration algorithms and deformation-correction methods may enable accurate reconstruction of continuous structures across adjacent sections, representing a promising direction for methodological development.

In summary, SynClear provides a chemically integrated approach for synchronizing tissue clearing and labeling in thick specimens. By addressing the mismatch between probe transport and labeling kinetics, it enables rapid, uniform, and scalable 3D imaging. With further development in probe compatibility and computational analysis, SynClear may facilitate broader adoption of volumetric imaging in both basic research and translational settings.

Materials and methods

Mice samples preparation

C57BL/6 and Thy1-GFP mice were used in this study for prototyping the sample preparation and imaging methods. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Advisory Committee at Zhejiang University and the National Institutes of Health's Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All mice were reserved in a stable condition of temperature (25 °C) and humidity (60%), witha 12-h light/dark cycle (9:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m.) and unlimited access to food and water. The mice were deeply anaesthetized with sodium pentobarbital [1%, wt/vol, intraperitoneal (i.p.)] and transcardially perfused with 0.01M phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Coolaber, SL61002) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Biosharp, BL539A) for fixation. Desired organs were excised from the perfused mice bodies. All harvest samples were post-fixed overnight in 4% PFA at 4 °C. Sample sections were sliced by using a commercial vibratome (Leica, VT1200S).

Collection of human brain tissue

The tissues used in this study were obtained under donor consent and provided by the National Health and Disease Human Brain Tissue Resource Center in China. Approximately 1 cm3 fresh brain blocks were collected and fixed in 4% PFA for one day before proceeding. Sample sections were sliced by using a commercial vibratome (Leica, VT1200S).

GBM model preparation

GL261 line glioma cells were used. The cells were taken from the logarithmic growth phase, the single-cell suspension with a concentration of 5 × 107/ml was prepared with PBS. The injection was completed within 30 min after the cell suspension was made. The C57 mice were placed and fixed in a stereotactic apparatus and tumor cells in a volume of 2 μL were injected 3.0 mm in depth over a period of 5 min 2.0 mm anterior and −1.0 mm lateral to the bregma. The incision was closed with surgical stables. After the operation, the mice were regularly observed for feeding and drinking, and complete brain tissue samples were taken 10 days later.

SynClear for clearing

The SynClear regent was prepared as 6M urea (Vetec, V900119) 15% (wt/vol) D-sorbitol (Vetec, V900390),50% (wt/vol) iohexol (Aladdin, I134719) dissolved in PBS. The corresponding fluorescent probe was dissolved in solution. The working concentration of DAPI (Sigma, D8417) was 1 μg/mL. SYTO 16 (Thermofisher, S7578), PI (Thermofisher, P3566), and TO-PRO-3 (Thermofisher, T3605) were diluted 1:100. Fixed samples were washed with PBS and then sopped up by KimWipes. As for clearing, samples were incubated in SynClear regent at 37 °C with gentle shaking. Heme-rich samples (mouse organs and human brain) can optionally be decolorized first with decolorization reagent which contains 10% w/v CHAPS (Aladdin, C141274) and 25% w/v N-MethyldiethanolaMine (N-MEDA, Aladdin, B106251) mixed with distilled water [5].

Other clearing methods

The clearing methods including SHANEL, iDISCO, CUBIC-L/R, and FOCM followed the protocol as below. Considering 500-μm-thick slices clearing, the incubation time of every method was reduced accordingly.

SHANEL [5]. Tissue slices were incubated at 37 °C in 200 mL of a 10% w/v CHAPS and 25% w/v N-MEDA solution for 30 min. After nuclear labeling, the dehydration of the sample was done with a series of solutions: 200 mL EtOH/DiH2O (50%, 70%, 100%, 100% v/v), followed by delipidation with DCM/MeOH (2:1 v/v), then proceeded with BABB solution for refractive index matching until complete transparency.

iDISCO [51]. Tissue slices were dehydrated in graded tetrahydrofuran (Sigma Aldrich, 186,562) series of 50%, 70%, and twice 100% (v/v) and then bleached with 5% H2O2 in 20% DMSO/methanol. After nuclear labeling, samples incubated in dichloromethane (DCM) (Sigma Aldrich, 270,997). Finally, samples were incubated in dibenzyl ether (DBE) (Sigma Aldrich, 108,014) until clear.

CUBIC-L/R [17]. CUBIC-L [a mixture of 10% wt% Triton X-100 and 10% wt% N-buthyldiethanolamine (Aladdin, B106251)] and CUBIC-R [a mixture of 45 wt% antipyrine (Aladdin, A110660), 30 wt% nicotinamide (Aladdin, N105042), 0.5 wt% N-buthyldiethanolamine] were prepared for tissue clearing. Tissue slices were incubated in CUBIC-L at 37 °C with gentle shaking followed by PBS washing. After nuclear labeling, the slices were incubated and stored in CUBIC-R for refractive index matching at room temperature.

FOCM [52]. FOCM reagent was prepared as 20% (wt/vol) urea (Vetec, V900119), 30% (wt/vol) D-sorbitol (Vetec, V900390),5% (wt/vol) glycerol (Vetec, V900122) dissolved in DMSO. After nuclear labeling, tissue slices were incubated in FOCM reagent.

Immunostaining

Mouse organ samples: fixed samples were pre-treated with PBST at room temperature for 3∼6 h and then subjected to immunostaining with the primary antibodies in 500 μL 0.2% PBST containing 1% donkey serum albumin at 37 °C for 2 days with rotation. Samples were washed with PBS for three times and incubated in secondary antibodies in the same condition.

Human brain samples: fixed samples were pre-treated with 10% w/v CHAPS and 25% w/v N-MEDA at 37 °C overnight. After washing with PBS for three times at room temperature, samples were transferred to blocking buffer (0.2% w/v Triton X-100, 10% v/v DMSO, 5% v/v donkey serum dissolved in PBS) at 37 °C for 12 h. Then the samples were incubated with primary antibodies solution (0.2% w/v Triton X-100, 5% v/v DMSO, 3% v/v donkey serum dissolved in PBS) at 37 °C for 2 days with rotation. After washed with PBS for three times, samples were incubated in secondary antibodies in the same condition. Antibodies was shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Imaging

Fluorescence images were used different kinds of confocal microscopes: the Olympus FV3000 was used for large-field imaging. The images were done using a 10× air objective lens (Olympus, UPLSAPO 10×, N.A. 0.4, WD 3.1 mm) and 20x air objective lens (Olympus, UPLSAPO 20×, N.A. 0.75, WD 0.6 mm). The second confocal microscope is the Olympus BX61 Upright Metallurgical Microscope for more precise imaging. The images were done using a 25x water objective and 40× oil objective lens (Olympus, UPLSAPO 40×, N.A. 1.40, WD 0.13 mm).

Quantifications

Calculation of relative intensity and signal-to-background ratios (SBR). The relative intensity and SBR were calculated to assess optical homogeneity of clearing and labeling. For the relative intensity, all area in the image was calculated as the fluorescence intensity. The relative intensity was calculated as the fluorescence intensity in each depth divided by the fluorescence intensity in the surface layer. For the SBR, the ‘threshold’ function in the Fiji software was used to extract signals from image, and the mean value of the signal area was calculated as the average intensity of the fluorescence signal. The region without signal area in the image was manually selected using the marking function, and the mean value of this region was calculated as the average intensity of the background. The SBR was calculated as the mean signal intensity divided by the mean background intensity.

Normalized intensity. The normalized intensity was calculated to assess the fluorescence preservation. We quantitatively calculated the normalized fluorescence intensity of the white dotted line at each time point. The maximum intensity projection (MIP) of image stack (10-μm-thick) was acquired from the surface of brain slice.

Imaging processing

All raw image data were collected in OIB format and transformed to 8-bit images with Fiji for further statistical analysis. We used Imaris (bitplane) for 3D reconstruction and segmentation. The number of nuclei and cells was calculated through the “Spot” function. The cell morphology and volume calculations were based on the automatic “Surface” function. Morphological analysis of microglia was performed using the ‘Sholl Analysis’ function in Fiji.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used to analyze the data, and all data were expressed as the mean ± SD. Inter-group comparisons were performed using One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Comparisons were performed by using paired t-tests. Images used by optical clearing methods to quantify the transparency effect of tissues were taken by a Sony A6000 camera on a cardboard with a white grid and a black background.
