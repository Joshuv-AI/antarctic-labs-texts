Prevalence and development of sarcopenia and malnutrition in neurological patients during inpatient rehabilitation: a prospective cohort with an embedded, cross-sectional concurrent validity study for regional sarcopenia measurements
Authors: Angela Cairoli, Martina Grinzinger, Chantal Coenegracht, Margret Hund-Georgiadis, Clare Maguire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence indicates that neurological patients exhibit high levels of sarcopenia due to disease, inactivity and malnutrition, which itself often occurs due to feeding impairments. Knowledge about the evolution of these pathologies during inpatient rehabilitation is lacking. Additionally, current gold-standard measures assess whole-body sarcopenia although regional presentations may be common in neurological patients.The aims of this study are to investigate the prevalence and development of sarcopenia and malnutrition over six months during in-patient neurological rehabilitation using 1.) gold-standard measurements (EWGSOP2 Criteria) and 2.) regional measurements (segmental BIA) for sarcopenia, to evaluate the concurrent-validity.MethodsPatients admitted to a neurological rehabilitation clinic between April-June 2023 were assessed for eligibility and, if eligible, followed for six months. Those eligible were screened for malnutrition and sarcopenia risk at admission, three weeks, three months, and six months or three days prior to discharge, those screening positive underwent diagnostic testing. Sarcopenia was assessed using gold-standard and regional (index) tests.ResultsFrom 105 individuals admitted, 45 (48.9% male) were included and analyzed. Malnutrition: baseline prevalence was 31.1% (95% CI 18.2-46.6), which dropped to 0% (95% CI 0-28.5) after 6 months. Sarcopenia: baseline prevalence was 37.8% (95% CI 23.8-53.5), which dropped to 26.2% (95% CI 13.9-42) at 3 weeks and did not change again. The baseline prevalence of regional sarcopenia was higher (42.2%), but the trends were similar. Regional measurements demonstrated high sensitivity (100%), specificity (93-100%) and levels of agreement (k=0.91-1.0) compared to gold standard.ConclusionMalnutrition and sarcopenia are common in neurological patients. Regional sarcopenia tests have similar validity to whole-body assessments and can find asymmetric sarcopenia, which makes it useful for targeted rehabilitation in neurological patients.Clinical trial numberRegistered at ClinicalTrials.gov with the identifier 2022-01098.Keywords: Malnutrition, Sarcopenia, Prevalence, Regional BIA Measurements, Inpatient Neurological Rehabilitation

Background

Neurological patients are at high risk of developing a combined malnutrition-sarcopenia syndrome due to reduced mobility, disease-related metabolic changes and frequent feeding difficulties. Malnutrition is associated with increased complications, prolonged hospitalization and higher morbidity and mortality [1]. In Switzerland, approximately 18% of hospitalized patients are affected, with prevalence rising with age [1].

Sarcopenia is defined as generalized skeletal muscle disorder characterized by progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength and function related to the aging processes (primary sarcopenia) or to pathogenetic mechanisms induced by diseases, as well as inactivity and malnutrition (secondary sarcopenia) [2, 3]. Sarcopenia is correlated with higher risk of falls, disability, and reduced quality of life [4–7].

Although sarcopenia prevalence has been widely studied at hospital admission [8],little is known about progression during inpatient rehabilitation. Muscle changes can occur within four hours after a neurological insult [9], and up to 10% of muscle mass may be lost within a week of bed-rest even in healthy individuals [10]. Stroke, traumatic brain injury (TBI) or spinal cord injury (SCI) patients are often bed-bound in the acute phase placing them at elevated risk.

Post-stroke muscle alterations, including mass loss, reduced fiber cross-sectional area, and increased intramuscular fat infiltration, have been documented within 3 weeks to 6 months, affecting both paretic and non-paretic limbs [11–13].

In SCI, immediate muscle disuse occurs due to motor neuron injury, muscle denervation, and microvascular damage, resulting in rapid morphological and histochemical muscle changes [14]. Muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) decline typically begins within one month post-injury [15].

Feeding difficulties and dysphagia further exacerbate the risk of malnutrition and sarcopenia in neurological patients. Contributing factors include cognitive dysfunctions, unconsciousness, oropharyngeal dysphagia, increased metabolic needs or perceptual deficits [16]. Impaired oral intake, measured via the Functional Oral Intake Scale, has been independently associated with post-stroke sarcopenia during acute hospitalization [17].

Despite these findings, detailed knowledge of sarcopenia and malnutrition prevalence and trajectory during neurological inpatient rehabilitation remains scarce.

The associated risks underscore the urgent need for comprehensive data on nutritional status and sarcopenia during neurological rehabilitation, alongside appropriate diagnostic tools and interventions tailored to this population.

A key challenge in assessing sarcopenia in neurological patients is the regional and asymmetric muscle loss due to pathophysiological mechanisms.

Studies in stroke, multiple sclerosis (MS), and SCI patients demonstrate differential muscle atrophy across body regions indicating that regional sarcopenia is more prevalent than whole-body sarcopenia in neurological patients [18–21].

These findings raise concerns about the validity of traditional whole-body screening and diagnosis tools in neurological populations. Functional tests may be confounded by neurological impairments rather than sarcopenia itself, risking misclassification. Tests with poor validity for neurological patients may lead to an inaccurate estimation of sarcopenia [14]. In addition, whole body bioelectrical impedance analysis/bioimpedance analysis (BIA) which is commonly used in the diagnosis of sarcopenia, may yield inaccurate estimates due to regional muscle mass disparities.

Regional or segmental BIA offers a promising alternative. Emerging evidence suggests that regional BIA measurements can detect localized differences in body composition in both neurological and non-neurological cohorts [22–26]. Segmental BIA isolates impedance values for individual limbs, allowing direct estimation of lean soft tissue in specific regions. This makes it possible to detect muscle asymmetry that whole-body calculations may mask, revealing clinically relevant regional deficits. Assessment of muscle symmetry and asymmetry via regional BIA has shown acceptable validity for monitoring muscle volume changes in healthy individuals [27–31]. Alternative regional measurements have also been proposed for stroke patients who can walk [32]. This approach offers a practical method for evaluating regional muscle mass alterations.

We propose that combining regional muscle mass assessments—examining side-to-side and upper-to-lower body differences—with regional muscle strength measurements could provide a more precise understanding of sarcopenia prevalence and progression, including regional sarcopenia, in neurological rehabilitation patients.

Given the extended length of stay typical in neurological rehabilitation, often several weeks to months [33, 34], there is a need for longitudinal data capturing how malnutrition and sarcopenia evolve over this period.

assess the prevalence and evolution of malnutrition and sarcopenia during neurological inpatient rehabilitation at baseline, 3 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months or three days before discharge using current gold-standard screening and diagnostic measurements using EWGSOP2 guidelines [3].

Methods

To evaluate the prevalence and evolution of malnutrition and sarcopenia in neurological patients using gold-standard measurements during in-patient rehabilitation, a prospective cohort study design using the Strobe guidelines [35] was carried out.

To determine the concurrent criterion validity of regional sarcopenia measurements, an embedded cohort selection, cross-sectional study design [36] was used based on the STARD guidelines [37].

The study protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with the identifier 2022 − 01098.

Inclusion criteria were female and male adults ≥ 18 years old, in-patients in neurological rehabilitation.

Recruitment took place between April 2023 and June 2023 at Rehab Basel a clinic for neurorehabilitation and paraplegiology, and recruited patients were followed for six months finishing at the end of December 2023.

Eligibility screening

Research staff were informed daily of all new admissions to the clinic per mail by the Patient Administration department. All new admissions were screened for eligibility by the research staff using the electronical patient record system. Research staff informed eligible patients verbally and in writing, and obtained their informed consent using the Ethics Committee-approved form before study inclusion. Informed consent to participate in the study was provided by all participants.

Screening and recruitment began within 72 h or if not feasible maximum four days after admission to rehabilitation. Following positive screening, diagnostic testing was conducted within five or if not feasible maximum seven days after entry.

Re-measurement was conducted after three weeks, three months, and six months following the initial assessments or within three days before discharge if discharge occurred earlier, to ensure a consistent reassessment schedule across participants.

Measurements that were routinely conducted during the rehabilitation stay (e.g. measurement of height and weight) were used for the study and were not duplicated if not required.

Statistical analyses

A precision-based approach was used for the sample size calculation for the prevalence of sarcopenia.

https://www.ctu.unibe.ch/about_us/news/presize_precision_based_sample_size_calculation/index_eng.html.

Current measurements of sarcopenia prevalence in patients with neurological disease are wide and imprecise. Cai Yingying et al. (2021) estimated prevalence between 6% and 55% in Parkinsons patients [38]. In stroke patients’ measurements indicate rates are 33%-55% [18], 76% in spinal cord injury patients [39] and between 10% and 20% in persons with multiple sclerosis [20]. Existing data must therefore be used cautiously when estimating possible prevalence levels in the broad neurological patient groups in REHAB. Our own point-prevalence measurements, which were done in 2023, indicated that sarcopenia in this clinic was at the level of approximately 25% across all patient groups [40].

Considering both published data and measurements in-house, to estimate a prevalence of 30%-50% with a wide confidence interval of less than 25%, 50 to 60 patients would be required. Assuming a drop-out rate of 10%, 70 patients would need to be recruited.

The study population was characterized using frequencies (n) and percentages (%) for categorical variables, and means and standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed, continuous data. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25). Normality was evaluated using Shapiro-Wilk tests and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. The risk, prevalence and co-occurence of sarcopenia and malnutrition, using both gold-standard and index tests (regional measurements) were reported as percentage (%) with [40] 95% confidence intervals (CI).

To evaluate if variations in prevalence across the four time points were statistically significant we used a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model for binary outcomes that took into account the correlation of repeated measurements within patients [41].

The concurrent criterion validity of the regional measurements for sarcopenia (index test) compared to whole-body measurements (gold-standard) was calculated using sensitivity and specificity and Cohen’s kappa (k) for measurement of agreement [42].

(κ) = (po – pc) / (1 – pc), po = observed agreement and pc = agreement expected by chance.

The kappa value reflects the level of agreement or reliability with a value from − 1 to + 1, where 1 represents perfect agreement, and 0 represents the agreement expected from random chance. κ = 0.10–20 indicates slight agreement, κ = 0.21–0.40 indicates fair agreement, κ = 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement, κ = 0.61–0.80 substantial agreement and κ = 0.80-1 almost perfect agreement [43].

While the total sample size was calculated to achieve the desired precision for prevalence estimates, the number of sarcopenia cases may limit the precision of sensitivity, specificity and kappa estimates, particularly at later follow-up time points.

Measurement procedures

Gold-standard, whole body measurements were conducted according to the European Guidelines of Sarcopenia: revised European consensus on definition and diagnosis, which was written by the Writing Group for the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) [3]. Screening and confirmatory diagnostic test for malnutrition were measured concurrently [44, 45].

Screening (within the first four days after rehabilitation entry):

The following measurements and assessments were conducted for screening: height and weight (BMI), Nutrition Risk Score (NRS-2002), SARC-F Questionnaire [6, 46], Hand grip strength with Martin-Vigorimeter on the dominant side, and 5x Chair-Stand Test. Further information about cut-off points and execution are available in the appendix.

When a test returned a positive result, it was considered a positive risk for malnutrition and/or sarcopenia and the diagnostic assessment was conducted.

Diagnostic and severity of disease assessment following positive screening results (within the first seven days after rehabilitation entry):

Malnutrition

The assessment for diagnosis and severity of malnutrition was determined according to the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria (GLIM) [2] and were completed by a trained dietician. GLIM is a reliable and valid measurement for the identification of malnutrition in a hospital setting [47].

The diagnosis of malnutrition is given if at least one phenotypic and one etiologic criterion is met [2].

The severity of malnutrition was graded into stage 1 (moderate) and stage 2 (severe) malnutrition following the thresholds for at least one phenotypic criterion by GLIM [2].

Gold-standard sarcopenia

In the case of elevated risk for sarcopenia, muscle mass was measured by bioimpedance analysis which enables the evaluation of the appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASMM) [48].

Bioimpedance analysis was performed using the BIA 101 BIVA Pro device from the Akern company with the classic tetrapolar technique. The analysis is a standard measurement for the determination of body composition with the advantage of being quick, simple, and non-invasive. By electrical resistance measurement, reactance (Rz), resistance (Xc) and phase angle (PA) were measured as basic data of impedance measurement. After gender, age, weight, and height were specified, fat-free mass (FFM), body-cell mass (BCM), fat mass (FM), total body water (BTW), and intra-cellular (ICW), and extra-cellular water were determined (BIA BIVA PRO, Software BODYGRAM HBO, Akern, distributed by Fresenius Kabi AG). For the evaluation of appendicular skeletal muscle mass the manufacturer algorithm was used. The calculation of ASMM was performed using the BODYGRAM HBO software, version 3.0.25 using the manufacturer’s proprietary equation for ASMM (Akern) distributed by Fresenius Kabi AG).

Cut-off points for low appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMMI), calculated from ASMM/height, were < 7 kg/m2 for men and < 5.5 kg/m2 for women [3].

Phase angle of muscle fibres (PhA) as well as impedance and resistance data enable the estimation of volumes of lean soft tissue (LST) and changes therein. This enables comparison of volumes of lean soft tissue between left and right body sides as well as between upper and lower body (above and below the anterior superior iliac spines). Volumetric alterations can also be traced. The error on the LST is estimated between from 0.2 to 0.7 kg [49].

Diagnosis of sarcopenia was confirmed when muscle strength (via handgrip strength or chair stand test) and muscle quantity (via BIA) were low according to the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) guidelines [3].

Measurement of severity

To determine severity of sarcopenia the timed-up-and-go-test (TUG) was carried out. The TUG is a reliable and valid measurement of mobility and balance [50].

Score interpretation: The EWGSOP2 criteria define a cut-off value of ≥ 20 s for the TUG as severe sarcopenia.

Index-test sarcopenia -regional measurements

Screening (within the first four days after rehabilitation entry):

The same measurements and assessments were conducted as for the gold-standard measurements. Additionally, hand grip strength and muscle strength of myotome L3 (M. quadriceps) was tested on both sides. A Grade 3 threshold was selected because it represents the lowest level at which anti-gravity movement is possible, an important functional boundary relevant for ambulation and lower-limb performance.

Muscle strength of myotome L3 (M. quadriceps) on both sides:

The L3 myotome (quadriceps) was tested bilaterally using the standardized procedure and 6-point grading scale described by the American Spinal Cord Injury Association (ASIA) testing procedure in the International Standards for the Classification of Spinal Cord Injury [51]. A measurement of three repetitions was completed with selection of the strongest measurement. To define low quadriceps strength, cut-off points of ≤ Grade three: active movement, full range of movement (ROM) against gravity, was applied for each leg.

Assessment (within the first seven days after rehabilitation entry):

Assessment for sarcopenia and severity

In addition, to the gold-standard measurements, two additional electrodes were placed between the joint bones of both wrist and ankle on the opposite side of the body.

Diagnosis of sarcopenia was confirmed when muscle strength (via handgrip strength, muscle strength of myotome L3 (M. quadriceps) or chair stand test) and muscle quantity (via BIA) were low according to the EWGSOP2 guidelines [3].

Measurement of severity

If differences in muscle strength were measured between limbs (upper-limb comparison and lower-limb comparison), regional BIA measurements was completed to evaluate muscle symmetry/asymmetry. Muscle asymmetry in the presence of differences in muscle strength, may indicate the presence of regional sarcopenia. If this was the case, in addition to the TUG, the Box and Block Test (BBT) was carried out to determine severity.

Box and block test

The BBT is a reliable and valid measure of arm function and gross manual dexterity. It can be used with a wide range of patient populations including neurological patients [52, 53].

Patients were scored based on the number of blocks transferred from one compartment to the other compartment in 60 s. Higher scores indicate better arm function and gross manual dexterity.

Score interpretation

No cut-off values have yet been defined for the BBT. However, normal age- and sex-matched values have been measured [52]. For Box and Block Test, we operationally defined severe sarcopenia as a performance more than 2 standard deviations below age- and sex-matched norms, since there are no established cut-offs for it. This is in line with methods used in related neurological function studies.

Prevalence of malnutrition

Malnutrition prevalence showed a clear and steady decline throughout the rehabilitation period, decreasing from 31.1% (18.2–46.6) at baseline to 0% at six months. 17.8% of confirmed cases were classified as severe at baseline, 11.1% as moderate. By 3 months no cases were classified as severe and moderate cases had dropped to 7.1%. (Table 6.) Participants who screened positive for risk of malnutrition were younger at baseline, had a lower BMI and a lower FIM total score. At the follow-up times, the positively screened participants were older than the negative ones, but BMI and FIM total score remained lower in the positively screened group. (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study we investigated the prevalence and development of malnutrition and sarcopenia during in-patient rehabilitation in neurological patients from admission to six months or discharge, using current gold-standard (whole body) sarcopenia assessments. Given that sarcopenia in neurological patients may show greater regional variations than in healthy aging, we also investigated the concurrent validity of regional sarcopenia assessments (including segmental BIA: index test) compared to gold-standard measures. To our knowledge, this is the first study to longitudinally assess regional sarcopenia in neurological patients during rehabilitation.

The most notable finding of this prospective cohort is the contrasting progression of malnutrition and sarcopenia during neurological rehabilitation. Malnutrition demonstrated a steady and complete resolution over six months, whereas sarcopenia showed an initial improvement followed by a persistent plateau, suggesting that standard nutritional support alone, although important, may be insufficient to reverse muscle deficits.

Whilst the reasons for the limited improvement in sarcopenia in this study remain unclear, it is likely they reflect both inadequate resistance-training intensity and underlying neuromuscular pathology. One potential contributing factor is the under-dosing of resistance training. The American Physical Therapy Association (APTA), through its “Choosing Wisely” campaign, identified under-dosed strength training in geriatric populations as one of five commonly used therapeutic interventions more likely to result in harm than benefit [55]. Although this recommendation targets older adults, it could also be relevant to individuals with neurological conditions and warrants further investigation in this context. Additionally, the relative impact of non-modifiable neuromuscular pathologies on sarcopenia development is uncertain and requires further elucidation.

Sarcopenia is an independent risk factor for death [56]. It is correlated with higher risk of falls, disability, reduced quality of life and can lead to the loss of independence in activities of daily living (ADL’s) [4–7]. The high levels of sarcopenia reported in this study, a high of 37.8% (95% CI 23.8–53.5) at baseline and a minimum of 26.2 (13.9–42) at 3 weeks, fall within previously reported ranges which vary from 10% to 57%, depending on diagnostic criteria and populations studied [27, 57, 58]. The high rates and serious consequences of persistent sarcopenia, which can include the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [59, 60] and sarcopenic obesity [39], emphasize the importance of deeper insights into this problem, to guide individual and efficacious interventions.

The complete resolution of malnutrition is particularly notable given the high prevalence of feeding and swallowing impairments in neurological populations [16, 61]. This suggests that structured nutritional support during rehabilitation can fully correct acute malnutrition, even in patients with complex needs.

Whilst prior studies have reported malnutrition rates in hospitalized neurological patients (Kang et al., 2018: 22%) [62], our study is distinct in its use of GLIM criteria and extended follow-up. Compared to Maruyama et al. (2017) [63], who reported a 27% malnutrition rate at discharge, none of the patients in this study were malnourished when leaving hospital.

Given that neurological patients frequently experience feeding and swallowing impairments [16, 61], these results are significant. The improvements in our study likely result from individualized nutritional support by dietitians and regular balanced meal provision. During rehabilitation, early and ongoing nutritional management is essential, particularly in the context of daily training to increase strength, endurance and functional abilities. Sufficient protein and caloric intake is vital to support muscle mass development [64].

Despite the improvements during in-patient stay, the high baseline prevalence is noteworthy. Whilst studies have measured the prevalence of malnutrition in hospitalized, neurological patients, data is scarce for those living in the community. Feeding and swallowing difficulties may lead to nutritional deficits at home in the absence of professional support justifying further studies in this area.

Our findings emphasize the importance of routine screening for malnutrition and sarcopenia in neurological rehabilitation. Confirmed cases should prompt individualized and correctly dosed interventions by dietitians and physiotherapists. Individual nutritional support during inpatient stays has been shown to improve clinical outcomes [65, 66] and reduce in-hospital mortality [67]. Nutritional support is particularly important for patients engaging in intensive strength training [64]. Despite the benefits, data from Kaegi-Braun et al. (2021) suggest that only 30.6% of undernourished patients received support from a dietitian.

Regional sarcopenia assessments yielded a baseline prevalence of 42.2% (95% CI: 27.7–57.8), decreasing to 31% (95% CI 17.6–47.1) at three weeks, then stabilizing until six months. These results closely align with gold-standard values, supporting their concurrent validity. Sensitivity, specificity and Level of Agreement measurements were excellent (100%, 93–100%, k = 0.91-1.0).

Regional assessments revealed clinically meaningful asymmetries that whole-body BIA could not detect. This supports their use in tailoring rehabilitation strategies, particularly for patients with unilateral weakness or SCI. Asymmetrical or limb-specific sarcopenia patterns were identified in 5 of 45 participants (11.1%). Of these five, two participants (4.4%) were not detected by the gold-standard whole-body method. Thus, the 11.1% reflects all cases where regional testing added clinically relevant localization detail, while the 4.4% represents cases missed entirely by the gold-standard. These findings highlight that neurological patients often exhibit limb-specific muscle loss, which may be masked when relying solely on whole-body measurements. The regional severity data further support this added diagnostic value. At baseline, severe sarcopenia was more prevalent in the upper extremities (35.6% bilaterally) than in the lower extremities (33.3%), and further discrepencies were identified across follow-up assessments (Table 9).

Regional strength assessments in this study were adapted from and based on the principles of gold-standard protocols. Handgrip strength is typically measured in the dominant hand, however, in neurological patients, the dominant side may be more affected. Therefore, we assessed both hands to capture potential asymmetries. For the lower limbs, the 5x Chair-Stand Test is commonly used, but many neurological patients are unable to stand from sitting independently due to other deficits than muscle weakness. To more accurately assess lower limb strength in this population, we measured L3 myotome strength using the American Spinal Cord Injury Association (ASIA) motor score. The cut-off of M3 was chosen as the minimal strength required for functional activites such as sit-stand and transfers.

To evaluate sarcopenia severity, the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test is standard. While TUG reflects lower limb and trunk function, it provides limited insight into upper limb performance. To address this, we incorporated the Box and Block Test, which includes an upper limb endurance component. These adaptations allowed for a more comprehensive assessment of regional muscle function.

Although regional testing required 5–10 additional minutes and more materials such as additional electrodes, it provided a more accurate indication of sarcopenia location in 11% of patients and detected two people unidentified by the gold-standard. In our opinion, the more precise diagnostic information justified the added time and cost and may enable more targeted rehabilitation interventions.

This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. The sample size, particularly at later follow-up points, was small and may limit the precision of longitudinal estimates. The heterogeneity of neurological diagnoses introduces variability that may influence sarcopenia presentation. The proprietary nature of the BIA algorithm restricts full methodological transparency. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results and designing future research.

Conclusion

Malnutrition-sarcopenia interactions are common in neurological patients. Whilst malnutrition can be effectively eradicated with appropriate nutritional support during in-patient rehabilitation, sarcopenia is more resistant to change. Larger studies are needed to determine the extent to which sarcopenia stems from non-modifiable neurological pathology versus modifiable rehabilitation-related factors.

In clinical practice, the added value of regional sarcopenia assessment lies in its ability to reveal limb-specific muscle deficits that whole-body methods may overlook, enabling more targeted and individualized rehabilitation strategies. These findings illustrate the potential for regional testing to guide precision therapy in neurological populations.

Further research should explore sarcopenia progression in homogeneous subgroups (e.g., hemiplegic or SCI patients) to evaluate muscle quality differences between body sides or segments. Optimal intervention strategies combining nutrition and progressive strength training should be defined and implemented.
