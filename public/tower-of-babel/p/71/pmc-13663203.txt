Coding regions are rarely predefined in the eukaryotic genomes: a note on simplified models of gene architecture
Authors: Qi Cao, Ziyi Wang, Yuange Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pedagogical depiction of eukaryotic gene structure seems to assume that coding sequences (CDSs) are predefined in the genome, with transcript diversity arising mainly from exon shuffling. However, whether such “predefined CDS” model is universal remains untested.MethodsWe systematically analyzed seven representative eukaryotic genomes to classify protein-coding genes (PCGs) into four classes based on the positional consistency of CDS start/stop sites. Both strict and loose criteria were applied, followed by cross-species comparisons of genomic feature and functional enrichment.ResultsPredefined CDS genes (Class 1) were unexpectedly rare, comprising < 10% of PCGs in most species but exceeding 25% in Drosophila. Class 1 genes displayed more exons, longer CDSs, but minimal splicing isoforms, indicating purifying selection on molecular diversity. Class 1 genes are enriched in housekeeping terms like neuronal and developmental processes, whereas highly variable Class 4 genes (with distinct CDS start/end positions across different transcripts) are associated with fast-evolving processes like metabolism and reproduction.ConclusionsIn contrast to the pedagogical simplification, our results show that CDSs are rarely predefined in the genome. The differential roles of predefined versus variable CDS architectures may reflect how natural selection balances molecular stability and functional innovation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13181-0.

Background

The central dogma of molecular biology provides the foundation for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying numerous biological processes. Through transcription and translation, genetic information is faithfully transmitted from DNA to RNA and ultimately to proteins [1]. The precise flow of genetic information is essential for maintaining homeostasis throughout evolution. In eukaryotes, transcript diversity is elevated by alternative promoter use, alternative splicing (AS), and alternative polyadenylation (APA) [2–5]. When these processes eventually affect coding sequences (CDSs), they further produce various protein isoforms, thereby fine-tuning and extending the functional repertoire of the host gene.

A pedagogical depiction of how mature mRNAs are formed, particularly when illustrating different CDS splicing patterns (Fig. 1A), typically present a simplified view: the boundaries of CDSs and untranslated regions (UTRs) are predefined within the genome. This notion is reflected by multiple resources such as the introduction of splicing in Wikipedia (Additional file 1: Supplementary Figure S1), the introduction of RNA processing in online books (Additional file 1: Supplementary Figure S2), and the image searching results of the term “alternative splicing” from websites (Additional file 1: Supplementary Figure S3). According to this simplified model, the diversity of protein isoforms merely arises from different combinations or exclusions of predefined exons. This intuitive representation conveys the impression that most protein-coding genes (PCGs) in the genome conform to this “predefined” architecture, where the boundary between CDS and UTR is fixed.

However, such a predefined model inherently constrains protein diversity, resulting in multiple isoforms with high sequence similarity and limited structural variation. In practice, increasing evidence suggests that not all PCGs strictly follow this predefined CDS model (Fig. 1B and D). Researchers found that the CDS boundary can be variable due to many different mechanisms such as alternative promoter [6], alternative splicing [2], alternative first exon usage [7], and alternative polyadenylation [8], and some of these molecular complexities were considered non-adaptive rather than functional [2, 9, 10]. Therefore, the predefined CDS model should be regarded as pedagogical simplifications rather than field consensus. Based on the complexity of CDS boundaries, several fundamental questions can be raised: To what extent do eukaryotic genomes rely on predefined CDS architectures? What are the biological and evolutionary implications of predefined versus non-predefined genes? Furthermore, do these gene types experience distinct patterns of selective pressure and functional specialization across lineages?

It is important to note that the issue we address here, whether the boundaries of CDSs/UTRs are fixed or variable within the genome, is conceptually distinct from alternative translation initiation (ATLI) [11] or upstream open reading frames (uORFs) [12]. The phenomenon discussed in this study concerns CDS variability at transcript level before translation, while ATLI and uORFs involve selective translation initiation on fully spliced mRNAs, a process that takes place in the cytoplasm.

In this work, we systematically examined the genomes of humans and six widely used model organisms spanning both animals and plants. Strikingly, we found that predefined genes constitute only a small fraction of all PCGs. This pattern holds when downsampling and different stringency were used. Compared with other gene types, predefined genes tend to possess longer CDSs with a higher number of exons, yet display a marked avoidance of alternative splicing, suggesting they might be subject to purifying selection. Functional enrichment analyses revealed that, in animals, predefined genes are predominantly involved in nervous system development, while in both animals and plants, they are enriched in metabolic and gene expression regulatory pathways.

Together, our findings suggest that the conventional model that “CDSs are predefined in the genome” is oversimplified. By integrating comparative genomic and evolutionary analyses across diverse species, this work provides new insights into the flexibility of the CDS-UTR boundary and highlights an underappreciated layer of molecular innovation shaping biological diversity.

Methods

Data acquisition

The reference genome assemblies (FASTA format) and their corresponding annotation files (GTF/GFF format) were obtained from publicly available databases as listed below. For Homo sapiens, we used the Ensembl genome assembly GRCh38 (release 85; hg38) downloaded from Ensembl Asia (data link: https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-85/fasta/homo_sapiens/). For Mus musculus, genome assembly GRCm38 (release 85; mm10) was retrieved from the same database (data link: https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-85/fasta/mus_musculus/). For Drosophila melanogaster, the r6.06 assembly and annotation were downloaded from FlyBase (data link: https://ftp.flybase.net/genomes/Drosophila_melanogaster/dmel_r6.06_FB2015_03/); note that the Ensembl version (BDGP6) corresponds to the same dataset curated by FlyBase. For Caenorhabditis elegans, we used the WBcel235 assembly available from Ensembl (data link: https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-85/fasta/caenorhabditis_elegans/). For Arabidopsis thaliana, genome assembly TAIR10 (release 59) was downloaded from Ensembl Plants. The genomes of Zea mays (assembly Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0) and Oryza sativa (assembly IRGSP-1.0) were also obtained from Ensembl Plants (data links: https://ftp.ensemblgenomes.ebi.ac.uk/pub/plants/release-60/fasta/zea_mays/ and https://ftp.ensemblgenomes.ebi.ac.uk/pub/plants/release-60/fasta/oryza_sativa/).

Classification of PCGs with multiple CDS isoforms

To systematically categorize PCGs with multiple CDS isoforms, we established four distinct classes based on the positional relationship between CDS start and stop sites across all annotated transcripts (Fig. 1). For each PCG, the genomic coordinates of CDS regions were extracted from the reference genome annotation (GTF/GFF files). Transcript-level CDS coordinates were compared pairwise within each gene to determine whether the CDS start and stop positions were identical or variable.

Class 1 (predefined CDS in the genome)

All transcript isoforms of a given gene share identical CDS start and stop positions, while internal exon composition may vary. These genes represent the predefined CDS model in which the CDS boundaries are fixed at the genomic level.

Class 2 (variable start, same end)

Transcripts differ in CDS start sites but share the same termination site. Such patterns typically arise when an exon containing the canonical start codon (ATG) is selectively skipped in some isoforms, forcing translation initiation from an alternative downstream ATG.

Class 3 (same start, variable end)

All isoforms share the same CDS start site but terminate at different genomic positions. This configuration usually results from the inclusion or exclusion of internal exons that are not multiples of three in length, leading to a frame shift and altered termination codon.

Class 4 (variable start and end)

Both the CDS start and stop positions differ among isoforms, representing the most structurally divergent CDS configuration.

To assess the robustness of our classification framework, we redefined the four CDS-based gene classes using a looser criterion that tolerates minor variability among transcript annotations. Specifically, a gene was assigned to Class 1 if at least 70% of its transcripts shared identical CDS start positions and at least 70% of its transcripts shared identical CDS stop positions. Genes not satisfying both conditions were classified into Classes 2–4, depending on whether variability occurred at the start site, stop site, or both.

Note that there are three stop codons TAA, TAG, and TGA, and we did not distinguish between them because our main focus is the variable CDS boundary which is not related to which stop codon is used.

Downsampling of transcripts

Although we selected species that are relatively well studied and have high-quality annotations, the average number of transcripts per gene still varies among species [13]. As the number of transcripts per gene increases, the probability that at least one isoform differs in CDS boundary also increases, and fewer genes can be assigned to Class 1. Therefore, cross-species comparisons may be biased by annotation depth. To control for this potential bias, we adopted a downsampling strategy. For each species, we first selected genes with at least k annotated transcripts, and then randomly sampled k transcripts from each gene. The sampled transcript sets were used to classify genes into the four classes and to calculate the proportion of genes in each class. We tested the robustness of the observed patterns by setting k from 3 to 7. For each value of k, the random sampling procedure was repeated 1,000 times, and the proportions of the four gene classes were demonstrated using the mean and variance across replicates.

Gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analyses were performed to investigate the biological processes and pathways associated with different classes of genes. The GO annotations were analyzed following the procedure described by our previous study [14]. Specifically, enrichment analyses were conducted using the established web-based platform DAVID bioinformatics resources (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/) [15]. For each species, genes from a given class (e.g., Class 1 or Class 4) were used as the query set, while all annotated protein-coding genes (PCGs) in the corresponding genome served as the background reference. GO terms from three major categories, biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), were examined. Only GO terms with an enrichment score greater than 1.0 and adjusted P value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched and retained for downstream comparisons.

Considering that gene length may be correlated with functional enrichment, for example, long genes themselves tend to be enriched for neuronal functions [16, 17], we controlled for gene length when defining the background gene set for GO enrichment analysis. Our guiding principle was to remove as few genes as possible, thereby retaining the largest possible number of background genes. For each target gene set used in the enrichment analysis, we calculated the median CDS length. If this median was greater than the median CDS length of all background genes, we progressively removed background genes starting from the shortest CDSs until the median CDS length of the remaining background increased to match that of the target gene set. Conversely, if the median CDS length of the target gene set was smaller than that of all background genes, we progressively removed background genes starting from the longest CDSs until the median CDS length of the remaining background decreased to match that of the target gene set. GO enrichment analysis was then performed for each target gene set using its corresponding length-adjusted background gene set.

Statistics and graphical works

Statistical tests and graphical works were all accomplished in R studio (R version 3.6.3). Code used in this study is available in Additional file 2: Supplementary Data S1.

Results

PCGs with multiple transcripts carrying non-identical CDSs

We downloaded seven well-annotated reference genomes: human (Homo sapiens), mouse (Mus musculus), fruitfly (Drosophila melanogaster), worm (Caenorhabditis elegans), mouse-ear cress (thale cress, Arabidopsis thaliana), corn (Zea mays), and rice (Oryza sativa). The comprehensive and high-quality annotations of these representative genomes help to minimize artifacts that may arise from incomplete or inconsistent annotations.

To investigate the phenomenon of predefined CDSs, we focused on PCGs that have at least two annotated transcripts and whose CDSs differ among these transcripts. The total numbers of genes, PCGs, and transcript isoforms for these seven species have been thoroughly reported in previous large-scale genomic studies [13]. As a first step, we excluded PCGs with only a single annotated transcript (Fig. 2). The proportion of such single-transcript PCGs varied widely among species, ranging from 23.5% in humans to 85.0% in rice. In general, the proportion followed the trend: human < mouse < fruit fly < nematode ≈ plants, suggesting greater transcriptomic complexity in animals compared to plants.

Among the remaining PCGs that encode at least two transcripts, a small subset exhibited identical CDSs across all isoforms. While such redundancy might appear unnecessary, previous studies have shown that variations in UTRs can still provide considerable regulatory flexibility over gene expression and translation efficiency [14]. After excluding these redundant cases, we identified the set of PCGs with non-identical CDSs which are the primary focus of our study. The proportion of such genes (among all PCGs) varied substantially among species, ranging from 11.3% to 72.8% (Fig. 2). This variability highlights the divergent strategies employed by different lineages to generate transcript and protein diversity. The following analyses are based on the PCGs with multiple transcripts and non-identical CDSs.

Defining four classes of genes with multiple CDS isoforms

We categorized PCGs with multiple CDS isoforms into four distinct classes based on the positional relationships between the start and stop codons of their CDSs (Fig. 1). We defined Class 1 as the predefined CDS type. In this configuration, all transcript isoforms share identical CDS start and stop positions, whereas internal exon combinations may differ (Fig. 1A).

In contrast, Class 2 genes exhibit distinct CDS start positions but share a common stop codon (Fig. 1B). Such cases may arise through “alternative first exons”: when an exon containing the canonical start codon (ATG) is selectively skipped in certain transcripts, forcing translation initiation from an alternative downstream ATG. Another possible mechanism of Class 2 is “alternative promoter use” [5] in which the first exon is not included in the pre-mRNA and this scenario is not related to splicing. Regardless of the mechanisms, for the different isoforms in Class 1, because their termination sites remain identical, most regions of the CDS remain in-frame, suggesting that the encoded protein isoforms are still highly similar.

Class 3 genes share the same CDS start position but differ in their termination sites (Fig. 1C). This pattern may be caused by the selective skipping of an internal exon. If the length of the skipped exon is not a multiple of three, the resulting frame shift alters the downstream reading frame, generating protein isoforms with markedly different C-terminal regions and distinct stop codon positions. In addition, alternative polyadenylation can also lead to the shift in the last exon and stop codon [9].

Finally, Class 4 genes display variability in both CDS start and stop positions (Fig. 1D), representing the most structurally divergent configuration among the four categories. This scenario can be produced through a combination of different mechanisms mentioned above. Because the number of annotated transcripts varies substantially across PCGs, it is challenging to apply a single universal rule for classification. To ensure robust results, we adopted two parallel schemes: one based on strict criteria and the other on loose criteria (see Methods for details). Analyses were performed independently under both definitions to validate consistency.

Conceptually, the sequence similarity between isoforms is expected to decrease progressively from Class 1 to Class 4. If higher similarity among isoforms confers an evolutionary or functional advantage, we would anticipate that species under stronger purifying selection would exhibit a relatively higher proportion of Class 1 genes compared to other lineages.

Predefined CDSs are unexpectedly rare and tend to be long but avoid splicing

We have classified all PCGs into Classes 1–4 according to whether the CDS start and stop positions of all transcripts were identical or not. Surprisingly, the proportion of Class 1 genes, the “predefined CDS” model, was remarkably low across all species examined (Fig. 3). Only in Drosophila melanogaster did Class 1 genes exceed 25%, whereas in Mus musculus this proportion dropped below 10%, and in Homo sapiens and Oryza sativa it fell below 5%. In contrast, Class 4 genes, whose CDSs differ in both start and stop sites, were highly prevalent in the human genome (62.5%), moderately abundant in mouse (46.0%), but relatively rare in Drosophila (10.3%) (Fig. 3).

Because genes with more annotated transcripts have a higher probability that at least one isoform differs in CDS boundary, they are less likely to be assigned to Class 1. Therefore, the observed cross-species differences could potentially reflect annotation depth rather than biological differences in CDS architecture. To address this issue, we restricted the analysis to genes with at least k annotated transcripts in each species and randomly sampled k transcripts from each gene. The sampled transcript sets were then used to classify genes into the four classes and to calculate the proportion of each class. We tried k values from 3 to 7, and for each k, the random sampling was repeated 1,000 times (Methods). Overall, the results show that the fraction of Class 1 genes decreased with the increase of k value, while Class 4 genes exhibited the opposite trend (Fig. 3 and Additional file 1: Supplementary Figure S4). For cross-species comparison, we constantly observed that Drosophila had the highest Class 1 proportion and the lowest Class 4 proportion, while in human, Class 4 was the highest and Class 1 was low (Fig. 3 and Additional file 1: Supplementary Figure S4).

Considering previous estimates of effective population size (Ne), with humans having the smallest Ne and Drosophila having the largest [18, 19], this trend is consistent with evolutionary expectations. If Class 1 genes represent functionally conserved configurations and Class 4 genes embody some tentative or high-risk innovations, then stronger purifying selection in large Ne species should favor Class 1 while suppressing Class 4, exactly as observed.

Genomic characteristics of four gene classes

To further explore genomic characteristics distinguishing these four classes, we compared several genomic features. The number of transcripts per gene followed a consistent pattern across species: lowest in Class 1 and highest in Class 4 (Fig. 4). This is partially attributable to our definition, as genes with more transcript isoforms are inherently more likely to exhibit variable CDS boundaries, and thus be classified as Class 4. Interestingly, although Class 1 genes tended to have the fewest transcripts, they possessed the largest number of exons per CDS in both humans and mice (Fig. 4). This result was unexpected because, in theory, a greater number of exons should correlate positively with the potential to generate multiple isoforms [13]. Instead, we observed an inverse relationship: Class 1 genes have more exons but appear to avoid alternative splicing, indicating potential evolutionary constraints to maintain CDS integrity. Consistent with this pattern, Class 1 genes also had the longest CDSs across most species analyzed, in line with their higher exon counts.

We next assessed the variation in CDS length among transcripts of the same gene, quantified as the standard deviation (SD) of CDS lengths. As shown in Fig. 4, Class 1 genes exhibited the lowest SD, while Class 4 genes displayed the largest. This trend is intriguing because there is no intrinsic reason why internal exonic variation should yield smaller length deviations compared to the 5’ or 3’ exonic variation. The observed pattern might reflect functional convergence driven by purifying selection: Class 1 isoforms tend to maintain similar functional domains and avoid large structural differences. It suggests that these gene might participate in biological processes with relatively constant functions.

Finally, we compared the GC content of CDS regions across classes and found no consistent differences among classes in most species examined (Fig. 4), suggesting that GC bias unlikely contribute to the observed patterns of CDS structural diversity.

Robust patterns of predefined CDSs under loose criteria

In the analyses described above, the definition of Class 1 was stringent, requiring that all annotated transcripts of a gene share identical CDS start and stop positions. To test the robustness of our findings, we relaxed this requirement by applying a looser classification criterion. Specifically, genes were assigned to Class 1 if at least 70% of their transcripts shared the same CDS start positions and 70% of their transcripts shared the same stop positions, while the remaining classes were defined analogously (see Methods for details).

Under these relaxed criteria, the number of Class 1 genes increased across all species, although the relative patterns among taxa remained consistent. Notably, Drosophila melanogaster still exhibited the highest proportion of Class 1 genes among the seven species examined (Fig. 5). The previously observed distinctions between Class 1 and Class 4 genes were preserved, and in several cases, even became more pronounced. With few exceptions, Class 1 genes consistently displayed fewer transcripts per gene compared to Class 4 genes, yet they contained more exons per CDS and exhibited longer CDS lengths (Fig. 5). This pattern reinforces the notion that Class 1 genes, despite their higher exon counts, might avoid alternative splicing events which would alter CDS boundaries.

Similarly, the standard deviation of CDS length among transcripts within the same gene remained the lowest for Class 1 genes, whereas Class 4 genes showed the highest variability. This suggests that even when allowing greater tolerance in boundary variability, Class 1 genes maintain structural stability among their isoforms, likely reflecting evolutionary constraints to preserve protein function.

Finally, as in the strict classification, here we found that GC content across CDSs showed no consistent trend among the four classes (Fig. 5), indicating that compositional bias does not account for the observed class-specific structural differences.

Notably, for genes with fewer transcripts, the loose criterion is barely looser than strict since the 70% cutoff might only exclude one out of a few transcripts; but for high-isoform genes this cutoff is substantially looser. We therefore tried another moderate criterion with an 80% cutoff, and found robust patterns as we observed under the strict and loose criteria (Additional file 1: Supplementary Figure S5). Overall, these results demonstrate that the genomic features of predefined CDSs are highly robust to variations in classification criteria.

Predefined CDS genes are enriched in developmental and neuronal functions

If Class 1 genes are indeed under stronger evolutionary constraints, such selective pressure should be reflected in their functional enrichment patterns. To test this hypothesis, we conducted functional enrichment analyses across representative species to explore the biological roles of these gene classes. Remarkably, Class 1 genes exhibited highly conserved functional profiles in mammals and insects, showing strong enrichment for neuronal system development, neurogenesis, and cell differentiation (Fig. 6). Across both animals and plants, a subset of functions, such as gene expression regulation and transcriptional control, were consistently overrepresented among Class 1 genes, highlighting their functional conservation and fundamental biological importance. Genes involved in these regulatory and developmental pathways are typically specialized and dosage-sensitive, implying that large structural or functional divergences among isoforms would be deleterious. Consequently, the maintenance of predefined CDS architectures in such genes might reflect selective pressure to preserve precise functional outcomes during development.

In contrast, Class 4 genes were enriched in pathways related to organelle function, metabolism, and in some cases reproductive processes (notably in Drosophila and Arabidopsis) (Fig. 6). Because reproductive systems are known to evolve much more rapidly than neural systems [20], this pattern is compatible with our hypothesis that Class 4 genes, with their highly variable CDS structures, may serve as an evolutionary playground facilitating functional innovation through greater isoform diversity.

Considering that neuronal genes are intrinsically longer than the genome-wide background [16, 17], they are likely to be enriched in the Class 1 genes with longer CDS length. To take this bias into account, we redid the GO analysis by controlling for the CDS length of background genes, and found analogous enrichments (Additional file 1: Supplementary Figure S6). Such pattern reinforced our implication of the gene functions.

Discussion

Eukaryotic gene models are usually simplified as the Class 1 category described in our study (Fig. 1A), which are the genes with predefined CDS boundaries (Additional file 1: Supplementary Figure S1-S3). However, our comprehensive comparative analysis demonstrates that Class 1 genes represent only a small fraction of all protein-coding genes across most species examined. This finding indicates that the pedagogical simplification does not reflect a universal pattern and should be interpreted with caution.

Interestingly, under various cutoffs, we constantly observed that Class 1 genes are most abundant in Drosophila melanogaster, the species with the largest effective population size Ne [18, 19], while the proportion of Class 4 genes, which exhibit the greatest CDS variability, is lowest in Drosophila. Since larger Ne correlates with stronger natural selection force, our observations indicate that predefined CDS architectures (Class 1) might be evolutionarily advantageous (or represent the relic after purifying selection), whereas highly variable configurations (Class 4) might be generally deleterious and thus selectively suppressed.

These observations further imply that during the early evolution of spliceosome-mediated splicing, the cellular machineries may have frequently targeted transcripts inaccurately, generating Class 4-like genes with inconsistent CDS boundaries. Such off-target and promiscuous events would have been largely non-adaptive, consistent with George Zhang’s molecular error hypothesis [21, 22]. Over time, purifying selection likely acted to eliminate deleterious regulatory patterns, either by removing non-adaptive isoforms or by favoring genes whose CDS boundaries became stabilized, eventually forming Class 1 genes. This molecular error view is also consistent with proteomics-based studies showing that, for many genes, one dominant protein isoform accounts for most detectable protein evidence, whereas many annotated transcript isoforms have limited proteomic support [23, 24]. Notably, we do not casually extend this error hypothesis to other molecular mechanisms like alternative promoter use or alternative polyadenylation. Both mechanisms are well-characterized processes that can generate functional protein isoforms with distinct N- or C-terminal regions [6, 8]. In our framework, alternative promoter use and APA are therefore best viewed as regulated sources of CDS-boundary variation that can produce isoforms across the Class 1-Class 4 spectrum for distinct biological reasons.

Nevertheless, while Class 4 genes as a group may generally represent non-adaptive outcomes, it is important to note that a minority of them may acquire functional significance, just like finding adaptive RNA editing sites from the overall non-adaptive RNA editing pools [25–28]. Evolution depends on innovation: when environmental conditions change, organisms must explore new functional configurations, even at the risk of generating harmful variants [29–31]. Such “risky” molecular experiments, although often deleterious, occasionally succeed, leading to innovations that provide a selective advantage [21]. Moreover, such imprecision of different mechanisms has been interpreted in two ways: as a source of exon shuffling and molecular innovation (a functional and productive view) [32, 33], or as a source of largely non-adaptive transcriptional noises [21]. Our framework is compatible with both views, because Class 4 may include weakly constrained isoforms for innovation or lineage-specific function, while Class 1 may represent the constrained ones surviving the evolutionary purging.

Together with our previous analyses of different mRNAs encoding identical proteins [14] and exon length variation [13], the present study represents another axis of eukaryotic transcript-architecture diversity: variability in CDS start and stop boundaries. These axes are complementary. The “exon length variation” captures structural constraints and length distribution of transcripts, with less focus on coding region [13]; while the “same protein with different mRNA variation” mainly reflects transcript diversity without protein sequence changes, focusing on the consistency rather than the difference in CDS [14]; in contrast, this currently discussed CDS boundary variability directly affects the mapping from transcript isoforms to protein isoforms, and try to interpret such variability in the light of evolution. Integrating these axes may help distinguish transcript diversity that primarily modulates RNA-level regulation from diversity that changes protein-coding potential. In the future, it would be interesting to interrogate the protein isoform diversity by considering different layers of regulation such as alternative transcription start site, exon inclusion/exclusion, and alternative polyadenylation.

Taken together, our findings suggest a potential division of functional roles between gene classes. Class 1 genes tend to participate in stable, conserved biological processes, such as neural development and cell differentiation, where precise regulation and functional robustness are critical. In contrast, Class 4 genes are often involved in rapidly evolving systems, including reproduction and metabolic adaptation, where structural flexibility and variation may facilitate innovation. Future functional experiments could test whether Class 4 genes are responsible for lineage-specific phenotypes recently evolved, whereas Class 1 genes tend to govern housekeeping phenotypes which often become lethal upon knock-out. The availability of more high-quality and sufficiently annotated reference genomes is also appreciated.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our systematic cross-species analysis not only reveals that the pedagogical notion that “the CDS is predefined in the genome” is oversimplified and appears to be rare across representative eukaryotic genome, but also provides deeper insight into how molecular mechanisms have evolved to balance stability and diversity. By revealing the contrasting functional landscape of the predefined versus variable CDS architectures, this study advances our understanding of how genomic organization and transcriptional regulation jointly shape biological complexity.
