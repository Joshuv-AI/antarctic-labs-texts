The persisting challenge of recurrent in-stent restenosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prevalence and prognostic risk factors
Authors: Hongxiu Chen, Zhenjun Yuan, Shuai Zhang, Xiuying Hu, Ji Bao, Guan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent in-stent restenosis (R-ISR) after percutaneous coronary intervention remains a clinically important failure mode, yet reported rates and predictors vary widely. We aimed to quantify the pooled incidence of R-ISR and synthesize multivariable risk factors.MethodsA comprehensive literature search of the electronic database was updated on May 3, 2025. Studies reporting the incidence or analyzing the risk factors of R-ISR were eligible. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Data synthesis was achieved via random-effects meta-analysis using the R studio (version 4.4.3). Subgroup analyses considered region, treatment, and follow-up, with leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. The systematic review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 checklist.ResultsTwenty-three studies were included. The overall pooled incidence of R-ISR was 21.7% (95%CI 17.1%–27.2%), with variations by region (highest in Europe), treatment type (lowest with DES), and follow-up duration (highest within 6 months). Significant risk factors included renal disease (OR 3.90; 95%CI 1.87–8.11), non-focal restenosis (OR 3.29; 95%CI 2.41–4.49), lesion length (OR 1.15; 95%CI 1.06–1.24), heterogeneous tissue pattern (OR 3.36; 95%CI 1.69–6.67), stent fracture (OR 2.21; 95%CI 1.29–3.79), and higher percentage diameter stenosis (OR 1.05; 95%CI 1.03–1.06). Age showed non-significant association (OR 0.96; 95%CI 0.93–1.00).ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is among the first systematic reviews and meta-analyses specifically focused on recurrent ISR after treatment of ISR lesions. Renal disease, non-focal morphology, lesion length, heterogeneous tissue pattern, stent fracture and greater post-procedural percentage diameter stenosis were consistently associated with higher risk of R-ISR. Background

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a cardiovascular condition characterized by atherosclerotic narrowing or occlusion of the coronary arteries, which impairs myocardial perfusion and can lead to myocardial infarction, angina, heart failure, or sudden cardiac death. CAD is one of the leading causes of global mortality. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), CAD accounts for more than nine million deaths annually, representing about 16% of global mortality. Global Burden of Disease statistics show that the number of prevalent cardiovascular disease cases rose from 271 million in 1990 to 523 million in 2019, and deaths from cardiovascular diseases increased from 12.1 million to 18.6 million over the same period [1]. With aging populations and lifestyle changes, the incidence and overall burden of CAD and related cardiovascular diseases continue to rise.

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is currently one of the core treatment modalities for CAD [2, 3]. With the widespread use of drug-eluting stents (DES), the incidence of in-stent restenosis (ISR) after PCI has declined significantly compared with bare-metal stents (BMS) [4, 5]. However, ISR has not been completely eliminated; its occurrence could lead to recurrent symptoms, myocardial ischemia, repeat interventions, and even sudden cardiac death [6]. After reintervention for ISR, a subset of patients experience recurrent in-stent restenosis (R-ISR); this phenomenon is substantially more challenging to manage than initial ISR. R-ISR patients have a poorer prognosis, with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular adverse events (e.g., myocardial infarction, heart failure) [7]. Therefore, R-ISR has emerged as an important factor limiting the long-term efficacy of PCI and remains a clinical challenge in contemporary interventional therapy.

ISR is typically defined as luminal diameter narrowing greater than 50% within the stented segment or within a 5-mm range adjacent to the stent.

R-ISR is generally referred to a second ISR event that occurs after successful treatment of the initial ISR lesion [8, 9], with angiographically confirmed stenosis of ≥ 50% of the vessel diameter, usually detected during follow-up after revascularization or pharmacotherapy. The occurrence of R-ISR reflects lesion complexity and progression and may indicate treatment refractoriness or suboptimal therapy selection. Previous study showed that, among ISR lesions treated with balloon angioplasty (BA), the 6-month incidence of R-ISR can be as high as 35.4% [10]. The pathogenesis of R-ISR likely involves persistent inflammatory responses in the vessel wall, recurrent neointimal hyperplasia, poor control of comorbidities (such as diabetes), suboptimal stent implantation strategies and other factors [11]. Moreover, therapeutic options for R-ISR are more limited; repeat stenting could create a “stent-in-stent” configuration, increasing the difficulty of subsequent interventions and the risk of vascular complications [7]. Therefore, identifying high-risk populations for R-ISR and clarifying its risk factors has important clinical implications for improving long-term PCI outcomes and patient prognosis.

In recent years, clinical studies on R-ISR have increased, with some investigations preliminarily identifying potential risk factors, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, renal dysfunction, lesion length, stent type, and target lesion characteristics (e.g., bifurcation lesions, small-vessel disease) [12–18]. However, most current studies are single-center and retrospective with limited sample sizes; differences in study populations, design, setting, treatment methods, or diagnostic criteria may contribute to substantial variation in reported R-ISR incidence and to inconsistencies in risk-factor conclusions. For example, some studies identify diabetes as an important independent risk factor of R-ISR, whereas others do not find a statistically significant association; certain investigations emphasize lesion anatomical features (such as chronic total occlusion and bifurcation lesions) as closely related to R-ISR, while others focus more on systemic metabolic factors such as lipoprotein(a) levels and the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) [19–21]. Moreover, there is currently a lack of systematic reviews and quantitative syntheses on R-ISR incidence and risk factors, which limits clinicians’ ability to identify high-risk populations and to formulate preventive strategies.

Therefore, the main aim of this study was to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesize existing evidence to clarify the incidence and major risk factors of R-ISR after PCI in patients with CAD, and thereafter to provide evidence base to support precise clinical interventions.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251043520). Seven databases were comprehensively searched up to May 3, 2025, in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020 checklist). A random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis, which was performed using R Studio (version 4.4.3). The meta-analysis pooled incidence and multivariable-adjusted risk factors, with subgroup and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses to assess heterogeneity and robustness. The quality of the studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Publication bias was examined using funnel plots and the Egger’s test.

Search strategy

Literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), VIP Information databases, and WanFang Data. A search strategy was developed using a combination of MeSH terms and free-text terms, including search terms appearing in titles, keywords, and abstracts. Search terms for ISR included: in-stent restenosis, coronary stenosis, coronary restenosis, in-stent restenosis, recurrent in-stent restenosis, ISR, and R-ISR. The literature search was initially conducted in all databases from inception to January 1, 2024, and an updated search was performed on May 3, 2025 to identify additional eligible studies published after the initial search. No date or language restrictions were applied at the search stage. Detailed search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table 1. In addition, manual screening of included articles’ or relevant reviews’ references was performed to identify additional eligible studies that were not captured by electronic searching.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies meeting the following criteria were included: (1) Study population involving patients with CAD treated with PCI; (2) Study design being observational studies (e.g., cross-sectional studies, case–control studies, and cohort studies); (3) Studies were eligible if they reported recurrent in-stent restenosis (R-ISR), defined operationally in this review as a second restenotic event occurring after treatment of an initial ISR lesion. Because outcome definitions were not fully uniform across the included studies, we accepted the definitions used by the original investigators when they referred to recurrent restenosis or equivalent recurrent ISR-related outcomes; (4) Published in Chinese or English. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Studies in which ISR and R-ISR were not distinguished; (2) Studies in which the outcomes were TLR (target-lesion revascularization), TVR (target-vessel revascularization) and clinically driven R-ISR; (3) Duplicate publications. For studies involving the same study population or overlapping study populations in time, the study with the most comprehensive results or the largest sample size would be included. Conference abstracts were included if they reported data on R-ISR incidence or related risk factors.

Study selection and data extraction

Articles identified from electronic databases were imported into EndNote X9 for management and screening. First, duplicates were removed using the software’s built-in de-duplication function. Next, the titles and abstracts were screened to exclude studies that clearly didn’t meet the inclusion criteria. Finally, the full texts of the screened articles were thoroughly assessed to further exclude ineligible studies. The screening process was conducted independently by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a field expert was consulted. Final decisions required consensus among all three.

A predesigned data extraction form was used. Extracted data included: first author’s name and country, year of publication, study design, sample size, follow-up duration, the definition of R-ISR (angiographic or clinical restenosis, patient-level or lesion-level recurrence, binary restenosis or repeat revascularization), the number of R-ISR events, incidence, statistical methods, and effect sizes for R-ISR risk factors with their 95% confidence intervals (OR, RR, HR). The detailed definitions of R-ISR for the included studies are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Data were extracted independently by two researchers and cross-checked. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion, if unresolved, relevant experts were consulted.

Quality evaluation

Quality assessment of the included studies was conducted using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [22]. The NOS evaluates study quality across three domains: Selection, Comparability, and Outcome. Studies scoring fewer than three stars were deemed to have a high risk of bias, four to six stars were deemed to have a moderate risk of bias, and studies scoring seven stars were deemed to have a low risk of bias.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed in R Studio (version 4.4.3). Meta-analysis was conducted primarily using the meta and metafor packages in R. Descriptive characteristics of the included studies were summarized as counts and percentages. For the reported incidence of R-ISR in the included studies (i.e., events / total follow-up), a random-effects meta-analysis was performed using the inverse-variance method on logit-transformed proportions, with the between-study variance (τ2) estimated by maximum likelihood, then the pooled estimate and its 95% confidence interval were back-transformed to the proportion scale. For the quantitative synthesis of risk factors, effect estimates reported in the original studies, including odds ratios (ORs) and hazard ratios (HRs), were extracted. Quantitative pooling was performed only when studies reported the same effect measure. Forest plots were used to present the pooled results for each risk factor. Heterogeneity was assessed using the between-study τ2 and I2 statistics. If I2 < 50%, a fixed-effects model would be used; if I2 > 50%, a random-effects model would be used. Moreover, when the number of eligible studies was at least two, subgroup analyses would be conducted by study region, design, and stent type. Sensitivity analyses were performed using leave-one-out analyses to evaluate the robustness of the pooled results. Publication bias was assessed with funnel plots, supplemented by Egger’s or Begg’s tests.

Results

Literature search and screening results

A total of 2,297 records were identified through electronic databases and manual searches. After deduplication, 1,811 records remained. Titles and abstracts were screened to exclude clearly irrelevant studies, resulting in 1,740 exclusions and 71 articles subjected to full-text review. Finally, 23 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis, including 1 conference abstract [23]. The basic characteristics of included studies

All included studies have documented the incidence of R-ISR, of which seven presented data based on the number of lesions, while the remainder presented data based on the number of patients. To ensure analytical consistency, only studies reporting patient-based data were included in the analysis of R-ISR incidence. Separately, 21 studies have reported risk factors for R-ISR. These studies primarily originated from Japan (n = 9), China (n = 4), Germany (n = 3), and the United States (n = 3). Eight studies were prospective, with sample sizes ranging from 56 to 608; the remaining 15 were retrospective, with sample sizes ranging from 51 to 2158. The meta-analysis result of the incidence rate of R-ISR

The reported incidence of R-ISR ranged from 10.1% to 49.0%. The pooled prevalence of R-ISR was 21.7% (95% CI 17.1%–27.2%) (Fig. 2). Subsequently, we performed subgroup analyses of R-ISR incidence stratified by region, treatment modality, and follow-up duration.

Forest plot of R-ISR incidence rate for all participants

Region

The reported incidences of R-ISR from Europe (n = 5), Asia (n = 8), and North America (n = 3) were 17.7%–49.0%, 10.1%–39.2%, and 12.8%–21.1%, respectively. The synthesized incidence of R-ISR for Europe, Asia, and North America were 29.7% (95% CI 21.0%–40.2%), 19.2% (95% CI 13.6%–26.4%), and 16.1% (95% CI 12.5%–20.5%), respectively (Fig. 3). Sensitivity analyses showed that removing any single study did not significantly change the pooled effect size.

Forest plots of R-ISR incidence categorized by region

Treatment

Among the included studies, three studies’ participants received DCB (drug-coated balloon) [15, 39, 40], four received DES (drug-eluting stents) [28, 29, 32, 34], three received IVBT (intravascular brachytherapy) [25, 26, 28], and two received conventional balloon angioplasty [10, 24]. Accordingly, the pooled incidences of R-ISR was 26.0% (95% CI 19.9%–33.2%) with DCB, 12.7% (95% CI 11.4%–14.1%) with DES, 21.2% (95% CI 17.1%–25.8%) with IVBT, and 43.1% (95% CI 34.0%–52.8%) with conventional balloon angioplasty (Fig. 4).

Forest plots of R-ISR incidence rate categorized by treatment method

Because the inclusion of historical treatment strategies may reduce the applicability of the findings to contemporary practice, an additional subgroup analysis restricted to contemporary treatment modalities (including DCB and second-generation DES) was performed using lesion-level data. The pooled incidence of R-ISR in this subgroup was 25.2% (95% CI 19.1%–32.4%) (Fig. 5).

Forest plots of R-ISR incidence rate categorized by contemporary treatment

DES type

Our study has exposed the superior effect of DES in reducing the R-ISR rates, so we made a comparison between the first and second generation of DES using lesion-level data. The reported R-ISR rates of first generation DES (n = 3) and second generation (n = 2) were 12.6%–25.8% and 14.7%–33.9%. The synthesized incidences of R-ISR were 17.6% (95% CI 12.9%–23.6%) with first generation DES, 22.7% (95% CI 15.5%–32.1%) with second generation DES (Fig. 6). However, this comparison is critically limited by profound heterogeneity, small study numbers and warrants careful interpretation.

Forest plots of R-ISR incidence rate categorized by DES type

Follow-up duration

Follow-up clustered into three strata: ≤6 months (n = 5), 6 to 12 months (n = 7), and ≥ 2 years (n = 3). The one to two-year interval was not analyzed because available reports were limited to lesion counts. The pooled incidence of R-ISR within ≤ 6 months was 24.9% (95% CI 15.5%–37.3%), 20.1% (95% CI 15.8%–25.3%) at 6 to 12 months, and 16.3% (95% CI 8.7%–28.3%) at ≥ 2 years (Fig. 7).

Forest plots of R-ISR incidence categorized by follow-up duration

The meta-analysis results of R-ISR risk factors

Previous studies have identified numerous factors associated with the risk of R-ISR. We categorized these factors into four groups: clinical substrate (e.g.,CKD/dialysis [23, 33], diabetes [42], age [26, 41]); anatomic burden (e.g.,diffuse/non-focal ISR [10], lesion length [15, 24, 31, 40]); tissue phenotype (e.g.,heterogeneous OCT pattern [36, 37]); and mechanical failure (e.g.,underexpansion, fracture [9, 31, 36], geographic miss [26, 35], percentage diameter stenosis [9, 33]). Subgroup analyses were restricted to factors for which at least two primary studies reported multivariable-adjusted effect estimates(Fig. 8).

Forest plot of risk factors for R-ISR

Synthesis results of clinical substrate related factors of R-ISR

According to two studies [26, 41], age showed non-significant association with R-ISR (pooled OR = 0.96; 95% CI 0.93–1.00; P = 0.075). Renal disease was associated with a higher risk of R-ISR based on three studies (pooled OR = 3.90; 95% CI 1.87–8.11; P < 0.001) [23, 29, 33].

Synthesis results of anatomic burden related factors of R-ISR

Evidence from two studies suggested that non-focal restenosis would increase 3.29 times of risk of R-ISR compared with focal restenosis (pooled OR = 3.29; 95% CI 2.41–4.49; P < 0.001) [9, 29]. Additionally, the longer the lesion, the higher the risk of R-ISR (pooled OR = 1.15; 95% CI 1.06–1.24; P < 0.001) [15, 23, 25, 31, 40, 43].

Synthesis results of tissue phenotype related factors of R-ISR

Furthermore, a heterogeneous tissue pattern on optical coherence tomography (OCT) was likewise associated with increased risk [36, 37] (pooled OR = 3.36; 95% CI 1.69–6.67; P < 0.001). This suggests that OCT assessment before reintervention may help identify high-risk lesions and guide treatment strategy.

Synthesis results of mechanical failure related factors of R-ISR

The synthesis of two studies indicated that having stent fracture increased the risk of R-ISR (pooled OR = 2.21; 95% CI 1.29–3.79; P = 0.004) [9, 36]. A greater percentage diameter stenosis suggested a higher tendency for R-ISR (pooled OR = 1.05; 95% CI 1.03–1.06; P < 0.001) [9, 33].

Quality assessment and publication bias assessment

All studies were assessed by NOS, revealing high methodological quality and a low risk of bias (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, six funnel plots were generated to assess the potential for publication bias and small-study effects(Supplementary Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7). Through the visual inspection, no substantial funnel asymmetry was evident in the analyses, but due to the small number of studies, the power of statistical tests is limited, and these findings should be interpreted cautiously. We also performed sensitivity analyses excluding the conference abstract from all quantitative syntheses ( Supplementary Fig. 8–9) [23]. The pooled OR of lesion length changed from 1.15 (95% CI 1.06–1.24) to 1.19 (95% CI 0.98–1.43), this suggests that the inclusion of the abstract may have led to an overestimation of the risk associated with lesion length. Therefore, the finding for lesion length should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This study systematically reviewed and analyzed the incidence and risk factors of R-ISR in CAD patients receiving PCI. We estimated an overall incidence of R-ISR after PCI of 21.7% (95% CI 17.1%–27.2%) by synthesizing 23 studies. Incidence rate varied by region and treatment modalities, i.e., higher in Europe than in Asia or North America; highest with conventional balloon angioplasty, lowest with DES. Stratified by follow-up, recurrence was more frequent within ≤ 6 months after PCI, indicating a predominantly early failure pattern. In pooled risk-factor analyses, renal disease, non-focal restenosis, lesion length, heterogeneous tissue patterns, stent fracture and greater percentage diameter stenosis were consistently associated with higher R-ISR risk, whereas age showed non-significant association. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses did not materially alter the summary estimates, supporting the robustness of the findings.

We observed a higher pooled incidence of R-ISR in European cohorts (29.7%) than in Asian (19.2%) and North American cohorts (16.1%). This regional difference should be interpreted cautiously. First, the European estimate may have been influenced by the inclusion of several studies with relatively high event rates, such as Dahl et al. (49.0%), Latib et al. (37.1%), and Samways et al. (31.8%), despite the small number of studies in this subgroup. Second, differences in follow-up duration may have contributed to the observed disparity. Several European studies had follow-up extending to 1 year or longer, including 2-year follow-up in Samways et al., which may have allowed more recurrent events to be captured. Third, treatment strategies varied substantially across regions and study periods, ranging from early techniques such as rotational atherectomy or intracoronary radiation therapy to more contemporary approaches using DES, DCB, or PCB, which may have affected recurrence risk. In addition, regional differences may also reflect variation in surveillance intensity, patient case mix, lesion complexity, and study design. Because these subgroup findings were derived from study-level rather than individual-level data and were accompanied by substantial heterogeneity, the underlying causes of the regional disparity cannot be determined with certainty.

Regarding treatment modality, the pooled results indicate the highest R-ISR incidence with plain old balloon angioplasty, the lowest with DES, and intermediate rates with radiation therapy and DCB. This pattern has a plausible biological and device-related rationale. A study demonstrated that radiation therapy could deliver ionizing radiation that induces direct and indirect injury to vascular smooth muscle cells, thereby suppressing neointimal hyperplasia [11]. DCB treatment could deliver antiproliferative agents (e.g., paclitaxel) locally and rapidly to inhibit intimal hyperplasia without permanent metallic implants transiently [44]; consequently, DCB may confer short-term advantages, particularly for small-vessel lesions or when repeat stent implanting is undesirable. For patients suffering from recurrent refractory ISR, DCB may be a therapeutic option to repeated interventional revascularization [45]. DES could provide a metallic scaffold that stabilizes lumen patency at the stenotic segment and enables sustained, controlled drug elution, which may contribute to more durable efficacy. However, although stents can reduce lumen loss by inhibiting constrictive remodeling, the vascular injury associated with implantation may also provoke proliferative and inflammatory responses that can culminate in R-ISR, and late stent thrombosis is more likely to occur with DES than with BMS [46, 47]. Therefore, treatment selection for ISR should be individualized according to patient-specific characteristics, balancing the respective strengths and limitations of DCB and DES [48].

Since several included studies evaluated historical PCI strategies, such as intravascular brachytherapy and conventional balloon angioplasty, the overall pooled estimate may not fully reflect current clinical practice. We additionally performed a subgroup analysis restricted to contemporary treatment modalities, including DCB and second-generation DES. In this subgroup, the pooled incidence of R-ISR was 25.2% (95% CI 19.1%–32.4%), which was slightly higher than the overall pooled incidence of 21.7% (95% CI 17.1%–27.2%). This suggests that the inclusion of older treatment strategies did not necessarily inflate the overall estimate, and that R-ISR remains clinically relevant even in the contemporary treatment era. Although second-generation DES has been shown to provide better stent deployment, safety, and efficacy in reducing stent thrombosis, target lesion failure, all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, and restenosis [49, 50], their pooled incidence of R-ISR was numerically higher than that of first-generation DES in our analysis. A plausible explanation is that contemporary devices, particularly second-generation DES, may have been preferentially used in more complex, refractory, or high-risk ISR lesions, which could partly offset their technological advantages. In addition, differences in endpoint definitions across studies are also relevant, as many previous studies focused on target lesion revascularization or major adverse cardiovascular events, whereas our analysis specifically evaluated R-ISR incidence. Variations in follow-up duration, patient selection, the limited number of studies, and substantial between-study heterogeneity may have further affected these pooled estimates. Importantly, the number of studies available for comparison between first- and second-generation DES was limited, and the sample size was insufficient to support a definitive comparison between stent generations. Therefore, the findings from the contemporary-treatment subgroup and the comparison between first- and second-generation DES should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as clinically informative, but not as evidence that newer devices are associated with a higher intrinsic risk of recurrence.

Regarding follow-up duration, our analysis also observed distinct temporal patterns in R-ISR incidence, with early-phase (≤ 6 months) incidence substantially higher than late-phase (≥ 12 months) incidence. A study reported that the onset time of R-ISR was approximately 10 months after the first ISR episode, with subsequent episodes displaying a similar, temporal pattern [34]. Biologically, this pattern was consistent with an early injury–inflammation–proliferation cascade: balloon dilation or stent implantation injured the intima/media, triggered vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) phenotypic switching, migration, and proliferation, and promoted neointimal formation, thereby increasing early recurrence risk [51]; by contrast, later restenosis was more likely related to mechanisms such as in-stent neoatherosclerosis, whose temporal dynamics differ from early neointimal hyperplasia [52]. Metabolic comorbidities may amplify early risk: diabetes and chronic kidney disease were associated with higher restenosis risk [53], suggesting that hyperglycemia or a uremic milieu can accelerate VSMC proliferation and sustain inflammation, effectively shifting recurrence earlier in time. Methodological factors may also contribute. Several studies implemented protocol-mandated angiographic follow-up at six to nine months or otherwise more intensive early surveillance, increasing detection of early events, whereas longer-term follow-up was typically symptom-driven and subject to competing risks (e.g., major adverse cardiovascular events, myocardial infarction, or death) and loss to follow-up/informative censoring, which may underestimate late R-ISR incidence if not modeled. Therefore, the first six months after PCI may represent a high-risk window during which intensified secondary prevention and, where appropriate, intravascular imaging are warranted; future studies should standardize endpoint definitions and follow-up strategies and incorporate competing-risk methods to yield more robust time-dependent risk estimates.

In our synthesis, age showed a non-significant association with R-ISR (pooled OR = 0.96, 95% CI 0.93–1.00); however, this result should be interpreted cautiously as it is only drawn from two studies. Several cohorts in the DES era have also linked younger age to a higher risk of repeat revascularization/target-lesion failure, implying that younger patients may be more prone to early post-procedural reintervention [54, 55]. At the same time, follow-up and ascertainment strategies may create apparent differences: protocol-mandated (routine) angiographic surveillance increases detection of asymptomatic lesions and the likelihood of repeat revascularization (the “oculostenotic reflex”) [56], whereas older patients were more likely over longer follow-up to experience competing events (e.g., death or myocardial infarction), both of which can depress the observed late R-ISR rate.

The non-focal ISR, including diffuse and proliferative patterns, showed a higher risk of R-ISR than focal ISR (pooled OR = 3.29, 95% CI 2.41–4.49). Diffuse/proliferative disease reflected more extensive neointimal hyperplasia and greater lesion burden [57, 58], and was frequently accompanied by underexpansion, metal overlap, or geographic miss, all of which provide an anatomic substrate for recurrence. Consistent with this, balloon angioplasty yielded higher recurrence in such lesions, particularly in diffuse/proliferative patterns [59, 60]. Notably, recurrent events often appear angiographically focal (e.g., at stent edges or fracture points) [34, 61]; this reflects local mechanical weak spots during recurrence and does not contradict the prognostic signal conveyed by a baseline non-focal morphology.

Across previous studies, longer lesion length has generally been associated with greater recurrence risk [15, 42, 62, 63]. In our synthesis, the association showed a positive trend with statistical significance (pooled OR = 1.15, 95% CI 1.06–1.24). Plausible mechanisms include a lower drug-to-tissue exposure ratio, more challenging apposition and full expansion, a higher likelihood of metal overlap and stent fracture, and a wider zone of injury/inflammation that promotes neointimal regrowth [40]. While a single study had suggested that each additional 5 mm of lesion length may increase R-ISR risk by 58% [15], these estimates should be interpreted cautiously given between-study heterogeneity and imprecision.

Our findings indicate that patients treated with DES had a lower incidence of R-ISR than those treated with DCB, suggesting that DES provides more durable lumen maintenance in many scenarios. However, DES is not universally superior: several device-related failure pathways are also linked to R-ISR. These include stent fracture, in which local mechanical instability and repetitive stress may provoke vascular injury and inflammation, thereby promoting recurrent restenosis, as well as heterogeneous tissue pattern on optical coherence tomography, which may reflect uneven healing and ongoing proliferative activity. In addition, post-procedural underexpansion and residual stenosis are important mechanical correlates; a higher post-procedural percentage diameter stenosis may indicate suboptimal expansion or a tendency toward recurrent luminal compromise and was associated with subsequent recurrence in the included studies. Furthermore, repeat stenting creates multilayer metal, which may concentrate stress, increase fracture risk, result in non-uniform drug delivery, and complicate future interventions.

Given that heterogeneous tissue pattern (OR 3.36; 95% CI 1.69–6.67) and stent fracture (OR 2.21; 95% CI 1.29–3.79) were among the strongest predictors of R-ISR in our analysis, intravascular imaging (IVUS/OCT) may be valuable before repeat intervention for ISR to identify high-risk morphologic and mechanical features, clarify the dominant mechanism of failure, and guide lesion preparation and device selection. Intravascular imaging may also be useful after the procedure to assess stent expansion, apposition, edge pathology, and residual lesion burden, thereby helping optimize the procedural result. In settings such as multilayer metal, diffuse or long ISR, small vessels, or clear evidence of fracture or edge pathology, a no-additional-metal strategy such as DCB may be an attractive alternative. Overall, the choice between DES and DCB should be individualized by integrating patient comorbidities, lesion morphology and length, evidence of fracture, underexpansion or edge disease, and imaging-guided lesion assessment and optimization, while balancing the sustained scaffolding and controlled drug elution of DES against the metal-sparing drug-delivery strategy of DCB [64].

In addition to the factors noted above, prior studies suggest that R-ISR may also be influenced by various biological and mechanical determinants (e.g., diabetes [23, 40], disordered bilirubin metabolism [7, 41], smaller reference vessel diameter [41], and the unique hemodynamics of bifurcation lesions [65]). However, owing to the limited number and consistency of primary studies, no quantitative pooling was performed.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the findings of this study were drawn from observational and predominantly retrospective studies, with variability in R-ISR definitions, stent generations, surveillance intensity, and treatment strategies across studies; therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Although we performed additional subgroup analysis restricted to contemporary treatment modalities, including DCB and second-generation DES, this subgroup remained based on a limited number of studies and was still subject to heterogeneity in patient selection, lesion complexity, follow-up duration, and endpoint definitions. Accordingly, the contemporary-treatment estimate should be regarded as a clinically relevant supplementary finding rather than a definitive benchmark for current practice. Second, several risk-factor syntheses were based on only a few studies, which limited precision and indicated that these associations should be considered preliminary. Larger prospective cohorts or adequately powered trials are needed to validate these findings and provide more reliable effect estimates. Third, restricting inclusion to Chinese- and English-language reports may have introduced language bias and may limit the global generalizability of our findings. Finally, medical adherence, particularly dual antiplatelet therapy and other secondary prevention therapies, may influence R-ISR risk, while this information was rarely and inconsistently reported in the included studies, and the available information was not standardized enough to support pooled analysis. Therefore, we could not assess the impact of medication adherence on R-ISR, and residual confounding related to post-PCI medical management cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In conclusion, R-ISR after treatment of an initial ISR lesion remains a clinically relevant problem. Its incidence varied across regions, treatment modalities, and follow-up duration, with lower pooled rates observed in DES-based studies and higher rates in studies using conventional balloon angioplasty. Renal disease, non-focal/diffuse morphology, longer lesion length, heterogeneous tissue pattern, stent fracture, and greater post-procedural percentage diameter stenosis were associated with a higher risk of R-ISR, whereas age showed no significant association. These findings support closer surveillance and individualized, intravascular imaging-guided management, especially within the first six months after reintervention. However, several risk-factor associations were derived from a limited number of studies and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Given the methodological heterogeneity across studies, larger prospective multicenter studies with harmonized definitions and standardized follow-up are needed to validate these findings and improve risk stratification in patients with R-ISR.
