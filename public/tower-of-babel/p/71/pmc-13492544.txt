Clinical decision support systems’ effect in reducing medication errors and adverse drug events: an expanded review from Making Healthcare Safer IV
Authors: Ania Syrowatka, Aneesa Motala, Emily Lawson, Paul G Shekelle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical decision support systems (CDSSs) are used to provide alerts and recommendations on prescribing and order verification with the intent of reducing the frequency and harm of medication errors (MEs) and adverse drug events (ADEs). This review assessed recent evidence on the effects of CDSSs on reducing MEs and ADEs. Related implementation outcomes such as alert overrides and unintended consequences of use were also assessed, and literature around the effective implementation of CDSSs was summarised. The review followed processes proposed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality Evidence-based Practice Center Programme. PubMed and the Cochrane Library were searched from 2015 to 2023. Results were narratively synthesised, and overall certainty of evidence was assessed for outcomes of interest. The search yielded 1335 unique abstracts, of which 34 articles met the criteria for inclusion. The type of clinical decision support and outcomes were heterogeneous between studies. Overall, computerised provider order entry with medication-related clinical decision support functions was associated with reduced MEs (moderate certainty of evidence) and prevention of ADEs (low certainty of evidence). Improved or targeted medication-related CDSSs were associated with reductions of MEs and ADEs (moderate certainty of evidence). However, alert override rates were high and varied between studies, and the appropriateness of the overrides was largely influenced by the type of alert. Other unintended consequences included CDSS-related errors, overdependence on alerts, alert fatigue, inappropriate alert overrides and provider burnout. An additional seven systematic reviews focused on barriers and facilitators of CDSS implementation. CDSSs reduce MEs and ADEs, with moderate-certainty and low-certainty evidence, respectively. However, there are several unintended consequences of CDSS use. PROSPERO registration number: CRD42023449710.Keywords: Decision Support Systems, Clinical

The evidence of benefits and harms was generally reported in different studies with varying contexts, making the net benefit difficult to estimate. Introduction

Adverse drug events (ADEs) are defined by the Institute of Medicine as ‘an injury resulting from medical intervention related to a drug’,1 and include non-preventable events, such as adverse drug reactions, and preventable events caused by medication errors (MEs). MEs are ‘any preventable event that may cause or lead to inappropriate medication use or patient harm’.2

ADEs are a leading type of healthcare-related harm, accounting for 39% and 64% of all adverse events in inpatient and outpatient settings, respectively.3 4 In the inpatient setting, 27% of ADEs are considered preventable, and 24% are assessed to have a severity of serious (defined as causing ‘harm that resulted in substantial intervention or prolonged recovery’) or higher.3 In the outpatient setting, 19% are considered preventable and 12% are categorised as serious or life-threatening.4 While not all MEs result in an ADE, they can occur at any point during the medication use process, including ordering or prescribing which accounts for about 50% of all MEs.5

Clinical decision support systems (CDSSs) can provide medication-related alerts to reduce the frequency and severity of MEs and ADEs and improve patient outcomes. CDSSs can provide a wide range of alerts such as drug interactions, drug allergies, potentially inappropriate prescribing or routes of administration, required therapeutic monitoring and relevant laboratory results.

CDSSs are used across various healthcare settings. In 2009, the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act incentivised health systems and providers to adopt a certified electronic health record (EHR) and make meaningful use of the information and technology to improve the quality and safety of healthcare, including medication safety. The most significant change in certified EHR uptake occurred from 2013 to 2014, corresponding with Stage 2 of meaningful use, which required use of CDSSs to ‘implement 5 clinical decision support interventions related to 4 or more clinical quality measures’ and ‘enable the functionality for drug–drug and drug–allergy interaction checks’.6 From 2013 to 2014, adoption of certified EHRs increased from 59% to 97% at non-Federal acute care hospitals and from 48% to 74% at office-based physicians, and has remained relatively constant since.7 8 At the time, CDSSs were not widespread in use, creating a need for scalable tools that could be integrated with various certified EHRs. Current CDSSs are vendor developed and less tailored to local contexts. These changes, incentives and vendor-specific designs have made development, implementation and evaluation of CDSSs a moving target.

The objective of this review was to assess the recent evidence on the effects of CDSSs on preventing MEs and ADEs as well as understanding related implementation outcomes such as alert overrides and unintended consequences of use. Literature around the effective implementation of CDSSs was also summarised.

Methods

This systematic review is an expanded version of a rapid review conducted for Making Healthcare Safer IV: A Continuous Updating of Patient Safety Harms and Practices.9 The review followed processes proposed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) Evidence-based Practice Center Programme. The detailed protocol is available on the AHRQ website10 and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023449710). AHRQ and a multidisciplinary technical expert panel advised on the scope of the rapid review. The expanded review followed the same protocol with the exception that it also included original research conducted in any Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development member countries, rather than focusing on original studies conducted in the USA and used dual independent review. This systematic review is reported according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guideline.11 Detailed methods are provided in online supplemental file 1. The detailed search strategy is provided in online supplemental file 2.

Results

27 systematic reviews, one overview of reviews and six primary research studies met the inclusion criteria (online supplemental file 3, figure S1). Evidence presented focused on the findings of good-quality or fair-quality systematic reviews and meta-analyses. These findings were supplemented with results from primary studies published after 2015 that did not make it in the systematic reviews. The evidence for effectiveness is presented by healthcare setting, content area and implementation outcomes. The list of excluded studies is in online supplemental file 4.

Across hospital settings

Six systematic reviews published in 2015 or later (table 1)12–17 and one overview of reviews18 reported on the effects of CDSSs on MEs and ADEs across hospital settings, including general inpatient, intensive care unit (ICU) and emergency department (ED). Abraham et al conducted an overview of seven systematic reviews published in 2017 or earlier that reported the effects of computerised provider order entry (CPOE) with or without clinical decision support (CDS) functions.18 Most included studies used uncontrolled pre–post or observational study designs; randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and controlled quasi-experimental designs were rarely used. The overview reported significant relative risk reductions with wide variation between systematic review pooled results for MEs (range: 54–92%) and ADEs (range: 35–53%). This review builds on the Abraham et al overview, with an overlap of only one systematic review.19

Our review focused on the findings of one good quality systematic review and meta-analysis that aligned with the inclusion criteria for experimental or controlled quasi-experimental study designs outlined in online supplemental file 1. Ciapponi et al covered a broad scope of interventions focused on reducing MEs and ADEs in hospital settings,16 including CPOE with CDS functions based on results from RCTs and interrupted time series (ITS) studies.

Medication errors

A Ciapponi et al meta-analysis compared CPOE with CDS functions to usual care, which showed a non-significant reduction in MEs (OR=0.74; 95% CI 0.31 to 1.79).16 The authors assigned a Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) certainty rating of moderate. Additional meta-analyses compared CPOE with standard versus ‘improved’ CDS functions, such as patient verification, e-prescribing with barcoding or enhanced clinical pharmacist surveillance for MEs. One meta-analysis of two RCTs demonstrated a significant reduction in MEs using ‘improved’ CDSSs (OR=0.85; 95% CI 0.74 to 0.97). Another meta-analysis of two ITS studies showed a reduction in MEs; however, the effect was not significant (OR=0.77; 95% CI 0.37 to 1.62). Overall, the authors assigned a GRADE of moderate to these findings.

The search identified four original studies reporting on MEs published since 2015 and not included in the Ciapponi et al meta-analyses (table 2). The Moja et al RCT evaluated a comprehensive commercial CDSS including medication-related alerts and found increased alert resolution rates (38.0% in intervention vs 33.7% in control group) and drug interaction resolution rates (OR=1.40; 95% CI 1.16 to 1.68).20 The Cossette et al RCT assessed CDSS alerts with a pharmacist-physician intervention; post hoc analyses of all hospitalisations showed significant increases in potentially inappropriate medication (PIM) discontinuation at 48-hours post-alert in the intervention (43.4%) versus the control group (23.1%).21

The McCourt et al ITS analysis evaluated the effect of CPOE with comprehensive medication-related CDS functions and showed significant reductions of 30.1% in orders with errors and 12.6% for non-intercepted serious prescribing errors postintervention.22 The Pierce et al ITS analysis assessed the effects of commercial CDSS alerts on opioid prescribing in various hospital settings.23 There were no reported differences in prescribing practices post implementation; however, the study showed improved guideline adherence with a small increase in provision of naloxone (p<0.001). Adverse drug events

Ciapponi et al meta-analysis of two RCTs showed a non-significant effect of CPOE with CDS functions on ADEs (OR=0.24; 95% CI 0.04 to 1.50).16 Both RCTs reported a significant reduction; however, the heterogeneity of the studies resulted in an overall non-significant effect. The authors assigned a GRADE of very low. Another meta-analysis of two ITS studies found that ‘improved’ CDSSs significantly reduced ADEs (OR=0.82; 95% CI 0.71 to 0.94, moderate certainty).16 One ITS study showed positive effects of prioritised alerts compared with non-prioritised alerts on resolving potential ADEs (mean difference=1.98; 95% CI 1.65 to 2.31, low GRADE).24

The search identified two original studies reporting on ADEs (table 2). McCourt et al showed significant reductions in non-intercepted ADEs with a crude relative reduction in actual preventable ADEs (0.8% preintervention vs 0.04% postintervention; p<0.001); however, the ITS change was not statistically significant.22 Pierce et al did not report any significant differences in rates of opioid-related ADEs post implementation.25

Intensive care unit

The Prgomet et al meta-analysis of five studies conducted in adult ICUs focused on the use of CPOE with or without CDS functions (table 1).19 They found a non-significant reduction in MEs (relative risk (RR)=0.11; 95% CI 0.00 to 3.41). The interventions varied from simple CPOE to complex CDSSs including medication alerts for duplicate orders, allergies and drug interactions. The pooled estimate and wide CI occurred due to the inclusion of one large pre–post study that accounted for ∼78% of the medication orders included in the meta-analysis and reported a 39% increase in MEs after implementation of CPOE only. Two studies included in the meta-analysis (one RCT and one uncontrolled pre–post design) included CDSS medication-related alerts.25 26 Both studies showed significant reductions in MEs compared with paper-based prescribing. The certainty of the evidence in this meta-analysis was very low due to heterogeneity of the included studies.

Emergency department

One systematic review focused on the effects of CPOE and CDSSs on MEs and ADEs in the ED (table 1).27 The CDSSs varied from simple dose checking to more advanced alerts, such as dosing for renal insufficiency. 10 studies, including 2 RCTs, 1 controlled before–after (CBA) study and 1 ITS design, generally demonstrated statistically significant reductions in various types of MEs (range: 2–31%; five studies) and improvements in appropriate prescribing or guideline concordance (range: 5.2–21.5%; five studies). One crossover study showed a significant decrease in ADEs with CDSS (7.1–3.4%; p value=0.02).28 The certainty of evidence from this narrative review was very low due to the heterogeneity of the included studies.

Ambulatory and outpatient care

Three systematic reviews reported on the effects of CDSSs in ambulatory and outpatient settings (table 1).29–31 Two covered limited scopes, focusing on potentially inappropriate prescribing31 and potential drug–drug interactions,29 and were not discussed in detail. The most informative review by Cerqueira et al narratively summarised the literature on CPOE with interruptive CDSS alerts (ie, ‘immediate notification of potential errors or risks at time of order entry’) in the outpatient setting.30 Five of six studies found significant improvements on reducing MEs using interruptive CDSS alerts. One pre–post study reported lower rates of prescribing errors in CDSS adopters versus non-adopters (1.5-fold decrease, p<0.001), and a CBA study showed greater reductions in potential drug–drug interactions post-CDSS implementation (RR=0.81, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.99) compared with controls (RR=0.91, 95% CI 0.32 to 1.29). One RCT showed an increase in appropriate drug dosing in the CDSS group (based on creatinine clearance) (OR=1.89, p<0.0001), and two pre–post studies showed reductions in inappropriate medication use (antibiotic prescribing for uncomplicated acute respiratory infection decreased from 22% to 3%, p=0.0001; glyburide prescribing in older adults decreased from 3.3% to 1.2%, p<0.001). However, one RCT showed interruptive alerts had a non-significant effect in reducing prescribing errors compared with an on-demand CDSS (OR=1.31, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.92).32 The certainty of evidence for this review’s conclusion of ‘a clear indication that many categories of alerts are effective at changing prescriber behavior’ was low due to the synthesis being narrative with observational studies included.

One original study by Friebe et al was not captured by these systematic reviews (table 2).33 The ITS analysis of the impact of migrating a CDSS based on the Beers’ criteria from a legacy EHR to a new EHR showed a significant reduction in PIM prescribing in older adults (18.5%). The risk of bias assessment for this study is in online supplemental file 5, table S6.

Effectiveness of CDSS with laboratory, medication allergy, anticoagulant and chemotherapy-related alerts

Several systematic reviews reported on the effectiveness of CDSSs for specific content areas: laboratory-related alerts (one meta-analysis),34 medication-related allergies (two reviews),35 36 anticoagulation (one review)37 and oncology (four reviews).38–41 Details are provided in table 3 and online supplemental file 6. CDSSs with laboratory-related alerts showed moderate and consistent certainty of evidence for improving rates of prescribing appropriate medication and dose. The systematic reviews focused on medication allergies, anticoagulation and oncology provided low or very low certainty of evidence that CDSSs improved MEs or ADEs.

Implementation outcomes: alert relevance and overrides

Six systematic reviews identified based on the criteria outlined in online supplemental table S-3 reported on implementation outcomes (table 3).15 35 36 42–44 Two focused specifically on implementation outcomes;43 44 notably, none of the studies included in these reviews met the original study design inclusion criteria outlined in online supplemental table S-3. Four were not discussed in detail; three focused on allergy alerts35 36 42 and one only provided medication alert acceptance rates for studies meeting the inclusion criteria for the primary focus of their review.15

Poly et al quantified the magnitude of medication-related alert overrides in CPOE systems and assessed the appropriateness.44 Override rates varied between studies ranging from 46% to 96%. The appropriateness of overrides ranged from 29% to 100%, influenced by the type of medication-related alert. The certainty of the evidence for a precise rate of overrides was very low due to heterogeneity of the included studies; however, the certainty of evidence that there is a clinically important high rate of alert overrides was moderate, as all reported override rates exceeded 46%. Carli et al assessed the clinical relevance of medication-related CDSS alerts in CPOE.43 The positive predictive values (PPVs) of alerts ranged from 8% to 83%, and sensitivities ranged from 38% to 91%. Higher PPVs were associated with alerts informed by patient-level data, such as comorbidities and laboratory results.

The Shah et al primary study reported implementation outcomes, which compared the clinical relevance of medication-related alerts from two commercial CDSSs.45 The evaluation was controlled by testing both applications on the same sample of patients. The study was assessed as having a low risk of bias using version 2 of the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) (online supplemental file 5, table S7). They found that the CDSS providing targeted and personalised alerts based on information in the patient record resulted in 93% fewer alerts with substantially and significantly improved sensitivity, specificity, PPVs and negative predictive values across inpatient and outpatient settings.

Unintended consequences

Box 1 summarises potential unintended consequences of implementing CPOE with CDS functions described in five systematic reviews.46–50 Study details are provided in the online supplemental file 7. Additionally, Korb-Savoldelli et al quantified the magnitude of CPOE-related medication prescribing errors for inpatient and outpatient settings46 and found that CPOE system-related MEs occurred in 0.3–6.3% of prescriptions across studies, accounting for 6.1–77.7% of prescribing errors. Jankovic and Chen reviewed the implications of CDSSs on provider burnout50 and identified four key harmful factors contributing to clinician burnout: interruptive or hard-stop alerts, lack of relevant or ‘right information’ and inadequate CDSS integration.

Overall certainty of evidence

All the certainty of evidence assessments are based on the included systematic reviews, most of which were assigned low or very low certainty of evidence (online supplemental file 5, table S-4). Overall assessments of the certainty of evidence for each outcome were based on the directionality of the association, and not precise estimates of the magnitude of the effect (table 4). These effects were contextually dependent and will vary from application to application. Provider outcomes, specifically changes in prescribing behaviour, were used to support assessment of the impact of CDSSs on MEs, since the two outcomes are highly correlated. High degrees of alert fatigue were rated as very low based on the evidence of the included studies of this review. Other observational studies not included in this review may provide stronger evidence of this unintended consequence.

Factors influencing CDSS success

Nine systematic reviews reported on various aspects of CDSS implementation.51–59 The most informative reviews covered different levels of implementation: interface, information and interaction designs of CDSSs,54 prescriber perceptions of medication-related CDSS58 and strategies used by hospitals to implement medication-related alerts.59 Details are provided in online supplemental file 8, table S5-S7.

Discussion

This systematic review builds on the published literature to provide a current assessment of the state of the evidence around the benefits and unintended consequences of medication-related CDSS implementation and use and identifies critical gaps in the literature and key directions for future research. Most of the literature published since 2015 focused on summarising rather than strengthening the evidence. The systematic reviews were generally of good or fair quality and showed reductions in MEs or ADEs across various healthcare settings and content areas. Most reviews predominantly included uncontrolled observational studies with narrative summaries, providing low or very low certainty of evidence. Only one review conducted meta-analyses using RCT and ITS designs and included comparatively low numbers of studies that demonstrated significant or non-significant reductions with a moderate level of certainty.

An important finding of our review was that the evidence about benefits and harms did not come from the same studies. Therefore, estimates of net effect of CDSSs relied on indirect comparisons of benefits in one set of studies versus harms in other studies, which covered diverse CDSS implementations, populations, outcomes, measurement approaches and contexts. This is fundamentally different from studies of most healthcare interventions where benefits and harms are measured in the same population receiving the same intervention, thus allowing for direct assessments of the net effect.

There was substantial heterogeneity between studies with regard to the quality of the CDSSs (basic vs advanced features and alerts), type of decision support provided (eg, drug–drug interactions, contraindications), populations under study, outcomes under investigation (eg, specific MEs or ADEs), outcomes measurement approaches and characteristics of the healthcare organisations, leading to heterogeneous results across studies and making it impractical to summarise the evidence into an overall effect. The key is to optimise CDSSs and implementation to maximise and realise the potential benefits in each context.

The systematic reviews primarily included studies focused on the effects of CDSSs on MEs which, from a methodological standpoint, are more easily identified through data extracted from CPOE systems or error reports. Although not all MEs cause ADEs, it is critical to understand the effect of CDSSs on patient harm, especially given the various unintended consequences associated with medication-related alerts.

High rates of alert overrides, including overrides of appropriate alerts, were observed, signalling missed opportunities to prevent MEs and potential ADEs. The effects of the CDSSs on MEs, ADEs, implementation outcomes and unintended consequences were studied in unique contexts, making the net benefit challenging to understand.

Limitations

This review relied mostly on evidence from systematic reviews, which predominantly included uncontrolled study designs, such as pre–post comparisons. As a result, most of the reviews were assessed as providing low or very low certainty of evidence. Many of the studies using RCTs and controlled quasi-experimental studies were conducted over 10 years ago, making the findings less relevant to the information technology landscape today. Another limitation was the multicomponent nature of many interventions, which evaluated the effects of CPOE with CDS functions, making it impractical to disentangle the direct effects of the CDS medication-related alerts.

The review findings must be interpreted in the context of the major shift from investigator-initiated development and implementation of medication-related CDSSs in the late 1990s to the largely vendor-based tools available today. Currently, CDSSs are generally purchased separately and integrated with EHRs to provide alerts at the time of prescribing or order verification. These tools vary widely in targets, scope and effectiveness. Given the current requirements for meaningful use of EHRs and implementation of CDSSs to improve patient outcomes, it is critical to understand the effects of CDSSs on the prevention of MEs and ADEs and addressing potential harms and unintended consequences of implementation.

Future directions

Future studies should measure and assess effects of CDSSs within the same context on all related outcomes (eg, clinical, implementation and unintended consequences). An RCT or controlled quasi-experimental design may provide stronger evidence around benefits and unintended consequences of CDSSs, which could help guide optimisation and implementation. Publications should provide detailed information about the context of the study to inform meaningful interpretation and assess generalisability of findings.

Given the widespread adoption of CPOE and CDSSs, future research should focus on how CDSSs could be made safer through successful collaborations between vendors, investigators and clinicians. The lessons learnt should be considered for CDSS development in different contexts. As artificial intelligence, such as natural language processing and complex machine learning, is being used more commonly in CDSSs, future studies should assess its impact on patient safety. Studies should also address equity by identifying and mitigating disparities between different types of health systems and care populations. For example, safety-net or rural hospitals may have less resources to build effective or sophisticated, interoperable CDSSs compared with large urban health systems.
