Efficacy of Antibiotic Regimens for Pneumonia in Young Infants Aged 0–59 Days: A Systematic Review
Authors: Krysten North, Alejandro Frade Garcia, Mark Crouch, Spencer Kimsen, Amber Hoey, Carrie Wade, Yumin Kim, Roger Chou, Karen M Edmond, Anne CC Lee, Chris A Rees
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCONTEXTPneumonia is a leading cause of death in young infants.OBJECTIVESTo evaluate the efficacy of different antibiotic regimens to treat young infant pneumonia on critical clinical outcomes.DATA SOURCESMEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, World Health Organization (WHO) Global Index Medicus, Cochrane Central Registry of Trials.STUDY SELECTIONWe included randomized controlled trials of young infants aged 0 to 59 days with pneumonia (population) comparing the efficacy of antibiotic regimens (intervention) with alternate regimens or management (control) on clinical outcomes.DATA EXTRACTIONWe extracted data and assessed risk of bias in duplicate. We used Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation to assess certainty of evidence.LIMITATIONSTrials were heterogeneous, which precluded data pooling.RESULTSOf 2601 publications screened, 10 randomized controlled trials were included. Seven trials were hospital-based (n = 869) and 3 were nonhospital-based (n = 4329). No hospital-based trials evaluated WHO-recommended first-choice regimens. One trial found the WHO-recommended second-choice antibiotic, cefotaxime, to have similar rates of treatment success as non-WHO-recommended regimens of either amoxicillin–clavulanate (RR 0.99, 95% confidence interval 0.82–1.10) or amoxicillin–clavulanate/cefotaxime (RR 1.02, 95% confidence interval 0.86–1.12). Among 3 nonhospital-based trials comparing oral amoxicillin to alternate regimens to treat isolated tachypnea among infants aged 7–59 days, there were no differences in treatment failure between amoxicillin and alternate regimens. Certainty of evidence was low or very low for all primary outcomes.CONCLUSIONSWe found limited evidence to support the superiority of any single antibiotic regimen over alternate regimens to treat young infant pneumonia.

Pneumonia is 1 of the leading causes of death in young infants, resulting in >200 000 deaths each year.1 Although pneumonia affects children of all ages, the greatest burden of years of life lost occurs among young infants (aged 0–59 days), who have a substantially higher case fatality rate than older children.2–4 Most pneumonia-related deaths among young infants are thought to be preventable through early and optimal treatment with supportive care, supplemental oxygen when indicated, and appropriate antibiotics.5

The isolated pathogens responsible for pneumonia in children may be viral in the majority of cases, but Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus agalactiae, and Enterobacteriaceae species are the leading bacterial pathogens associated with community- and hospital-acquired pneumonia in young infants.6–9 Young infants are particularly vulnerable to poor outcomes from bacterial pneumonia, because vaccines targeting pneumonia-causing pathogens are not widely available for infants under 6 weeks of age.

On the basis of the assumption that a high proportion of fatal pneumonia in young infants may be of bacterial origin, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that young infants with suspected pneumonia be managed with antibiotics (Table 1).10–12 Among hospitalized young infants, the WHO recommends first-choice treatment with intramuscular (IM) or intravenous (IV) ampicillin or penicillin plus gentamicin. For first-choice treatment of pneumonia among nonhospitalized young infants, specifically the subgroup of young infants aged 7 to 59 days who have tachypnea as their only clinical sign, the WHO recommends treatment with per os/oral (PO) amoxicillin for 7 days. Infants aged 0 to 6 days are classified as possible serious bacterial infection and treated with IM penicillin and gentamicin given higher mortality rates among this younger age group.7,8,11

A contemporary understanding of optimal antibiotic treatment regimens for young infants with suspected pneumonia is important given the growing recognition of antimicrobial resistance among pathogens that cause infections in young children.13–15 Our objective was to systematically review the published literature to determine the efficacy of commonly used antibiotic regimens for pneumonia among young infants compared with alternate regimens or compare the efficacy of different antibiotic duration regimens. We examined randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in 3 categories: (1) WHO-recommended regimens compared with alternate regimens, (2) other comparisons of non-WHO–recommended regimens, and (3) antibiotic duration comparisons.

Methods

Study Design

The protocol for this systematic review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023417564).16 Our population, intervention, comparator, outcome question was: Among young infants aged 0 to 59 days with pneumonia (population), do antibiotic regimens for the treatment of pneumonia (intervention) compared with alternate antibiotic regimens or alternate management such as observation, referral, or placebo (comparator) improve critical outcomes (outcome)?

This systematic review was commissioned by the WHO to inform guidelines regarding treatment of young infant pneumonia.

Search Methods

We searched MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, the WHO Global Index Medicus, and the Cochrane Central Registry of Trials. All databases were searched from inception to April 19, 2023. The full search strategy is available in Supplemental Appendix 1. We also hand-searched key reviews and systematic reviews related to young infant pneumonia to identify additional studies that met our inclusion criteria.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included published RCTs, individual or cluster randomized, for which a full-text manuscript was available that reported the results of trials comparing antibiotic efficacy for pneumonia among young infants aged 0 to 59 days. We excluded unpublished gray literature, nonrandomized, and pharmacokinetic studies. We applied no limitations on the basis of language or publication date.

Study Setting

We did not limit inclusion on the basis of the study setting. Enrollment and treatment could occur in any location including the home, health center, outpatient health facility, or hospital. We define nonhospital-based trials as those in which infants received their antibiotic treatment in any setting not self-identified as a hospital facility, including a health center, health post, or the home. We define hospital-based trials as those in which infants received their antibiotic treatment while inpatient in a hospital, as defined by the study investigators.

Participants

We included RCTs of infants aged 0 to 59 days postnatal age who were diagnosed with pneumonia. Studies enrolling infants over 60 days were included only if:

1. there was a subgroup analysis presenting outcomes among infants aged 0 to 59 days; or

2. the mean postnatal age in days plus 1 SD above the mean was less than 60 days.

Pneumonia Definition

We did not apply an a priori definition for pneumonia, and pneumonia definitions varied by individual trial. Some trials required radiologic criteria, such as chest radiograph findings, for the diagnosis of pneumonia, whereas others used clinical criteria. The nonhospital trials typically used the WHO clinical criteria of fast breathing pneumonia, defined as isolated tachypnea (>60 breaths per minute), with no other clinical signs among infants aged 7 to 59 days.12

Interventions

We included publications that evaluated any antibiotic regimen to treat pneumonia in young infants, as well as those that evaluated antibiotic regimens as a component of a package of interventions. We excluded studies that only enrolled infants who were treated prophylactically or on the basis of risk factors without clinical evidence of pneumonia. We also excluded studies that evaluated antiviral, antifungal, or antimalarial interventions. WHO-recommended antibiotic regimens are defined in Table 1.

Comparator

We included studies with a comparator group that was either the standard of care, an alternate antibiotic regimen, placebo, or an alternate management strategy such as referral or observation.

Outcomes

Critical outcomes were: (1) mortality (all-cause or infection-specific), (2) clinical improvement or deterioration, (3) neurodevelopmental outcomes, or (4) adverse antibiotic effects. Secondary outcomes included (a) hospital utilization (ie, length of stay or readmission), (b) relapse, and (c) cost effectiveness. We preferentially reported the latest time point available for all outcomes. Studies reporting only pharmacokinetic outcomes were excluded.

Study Selection

We initially screened studies on the basis of title and abstract, then by full text. All study screening was conducted in duplicate and adjudicated by a third author, using Covidence systematic review software.

Data Extraction and Management

We extracted data into a predesigned extraction spreadsheet. All data were independently extracted in duplicate, with discrepancies adjudicated by a third author. For published studies of infants with isolated tachypnea aged 0 to 59 days, we contacted authors to request data disaggregated into age groups of 0 to 6 days and 7 to 59 days to enable analysis of data according to WHO Integrated Management of Childhood Illness case definitions (as described in the definitions section above).12

For dichotomous outcomes, we extracted event rates (n of N, %). For continuous outcomes, we extracted mean/SD or median/interquartile range. We extracted both unadjusted and adjusted effect sizes, as well as both intention-to-treat and per-protocol outcomes as reported.

Risk of Bias Assessment

We determined the risk of bias (RoB) among included studies using the revised Cochrane RoB tool for randomized trials (RoB-2)17 and the revised Cochrane RoB-2 tool for cluster-randomized trials.18 RoB was assessed by 2 independent assessors and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Consistent with Cochrane guidance, a study was deemed to have an overall high RoB if there was high risk in any 1 domain or some risk in 3 or more domains.18 A study was defined to have overall some RoB if there was some risk in 1 or 2 domains, and overall low RoB if there was low risk in every domain.

Data Analysis and Synthesis

We calculated relative and absolute effect sizes for reported outcomes using the numbers of study participants and events for the individually randomized trials. For dichotomous outcomes, we calculated the relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) and risk difference and 95% CI. For continuous outcomes, we calculated the mean difference (MD) and 95% CI. For cluster randomized trials (CRTs), we reported effect sizes adjusted for clustering effects where available. We preferentially used intention-to-treat over per-protocol outcomes for calculating effect sizes and determining certainty of evidence.19 Meta-analysis was not conducted because of the small number of heterogeneous studies for each comparison.

The certainty of evidence for each comparison and prioritized outcomes was assigned using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation methods and categorized certainty as very low, low, moderate, or high certainty.20 The grading of the certainty of the evidence was based on RoB, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and presence of publication bias. See Supplemental Appendix 2 for additional details.

Results

After deduplication, we screened 2601 title/abstracts and 47 full-text manuscripts (Fig 1). Ten RCTs met study inclusion criteria; 9 were individually randomized and 1 was cluster-randomized. See Supplemental Table 1 for a list of excluded full-text studies and reasons for exclusion.

Characteristics of Included Studies

Among the 10 included trials, 7 (n = 869 infants) were hospital-based and 3 (n = 4329 infants) were nonhospital-based (Table 2). Studies were published from 1998 to 2019. Trials were conducted in geographically diverse regions (Americas, 2; Western Pacific, 4; Southeast Asia, 4; Eastern Mediterranean, 1; and Africa, 3), including 2 multicountry studies. No studies were restricted to either low birth weight or preterm infants, or reported subgroup analyses related to these populations.

Risk of Bias

Six studies were at high overall RoB,21–26 3 studies had some overall RoB,27–29 and 1 study had low overall RoB30 (Fig 2, Supplemental Table 5). For the domain of “bias arising from the randomization process,” 2 trials were deemed high RoB and 4 trials were deemed to be at some RoB, primarily because of lack of information about allocation concealment during randomization. For the domain of “bias because of deviations from the intended interventions,” 3 studies were deemed to be at high RoB, primarily because of high rates of protocol deviations, and 5 studies were deemed to be at some RoB, primarily because of lack of information about protocol deviations. Only 1 study was deemed to be at some risk for missing outcome data and the remainder were deemed to be at low risk. For the domain of “bias in measurement of the outcome,” 4 studies were deemed to be at some or high RoB, largely because of ill-defined or subjective primary outcomes with unblinded outcome assessors. For the domain of “bias in selection of the reported result,” 4 studies were deemed to be at some or high RoB, primarily because of a lack of information related to trial registration, no protocol article, and no clear reference to a predetermined data analysis plan. Overall, trials with a composite high RoB used unblinded, subjective metrics for outcome measurement; were lacking information about protocol deviations; or did not present information about predefined data analysis plans.

Hospital Trials

WHO-Recommended Antibiotic Regimens

No trials evaluated WHO-recommended first-choice antibiotics.

One trial (Zou 2017) evaluated an alternate regimen versus a WHO-recommended second-choice antibiotic. This single-center trial in China found similar rates of treatment success when comparing IV amoxicillin–clavulanate or IV amoxicillin–clavulanate/cefotaxime to WHO-recommended second-choice antibiotic IV cefotaxime (IV amoxicillin–clavulanate: RR 1.02 [95% CI 0.86–1.12], n = 205, very low certainty of evidence; IV amoxicillin–clavulanate/cefotaxime: RR 0.99 [0.82–1.10], n = 210, very low certainty of evidence) (Table 3, Supplemental Tables 3 and 4). Other critical and secondary outcomes including mortality, treatment failure, hospital utilization, or other adverse antibiotic effects were not evaluated.21

Non-WHO–Recommended Antibiotic Regimens

Three hospital-based trials compared the efficacy of different non-WHO–recommended antibiotic regimens (meropenem versus imipenem,22 cephalosporin versus penicillin,23 meropenem versus cefoperazone24; Table 3, Supplemental Tables 3–6).

Lina 2016, a study of uncomplicated pneumonia in China, demonstrated that neither cephalosporin/penicillin in combination nor cephalosporin monotherapy was more effective for increasing treatment success than penicillin monotherapy (cephalosporin/penicillin: RR 1.00 [95% CI 0.71–1.21], n = 100, very low certainty of evidence; cephalosporin: RR 1.07 [95% CI 0.79–1.26], n = 98, very low certainty of evidence). Other critical and secondary outcomes including mortality, treatment failure, hospital utilization, or other adverse antibiotic effects were not evaluated.23

Wu 2018, a study of severe pneumonia in China, demonstrated higher treatment success, lower relapse rates, and less adverse antibiotic effects among infants treated with IV meropenem compared with IV imipenem (treatment success: RR 1.26 [95% CI 1.13–1.30], n = 110, very low certainty of evidence; relapse: RR 0.19 [95% CI 0.02–0.71], n = 110, very low certainty of evidence; adverse antibiotic effects: RR 0.19 [95% CI 0.02–0.71], n = 110, very low certainty of evidence), whereas mortality was not compared in the study.22 Similarly, Lin 2021 demonstrated that IV meropenem plus cefoperazone was more effective than IV cefoperazone monotherapy with regard to treatment success among infants with radiographic pneumonia (RR 1.19 [95% CI 1.09–1.22], n = 130, very low certainty of evidence) and was associated with a lower likelihood of adverse antibiotic effects (RR 0.26 [95% CI 0.07–0.75], n = 130, very low certainty of evidence), whereas mortality and treatment failure rates were not compared in the study.24

Duration of Antibiotic Regimens

Engle 2003, Engle 2000, and Mathur 2018 compared the efficacy of different antibiotic durations (Table 3, Supplemental Tables 3–5, 7).26,28,29 All 3 trials limited their study population to hospitalized newborn infants who showed 48-hour improvement in pneumonia signs. Engle 2000, using ampicillin plus gentamicin in the United States, and Mathur 2018, using ceftriaxone plus amikacin in India, compared 4-day to 7-day treatment among infants undergoing treatment of pneumonia and found no difference in treatment failure between the 2 groups (Engle 2000: 0% treatment failure in both arms, RR not estimable, n = 73, low certainty of evidence; Mathur 2018: 0% treatment failure in both arms, RR not estimable, n = 70, very low certainty of evidence), but both found lower length of hospitalization among the shorter treatment group (Engle 2000: MD −2.1 days [95% CI −2.7 to −1.5], n = 73, moderate certainty of evidence; Mathur 2018: MD −2.1 [95% CI −2.2 to −2.0 days], n = 70, moderate certainty of evidence). Mortality and adverse antibiotic effects were not evaluated in both studies.28,29 Engle 2003 compared 2 days to 4 days of ampicillin and gentamicin with infants in the United States, but data were insufficient to draw conclusions (relapse: 21.4% in 2-days group and 0% in 4-days group, RR not estimable, n = 26, very low certainty of evidence; other critical or secondary outcomes not evaluated).26

Nonhospital Trials

Among the nonhospital trials, all 3 trials compared the effect of PO amoxicillin to either placebo (Tikmani 2017), IM penicillin plus gentamicin (Afrinest 2015), or standard care (Enhanced Management of Pneumonia in Community 2021) (Table 3, Supplemental Tables 5–9).27,30,31 All 3 trials included infants with fast breathing as the only clinical sign of illness and recruited infants at home or primary health care clinics and followed up at home.

Tikmani 2017 was a multicenter trial in Pakistan comparing PO amoxicillin versus placebo, which found similar rates of treatment failure among infants aged 7 to 59 days, although the point estimate suggested a possible benefit of amoxicillin (RR 0.51 [95% CI 0.20–1.35], n = 544, very low certainty of evidence).27 Comparisons on other critical and secondary outcomes were reported but the differences were not significant (mortality: 0.0% in PO amoxicillin and 0.4% in placebo, RR not estimable, n = 544, very low certainty of evidence; relapse: RR 1.18 [95% CI 0.43–3.20], n = 544, very low certainty of evidence; hospital admission: RR 0.77 [95% CI 0.17–3.42], n = 544, very low certainty of evidence; adverse antibiotic effects not evaluated).27 This trial enrolled infants aged 0 to 59 days, but disaggregated data were provided by authors for the purpose of this review for infants aged 7 to 59 days. Notably, in the full age group 0 to 59 days, the trialists reported that amoxicillin was more effective than placebo (risk difference 3.1% [95% CI 0.3%–5.8%]) for preventing treatment failure, prompting an early trial stop by the Data Safety and Monitoring Board.27

Afrinest 2015 compared PO amoxicillin versus IM penicillin and gentamicin among 1451 infants in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kenya, and Nigeria.31 This trial similarly enrolled infants aged 0 to 59 days but provided disaggregated data for the 7 to 59 days group per request for this review. Treatment failure was similar among infants treated with PO amoxicillin compared with IM penicillin/gentamicin (RR 0.86 [95% CI 0.70–1.07], n = 1451, very low certainty of evidence). The evidence from other outcomes was insufficient to draw conclusions (mortality: RR 0.50 [95% CI 0.05–5.56], n = 1451, very low certainty of evidence; relapse: RR 0.98 [95% CI 0.41–2.33], n = 1451, very low certainty of evidence; no adverse antibiotic effects reported).

Enhanced Management of Pneumonia in Community 2021 was a CRT in Bangladesh, Ethiopia, India, and Malawi (n = 2334) comparing PO amoxicillin and enhanced case management by community health workers versus standard care (referral to a health facility after a prereferral antibiotic dose).30 All critical outcomes were similar between study arms (mortality: adjusted RR [aRR] 0.99 [95% CI 0.14–6.97], n = 2312, low certainty of evidence; treatment failure: aRR 0.86 [95% CI 0.58–1.26], n = 2312, low certainty of evidence; hospitalization: aRR 0.63 [95% CI 0.29–1.33], n = 2312, very low certainty of evidence). Risk ratios were adjusted for clustering. There were no adverse antibiotic effects reported for this trial.

Discussion

Overall, the clinical trials included in this systematic review did not identify routinely used antibiotic regimens that are more efficacious than others for the treatment of pneumonia in young infants. Relatively few trials have examined the efficacy of antibiotic regimens for the treatment of pneumonia in young infants. Because of heterogeneity of trials, meta-analyses were not conducted in this systematic review.

Importantly, to date, there have been no hospital-based studies examining the efficacy of WHO-recommended first-choice antibiotic regimen (penicillin or ampicillin, plus gentamicin) for hospitalized young infants with pneumonia compared with other regimens. Additionally, we found only 1 study examining a WHO-recommended second-choice regimen.21 In this age of evolving antimicrobial resistance, it is critical to understand the efficacy of first- and second-choice antibiotic regimens compared with other therapies to optimize pneumonia treatment of hospitalized young infants.

All of the nonhospital-based trials evaluated the efficacy of first-choice treatment of nonhospitalized young infants (PO amoxicillin) as compared with alternative regimens. In all 3 trials, PO amoxicillin was equivalent to the comparator group for the treatment of young infants with isolated tachypnea aged 7 to 59 days with low or very low certainty of evidence for all outcomes. We focused on isolated tachypnea in infants aged 7 to 59 days to inform WHO guidelines for this specific presentation and age group; however, our estimates were based on a subset of the full 0 to 59-day population reported in the original Afrinest (2015) and Tikmani (2017) studies and were less precise. Our findings were similar to those reported in the full cohort of infants aged 0 to 59 days in Afrinest, but Tikmani reported significantly lower rates of treatment failure in the full 0 to 59-day cohort in the PO amoxicillin group compared with placebo.25,27

Although there have been several systematic reviews on antibiotic regimens for pneumonia in older infants and children aged >2 months,32–34 to our knowledge, this is the first systematic review comparing the efficacy of different antibiotic regimens for the treatment of pneumonia in young infants aged 0 to 59 days.

The lack of evidence related to the optimal antibiotic regimen to treat pneumonia in young infants may stem, in part, from the difficulty in clinically distinguishing neonatal pneumonia from other infectious and noninfectious causes of respiratory distress in a young infant. The hospital-based studies in this review diagnosed pneumonia on the basis of varying combinations of clinical or laboratory signs plus chest radiograph findings, despite studies demonstrating the limitations of chest radiograph for diagnosing pneumonia.35,36

In the hospitalized newborn, respiratory pathologies such as respiratory distress syndrome or meconium aspiration syndrome may closely resemble pneumonia among infants. In all newborns, other infectious etiologies such as viral illnesses may also mimic bacterial pneumonia. Among young infants aged 0 to 59 days with infectious pneumonia, it is possible that there was no clearly superior antibiotic regimen identified in this review because most cases of pneumonia in young infants may be viral and not bacterial in nature.6 The WHO defines pneumonia in young infants as isolated tachypnea in an infant aged 7 to 59 days, which circumvents the need for laboratory or radiographic evaluations to diagnose pneumonia, but also likely captures many infants without bacterial pathology.37 Although etiologies of pneumonia are rarely confirmed when a diagnosis of pneumonia is made clinically, future studies that assess the efficacy of antibiotics among children may consider incorporating testing for pathogens to address this important question.

This review was limited by the small number of studies available and the heterogeneity of the antibiotic regimens in the included studies, which prohibited our ability to pool results or to conduct subgroup analyses. Most studies had a high RoB and a high degree of imprecision among outcome estimates. Many studies did not report outcomes related to adverse events, an important consideration when choosing antibiotic regimens.

The applicability of the review findings is subject to the context (high-resource versus low-resource) and settings (hospital versus community) in which each trial occurred. For example, studies conducted in hospitals of a higher-resource setting would not have generalized applicability to community management in lower-resource settings and vice versa. Despite these limitations, this systematic review adds a novel synthesis of the literature related to pneumonia management among the uniquely vulnerable population of young infants 0 to 59 days of age.

Conclusions

In this systematic review, we identified 10 studies of different antibiotic regimens for the treatment of pneumonia in young infants. Overall, the included trials did not identify routinely used antibiotic regimens more efficacious than other antibiotic regimens for the treatment of pneumonia in young infants. More evidence is needed to clarify optimal antibiotic regimens for the treatment of pneumonia in young infants.
