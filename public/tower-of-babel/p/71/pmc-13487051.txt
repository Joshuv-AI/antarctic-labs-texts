Distinct triglyceride-associated lipid profiles in coronary artery ectasia and ascending thoracic aortic aneurysm: A 10-year retrospective single-center case-control study
Authors: Yuhan Qin, Ziqi Sha, Xudong Li, Dong Wang, Yong Qiao, Gaoliang Yan, Chengchun Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCoronary artery ectasia (CAE) and ascending thoracic aortic aneurysm (ATAA) are vascular dilative disorders with unclear lipid profiles. We compared lipid parameters among patients with CAE, ATAA, and controls, focusing on triglycerides (TG).MethodsThis retrospective single-center case-control study included 490 patients with CAE, 490 with ATAA, and 490 controls from January 2016 to December 2025. Lipid associations with CAE and ATAA were assessed using multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic splines, and subgroup analyses.ResultsCompared with controls, CAE patients had higher TG and lower HDL-C, whereas ATAA patients had higher lp(a), lower TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C. In fully adjusted models, TG was independently associated with CAE but not ATAA. Each 1-SD increase in TG was associated with higher odds of CAE (OR=1.63, 95% CI: 1.34–1.91), with a graded increase across TG quartiles (p for trend <0.001) and a non-linear dose-response pattern.ConclusionsCAE and ATAA show distinct lipid profiles. Elevated TG is independently associated with CAE, but not consistently with ATAA, suggesting different triglyceride-related metabolic patterns in coronary and aortic dilative disorders.Keywords: coronary artery ectasia, ascending thoracic aortic aneurysm, triglyceride, lipid

Introduction

Coronary artery ectasia (CAE) and ascending thoracic aortic aneurysm (ATAA) are two clinically important vascular dilative disorders characterized by abnormal enlargement of the coronary arteries and the ascending aorta, respectively.1,2 CAE is generally defined as dilatation of a coronary segment to at least 1.5 times the diameter of the adjacent normal vessel, 3 whereas ATAA refers to pathological enlargement of the ascending thoracic aorta beyond the expected normal range. 4 Both conditions are associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. CAE has increasingly been recognized as a high-risk coronary phenotype associated with impaired coronary flow, thrombosis, myocardial ischemia, and adverse cardiac events, 5 whereas ATAA may remain clinically silent for years but carries a substantial risk of dissection, rupture, and other life-threatening complications once progressive aortic enlargement occurs. 6

The pathogenesis of CAE and ATAA is complex and not yet fully understood. Traditionally, CAE has often been considered a variant of atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, 7 whereas ATAA has been more commonly linked to medial degeneration, genetic susceptibility, bicuspid aortic valve, hypertension, and altered aortic wall biomechanics.8–10 However, accumulating evidence suggests that these two disorders may share several important pathophysiological features, including chronic vascular inflammation, extracellular matrix degradation, smooth muscle cell dysfunction or loss, and progressive weakening of the vessel wall.11,12 Matrix metalloproteinase activation and imbalance between matrix degradation and repair have also been implicated in both coronary and aortic dilative remodeling.13,14

Recent studies have explored clinical risk stratification tools for CAE. Kilic et al. reported that the Intermountain Risk Score may help identify patients at risk of CAE. 15 Among the candidate mechanisms linking these disorders, dysregulated lipid metabolism is of particular interest. Lipid abnormalities are well-established contributors to atherosclerotic occlusive disease, but their pathogenic role may extend beyond plaque formation and luminal stenosis. 16 Experimental studies have shown that hyperlipidemia can accelerate vascular aging and endothelial dysfunction even before overt atherosclerosis becomes apparent. 17 In addition, fluctuating lipid exposure has been reported to induce endothelial inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired repair capacity, and apoptosis. 18 These findings suggest that lipid metabolic disturbances may promote vascular injury not only through atherogenesis.

The potential role of lipid dysregulation in CAE has attracted increasing attention. Although CAE shares certain risk factors with conventional coronary artery disease, its metabolic profile may not be identical to that of obstructive atherosclerosis. 19 Previous clinical studies have suggested associations between CAE and hyperlipidemia, inflammatory activity, and other markers of metabolic disturbance, 20 but the available evidence remains limited and sometimes inconsistent. In our previous work, we found that elevated triglyceride (TG) levels were significantly associated with CAE, suggesting that abnormalities in lipid metabolism may contribute to its pathogenesis. 21

Compared with CAE, the relationship between lipid metabolism and ATAA has been even less clearly defined. Established risk factors for ATAA include aging, male sex, hypertension, inherited predisposition, and structural abnormalities such as bicuspid aortic valve. 22 Nevertheless, emerging metabolomic studies have suggested that metabolic alterations, particularly in lipid-related pathways, may also participate in thoracic aortic aneurysm formation and progression. Preliminary evidence has implicated disturbances in sphingolipid metabolism and other metabolic networks in aneurysmal aortic tissue. 23 However, current evidence remains sparse, particularly in large clinical populations, and the relevance of routine circulating lipid parameters to ascending aortic dilatation has not been adequately characterized.

Taken together, these observations raise the possibility that CAE and ATAA may share common lipid-related metabolic features as part of a broader process of dilative vascular remodeling. However, this hypothesis has not been systematically examined. Despite possible overlap in vascular remodeling pathways, it remains unknown whether CAE and ATAA share a common circulating lipid phenotype or represent biologically distinct dyslipidemic patterns. Accordingly, the present study was designed to investigate the associations of TG and other lipid parameters with CAE and ATAA in a larger clinical cohort. By comparing the lipid spectra among control subjects, patients with CAE, and patients with ascending aortic dilatation, we aimed to better characterize the lipid-related metabolic features of these two vascular dilative diseases and to explore whether they share common or distinct patterns of dyslipidemia.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective single-center case-control study was conducted at Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University. Because this was a retrospective single-center case-control study, the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients available during the study period. An additional sample size estimation was performed using the formula for comparing two independent proportions:

where p1 and p0 represent the expected exposure proportions in the case and control groups, respectively, and pˉ=(p1+p0)/2 . With a two-sided α=0.05 , 80% power, p1=0.50 , and p0=0.40 , the required sample size was approximately 388 participants per group. The final sample size of 490 participants per group exceeded this requirement for the primary comparison.

No formal frequency matching by age, sex, or other baseline variables was performed. Instead, an equal number of subjects was selected for each group from the same study period and clinical setting, and potential confounding due to baseline differences was addressed using multivariable regression models. Adult patients evaluated between January 2016 and December 2025 were screened for eligibility. All consecutive patients with coronary artery ectasia (CAE) or ascending thoracic aortic aneurysm (ATAA) who met the predefined diagnostic criteria during the study period were included. A total of 490 patients with CAE and 490 patients with ATAA were consecutively enrolled. Patients with documented prior aortic surgery or acute aortic dissection in the available medical records were excluded. The control group consisted of 490 individuals who presented with chest pain or chest discomfort, underwent coronary angiography that excluded coronary artery disease, and had transthoracic echocardiography excluding ATAA. Patients with missing lipid-related parameters, missing coronary angiography data, or missing other key clinical variables were excluded, as shown in the study flowchart. Complete-case analysis was performed. Because fasting lipid profiles were routinely measured in patients undergoing coronary angiography or CTA in our clinical setting, the proportion of missing lipid-related data was relatively low, and no imputation was applied. After screening, 490 participants were included in each group, resulting in a total study population of 1,470 subjects. The reporting of this study conforms to the STROBE guidelines. 24

Diagnostic criteria

CAE was defined as diffuse or focal dilatation of a coronary artery segment with a diameter at least 1.5 times that of the adjacent normal reference segment on coronary angiography. 25 ATAA was defined as localized dilatation of the ascending aorta with a maximal diameter exceeding 1.5 times the expected normal diameter. Diagnosis of ATAA was diagnosed using clinically available imaging modalities, including echocardiography, CT, CTA, and MRI, according to routine institutional protocols. 26 When multiple examinations were available, the report with the most complete visualization and measurement of the ascending aorta was used. Nevertheless, intermodality differences in measurement plane, edge definition, and imaging conditions may have introduced diagnostic heterogeneity, and future prospective studies using standardized imaging protocols are needed. Control subjects were individuals with angiographically normal coronary arteries and without evidence of CAE or thoracic aortic aneurysmal disease. Coronary angiograms and aortic imaging studies were reviewed by experienced cardiologists and radiologists according to predefined diagnostic criteria.

Data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were extracted from the electronic medical record system, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, history of hypertension, history of diabetes mellitus, history of chronic kidney disease (CKD), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Lipid parameters included triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)]. Clinical and laboratory data were obtained from routine assessments performed during hospitalization or diagnostic evaluation. Fasting venous blood samples were collected from all participants on the morning of the second day after admission as part of routine comprehensive biochemical testing.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were summarized as number (percentage). Differences among the control, CAE, and ATAA groups were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with multiple-comparison adjustment.

Scatter plots with fitted regression lines were used to visualize the relationships between age and major lipid parameters across the three groups. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess correlations between TG and major clinical covariates.

Binary logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between lipid parameters and disease status, with the control group as the reference category. For continuous analyses, lipid parameters were standardized as z scores, and ORs were estimated per 1-standard deviation increase. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated separately for CAE versus control and ATAA versus control. For the analyses of lipid parameters, three models were constructed: a crude model with no adjustment; a partially adjusted model adjusted for age and sex; and a fully adjusted model adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, CKD, SBP, DBP, FPG, and eGFR. Lipid parameters were analyzed as continuous variables. Because of collinearity, particularly between TC and LDL-C, each lipid parameter was entered separately into multivariable models. Because multiple lipid parameters and disease comparisons were evaluated, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the primary logistic regression analyses. The fully adjusted model was considered the primary model for statistical inference, whereas the crude and partially adjusted models were presented to show the influence of covariate adjustment and the consistency of associations across different adjustment levels. Therefore, FDR correction was applied to the 10 primary comparisons derived from the fully adjusted models, comprising five lipid parameters across two disease comparisons.

To further evaluate dose-response associations, TG was additionally categorized into quartiles according to its distribution in the overall study population, with the lowest quartile (Q1) used as the reference group. ORs and 95% CIs were calculated for Q2, Q3, and Q4 relative to Q1. Tests for trend across TG quartiles were performed by entering the quartile variable as an ordinal term in the regression models.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses based on the fully adjusted model were conducted to explore potential non-linear associations between TG levels and the odds of CAE or ATAA. Knots were placed at prespecified percentiles of the TG distribution, and the median TG level or the reference TG value was used as the reference point. A Wald test was used to assess overall association and non-linearity.

Subgroup analyses were performed using the fully adjusted model according to age, sex, BMI, smoking status, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus. Stratified ORs and 95% CIs were estimated within each subgroup. Interaction analyses were additionally performed by including cross-product terms between TG and subgroup variables in the regression models, where appropriate.

All tests were two-sided, and a p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (approval No.2023ZDSYLL264-P01). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongda Hospital, which is affiliated with Southeast University No:2023ZDSYLL264−P01 . The ethics approval was granted on August 9, 2023. As this was a retrospective case-control study, the ethics approval document specified a validity period of three years. Therefore, the data included in the present study, all of which were collected before December 2025, were covered by the approved ethics period. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in 2024. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. All patient details were de-identified prior to data analysis to ensure that individual patients could not be identified in any way.

Comparison of lipid parameters across groups

Given the study focus on lipid metabolism, we further compared lipid-related parameters across the three groups. To better characterize between-group differences, pairwise comparisons were additionally performed.

As shown in Table 1 and Figure 2, unadjusted lipid parameters differed significantly across the three groups. TG was highest in the CAE group [1.44 (1.07-1.99) mmol/L], followed by controls [1.21 (0.92-1.75) mmol/L], and lowest in the ATAA group [1.13 (0.94-1.76) mmol/L]. TC and LDL-C were lowest in the ATAA group, while HDL-C was lowest in the CAE group. Lp(a) was higher in the CAE and ATAA groups than in controls.

Lipid profiles differed among the three groups. Boxplots with individual data points show TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, and Lp(a) levels in the Control, CAE, and ATAA groups; black diamonds indicate mean ± SE, and p values were calculated using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Correlations among TG, lipid parameters, and major clinical covariates

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to further characterize the relationships among TG, other lipid parameters, and major clinical covariates (Figure 3). TG was negatively correlated with HDL-C (r=-0.39, p<0.001) and age (r=-0.20, p<0.001), whereas positive correlations were observed between TG and TC (r=0.30, p<0.001), BMI (r=0.21, p<0.001), and FPG (r=0.20, p<0.001). TG also showed weaker positive correlations with diabetes, hypertension, and LDL-C. Overall, the observed correlations were generally weak to moderate. Considering the interrelationships among lipid variables, each lipid parameter was subsequently analyzed in a separate multivariable logistic regression model to minimize potential collinearity.

Spearman correlation heatmap showing the associations of TG with demographic characteristics, clinical variables, and lipid parameters, with cell color indicating the correlation coefficient and statistical significance.

Collinearity assessment of lipid parameters

In the exploratory model including all lipid parameters simultaneously, substantial multicollinearity was observed for TC and LDL-C (VIF=18.23 and 14.60, respectively), whereas the remaining lipid variables and clinical covariates showed low VIF values (Table 2). In contrast, VIFs remained low in the fully adjusted models in which each lipid parameter was entered separately (Supplementary Table 1). These findings supported the use of separate multivariable logistic regression models for individual lipid parameters.

Multivariable-adjusted associations of lipid parameters with CAE and ATAA

To further account for potential confounding by age and other baseline variables, crude, age-adjusted, and fully adjusted regression models were fitted for each lipid parameter as a continuous variable. Continuous lipid variables were standardized to per 1-standard deviation increase before logistic regression analysis (Figure 4). Based on the observed interrelationships among lipid variables, each lipid parameter was entered separately into multivariable logistic regression models to evaluate its association with CAE and ATAA.

Lipid markers showed distinct associations with CAE and ATAA across regression models. Forest plots show odds ratios per 1-SD increase in TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, and Lp(a) for CAE vs Control and ATAA vs Control in crude, partially adjusted, and fully adjusted models; points indicate odds ratios, horizontal bars indicate 95% confidence intervals, and the dashed vertical line indicates an odds ratio of 1.0.

In the fully adjusted model, CAE remained significantly associated with higher TG levels (OR=1.631, 95%CI:1.341-1.913, p<0.001; FDR-adjusted p=0.002)and lower HDL-C levels (OR=0.586, 95%CI:0.486-0.701, p<0.001; FDR-adjusted p=0.002),and both associations remained statistically significant after FDR correction. Higher Lp(a) levels showed a nominal association with CAE (OR=1.190, 95%CI:1.012-1403, p=0.037; FDR-adjusted p=0.053), however, this association did not remain statistically significant after FDR correction and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. By contrast, the associations of TC and LDL-C were no longer statistically associated with CAE after multivariable adjustment (Tables 3 and 4). In the comparison of ATAA versus controls, lower TC (OR=0.439, 95%CI:0.345-0.552, p<0.001; FDR-adjusted p=0.002), lower HDL-C (OR=0.503, 95%CI:0.398-0.630, p<0.001; FDR-adjusted p=0.002), and lower LDL-C (OR=0.539, 95%CI:0.433-0.667, p<0.001; FDR-adjusted p=0.002) and higher Lp(a) levels (OR=1.421, 95%CI:1.139-1.784, p=0.002; FDR-adjusted p=0.003) remained significantly associated with ATAA after FDR correction. However, TG was not significantly associated with ATAA after full adjustment (Tables 3 and 4).

Non-linear association between TG, CAE and ATAA

We further used RCS analysis to explore the potential dose-response relationship between TG and the odds of CAE (Figure 5). TG was significantly associated with the odds of CAE (overall p<0.001), and the association was non-linear (non-linear p=0.031). The odds ratio for CAE increased with increasing TG levels, with a more pronounced increase at higher TG concentrations (TG>1.31mmol/L).

RCS analyses revealed distinct nonlinear associations of TG withthe odds of CAE and ATAA. Solid lines indicate adjusted odds ratios, shaded areas represent 95% confidence intervals, horizontal dashed lines indicate an odds ratio of 1.0, and overall and non-linear p values together with the reference TG value are shown in each panel.

Furthermore, RCS analysis demonstrated a significant non-linear association between TG and ATAA (overall p<0.001; non-linear p<0.001) (Figure 5), with an inverted U-shapedpattern and wider confidence intervals at extreme TG levels, indicating greater uncertainty in these estimates.

Association of TG quartiles with odds of CAE and ATAA

To further characterize the relationship between TG and CAE odds, TG levels were divided into 4 quartiles and analyzed in fully adjusted logistic regression models using Q1 as the reference category (Figure 6).

Increasing TG quartiles were associated with higher odds of CAE but not ATAA. Forest plots show adjusted ORs and 95% CIs for ATAA vs Control and CAE vs Control across TG quartiles using Q1 as the reference; the dashed line indicates OR=1.0, and trend p values are shown.

For CAE, the adjusted ORs were 1.85 (95%CI: 1.21-2.85) for Q2, 2.00 (95% CI: 1.31-3.08) for Q3, and 2.43 (95% CI: 1.69-3.69) for Q4, compared with Q1. A graded increase in the odds of CAE was observed across increasing TG quartiles, and the trend test was statistically significant (p for trend <0.001). For ATAA, the association pattern differed from that observed for CAE. Relative to Q1, the adjusted ORs were 1.36 (95% CI: 0.88-2.11) for Q2, 1.76 (95% CI: 1.14-2.71) for Q3, and 0.86 (95% CI: 0.54-1.37) for Q4. The odds ratios were higher in the middle quartiles, while no further increase was observed in Q4, suggesting a non-monotonic association (p for trend=0.86) (Table 5). Overall, these quartile-based results were in line with the restricted cubic spline analyses.

Subgroup analyses

We further conducted subgroup analyses stratified by smoking status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, BMI, sex, and age to evaluate the robustness of the association between TG and the odds of CAE. Overall, the direction of the associations remained largely consistent across subgroups. The TG–CAE association appeared generally consistent across subgroups., For ATAA, potential interactions were observed for sex and BMI(Figure 7). In the CAE group, the association between TG and CAE remained broadly consistent across subgroups, with no evidence of significant effect modification by any of the stratification factors (all p for interaction>0.05). In contrast, in the ATAA group, significant interactions were observed for BMI (p for interaction=0.009) and sex (p for interaction=0.017) suggesting that the association between TG and ATAA differed according to BMI category and sex. No significant interaction was found for smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, or age. However, given the exploratory nature of these analyses and the multiple interaction tests performed, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Lesion distribution and TG levels in patients with CAE

We further analyzed the relationship between TG levels and CAE lesion distribution. Discussion

In this retrospective observational study, we compared lipid profiles among patients with CAE, patients with ATAA, and angiographically normal controls, and further evaluated the associations of TG and other conventional lipid parameters with these two vascular dilative disorders. Several key findings emerged. First, CAE was characterized by higher TG and lower HDL-C, and the association between TG and CAE remained significant after multivariable adjustment. Second, the relationship between TG and CAE appeared non-linear and dose-dependent, with the odds increasing more noticeably at higher TG levels. Third, compared with CAE, ATAA showed a different lipid profile, with lower TC, LDL-C, HDL-C. and higher Lp(a) levels being independently associated with disease status, whereas the association between TG and ATAA was weaker and more heterogeneous. Finally, subgroup analyses suggested that the TG–CAE association was relatively stable, whereas the TG–ATAA association varied more across patient subgroups. These findings indicate distinct lipid-related metabolic signatures in CAE and ATAA, with TG being more relevant to CAE.

Given the retrospective case-control design, our findings indicate associations rather than causality. Although elevated TG levels were independently associated with CAE, whether TG directly contributes to CAE development or reflects related metabolic abnormalities or residual confounding remains unclear. Therefore, biological explanations involving endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, or vascular remodeling should be considered hypothesis-generating, and prospective mechanistic studies are needed. Although CAE has traditionally been viewed as a variant of coronary atherosclerotic disease, it may also represent a distinct vascular phenotype involving expansive remodeling and chronic inflammatory injury.27,28 Against this background, our findings support the possibility that TG-related metabolic disturbance may participate in the pathophysiology of CAE rather than simply serving as a marker of an unfavorable cardiometabolic profile. Similarly, a recent study in NSTEMI patients showed that the atherogenic index of plasma, a TG/HDL-C-derived marker, was independently associated with critical multivessel coronary artery disease. 15 Although conducted in a different clinical context, this finding supports our observation that TG-related lipid metabolism may be involved in CAE. Future studies should assess the value of AIP in CAE risk stratification.

Although the present study cannot establish causality, several potential mechanisms may help explain the observed association between TG and CAE. TG-rich lipoproteins and remnant particles can promote endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress and vascular inflammation which may contribute to vascular wall injury and remodeling.28–30 These processes may also interact with extracellular matrix remodeling relevant to coronary ectatic change. 31 The concomitant pattern of elevated TG and reduced HDL-C observed in the CAE group may therefore reflect a broader pro-atherogenic dyslipidemic profile. 32 In addition, the independent association between higher Lp(a) and CAE may further support the involvement of lipid-related inflammatory and vascular remodeling pathways in coronary arterial dilatation.33,34

Our findings should be viewed in light of contemporary evidence on triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and ApoB-containing particles. Plasma TG may partly reflect cholesterol-enriched remnant particles and ApoB-containing particle burden, which have been implicated in endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, oxidative stress, and arterial wall lipid accumulation.35,36 Thus, the association between TG and the odds of CAE may represent a broader adverse lipid-metabolic phenotype rather than an effect of TG alone. However, ApoB, remnant cholesterol, particle number, and lipidomic species were not measured; therefore, these findings remain hypothesis-generating. Future studies integrating advanced lipid profiling and genetic approaches are needed to clarify the biological relevance of TG-related pathways in CAE and ATAA.

By contrast, ATAA showed a lipid pattern distinct from that of CAE, with lower TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C remaining independently associated, whereas the association with TG was weaker, non-linear, and more heterogeneous. This difference suggests that the biological background of ATAA may not be adequately explained by conventional atherosclerotic paradigms alone. Rather, ATAA is frequently linked to aortic valve abnormalities, chronic hemodynamic stress, and age-related degeneration of the aortic wall.37–39 In this setting, circulating lipid concentrations may reflect systemic condition, comorbidity burden, or complex vascular remodeling responses, which maypartly explain why lower rather than higher concentrations of several conventional lipid parameters were associated with ATAA in our cohort.

The contrast between CAE and ATAA is an important implication of the present study. In CAE, the association with TG was stronger, more consistent, whereas lipid associations in ATAA were less straightforward and more dependent on patient context. These differences likely reflect the distinct biological properties of coronary arteries and the ascending aorta. Accordingly, CAE and ATAA should not be viewed as metabolically equivalent manifestations of a single dilative vasculopathy. These observations may have both clinical and research implications. From a clinical perspective, elevated TG, especially when accompanied by low HDL-C and elevated Lp(a), may reflect an adverse lipid-metabolic profile in patients with CAE. However, because the present study did not assess discrimination, calibration, net reclassification improvement, or decision-curve analysis, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that TG has established diagnostic or prognostic utility as a biomarker for CAE. In contrast, lipid abnormalities in ATAA may require more cautious interpretation and should be understood within a broader structural and hemodynamic context. From a research perspective, our findings support a phenotype-specific approach to lipid metabolism in vascular dilative disease and suggest that analytical strategies restricted to simple linear assumptions may overlook clinically relevant associations, particularly in heterogeneous conditions such as ATAA. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, The retrospective observational case-control design precludes causal inference; therefore, our findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal relationships. Controls were symptomatic patients with angiographically normal coronary arteries rather than healthy community-based individuals, which may limit generalizability to asymptomatic healthy populations. However, given the invasive nature of coronary angiography, this represents a practical control group for an angiography-based study. Second, lipid measurements were based on a single time point, without information on longitudinal exposure or variability. Repeated lipid measurements were unavailable for most patients, and longitudinal lipid changes could not be evaluated. Therefore, exposure misclassification and regression dilution bias cannot be excluded. Third, residual confounding may remain due to unmeasured or incompletely measured factors, including medication use, inflammatory status, nutritional factors, and genetic background. In addition, the complete-case analysis may have introduced selection bias related to missing data. Fourth, Detailed ATAA characteristics, including maximum aortic diameter, aneurysm severity, valve morphology, etiology, and surgical indications, were not systematically available due to the retrospective design and heterogeneous diagnostic modalities. Therefore, subgroup analyses according to aneurysm phenotype could not be reliably performed. Future prospective studies with standardized imaging and complete phenotypic characterization are warranted. In addition, lipid variables were modeled separately to reduce multicollinearity, so their joint effects could not be fully evaluated. 20 Finally, the lack of reliable information on lipid-lowering medication use is another important limitation. Because of the retrospective design and long study period, prior lipid profiles, medication type, treatment duration, adherence, and regularity of lipid-lowering therapy were not consistently documented. As statins and other lipid-modifying therapies directly affect lipid levels, residual confounding from medication exposure cannot be excluded. Future prospective studies should systematically collect and adjust for lipid-lowering therapy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CAE and ATAA, despite both being vascular dilative disorders, showed distinct lipid-related profiles. Elevated TG, together with lower HDL-C and higher Lp(a), was independently associated with CAE and exhibited a dose-response relationship. In contrast, ATAA was more strongly associated with lower TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C, whereas its relationship with TG was weaker, non-linear, and heterogeneous. These findings suggest that lipid dysregulation may contribute differently to coronary and aortic dilative remodeling, with TG being of particular relevance in CAE. Further prospective and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify these relationships and their potential clinical significance.
