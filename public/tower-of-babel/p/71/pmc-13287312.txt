Preoperative Pulmonary Function and Postoperative Pulmonary Complications in Noncardiac Surgery: A Propensity Score–Weighted Analysis
Authors: Sung Ae Cho, Hee Eun Kim, Moon Ok Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPostoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) increase surgical morbidity and mortality. While the predictive value of preoperative pulmonary function tests (PFTs) in noncardiac surgery remains unclear, we evaluated whether abnormal PFT patterns and spirometric indices are independently associated with PPCs.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed adult patients undergoing noncardiac surgery under general or spinal anesthesia between June and November 2022 with preoperative spirometry. PFTs were classified as normal or abnormal using lower limit of normal criteria. PPCs within 7 postoperative days were defined using European Perioperative Clinical Outcome criteria. Associations were assessed using multivariable logistic regression before and after propensity score–based overlap weighting. Continuous spirometric indices were also analyzed.ResultsAmong 729 patients, 64 (8.8%) had abnormal PFTs. PPCs were significantly more frequent in the abnormal PFTs group (29.7% vs. 8.7%; p < 0.001). After overlap weighting, abnormal PFTs remained independently associated with PPCs (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 4.05, 95% CI 1.72–9.53; p < 0.001), along with open surgery (aOR 8.94, 95% CI 2.38–33.59; p = 0.001). Lower FEV1 and %FEV1 were consistently associated with increased PPC risk across all models, whereas associations with FVC and FEV1/FVC were inconsistent.ConclusionsAbnormal preoperative PFTs and reduced FEV1 are independently associated with PPCs after noncardiac surgery. Preoperative spirometry aids perioperative risk stratification, particularly for invasive surgery.

Introduction

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are among the most common and clinically important adverse events after surgery. PPCs are associated with prolonged hospital stay, increased need for intensive care, higher healthcare costs, and substantial perioperative morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Because of this clinical impact, accurate identification of patients at high risk for PPCs is a major goal in perioperative care.

Preoperative risk stratification usually considers demographic factors such as age and sex, lifestyle factors such as smoking, and comorbid conditions including cardiovascular and respiratory disease [2, 3, 4]. In addition, the invasiveness of surgery and the type and duration of anesthesia are well‐known determinants of pulmonary risk [1, 3, 5]. However, although spirometry is widely available, the role of preoperative pulmonary function tests (PFTs) in predicting PPCs has not been clearly defined [6, 7, 8, 9]. Several studies in thoracic surgery have reported that impaired lung function, especially reduced forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), is associated with an increased incidence of PPCs, but findings in more general surgical populations have been inconsistent [6, 7, 9, 10, 11].

Current guidelines do not recommend routine PFTs for all patients undergoing noncardiac surgery, and clinicians often rely on clinical judgment to decide when spirometry is needed [12, 13]. As a result, the potential value of PFTs as an objective and quantitative tool for perioperative risk assessment may not be fully realized. In particular, it is unclear whether an abnormal PFT pattern defined by the lower limit of normal (LLN) provides additional risk information beyond traditional clinical variables and surgical invasiveness, and whether individual spirometric indices such as FEV1 or forced vital capacity (FVC) show a graded association with PPCs in a general surgical cohort [14]. Therefore, this study aimed to identify independent predictors of PPCs, focusing on preoperative PFT patterns and spirometric indices in the patients undergoing noncardiac surgery.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board. The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. We reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent surgery between June and November 2022 at our hospital. Among these, patients who had preoperative spirometry as part of their preoperative assessment were included in the analysis. We excluded cases involving emergency surgery, cardiac surgery, age < 18 years, intraoperative cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), surgeries performed under anesthesia other than general or spinal anesthesia, and cases with incomplete or duplicate medical records.

Preoperative PFTs

In our institution, anesthesiologists recommend preoperative PFTs for elderly patients (≥ 70 years) and for those with chronic respiratory disease. However, the final decision to perform PFTs is made by the surgeon. Preoperative PFTs were performed in the pulmonary function laboratory according to standard guidelines [15]. Spirometric measurements included FVC, FEV1, and the FEV1/FVC ratio. Predicted values for FVC and FEV1 were calculated using reference equations appropriate for age, sex, and body size, and were expressed as percent predicted (%FVC and %FEV1). PFT results were classified as normal or abnormal based on the LLN for FEV1, FVC, and the FEV1/FVC ratio [14]. Patients whose spirometric values were within the LLN were assigned to the normal PFT group, and those who fell below the LLN for any of these indices were assigned to the abnormal PFT group.

Variables and Data Collection

Potential predictors of PPCs were selected based on previous studies [1, 2, 3, 4]. Baseline demographic and clinical variables were collected from the electronic medical record, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, underlying chronic diseases, history of malignancy, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, hemoglobin, serum albumin, and preoperative PFT results. Smoking status was categorized as non‐smoker, ex‐smoker (stopped ≥ 8 weeks before surgery), or current smoker. Alcohol consumption was recorded as a binary variable (yes or no), regardless of the type or amount of alcohol. Underlying chronic diseases included chronic lung disease, heart disease, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus. ASA physical status was recorded by the attending anesthesiologist at the time of surgery. Anemia was defined as hemoglobin < 10 g/dL, and hypoalbuminemia was defined as serum albumin < 3.5 g/dL.

Operative variables included type of anesthesia (general or spinal), duration of anesthesia, surgical site (thoracic, abdominal, or other), surgical approach (open surgery, minimally invasive surgery as laparoscopic or video‐assisted thoracic surgery [VATS] for thoracic/abdominal procedures, or others), use of intravenous patient‐controlled analgesia (IV‐PCA), and type of neuromuscular blockade reversal agent (anticholinesterase or sugammadex) [16, 17, 18, 19].

Outcomes

PPCs were defined as in‐hospital postoperative respiratory events occurring within the first 7 postoperative days. According to the European Perioperative Clinical Outcome (EPCO) definitions, PPCs included respiratory infection, respiratory failure, pleural effusion, atelectasis, pneumothorax, bronchospasm, aspiration pneumonitis, and pneumonia [20]. PPCs were identified through review of progress notes, discharge summaries, radiology reports, and consultation notes. Events classified as PPCs followed institutional criteria and included only pulmonary complications documented as clinically significant by the treating physicians. If multiple PPCs occurred in a single patient, the patient was counted once for the primary outcome. Mechanical ventilation for non‐respiratory reasons, such as planned postoperative ventilation or ventilation initiated for hemodynamic instability rather than respiratory failure, was not classified as a PPC.

The primary outcome was the occurrence of PPCs. We also evaluated independent predictors of PPCs. In addition, spirometric variables including FVC, %FVC, FEV1, %FEV1, and the FEV1/FVC ratio were analyzed as continuous predictors.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized separately for the normal and abnormal PFT groups. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Between‐group comparisons were performed using the independent t‐test for continuous variables and Pearson's χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

To adjust for baseline differences between the normal and abnormal PFT groups, we applied propensity score–based overlap weighting. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, comorbidities, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, malignancy, and ASA physical status. The balance after applying overlap weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences. Unlike traditional propensity score matching, overlap weighting retains all patients while emphasizing patients with substantial covariate overlap between groups, thereby improving covariate balance and statistical efficiency.

Associations between PFT patterns and PPCs were evaluated using univariate and multivariable logistic regression in both the original cohort and the overlap‐weighted cohort. Variables that were significant in univariate analyses were included in the multivariable model. Spirometric indices (FVC, %FVC, FEV1, %FEV1, and FEV1/FVC) were also analyzed as continuous variables to assess their linear relationship with PPCs. To evaluate the robustness of the findings, a secondary analysis using 1:4 propensity score matching was performed.

All analyses were conducted using Stata 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 3940 surgical cases were recorded, and 936 patients underwent preoperative PFTs. After excluding 207 patients who met the exclusion criteria, 729 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Of these, 665 patients (91.2%) had normal PFTs according to the LLN criteria, and 64 patients (8.8%) were classified as having abnormal PFTs.

Flow diagram of patient selection.

Baseline characteristics of the normal and abnormal PFT groups are presented in Tables 1A and 1B. Patients in the abnormal PFT group were older and had higher rates of chronic underlying diseases and malignancy. Anemia and hypoalbuminemia were also more frequent in the abnormal group. Regarding surgical variables, the proportion of open surgery did not differ significantly between groups, but the distribution of neuromuscular reversal agents also differed between groups. The incidence of PPCs was 29.7% in patients with abnormal PFTs and 8.7% in those with normal PFTs, showing a significant difference between the two groups.

In the overlap‐weighted cohort, univariate analyses again identified abnormal PFT, ex‐smoker status, use of IV‐PCA, open surgery, and lower FEV1 and %FEV1 as significant predictors of PPCs. In the overlap‐weighted multivariable analysis, open surgery (aOR 8.94, 95% CI 2.38–33.59; p = 0.001) and abnormal PFT pattern (aOR 4.05, 95% CI 1.72–9.53; p < 0.001) remained independent risk factors for PPCs (Table 2).

The analysis of continuous spirometric indices is shown in Table 3. Decreases in FEV1 and %FEV1 were consistently associated with an increased risk of PPCs in all three settings: before weighting, after overlap weighting, and after propensity score matching. The ORs for FEV1 were 0.35 (95% CI 0.20–0.61; p < 0.001) in the original cohort, 0.47 (95% CI 0.28–0.79; p = 0.004) after overlap weighting, and 0.45 (95% CI 0.26–0.77; p = 0.004) after matching, all of which were statistically significant (Table 3). In contrast, FVC and FEV1/FVC showed less consistent associations across analytic steps and were not significantly related to PPCs.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 729 adult patients undergoing surgery under general or spinal anesthesia, we evaluated the association between preoperative spirometry and PPCs. Overall, 8.8% of patients had an abnormal PFT pattern, and the incidence of PPCs was higher in the abnormal PFT group than in the normal group. In the overlap‐weighted multivariable analysis, open surgery was the strongest predictor of PPCs, and an abnormal PFT pattern remained an independent risk factor.

These findings suggest that not only surgical invasiveness but also the patient's underlying respiratory reserve may be related to the risk of PPCs. Recent studies have also shown that oxygen reserve indicators, such as the Oxygen Reserve Index (ORI), may be related to perioperative hypoxemia and pulmonary complications. This suggests that reduced respiratory reserve could influence the risk of PPCs through several different mechanisms [21]. The association between open or prolonged surgery and PPCs has been consistently reported in previous studies [1, 2, 3, 5]. In our study, impaired preoperative lung function continued to add relevant information even after adjustment for these surgical factors. Reduced pulmonary function may influence the ability to tolerate perioperative stressors such as atelectasis, secretion retention, and hypoventilation [21].

This study also applied LLN‐based criteria to classify PFTs, which may be more practical in clinical settings. While many previous studies evaluated spirometric values only as continuous measures, LLN provides a simple cut‐off that accounts for age, sex, and body size [6, 7, 8, 9]. In a heterogeneous surgical population, such standardized definitions may help clinicians judge whether lung function falls within the expected range [14, 22]. Although LLN‐based spirometric criteria are frequently used to distinguish obstructive and restrictive patterns, we used a binary classification of normal versus abnormal PFTs because the number of patients with abnormal findings was relatively small, limiting the statistical stability of subgroup analyses. In addition to the LLN‐based categorical analysis, our continuous analyses of spirometric indices showed similar patterns. Lower FEV1 and %FEV1 were consistently associated with a higher risk of PPCs, suggesting that even modest reductions in ventilatory function may be clinically relevant. These results indicate that assessing the extent of functional decline across the full spirometric range may offer additional clinical insight beyond a binary classification. The association between FEV1 and PPCs is in line with previous studies in thoracic surgery patients, and our study extends this relationship to a broader surgical population [10, 11].

One useful aspect of this study is the use of overlap weighting to reduce baseline imbalances between groups. Retrospective studies are inherently vulnerable to confounding, but overlap weighting allowed us to adjust for differences in key clinical variables between the normal and abnormal PFT groups. The persistence of abnormal PFT as an independent predictor after weighting supports the robustness of the findings. Furthermore, by analyzing both PFT patterns (categorical) and FEV1/%FEV1 (continuous), we were able to capture different aspects of how lung function relates to PPC risk.

From a clinical perspective, our results suggest that preoperative PFT results may have a useful role in perioperative risk assessment. In particular, patients with abnormal PFTs who are scheduled for open surgery or long procedures may be at especially high risk. Previous studies have reported potential benefits of preoperative interventions such as smoking cessation, optimization of reversible airway disease, and preoperative respiratory rehabilitation [23, 24]. Lung‐protective ventilation strategies and appropriate fluid management during surgery, as well as postoperative respiratory physiotherapy and early mobilization, have also been recommended to help reduce PPC risk [23, 25]. Patients with abnormal PFTs undergoing open surgery may therefore be reasonable candidates for closer monitoring or targeted preventive strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutions or healthcare systems. Second, although we used overlap weighting to adjust for measured confounders, residual confounding due to unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Third, a wide variety of surgical procedures were included, and we were not able to perform detailed subgroup analyses by specific surgery type.

Despite these limitations, this study provides evidence that preoperative impairment of lung function, especially an abnormal PFT pattern and reduced FEV1, is associated with an increased risk of PPCs, independent of traditional clinical variables and surgical invasiveness. These findings support the idea that objective pulmonary function assessment may be a useful component of perioperative risk evaluation. Future prospective, multicenter studies are needed to establish optimal spirometric thresholds and to define how best to integrate PFT results into clinical practice.

In conclusion, surgical invasiveness was the factor most strongly associated with PPCs, with open surgery showing the highest risk. In addition, an abnormal PFT pattern and reduced FEV1 were significantly related to the occurrence of PPCs, suggesting that preoperative lung function is an important factor to consider in perioperative risk assessment. These results imply that patients with impaired lung function who are scheduled to undergo invasive surgery may require closer evaluation and more careful perioperative management.
