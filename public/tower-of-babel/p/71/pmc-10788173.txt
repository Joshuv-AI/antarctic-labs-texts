Safety and pharmacokinetics of thalidomide in tumor-bearing dogs
Authors: Fukiko MATSUYAMA, Yukiyoshi FUJITA, Eri FUKAZAWA, Tetsuya KOBAYASHI
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThalidomide, an angiogenesis inhibitor, has recently been used to treat malignant canine tumors. This study retrospectively investigated the adverse events (AEs) of thalidomide administered to tumor-bearing dogs. We investigated the pharmacokinetics of thalidomide after administration and the rate of body weight change before and after administration. The initial thalidomide dose was 5 mg/kg per os once daily, which was increased to 10 mg/kg once daily in dogs with no significant AEs. Pharmacokinetics were measured in four dogs after the 5 mg/kg or 10 mg/kg dose. We evaluated AEs related to clinical signs in 51 patients; 9/51 had lethargy, 6/51 had tremor, 4/51 had dizziness, 31/51 had decreased appetite, 8/51 had vomiting, and 16/49 had soft stool/diarrhea. We evaluated hematologic toxicity in 44 patients with grade 3 or higher adverse events; 1/44 had thrombocytopenia, 1/44 had increased blood urea nitrogen concentrations, and 5/44 had increased alanine aminotransferase activities. The mean thalidomide blood levels were Cmax=1.4 ± 0.7 μg/mL (Area under the curve [AUC]0–24=8.5 ± 4.7 μg•hr /mL) and Cmax=3.2 ± 2.1 μg/mL (AUC0–24=22.0 ± 14.7 μg•hr/mL) in the 5 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg groups, respectively. The Cmax and AUC in the 10 mg/kg group were comparable to the effective blood concentrations seen in humans administered thalidomide. The weight fluctuation rates were assessed in 24 dogs approximately 1 month after the start of thalidomide administration; more than half showed weight maintenance or gain. Most AEs were clinically acceptable; however, peripheral nerve signs were seen in some dogs.Keywords: adverse event, malignant tumor, pharmacokinetics, thalidomide

Thalidomide (alpha-phthalidoglutarimide) was marketed in more than 40 countries as a sedative in the late 1950s and prescribed to pregnant women with morning sickness symptoms from 1957 to 1961 [19]. However, newborns born to mothers who had taken thalidomide often developed deformities of the limbs, ears, heart, and gastrointestinal tract [13, 14]. These teratogenic properties led to the withdrawal of thalidomide from the global markets in 1961. However, thalidomide was remarketed in 1965 due to its remarkable effectiveness against lepromatous erythema nodosum, an acute inflammatory reaction of leprosy [10, 29, 34].

Thirty years later, scientists observed that thalidomide inhibited cachexia-inducing tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α in stimulated human monocytes [28], and angiogenesis in rabbit corneas and chorioallantoic membranes of chickens, indicating the possible therapeutic use of this drug for malignant tumors [5, 12]. Then, in 2006, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the combination of thalidomide and dexamethasone for the treatment of multiple myeloma [30].

In dogs, thalidomide has been used as part of a multimodal treatment for hemangiosarcoma, mammary gland tumors, and lung tumors [2, 4, 25]. Thalidomide administered as adjuvant chemotherapy after splenectomy for splenic hemangiosarcoma may provide a survival benefit compared with using doxorubicin as an adjuvant [2], which is supported by the observation that the vascular endothelial growth factor produced by hemangiosarcoma tumor cells is reduced after thalidomide administration [1].

Adverse events associated with thalidomide, other than teratogenicity and lethargy, have been reported in humans, including neurologic effects, such as dizziness, tremor, and lip and hand numbness, and hematologic effects, such as neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia [7, 21, 28, 34]. In dogs, thalidomide has been reported as a well-tolerated drug, and when 43, 200, and 1,000 mg/kg were administered orally to healthy beagle dogs for 53 weeks, no deaths occurred [32]. The same study reported a “No Observed Adverse Effect Level” for thalidomide in dogs receiving 200 mg/kg/day. A retrospective study of 15 dogs with hemangiosarcoma also found no adverse events associated with thalidomide other than lethargy [2]. In contrast, in a study investigating the pharmacokinetics of thalidomide in experimental dogs, tremor and dizziness were observed after thalidomide administration [24], indicating that dogs can develop peripheral nerve signs similar to those observed in humans.

The main objective of this study was to retrospectively investigate adverse events after thalidomide administration in dogs with malignant tumors. In addition, pharmacokinetics after thalidomide administration and the rate of change in body weight before and after thalidomide administration were also investigated.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Adverse events associated with thalidomide.

Study animals

Dogs that were presented to the Japanese Small Animal Cancer Center between December 2004 and August 2010 and prescribed thalidomide for the treatment of malignant tumors were retrospectively studied. Malignancy was diagnosed by cytology or histopathology. Dogs that received at least one dose of thalidomide and had at least one visit after the dose were included in the study. Dogs with concomitant radiation therapy or cytotoxic anticancer agents and those with incomplete medical records were excluded.

Thalidomide treatment and follow-up

When thalidomide was prescribed, we explained that thalidomide is a teratogenic drug and obtained the owner’s written consent. When administering thalidomide, owners were instructed to wear gloves to prevent direct contact with the drug. The initial dose of thalidomide (Talizer®, Laboratories Serral, Ciudad de Mexico, Mexico) was 5 mg/kg per os once daily before bedtime. After several days of treatment, the dose was increased to 10 mg/kg in dogs with no apparent adverse events. Physical examination and blood tests were performed 1–2 weeks after the start of treatment, followed by regular monthly checkups.

Assessment of adverse events

Thalidomide adverse events were examined for the presence and severity of lethargy, tremor, dizziness, decreased appetite (anorexia), vomiting, diarrhea, and hematologic toxicity. Packed cell volumes (PCVs) and complete blood counts (CBCs) were measured to assess for hematologic toxicity. Serum chemistry panels were also measured to assess for liver and kidney toxicities. Adverse events were evaluated using the Veterinary Cooperative Oncology Group Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (VCOG-CTCAE v1.1) [22]. The timing of adverse events was subdivided into short-term (within 7 days after the start of treatment), medium-term (8–28 days), and long-term (29 days or later).

Rates of weight change after thalidomide administration

To compare the weight before and approximately one month after thalidomide administration, the percentage change in weight (weight after thalidomide administration/weight at the start of administration ×100) was calculated and analyzed. A Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used to compare body weights before and after thalidomide administration using STATA version 14.2 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). In addition, dogs were examined for changes in body weight, decreased appetite, gross lesions, tumor types, and concomitant medications.

Thalidomide pharmacokinetics study

Patient selection: Dogs that presented to the Japanese Small Animal Cancer Center between August 2011 and April 2013 and were prescribed thalidomide for the treatment of malignant tumors were included in the study. The pharmacokinetic study was approved by the hospital board, and written consent was obtained from the owners. The pharmacokinetics of a single oral dose of thalidomide (5 or 10 mg/kg) were analyzed. The first four patients were given 10 mg/kg thalidomide, and the next four were administered 5 mg/kg. Thalidomide dispensers wore gloves, masks, caps, and gowns, and the splitting was carried out in a biologic safety cabinet. Dogs were fasted for 2 hr before thalidomide administration and provided food 12 hr after administration. Dogs with a body condition score of 5/5, weighing less than 3 kg, or with gastrointestinal signs, such as diarrhea or vomiting, were excluded from the pharmacokinetic study. In addition, dogs with a history of be administered thalidomide and those with blood chemistry tests showing total protein, albumin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), T-bilirubin, creatinine, calcium, and glucose levels deviating from the reference values were excluded from the pharmacokinetic study. There were no restrictions on concomitant medications at the time of the survey.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

One milliliter of blood was drawn immediately before and 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 hr after thalidomide administration. However, since measurements taken from the third subject receiving the 10 mg/kg dose revealed that serum concentrations were below detectable limits at the 24 hr time point, an 18-hr blood draw was included in the fourth and following (5 mg/kg) subjects. Following blood collection, the blood samples were immediately centrifuged at 6,200 rpm for 15 min. Serum was mixed with an equal volume of 25 mM citrate buffer (pH 1.5), and the mixed samples were stored at −20°C for up to 1 month until analysis [8]. From the time of thalidomide administration until 24 hr post-administration, the animals were supervised by a registered nurse and veterinarian at the hospital, and adverse effects were noted.

The mixed sample (200 μL) was added to 50 μL of an internal standard solution (100 μg/mL phenacetin in methanol), and 1 mL of dichloromethane and n-hexane (1:1 vol/vol), and the mixture was shaken for 10 min. After the solvent layers were separated by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min, the organic layer was transferred to another tube and evaporated under reduced pressure using a centrifuge evaporator. The residue was dissolved in 100 μL of the mobile phase, and 30 μL of the solution was injected into the high-performance liquid chromatography system. Separation was carried out using a Symmetry C18 Column (150 × 4.6 mm ID, Waters, MA, USA), and the column temperature was maintained at 40°C. The mobile phase consisted of a mixture of acetonitrile and water (32:68, vol/vol) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Absorbance was measured at 220 nm using an ultraviolet absorption detector [8].

A signal-to-noise between 2:1 and 3:1 is generally considered acceptable for estimating the limit of detection [6]. Therefore, 0.01 mg/mL, which had a signal-to-noise ratio of 3 or higher, was deemed to be the detection limit.

As pharmacokinetic parameters, time of peak concentration (Tmax), peak concentration (Cmax), terminal half-life (t1/2,) mean residence time (MRT), oral total clearance (CL/F), volume of distribution (Vd/F), and area under the curve (AUC) were calculated by non-compartmental analysis [31]. Since the third subject in the 10 mg/kg group could not be analyzed using the serum concentration at 24 hr after administration, the pharmacokinetic parameters were analyzed for n=3, except for the third subject. Terminal half-life was calculated using serum concentrations at 12 to 24 hr after administration.

RESULTS

Study demographics

Fifty-one dogs with various malignancies were included in this study. Age, weight, sex, breed, and tumor type are listed in Table 1. The median initial thalidomide dose was 5.3 mg/kg (range 3.4–8.1). The median drug dose after increasing the thalidomide dose was 9.4 mg/kg (range 6.7–13.7). Thalidomide was not increased in 16 cases: seven had tumor progression, one was started on radiotherapy, one had tremor, one had dizziness, one had lethargy, one had tremor and dizziness, and one had anorexia. The reason for not increasing the dose was not given in three of the dogs. The median overall duration of thalidomide treatment was 27 days (mean 79 days, range 5–449 days). Prednisolone and Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) were given concomitantly while receiving thalidomide in 7 and 33 dogs, respectively.

Hematologic toxicity

Hematologic toxicity was only investigated in 44/51 dogs because thalidomide was withdrawn after less than 2 weeks of administration in seven of the dogs, and no blood tests were performed during that time. In one case, blood tests could not be performed within 14 days and were performed on day 16. Blood tests were performed every month after the thalidomide prescription, but one dog had not been tested for 56 days, one for 57 days, and two for 77 days. The incidence of hematologic toxicity and timing of the adverse events are summarized in Table 3. The adverse events related to CBCs included 20.4% (9/44) that had deviations from baseline measurements before thalidomide initiation (no duplicates) (Table 3): six dogs had decreased PCVs (grade 1=4, grade 2=2), one dog had neutropenia (grade 1=1), and two dogs had thrombocytopenia (grade 2=1, grade 3=1). One dog with grade 3 thrombocytopenia had persistent thrombocytopenia that occurred after lomustine treatment; the thrombocytopenia progressed to grade 4 after thalidomide treatment was started. Among the adverse events related to serum chemistry tests, 20.4% (9/44) of the dogs had measurements that deviated from reference values before thalidomide initiation (overlapping); hypoalbuminemia (grade 1=1) was observed in one dog, elevated ALT (grade 1=3, grade 2=1, grade 3=2) in six dogs, elevated total bilirubin (grade 1=1) in one dog, elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (grade 1=2) in two dogs, elevated plasma creatinine (grade 1=1) in one dog, and hypoglycemia (grade 1=1) in one dog. Three of the 19 dogs with confirmed elevated ALT activities were diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma. Pharmacokinetic serum analysis

Pharmacokinetic analyses after thalidomide administration were measured in four patients, each in the 5 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg groups. The 5 mg/kg group included one Bernese Mountain Dog, one Welsh Corgi Pembroke, one Miniature Dachshund, and one Shetland Sheepdog. The median weight was 11.3 kg (range 5.5–25.5 kg), and the sexes consisted of one each of a male, castrated male, female, and spayed female. The median age was 11 years (range 10–13 years). The tumor types included prostatic carcinoma (n=1), oral adenocarcinoma (n=1), oral malignant melanoma (n=1), and transitional cell carcinoma of the urinary bladder (n=1). The median drug dose was 5.4 mg/kg (4.5–6.0 mg/kg). The 10 mg/kg group included one Golden Retriever, two Welsh Corgi Pembroke, and one Cavalier King Charles Spaniel. The median weight was 13.8 kg (range 8.42–39.8 kg), and the sexes consisted of one male, one castrated male, and two spayed females. The median age was 11.5 years (range 10–13 years). The tumor types were hemangiosarcoma (n=1), mammary gland carcinoma (n=1), soft tissue sarcoma (n=1), and sarcoma (suspected osteosarcoma) (n=1). The median drug dose was 9.9 mg/kg (range 9.7–10.2 mg/kg). One Cavalier King Charles Spaniel was treated with pimobendan, enalapril, spironolactone, gabapentin, and firocoxib on thalidomide days for mitral regurgitation and cancer pain relief. None of the other dogs received any concomitant medication. The mean change in thalidomide serum concentration for both the 5 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg groups is shown graphically (Table 4, Fig. 1). From the measured thalidomide serum concentrations, the mean values of each item were calculated. In the 5 mg/kg group, Tmax=1.5 ± 0.6 hr, Cmax=1.4 ± 0.7 μg/mL, t1/2=10.6 ± 6.1 hr, and AUC0–24=8.5 ± 4.7 μg·hr/mL; in the 10 mg/kg group, Tmax=2.3 ± 1.5 hr, Cmax=3.2 ± 2.1 μg/mL, t1/2=4.8 ± 1.2 hr, and AUC0–24=22.0 ± 14.7 μg·hr/mL. Lethargy was observed 2 hr after administration in one patient treated with 10 mg/kg thalidomide.

DISCUSSION

The results of the present study suggest that adverse events are considered clinically acceptable when thalidomide is administered in tumor-bearing dogs at 5–10 mg/kg. In addition, we considered an initial thalidomide dose of 10 mg/kg once daily to be appropriate, based on the blood levels of thalidomide obtained in this study and the frequency and severity of adverse events. However, since a strong tendency toward lethargy and peripheral nerve signs was observed in some dogs, the dose should be adjusted depending on the severity of the adverse events. In more than half of the dogs, weight was maintained or increased one month after thalidomide administration, suggesting that thalidomide might contribute to weight maintenance in tumor-bearing dogs.

Adverse events associated with thalidomide have been reported in people and include peripheral neurologic symptoms, such as numbness and tremor [7, 28, 33]. Although there are reports of no peripheral nerve signs in dogs [2, 32], tremor, stiff walking, dizziness, and whining have been observed experimentally after a single dose of thalidomide [24]. However, these signs were transient after administration, and their exact relationship with thalidomide remains unclear. In our study, tremor and dizziness were observed in six and four dogs, respectively. In some dogs, these signs improved with thalidomide reduction or withdrawal, suggesting that thalidomide may also cause peripheral neurologic symptoms in dogs. Peripheral nerve signs after thalidomide administration have been reported in people to develop cumulatively [18, 20]. Three of the six dogs with tremor identified in this study showed signs after receiving thalidomide for more than a month rather than immediately after starting thalidomide, raising the possibility that some adverse events are cumulative in dogs.

Thalidomide has been reported to have low gastrointestinal toxicity [2, 32]. Most anorexia in the present study was also grades 1–2 and given the use of thalidomide in tumor-bearing dogs, we considered these adverse events clinically acceptable. In contrast, grade 3 appetite loss was also observed in four dogs in this study; a dog with advanced lung metastases from mammary gland carcinoma, a dog with advanced inflammatory mammary carcinoma, a dog with pharynx pain from squamous cell carcinoma of the tonsils, and a dog with prostate cancer and pain from vertebral metastases. Since tumor-bearing dogs often experience pain [15, 17, 26], we cannot rule out that part of the loss of appetite after thalidomide administration may be related to cancer pain. Vomiting and diarrhea were observed in 15.7% and 32.7% of the patients, respectively; however, similar to anorexia, most adverse events were mild (grade 1 to 2). Six of the eight patients with vomiting were treated with concomitant NSAIDs or steroids. Of the 16 dogs with diarrhea, 11 received concomitant NSAIDs, steroids, or antibacterial agents, and the possibility that some of these adverse events were adverse events caused by concomitant medications could not be ruled out. The current study included dogs with relatively advanced cancer that had been on thalidomide for a long duration. Therefore, it was difficult differentiating between tumor-associated effects, the effects of concomitant medications, or those caused by thalidomide.

Hematologic toxicity has been reported in dogs treated with thalidomide [3, 4]. In the present study, most hematologic toxicities were grade 2 or lower, and hematologic toxicity due to thalidomide was considered acceptable, although thrombocytopenia was observed as grade 4 in one dog. Grade 3 or higher adverse events were also observed in dogs with increased ALT activity and BUN concentration. One patient with thrombocytopenia was thrombocytopenic before thalidomide administration; lomustine-induced thrombocytopenia was considered the primary cause. The possibility of nephrotoxicity due to piroxicam could not be ruled out in one dog, in which a grade 3 BUN increase was observed. A grade 3 or higher ALT activity was seen in five patients (11.3%): three dogs with increased ALT activity prior to treatment, three dogs receiving concomitant prednisolone, and one dog with a liver mass (overlapping). In people, hepatotoxicity due to thalidomide is rare; however, it has been reported to cause severe liver damage [11, 16]. Based on the present study, the possibility of thalidomide induced ALT elevations could not be completely excluded, and we suggest that liver enzymes be monitored regularly during thalidomide administration.

Thalidomide has both immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects and may be beneficial in the treatment of cachexia in patients with cancer [9, 27]. In the present study, the rate of change in the body weight of dogs taking thalidomide for approximately one month was investigated to determine the weight-maintaining effects of thalidomide. The results showed that more than half of the dogs maintained or increased their body weight. Interestingly, in dogs with decreased appetites and a large number of tumors, weight was maintained for approximately one month. However, our results do not clarify the anti-cachexia effect of thalidomide or its underlying mechanism. The sample size of our study was also relatively small, and the possibility of a Type II error due to sample size or that weight was maintained by concomitant medications cannot be ruled out. In future studies, we will evaluate the anti-cachexia effect of thalidomide in a larger population against a control group not receiving the drug.

Blood levels of thalidomide have not been reported in dogs in clinical medicine. We found that the Cmax (3.2 ± 2.1 µg/mL) and AUC0–24 (22.0 ± 14.7 µg-hr/mL) of thalidomide administered at 10 mg/kg were comparable to those at a therapeutic dose in human patients (Cmax=1.68–2.0 µg/mL, AUC=15.8–20.1 µg•hr /mL) [21, 23, 33]. It is unclear whether the effective blood levels for malignant tumors in dogs are comparable to those in humans. However, we used an equivalent dose of thalidomide (8.4 mg/kg) in a study where thalidomide was administered postoperatively for splenic hemangiosarcoma in dogs, showing potentially prolonged survival [2]. Thalidomide has a wide safety margin, and the maximum tolerated dose in dogs has not been determined. Based on blood levels that were similar to the therapeutic dose in humans and the frequency and extent of adverse events in tumor-bearing dogs, we considered it reasonable to set the initial dose of thalidomide in dogs at 10 mg/kg.

In our study, the half-life was shorter in the 10 mg/kg group than in the 5 mg/kg group. One possible reason for this is that, in general, the lower the blood concentration of a drug, the longer the half-life. In other words, when the pharmacokinetics of the drug can be approximated by a two-compartment model, the half-life is longer in the beta phase than in the alpha phase, and the non-compartment analysis calculates the half-life by calculating the slope of the serum concentration from the latter three points. Therefore, the half-life is shorter in the 10 mg/kg group, but the slopes from 18 to 24 hr are similar, which means that the half-lives are considered to be similar. The small number of cases may be another reason why the results are difficult to interpret.

In healthy dogs, thalidomide was reported to increase blood levels of thalidomide when administered with food. In this study, thalidomide dosed at 400 mg/head=11.74 ± 0.56 mg/kg with food had an AUC of 42.46 ± 6.64 mg•hr/L, whereas the AUC after overnight fasting was only 12.38 ± 1.13 mg•hr/L, indicating that thalidomide dosed with food was significantly higher than when it was dosed in a fasted state [24]. In our study, the AUC0–24 at 10 mg/kg of thalidomide was 22.0 ± 14.7 μg•hr /mL at an intermediate state between a non-fasting and fasting state, as performed in the study by Pierini et al. Thalidomide also causes drowsiness relatively early after administration, making it difficult for dogs and families to share meaningful time together if administered during the day. Therefore, we directed the timing of thalidomide dosing to be at the owner’s bedtime, and many dogs were dosed several hours after eating. However, if thalidomide is administered to dogs with their meals, the timing of meals and thalidomide dosing should be carefully considered, as the incidence and severity of adverse events may change as thalidomide blood concentrations increase.

The limitations of this study include the retrospective nature of the investigation into adverse events and the possibility that the assessment of adverse events as related to clinical signs may have been underestimated (recall bias). It was also difficult to differentiate the side effects of thalidomide from those caused by tumors or concomitant medications. Because thalidomide pharmacokinetic measurements are lengthy, few owners were willing to agree to hospitalization, and it was not possible to assess pharmacokinetics in a sufficient number of dogs. In addition, because the tumor type, underlying disease, and concomitant medications were not standardized, the effects of these parameters on pharmacokinetics could not be examined.

Thalidomide is a drug that has caused worldwide drug-related harm due to teratogenicity, and veterinarians are obligated to use thalidomide appropriately when it is used in tumor-bearing dogs. The drug dose for tumor-bearing dogs in this study was considered appropriate at 10 mg/kg once daily, based on the blood levels obtained and the frequency and extent of adverse events. On the other hand, excretion in dogs is still unknown and great care should be taken when handling excreta after thalidomide administration. As a next step, we will investigate the anti-tumor effects of thalidomide as a phase 2 study and evaluate the potential contribution of thalidomide to weight maintenance in tumor-bearing dogs. We will include a larger population of dogs and a control group.
