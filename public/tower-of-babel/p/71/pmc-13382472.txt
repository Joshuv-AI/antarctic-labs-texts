Proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal IgG3 deposits: A case series
Authors: Rui Zhang, Lifen Xu, Jun Xu, Xingyan Zhou, Ziwei Guo, Haiyan Yu, Wenyu Song, Zhipeng Feng, Yi Bao, Rui Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits (PGNMID) caused by IgG3-dominant is rare and incompletely characterized. We retrospectively reported seven male patients (median age 63 years) with IgG3-dominant monoclonal immunoglobulin–associated glomerulonephritis. All patients presented with proteinuria (1.2–16.4 g/24 h), five with nephrotic-range proteinuria, and serum creatinine at diagnosis ranged from 88 to 294 μmol/L. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis was negative in all cases, while urinary free light chains were variably increased. Renal biopsy predominantly demonstrated a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern (6/7), frequently accompanied by crescents and variable degrees of chronic glomerulosclerosis. Immunofluorescence consistently showed dominant IgG3 deposition. Therapeutic approaches included supportive therapy, corticosteroids combined with cyclophosphamide, ripertamab-based regimens, and bortezomib. Renal outcomes were heterogeneous, ranging from sustained remission to progression to end-stage renal disease or death from severe infectious complications. Patients receiving bortezomib-based clone-directed therapy achieved the most reduction in proteinuria. These findings highlight the marked clinicopathological heterogeneity of IgG3-dominant PGNMID and underscore the importance of early recognition, assessment of chronic histologic injury, and individualized clone-directed treatment to improve renal outcomes.

Introduction

Proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits (PGNMID) is a rare form of monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance, characterized by glomerular deposition of a single immunoglobulin isotype and light chain in the absence of overt hematologic malignancy [1,2]. Since its initial description by Nasr et al. PGNMID has been increasingly recognized as a clinicopathologic disease, often presenting with proteinuria, hematuria, and variable degrees of renal dysfunction [[1], [2], [3]].

Among the IgG subclasses, IgG3 is represented in PGNMID and is associated with a more aggressive clinical course [4,5]. IgG3 is characterized by a large molecular size, an extended hinge region, a tendency to self-aggregate, and strong activation of the classical complement pathway, features that favor glomerular deposition and amplify local inflammatory responses [6]. Consistent with these features, IgG3-dominant PGNMID frequently exhibits a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN) on renal pathology, accompanied by prominent C3 and C1q co-deposition on immunofluorescence, reflecting complement activation [7].

Despite its classification within the Monoclonal Gammopathy of Renal Significance (MGRS) spectrum, the underlying monoclonal clone in PGNMID is often difficult to detect, as serum and urine immunofixation studies are often negative [8]. This diagnostic challenge complicates treatment decisions, and optimal therapeutic strategies remain undefined. While clone-directed therapies, including bortezomib, anti-CD38 and anti-CD20 antibodies, have shown promise in selected cases, treatment responses are heterogeneous and depend on the degree of chronic histologic injury and treatment-related complications [6,9].

Given the rarity of PGNMID and the limited data on IgG3-dominant disease, particularly in Asian populations, more studies are needed to better define its clinicopathologic features and clinical outcomes. Here, we present a case series of patients with biopsy-proven PGNMID, with a focus on IgG3-dominant disease, illustrating the diversity of clinical presentation, renal pathology, therapeutic strategy and outcomes.

Clinical features and laboratory findings

All 7 cases were males, with a median age of 63 years (range: 53–72) at presentation. The median serum creatinine and proteinuria at initial diagnosis were 150 μmol/L (range: 88–294 μmol/L) and 4454 mg/24 hours (range: 1182–16,416), respectively. All patients had a history of hypertension, and most were receiving antihypertensive medications at presentation. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis was negative in all cases, while urinary free light chains were variably increased. None of the patients had evidence of overt hematologic malignancy at diagnosis. Renal biopsy predominantly demonstrated a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern, with dominant IgG3 deposition and complement activation. A summary of each case is described below

Case 1

A 61-year-old man presented with a history of hypertension and nocturia. On admission, laboratory evaluation revealed impaired renal function, with a serum creatinine level of 150 μmol/L and a blood urea level of 12.46 mmol/L. 24-hour urinary protein was 2680 mg/24h. Serum albumin was 35.1 g/L, and hemoglobin was reduced to 92 g/L. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis was negative. Serum free light chain analysis showed kappa 2.24 g/L and lambda 1.29 g/L. Urinary free light chains were kappa 4.43 g/L and lambda 2.24 g/L. Renal ultrasonography demonstrated mildly increased renal parenchymal echogenicity with poor corticomedullary differentiation, suggestive of chronic renal parenchymal involvement.

The patient underwent kidney biopsy. Glomeruli demonstrated diffuse and global granular deposits distributed in a clustered pattern within the mesangial areas and along the glomerular capillary walls. Light microscopy examination of the renal biopsy revealed 11 glomeruli exhibiting a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern, characterized by diffuse moderate-to-severe mesangial cell and matrix proliferation, extensive mesangial interposition, and double-contour formation of the glomerular basement membranes. Segmental endocapillary hypercellularity was also observed. Transmission electron microscopy demonstrated mesangial expansion with mesangial interposition into the capillary wall, accompanied by subendothelial electron-dense deposits.

Given the patient's comorbid hypertension and hypertensive heart disease, treatment was initiated with oral prednisone acetate, along with optimized supportive therapy including irbesartan and dapagliflozin. After 2 months of oral glucocorticoid therapy, the patient developed coronary atherosclerotic heart disease. The glucocorticoid dose was tapered to 5 mg/day. The patient was treated with aspirin, ticagrelor, and atorvastatin for coronary artery disease. At 3-month follow-up, the serum creatinine was 167.51 μmol/L and the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was 1447.56 mg/g.

Case 2

A 69-year-old man presented with bilateral lower extremity edema. He had a history of hypertension and had been receiving long-term treatment with irbesartan for blood pressure control. On admission, laboratory investigations revealed impaired renal function, with a serum creatinine level of 125.9 μmol/L and a blood urea level of 10.71 mmol/L. 24-hour urinary protein was 2426.85 mg/24h. Serum albumin was markedly decreased at 18.1 g/L, and hemoglobin was 108 g/L. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis was negative. Serum free light chain analysis showed kappa 1.81 g/L and lambda 1.09 g/L, while urinary free light chains were kappa 10.82 g/L and lambda 5.8 g/L. Renal ultrasonography demonstrated bilateral renal calculi and multiple cystic structures in the right kidney.

Light microscopy examination showed that 1 of 24 glomeruli was globally sclerosed, and 3 glomeruli exhibited ischemic glomerular sclerosis. The remaining glomeruli demonstrated a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern, with the presence of two cellular crescents. A subset of glomeruli showed capsular adhesions. Transmission electron microscopy demonstrated mesangial expansion with mesangial interposition into the capillary wall, accompanied by subendothelial electron-dense deposits.

The patient received supportive therapy, including leflunomide at a dose of 20 mg/day, indobufen for antithrombotic prophylaxis, irbesartan and sustained-release nifedipine for blood pressure control, spironolactone for diuresis, and atorvastatin for lipid lowering. After 3 months of treatment, bilateral lower extremity edema had resolved; though the patient did not attend further in-person follow up. On telephone follow-up two years later, the patient reported no recurrence of lower extremity edema or foamy urine.

Case 3

A 63-year-old man was admitted for evaluation of elevated serum creatinine. He had a history of hypertension and had been receiving long-term treatment with sustained-release nifedipine and terazosin for blood pressure control. On admission, laboratory investigations revealed advanced renal dysfunction, with a serum creatinine level of 294 μmol/L and a blood urea level of 11.56 mmol/L. 24h urinary protein was 5946.26 mg/24 h. Serum albumin was 32.98 g/L, and hemoglobin was 112 g/L. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis was negative. Urinary free light chain analysis revealed elevated kappa (22.7 g/L) and lambda (20.81 g/L) levels. Renal ultrasonography demonstrated bilateral renal echogenic changes.

Light microscopy examination included 18 glomeruli, of which 10 showed ischemic glomerular sclerosis and 1 exhibited segmental glomerulosclerosis. The remaining glomeruli were hypertrophic, with mild diffuse mesangial cell and matrix expansion and segmental moderate accentuation accompanied by endocapillary hypercellularity. The glomerular basement membranes exhibited ribbon-like changes. The pathological diagnosis was focal proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal IgG deposition. Transmission electron microscopy did not include glomerular tissue, precluding evaluation of mesangial or subendothelial electron-dense deposits.

At presentation, eGFR was approximately 20mL/min/1.73m2. The patient received supportive therapy, including sacubitril/valsartan and sustained-release nifedipine for blood pressure and cardiorenal management. Despite treatment, renal function progressively deteriorated, and the patient advanced to end-stage renal disease requiring initiation of renal replacement therapy 2 years later.

Case 4

A 53-year-old man was admitted with generalized edema. He had a history of hypertension and had been receiving long-term treatment with telmisartan and amlodipine besylate for blood pressure control. On admission, laboratory evaluation showed a serum creatinine level of 94μmol/L and a blood urea level of 10.63 mmol/L. 24-hour urinary protein was 1181.58 mg/24 h. Serum albumin was 35.81 g/L, and hemoglobin was 87 g/L. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis was negative. Serum free light chain analysis revealed kappa 3.04 g/L and lambda 1.84 g/L, while urinary free light chains were kappa 4.4 g/L and lambda 1.67 g/L. Renal ultrasonography showed no structural abnormalities. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated mild tricuspid regurgitation.

Light microscopy examination included 31 glomeruli, of which 2 showed segmental glomerulosclerosis and 5 exhibited ischemic glomerular sclerosis. The remaining glomeruli demonstrated a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern, with the presence of four fibrous crescents. Transmission electron microscopy demonstrated mesangial expansion with mesangial interposition into the capillary wall, accompanied by subendothelial electron-dense deposits.

During hospitalization, the patient received supportive therapy including diuretics and correction of anemia, after which generalized edema resolved. After discharge, he continued long-term treatment with telmisartan and amlodipine besylate. The patient declined follow-up laboratory assessment. On phone follow-up 21 months later, he reported no recurrence of edema, and home blood pressure measurements were well controlled, ranging from 125136/70–84 mmHg.

Case 5

A 55-year-old man was admitted with bilateral lower extremity edema. He had a history of hypertension but had not been taking antihypertensive medications regularly. On admission, laboratory evaluation revealed impaired renal function, with a serum creatinine level of 202μmol/L and a blood urea level of 10.36mmol/L. 24–hour urinary protein excretion was 7136.63mg/24 h. Serum albumin was markedly decreased at 19.03g/L, and hemoglobin was 125g/L. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis was negative. Serum free light chain analysis showed kappa 1.67g/L and lambda 1.19g/L, while urinary free light chains were markedly increased, with kappa 42.6g/L and lambda 27g/L. Renal ultrasonography demonstrated bilateral increased renal echogenicity. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed relatively reduced motion of the left ventricular posterior wall and a small pericardial effusion.

Light microscopy examination included 19 glomeruli, of which 7 showed global glomerulosclerosis, 6 exhibited ischemic glomerular sclerosis, and 1 showed segmental glomerulosclerosis. The remaining glomeruli demonstrated a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern, with the presence of one fibrous crescent and two small cellular crescents. Transmission electron microscopy demonstrated mesangial expansion with mesangial interposition into the capillary wall, accompanied by subendothelial electron-dense deposits.

The patient was treated with oral prednisone at an initial dose of 50 mg/day combined with intravenous cyclophosphamide at a dose of 0.2g weekly. After 4 months of therapy, the cumulative cyclophosphamide dose reached 4.8g. Follow-up laboratory evaluation on July 25, 2024 showed improvement in renal function, with a serum creatinine level of 103.3 μmol/L; however, nephrotic-range proteinuria persisted (24-h urinary protein excretion 7391.48 mg/24 h), and serum albumin remained low at 24.13 g/L. Six months after treatment initiation, the patient developed recurrent bilateral lower extremity edema, accompanied by worsening renal function (serum creatinine 175.04 μmol/L). Ripertamab was subsequently administered, with a total dose of 400 mg, in combination with prednisone. B-cell depletion was confirmed, with a peripheral B-cell count of 2 cells/μL on September 23, 2024. Subsequent follow-up demonstrated a partial reduction in proteinuria (24-h urinary protein 1939.16 mg/24 h on October 12, 2024), while serum creatinine remained elevated at 197.5 μmol/L (October 10, 2024). In the following months, the patient experienced recurrent pulmonary infections. Chest computed tomography performed on January 14, 2025 revealed bilateral pleural effusions, emphysematous changes with pulmonary bullae, and interstitial lung abnormalities. Despite repeated hospitalizations for pulmonary infections, the patient's condition progressively deteriorated, and he died approximately 20 months after diagnosis.

Case 6

A 72-year-old man was admitted with bilateral lower extremity edema. He had a history of hypertension and had been receiving long-term treatment with valsartan/hydrochlorothiazide, amlodipine besylate, and bisoprolol for blood pressure control. On admission, laboratory evaluation showed a serum creatinine level of 88.35 μmol/L and a blood urea level of 7.12 mmol/L. 24hour urinary protein was 4453.53 mg/24 h, and serum albumin was 32.5 g/L. Hemoglobin was 127 g/L. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis was negative. Serum free light chain analysis demonstrated kappa 1.33g/L and lambda 0.781g/L. Renal ultrasonography revealed mildly increased renal parenchymal echogenicity. Bone marrow flow cytometry revealed no significant abnormalities.

Light microscopy examination included 32 glomeruli, of which 6 showed ischemic glomerular sclerosis. The remaining glomeruli demonstrated a MPGN pattern. Transmission electron microscopy demonstrated mesangial expansion with mesangial interposition into the capillary wall, accompanied by subendothelial electron-dense deposits.

Daratumumab-based therapy was initially planned; however the patient declined further treatment at our center. One month later, he received treatment at an outside hospital, where he was administered bortezomib (2 mg), cyclophosphamide (500 mg), and dexamethasone (10 mg) for two cycles. During treatment, sputum testing was positive for Pneumocystis jirovecii nucleic acid, and caspofungin was initiated for anti-infective therapy. The patient subsequently developed oliguria with worsening bilateral lower extremity edema, chest tightness, and dyspnea, and acute heart failure was suspected. Hemodialysis was initiated, after which his clinical condition improved. After discharge, the patient continued long-term outpatient therapy with sacubitril/valsartan and terazosin. On phone follow-up 9 months later, he reported no recurrence of edema. Repeat laboratory testing showed stable renal function, with a serum creatinine level of 88 μmol/L, and a marked reduction in proteinuria (24-h urinary protein 318.56 mg/24 h).

Case 7

A 65-year-old man was admitted with bilateral lower extremity edema. He had a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and was on regular salbutamol therapy. On admission, laboratory evaluation revealed impaired renal function, with a serum creatinine level of 136 μmol/L and a blood urea level of 14.83 mmol/L. 24hour urinary protein was markedly increased at 16,416mg/24 h. Serum albumin was reduced to 21.6 g/L, and hemoglobin was 86 g/L. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis was negative. Serum free light chain analysis showed kappa 1.04 g/L and lambda 0.777 g/L, while urinary free light chains were elevated (kappa 24.21 g/L and lambda 19.74 g/L). Renal ultrasonography demonstrated bilateral renal parenchymal changes. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, left atrial enlargement with mild mitral regurgitation, and mild pulmonary hypertension with mild tricuspid regurgitation. Bone marrow flow cytometry revealed no significant abnormalities.

Light microscopy examination included 65 glomeruli, of which 1 showed global glomerulosclerosis, 4 showed segmental glomerulosclerosis, and 22 exhibited ischemic glomerular sclerosis. The remaining glomeruli demonstrated a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern.

The patient was treated with oral prednisone at a dose of 50mg/day combined with supportive therapy including furosemide. Clinical symptoms improved with resolution of lower-extremity edema after one month. However, the patient later discontinued treatment, and generalized edema recurred five months thereafter.

Discussion

In this case series, we describe patients with biopsy-proven proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits, most of whom exhibited IgG3-dominant deposition and a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern on renal pathology. None of the patients showed clinical features suggestive of cryoglobulinemia, such as purpura, arthralgia, or characteristic hypocomplementemia. Electron microscopy did not demonstrate organized deposits typical of cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis (e.g., microtubular, fibrillary, or annular structures). In addition, secondary causes of cryoglobulinemia, including hepatitis B virus infection, human immunodeficiency virus infection, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren's syndrome, and rheumatoid arthritis, were excluded in all cases. However, serum cryoglobulin testing was not systematically performed, which represents a limitation of this study.

Recent advances have refined the conceptual framework of PGNMID by distinguishing between MGRS and non-MGRS forms [10]. Using immunoglobulin repertoire sequencing, a subset of patients has been shown to harbor a true pathogenic B-cell or plasma cell clone (MGRS-PGNMID), whereas others lack detectable clonality and may represent oligoclonal or antigen-driven immune responses (non-MGRS PGNMID). This distinction has important therapeutic implications, as clone-directed therapy appears more rational in MGRS-associated disease, while immunosuppressive or supportive approaches may be considered in non-MGRS cases. In our series, conventional hematologic evaluation failed to identify a monoclonal protein in most patients, and bone marrow studies were not uniformly performed, precluding definitive classification. In addition, immunoglobulin repertoire sequencing was not available in our cohort, which represents an important limitation in accurately distinguishing between MGRS and non-MGRS PGNMID. The heterogeneous clinical courses and treatment responses observed across our cases may, therefore, reflect underlying biological differences between these subgroups. These findings highlight the need for more sensitive clonal detection methods to better stratify patients and guide individualized therapy.

A notable finding in our series is the predominance of IgG3-dominant disease, accompanied by prominent C3 and C1q deposition on immunofluorescence. IgG3 is known to possess structural features that favor glomerular deposition and strong activation of the classical complement pathway, which likely contributes to the characteristic membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern observed in these cases. The frequent coexistence of mesangial, subendothelial, and occasionally subepithelial immune deposits in our patients supports a model in which monoclonal IgG3 triggers sustained complement activation, leading to endothelial injury, mesangial proliferation, and capillary wall remodeling. The Case 3 showed a focal proliferative glomerulonephritis pattern on light microscopy, without evidence of double-contour formation. Because no glomeruli were available for electron microscopy in this case, the presence of subendothelial electron-dense deposits could not be assessed. In contrast, Cases 1, 2, and 4–7 demonstrated membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis pattern, and subendothelial electron-dense deposits were identified by electron microscopy in all of these cases.

Therapeutic strategies in PGNMID are largely guided by expert consensus and depend on the presumed nature of the underlying clone, the presence or absence of circulating monoclonal immunoglobulin, and the risk of progressive kidney disease. For patients without detectable monoclonal immunoglobulin and with a low risk of disease progression (defined as chronic kidney disease stage 1–2 and proteinuria <1.0 g/day) renin–angiotensin system blockade and supportive therapy are recommended. Case 4, managed conservatively, has remained clinically stable to date. The chemotherapy is generally recommended for patients with detectable monoclonal immunoglobulin or those at higher risk of disease progression. Plasma cell derived PGNMID, most commonly associated with IgG monoclonal deposits, is typically treated with bortezomib-based regimens and daratumumab. In our series, Case 6 achieved clinical stabilization after two cycles of bortezomib, cyclophosphamide, and dexamethasone (BCD).

For B cell–derived PGNMID, anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody (rituximab, repertamab) therapy is commonly recommended by expert consensus and international working groups [2,4,11,12]. However, rituximab or repertamab treatment is associated with a substantial risk of infectious complications and pulmonary toxicity. Infectious adverse events include bacterial, viral, and opportunistic infections, such as Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, which are related to prolonged B-cell depletion and secondary hypogammaglobulinemia [[13], [14], [15]]. In addition, rituximab-associated interstitial lung disease, although uncommon, has been reported and may present with progressive dyspnea and interstitial changes on chest imaging [15]. Case 5 developed interstitial lung disease and recurrent pulmonary infections following ripertamab therapy and ultimately died. For patients with PGNMID in whom no monoclonal clone can be identified but empiric therapy is considered necessary, current practice trends favor plasma cell–directed approaches, such as CD38 monoclonal antibodies or bortezomib-based regimens, or a strategy of early transition to plasma cell–targeted therapy in cases refractory to rituximab.

Several patients in our series experienced significant treatment-related complications, particularly infectious events, highlighting the narrow therapeutic window in PGNMID. This is especially relevant in older patients and those with comorbid conditions, in whom aggressive immunosuppression or chemotherapy may carry substantial risk. Our findings emphasize the need for careful patient selection and individualized treatment strategies, balancing potential renal benefit against treatment-related toxicity. PGNMID, particularly IgG3-dominant disease, is a heterogeneous entity with variable clinical outcomes. Emerging evidence suggests that both MGRS and non-MGRS forms may exist, reflecting distinct pathogenic mechanisms. Careful assessment of clonality is therefore important. Further studies are needed to validate this classification and to guide mechanism-based therapeutic strategies.
