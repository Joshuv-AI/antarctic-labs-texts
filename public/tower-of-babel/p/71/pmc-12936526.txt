Hypertension across Africa: beyond the DASH diet
Authors: Emmanuel Baah
Published: 2025

High blood pressure is certainly a major public health issue, especially in Africa, where eating habits, economic conditions and cultural traditions impact health results. The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, which emphasises a variety of fruits, vegetables, whole grains and low-fat dairy, has been extensively advocated as a successful strategy for lowering blood pressure.1 2 Although research robustly backs its effectiveness, sticking to the diet continues to be a significant hurdle, as indicated by recent studies investigating its application in African communities.

While sodium is a key dietary component that affects blood pressure for many, emerging research suggests other nutrients and food items may also raise blood pressure but are not specifically covered by the DASH diet. Riboflavin (vitamin B2) is one such example, as it regulates blood pressure in people with the minor genotype of the MTHFR C677T polymorphism.3,5 Endothelial function and folate metabolism are impacted by this genetic variation, and research indicates that riboflavin supplementation may help lower blood pressure in those who are impacted.3,5 Even though there is still a dearth of research on nutrient–gene interaction, especially in African populations, investigating these lesser-known dietary factors that affect blood pressure can improve our comprehension of individualised dietary strategies for managing hypertension.

The promise

Numerous studies have confirmed that the DASH diet is beneficial in decreasing blood pressure. According to the National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute, people who follow the diet have notable drops in both their systolic and diastolic blood pressure.1 2 The success of hypertensive control using the DASH diet depends on increasing the consumption of potassium-rich foods and fibres, while reducing the intake of sodium. As a cardiovascular disease risk factor, any measures aimed at hypertension reduction can be a pivotal strategy in hypertension-related morbidity and mortality, especially in light of the rising prevalence of cardiovascular diseases in Africa.

Challenges

The DASH diet’s implementation in Northern and sub-Saharan Africa encounters substantial obstacles:

Dietary and Cultural Barriers: Traditional African diets often centre around staple foods such as cassava, maize and millet, which are frequently prepared with elevated levels of salt and fat. The transition towards Western-style diets, which includes the increased consumption of processed foods with high sodium content, further impedes adherence.

Genetic, physiological and environmental differences present challenges to the adoption of the DASH diet in these regions; a comprehensive understanding of these obstacles is therefore very vital for devising strategies to facilitate a more vibrant and vital adoption of the DASH diet in Northern and sub-Saharan Africa.

Alternate approach

Culturally and economically feasible options that resonate with local eating habits while fostering heart health need to be promoted:

Traditional African diets with a health-conscious approach: Certain traditional African foods naturally align with DASH dietary guidelines. Diets that prioritise legumes, leafy greens (such as amaranth and moringa) and lean proteins (like fish and poultry) can be enhanced for hypertension prevention without requiring major dietary changes.

Community-centred nutritional initiatives: Governments and NGOs should prioritise locally sourced, budget-friendly meal plans that promote sustainable, nutritious eating habits tailored to the unique needs of each region. For example, rather than promoting an entirely new diet, public health initiatives should concentrate on decreasing high salt intake in frequently consumed meals like stews and soups.

Conclusions

Although the DASH diet is a good way to manage hypertension, there are major cultural and financial obstacles to its widespread use in Northern and Sub-Saharan Africa. Public health initiatives should prioritise locally available, reasonably priced and culturally appropriate alternatives to the strict promotion of Western dietary paradigms. We can manage hypertension in these areas more effectively and sustainably by combining traditional dietary knowledge with contemporary nutritional understanding. The availability of healthful foods, community-based education and culturally appropriate dietary adjustments must be given top priority in future studies and policy initiatives. We can only make significant headway in the battle against hypertension by implementing such customised strategies.
