Heerfordt–Waldenstrom Syndrome of Neurosarcoidosis
Authors: Ayush Agarwal, Shubham Bansal, Divyani Garg, Mehul Taggar, Shamim A Shamim, Ajay Garg, Achal K Srivastava
Published: 2026

A 32-year-old gentleman presented to us with fever associated with occasional chills for the past 6 months, bifacial weakness (onset to peak: 3 days) with swelling of both cheeks and headache for the past 2 months. Examination revealed swelling of both parotid glands and bilateral lower motor neuron type facial weakness. The remaining neurological examination was normal.

His routine blood investigations were normal apart from an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (60 mm/hour) and C reactive protein (15 mg/dl). Antinuclear antibody, extractable nuclear antigen profile, anti-neutrophilic cytoplasmic antigen, angiotensin converting enzyme, Lyme’s serology, and viral markers for HIV and hepatitis B and C were all negative. Magnetic resonance imaging brain revealed abnormal enhancement along the course of the facial nerve and parotid glands [Figure 1]. His whole body fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography demonstrated bilateral, multiple FDG-avid supraclavicular, cervical, mediastinal, and abdominal lymph nodes along with bilateral parotid hypermetabolism [Figure 2]. An endobronchial ultrasound guided biopsy of the mediastinal lymph nodes revealed mild edema in the endobronchial mucosa with fibrin exudates and non-caseating granulomas suggestive of sarcoidosis. He was treated with pulse steroids (methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 5 days) followed by weight based oral steroids with gradual taper and concomitant azathioprine. He showed good response to treatment and was asymptomatic at 2 months follow up.

Heerfordt–Waldenstrom syndrome (HWS) comprises a clinical tetrad of fever, parotid enlargement, facial nerve palsy, and anterior uveitis.[1] HWS is considered pathognomonic of sarcoidosis, but is a rare manifestation affecting 4.1%–5.6% sarcoidosis patients.[2] Complete HWS entails the presence of all four features and is even rarer (0.3% cases). A diagnosis of incomplete HWS requires the presence of two out of the following three manifestations: anterior uveitis, parotid enlargement, and facial nerve palsy.[13] The pathophysiology of facial palsy in HWS is attributed either to perineural inflammatory infiltrates and epineural granulomas or direct compression by the enlarged parotid gland/lesions in the facial canal.[4] HWS patients usually show good response to steroids and immunosuppressants like azathioprine and methotrexate.[5] Recognition of this clinical syndrome may lead to early diagnosis, leading to better outcomes and prevention of unnecessary investigations.
