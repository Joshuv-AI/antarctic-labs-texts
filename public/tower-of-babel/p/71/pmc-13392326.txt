Disparities in the quality of care in patients with empty sella based on etiology: A cross-sectional study from Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abrar Alfaifi, Amal Alturkistani, Fatimah Altaleb, Nouf Aboalsamh, Mussa H Almalki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmpty sella (ES) is characterized by the replacement of pituitary tissue with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the sella turcica. Despite its frequency in clinical settings, ES is often underrecognized and incompletely evaluated. The aim of this study was to examine patterns of care and potential disparities across different ES etiologies.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate differences in hormonal assessment and referral patterns among patients with various etiologies of empty sella. All patients aged ≥14 years diagnosed with ES between February 2021 and December 2022 at King Fahad Medical City (KFMC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, were included. Relevant data were extracted from electronic medical records, and comparisons of hormonal assessment were made based on etiology and reason for endocrine referral.ResultsAmong 287 patients (75.3% female, mean age 49.7 years), 84.6% had primary ES. Only 29.9% were referred to endocrinology (52.4% for ES-specific evaluation). Secondary ES patients received more comprehensive hormonal assessments than primary ES (P<0.001). Primary ES referrals for ES-specific evaluation correlated with thorough testing (P<0.001).ConclusionsSignificant disparities exist in ES management, driven by etiology and referral intent. Standardized referral pathway and universal screening are essential to improve care.Keywords: empty sella, pituitary dysfunction, hypopituitarism, cerebrospinal fluid, obesity, headache

Background

Empty sella (ES) is a radiological finding characterized by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) replacing the pituitary tissue in the sella turcica, is classified as partial (≤50% CSF filling, pituitary tissue ≥3 mm) or complete (>50% CSF filling, pituitary tissue ≤2 mm).1,2 Etiologically, ES is primary (idiopathic) or secondary (post-surgery, radiation, or tumor-related).3–8

The reported ES prevalence reaches 38% in the general population, likely due to increased neuroimaging utilization.9,10 Risk factors include female sex (4:1–6:1 ratio), obesity, hypertension, and idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH).1,4,9,11,12

ES is a heterogeneous disease entity that extends from an asymptomatic, incidental radiological finding to a full clinical syndrome with hormonal disturbances (i.e. ES syndrome). 13 Reports on ES in the literature are wide-ranging, with approximately 9% to 60% of patients reported to have one or more pituitary hormone abnormalities.9,14 The majority of ES patients present with non-specific symptoms, or in rare instances seizures and psychiatric manifestations,4,13 and it remains difficult to tease out whether these symptoms are directly related to ES or attributed to other conditions for which the radiological study was requested. These findings present a challenge in managing ES patients and mandate referral to a specialist for appropriate evaluation. Recently, Ekhzaimy et al. reported that only 20% of primary ES receive appropriate referral to an endocrinologist in Saudi Arabia, and within this group hormonal assessment was still markedly under-evaluated. 15

The aim of this study was to describe the characteristics and presentations of ES patients according to their etiology and subtype. Furthermore, we aimed to conduct a cross-sectional evaluation of the quality of care that ES patients receive, and whether there were disparities in endocrine referrals and hormonal assessment according to etiology. Given the wide variability in reported rates of pituitary dysfunction and the potential for subclinical or asymptomatic hormonal abnormalities, a comprehensive baseline hormonal evaluation is warranted for all patients with ES, regardless of clinical presentation or radiological subtype, to avoid missed diagnoses of hypopituitarism.

Material and methods

Study population

This retrospective cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate differences in hormonal assessment and referral patterns among patients with various etiologies of ES. All patients ≥14 years diagnosed with ES from February 2021 to December 2022 at King Fahad Medical City (KFMC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, were included. The study individuals were identified via screening all magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reports for the keyword “empty sella.” Patients were not referred through a standardized pathway, and referrals to endocrinology were made at the discretion of the treating physician. All patients with available MRI confirmed diagnoses of ES were included, and no patients were excluded due to incomplete MRI or missing hormonal data.

Given the retrospective and observational nature of the study, all consecutive eligible patients identified during the study period were included, and a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. The final sample size therefore reflects the total number of patients meeting the inclusion criteria within the predefined timeframe.

This study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of King Fahad Medical City and was determined to be exempt from the requirement for full ethical approval due to its retrospective design and use of de-identified data (IRB Log Number: 23-183). In accordance with local regulations in Saudi Arabia, individuals aged 14 years and older are considered legally able to consent to medical care and were therefore not classified as minors. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the Epic electronic medical records system (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI). Information gathered included details on ES such as etiology (primary vs. secondary) and radiological subtype (partial vs. complete). The dataset also included demographics, clinical presentation, and hormonal assessment. Thyroid function was assessed using thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and free thyroxine (FT4). Hypothyroidism was classified as primary or central based on biochemical criteria, with central hypothyroidism defined as low FT4 with inappropriately low or normal TSH, after excluding primary hypothyroidism (high TSH) and medication effects (e.g., glucocorticoids, dopamine agonists); no further dynamic testing (e.g., TRH stimulation) was performed.

Adrenal function was assessed using adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and morning cortisol levels, and short Synacthen test if performed. Adrenal insufficiency was defined based on low morning cortisol levels with a concomitantly low or inappropriately normal ACTH) and/or an inadequate response to short Synacthen testing when performed Gonadal function was evaluated using luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle stimulating hormone (FSH), total testosterone in males, and estradiol in females. Hypogonadism was considered central in origin when FSH and LH were low or inappropriately normal with low sex hormones. Growth hormone deficiency was inferred from an age- and sex-adjusted insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) level below the lower limit of the normal reference range in the setting of multiple pituitary hormones deficits; GH stimulation testing was not routinely performed. Hyperprolactinemia was defined as a single prolactin level above the institutional reference range after excluding common secondary causes. Posterior pituitary function was not systematically assessed. Referral details to endocrinology were recorded and categorized as either ES-specific or for other endocrinopathies. All hormonal cutoffs and reference ranges were based on institutional laboratory standards at KFMC.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while quantitative data were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD). Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test and Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous data. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) applied for inferential statistics. All analysis were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 (IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY).

Results

A total of 287 patients were identified, with all eligible for final analysis. The cohort had a mean age of 49.7 ± 14.6 years and was predominantly females (75.3%). Most patients had primary ES (84.6%), while secondary ES etiologies included radiation therapy (47.7%), compressive mass lesion (45.5%), and prior surgeries (22.7%). Some patients had more than one contributing etiology; therefore, percentages may not total 100%.

Primary vs secondary ES

Patients with primary ES were older (50.5 vs. 45.8 years; P = 0.044), more frequently female (77.8% vs. 61.4%; P = 0.02), and had lower rates of hypothyroidism (30.7% vs. 48.6%, P = 0.036), including both primary and central forms. Rates of adrenal insufficiency, hypogonadism, growth hormone deficiency, and hyperprolactinemia were comparable (Table-1). Primary ES patients had higher rates of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (28.8% vs 13.6, P = 0.036), hypertension (36.6% vs 20.5%, P = 0.037), and MRI signs of elevated intracranial pressure (33.7% vs 9.1%, P = 0.001). No differences were observed in ES subtype (partial vs. complete), body mass index (BMI), history of pregnancy, symptomatic presentation, or psychiatric comorbidities (Table 1).

Partial vs. complete ES (primary)

Among primary ES patients (n = 243), 84.3% (n = 205) had partial ES. Partial ES patients were younger (49.7 vs. 54.5 years; P = 0.074), but had similar rates of endocrinopathies, comorbidities, and history of pregnancy (Table 2). Symptomatic presentation (34.2% overall) did not differ between subtypes (23.7% vs. 36.1%, P = 0.138). Symptoms included headaches (66.3%), seizures (15.7%), and tinnitus/visual disturbances and sleep apnea (13.3%) (Table 2).

Endocrine referral and hormonal assessment

Overall, 29.9% (n = 84) were referred to endocrinology: 52.4% (n = 44) for ES- specific evaluation and 47.6% (n = 40) for other endocrine disorders. Referrals for ES-specific evaluation were more common in secondary ES (52.3% vs. 8.6%, P < 0.001) (Table 3).

To further explore disparities in ES patients care quality, we conducted a subgroup analysis of patients referred to the endocrinology (n = 84, 29.9%). Compared to those referred for non-ES endocrine disorders, patients referred specifically for ES evaluation demonstrated significantly higher rates of adrenal function testing (81.8% vs. 22.5%, P < 0.001), gonadal axis assessment (88.6% vs. 17.5%, P < 0.001), IGF-1 measurement (79.5% vs. 10.0%, P < 0.001), and prolactin level evaluation (97.7% vs. 30.0%, P < 0.001). Thyroid function assessment, however, did not differ significantly between groups (95.5% vs. 97.5%, P = 0.614).

Discussion

In this study, we report the clinical presentation, hormonal assessment, and referral rates of 287 consecutive patients diagnosed with ES via brain MRI, while comparing demographic and clinical differences between primary and secondary ES. We highlight the following key findings. First, under recognition of ES by non-endocrine physicians, evidenced by low referral rates. Second, disparities in care quality, driven by etiology. Third, higher comorbidities rates (e.g., diabetes, hypertension) in primary ES. Lastly, no clinical differences between partial and complete primary ES subtypes.

Only 29.9% of the patients were referred to endocrinology, with < 50% of referrals targeting ES-specific evaluation. These findings align with Ekhuzaimy et al., where only 20% of primary ES patients received endocrine referrals, 15 underscoring primary ES as a clinically significant pituitary disorder requiring systematic hormonal evaluation, even when asymptomatic. While our study focused on testing rates rather than pituitary impairment prevalence. Nonetheless, secondary ES patients exhibited higher rates of thyrotroph axis dysfunction (central hypothyroidism) compared to primary ES, aligning with Cannavò et al., who reported rare thyrotroph involvement in primary ES. 16

The disparity in evaluations between primary and secondary ES likely reflects context-driven diagnostic bias. Secondary ES evaluations were more thorough due to overt clinical triggers (e.g., tumors, surgery), whereas primary ES—often perceived as incidental—was underassessed. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study comparing care quality between ES etiologies. Subgroup analysis revealed that referral intent (ES-specific vs. other) critically influenced evaluation completeness, emphasizing the need for structured communication between referring and receiving clinicians.

Within the primary ES subgroup, no significant differences between partial and complete ES in age, comorbidities, pituitary dysfunction, or symptomatic burden, consistent with previous study. 11 This challenges assumptions that complete ES is more severe. While Zuhur et al. reported higher hormonal deficits in complete ES, 2 our data support universal hormonal screening for all primary ES patients, regardless of subtype.

Our cohort demonstrated high rates of comorbidities, including T2DM, hypertension, and radiological signs of intracranial hypertension in primary ES, consistent with prior studies.9,15 While these conditions are prevalent in the general population, they may contribute to ES pathogenesis through CSF dynamic disturbances. For instance, 94% of IIH patients exhibit ES on MRI, and 77% of primary ES cases show CSF abnormalities, though clinical IIH develops in only a subset. 4 This overlap suggests shared mechanisms, such as hypertension-driven disruptions in CSF pulsatility, which may compress the subarachnoid space into the sella turcica. 17 Observational data further support hypertension as an independent risk factor for primary ES. 5 While no direct link exists between T2DM and ES, this association aligns with metabolic syndrome pathways, often linked to obesity. In our cohort, obesity—a hallmark of metabolic syndrome—was prevalent among primary ES patients, corroborating earlier reports.1,9 Proposed mechanisms include hypercapnia and chronic CSF pressure elevation due to obesity, potentially driving subarachnoid space herniation into the sella. 18

ES presentation varied widely, with most cases incidentally detected, consistent with prior study. 19 Only 34% of primary ES patients reported symptoms prompting imaging, predominantly headaches (66.3%)—a finding supported by some studies4,9,20 but contested by others. 13 Headache mechanisms remain debated, ranging from sellar traction to IIH. 21 Seizures (15%), though atypical, showed a significant association with primary ES, 13 potentially reflecting shared pathophysiology similar to that seen with IIH, incidental coexistence, or direct causation. Visual disturbances, tinnitus, and sleep apnea were also reported but lacked specificity. Distinguishing ES-related symptoms from incidental findings is complicated by increasing MRI use, necessitating population-based studies to clarify true prevalence.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design carries inherent risk of data inaccuracies and recall bias. Second, incomplete hormonal assessments due to low referral rates precluded accurate estimation of pituitary dysfunction prevalence. Third, the absence of a control group limits causal attribution of symptoms and comorbidities to ES. Fourth, the retrospective observational design, with inclusion of all eligible patients during the study period, precluded the use of a formal a priori sample size calculation. Fifth, referral bias at our institution, being a tertiary hospital may lead to an overrepresent of complex cases, which could potentially inflating CNS symptom reports. Lastly, posterior pituitary function was not systematically evaluated, and therefore disorders such as diabetes insipidus may have been underrecognized.

Conclusion

Our study reveals significant disparities in ES management, driven by etiology and referral context. Despite similar radiological features, secondary ES patients received more thorough evaluations due to overt clinical triggers (e.g., pituitary tumors), while primary ES often perceived as an incidental finding. Targeted referrals for ES correlated with more complete hormonal assessments, highlighting the need for standardized referral pathways to ensure equitable evaluation across all ES subtypes.
