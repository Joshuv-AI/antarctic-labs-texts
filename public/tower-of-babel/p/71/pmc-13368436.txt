Plasma pathways for Low and Middle Income Countries – needed more than ever
Authors: Albert Farrugia, Leni von Bonsdorff
Published: 2026

Plasma Derived Medicinal Products (PDMPs) are a class of therapeutics, some of which are included in the World Health Organization’s (WHO) list of Essential Medicines (EML). PDMPs are mostly indicated for a number of rare, chronic plasma protein deficiencies, such as Hemophilia A and B, Primary Immune Deficiency and others1. These and additional examples of PDMPs are included in the recently published list of Critical Medicines of the European Medicines Agency (EMA)2. These various lists have different aims and are intended for different audiences3. Access to most of the PDMPs on the global market is through a small number of large pharmaceutical companies, extracting PDMPs from, predominantly, paid plasma donors in the United States (USA). The market is currently valued at ca 31.5 billion dollars in 2024, with a projected Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 7.6% between 2025 and 20314. PDMPs are expensive medicines which are generally beyond the budgetary reach of many Low and Middle Income Countries (LMIC) as defined by the World Bank5. Despite these financial constraints, the WHO has recognised the importance of generating a capacity for the provision of these medicines in LMIC6.

In previous work we have surveyed a number of blood systems in High Income Countries (HIC), analyzing the pathways which blood systems in these countries have developed to generate a supply of PDMPs for the public health system7. We have also assessed the stepwise progression of the generation of such a supply8,9 (Figure 1). This approach is particularly relevant to LMIC, for which the abbreviation of this process through direct purchase of PDMPs from the commercial market is not always an option.

In this process, the collection and provision of sufficient and safe blood for transfusion is the first priority of a public blood service. Once this is met, the provision of red cells and platelets through separation of whole blood into components may be considered, if conformant with transfusion needs. A rigorous assessment of such needs is essential, particularly for LMIC where these vary significantly from those of HIC8.

In this issue of Blood Transfusion, Roestan et al10 present an assessment of the operational readiness of five Indonesian blood establishments to supply plasma for the ongoing project aiming to establish a domestic fractionation capacity in that country. The study described the methodology, including a highly relevant questionnaire which was used to assess the readiness of the blood establishments, leading to evaluation across preparation, inventory and transportation of plasma. Validity was overseen through review by external experts from the WHO and the Indonesian regulatory agency. Robust methodological applications are evident in the study, using well-established guidelines for ensuring transparency and reproducibility11. The blood centres are to be strongly commended for achieving this state of preparedness, reflecting a high level of quality in their operations, particularly when considering the challenges involved in progressing from the relatively recent situation when plasma was not utilized for fractionation12,13. It is noted that all five of these establishments are situated on Java, an island of 158 million people, representing 56% of the Indonesian population in 7% of the land area of this vast archipelago of over 17,000 islands. The Indonesian authorities’ policy to initiate their plasma fractionation project by enhancing the quality and supply of plasma from Java in the first instance is a wise one, reflecting an appreciation of the geographical challenges, with a possible stepwise progression to other sources of plasma once the centres on Java are brought into the project. It provides a possible template for other countries faced with similar challenges, particularly LMIC with high population density urban centres.

The establishment of a constant and stable stream of plasma, for which preparedness is only one component, is fundamental to the generation of domestically sourced PDMPs. In our survey7, we noted that fractionation of domestically sourced plasma by a manufacturer under contract is the most widely adopted model in HICs. Indonesia, with the world’s fourth largest population, is justified in including the establishment of a domestic fractionation capacity in its quest for strategic independence in PDMPs. This has been advanced through a public-private partnership, with the choice of a manufacturer committed to technology transfer being made through a public tender. The process has been initiated by the manufacture of PDMPs under contract by the same manufacturer14. PDMPs from this contract are already entering the Indonesian clinical landscape15. The domestic plant, which is projected to commence operations in late 2026, is reported to have an annual manufacturing capacity of 600,000 litres of plasma16. This is insufficient for Indonesia’s eventual needs for PDMPs. Plans for a second plant are currently in abeyance17. The commissioning of such a plant is essential to ensure a continuing, secure and adequate supply of PDMPs.

The product range of this project needs to reflect Indonesia’s current needs while allowing flexibility as these needs may shift, in product types and amounts, with the evolution of the health system. Needs vary between countries, and Indonesia’s current emphasis on albumin18 (Figure 2), while reflecting practice in LMIC, contrasts with the situation in HICs where the need for immunoglobulin (IG) dominates. This situation may lend itself to the potential for commercialising excess IG by export. Other countries have ploughed the revenue from commercialising excess PDMPs into investments in the safety of the blood system19. Continued monitoring of clinical needs and control by the public system are key for such arrangements, as these needs may be expected to change the usage profile of products, including IG, which may be generated in excess in the initial phase of domestic manufacturing.

The path to strategic independence for PDMPs is necessarily dependant on local factors, and no size fits all, as is observed by the plethora of arrangements in HIC7. The establishment of such a path, however, is imperative for countries wishing to provide their populations with these therapies in an increasingly tense and protectionist global environment. The current dependence on the USA is excessive and potentially unstable20, even if the purchase of PDMPs on the commercial market was within the financial reach of LMIC. The Indonesian experience shows how a national effort, drawing on the public-private partnership constructed between the Indonesian Investment Authority and the chosen manufacturer, can establish a base for strategic independence. Generating a path to PDMP supply based on a nationally organised blood system, rather than depending on the poorest of America’s increasingly disadvantaged social classes, should be recognised and embraced as a high priority for public health policy.
