Minimal invasive medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction using quadriceps tendon with de novo technique and arthroscopic guided: A case report
Authors: Demy Faheem Dasril, Randy, Ivana Ariella Nita Hadi, Aswin Guntara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMedial Patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction, the primary procedure for restoring patellar stability and preventing further dislocation, has seen numerous methods and grafts. However, a consensus on the technique and graft remains elusive. This study introduces a novel approach to MPFL reconstruction, utilizing a unique combination of minimal invasiveness, de novo method, and arthroscopic guidance to harvest a quadriceps tendon (QT) graft without the need for a harvesting tool.Case illustrationA 37-year-old female presented to the emergency room with the chief complaints of left knee pain and swelling following the third spontaneous dislocation of the patella. The patient had a few episodes of locking sensation and instability of the left knee while walking, especially while climbing stairs. A knee MRI revealed MPFL tear in the left knee. A modified minimal invasive MPFL reconstruction technique was performed. The IKDC, Lysholm, and Modified Cincinnati scores improved significantly from preoperatively 48.3 %, 49 %, and 51 % to 89.7 %, 90 %, and 95 % consecutively. No recurrent patellar dislocations or swelling were reported during follow-up.DiscussionWhile quadriceps graft harvesting for MPFL reconstruction is typically highly invasive, using quadriceps tendons has consistently shown excellent outcomes. This study, however, takes it a step further by demonstrating that a minimally invasive procedure for harvesting quadriceps tendons, even without specialized instrumentation, can also yield excellent results.ConclusionMPFL reconstruction using superficial quadriceps tendon autograft can be achieved using a minimally invasive technique without the use of specialized instrumentation and has shown excellent knee functional improvement.

Introduction

The Medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) is the primary soft tissue restraint to lateral patellar dislocation. Rupture of the MPFL occurs in over 90 % of patients with lateral patella dislocation [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Recurrent patellar instability is correlated with further patellar dislocation, requiring surgical treatment. MPFL reconstruction is the primary procedure to restore patellar stability and prevent further dislocation [1,4,6].

Many techniques have been used for the MPFL reconstruction, but there has yet to be a consensus regarding the choice of graft, type of fixation, or which technique is deemed superior [1,[6], [7], [8]].

This study's objective was to provide another technique for MPFL reconstruction using a minimally invasive technique de novo and arthroscopic guided to harvest quadriceps tendon (QT) graft without a harvesting tool.

This study was reported following the SCARE Criteria and guidelines [9].

Case illustration

A 37-year-old female presented in the emergency room with left knee pain and swelling after her third spontaneous patellar dislocation. She had a history of previous recurrent atraumatic patellar dislocation that occurred twice in the past five years. After the second dislocation, the patient started to have a few episodes of locking sensation and instability of the left knee while walking, especially while climbing stairs. The patient was diagnosed with systemic lupus erythematosus and had been taking Methylprednisolone 12–48 mg daily, for about ten years.

On physical examination, the patient exhibited no signs of hypermobility joint syndrome or hyperlaxity. However, she displayed patellar apprehension. Her examination was otherwise unremarkable. The MRI (Fig. 1) showed an MPFL tear and rupture of the medial and lateral posterior meniscus, grade II, with patellar subluxation. Fluid collection within the supra-infrapatellar, femorotibial, and patellofemoral bursa was found.Fig. 1Pre-operative MRI of left knee showing patellar subluxation and MPFL tear.Fig. 1

Arthroscopic MPFL reconstruction with quadriceps tendon autograft was performed using a novel technique, which the authors propose to name the Faheem T-incision. The patient was positioned supine on the operating table under regional anesthesia, with a thigh tourniquet. Knee arthroscopy was performed through anterolateral and anteromedial portals to verify MPFL injury and other pathologies. Lateral retinacular release was performed arthroscopically using radiofrequency. A 2 cm longitudinal incision was made on the superomedial pole of the patella, and another 2 cm transverse incision was made 8–10 cm above the superomedial pole of the patella; the latter was used to help graft the quadriceps tendon.

Arthroscopic-guided graft harvest was conducted to maintain visualization and provide better precision when harvesting the QT. The partial layer of the QT autograft was performed; the graft harvested was 9.5 cm long and 7 mm thick, attached distally (Fig. 3). The QT was routed medially toward Schottle's point (Fig. 2), and the graft was then sutured with the retinaculum to stabilize the structure. It was then passed through the retinaculum to re-route toward the Schottle's point. Then the graft was fixated under fluoroscopic guidance with a biodegradable interference screw into the Schottle's point of the medial femoral condyle. Patellar trochlear groove alignment was confirmed arthroscopically via accessory superolateral portal during knee motion, showing no patellar tilt or translation (Fig. 4). A physical rehabilitation program was initiated from immediate post-op to 4 weeks to achieve normal ADL and was continued to gain an early return to sport within 3–6 months postoperative [10]. Discussion

MPFL is critical for patellar stability, resisting the lateral migration of the patella, with rupture of this ligament found in as many as 78–100 % of acute lateral patellar dislocations. The disruption of this ligament mainly occurs during sports or physical activity involving a direct blow to the knee [15]. Other predisposing factors of patellar dislocation include anatomical abnormalities such as lateral patellar tilt, trochlear dysplasia, patellar alta, increased Q-angle, ligament hyperlaxity, etc. Moreover, long-term steroid consumption is known to be associated with ligament injury, as described in a study by Xian Li, who reported that dexamethasone promoted calcification of anterior cruciate ligament cells and caused degeneration [16]. Systemic steroids are recognized to reduce the number of fibroblasts and induce partial necrosis within the tendon or ligament. Our patient, suffering from systemic lupus erythematosus with long-term steroid medications, must be implicated as the most probable cause of the spontaneous MPFL rupture and, thus, might cause impaired healing or re-rupture of the reconstructed ligament [17]. Ushio et al. published a case report of ACL reconstruction in patients with systemic steroid administration who experienced spontaneous ACL injury and reported a satisfactory outcome with no re-rupture after three years of follow-up [17]. Thus, MPFL reconstruction is the treatment of choice in this patient. However, there are no published reports on MPFL reconstruction in this particular case; therefore, clinical experiences for such cases should be shared.

There are various techniques for MPFL reconstruction and many graft choices. There is yet to be a consensus regarding which method is best for the procedure. The hamstring has been the most widely used graft for MPFL reconstruction, showing a high success rate in treating patellar instability. However, the complication rate was reported to be 25.8–26.1 %. The most common complications were patellar fractures through bone tunnels and loss of knee flexion [2,4,7,18]. Thus, quadriceps tendon autograft has been increasingly used in recent days. Shani et al. reported that the QT tendon graft has a greater cross-section area compared to the patellar tendon, and thus offers a more significant bio-mechanical advantage in terms of load to failure and stiffness [19].

However, because of the highly invasive nature of the QT graft harvesting technique, many surgeons do not prefer it as a graft of choice [20]. Conventionally, QT autograft is harvested with a long incision from the superior pole of the patella, extending proximally for about 6-7 cm, which produces an unsightly scar and might delay rehabilitation [20,21]. To address this, the author devised a modified technique for quadriceps harvesting by implementing the Faheem T incision. A minimally invasive technique for QT graft was introduced by Fink et al. in 2014, with around 2–2.5 cm long incision. However, it requires specialized harvesting tools such as a tendon separator, a special tendon cutter (Karl Storz), and a double knife, which can be averted by utilizing a Faheem T-incision. Our technique allowed comparable outcomes to be achieved without any specialized instrumentations. We believe this technique is reliable and reproducible across medical facilities, particularly those lacking the technology for specialized harvesting tools [4].

The technique used in this study does not require a bone tunnel in the patella, therefore avoiding the risk of patellar fracture. Moreover, its minimally invasive approach facilitates faster recovery, fewer complications, and better cosmetic outcomes. This technique does not require specialized instrumentation like harvesting tools and is, thus, simpler and more accessible.

We acknowledge several potential complications with this technique. Harvesting the graft without specialized tools may be challenging in terms of measuring the thickness, and thus minimal invasive conventional graft harvest with arthroscopy-guided procedures provides better visualization of the depth during grafting, to be able to obtain the partial thickness with higher precision. Moreover, reasonable force must be applied to harvest the graft manually. And although we have not come across it. Theoretically, a contracture of the quadriceps might occur. It also highly depends on the operator's skills as we did not use the commonly used assisting tools.

Our study showed significant improvements at the four months postoperative follow-up in Functional scores, i.e., IKDC score, Tegner-Lysholm score, and Modified Cincinnati score and there are no complications found in the study including recurrent patellar dislocation, patellar fracture, and decrease in knee ROM. This result was similar to the study by Lubis et al., Rhatomy et al., Vavalle et al., and Fink et al. [1,18,21,22] Lubis et al., report results in 3 patients who underwent MPFL reconstruction with superficial QT autograft. At the 1-year follow-up, there is no re-dislocation and significant improvement in functional scores (IKDC, Tegner-lysholm, and Modified Cincinnati) [1]. study by Rhatomy et al., showing significant improvement in IKDC and Kujala scores after 2 years follow-up [18]. Another study conducted by Vavalle et al., with 16 consecutive patients who underwent MPFL reconstruction with superficial layer QT autograft showed no re-dislocation or subluxation, and significant improvement of Kujala and Lysholm scores after 38 months of follow-up [21]. The study conducted by Fink et al., reported significant improvement in Lysholm score, Kujala score, and Tegner score from 12 months follow-up of 17 patients who underwent MPFL reconstruction using QT autograft. However, complications were reported; one patient reported with decrease in knee flexion of 10o compared to the other side at 12 months follow-up, and one patient had an infection [4,22]. We acknowledge that longer follow-up would provide a better long-term projection of the surgery outcome. However, all targeted outcomes were achieved at the four-month postoperative follow-up. Thus, despite its minimally invasive nature and utilization of simplified tools, the Faheem technique yields comparable outcomes with those reported in similar studies. This unequivocally underscores the profound benefits of this approach.

Conclusion

Faheem T-incision can be a valuable alternative for MPFL reconstruction. This technique does not require special harvesting instruments, making it simpler and more accessible with no patellar drilling which prevents patellar fracture. It provides advantages such as excellent knee functional score at a 4-month follow-up evaluation.
