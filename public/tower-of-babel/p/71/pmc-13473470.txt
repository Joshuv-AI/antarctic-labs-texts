Vicariant establishment of Dactylopterus in the western Atlantic linked to shallowing of Central American Seaway (CAS)
Authors: Orangel Aguilera, Carlos De Gracia, Félix Rodriguez, Olga Oliveira de Araújo, Philippe Béarez, Paulo Andreas Buckup, Yostin Añino, Yutao Xing, Mateus Andrade Rodriguez, Carla Semiramis Silveira, Ricardo Tadeu Lopes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDactylopteridae are marine fishes distributed in tropical and subtropical waters composed of two genera: Dactyloptena, in the Indian Ocean and western and central Pacific Ocean, and Dactylopterus, in the eastern and western Atlantic. Here, we describe the first fossil species from the Atlantic found in the Late Miocene Chagres Formation of Panama. Through morphological comparisons using micro-CT scans and two-dimensional geometric morphometric analysis, we demonstrate that the specimen represents a new species of Dactylopterus. The paleoenvironmental characterization based on petrographic thin sections analyzed using scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), the geochemical data, and associated microfossil assemblages integrated with an assessment of the environmental data from extant species indicate that this extinct species evolved in a deep-water environment. The finding of †Dactylopterus decrozi n. sp. shows that the genus was already established along the Atlantic margin by the Late Miocene. Current hypotheses propose that dactylopterids colonized the Atlantic through the Tethys Seaway during the Middle Miocene, but our new evidence supports an alternative vicariant scenario associated with the formation of the Panamanian Isthmus. This result is consistent with the previously inferred divergence age of 10.9 Ma that links with the shallowing of the Central American Seaway (CAS).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47283-2.Keywords: Panamanian isthmus, Paleogeography, MicroCT, Flying gurnard, Vicariance, EvolutionSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Ocean sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The family Dactylopteridae Gill, 1861, including the marine fishes known as flying gurnards, is widely distributed in tropical, subtropical, and warm temperate waters1 and consists of two genera: Dactyloptena Jordan & Richardson 1908, distributed in the Indian Ocean and in the western and central regions of the Pacific Ocean; and Dactylopterus Lacepède, 1801, from the eastern and western Atlantic Ocean. Dactylopterus is monotypic and includes Dactylopterus volitans (Linnaeus, 1758) as a valid species. Dactyloptena comprises six extant species, namely Dactyloptena orientalis Cuvier, 1829; D. macracantha Bleeker, 1855; D. peterseni Nyström, 1887; D. gilberti Snyder, 1909; D. papilio Ogilby, 1910; and D. tiltoni Eschmeyer, 19972. Dactylopteridae is a monophyletic percomorph family of benthic fishes3,4 currently included in the order Syngnathiformes5,6. Phylogenetic evidence addressing the relationships of Dactylopteridae first placed them in the suborder Dactylopteroidei as sister of Syngnathoidei in the Percomorpha clade without proposing explicit relationships7. More recently, phylogenomic and paleogeographic analyses place Dactylopteridae in the clade Syngnatharia (= Syngnathiformes) as sister of Pegasidae and suggest that they originated in the Tethys Sea at Indo-Pacific Ocean, ~ 48.5 Ma with subsequent colonization of the Atlantic during the Middle Miocene (14.6 Ma)6.

In modern oceans, the wide geographic distribution of extant Dactylopteridae contrast with their absence of fossil records in tropical America. Sampling bias across various Neogene sedimentary basins, poor specimen preservation or, in the case of isolated otoliths, poor recognizability likely contribute to minimizing the number of available flying gurnard records. In Europe, the fossiliferous record of Dactylopteridae is scarce and only known from isolate bones from the Pliocene of Greece8 and the Pliocene of Italy and Cyprus9,10.

Here we describe the first fossil species of the genus Dactylopterus from the northwestern outcrop of Caribbean Panama, deposited in deep-water sediments of the Chagres Formation (Late Miocene). The new record suggests an ancestral deep-sea origin for the extant Dactylopterus associated with the ancient Central American Seaway (CAS)11 that served as a former biogeographic connection with the Dactyloptena group of species from the Indo-Pacific Ocean12.

Geological setting

The Chagres Formation13 has a thickness of ~ 250 m and unconformably overlies the Gatun Formation14. It is late Miocene in age, extending from ~ 8.3 to 5.3 Ma14–17, and consists of three members: Toro, Rio Indio, and Chagres Sandstone (Fig. 1). The fossil specimen was collected from the Chagres Sandstone Member on the northwestern coast of the town of Piña, Chagres District, Colón Province, on the Caribbean coast of Panamá (Fig. 1). The Chagres Sandstone Member consists of arkosic sandstones characterized by the presence of plagioclase feldspar, volcanic zeolites, smectite, and low calcite content associated with microfossils and bioclasts17. It is of the Late Miocene age (Tortonian to Messinian)14,15,17. The diversity of the fossil marine vertebrate assemblage includes chondrichthyan shark teeth18, teleostean otoliths19–21, billfish cranial and postcranial bones22–24, snapper skeletons17, turtles25, and cetaceans26–29.

Materials and methods

The fossil specimen studied here is housed in the paleontological collection of the Universidad de Panamá, Panamá (UP-Catalog number MUPAN–STRI 55403). It was collected by Carlos De Gracia at the Universidad de Panamá (UP) and Smithsonian Tropical Research (STRI) locality 620040 (N 9.27º, W −80.059º) (Fig. 1). The outcrop is located in the tidal zone, about 1.5 km southwest of the mouth of the Piña River in Costa Bajo, Colón, Chagres District, Colón Province, Panamá, and belongs to the Chagres Sandstone Member of the Chagres Formation (Fig. 2). Morphological comparisons with extant species were based on microCT images of the skeletons of the following specimens: Dactylopterus volitans, MNHN-IC-2003-0100 (Fig. 3), from the Atlantic Ocean deposited in the ichthyological collection of the Museum national d’histoire naturelle (MNHM), Paris, France; Dactyloptena macracantha, MNHN-IC-2006-0255 (Fig. 4), from the Indo-Pacific Ocean; Dactyloptena peterseni, MNHN-IC-2010-0880 (Fig. 5), from the Indo-Pacific Ocean.

A v|tome|x M 300 microcomputer tomograph, paired with an X-ray microfocus CT system (BHGE), was used to capture microCT images of the fossil and modern specimens. The Phoenix Data X Reconstruction v. 2.2 (GE) software was used to produce 3D graphic reconstructions, which included slice alignment, beam hardening correction, and ring artifact reduction. VG Studio Max v. 3.0 and Avizo v. 2021.1 software was used for 3D visualizations, and Photoshop 2020 was used for final plate preparation.

Four petrographic thin sections were prepared from outcrop samples. Two rocks, measuring approximately three cubic centimeters in volume, were processed following standard methods for thin section preparation. The petrographic analysis included descriptions of foraminiferal assemblages, fish otoliths, and bioclastic contents. Microphotographs were taken using a digital system connected to a petrographic microscope.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses were performed on thin sections plated with a thin gold film. The images were acquired using a Tescan Amber focused ion beam (FIB) SEM operating under high-vacuum condition. The accelerating beam energy was adjusted to 10 keV, and the magnifications ranged from 500x to 20 kx, allowing the observation of both general features and microstructural details of the sample components. In addition, energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), including both elemental mapping and point analyses, was conducted to assist in the identification of mineral phases.

The information about the microfossil assemblages from disaggregated rock samples and the geochemistry characterization of the rock matrix where the fossils from Piña Norte were obtained comes from the Aguilera et al.17 database.

Geometric morphometrics, statistical, and graphical analyses were performed in R v4.4.230. Nine specimens of D. volitans from the Western Atlantic (Rio de Janeiro coast) and the dorsal view of the skull of the fossil specimen from Panama were photographed in the Fluminense Federal University to conduct a morphometric comparison of the two species. Dactyloptena species were excluded from the analysis because their skull configuration is different and include then was unnecessary to achieve the analysis objective. Image features were digitized in R with StereoMorph31, including 16 landmarks and 14 curves sampled into 84 semilandmarks (100 points per specimen). When a curve was missing, it was modeled as a straight segment between the anchoring landmarks to preserve cardinality and order. Scale was defined in millimeters so that magnitudes in Procrustes space could be re-expressed in physical units via centroid size32. Shape configurations were aligned using Generalized Procrustes Analysis with semi-landmark sliding, employing a slider matrix that links each semi-landmark to its left/right neighbors and minimizes thin-plate spline bending energy; we obtained Procrustes coordinates, centroid size, and the consensus shape with the geomorph package33. Variation was explored with PCA on Procrustes coordinates and interpreted with TPS deformations at ± 2 SD. To identify spatial differences between group means and individuals, we generated heatmaps of Procrustes magnitude modeled as thin-plate splines, masked to the convex hull. Where appropriate, differences were reported in millimeters, obtained through their multiplication by the centroid size of the reference group. Morphometric inferences in Procrustes space were tested with ANOVA via residual randomization (RRPP) for global effects and pairwise comparisons, reporting Procrustes distances, test statistics, and permutation-based p-values suitable for high dimensionality and weak distributional assumptions34,35. Complementarily, fossil-modern distances were bootstrapped to situate the observation within an empirical reference distribution.

Body size was estimated based on linear measurements of anterior skull width (ASW), posterior skull width (PSW), cranium length (CL), standard length (SL), and total length (TL) of 15 specimens of Dactylopterus volitans, including seven specimens from the biology institute of the Universidade Federal Fluminense and eight from the Ichthyologic Collection (MNRJ) of the Museu Nacional, Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro (Table S1). We used the R packages “dplyr,” “tidyr,” “ggplot2,” and “ggpmisc,”36–39 to produce and visualize two regression models. These regressions were used to calculate the SL and TL of the fossil specimen based on its CL.

Data for environmental variables of six species of Dactylopteridae were obtained from FishBase and AquaMaps40,41. A Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using the environmental variables using the “prcomp” function in R (R Core Team) with catering and scaling of all variables to evaluate their relative contribution to species distribution. Two-dimensional non-parametric density estimates (KDEs)42,43 were computed as implemented in the function “kde2d” of the MASS package44 for bottom salinity (psu), bottom temperature (°C) and depth (m). KDE is a distribution – free smoothing technique that estimates the underlying data density without assuming statistical model42,43. Highest density regions (HDRs) corresponding to cumulative probabilities from 0.1 to 0.9 and 0.05, 0.01 were calculated and probability contours were labeled using metR package45. Final figures were arranged with gridExtra46. Environmental preferences among species were further evaluated using non-parametric KDE to characterize the probability density functions of bottom salinity (psu) and depth (m). Gaussian kernels were applied using the “density ()” function of the base R package30, to provide approximations of each species environmental preferences. Optimal bandwidths were selected using a sequential adaptative procedure testing the Sheather-Jones “bw.SJ”, unbiased cross-validation “bw.ucv”, and normal reference “bw.nrd0” methods to ensure robust performance across varying sample sizes. Depth values were log-transformed [loge(1 + x)] to mitigate right-skewness and reflect the multiplicative nature of ecological gradients whereas salinity was plotted on a fixed linear range (32–38 psu). This approach allowed empirical comparison of environmental distributions among species, integrating this result with the paleoenvironmental information from the Chagres Formation. All code for the analysis is included on the supplementary files.

Etymology

In posthumous honor of Luis D’Croz in recognition of his academic trajectory of marine sciences at the University of Panama and in the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute, and for his contribution to the training of an entire generation of marine scientists.

Diagnosis

†Dactylopterus decrozi sp. nov. is distinguished from other members of the family by having the following combination of characters: (1) in dorsal view, there is a small, deep, and vertical olfactory groove at the suture between the nasal and lateral ethmoid; (2) the nasal is large, triangular, and slightly arched in lateral view; (3) in lateral view, the nasal forms a smooth arch along the anterior portion of the skull, continuous with the frontal; (4) the nasal has a medial foramen; (5) the posterior tip of the supraoccipital doesn’t reach the margin of the deep “U” clef formed by the posttemporals (vs. supraoccipital reaches the margin of the deep “U” clef in Dactyloptena species); (6) supraoccipital has an hexagonal shape, with convex lateral margin forming the contact with the parietals; (7) the posterior outline of the skull has a large “U” shape notch formed by the extrascapular and the posttemporal bones; (8) the parasphenoid has a long ventral keel with large expanded lateral laminae; (9) the ascending process of the parasphenoid is almost as long as its ventral keel and angularly projected backward; (10) the basioccipital is short, slender, and ends below of the supraoccipital.

Holotype

MUPAN–STRI 55403. Neurocranium with maximum preserved length 157 mm.

Type locality and horizon

The specimen was collected in the intertidal zone at STRI locality 620040 (N 9.27 ͦ, W 80.059 ͦ), late Miocene Chagres Formation, Piña Northwestern, Chagres District, Colón Province, Panamá.

Description

Neurocranium dorsal view. Bones are strongly sutured, forming a rectangular and flattened cephalic shield, densely ornamented with a coarse mesh of reticular ridges surrounding rounded small pits; the ornamentation is homogeneous across the entire shield surface (Fig. 6). Anteriorly, the skull is formed by a triangular shield with an acute tip; the lateral-posterior margin of the nasal and the anterior margin of the lateral ethmoids form a deep vertical notch surrounding the olfactory groove; the outer surface of the nasal is slightly convex, ornamented, and features a large medial foramen; the inner surface of the nasal is concave, with a pair of elongate articular surfaces for the palatine at the antero-lateral margin. The lateral ethmoid has a bulbous dorsal projection that stands out anteriorly and laterally and forms the anterior orbital margin; the bone has a pronounced anteroventral projection that forms the posterior rim of the olfactory groove. The dorsal portion of the orbital margin is highly arched, formed by the lateral ethmoid, the frontal, and the sphenoid bones. The frontal is large, hexagonal, and densely ornamented with distinct rounded small pits. The supraoccipital is an elongate, flat, and ornamented bone; its posterior outline is enclosed by the extrascapular. The extrascapular are elongate, slightly expanded and twisted laterally. The sutures between the parietal and pterotic are not distinguished in the massive shield. The posttemporal forms a massive lateral posterior process with a prominent longitudinal central keel, ending in a robust spine. The medial edge of the posttemporal and the posterior edge of the extrascapular form a deep-edged U-shaped clef between the left and the right posttemporals (Fig. 6). In lateral view, the dorsal profile of the skull forms a gentle arch anteriorly and is slightly straight posteriorly.

Neurocranium ventral view. The vomer consists of a small and transversal tooth plate and a lateral laminar bone expansion (Fig. 6C–D). The parasphenoid shaft is narrow and formed by dense bone; it is wedged and sutured posteriorly with the basioccipital. The parasphenoid forms a large laminar bridge sutured with the medial surface of the postemporal (Fig. 6C–D). The basioccipital is short and, posteriorly, it forms the articular facets to receive the first vertebra. The prootic has a divergent large laminar bone bridge sutured with the inner surface of the postemporal (Fig. 6C–D).

Taxonomic position

The newly described species is included in Dactylopteridae by having a neurocranium forming a rectangular and flattened cephalic shield with strongly sutured bones that are densely ornamented with coarse mesh of reticular ridges surrounded by small pits. The posttemporal bone forms a massive dorsolateral posterior process with a prominent longitudinal central keel, ending in a robust spine. The specimen is included in Dactylopterus by having the supraoccipital bone enclosed by the in its posterior margin, by having the external outline at the posterior end of the skull formed by posttemporals and the epiotics that form a large “U” shaped cleft similar to the one observed in Dactylopterus volitans. The extant and fossil species of Dactylopterus (D. volitans and †D. decrozi sp.nov.) can be distinguished from species of Dactyloptena of the Indo-Pacific Ocean (Table 1; Figs. 4 and 5) by the shape of the large indentation in the posterior margin of the skull, which is shaped as “V” in the latter genus.

Statistical results

Linear regressions using skull length as estimator of total length (TL, y = 10.2 + 4.04x) and standard length (SL, y = 15.3 + 3.26x) length on skull in extant specimens of D. volitans (Fig. 8) suggest that the fossil skull represents an individual with an estimated TL of ~ 463.8 mm and SL of ~ 381.0 mm. These estimates fall within the size range of adult individuals of the extant species, supporting the interpretation that the fossil represents a fully developed specimen rather than a juvenile. Standard deviation in the heatmaps among individuals of D. volitans and the Δ change (mm) compared with the fossil species shows that the major differences between the two species are expressed in the anterolateral region of the skull. These regions also show major deformation in the thin-plate spline grids but also reveal differences in the posterior region (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Petrography framework, geochemistry, and paleoredox condition

Thin sections from Chagres Formation rocks consist mainly of coarse-grained volcanic sandstone with feldspar, lithic angular grains, and relatively abundance of bioclast. The microfossil contents of samples from the Chagres Formation include remarkable foraminifera assemblages dominated by planktonic species (e.g., Globigerinoides, Globorotalia, Neogloboquadrina, and Orbulina) and a less abundant but diverse assemblage of benthic taxa (e.g., Bolivina, Cibicidoides, Epistominella, Globocassidulina, Hanzawaia, Lenticulina, Pyrgo, Siphogenerina, Transversigerina, Uvigerina, and Vulvulina) (Fig. 10). This foraminifera assemblage indicates deposition in an open-marine setting influenced by significant biogenic input.

The micropaleontological assemblage is dominated by abundant planktonic and benthic foraminifera: A, Globigerinoides; B, C, Neogloboquadrina; D, indeterminate benthic form;E, Globorotalia; F, Bolivina; G, Orbulina and Globorotalia; H, Globigerinoides and Cibicides; I, Neogloboquadrina; J, indeterminate; K, Globocassidulina; L, myctophid fish otoliths; M, Globigerinoides; N, indeterminate planktonic form.

The rock matrix of the Chagres Sandstone Member, exposed at Tortuguilla, Fuerte San Lorenzo, Piña Northwestern, and Piña Southwestern (Chagres Formation), is classified as arkosic sandstones (Fig. 11). This classification is supported by a previous analysis17 and new complementary specific analytical review of the samples from Piña Northwestern that demonstrated the predominance of plagioclase feldspar (andesine, An50, C1 structure), which reaches up to 75.59% (Figs. 10 and 11). Accessory components include volcanic zeolites (clinoptilolite, up to 9.59%) and clay minerals, predominantly smectite (montmorillonite, up to 10.18%) (Fig. 11). Carbonate phases are minor, with calcite at 3%, while quartz content is notably low (1.66%) (Fig. 11).

The mineralogical composition of the rock matrix indicates a strong volcanic contribution to the sediment supply, consistent with the presence of nearby magmatic sources and the active tectonic setting of the Isthmus of Panama during the late Miocene (Figs. 10 and 11). The high proportion of unstable feldspars, together with zeolite and smectite, suggests rapid deposition and limited sedimentary reworking, followed by diagenetic alteration under conditions favorable to authigenic clay and zeolite formation (Figs. 10, 11 and 12). The scarcity of quartz and the low carbonate content further reinforce the interpretation of a volcaniclastic-dominated depositional system rather than a mature siliciclastic or bioclastic shelf environment (Fig. 10). X-ray diffractograms and quantitative analyses are consistent across all studied outcrops, confirming the persistence of this arkosic–volcaniclastic signature throughout the Chagres Formation exposures along the Panamanian Caribbean margin (Fig. 11). These features reflect a dynamic depositional setting at the interface of volcanic arc activity and marine sedimentation, which played a central role in shaping the paleoenvironmental conditions of the Last Interoceanic Central American Deep Strait (LICADS)17.

Redox conditions of ancient oceans have been reconstructed through the analysis of pyrite, a mineral highly sensitive to variations in oxygen availability51–55. Pyritization in the deep-water sediments of the Chagres Formation provides a robust proxy for interpreting past ocean redox states and sedimentation processes. Pyritization of microfossils occurs via bacterial sulfate reduction in anoxic environments, where hydrogen sulfide reacts with detrital iron. This process is influenced by the availability of redox-sensitive elements, the quantity and quality of organic matter43,51,54,56, and the degree of bottom-water oxygenation53.

A distinctive feature of the formation is the widespread occurrence of authigenic pyrite associated with both planktonic and benthic foraminiferal tests, which are abundant throughout the outcrop sections under persistently euxinic conditions (Figs. 12 and 13). Scanning electron microscopy results shows that pyrite typically fills the internal chambers of foraminifera as octahedral microcrystals (< 2 μm) forming dense framboidal aggregates ranging from 4 to 15 μm in diameter (Fig. 12). Such small and narrowly distributed framboid sizes are characteristic of formation within euxinic–anoxic bottom waters and anoxic sedimentary settings54,57. Examination of individual well-preserved foraminiferal samples (n = 117) reveals that their external surfaces are not pyritized. Preservation of the test walls may be associated with surrounding chlorite; however, the petrographic microscopy shows that these chambers appear uniformly dark and completely filled.

Semi-quantitative EDS elemental maps of foraminiferal tests, internal chambers, and the surrounding matrix show the spatial distribution of C, O, Mg, Ca, Al, Si, Fe, S, K, and Na (Figs. 12 and 13). The analyses highlight the calcitic composition of the foraminiferal test (CaCO₃), pyrite infilling the chambers (FeS₂), and a calcic-feldspar or anorthite-rich matrix (approximately CaAl₂Si₂O₈). Representative EDS spectra (wt%) include framboidal pyrite aggregates (Fe: 15.0; S: 12.8; O: 21.1; C: 40.9 wt%), feldspar (O: 48.3; Si: 20.8; Al: 13.6; Ca: 8.0 wt%), and chlorite (O: 44.6; C: 29.0; Fe: 2.1; Mg: 1.8; Al: 1.1 wt%) (Table S2).

MicroCT analyses reveal dispersed and abundant grains of high radiologic density within the rock matrix, consistent with pyrite grains that could be supporting the interpretation of reducing, organic-rich depositional conditions.

Overall, the lithological, mineralogical, and micropaleontological characteristics of the Chagres Formation collectively highlight a dynamic marine setting influenced by volcanic input and conducive to exceptional fossil preservation.

Macroevolutionary diversification of Dactylopteridae and associated paleoichthyofaunal assemblage

The present-day amphi-Atlantic distribution of the monospecific Dactylopterus genus contrasts sharply with the scarce fossil record of the family in the Atlantic Ocean. Fossil occurrences of dactylopterids are limited to the late Miocene Caribbean (†D. decrozi described in this study) and the Pliocene Mediterranean Dactylopterus sp.8–10, with no records from the African margin, despite the characteristic massive cranial shield that should enhance the preservation potential of these fishes. Furthermore, no fossil representatives of Dactyloptena from the Indo-Pacific region have been reported to date. This scarcity of fossils is likely due to sampling bias across various sedimentary basins, unknown taphonomic limitations for skeletal preservation, and, in the case of otoliths, the difficulty of taxonomic recognition. Regardless of its cause, the paucity of dactylopterid fossils has been an impediment for their use in recent time calibrations of phylogenetic hypotheses in evolutionary studies of the family.

Current time-calibrated phylogenies combined with biogeographic analyses suggest that the family Dactylopteridae originated in the Tethys Sea (Indo-Pacific), with the divergence between Dactylopterus and Dactyloptena occurring during the Middle Miocene (10.9–20 Ma; mean age 15.5 Ma), corresponding to the colonization of the Atlantic through the Tethys Seaway6. Up to the date, there is no fossil evidence for Dactylopteridae during the Middle Miocene to support this Tethyan dispersal scenario, and the fossils of Dactylopterus from the Mediterranean region have a Pliocene age that coincides with the establishment of the modern fauna in that basin8–10. The late Miocene assemblage from the Chagres Formation on the northwestern Caribbean coast of Panama17, including the new record described here, suggests an alternative scenario that fits better with the minimal divergence age calculated (~ 10.9 Ma) from molecular data6: vicariance associated with the formation of the Panamanian Isthmus by closure of the Central American Seaway (CAS)11. The origin of the Dactylopteridae dates back to the Cretaceous when this lineage diverged from the Pegasidae6. This old age provided ample opportunity for the ancestral Dactylopteridae to colonize the Atlantic Ocean since its early genesis. The discovery of †Dactylopterus decrozi on the Atlantic side of the Isthmus of Panama suggests that the divergence between the Indo-Pacific (Dactyloptena) and the Atlantic (Dactylopterus) clades was associated with the volcanism that led to the closure of the deep-sea marine connections during the Late Miocene15,17.

Although extant Dactylopterus volitans typically inhabits shallow waters up to 100 m depth40,47,48, the depositional environment of †D. decrozi corresponds to the deep-sea setting of the Chagres Formation, specifically the Last Interoceanic Central American Deep Strait (LICADS)17. This interpretation is corroborated by the wide depth distribution range of the family, which includes four species that are shelf and shelf edge inhabitants40(Figs. 3, 4 and 5). For example, extant Dactyloptena species in the Indo-Pacific are captured at depths of 565 m by shrimp trawlers50. This idea is supported by the associated fish assemblage preserved in the same rock block as the specimen.

The Principal Component Analyses of environmental variables associated with the distribution of living Dactylopteridae (Fig. 14) suggest that surface salinity, bottom salinity, bottom temperature, depth, and distance from the coastline are the most influential factors determining their distribution. Bivariate plots demonstrate the relationships of dactylopterids with environmental variables: (a) bottom salinity vs. bottom temperature reveals that high-salinity levels are related to the presence of Dactylopteridae regardless of temperature (Fig. 14B); (b) bottom salinity vs. depth indicates that peaks of occurrences occur in shallower environments but that they are still abundant at ~ 250 m and occur in deeper layers (Fig. 14C); and (c) bottom temperature vs. depth shows a preference for warmer waters between 20 and 30 °C. Overall, salinity seems to be the key limiting factor that drives the presence of Dactylopteridae, with species occurring mostly in waters of 34.3–36.3 psu (mean = 35.14 psu) (Fig. 14D, Table S3). Despite a general preference for shallow habitats, Dactylopteridae also occupy deep-water environments, with Dactyloptena tiltoni and Dactylopterus volitans showing the broadest distribution ranges (Fig. 14F), lending further support to the hypothesis that †D. decrozi occupied deep-water habitats during the late Miocene and belongs to the Chagres Sandstone assemblage.

Despite the relatively low fish diversity associated with the sampled block containing the fossil specimen, several well-preserved specimens were directly identified in the rock matrix, including shark teeth (mainly Isistius, Hemipristis, and Carcharhinus), teleost teeth (Acanthocybium, cf. Sphyraena, and cf. Thrichiurus), and otoliths (Diaphus, Hoplostethus, Coelorhinchus, and Steindachneria). These taxa are typically associated with bathypelagic paleoenvironment. Demersal taxa such as Cynoscion (to 800 m40,58 and Umbrina to 300 m40,59 further support this interpretation. These additional records improve previous fish taxa records from the Chagres Sandstone Member, which includes diverse elasmobranchs18 and teleosts such as Bathygadidae, Lutjanidae, Macrouridae, Myctophidae, and Istiophoridae17,19–24.

Paleobiogeographic implications of Dactylopterus decrozi from the Late Miocene of Panama

The record of †Dactylopterus decrozi (Fig. 15) from the Late Miocene holds significant paleobiogeographic implications for understanding the evolution and dispersal of Dactylopteridae within the context of the progressive closure of the Isthmus of Panama. During the Neogene, the Central American Caribbean region constituted a deep marine corridor, the Central American Seaway (CAS), that facilitated marine faunal exchange between the Pacific and Atlantic oceans14,15,17. The occurrence of Dactylopterus in the Chagres Formation shows that this Atlantic lineage was already established along the western margin of the Caribbean prior to the final closure of the Panama Isthmus at ~ 2.8 Ma and prior its presence in the LICADS6,15. Our finding does not question the Tethyan origin of the lineage6; however, rather than a westward, we propose an eastward colonization of the Atlantic via the Panama Isthmus, followed by geographic vicariance associated with a major exhumation pulse that led to the closure of the Central American Seaway at ~ 10.0 Ma and the progressive restriction of the last deep interoceanic passage6,11,15.

Phylogenomic analysis using the definitive closure of the Panama Isthmus (~ 2.8 Ma) as an age constraint shows that colonization of the Atlantic occurred during the Middle Miocene (mean ~ 15.5 Ma) with the minimal divergence age between Dactylopterus and Dactyloptena being ~ 10.9 Ma6. This age estimate was not evaluated considering the shallowing of the CAS and our new fossil evidence from the Chagres Sandstone Member matches geologic evidence of tectonic exhumation and basin isolation processes that characterized the closure of the Isthmus providing a wider compression of these previously calculated divergence ages5,60.

Conclusion

The newly described species †Dactylopterus decrozi from the Chagres Formation represents the first fossil record of the genus from the Caribbean region and the American Continent. It also constitutes the most informative fossil representative of the family Dactylopteridae described to the date. While a previous hypothesis proposes that dactylopterids originated in Tethys and colonized the Atlantic in westward direction during the Middle Miocene, the evidence presented here proposes an alternative vicariance scenario of eastward migration linked to the formation of the Panamanian Isthmus. Despite the limitations of the fossil record of Dactylopteridae, our finding indicates that Dactylopterus was established along the Atlantic margin by the Late Miocene as evidenced by the well differentiated morphology of †D. decrozi. This evidence is linked here with previously estimated minimal divergence age (10.9 Ma) and with the closure of the CAS estimated at 10.0 Ma. The occurrence of †D. decrozi expands the paleobiogeographic range of the group and demonstrates that the Atlantic lineage inhabited deep-marine environments of the Isthmus of Panama prior to the final closure of the marine interoceanic connections at 2.8 Ma. The sedimentary and faunal context of the Chagres Formation, dominated by planktonic macrofossils and bathypelagic fishes, reflects an open deep-marine setting and supports the hypothesis that ancestral Dactylopterus inhabited deep-water habitats before adapting to shallower environmets. Overall †D. decrozi provides key insights into the diversification, distribution, and ecological evolution of Dactylopteridae and underscores the paleobiogeographic significance of the Panama Isthmus formation during the Neogene. Future fossil findings and new phylogenetic analysis will help to refine the paleogeographic framework proposed in this study (Fig. 14, 15).
