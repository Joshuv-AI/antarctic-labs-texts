Nationwide survey of Japanese Physicians on Kampo medicine use in frailty care
Authors: Tatsuya Hosoi, Mitsutaka Yakabe, Yoshitaka Kase, Masashi Miyawaki, Seiji Hashimoto, Shoya Matsumoto, Makoto Yunoki, Soichiro Kondo, Masaki Ishii, Sumito Ogawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrailty is a multidimensional, progressive syndrome that has become a priority for early intervention in aging societies. Kampo medicine, a traditional multi-herbal therapy, may offer an additional treatment option. However, limited evidence exists on how physicians perceive and apply it in frailty care. We conducted a nationwide web-based survey of physicians in Japan (n = 8,351) to evaluate frailty recognition, Kampo prescription patterns, and treatment assessments. Overall, 46.4% reported diagnosing frailty. Clinic-based physicians prescribed Kampo medicine more often than hospital-based physicians (14.1% vs. 10.5%; P < 0.001) and most commonly for appetite loss, malnutrition, and sarcopenia. They also assessed effects earlier (within 2 weeks: 19.5% vs. 13.5%; P = 0.002). In adjusted analyses, Kampo prescribing was more likely among clinic-based physicians and those in internal medicine or surgery, and less likely in larger facilities (≥ 200 beds). Clinic-based physicians were also more likely to rate Kampo as highly effective. In contrast, physicians aged ≥ 60 years prescribed Kampo less often but were more likely to choose it for their own frailty management. Introduction

Frailty is a clinical syndrome characterized by increased vulnerability to stress due to age-related declines in function and physiological reserve1,2. The condition is reversible in its early stages. However, it often progresses with age, leading to reduced physical capacity, greater care needs, and increased mortality risk3,4. It is conceptually distinct from disability. Disability reflects a fixed loss of function, whereas frailty depicts a dynamic and potentially modifiable state that may precede it5,6. As global populations continue to age, the importance of early detection and personalized interventions has become increasingly clear. Notably, frailty is a heterogeneous condition that encompasses diverse physical, nutritional, cognitive, and psychosocial domains, and its clinical definition remains variable and context-dependent7,8.

Exercise and nutritional support remain the primary interventions for frailty and sarcopenia. Nevertheless, there are no approved drugs for these conditions, and only a few investigational agents have shown clinically significant effects8–10. In this context, Kampo medicine—a traditional multi-herbal therapy—is gaining attention as a complementary option, particularly for older adults with multimorbidity11. Formulas such as Ninjin’yoeito and Hochuekkito are used for fatigue, appetite loss, and sarcopenia, with evidence indicating that they are beneficial for muscle mass, strength, and vitality12–14. Moreover, frailty is associated with hormonal imbalances, including reductions in sex hormones, for which certain Kampo formulations may provide complementary therapeutic support15,16.

In Japan, Kampo formulas are covered under the national health insurance system and are prescribed within physician-led care, with more than 80% of physicians reportedly using Kampo medicines in daily clinical practice17–19. Kampo was formally incorporated into undergraduate medical curricula in 2001, with core competencies specified in 201220. This contrasts with many Western health systems, where herbal therapies are typically accessed outside physician-led care. In Western contexts, traditional Chinese medicine and related herbal practices have faced regulatory and educational challenges, including a shortage of trained practitioners and limited familiarity among clinicians with traditional medical philosophies21–23. Nevertheless, some Japanese physicians still lack formal training in Kampo in postgraduate or continuing medical education, and no previous study has comprehensively examined how physicians perceive frailty or how Kampo formulas are selected, dosed, administered, and evaluated in frailty care24,25. This study conducted a large-scale survey on Japanese physicians to assess their recognition of frailty in clinical practice and their current prescription patterns and perceptions regarding Kampo medicine for frailty-related symptoms, with the aim of informing integrative care strategies in aging societies.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional, web-based questionnaire survey targeted Japanese physicians. The survey was conducted by Nikkei BP from November 16, 2023, to November 22, 2023, using the Nikkei Medical Online (NMO) platform. NMO is a membership-based professional portal primarily used by licensed physicians in Japan; as of 2022 it reported over 200,000 physician members, compared with 343,275 physicians registered nationwide in 202226,27. The survey was administered to logged-in members via their secure accounts; the questionnaire was posted during the study period, and participation was voluntary and anonymous. Because the number of members who viewed the invitation was not recorded, the response rate could not be calculated. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Tokyo (approval number: 2024459NIe). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations28. Informed consent was obtained from all participants through their voluntary completion of the anonymous web-based questionnaire.

Questionnaire content

The questionnaire comprised 14 questions, including both multiple-choice and single-answer formats, as shown in Supplementary Table S1. Questions 1–10 assessed the physicians’ recognition of frailty and their prescribing practices of Kampo medicine for frailty-related symptoms. For Q5, we listed eight formulas commonly used in Japan for frailty-related symptoms (tonifying formulas: Hochuekkito, Ninjin’yoeito, Hachimijiogan, Goshajinkigan, Rikkunshito, Juzentaihoto, Kamikihito, and Kihito) to reflect routine clinical practice. Multiple selections and “Other” were permitted. Q9 measured perceived effectiveness using a single-item, non-validated 0–10 numerical rating scale (0 = no effect; 10 = maximum effect); scores of 7–10 were classified as “high” for analysis. Question 10 asked how physicians would personally respond if they themselves became frail, thereby reflecting their individual preferences. Questions 11–14 explored the demographic and professional characteristics of the physicians. For analytical purposes, medical specialties were grouped into three categories: internal medicine-related (e.g., geriatric medicine, respiratory medicine, and cardiology), surgical (e.g., surgery, neurosurgery), and other specialties (e.g., psychiatry, pediatrics, and dermatology). Facility size was categorized according to the number of beds at the physician’s workplace, from no beds to ≥ 500 beds. For a subgroup analysis, facilities with ≥ 200 beds were classified as large-scale hospitals, consistent with Japanese policy frameworks that apply specific classifications and charges to hospitals with ≥ 200 general beds29,30.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. For group comparisons, the Pearson chi-square test was used for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for ordinal data. Subgroup analyses, such as comparisons between clinic- and hospital-based physicians, were performed using cross-tabulations. From contingency tables, we computed standardized residuals (SRs) to identify cells departing from expected frequencies; |SR| > 2 was prespecified as a notable departure. For questions allowing multiple responses, percentages were based on the total number of respondents. “None” and “Other” are shown explicitly for transparency. Analyses of dose (Q7) and timing of effect assessment (Q8) were restricted to physicians who prescribed Kampo. Models of perceived effectiveness (Q9) included only respondents who provided a rating.

Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted for Q5 (any Kampo prescription: yes/no), Q9 (high perceived efficacy (score 7–10): yes/no), and Q10 (separate models for each self-management option, selected vs. not selected) to identify physician-related factors. Models were adjusted for practice setting (clinic-based vs. hospital-based; reference, hospital-based), medical specialty (internal medicine, surgery, other; reference, other), facility size (≥ 200 vs. < 200 beds; reference, < 200 beds), and age group (20 s, 30 s, 40 s, 50 s, 60 s, ≥ 70; reference, 20 s). Unless otherwise specified, between-group comparisons are reported as n (%) vs. n (%); P value, and multivariable results as aOR (95% CI); P value. Percentages are rounded to one decimal place and P is capitalized. Full model outputs are provided in Supplementary Tables S3 (Q5), S5 (Q9), and S6 (Q10). All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 30.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Characteristics of the respondents

In total, 8,616 responses were collected. After excluding 265 respondents who were neither clinic-based nor hospital-based, data from 7,218 hospital-based (86.4%) and 1,133 clinic-based (13.6%) physicians were included in the final analysis. Within the analytic dataset, there were no missing data, and all submitted responses were included in the analyses. Figure 1 shows the demographic and professional characteristics of the participants, with a summary provided in Table 1. Most respondents were aged 40–60 years. Clinic-based physicians primarily worked in facilities without beds (79.5%). Meanwhile, hospital-based physicians were more evenly distributed across medium- to large-scale hospitals: 100–199 beds (14.5%), 200–299 (12.8%), 300–499 (21.3%), and ≥ 500 (29.9%). Internal medicine-related specialties were the most common in both groups (clinic-based, 56.8%; hospital-based, 48.0%).

Recognition and management of frailty (Q1–Q4)

A total of 3,878 of 8,351 respondents (46.4%) reported diagnosing frailty in routine practice, with no significant difference observed between clinic-based and hospital-based physicians (47.7% vs. 46.2%; P = 0.374). By specialty, internal medicine physicians were more likely to diagnose frailty (61.0%) than surgical (47.5%) or other specialties (28.6%; P < 0.001) (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S2).

Recognition and management of frailty (Q1–Q4).

The most common interventions (Q2) were exercise therapy (43.9%) and dietary guidance (41.8%). Hospital-based physicians more frequently implemented exercise (44.5% vs. 40.1%; P = 0.005) and enteral nutrition (9.7% vs. 7.3%; P = 0.01) than clinic-based physicians. Meanwhile, clinic-based physicians more often prescribed Kampo medicine (14.1% vs. 10.5%; P < 0.001) and recommended over-the-counter Kampo (2.9% vs. 1.8%; P = 0.008) than hospital-based physicians (Table 2).

Regarding frailty-associated conditions (Q3) and geriatric syndromes (Q4), hospital-based physicians more often cited sarcopenia, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, stroke, malnutrition, dysphagia, and oral hypofunction. Meanwhile, clinic-based physicians more commonly cited diabetes mellitus and insomnia (Table 2).

Use and evaluation of Kampo Medicine for Frailty (Q5–Q9)

Hospital-based physicians were significantly more likely to report not using Kampo medicine for frailty than clinic-based physicians (60.3% vs. 51.5%; P < 0.001) (Q5). In contrast, clinic-based physicians more frequently prescribed tonifying Kampo formulas than hospital-based physicians, including Hochuekkito (30.5% vs. 25.2%; P < 0.001), Ninjin’yoeito (20.4% vs. 14.7%; P < 0.001), Hachimijiogan (13.7% vs. 7.1%; P < 0.001), and Goshajinkigan (7.2% vs. 5.3%; P = 0.007) (Table 3). Multivariable logistic regression identified independent factors associated with Kampo prescription (Q5; reference categories: hospital-based, other specialties, < 200 beds, and age 20 s): clinic-based practice (aOR 1.40, 95% CI 1.21–1.61; P < 0.001), internal medicine specialty (aOR 3.01, 95% CI 2.73–3.33; P < 0.001), and surgical specialty (aOR 2.25, 95% CI 1.92–2.63; P < 0.001); working in a larger facility (≥ 200 beds) was associated with lower odds (aOR 0.85, 95% CI 0.76–0.94; P = 0.002). Compared with physicians in their 20 s, those in their 60 s (aOR 0.78, 95% CI 0.64–0.97; P = 0.023) and ≥ 70 (aOR 0.68, 95% CI 0.51–0.92; P = 0.011) had lower odds. A concise summary of adjusted associations (Q5, Q9, Q10) is provided in Table 4; full model outputs are available in Supplementary Tables S3, S5, and S6, and are additionally visualized in Supplementary Figures S1–S3.

Kampo medicine was most commonly prescribed for frailty-related symptoms such as appetite loss, low intake, and malnutrition (Q6). Compared to hospital-based physicians, clinic-based physicians were significantly more likely to prescribe Kampo for appetite loss (31.9% vs. 27.6%; P = 0.003), sarcopenia (16.2% vs. 13.6%; P = 0.021), low vitality (16.7% vs. 13.2%; P = 0.002), fatigue (15.3% vs. 11.7%; P < 0.001), and weight loss (14.8% vs. 11.3%; P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Among physicians who prescribed Kampo for frailty, 61.0% used a full dose, 27.6% used a 2/3 dose, and 11.4% used a 1/3 dose (Q7). Although clinic-based physicians were more likely to prescribe higher doses than hospital-based physicians, dose distribution did not differ by practice setting (omnibus χ2(3) = 0.99; P = 0.803). By specialty, the distribution differed significantly (Pearson χ2 (2) = 19.14; P < 0.001). Surgical specialists more frequently used a full dose (70.4%) than internal-medicine–related (58.8%) or other specialties (62.6%). In contrast, internal-medicine physicians more often prescribed reduced doses (1/3 or 2/3), with 41.2% doing so compared with 29.6% among surgical specialists (Supplementary Table S4).

Among physicians who prescribed Kampo for frailty, 57.0% evaluated treatment effects within 1 month and 14.4% within 2 weeks (Q8). Clinic-based physicians were more likely than hospital-based physicians to assess treatment effects within 2 weeks (19.5% vs. 13.5%; P = 0.002), with a positive standardized residual for clinic-based physicians (SR = + 3.1), indicating a greater-than-expected proportion.

For perceived effectiveness (Q9), scores of 5–7 were most common (5: 22.5%; 6: 21.3%; 7: 18.7%). The distribution of ratings differed significantly by practice setting (χ2 = 34.686; P < 0.001), with clinic-based physicians tending to provide higher ratings. The SRs indicated significant positive deviations for scores of 9 (SR = + 2.7) and 10 (SR = + 2.9) among clinic-based respondents. A Mann–Whitney U test likewise indicated lower rating ranks among hospital-based physicians (P < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, clinic-based physicians had higher odds of rating Kampo as highly effective (aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.24–1.83; P < 0.001), whereas physicians in their 40s, 50s, and 60s had lower odds than those in their 20s (aOR 0.70, 95% CI 0.52–0.93; P = 0.015; aOR 0.59, 95% CI 0.44–0.79; P < 0.001; aOR 0.59, 95% CI 0.43–0.80; P < 0.001, respectively). No significant associations were observed for specialty or facility size (P ≥ 0.091) (Tables 3, 4; Supplementary Table S5; Supplementary Figure S2).

Physicians’ willingness to use Kampo Medicine for Frailty (Q10)

When the physicians were asked how they would manage frailty if they themselves became frail (Q10), hospital-based physicians more often selected non-pharmacological strategies, including exercise (60.9% vs. 55.4%; P < 0.001), diet (50.8% vs. 46.7%; P = 0.01), and social participation (28.9% vs. 23.5%; P < 0.001) than clinic-based physicians. In contrast, clinic-based physicians were more likely to choose Kampo medicine than hospital-based physicians (17.9% vs. 12.7%; P < 0.001) (Table 3).

In multivariable models, clinic-based practice was associated with greater odds of selecting Kampo (aOR 1.28, 95% CI 1.07–1.54; P = 0.008), but lower odds of choosing exercise (aOR 0.72, 95% CI 0.62–0.83; P < 0.001), diet (aOR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73–0.96; P = 0.009), and social participation (aOR 0.80, 95% CI 0.68–0.94; P = 0.006). Older age (≥ 40 years) was associated with greater odds of choosing Kampo for self-management (40 s: aOR 2.01, 95% CI 1.42–2.86; P < 0.001; 50 s: aOR 2.09, 95% CI 1.47–2.96; P < 0.001; 60 s: aOR 2.24, 95% CI 1.57–3.20; P < 0.001; ≥ 70: aOR 2.09, 95% CI 1.34–3.25; P = 0.001). Physicians in their 70 s were less likely to select supplements (aOR 0.50, 95% CI 0.31–0.81; P = 0.004). For enteral nutrition, internal medicine and surgical specialties had higher odds of selection (aOR 1.96, 95% CI 1.63–2.37; P < 0.001 and aOR 2.24, 95% CI 1.72–2.93; P < 0.001, respectively). Across most outcomes, facility size showed no significant associations; the only exception was prescription Kampo, for which physicians in large hospitals had lower odds than those in smaller facilities (aOR 0.83, 95% CI 0.72–0.96; P = 0.012) (Table 4; Supplementary Table S6; Supplementary Figure S3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study that examined how the characteristics of physicians influence the recognition and management of frailty, including the use of Kampo medicine in clinical practice.

Our main findings are as follows: Approximately half of all respondents reported diagnosing frailty (Q1), with significant differences in the results based on specialty. Notably, surgeons, although not specialized in geriatrics, frequently reported diagnosing frailty, which is likely due to their involvement in perioperative care31. This widespread recognition indicates its growing relevance across various clinical disciplines32–34. In contrast, physicians with other specialties (e.g., psychiatry, pediatrics, dermatology) may care for younger or less frail populations, accounting for their lower diagnostic rates.

Hospital-based physicians more frequently associated frailty with advanced medical conditions such as chronic kidney disease, heart failure, stroke, and sarcopenia. They were also more likely to report geriatric syndromes such as malnutrition, dysphagia, and oral hypofunction, which are more readily identified during hospitalization or inpatient assessment (Q3–Q4). In contrast, clinic-based physicians more often cited diabetes mellitus and insomnia, reflecting their emphasis on outpatient chronic disease and psychosocial care. These differences illustrate how physicians’ perceptions of frailty are shaped by their clinical environments and patient populations. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports that highlight the evolving and context-specific nature of frailty in clinical practice7. Despite such variation, common features of frailty—particularly falls and malnutrition—were reported across all settings. This finding is in accordance with established conceptual frameworks that emphasize sarcopenia and the related domains of physical, nutritional, and functional decline as central to frailty. These perspectives may also influence how Kampo medicine is applied. When physicians were asked which symptoms they treated with Kampo (Q6), the most frequent answers were appetite loss, low intake, malnutrition, and sarcopenia. These symptoms closely reflect core frailty domains.

Second, as shown in Q2, clinic-based physicians more frequently prescribed Kampo medicine (14.1% vs. 10.5%; P < 0.001), whereas hospital-based physicians were more likely to prefer non-pharmacological interventions. When Kampo was prescribed (Q5), the most commonly selected were tonifying formulas, consistent with basic and clinical studies supporting their efficacy for frailty12–14,35,36. These formulas are traditionally used for fatigue, appetite loss, and urinary symptoms—issues identified in Q4 and Q6. Although international comparisons were beyond the scope of this study, the routine use of Kampo in Japan reflects its integration into a physician-led, insurance-covered medical system17–20. Recent national surveys in Italy and France also indicate increasing interest in complementary and integrative medicine among both clinicians and the general population37,38. However, unlike in Japan—where Kampo is fully integrated into physician-led, insurance-covered care—Western healthcare systems continue to incorporate such practices more gradually. In this context, Japan’s system represents a unique model of integrative medicine, with potential relevance for other aging societies.

In adjusted analyses, Kampo was more likely to be prescribed by clinic-based physicians, those specializing in internal medicine or surgery, those working in smaller facilities, and younger physicians. Notably, the higher prescription rate among younger physicians may reflect recent changes in medical education and the greater inclusion of Kampo in training curricula39. The age gradient likely reflects cohort effects related to education reforms: Kampo entered undergraduate curricula in 2001, and national core competencies were specified in 201220,39. Physicians now in their 20s–30s trained under this framework and may have greater formal exposure and comfort prescribing Kampo, whereas many older physicians trained before these reforms. Professional culture and organizational constraints may also matter—hospital environments often emphasize standardized protocols and formularies, whereas clinics afford greater therapeutic autonomy. These mechanisms may also help explain the divergence we observed: younger physicians prescribed Kampo more often, whereas older physicians were more likely to choose it for their own care. Because this is a single time-point survey, age, cohort, and period effects cannot be disentangled; repeated national surveys and longitudinal cohorts linked to prescribing or dispensing data should evaluate whether attitudes and prescribing change over time.

Among physicians who prescribed Kampo, the prescribed dosage did not significantly differ by practice setting. In contrast, the timing of treatment effect assessment differed significantly (Q8): clinic-based physicians were more likely to assess treatment effects within 2 weeks, likely reflecting more frequent or closer outpatient follow-up. Across specialties, surgical specialists more often prescribed full doses compared with internal medicine physicians. This finding may reflect differences in clinical orientation; surgical departments often emphasize standardized treatment protocols, whereas internal medicine physicians may adjust Kampo dosage based on patient-specific factors such as physical strength and constitution. Perceived effectiveness (Q9) was generally rated as moderate to high, with clinic-based physicians more likely to report high scores (7–10), which is consistent with their higher use of Kampo. In multivariable logistic regression, higher ratings were significantly associated with clinic-based practice and younger age, which likely reflects enhanced exposure to Kampo in recent education. However, these patterns should be interpreted with caution because Q9 assesses perceived effectiveness using a subjective, non-validated single-item measure.

Finally, the physicians’ responses to how they would manage their own frailty (Q10) broadly reflected their clinical practices. Hospital-based physicians preferred non-pharmacological strategies, whereas clinic-based physicians were more likely to choose prescription Kampo medicine. Age-related differences were notable: older physicians (≥ 40 years) were more likely to choose Kampo for personal use (aORs > 2.0; Supplementary Table S6) despite lower prescribing rates and lower odds of high efficacy ratings than younger physicians (Q5, Q9). This divergence may reflect cohort effects from Kampo education reforms (2001/2012), professional norms and accountability in clinical prescribing, and hospital formulary/protocol constraints; life-stage factors may also contribute. Qualitative or mixed-methods studies linking attitudes to prescribing/dispensing data should test these mechanisms.

The current study had several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although the study population was large, the data were self-reported and may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Social desirability bias may have led to overreporting of Kampo use or perceived effectiveness, whereas cautious or conservative reporting could have resulted in underestimation. Anonymity likely reduced, but did not eliminate, such influences. Second, because the number of NMO members who viewed the survey invitation was not recorded, the denominator for a response rate was unavailable; thus, a response rate could not be calculated, and nonresponse bias could not be assessed. Given voluntary participation and the NMO membership frame, coverage and selection biases are possible, and the representativeness of the sample may be limited. Nonetheless, observed patterns were consistent across multiple items and remained significant after multivariable adjustment. Third, the causality between the characteristics of the physicians and prescribing behaviors could not be identified due to the cross-sectional design of the study. Fourth, although physician-related factors were examined in detail, patient-level information, such as frailty severity, comorbidities, and clinical outcomes was not collected. Future studies should triangulate self-report with objective prescribing data and standardized frailty measures in prospective cohorts or pragmatic trials to evaluate clinical outcomes and dose–response, including formula-specific effects. Finally, despite efforts to categorize medical specialties and care settings, heterogeneity within these groups likely remains. For example, hospital-based physicians include a wide range of roles and facility types.

Conclusion

This is the first nationwide survey to examine how the characteristics of physicians influence the recognition and management of frailty as well as Kampo use. Our findings showed that clinical perspectives on frailty and treatment strategies vary substantially according to practice setting, specialty, facility size, and physician’s age. Kampo medicine was more frequently prescribed by younger, clinic-based physicians specializing in internal medicine or surgery. Meanwhile, older physicians selected Kampo medicine for personal frailty management. These results underscore the context-dependent nature of frailty care in Japan and emphasize the need for continued advancement in Kampo-related education and evidence development to support its appropriate and effective use in frailty care among aging populations.
