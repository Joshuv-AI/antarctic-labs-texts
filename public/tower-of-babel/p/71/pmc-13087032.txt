Stress-induced iron-sulfur cluster damage as a conserved trigger of the bacterial stringent response
Authors: Eva Michaud, Lorena Ricci, Claire Lallement, Lars Barquist, Vincent Cattoir, Charlotte Michaux, Régis Hallez, Séverin Ronneau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pathogenic bacteria rely on the stringent response to adapt to hostile environments encountered within the host. However, the mechanisms by which host-induced stress activates this response remain poorly understood. Here, we identify iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster damage as a conserved trigger of the stringent response in major Gram-negative pathogens, including Salmonella enterica, Enterobacter cloacae, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. We demonstrate that Fe-S cluster disruption—triggered by oxidative stress or metal imbalance—limits intracellular pools of sulfur-containing and branched-chain amino acids, thereby activating the (p)ppGpp synthetase RelA. We further show that during Fe-S cluster stress, (p)ppGpp plays a dual role: enhancing bacterial fitness and promoting virulence by upregulating the Salmonella SPI-2 type III secretion system. These findings reveal a conserved mechanism by which pathogenic bacteria integrate host-associated stresses into an adaptive transcriptional response that promotes fitness and virulence, highlighting Fe-S cluster integrity as a central hub for environmental sensing during infection.

Introduction

To survive the hostile environments encountered within the host, bacterial pathogens have evolved sophisticated strategies to sense and adapt to host-induced stresses1. One of the most conserved is the stringent response, which is mediated by the alarmone guanosine tetra- or penta-phosphate, collectively known as (p)ppGpp. This second messenger accumulates in response to nutrient starvation and transiently reprograms bacterial physiology by modulating transcription, translation, and DNA replication2–6. Tight regulation of (p)ppGpp enables bacteria to balance growth and survival, positioning the stringent response as a key adaptive strategy7,8. Consistently, mutants that fail to properly regulate (p)ppGpp struggle to proliferate in host tissues and exhibit poor survival during infection3.

In γ-proteobacteria such as Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (henceforth Salmonella), (p)ppGpp levels are regulated by two enzymes: RelA, which synthesizes (p)ppGpp in response to amino acid starvation, and SpoT, which both synthesizes and degrades (p)ppGpp in response to various stress signals, including fatty acid and carbon source starvation9–12. An emerging body of evidence suggests that metal availability also influences (p)ppGpp metabolism across several species, including Escherichia coli13, Salmonella enterica14, Bacillus subtilis15, Enterococcus faecalis16, Streptococcus pneumoniae17, and Staphylococcus aureus18. However, the molecular mechanisms by which metal availability regulates the intracellular pool of (p)ppGpp remain poorly understood.

Here, we demonstrate that disruption of iron–sulfur (Fe–S) clusters activates the stringent response in clinically relevant γ-proteobacteria, including Salmonella enterica, Enterobacter cloacae, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. We show that multiple environmental stressors, such as oxidative stress and metal imbalance, promote (p)ppGpp accumulation via the RelA protein by depleting branched-chain and sulfur-containing amino acids. This response reprograms transcription to enhance stress adaptation and virulence, through upregulation of the SPI-2 type III secretion system. Together, our findings establish a mechanistic link between Fe–S cluster damage and (p)ppGpp signaling, uncovering how bacterial pathogens sense and respond to environmental challenges during infection by rewiring their physiology to promote virulence.

Results

Manganese stress triggers (p)ppGpp accumulation by restricting the pool of amino acids

Since the stringent response has been associated with metal homeostasis in numerous bacterial species13–18, we hypothesized that fluctuations in metal availability could trigger (p)ppGpp accumulation. To test this, we first identified conditions where Salmonella experiences metal stress, either through starvation or excess, using growth as an indicator of metal-induced stress.

We initially induced metal starvation by growing Salmonella in MOPS lacking magnesium (-Mg), iron (-Fe), zinc (-Zn), copper (-Cu), manganese (-Mn), or cobalt (-Co). Surprisingly, only Mg depletion significantly reduced bacterial growth in our conditions (Fig. S1a–e). Because trace metals could potentially support Salmonella growth, we used specific chelators: DIP (Fe), TPEN (Zn), and BCS (Cu)19–21. Each chelator impaired bacterial growth, which was restored upon supplementation with the corresponding metal, confirming effective metal limitation (Fig. S1b–d). For Mn and Co, we were unable to identify any specific chelators for which the exogenous addition of the corresponding metal could restore growth. Beyond starvation, we also assessed whether metal excess (+Mg, +Fe, +Zn, +Cu, +Mn, +Co) was toxic to Salmonella. We found that excess Zn, Cu, Mn and Co severely inhibited bacterial growth, while supplementation with elevated levels of Mg or Fe (1 mM) did not affect growth under our experimental conditions (Fig. S1f).

Next, we monitored activation of the stringent response under the identified metal stress conditions by measuring (p)ppGpp accumulation using thin-layer chromatography (TLC). To prevent indirect effects of stress on (p)ppGpp accumulation due to reduced uptake of radioactive ³²P, we first cultivated Salmonella with ³²P under non-stress conditions (MOPS). This approach ensured efficient incorporation of the radiolabel into the nucleotide pool, as evidenced by successful labeling of GTP (first lane in Fig. 1a). Then, we diluted the radiolabeled Salmonella into stress-inducing conditions and monitored variations in (p)ppGpp levels. To determine whether (p)ppGpp production was mediated by RelA or SpoT, we monitored (p)ppGpp levels in a relA deletion mutant (ΔrelA) and in a strain containing a synthetase-dead version of SpoT (spoTE319Q) during Mn intoxication22. While no difference was observed between the WT and the spoTE319Q mutant, the absence of RelA completely abolished (p)ppGpp accumulation in response to elevated levels of Mn (Fig. 1b). Since RelA is primarily activated during amino acid starvation by uncharged tRNAs entering the ribosomal A-site23,24, we tested whether the addition of tetracycline, an antibiotic that blocks the entry of uncharged tRNAs into the A-site, could prevent RelA activation under our conditions (Fig. 1c). In agreement with this hypothesis, tetracycline completely abolished (p)ppGpp accumulation in the presence of elevated Mn levels (Fig. 1c, d). Furthermore, supplementation with amino acids completely abolished (p)ppGpp accumulation and rescued the associated growth defect of Mn-intoxicated cells (Fig. 1d, e). Together, these results show that excess Mn triggers RelA-dependent (p)ppGpp production by restricting the pool of amino acids.

Disruption of Fe-S clusters leads to (p)ppGpp accumulation in Mn-intoxicated cells

Previous studies have suggested that Mn toxicity stems from its ability to outcompete Fe for binding sites on proteins that cannot use Mn as a cofactor25,26. For instance, Mn excess has been shown to interfere with heme synthesis, leading to depletion of cytochrome oxidase activity and ultimately to the failure of aerobic respiration25. To determine whether Mn intoxication triggers the stringent response by disrupting bacterial respiration, we measured (p)ppGpp levels after treating cells with potassium cyanide (KCN), which inhibits cytochrome oxidase by binding to its iron center27. Similar to Mn excess, KCN treatment led to RelA-dependent (p)ppGpp accumulation (Fig. S2a), which was fully abolished by the addition of tetracycline or amino acids (Fig. S2b). To test whether this accumulation resulted specifically from cytochrome oxidase inhibition, we examined a strain lacking all three terminal cytochrome oxidases (ΔcyoAΔcydBΔappB). Although this strain exhibited a severe growth defect (Fig. S2c), we did not observe any increase in (p)ppGpp levels in the absence of stress (Fig. S2d). Moreover, this strain accumulated (p)ppGpp in the presence of KCN or excess Mn (Fig. S2d), indicating that inhibition of cytochrome oxidases is not the trigger for (p)ppGpp accumulation under these conditions.

Beyond heme synthesis, we reasoned that Mn might also directly interfere with Fe–S cluster-containing proteins (Fig. 2a). To explore this hypothesis, we performed aconitase assays, as aconitase activity depends on a functional Fe–S cluster28. In agreement with previous work26, Mn excess led to reduced aconitase activity, supporting a negative impact of Mn on Fe–S cluster-containing proteins (Fig. 2b). Interestingly, it has been shown that disruption of Fe–S clusters causes multiple auxotrophies29, a phenotype we also observed under Mn intoxication (Fig. 1e). This effect can be explained, at least in part, by the inactivation of the Fe–S-dependent enzymes dihydroxyacid dehydratase (IlvD) and isopropylmalate dehydratase (LeuC), which are involved in the biosynthesis of the branched-chain amino acids isoleucine, leucine, and valine (ILV)24,30–32 (Fig. 2a). In line with this model, we observed a rapid decrease in the pool of branched-chain amino acids in Mn-intoxicated cells (Fig. 2c). However, supplementation with ILV only partially rescued growth under Mn stress, suggesting additional auxotrophies (Fig. 2d). Since Fe–S cluster biogenesis requires sulfur atoms from amino acids (Fig. 2a), we wondered whether the addition of the sulfur-containing amino acids cysteine (C) and methionine (M) could reduce Mn toxicity. In contrast with ILV supplementation, we observed that addition of CM substantially rescued bacterial growth in the presence of elevated Mn levels (Fig. 2d). These results suggest that sulfur-containing amino acids mitigate Mn toxicity by promoting the regeneration of a functional pool of Fe–S clusters. To test this hypothesis, we deleted hscA, which encodes a chaperone involved in Fe–S cluster maturation33,34, and tested whether CM supplementation could still rescue bacterial growth under Mn excess in this genetic background. As expected, the growth defect of ΔhscA under Mn excess was not rescued by supplementation with sulfur-containing amino acids (Fig. 2e), supporting that cysteine and methionine mitigate Mn toxicity by supplying sulfur atoms required for Fe–S cluster assembly. In addition, the hscA mutant also displayed a higher susceptibility than the WT to Mn toxicity (Fig. S3a, b), leading to (p)ppGpp accumulation at lower doses (Fig. S3c), which emphazise the importance of Fe–S assembly during Mn excess. Remarkably, the combined supplementation of both branched-chain (ILV) and sulfur-containing (CM) amino acids provided the most effective protection against Mn-induced growth inhibition (Fig. 2d). Since reducing environments promote Fe–S cluster assembly and stability in vitro35,36, we hypothesized that the addition of the reducing agent thiourea might help bacteria preserve a functional pool of Fe–S cluster-containing proteins, thereby limiting the subsequent activation of RelA. Consistent with this hypothesis, we observed that addition of thiourea abolished (p)ppGpp accumulation in Salmonella during Mn stress (Fig. 2f). Interestingly, thiourea also prevented the elevation of (p)ppGpp levels in response to KCN (Fig. S3d), which also interferes with Fe–S cluster-containing proteins due to its strong binding to iron atoms37. As a control, we confirmed that thiourea had no effect on (p)ppGpp accumulation induced by serine hydroxamate (SHX), a serine analog that triggers amino acid starvation by inhibiting seryl-tRNA synthetase38 (Fig. 2f). In line with these assays, thiourea specifically alleviated the growth defects associated with excess Mn, supporting the functionality of Fe–S cluster-containing proteins under these conditions (Figs. 2g, S3e). Likewise, another reducing agent—ascorbic acid—also effectively suppressed Mn-induced growth defects (Fig. S3f). Thus, these data support the role of Fe–S cluster status in regulating (p)ppGpp levels in Salmonella.

Next, we investigated if other clinically relevant Gram-negative pathogens could modulate the stringent response in response to Fe–S cluster damage. To test this, we measured (p)ppGpp levels in two other pathogenic γ-proteobacteria, Enterobacter cloacae and Klebsiella pneumoniae, in the presence or absence of Mn excess. Similar to our findings in Salmonella, we observed that Mn excess and SHX induced (p)ppGpp accumulation in these species (Figs. 2h, i and S3g, h). Moreover, supplementation with thiourea completely abolished (p)ppGpp accumulation during Mn stress, but not in the presence of SHX (Figs. 2h, i and S3g, h). Collectively, these results indicate that the regulation of the stringent response by Fe–S cluster damage is not limited to Salmonella but is also conserved across other clinically relevant pathogens.

Iron limitation promotes the stringent response upon conditions inducing Fe–S cluster damage

Having identified that the disruption of Fe–S clusters homeostasis can elevate (p)ppGpp levels, we wondered why iron starvation did not lead to (p)ppGpp accumulation under our experimental conditions (Fig. 1a). One possibility is that pre-existing Fe–S clusters are sufficient to sustain amino acid metabolism during iron starvation, thereby preventing RelA activation. To test this hypothesis, we quantified branched-chain amino acids under conditions of iron limitation and manganese excess. Although both conditions restricted bacterial growth (Fig. S1b, f), the intracellular BCAA pool decreased only in Mn-intoxicated cells and not in iron-starved bacteria (Fig. 3a). Since iron is required for Fe–S cluster assembly, we reasoned that restricted iron availability could influence the stringent response under conditions that destabilize Fe–S clusters and necessitate their de novo biogenesis. To investigate this hypothesis, we first identified a condition that induces a transient disruption of Fe–S clusters which allows bacteria to resume growth after stress. We found that the addition of 0.5 mM Mn significantly extends the lag phase of Salmonella but allows recovery, supporting that bacteria successfully restored a functional pool of Fe–S clusters (Fig. 3b). Under these conditions, (p)ppGpp levels rise rapidly in response to Mn excess and return to their pre-stress levels within two h (Figs. 3c,S4). In contrast, higher concentrations of Mn (1 mM) were bacteriostatic and led to the maintenance of elevated levels of (p)ppGpp over time (Fig. 3b, c). Next, we determined how iron availability might affect the recovery of Salmonella after mild Mn excess. To reduce iron availability in the medium, we used a concentration of the Fe-chelator DIP that does not affect bacterial growth in the absence of stress (Fig. 3d). Strikingly, limiting iron availability using DIP drastically increased the lag phase following mild Mn excess and promoted the maintenance of the stringent response, as observed at higher concentrations of Mn (Fig. 3d, e).

We next wondered whether iron limitation might also extend the stringent response under infection-relevant stresses that destabilize Fe-S clusters. To explore this, we examined the role of iron availability during oxidative stress, which destabilizes Fe–S clusters39,40 and elevates ppGpp levels via RelA by generating an amino acid starvation41 (Fig. S5a–d). In line with our model, iron limitation drastically delayed bacterial regrowth following mild H₂O₂ stress and led to the maintenance of elevated levels of ppGpp over time (Fig. 3f, g). Remarkably, both phenotypes were also observed during more severe H₂O₂ stress (Fig. S5e, f). Given the similar response of Salmonella treated with Mn excess or H₂O₂, we examined whether Mn excess induces oxidative stress under our experimental conditions. Using the PkatG_gfpOVA ROS-specific biosensor42 in the WT strain, we observed robust GFP accumulation following H₂O₂ treatment but no increase after Mn exposure (Fig. S5g), indicating that Mn stress does not generate oxidative stress in our experimental conditions.

To extend our findings to other pathogens, we performed the same approach for Enterobacter cloacae and Klebsiella pneumoniae, both of which accumulating (p)ppGpp in response to Fe–S cluster damage (Figs. 2h, i and S3g, h). As shown for Salmonella, we observed that both species accumulate ppGpp–but not pppGpp–in response to H₂O₂ exposure and that limiting iron availability with DIP delayed bacterial recovery and promoted the stringent response, as observed when H₂O₂ concentration was higher (Figs. 3h, i and S6a, b). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that (p)ppGpp levels are regulated by iron availability, which governs the ability of bacteria to regenerate a functional pool of Fe–S clusters following stress exposure, especially during oxidative stress.

Elevated (p)ppGpp levels reprogram bacterial transcription in response to Fe–S cluster damage

Our previous data demonstrated that Fe–S cluster damage dynamically modulates (p)ppGpp levels. However, whether activation of the stringent response contributes to bacterial fitness under these conditions remained unclear. To address this, we first compared WT and ΔrelA strains under Mn excess. High Mn concentrations exerted a bacteriostatic effect on both strains (Fig. S7a). While WT and ΔrelA displayed similar growth in the absence of stress, the ΔrelA mutant failed to grow at Mn concentrations that still supported WT growth (Figs. 4a and S7b). Similarly, under oxidative stress, ΔrelA was unable to grow at concentrations that allowed WT growth (Fig. 4a), supporting the idea that (p)ppGpp contributes to bacterial fitness under conditions that damage Fe–S clusters. We also tested the ability of both strains to produce an inducible GFP in the presence or absence of stress. While GFP production was comparable between strains in the absence of stress, both Mn excess and H₂O₂ completely abolished GFP synthesis in ΔrelA but not in the WT strain, which sustained substantial GFP expression under stress (Fig. 4b). Next, to elucidate how (p)ppGpp facilitates adaptation under Fe–S cluster damaging conditions, we performed RNA-seq on WT and ΔrelA strains exposed to Mn excess. We selected Mn over H₂O₂ to avoid confounding effects from ROS-specific genes (e.g., katG) and used a mild Mn concentration (0.5 mM) that induces (p)ppGpp accumulation (Fig. 3b) while still permitting transcriptional activity in the ΔrelA mutant (Fig. S7c), thereby minimizing transcriptomic artifacts. Under these conditions, the WT strain significantly upregulated 255 coding sequences (CDS) and downregulated 55 CDS, whereas the ΔrelA mutant only upregulated 33 CDS and downregulated 32 CDS (Figs. 4c, d and S7d–f; and Supplementary Data 1–4). As expected, both strains similarly upregulated mntP (formerly yebN), which encodes the main bacterial Mn efflux pump and is under the control of the MntR regulator43 (Fig. 4e). This confirmed that both strains experienced Mn stress and validated that our experimental conditions still allowed the ΔrelA strain to mount a transcriptional response to environmental cues. Additionally, both strains upregulated the iscR regulon (Fig. 4e), which governs Fe–S cluster biogenesis33,34 and is activated under conditions that compromise Fe–S cluster integrity44. Together, these data ruled out the possibility that the increased Mn susceptibility of ΔrelA results from defective expression of mntP or the iscR regulon.

Despite both strains upregulating mntP and the iscR regulon, the WT exhibited a broader transcriptional response (Figs. 4c, d and S7d–f; and Supplementary Data 1–4). Our analysis revealed significant upregulation of genes involved in substrate transport, metabolism, signal transduction, and stress responses (Fig. 4f). In accordance with previous work45–47, we also observed increased expression of the SPI-2 type III secretion system (SPI2 T3SS) apparatus (Fig. 4g–h), which is essential for intracellular survival within macrophages and the establishment of systemic infection by Salmonella48,49. This induction was exclusive to the WT strain and absent in the relA mutant (Fig. 4h), suggesting that perturbation of Fe–S cluster homeostasis may serve as a (p)ppGpp-mediated signal to promote SPI2 T3SS assembly during infection. In agreement with this hypothesis, a similar trend was observed under mild H₂O₂ stress (Fig. S7g). Together, these findings highlight how (p)ppGpp enhances bacterial fitness by reprogramming transcription to support stress adaptation and virulence.

Discussion

The stringent response is one of the most conserved bacterial adaptive mechanisms, enabling pathogens to sense stress and reprogram gene expression to promote host colonization and survival3. However, the molecular mechanisms by which host-associated stress activates the stringent response remain unclear. Here, we identify Fe–S cluster damage as a key trigger of (p)ppGpp accumulation. Environmental stressors such as oxidative stress and metal imbalance compromise Fe–S cluster integrity, leading to branched-chain (ILV) and sulfur-containing (CM) amino acids. These deficits result from inactivation of Fe–S-dependent enzymes as well as the increased sulfur demand required for Fe-S cluster repair. The resulting amino acid restriction promote RelA stabilization at the ribosomal A-site, triggering a rapid burst of (p)ppGpp synthesis. This alarmone transiently reprograms bacterial transcription toward a stress-adaptive state, including induction of stress-responsive and virulence genes (Fig. 5). Model of (p)ppGpp regulation in response to Fe–S cluster damage.

Environmental stressors such as oxidative stress and metal imbalance compromise Fe–S cluster integrity, resulting in amino acid auxotrophies—most notably for branched-chain (ILV) and sulfur-containing (CM) amino acids. These auxotrophies arise from the inactivation of Fe–S-dependent enzymes (e.g., IlvD, LeuC) and increased sulfur demand required for Fe–S cluster repair. The resulting amino acid limitation activates RelA, triggering a rapid accumulation of (p)ppGpp. This alarmone burst reprograms bacterial gene expression to promote stress adaptation and virulence.

By identifying Fe–S cluster damage as a trigger for (p)ppGpp accumulation, our study expands current understanding of how host-associated stresses are converted into adaptive bacterial responses during infection. Pathogens are routinely exposed to Fe–S-damaging conditions, such as reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS and RNS) and metal fluctuations especially within phagocytic immune cells1. While the stringent response is classically associated with amino acid starvation, many pathogens are prototrophic and capable of synthesizing all amino acids. Our findings support an expanded model in which (p)ppGpp functions as a broad stress integrator that responds not only to nutrient limitation but also to oxidative and nitrosative stress24,41. This model may explain why (p)ppGpp has been implicated in oxidative stress resistance across phylogenetically-distant pathogens16,18,50,51. Moreover, we show that (p)ppGpp accumulation following Fe–S cluster damage promotes the expression of Salmonella Pathogenicity Island 2 apparatus, which is essential for intracellular survival48. Building on earlier findings45–47, these observations suggest that (p)ppGpp integrates stress adaptation with virulence gene expression in response to host-derived environmental cues. The connection between Fe–S cluster integrity and stringent signaling highlights how pathogens exploit metabolic stress to fine-tune global regulatory networks during infection.

While multiple stresses trigger the stringent response via RelA, we observed that the nature of the stress determines whether bacteria accumulate both ppGpp and pppGpp (as observed under Mn excess, KCN or SHX treatment) or ppGpp alone (in the presence of ROS). This difference has been suggested to reflect the impact of the stress on the intracellular balance between GDP and GTP, which serve as substrates for ppGpp and pppGpp synthesis, respectively24. Moreover, stress-specific regulation of GppA, which converts pppGpp into ppGpp52, may also contribute to establishing the ppGpp/pppGpp ratio under different stresses. Given the differential potency of these molecules toward common targets3, it remains unclear how the selective accumulation of ppGpp alone versus both alarmones enables bacteria to fine-tune their physiology in response to distinct stresses.

A previous study reported that the activity of SpoT, the bifunctional (p)ppGpp synthetase/hydrolase, is modulated by iron availability in E. coli13; however, the underlying mechanism remains elusive. Although we did not directly investigate the contribution of SpoT under iron limitation, we hypothesize that its hydrolase activity is reduced under our experimental conditions and may contribute to the maintenance of high (p)ppGpp levels over time (Figs. 3e, h–i). Regulation of SpoT hydrolase activity by iron availability may help prevent a futile cycle in which (p)ppGpp is simultaneously synthesized and degraded. By limiting hydrolysis when iron is scarce, this regulation would allow (p)ppGpp synthesis to proceed without being counteracted by SpoT during Fe–S cluster stress. While direct evidence remains limited, analogous regulatory crosstalk has been observed in other conditions: during fatty acid starvation, RelA responds to lysine depletion53, while SpoT is regulated by an acyl-carrier protein (ACP), which senses fatty acid intermediates9,10. A similar model may apply under Fe–S stress, with RelA sensing amino acid limitation and SpoT responding to Fe availability, but this requires further investigation.

Our findings also raise new questions about the involvement of (p)ppGpp in Fe–S cluster biogenesis or repair (Fig. 5). One possibility is that (p)ppGpp promotes metabolic pathways that generate sulfur and iron precursors essential for Fe–S assembly. However, we did not observe a significant (p)ppGpp-dependent regulation of ILV or CM biosynthetic genes, with the exception of cdsH, a cysteine desulfhydrase that could facilitate sulfur mobilization (Fig. S8a). Alternatively, (p)ppGpp may regulate iron homeostasis, as indicated by significantly increased expression of iron-storage proteins such as FtnB and DPS in WT but not in the relA mutant (Fig. S8b). Unlike Bfr and FtnA, the two other iron-storage protein of Salmonella, both FtnB and DPS have been linked to tolerance to oxidative stress and Salmonella virulence54. Notably, (p)ppGpp also strongly induces the SPI-2 effector SpvB (Supplementary Data 1), which may aid bacterial acquisition of host-derived iron55. Lastly, approximately one-third of the (p)ppGpp-induced genes under Fe–S stress remain completely uncharacterized (Fig. 4f). Investigating their functions could reveal novel effectors of stress tolerance, virulence, or metabolic adaptation.

Altogether, our study identifies Fe–S cluster damage as a physiologically relevant and previously underappreciated trigger of the stringent response. These findings expand the canonical view of (p)ppGpp signaling beyond nutrient deprivation and demonstrate how intracellular pathogens exploit metabolic damage to activate coordinated stress responses and virulence programs. By revealing how host-imposed environmental challenges are transduced into adaptive gene expression via (p)ppGpp, our work provides new insight into bacterial pathogenesis and highlights (p)ppGpp metabolism as a promising target for antimicrobial development.

Methods

Bacterial strains and plasmids

Oligonucleotides, strains, and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables 1, 2, and 3. Additional construction details are provided in the Supplementary Methods. E. coli EC100D pir-116 was used for cloning purposes with pTOX2 and grown aerobically at 37 °C in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth (Sigma) supplemented with 2% glucose. Salmonella strains were grown aerobically at 37 °C in either LB or MOPS minimal medium (40 mM MOPS buffer, 0.4 mM tricine, 0.4 mM K₂HPO₄, 10 µM FeSO₄•7H₂O, 9.5 mM NH₄Cl, 276 µM K₂SO₄, 500 nM CaCl₂, 525 µM MgCl₂, 50 mM NaCl, 2.9 nM (NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄•4H₂O, 400 nM H₃BO₃, 30 nM CoCl₂, 9.6 nM CuSO₄, 80.8 nM MnCl₂, and 9.74 nM ZnSO₄; pH 7.2), supplemented with 0.2% glucose as the sole carbon source (referred to as MOPS throughout the manuscript). When indicated, the medium was supplemented with additional compounds at the following concentrations: amino acid (aa) mixture (40 µg/ml of each aa), thiourea (100 mM), reduced glutathione (50 mM), ascorbic acid (50 mM), serine hydroxamate (SHX, 0.4 mg/ml), MnSO₄ (0.5 or 1 mM), H₂O₂ (0.5, 1, or 2 mM), MgCl₂ (1 mM), FeSO₄ (1 mM), ZnSO₄ (1 mM), CuSO₄ (1 mM), CoCl₂ (1 mM), MnCl₂ (1 mM), N,N,N = ,N = -tetrakis(2-pyridylmethyl)ethylenediamine (TPEN, 200 µM), bathocuproinedisulfonic acid disodium salt (BCS, 100 mM) and 2,2’-dipyridyl (DIP, 0.1 mM or 1 mM). Electrocompetent cells were used for the transformation of E. coli and Salmonella. Antibiotics were used at the following concentrations where appropriate: ampicillin (100 µg/ml), kanamycin (50 µg/ml), and chloramphenicol (34 µg/ml).

Genomic mutations were construct with pTOX2 vectors as previously described56. Briefly, the E. coli donor strain was mixed with the Salmonella recipient strain at a 1:5 or 1:20 ratio in a total volume of 1 ml. Cells were centrifuged at 6,000 g for 2 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded. The remaining 100 µl was plated on LB agar containing 0.3 mM diaminopimelic acid (DAP) and 2% glucose and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, cells were scraped and restreaked on LB plates containing 2% glucose and chloramphenicol. Single Salmonella colonies were then restreaked on LB with 2% rhamnose. Finally, chloramphenicol-sensitive (CamS) clones were screened by PCR.

Growth assays

Bacteria were grown overnight in MOPS. The overnight culture (10 µl) was diluted into 500 µl of fresh MOPS, supplemented with additional compounds when indicated. 200 µl of each sample was transferred into a flat-bottom 96-well plate (Greiner). Optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀nm) was measured every hour for 15 h at 37 °C using a plate reader (BioTek Epoch 2). For panel 4a, Salmonella strains (WT or ΔrelA) were grown overnight in MOPS. Then, 10 µl of the overnight culture were diluted in 500 µl of MOPS supplemented with MnSO4 (0, 0.5 or 1 mM) or H2O2 (0, 1 or 2 mM) in 1.5 ml eppendorf. Samples were incubated in thermal shaker at 37 °C with shaking for 15 h before OD₆₀₀nm measurement.

Measurement of (p)ppGpp

Bacteria were grown overnight in MOPS. The overnight culture was then diluted 1:50 in fresh MOPS (100 µl of overnight culture into 5 ml) and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 90 min. Next, 20 µl of a 1 mCi KH₂³²PO₄ solution was added to 480 µl of bacterial culture, and the cells were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 60 min. Following incubation, cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 16,000 g for 3 min and resuspended in 25 µl of MOPS. For stress treatment, 5 µl of resuspended cells were diluted in 100 µl of MOPS containing the indicated stress conditions and incubated at 37 °C with shaking for 30 min. After incubation, 40 µl of ice-cold 50% formic acid was added to each sample, thoroughly mixed, followed by an additional 30-minute incubation on ice. Samples were then centrifuged at 16,000 g for 5 minutes and then returned to ice. For thin-layer chromatography (TLC), 2.5 µl x4 of each sample was spotted onto a polyethyleneimine (PEI) cellulose plate (Macherey-Nagel). The PEI plates were developed in 1.25 M KH₂PO₄ (pH 3.4) at room temperature. Finally, TLC plates were air dried, wrapped in plastic and exposed against MS Storage Phosphor Screen (GE Healthcare) overnight. Phosphor screen were visualized using an Amersham Typhoon (Cytiva). Spot intensities were quantified with ImageJ software. Background was always subtracted from the original value before further calculation.

Aconitase assays

Aconitase activity was measured using an Aconitase Activity Assay Kit (Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. Briefly, WT bacteria were grown in MOPS medium, and 1 mL of culture was diluted into 10 mL of fresh MOPS and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C with shaking. Cultures were then divided into two 5 mL aliquots, and 1 mM MnSO₄ was added to one aliquot while the other served as the untreated control. After 30 min incubation at 37 °C with shaking, 1 mL of cells from each condition was harvested, washed once with cold PBS, and resuspended in 100 µL of assay buffer 7. Following a 20 min incubation on ice, samples were supplemented with 10 µL of aconitase activation solution and incubated on ice for 60 min. Activated samples (50 µL) were mixed with 50 µL of reaction mix and incubated at 25 °C for 60 min, after which 10 µL of developer II was added and samples were incubated for an additional 10 min at 25 °C. Reactions were centrifuged for 3 min at 16,000 g, and 50 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate for measurement at 450 nm. Blank values were subtracted from raw OD450nm readings, and results were normalized to the control.

Branched-Chain Amino Acids (BCAA) quantification

Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) were quantified using a Branched-Chain Amino Acid Assay Kit (Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with minor modifications. Briefly, wild-type (WT) bacteria were grown in MOPS medium, and 1 mL of culture was diluted into 10 mL of fresh MOPS and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C with shaking. Cultures were then treated with or without 1 mM MnSO₄ or 1 mM 2,2′-dipyridyl (DIP) and further incubated at 37 °C with shaking. At the indicated time points, 1 mL of cells from each condition was harvested, washed once with cold PBS, resuspended in 200 µL of assay buffer 4, and lysed using a FastPrep instrument (2 × 30 s at 6.0 m s⁻¹). Lysates were centrifuged for 3 min at 16,000 g, and 50 µL of the resulting supernatant was mixed with 50 µL of reaction mix in a 96-well plate and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Blank values were subtracted from raw OD450nm readings, and results were normalized to the untreated control.

Fluorescence accumulation in Salmonella

Salmonella strains (WT or ΔrelA) containing the pFCcGi reporter were grown overnight in MOPS with ampicillin. Bacteria were then diluted (1:50) in MOPS containing 0.2 % glycerol instead of glucose and grow aerobically for 90 min at 37 °C. Then, media was supplemented with MnSO4 (0.5 or 1 mM) or H2O2 (0.5 or 1 mM). After 30 m of stress, 0.2 % arabinose was added to the media to induce the production of GFP. Each sample was collected after 30 min of induction and stored at 4 °C in PBS prior analysis on an Attune NxT flow cytometer.

Promoter activity assay

Salmonella strain containing the PkatG_gfpOVA was grown overnight in MOPS and ampicillin. Bacteria were then diluted in MOPS (20 µl overnight in 1 ml) and grow aerobically for 90 min at 37 °C. Then, media was supplemented with 0.5 mM H2O2 and/or 0.1 mM DIP. Each sample was collected after 30 min of stress exposure and stored at 4 °C in PBS prior analysis on an Attune NxT flow cytometer.

Survival assay

Salmonella strains (WT or ΔrelA) were grown overnight in MOPS. Bacteria were then diluted in MOPS (1:50) and grow aerobically for 90 min at 37 °C. Then, media was supplemented with 1 mM MnSO4 for 4 h. Ten-fold serial dilutions in PBS were performed and drops of 20 μl were plated on LB agar to determine the number of CFU before (T0) and after Mn treatment (T4).

RNA-Seq and analysis

Salmonella strains (WT or ΔrelA) were grown overnight in MOPS in three biological replicates. Bacteria were then diluted in MOPS (1:50) and grow aerobically for 90 min at 37 °C to reach an OD600nm of 0.2-0.3 in 50 ml. Then, media was supplemented with 0.5 MnSO4. After 30 min of stress, bacteria were centrifuged and the pellets froze at −80 °C. RNA extraction was performed using the zymo kit for quick RNA extraction. DNase treatment was carried out using the Turbo DNase kit from Invitrogen. After quality checking with a TapeStation (Agilent), RNA samples were sent to Genewiz for rRNA depletion, followed by cDNA library preparation and strand-specific RNA sequencing. Raw data were paired, trimmed, and mapped against the reference genome of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (accession numbers CP001362 and CP001363) using CLC software (Qiagen). Differential expression analysis (DE) and volcano plots were generated using CLC, with a chosen cutoff of log2 fold change and FDR < 0.05 to narrow down the deregulated transcripts. Unsupervised heatmaps were generated using the mean of Transcript Per Million (TPM) in log10 of each condition. RNA-seq analysis comparing MOPS medium to excess manganese (0.5 mM MnSO₄) is presented in Supplementary Data 1 (WT) and Supplementary Data 2 (ΔrelA). RNA-seq analysis comparing WT to ΔrelA is presented in Supplementary Data 3 (MOPS medium) and Supplementary Data 4 (0.5 mM MnSO₄). For qRT-PCR validation, expression levels of selected mRNA targets were quantified by one-step quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) using the Power SYBR® Green RNA-to-CT™ 1-Step Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Relative transcript levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method and normalized to the housekeeping gene rfaH.
