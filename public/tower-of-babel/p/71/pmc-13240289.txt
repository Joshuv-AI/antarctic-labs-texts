Prenatal Diagnosis of Short Rib‐Polydactyly Syndrome (SRPS), DYNC2I1‐Related: Identification of a Novel Homozygous Missense Variant by Clinical Exome Sequencing
Authors: Shiyao Xian, Qi Zhao, Yanfang Li, Lihua Li, Zhongwei Li, Miaolian Zhang, Suihua Feng, Qiang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTShort rib‐polydactyly syndrome (SRPS), with or without polydactyly, encompasses a range of autosomal recessive skeletal dysplasias characterized by shortened limbs, narrow thorax, and visceral abnormalities. Accurate genetic testing is crucial for the diagnosis and treatment of different clinical subtypes. This study investigates gene variants associated with the clinical features in a Chinese family affected by short rib‐polydactyly syndrome (SRPS), DYNC2I1‐related. A retrospective analysis of two adverse pregnancy histories in the family was conducted using genetic testing methods, including clinical exome sequencing, Sanger sequencing, amniotic fluid cell karyotype analysis, and chromosomal microarray analysis. Variants detected in the family were scored and classified according to ACMG guidelines, and a systematic analysis of clinically relevant variants was performed. A homozygous variant in the DYNC2I1 gene, NM_018051.5:c.1526A>T p.(Asp509Val), was identified in both fetuses affected by polydactyly and short rib‐polydactyly syndrome in the family and inherited from asymptomatic parents, consistent with a recessive inheritance pattern. This variant has not been reported in HGMD or PubMed and is classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines. Based on PolyPhen‐2 and PROVEAN pathogenicity predictions, it is considered a deleterious variant. We report a novel homozygous variant in the DYNC2I1 gene inherited from asymptomatic parents. This finding expands the spectrum of genetic variants seen in SRPS patients related to DYNC2I1 and demonstrates the feasibility of clinical exome sequencing for prenatal diagnosis of SRPS.

Introduction

Primary cilia are antenna‐like organelles on eukaryotic cells that sense external signals and relay developmental pathways [1, 2]. Their complex structure involves many proteins [3], and defects in these proteins cause ciliary disorders. These diseases are clinically and genetically diverse, affecting multiple organs like the brain, kidneys, and skeleton, sometimes with features like organ reversal and polydactyly [4].

Skeletal ciliopathies are rare genetic disorders from primary cilia defects. Short rib thoracic dysplasia (SRTD) includes skeletal dysplasias with short limbs, narrow thorax, and visceral abnormalities, such as short rib‐polydactyly syndrome (SRPS), asphyxiating thoracic dystrophy (ATD), Ellis‐van Creveld syndrome (EVC), Mainzer‐Saldino syndrome (MZSDS), and cranioectodermal dysplasia (CED). SRTD varies from mild to lethal, mostly autosomal recessive [5, 6, 7]. SRPS has complex symptoms and genetic heterogeneity, making diagnosis challenging [8, 9], with classification based on causative genes [10]. There are 19 reported SRPS causative genes in OMIM, and accurate genetic diagnosis is crucial for clinical classification, treatment, and prenatal diagnosis.

In this study, we describe a Han Chinese family with a history of four adverse pregnancy outcomes, demonstrating that the ultrasound findings of the fetuses in the last two pregnancies were consistent with SRPS. We performed prenatal diagnosis using a series of genetic tests, including clinical exome sequencing; ultimately, we identified a novel homozygous variant in the DYNC2I1 gene. We systematically analyzed the pathogenicity of this variant according to ACMG guidelines and discussed the feasibility of using clinical exome sequencing for prenatal diagnosis related to this variant.

Case History/Examination

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of cases from the prenatal diagnosis center of Jiangmen Central Hospital. A 31‐year‐old pregnant woman has a history of four pregnancies, including one biochemical pregnancy and one early pregnancy loss. The last two pregnancies showed ultrasound findings consistent with the short rib‐polydactyly syndrome (SRPS) phenotype. The woman and her husband were both healthy and not consanguineous.

Fetus 1: Conceived via IVF‐ET in 2021, ultrasound at 12 weeks showed short fetal long bones (Figure 1A). At 16 weeks, ultrasound showed a narrow thorax (Figure 1B). Amniocentesis was performed at 16 + 4 weeks, followed by clinical exome sequencing, chromosomal karyotyping, and microarray analysis of the amniotic fluid. At 18 + 3 weeks, ultrasound measurements included biparietal diameter 42 mm (+0.33 SD), head circumference 161 mm (+1.36 SD), abdominal circumference 127 mm (−0.37 SD), femur length 16 mm (−5.34 SD) [11, 12, 13] (Figure 1C), and humerus length 14 mm (−6.41 SD) (Figure 1D). Given these circumstances, although all genetic testing results were either negative or indicated variants of uncertain significance, the family elected to terminate the pregnancy based on serial fetal ultrasound findings.

Fetus 2: Conceived via IVF‐ET in 2022, ultrasound at 12 + 3 weeks showed fetal limbs fixed in position, with long bones slightly short and curved (Figure 2A). At 19 + 2 weeks, ultrasound measurements included biparietal diameter 45 mm (+0.32 SD), head circumference 168 mm (+0.86 SD), abdominal circumference 133 mm (−0.84 SD), femur length 17 mm (−6.07 SD) (Figure 2B), and humerus length 16 mm (−6.43 SD) (Figure 2C), with abnormal morphology of both hands and a narrow thorax. Amniocentesis was performed at 19 + 1 weeks of gestation, followed by clinical exome sequencing, chromosomal karyotyping analysis, and microarray analysis on the amniotic fluid. At 19 + 2 weeks, before the genetic testing results were obtained, the family decided to terminate the pregnancy based on the results of continuous fetal ultrasound examinations.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis and Investigations)

Clinical exome sequencing was performed using a panel of 5289 OMIM genes, 865 of which were identified by searching the HPO database for diseases associated with SRTD and skeletal development. After obtaining parental consent, genomic DNA was extracted from amniotic fluid and peripheral blood of the parents using a blood DNA extraction kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Approximately 1.5 μg of genomic DNA was fragmented using a Q800R ultrasonic disruptor (Qsonica). Targeted regions of relevant genes were enriched using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) for hybrid capture, followed by PCR amplification with indexed primers. Paired‐end libraries were then prepared according to the Illumina library preparation protocol and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Base calling, image data conversion, and deduplication were performed using CASAVA version 2.0 (Illumina), which is part of Illumina's preliminary data analysis pipeline. Low‐quality reads (Phred quality score < 20) were removed before deduplication. Sequences were aligned to the hg19 reference genome using NextGENe software (SoftGenetics, Penn State University) with recommended standard settings for single nucleotide variant and insertion/deletion discovery. This study utilized the hg19 reference genome for the following reasons: the research commenced in 2021, with laboratory reagents and procedures based on hg19 to ensure consistency and comparability with historical data; the analytical software NextGENe demonstrated superior compatibility with hg19 at that time; and relevant clinical annotation databases were more robust on hg19. Variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 1% in Asian populations were filtered out, and synonymous variants were also excluded; both filters were applied sequentially. Variant annotations were further confirmed through literature and population databases, including GnomAD, ClinVar, HGMD, and OMIM. The pathogenicity of candidate gene variants was assessed using multiple computational algorithms that predict variant impact, including SIFT, PolyPhen‐2, MutationTaster, and CADD. Variant pathogenicity was evaluated according to the ACMG guidelines [12].

Detection of copy number variations (CNVs) was based on the coverage depth of exome sequencing. The relative coverage depth of sex‐matched samples was first calculated, typically including at least 5 males and 5 females, for normalization without GC correction. Z‐scores were calculated for mapped reads in each 100‐bp window based on a two‐tailed normal distribution. When the Z‐score was below 2 (suspicious deletion) or above 2 (duplication), it was flagged for further manual review.

We validated candidate causative gene variants in the patient and her parents by Sanger sequencing using an ABI 3730xl sequencer. Primers were designed using Primer‐BLAST. The human reference genome version was GRCh37/hg19. PCR amplification was performed in a thermal cycler using commercial PCR reagents (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and then tested according to the Affymetrix Cytoscan 750 K detection manual, which included 550,000 oligonucleotide probes and 750,000 single nucleotide polymorphism probes. Data were analyzed using Chas v4.2. CNVs greater than 100 kb or containing more than 50 consecutive probes were considered clinically pathogenic, with reference annotations based on the GRCh37 (hg19) genome. CNVs were classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variants of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, and benign according to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics.

Amniotic fluid (15 mL) was collected via ultrasound‐guided amniocentesis and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 8 min. After removing the supernatant, the remaining fluid was mixed and inoculated into two 3.5 mL culture bottles containing AmnioMAX II complete culture medium. The culture medium was changed daily for approximately 6 or 7 days. When more than 15 cell clones were obtained, cells were harvested and subjected to Giemsa staining. For each sample, 5 mitotic figures were analyzed, with each figure analyzed 30 times.

Results and Outcome

Chromosomal karyotyping analysis and microarray analysis of both fetuses revealed no clinically significant numerical or structural abnormalities, ruling out aneuploidy and pathogenic copy number variations as the cause of the phenotype. Clinical exome sequencing results of amniotic fluid from both fetuses revealed a homozygous missense variant in the DYNC2I1 gene, NM_018051.5:c.1526A>T p.(Asp509Val), which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure 3). According to ACMG guidelines, this variant meets the following evidence criteria: (1) PM2_Supporting: The fetuses inherited the homozygous variant from both parents, consistent with the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. This variant was not reported in published literature, the HGMD database, or the 1000 Genomes Project database. It exhibited extremely low frequency in normal reference population databases, such as gnomAD V4.1.0 MAF 0.000004338 (East Asia 0.0001560, 0 in other populations), and gnomAD v2.1.1 MAF 0.00001427 (East Asia 0.0002048, 0 in other populations), with no homozygous variants reported. (2) PM3_Supporting: Both fetuses are homozygous, exhibiting clinical phenotypes consistent with DYNC2I1 gene‐related disorders. The population frequency of this variant meets PM2 criteria, scoring 0.5 points under PM3 guidelines, meeting the threshold for supporting strength. (3) PP3_Moderate: The score obtained from the pathogenicity prediction program REVEL is 0.91, falling within the range of (0.773, 0.932), indicating moderate strength of evidence. (4) PP1: In this family, the parents are heterozygous for the mutation, and both fetuses with the same phenotype are homozygous. The number of times the mutation and disease cosegregated is 1. (5) PP4: The first fetus exhibited a phenotype of short limbs and narrow chest, while the second fetus showed a phenotype of short limbs, narrow chest, and abnormal morphology, highly consistent with the clinical phenotype of SRPS. Therefore, based on the above evidence, pathogenicity is classified as likely pathogenic.

Sanger sequencing was performed on the DYNC2I1 gene. DNA was extracted from amniotic fluid and the blood of the parents. The results showed that both fetuses have a homozygous missense variant, a specific type of genetic variant, c.1526A>T (which results in the amino acid change p.(Asp509Val)) in exon 12, while both parents have a heterozygous variant.

This variant results in the substitution of aspartic acid at position 509 of the DYNC2I1 protein with valine. Compared to the wild‐type p.(Asp509), the p.(Val509) variant causes the existing hydrogen bond between the 509th and 510th amino acids to disappear (indicated by the red dot in Figure 4A), suggesting that this variant might cause local structural instability in the protein, affecting its function (Figure 4B). Additionally, we mapped the predicted electrostatic potential of the DYNC2I1 wild‐type p.(Asp509) (Figure 5A) and p.(Val509) (Figure 5B) onto the surface using a color gradient from red (negative) to blue (positive). The results of the electrostatic potential calculations show that the region near the variant site partially shifts from negative to neutral, which could weaken interactions with positively charged regions of nucleic acid molecules.

Schematic diagram of the three‐dimensional structure of wild‐type (A) and mutated (B) DYNC2I1 proteins. We conducted a three‐dimensional structural analysis of the unreported DYNC2I1 p.(Asp509Val) variant using PyMOL. Compared to the wild‐type version, the p.Asp509Val variant causes existing hydrogen bonds (red dots in figure A) to disappear, which suggests that this variant might cause local structural instability in the protein and that could affect how the protein works.

Schematic diagram showing the charge of the DYNC2I1 protein before and after the variant, using PyMOL to map the predicted electrostatic potential of the DYNC2I1 wild‐type (A) and the p.(Asp509Val) (B) onto its surface, with a color gradient from red (for negative charge) to blue (for positive charge). Based on the electrostatic potential calculation results, some areas around the variant site shift from negative to neutral.

At the request of the pregnant woman, both pregnancies were terminated. Fetus 1 was not videotaped after the induced labor without the parents' consent. With the parents' consent, Fetus 2 underwent an external examination and was videotaped following the induced labor. The fetus 2 exhibited features including mild macrocephaly, hypertelorism, low‐set ears, and increased nuchal skin thickness [13]. The fetus had a narrow thorax and markedly shortened limbs. Postaxial polydactyly was present in both hands, with six digits on the right foot and five toes on the left foot (Figure 6). DNA, extracted from the placenta and subjected to Sanger sequencing, also confirmed the DYNC2I1 gene: c.1526A>T p.(Asp509Val) homozygous variant.

Induced labor results of fetus 2 showed that the fetal head was a bit larger, the distance between the eyes was a bit wider, the ears were set low, the skin at the back of the neck was thickened, the chest was narrow, the limbs were really short, both hands had six fingers each, and the right foot had six toes, while the left foot had five.

Discussion and Conclusion

Short rib‐polydactyly syndrome (SRPS), with or without polydactyly, is a group of autosomal recessive skeletal ciliopathies. SRPS is characterized by thoracic constriction, short ribs, short tubular bones, and a ‘trident’ appearance of the acetabular roof. Various forms of SRPS exhibit phenotypic overlap in visceral malformations and metaphyseal appearances. Nonskeletal manifestations may include cleft lip/palate and abnormalities of major organs (such as the brain, eyes, heart, kidneys, liver, pancreas, intestines, and reproductive organs), with polydactyly phenotypes showing strong heterogeneity among different families. Some forms of SRPS can be lethal in the neonatal period due to severe thoracic constriction leading to respiratory failure [14, 15]. Because SRPS has complex and varied symptoms, basic ultrasound diagnosis has its limits in early detection and targeted treatment [16, 17]. For example, both EVC syndrome and DYNC2I1‐related SRPS [18] share common features such as polydactyly, narrow thorax, and short limbs. However, EVC syndrome is often accompanied by congenital heart defect (particularly atrial septal defect), nail dysplasia, and more typical facial features (such as a short upper lip frenulum), whereas DYNC2I1‐related SRPS presents with more severe thoracic issues. However, these differential diagnoses are difficult to complete during the fetal period, making it feasible to achieve the corresponding diagnosis through genetic testing.

In this study, amniocentesis was performed on a pregnant woman with an SRPS phenotype in both pregnancies, and clinical exome sequencing was used for prenatal diagnosis. This clinical exome sequencing included 5289 genes recorded in the OMIM database. Compared to whole‐genome sequencing and whole exome sequencing, it significantly reduced the time required for bioinformatics and variant genetic analysis by excluding genes of currently unclear clinical significance, thereby greatly improving the efficiency of the entire prenatal diagnosis process [19, 20]. In this study, the test results can be obtained within 14 natural days. A homozygous variant in the DYNC2I1 gene: c.1526A>T p.(Asp509Val) was identified in this family. No previous clinical case reports of this variant have been documented. This variant is present in both parents of the proband. Although the parents of the proband report no known consanguinity, they come from geographically close regions in Hubei Province, China. We speculate that this may be due to a founder effect in the population of their region or the existence of undisclosed distant shared ancestry. The homozygous variant was detected in the first pregnancy, but at that time, only this fetus in the pedigree carried the homozygous variant, making it impossible to use the PM3 evidence item. Therefore, it was classified by us as a variant of uncertain clinical significance. In the second pregnancy, the homozygous variant was detected again, and at this time, we used the PM3_Supporting evidence item, thus classifying it as likely pathogenic for the first time.

DYNC2I1, also known as the gene encoding WD repeat‐containing protein 60 (WDR60), is the causative gene for SRPS6 and is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. Currently, there are few reported clinical cases worldwide of short rib‐polydactyly syndrome (SRPS), DYNC2I1‐related [21, 22]. The clinical phenotypes vary widely. Besides causing SRPS6, variants in this gene can also cause milder ciliopathies characterized by retinal disease and polydactyly. These milder ciliopathies exhibit normal cognitive function and lack chondrodysplasia [21].

In nearly 20 years of prenatal diagnosis work at our center, only one SRPS family has been identified, with prenatal ultrasounds from both pregnancies showing typical SRPS phenotypes, such as short limbs, short ribs, curved long bones, polydactyly, and a narrow chest. Notably, both pregnancies in this family exhibited polydactyly phenotype, while the aborted fetus from the second pregnancy also exhibited asymmetry in both feet. This study reports two fetuses from the same family, both carrying the DYNC2I1 gene c.1526A>T p.(Asp509Val) variant, and provides a detailed description of the fetal ultrasound findings at different gestational weeks for these two pregnancies. It demonstrates the feasibility of clinical exome sequencing in the prenatal diagnosis of SRPS, and these findings provide a foundation for future genetic counseling and prenatal testing.
