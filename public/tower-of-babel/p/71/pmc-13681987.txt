Risperidone for children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and severe behavioral dysregulation: a randomized placebo-controlled assessor-blinded trial
Authors: Ezatollah Abbasi, Alireza Abdi, Rana Eskandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder in childhood, and treatment of children with severe behavioral dysregulation remains challenging. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of adjunctive risperidone specifically in children with ADHD and clinically significant behavioral dysregulation — including irritability, impulsive aggression, and oppositional symptoms — refractory to behavioral intervention alone.MethodsIn this 48-week randomized, placebo-controlled, assessor-blinded trial, children aged 3–12 years with moderate-to-severe ADHD and significant behavioral dysregulation refractory to behavioral intervention alone were allocated to risperidone (n = 48) or matched placebo (n = 46), both in addition to ongoing structured behavioral intervention and parental psychoeducation. The primary outcome was change in clinician-rated ADHD-RS-IV score from baseline to week 48. Secondary outcomes included parent-rated Conners’ scores, Children’s Global Assessment Scale (CGAS), treatment response, adverse events, and metabolic parameters.ResultsRisperidone produced a significantly greater reduction in ADHD-RS-IV scores than placebo over time (baseline: 28.85 ± 2.62 vs. 28.70 ± 2.50; week 48: 15.63 ± 1.82 vs. 26.47 ± 2.92; p < 0.001). Parent-rated Conners’ scores also improved more in the risperidone group (week 48: 16.94 ± 1.81 vs. 28.42 ± 3.04; p < 0.001). Functional improvement was greater with risperidone, with higher CGAS scores and a higher clinical response rate (70.8% vs. 21.7%; p < 0.001). The most common adverse effects were somnolence and increased appetite. Risperidone was associated with greater weight gain and prolactin elevation than placebo, but no serious adverse events occurred.ConclusionIn this study, risperidone was associated with improvement in ADHD symptom severity and global functioning in carefully selected children with ADHD and prominent behavioral dysregulation. However, treatment was also associated with clinically relevant metabolic and endocrine adverse effects. These findings support selective use as a pharmacological adjunct to behavioral intervention in closely monitored patients with significant behavioral comorbidity, and do not justify routine use for uncomplicated ADHD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08433-y.Keywords: Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, Risperidone, Children, Randomized controlled trial, Behavioral dysregulation

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most prevalent neurodevelopmental disorders in childhood and adolescence, characterized by persistent symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that interfere with academic performance, social relationships, and emotional functioning [1]. According to recent epidemiological studies, the worldwide prevalence of ADHD is estimated to range between 5% and 7% among school-aged children, making it a major psychiatric and public health concern [2]. Although ADHD symptoms often begin in early childhood, the disorder may persist into adolescence and adulthood, contributing to long-term impairments in educational achievement, occupational performance, interpersonal relationships, and quality of life [3].

The pathophysiology of ADHD is multifactorial and involves genetic susceptibility, environmental influences, and neurobiological dysfunction in dopaminergic and noradrenergic pathways, particularly within frontostriatal neural circuits [4]. In addition to the core symptoms, many children with ADHD experience emotional dysregulation, aggression, oppositional behaviors, and psychiatric comorbidities such as oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), conduct disorder, anxiety disorders, and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [5]. The presence of these comorbidities significantly complicates clinical management and is associated with poorer functional outcomes and increased caregiver burden [6].

Pharmacological treatment remains the cornerstone of ADHD management, especially in moderate-to-severe cases. Psychostimulants such as methylphenidate and amphetamine derivatives are currently considered first-line therapies because of their well-established efficacy in reducing inattentiveness and hyperactivity symptoms [7]. However, despite their effectiveness, a substantial proportion of children either show inadequate response or experience adverse effects including appetite suppression, sleep disturbances, irritability, emotional lability, and cardiovascular concerns [8]. Furthermore, stimulant medications may not sufficiently control severe aggression, disruptive behaviors, or emotional dysregulation in children with complex psychiatric comorbidities [9]. These limitations have led clinicians and researchers to investigate alternative or adjunctive pharmacological strategies.

Among atypical antipsychotics, risperidone has become one of the most frequently prescribed agents in pediatric psychiatry because of its efficacy in controlling aggression, irritability, impulsivity, and behavioral dysregulation [10]. Risperidone acts primarily through antagonism of dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT2A receptors and holds FDA approval for irritability associated with autism spectrum disorder in children aged five years and older — the largest regulatory approval of any atypical antipsychotic in a pediatric psychiatric indication. It also has the most extensive evidence base of any atypical antipsychotic for pediatric disruptive behavior disorders, including multiple randomized controlled trials and long-term safety data. Although newer agents such as aripiprazole and alpha-2 agonists such as guanfacine extended-release represent important therapeutic alternatives with potentially more favorable risk-benefit profiles in some clinical contexts, risperidone remains the most widely available, most commonly used, and most extensively studied atypical antipsychotic for pediatric behavioral dysregulation in many regions, including Iran, where regulatory approval and clinical availability of these newer agents remain limited. In recent years, off-label use of risperidone in children with ADHD has increased, particularly in patients presenting with severe impulsivity, oppositional symptoms, or inadequate response to stimulant therapy, making a rigorous evaluation of its long-term efficacy and safety in this population both timely and clinically necessary [11, 12].

Although stimulant medications remain the first-line pharmacological treatment for ADHD, a subset of children exhibit severe behavioral dysregulation, irritability, aggression, or oppositional symptoms that may not adequately respond to behavioral interventions or conventional ADHD medications alone. In such complex clinical presentations, atypical antipsychotics, particularly risperidone, have occasionally been used as adjunctive or alternative therapeutic options to reduce disruptive behaviors and improve emotional regulation. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the long-term efficacy and safety of risperidone in pediatric ADHD populations remains limited, particularly in randomized controlled settings with extended follow-up durations.

Several clinical studies have evaluated the efficacy of risperidone in pediatric ADHD populations. Previous investigations have demonstrated that risperidone may improve behavioral symptoms and reduce ADHD severity scores, either as monotherapy or as adjunctive treatment combined with stimulants [13–15]. However, findings across studies remain inconsistent because of methodological limitations such as small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, heterogeneous patient populations, and lack of placebo-controlled designs [5]. In addition, concerns regarding metabolic adverse effects, sedation, weight gain, and hyperprolactinemia continue to limit widespread use of atypical antipsychotics in children [16]. Therefore, careful evaluation of both efficacy and safety outcomes remains essential when considering risperidone for pediatric ADHD treatment.

Despite increasing clinical use of risperidone, evidence regarding its therapeutic role in children with ADHD remains limited, particularly in Middle Eastern populations where high-quality randomized controlled trials are scarce. Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of risperidone as a pharmacological adjunct to structured behavioral intervention in children with ADHD and prominent behavioral dysregulation — including irritability, impulsive aggression, and oppositional symptoms — who exhibited inadequate response to behavioral intervention alone, using a randomized placebo-controlled design and standardized outcome measures.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a 48-week, randomized, placebo-controlled, assessor-blinded clinical trial conducted at the pediatric neurology outpatient clinics affiliated with Urmia University of Medical Sciences, Urmia, Iran. The study protocol was developed in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for randomized clinical trials (Supplementary file 1).

Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either risperidone or matched placebo in addition to standard behavioral intervention and parental psychoeducation. Because risperidone dose titration required individualized adjustment according to body weight, clinical response, and tolerability, treating clinicians were not blinded to allocation. Participants, caregivers, outcome assessors, and data analysts remained blinded throughout the study period.

Participants

Children aged 3–12 years who met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) [1] criteria for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) were eligible for enrollment in the study. Diagnosis was confirmed independently by a pediatric neurologist and a child and adolescent psychiatrist using structured clinical interviews developed for this study (Supplementary File 1) and standardized ADHD assessment tools. Participants were required to have moderate-to-severe ADHD symptoms, defined as a baseline ADHD Rating Scale-IV (ADHD-RS-IV) [17] total score of ≥ 28, accompanied by clinically significant behavioral dysregulation, irritability, impulsive aggression, or oppositional symptoms causing functional impairment despite behavioral intervention. These co-occurring behavioral difficulties were formally evaluated through structured clinical interviews conducted independently by a pediatric neurologist and a child and adolescent psychiatrist using DSM-5-TR criteria, supplemented by parent-reported CPRS-R: L subscale scores [18]. Eligibility specifically required Oppositional subscale scores exceeding the 93rd percentile for age and sex on the CPRS-R: L — consistent with the clinically significant threshold recommended for this instrument — in addition to clinician-confirmed presence of oppositional, aggressive, or emotionally dysregulated behaviors causing functional impairment. In the majority of affected participants, these symptoms met diagnostic threshold for a co-occurring DSM-5-TR diagnosis, most commonly Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD); isolated subsyndromal behavioral symptoms without a formal comorbid diagnosis were present in a smaller subset of participants. Both treatment-naïve children and those who had discontinued previous ADHD medications for at least two weeks before enrollment were eligible to participate. In the enrolled sample, the majority of participants (approximately 80%) were treatment-naïve at the time of enrollment; the remaining participants had prior stimulant exposure but had discontinued treatment prior to study entry. Enrollment of treatment-naïve participants was considered clinically appropriate given the severity of behavioral dysregulation and the documented failure of behavioral intervention alone to achieve adequate symptom control, as confirmed by multidisciplinary assessment. Concomitant use of stimulant or other psychotropic medications during the trial period was not permitted. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians prior to study inclusion.

Children were excluded if they had intellectual disability (IQ < 70), autism spectrum disorder requiring antipsychotic treatment, primary psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder as assessed through structured DSM-5-TR clinical interview, Tourette syndrome or chronic tic disorders, severe conduct disorder requiring antipsychotic therapy independent of ADHD management, epilepsy, other major neurological diseases, or clinically significant cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, or endocrine disorders. Additional exclusion criteria included previous treatment with atypical antipsychotic medications during the preceding six months, clinically significant cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, or endocrine disorders, severe obesity defined as body mass index above the 95th percentile for age and sex, or inability to complete scheduled follow-up assessments, or concurrent use of stimulant medications during the trial period. Participants with mild-to-moderate conduct symptoms occurring in the context of a co-occurring ODD diagnosis were eligible for enrollment, provided that their behavioral presentation did not independently require antipsychotic therapy. Participants who developed severe adverse drug reactions or whose parents withdrew consent during the study period were also excluded from further analysis.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on detecting a clinically meaningful difference in the change of ADHD-RS-IV total score between the risperidone and placebo groups at week 48. Calculation was performed using a superiority trial design with a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and statistical power of 80%. Assuming a moderate standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.60 based on previous pediatric psychopharmacology studies, the estimated minimum sample size was 45 participants per group. Considering a projected attrition rate of 15%, the final enrollment target was increased to 52 participants in each arm, resulting in a total sample size of 104 children.

Randomization and blinding

The allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistician not involved in participant enrollment or outcome assessment, using a computer-generated block randomization procedure with variable block sizes of four and six. Eligible participants were enrolled by the treating pediatric neurologist following confirmation of all inclusion and exclusion criteria. Assignment to interventions was performed by a designated research coordinator who opened the next sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelope only after participant enrollment was confirmed and irreversible. This procedure ensured that the enrolling clinician had no foreknowledge of upcoming allocations, maintaining adequate allocation concealment throughout the recruitment phase.

Owing to individualized dose titration requirements based on body weight and clinical response, complete blinding of treating clinicians was not feasible; this represents an inherent limitation of pragmatic dose-titration trials. However, all primary and secondary outcome assessments were conducted by independent evaluators who were formally trained, had no access to treatment allocation records, and were instructed not to inquire about medication use. Participants, caregivers, and statistical analysts also remained blinded throughout the study period. Although formal assessment of blinding integrity — for example through participant or assessor guessing of allocation — was not performed, the use of matched placebo preparations identical in appearance, taste, and administration schedule was intended to preserve masking at the participant and caregiver level. The assessor-blinded rather than fully double-blinded design is acknowledged as a study limitation.

Intervention

Participants assigned to the risperidone group received oral risperidone initiated at 0.25 mg/day for children weighing < 20 kg and 0.5 mg/day for those weighing ≥ 20 kg. Dosage was titrated gradually at weekly intervals according to clinical response and tolerability, with a maximum dose of 1.5 mg/day. Dose adjustments were performed by the treating pediatric neurologist throughout the follow-up period.

Participants allocated to the control arm received a matched placebo following the same administration schedule and titration protocol. Both groups additionally received a structured behavioral parent training and psychoeducation program delivered by a trained child psychologist throughout the 48-week follow-up period. The program was adapted from the evidence-based Barkley Parent Training Protocol for ADHD and consisted of eight bi-weekly group sessions during the first four months, followed by monthly individual reinforcement sessions thereafter. Core components included: [1] psychoeducation about ADHD neurobiology, symptom presentation, and prognosis; [2] positive reinforcement strategies and contingency management techniques; [3] consistent limit-setting and structured daily routine implementation; [4] home-based token economy systems for behavior modification; [5] strategies for managing oppositional, aggressive, and emotionally dysregulated behaviors; and [6] school-home coordination and teacher consultation guidance. Session content, attendance, and adherence were documented for each family throughout the study period. Both groups received identical behavioral intervention components to ensure that any observed between-group differences in outcome measures were attributable to pharmacological treatment rather than differential access to psychosocial support.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was the change in ADHD symptom severity from baseline to week 48, assessed using the clinician-administered Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder Rating Scale-IV (ADHD-RS-IV). This standardized instrument evaluates the severity of inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms based on DSM-5 criteria and is widely used in pediatric ADHD clinical trials. The primary efficacy endpoint was defined as the mean difference in ADHD-RS-IV total score between baseline and the end of follow-up. The primary endpoint analysis focused on the between-group difference in change from baseline to week 48 in ADHD-RS-IV total score.

Secondary outcomes included parent-reported behavioral and emotional symptoms measured using the long form of the Conners’ Parent Rating Scale–Revised (CPRS-R: L), which comprises 80 items and yields scores across multiple clinically relevant subscales. In addition to the Global Index total score, four subscales were specifically analyzed throughout the follow-up period: [1] the Oppositional subscale, reflecting defiant, hostile, and oppositional behaviors; [2] the Cognitive Problems/Inattention subscale, capturing attention and executive function difficulties; [3] the Hyperactivity subscale, assessing impulsive and overactive behaviors; and [4] the ADHD Index, providing an overall severity estimate sensitive to treatment change. Emotional dysregulation and impulsivity-related symptoms were primarily captured through the Hyperactivity and Oppositional subscales, as these represent the core behavioral domains assessed by the CPRS-R: L in this age group. All subscale scores were recorded at baseline and at weeks 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48. Functional impairment and overall psychosocial functioning were additionally evaluated using the Children’s Global Assessment Scale (CGAS). Clinical response was defined as at least a 30% reduction in ADHD-RS-IV total score compared with baseline values.

Safety and tolerability outcomes were also assessed throughout the study period. Adverse events including sedation, extrapyramidal symptoms, appetite changes, gastrointestinal complaints, and behavioral worsening were systematically recorded at each follow-up visit using standardized clinical assessments. Metabolic and anthropometric outcomes, including body weight, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, fasting blood glucose, lipid profile, and serum prolactin levels, were measured to evaluate potential treatment-related metabolic complications associated with risperidone therapy. Adverse events were recorded at each follow-up visit through structured parental interviews and standardized clinical safety assessments conducted by the treating pediatric neurologist.

Follow-up and assessment schedule

Clinical evaluations were conducted at baseline and at weeks 4, 8, 12, 24, 36 and 48 after treatment initiation. At each visit, symptom severity, treatment adherence, adverse events, and vital signs were assessed. Anthropometric measurements were recorded at every follow-up visit, while laboratory metabolic evaluations were performed at baseline, and at weeks 12, 24, 36 and 48.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The primary efficacy analysis was conducted on an intention-to-treat (ITT) basis, including all randomized participants regardless of protocol adherence or completeness of follow-up. Missing outcome data were handled using the mixed-effects repeated-measures model, which provides valid estimates under the missing-at-random assumption without requiring complete-case exclusion. A per-protocol sensitivity analysis, restricted to participants who completed at least 80% of scheduled visits and maintained medication adherence above 85%, was additionally performed to assess the robustness of primary findings. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

Longitudinal changes in outcomes were analyzed using mixed-effects repeated-measures models to account for within-subject correlations over time. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d and partial eta squared (η²). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Urmia University of Medical Sciences (IR.UMSU.REC.1402.388) and registered in the Iranian Clinical Trials Registry (IRCT20241228064189N1; registered on 2025-10-16- Retrospectively registered). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants prior to enrollment. Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study, and patients were free to withdraw at any time without consequences.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 126 children were screened for eligibility. Of these, 104 participants met the inclusion criteria and underwent randomization in a 1:1 ratio to receive either risperidone or matched placebo. During follow-up, 10 participants discontinued the study, including 4 participants in the risperidone group and 6 participants in the placebo group. Reasons for discontinuation included loss to follow-up (n = 6), withdrawal of parental consent (n = 3), and adverse effects leading to treatment discontinuation (n = 1). Consequently, the final analysis included data from 94 participants, comprising 48 children in the risperidone group and 46 children in the placebo group. The flow of participants through each stage of the trial — including screening, eligibility assessment, randomization, allocation, follow-up, and final analysis — is illustrated in Fig. 1 (CONSORT flow diagram). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed regarding age, sex distribution, anthropometric indices, baseline ADHD severity scores, or previous stimulant exposure (all p > 0.05) (Table 1). Comorbidity status was also comparably distributed, with 40 (83.3%) and 36 (78.3%) participants carrying a formal ODD diagnosis in the risperidone and placebo groups, respectively (χ² = 0.390, p = 0.53) (Table 1).

Primary outcome

A significant reduction in clinician-rated ADHD symptom severity was observed in the risperidone group during the 48-week follow-up period. Mean ADHD-RS-IV scores progressively decreased from 28.85 ± 2.62 at baseline to 15.63 ± 1.82 at week 48. In contrast, only minimal improvement was observed in the placebo group.

Mixed-effects repeated-measures analysis demonstrated a significant group × time interaction effect for ADHD-RS-IV scores (F = 42.7, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.39), indicating substantially greater symptom improvement in the risperidone group compared with placebo over time (Table 2). The magnitude of treatment effect at week 48 corresponded to a large between-group effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.84).

Secondary outcomes

Parent-reported behavioral symptoms

Parent-reported behavioral and emotional symptoms assessed using the CPRS-R: L demonstrated significant and progressive improvement in the risperidone group across all four analyzed subscales compared with placebo. Mean scores on the Oppositional, Cognitive Problems/Inattention, Hyperactivity, and ADHD Index subscales decreased steadily across all follow-up visits in the risperidone arm, whereas only modest and non-progressive changes were observed in the placebo arm. The most pronounced between-group differences were observed on the Oppositional and Hyperactivity subscales, consistent with the primary behavioral phenotype targeted in this trial. Repeated-measures mixed-effects analysis revealed statistically significant group × time interaction effects for all four subscales (all p < 0.001), with large between-group effect sizes at week 48 (Table 3). The overall ADHD Index between-group effect size at week 48 remained large (Cohen’s d = 1.76).

Functional outcomes and clinical response

Functional status assessed using the Children’s Global Assessment Scale (CGAS) improved significantly in the risperidone group. Mean CGAS scores increased from 51.2 ± 5.8 at baseline to 68.4 ± 6.1 at week 48 (change: +17.2 ± 5.4 points), compared with a change of + 5.2 ± 4.8 points in the placebo group (p < 0.001). At week 48, the clinical response rate, defined as ≥ 30% reduction in ADHD-RS-IV score, was substantially higher in the risperidone group compared with placebo (Table 4).

Anthropometric and metabolic outcomes

Participants receiving risperidone experienced significantly greater increases in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, and serum prolactin levels compared with placebo-treated participants. Despite statistically significant between-group differences in metabolic parameters, no participant developed clinically significant metabolic syndrome, diabetes mellitus, or severe obesity requiring treatment discontinuation during follow-up.

Safety and tolerability outcomes

Risperidone treatment was generally well tolerated. Most adverse events were mild to moderate in severity and did not necessitate treatment discontinuation. Somnolence and increased appetite were the most frequently reported adverse effects in the risperidone group. Adverse events in the placebo group were less frequent and predominantly nonspecific.

Most adverse events observed in the risperidone group were transient and mild-to-moderate in severity. Sedation and appetite increase were generally observed during the early titration phase and improved over time without requiring dose discontinuation in most participants (Table 6).

Treatment adherence

Medication adherence exceeded 85% in both study groups based on pill counts and caregiver reports. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in adherence rates during the follow-up period (p = 0.44).

Discussion

In this randomized, placebo-controlled, assessor-blinded trial, adjunctive risperidone produced a statistically significant and progressive reduction in clinician-rated ADHD symptom severity over 48 weeks of follow-up, as measured by the ADHD-RS-IV (mean score: 28.85 at baseline vs. 15.63 at week 48 in the risperidone group, compared with 28.70 vs. 26.47 in the placebo group; group × time interaction F = 42.7, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.39). The treatment effect was not transient: between-group differences emerged by week 12 and continued to widen through week 48, suggesting a progressive rather than immediate pharmacological effect. Parent-reported behavioral symptoms assessed using the CPRS-R: L demonstrated significant improvement across all four subscales — Oppositional, Cognitive Problems/Inattention, Hyperactivity, and ADHD Index — with large between-group effect sizes at week 48 (Cohen’s d = 1.76 for the ADHD Index). Global functional status, assessed using the Children’s Global Assessment Scale (CGAS), also improved significantly in the risperidone group. Mean CGAS scores increased from 51.2 ± 5.8 at baseline to 68.4 ± 6.1 at week 48 (change: +17.2 ± 5.4), compared with a change of + 5.2 ± 4.8 points in the placebo group (p < 0.001). According to established CGAS anchor points, a mean baseline score in the 51–60 range corresponds to “variable functioning with sporadic difficulties or symptoms in several areas,” whereas the mean endpoint score of 68.4 in the risperidone group falls within the 61–70 range, denoting “some difficulty in a single area but generally functioning well” — representing a clinically meaningful upward transition in functional category [19]. The clinical response rate — defined as ≥ 30% reduction in ADHD-RS-IV total score — was 70.8% in the risperidone group compared with 21.7% in the placebo group (p < 0.001).

The unusually large effect sizes observed in this trial — Cohen’s d of 1.84 for the primary outcome and 1.76 for parent-reported behavioral symptoms — warrant explicit discussion. These values substantially exceed the effect sizes of 0.4 to 0.9 typically reported in prior randomized trials of risperidone in pediatric populations and in meta-analyses of atypical antipsychotics for disruptive behavior disorders. Several methodological and clinical factors may account for this discrepancy. The highly selected study population, which required both elevated ADHD-RS-IV scores and confirmed behavioral comorbidity, ensured high baseline symptom burden and consequently greater absolute room for measurable improvement. The extended 48-week follow-up duration, considerably longer than the 4–16 week windows of most prior trials, allowed cumulative treatment effects to accumulate over time in a way that short-term studies cannot capture. Additionally, the minimal improvement observed in the placebo group — suggesting that behavioral intervention alone was genuinely insufficient for this severity of presentation — arithmetically amplified the between-group difference without necessarily reflecting an unusually potent drug effect. Furthermore, effect sizes based on endpoint between-group differences rather than change-from-baseline analyses adjusted for regression to the mean may overestimate the true treatment effect magnitude. Finally, the relatively modest sample size increases the probability of effect size inflation in positive trials. We cannot exclude the possibility that differential expectancy effects, despite assessor blinding, or unmeasured confounders contributed to the observed magnitude. These findings should therefore be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, and independent replication in larger, fully double-blinded multicenter trials is warranted before these effect sizes are accepted at face value.

The present findings are broadly consistent with, yet extend, the existing evidence base for risperidone in children with ADHD and comorbid disruptive behavior. Aman and colleagues [20] demonstrated in a randomized trial that risperidone reduced disruptive behavior and aggression in children with ADHD regardless of concurrent stimulant use, supporting a pharmacological effect that is at least partially independent of stimulant co-administration. Arabgol et al. [13] found comparable short-term symptom improvement between risperidone and methylphenidate in preschool children with ADHD, while Jahangard et al. [14] reported that adjunctive risperidone improved behavioral outcomes specifically in children with ADHD and co-occurring ODD. Importantly, a meta-analysis by Gorman and colleagues reported a pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) of 0.60 (95% CI 0.31–0.89) for risperidone versus placebo on disruptive behavior and aggression in children with average IQ, representing a moderate-to-large effect [21]. The substantially larger effect sizes observed in the present trial — Cohen’s d of 1.84 for the primary outcome — likely reflect several contextual factors discussed above, including the highly selected patient population, the extended 48-week follow-up, and the minimal placebo response, rather than a fundamentally greater drug potency. The most methodologically rigorous recent evidence comes from Blader and colleagues [22], who conducted a stepped-treatment RCT in which adjunctive risperidone — added only after stimulant optimization and behavioral therapy had proven insufficient — produced significant reductions in aggressive behavior compared with placebo [22]. That trial enrolled a stimulant-optimized, behavior-therapy-exposed population and provides the most ecologically valid evidence for the therapeutic niche of risperidone as a truly adjunctive agent in refractory cases. The present study differs importantly in that the majority of enrolled participants were treatment-naïve at baseline, meaning findings apply to a broader ADHD population with severe behavioral comorbidity rather than specifically to stimulant-refractory cases. Analysis of the Oppositional subscale of the CPRS-R: L in the present trial demonstrated the most pronounced between-group differences among all subscales (mean difference at week 48: −7.96 points, 95% CI − 8.82 to − 7.10; p < 0.001), consistent with prior evidence that risperidone exerts preferential effects on oppositional, aggressive, and irritability-related symptom dimensions in children with ODD comorbidity [14, 20, 21]. Taken together, these data support the conclusion that the primary clinical niche for risperidone in ADHD is not the management of core inattentive or hyperactive symptoms per se, but rather the reduction of superimposed behavioral dysregulation in children who have not responded adequately to first-line approaches.

The clinical implications of these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Stimulants remain the preferred first-line pharmacologic treatment for ADHD, and risperidone should not be viewed as a routine substitute for standard ADHD therapy. Rather, the present results — alongside the evidence from Blader et al. [22] and earlier trials — support a selective role for risperidone as a pharmacological adjunct to behavioral intervention and optimized stimulant therapy in children with ADHD who exhibit significant disruptive behavioral comorbidity and inadequate response to these first-line approaches. Importantly, given that 80.9% of enrolled participants carried a formal DSM-5-TR diagnosis of ODD and the remaining 19.1% presented with subsyndromal behavioral dysregulation, the observed improvement in ADHD-RS-IV scores may partly reflect the amelioration of co-occurring oppositional and disruptive symptoms rather than a direct pharmacological effect on core ADHD neurobiology. Disentangling these mechanisms would require inclusion of a comparator group of children with uncomplicated ADHD — an element absent from the present design. Clinicians should therefore exercise particular caution when extrapolating these findings to children with uncomplicated ADHD who do not present with these comorbid behavioral features. This distinction is important because it aligns the therapeutic rationale with the behavioral phenotype being treated and reduces the risk of overgeneralizing the results to uncomplicated ADHD.

Safety findings are equally important. Although risperidone was generally well tolerated in this trial, treatment was associated with greater weight gain, increases in BMI and waist circumference, and higher prolactin levels than placebo. This adverse-effect profile is consistent with the known metabolic and endocrine liabilities of second-generation antipsychotics in children. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported a fivefold increased risk of clinically significant weight gain among children and adolescents receiving risperidone, with younger and antipsychotic-naïve patients identified as particularly susceptible [23]. Pediatric studies have consistently demonstrated early-onset hyperprolactinemia with risperidone, with prolactin elevation frequently observed within the first weeks of treatment [16, 24]. The weight gain of 3.8 ± 1.7 kg and prolactin elevation of + 10.3 ± 4.2 ng/mL observed in the present trial are consistent with these established profiles and underscore the need for structured metabolic and endocrine surveillance throughout treatment. Therefore, the observed safety signal in the present study does not negate the efficacy findings, but it reinforces that any potential benefit must be balanced against the need for careful longitudinal monitoring and conservative patient selection.

The tolerability profile observed here was acceptable, with most adverse events being mild to moderate and occurring early in treatment. Somnolence and increased appetite were the most frequent adverse effects, and no serious adverse events were observed. This pattern is broadly consistent with prior pediatric studies of risperidone and supports the practical view that the drug can be used safely in selected patients when dose titration and follow-up are closely supervised [25, 26]. Nevertheless, the metabolic burden observed in this trial indicates that even when symptoms improve, the treatment strategy must remain conservative, individualized, and time-limited whenever possible.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was assessor-blinded rather than fully double-blinded, because dose titration required awareness of allocation by the treating clinician. Second, the trial was conducted at a single center, which may limit generalizability. Third, the ADHD-RS-IV is a validated measure of ADHD symptom severity, but it does not fully capture aggression, irritability, or oppositional dysregulation; inclusion of a dedicated aggression or oppositionality scale — such as the Nisonger Child Behavior Rating Form or the Aberrant Behavior Checklist — would have strengthened phenotype-specific interpretation. Furthermore, although co-occurring behavioral comorbidities were categorized through structured DSM-5-TR clinical interviews, a formal standardized diagnostic instrument such as the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (K-SADS) was not systematically administered. While the observed comorbidity distribution is consistent with rates reported in clinical samples of children with ADHD and severe behavioral dysregulation, the absence of a structured diagnostic interview may limit the precision and cross-site reproducibility of comorbidity characterization. This represents a methodological limitation that may affect the precision of comorbidity characterization and the reproducibility of our inclusion criteria across settings. Fourth, although follow-up was longer than in many pediatric psychopharmacology studies, longer-term data are still needed to determine whether benefits persist and whether metabolic effects continue to accumulate. Fifth, because the study enrolled exclusively children with ADHD accompanied by significant behavioral comorbidities, it is not possible to determine whether the observed improvement in ADHD-RS-IV scores reflects a direct effect of risperidone on core ADHD symptomatology or whether it is mediated through reduction in behavioral dysregulation, irritability, and oppositional symptoms. The absence of a comparator group of children with uncomplicated ADHD means that the present findings cannot be generalized to the broader ADHD population, and the question of whether risperidone improves core ADHD symptoms independently of its behavioral effects remains unanswered. Sixth, this trial did not include an active comparator arm. Newer therapeutic options including aripiprazole — which carries a more favorable metabolic profile than risperidone — and guanfacine extended-release — an alpha-2 agonist with FDA approval for ADHD and demonstrated efficacy in reducing oppositional and hyperactive symptoms — represent clinically relevant alternatives that were not evaluated in this study. The absence of head-to-head comparison with these agents limits conclusions about the relative efficacy and safety of risperidone compared with currently available alternatives. Future trials should incorporate active comparator designs to better inform clinical decision-making regarding agent selection in this population. Seventh, the unusually large effect sizes observed in this trial — substantially exceeding those reported in prior meta-analyses — may reflect effect size inflation due to the relatively small sample size, endpoint-based rather than change-score-based effect size calculation, the highly selected study population, or residual confounding despite assessor blinding. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary pending replication in larger independent trials.

An important limitation that warrants explicit acknowledgment concerns the treatment history of enrolled participants. Approximately 80% of participants were treatment-naïve at enrollment, meaning that the majority had not received a prior adequate stimulant trial before receiving risperidone. This represents a meaningful deviation from standard clinical practice, in which stimulant pharmacotherapy is typically required as a first-line intervention before atypical antipsychotics are considered. As a result, this trial cannot be interpreted as evaluating risperidone specifically in a stimulant-refractory population. Rather, findings apply to a broader phenotype of children with ADHD and severe behavioral dysregulation, regardless of prior treatment history. The absence of systematic data on reasons for stimulant discontinuation, formal contraindication documentation, and concomitant medication use further limits the characterization of the enrolled population in this respect. Future trials in this area should prospectively document prior treatment history using standardized criteria and should, where feasible, restrict enrollment to participants who have completed at least one adequate stimulant trial. Finally, the study did not compare risperidone with stimulant-based regimens or with other active treatment strategies, so comparative effectiveness remains uncertain.

Considered together, the findings of this trial indicate that adjunctive risperidone produced significant and progressive reductions in ADHD symptom severity, parent-reported behavioral dysregulation, and global functional impairment over 48 weeks in children with ADHD and prominent behavioral comorbidity who had not responded adequately to behavioral intervention alone. The clinical response rate of 70.8% in the risperidone group — compared with 21.7% in the placebo group — and the observed upward transition in mean CGAS functional category suggest that the magnitude of benefit, in this selected population, was clinically meaningful and not merely statistically significant. However, this benefit was accompanied by significantly greater weight gain (Δ3.8 ± 1.7 kg), BMI increase, and prolactin elevation than placebo, consistent with the known metabolic and endocrine liabilities of risperidone in pediatric populations. Given that the majority of enrolled participants were treatment-naïve, the present findings do not specifically characterize risperidone’s role in a stimulant-refractory population; rather, they describe outcomes in a broader phenotype of children with ADHD and severe behavioral dysregulation. Risperidone should therefore be considered only in children with ADHD who exhibit clinically significant behavioral comorbidity — particularly ODD or subsyndromal oppositional dysregulation causing functional impairment — after an adequate trial of stimulant pharmacotherapy and structured behavioral intervention has proven insufficient, or when such approaches are contraindicated. Routine use in uncomplicated ADHD is not supported by these data, and close metabolic monitoring remains essential throughout the treatment course.

Conclusion

Risperidone was associated with significant improvement in ADHD symptom severity and global functioning in children with ADHD and prominent behavioral dysregulation over 48 weeks of follow-up. However, treatment was also associated with clinically relevant metabolic and endocrine adverse effects, particularly weight gain and prolactin elevation. Given that the majority of enrolled participants were treatment-naïve, these findings apply to a broad population of children with ADHD and severe behavioral dysregulation rather than specifically to those who have failed stimulant therapy. Accordingly, risperidone should be considered only after an adequate stimulant trial has been completed, except in cases where stimulant use is contraindicated or clinically inappropriate. Routine use for uncomplicated ADHD is not justified, and ongoing safety monitoring remains essential. Given the unusually large effect sizes observed, independent replication in larger multicenter trials is needed before these findings can be considered definitive.
