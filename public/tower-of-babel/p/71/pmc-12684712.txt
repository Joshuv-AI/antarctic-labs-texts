Pulmonary vein isolation as the fifth column of heart failure therapy: a case report
Authors: Viktoria Santner, Martin Manninger, Anna-Sophie Eberl, Laura Stix, Daniel Scherr, Andreas Metzner, Charlotte Eitel, Guram Imnadze, Henning Jansen, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) and atrial fibrillation often coexist and exacerbate each other, creating a therapeutic challenge when both are present. This case presents a patient with suspected tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy (TIC) progressing to cardiogenic shock, managed with guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for HF and pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) as an additional therapeutic pillar.Case summaryWe report the case of a 57-year-old male with de novo atrial fibrillation and TIC leading to cardiogenic shock. As rhythm control was initially unsuccessful, mechanical circulatory support was required despite GDMT. A thorough diagnostic work-up was performed, excluding coronary artery disease and structural heart diseases as underlying HF causes, making TIC the most likely diagnosis. After rhythm stabilization was achieved, haemodynamic stabilization followed subsequently. Later on, the patient underwent successful PVI. At the 12-month follow-up, no AF recurrence occurred, and the patient presented with HF with improved ejection fraction.ConclusionTreatment of HF with GDMT should be performed simultaneously and harmonized with rhythm and rate control. Introduction

Heart failure (HF) and atrial fibrillation (AF) frequently coexist and are closely interrelated. Each condition predisposes to the other, often resulting in a vicious cycle of worsening symptoms and clinical outcomes. While treatment strategies for both entities are clearly recommended in the respective guidelines, recommendations diverge when both conditions coexist promoting each other.1–4 Here, we present a case of tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy (TIC) leading to cardiogenic shock treated with guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for HF and pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) as the fifth column of HF therapy in this patient.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old male presented to the emergency department of a referring hospital due to increasing exertional dyspnoea [New York Heart Association (NYHA) Classification III] over the prior week. He had no prior medical history or concomitant medication and a negative family history for cardiac diseases. The initial electrocardiogram (ECG) showed AF with a heart rate of 183 beats per minute (b.p.m.) without significant ST-deviation (Figure 1). N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) was increased with 9323 pg/mL and high-sensitive Troponin I (hsTropI) remained increased but stable with 48 and 47 ng/mL. Echocardiography showed an initial left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 25%. The diagnosis of de novo AF with concomitant cardiac decompensation was made. During hospitalization, an initial treatment with beta-blocker and digoxin could not achieve adequate rate control. Despite the newly initiated anticoagulation, in transoesophageal echocardiography, a thrombus in the left atrial appendage was detected and electrical cardioversion could not be performed. After adaption of rate control, spontaneous conversion into sinus rhythm occurred, and no neurological event occurred. Guideline-directed medical therapy for HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), including a beta-blocker, angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor (SGLT2i), and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA), was initiated.1 After 1 week of hospitalization, AF recurred alternating with typical right atrial flutter, and the patient complained about increasing chest pain. High-sensitive Troponin I was increased with 447 ng/mL but remained stable. The heart rate was 170/min, and the ECG again revealed no significant ST-deviation. The patient presented with signs of beginning cardiogenic shock with a mean arterial pressure of 60 mmHg and slightly increased lactate levels with 2.2 mmol/L. The diagnosis of a high-risk non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) with incipient cardiogenic shock was made, and the patient was transferred to the local university hospital. At admission to the cardiac catheter laboratory, the patient presented responsive, but in reduced general condition. In echocardiography, a severely impaired LVEF of 10% was measured. In coronary angiography, a clinically relevant coronary artery disease was ruled out (Figure 2). A ventriculography ruled out Takotsubo cardiomyopathy. The patient became increasingly haemodynamically unstable with an increasing catecholamine demand, whereupon the installation of a microaxial flow pump was made, which was upgraded with a venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) device the following night (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1). As needed, medical and mechanic circulatory support was adapted. In the beginning, heart rhythm changed between sinus rhythm and episodes of AF with again high heart rates with up to 180 b.p.m. As amiodarone was up-titrated, the patient finally remained in sinus rhythm with normal heart rates leading to an increase in LVEF and a decrease in catecholamine demand. After a period of 6 days, VA-ECMO and the microaxial flow pump could be sequentially explanted, and the patient’s general condition improved rapidly. Guideline-directed medical therapy was up-titrated and medical rate and rhythm control was adjusted. At the time of transfer to the normal ward, LVEF was measured at 35%.

In the simultaneous diagnostic work-up, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI) was performed showing no signs of fibrosis and no late gadolinium enhancement. In endomyocardial biopsy (EMB), no cells indicative for myocarditis could be detected. Clinically relevant coronary artery disease was already ruled out by coronary angiography. In a structured echocardiographic assessment, there were no signs of structural heart disease with normal end-diastolic diameters, a mildly increased left atrial volume index, and no signs of valvular heart disease (Figure 3). As there was no signs of ischaemic or structural heart disease, TIC was the most likely diagnosis in this patient.

Echocardiography showing no signs of structural heart disease. IVSd, diastolic diameter of the interventricular septum; LAVI, left atrial volume index; LVEDD, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter; RVEDD, right ventricular end-diastolic diameter.

According to the 2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of AF, catheter ablation is recommended in patients with AF and HFrEF with high probability of TIC as a Class 1 B recommendation.2 In order to predict LVEF improvement after AF catheter ablation, the Antwerp prediction score based on known aetiology (2 points), wide QRS complex (2 points), severe atrial dilatation (1 point), and paroxysmal AF (1 point) was published in 2023.5 In our patient (1 point for paroxysmal AF), the Antwerp score predicted a 92% rate of LVEF improvement after AF catheter ablation. After a shared decision-making process with the patient, he was planned for catheter ablation, which was performed a few days later. Pulmonary vein isolation and ablation of typical right atrial flutter was performed using a Biosense Webster QDOT Micro catheter. Point-by-point very high power short duration (90 W, 4 s) PVI and point-by-point cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) ablation (40 W, ablation index target 500) were performed uneventfully. Entrance and exit block of all pulmonary veins and bidirectional CTI block were confirmed (Figure 4). The following day, there was no pericardial effusion, and the patient was in sinus rhythm. One month after the initial presentation, the patient could be discharged in a recovered general state, with sinus rhythm, a normal heart rate, and a LVEF of 39%. He was discharged on GDMT for HF and amiodarone as an anti-arrhythmic drug for the following 3-month blanking period after PVI.

Electroanatomical map of catheter ablation of atrial fibrillation and typical right atrial flutter.

At the 12-month follow-up visit, the patient presented in good general condition without cardiac symptoms denying exertional dyspnoea, chest pain, palpitations, and syncopes. In the last 12 months, no recurrence of AF was detected in either casual ECG controls or 24-h ECG controls. Physical examination was unremarkable showing no signs of cardiac decompensation. In ECG, a sinus rhythm with normal heart rate was present. N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide was 141 pg/mL. Echocardiography remained unremarkable as well with a LVEF of 53%. To summarize, the 12-month follow-up showed good results with no AF recurrence and HF with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF).

Discussion

Atrial fibrillation with high heart rates can have a strong effect on LVEF potentially leading to cardiogenic shock with the need for cardiac assist devices as highlighted in previous case reports.6,7 In such patients, it is crucial to rule out all potential causes of HF leading to cardiogenic shock including coronary artery disease and structural heart diseases. The diagnosis of TIC can only be made after exclusion of all other causes ruled out by a thorough diagnostic work-up.8 Our case highlights the possibility of LVEF improvement in patients with AF and HFrEF after early AF catheter ablation. The Antwerp score is a useful tool to predict LVEF recovery after AF catheter ablation and can help in selecting HF patients most likely to benefit from ablation.5 Early catheter ablation for AF should be considered promptly in patients with TIC, as timely rhythm control can accelerate left ventricular recovery. Antiarrhythmic drug therapy, such as amiodarone, plays a stabilizing role in unstable TIC patients by maintaining sinus rhythm until catheter ablation can be performed safely.2 Guideline-directed medical therapy for HF remains essential in TIC management and should be initiated early and simultaneously with rhythm control to optimize myocardial recovery. The benefit of its long-term use after LVEF recovery in TIC patients remains unclear.9 There is emerging evidence that discontinuation of GDMT is not associated with an LVEF decline in those patients, as recently published by Segan et al. presenting the results from the WITHDRAW-AF-trial. Still, this was shown only in a small sample size of 60 patients with a short-term follow-up of 12 months.10 Future trials to confirm safety of discontinuation of GDMT in patients with TIC after adequate rhythm control are still needed.

Conclusion

Treatment of HF with GDMT should be performed simultaneously and harmonized with rhythm and rate control according to guidelines.1,2 This case highlights the role of early rhythm control and the potential of LVEF improvement in patients with TIC with PVI as the fifth column of HF therapy in those patients.
