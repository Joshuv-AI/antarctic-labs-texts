Cellular cannibalism in cytology: a distinctive feature of large cell/anaplastic medulloblastoma in a pediatric case
Authors: Gabriele Gaggero, Chiara Trambaiolo Antonelli, Gianluca Piatelli, Claudia Milanaccio
Published: 2025

Medulloblastoma (MB) is the second most common malignant tumor of the central nervous system (CNS) in childhood, accounting for 20% of all intracranial tumors. MB is often associated with several inherited syndromes and can arise in different locations, the cerebellum being one of the most frequent [1]. From both a histological and molecular point of view, MB is an extremely heterogeneous group, even with different responses to therapy depending on the subgroup [2]; however, MB are collectively classified as grade 4 embryonal tumors by the World Health Organization 2021 classification of CNS tumors (WHO-CNS2021). Among the histologic subtypes, the large cell/anaplastic variant (MBLC/A) is one of the most aggressive, characterized by a high mitotic/carioretic index and a distinctive cytologic feature: cell wrapping, also known as cellular cannibalism. Even today, despite the rise of the emerging “omic” sciences, intraoperative diagnosis of a brain mass can significantly alter the surgical strategy, with intraoperative cytological examination playing a crucial role. The cytological case described here is placed in this context.

The image presented here refers to the case of a 5-year-old child with a right lobar expansive cerebellar lesion whose neuro-radiologic imaging (MRI) showed features suggestive of high cellularity. The patient underwent surgery and an intraoperative evaluation was decided. A cytological specimen was obtained by the squash method (a technique used in cytology in which a sample of cells is prepared by squeezing or pressing tissue or fluid onto a microscope slide) and rapidly stained with toluidine blue. Microscopy revealed a neoplasm with markedly increased cellularity consisting of cells of various sizes ranging from medium to large. These cells had scant cytoplasm and salt-and-pepper chromatin. The tumor was characterized by scattered images of cellular cannibalism (Fig. 1AB), numerous mitoses (Fig. 1C), and frequent apoptotic figures. These findings were consistent with a primitive small round cell tumor, and the cellular cannibalism led to the diagnosis of MB-LC/A. The mass was excised and histology confirmed the diagnosis both morphologically (Fig. 1D) and immunohistochemically: GFAP−, Olig2−, synaptophysin+ (focal), beta-catenin+, INI1+ (retained nuclear expression), p53+ (nuclear expression in scattered elements), Ki-67 extremely high (proliferation index of 80%).

Intraoperative cytologic evaluation of CNS lesions has important implications for diagnosis and treatment planning [3]. MB-LC/A, which is characterized by its aggressive nature, often presents with atypical features that can be identified by cytological examination. One of the features observed in this case was cellular cannibalism, which is recognized as a hallmark of the anaplastic variant [1]. Cellular cannibalism refers to the phenomenon whereby tumor cells phagocytose neighboring apoptotic or necrotic cells. This process is thought to confer a survival advantage to the neoplastic cells, increasing their proliferative capacity and contributing to the aggressive behavior of the tumor [4, 5]. In the context of medulloblastoma, the presence of cellular cannibalism may serve as a critical cytologic indicator to distinguish the MB-LC/A subtype from other variants. The use of intraoperative squash cytology has proven invaluable in providing rapid and reliable diagnostic information. It allows immediate decision making during surgery, ensuring that appropriate therapeutic strategies can be implemented without delay. In this case, the cytologic findings of high cellularity, mitotic activity, and cellular cannibalism guided the histologic diagnosis and thus the surgical team in their approach. In conclusion, the case of this 5-year-old girl highlights the importance of cytologic features in the diagnosis of MB-LC/A. The observation of cell cannibalism not only helps to differentiate this aggressive subtype but also underlines the utility of intraoperative squash cytology in the management of pediatric brain tumors.
