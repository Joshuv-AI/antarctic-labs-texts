Retinal Microvasculature and Emotional, Behavioral, and Cognitive Outcomes at School Age
Authors: Romy Gonçalves, Serena Defina, Charlotte A. M. Cecil, Eric A. P. Steegers, Romy Gaillard, Vincent W. V. Jaddoe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe retinal microvasculature can serve as a window into the intracerebral vasculature, and an adverse retinal microvasculature is linked to neurocognitive disease and psychopathology in adults. We hypothesized that associations of retinal vessel calibers with emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes may be present in school‐age children.MethodsThis study among 3866 children was embedded in a population‐based cohort study from early fetal life. Retinal microvasculature measurements were obtained from retinal photographs at age 6. At ages 6, 10, and 14, total, internalizing, and externalizing problems and attention‐deficit hyperactivity disorder symptoms were measured using the Child Behavior Checklist/6–18. Autism symptoms were assessed by the Social Responsiveness Scale at ages 6 and 14. Estimated full‐scale intelligence quotient was assessed using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children‐Fifth Edition at age 14.ResultsA 1‐SD score wider retinal arteriolar caliber was associated with a −0.034 SD score (95% CI, −0.068 to −0.001; P value <0.05) lower externalizing problems (Child Behavior Checklist) score. However, this association attenuated into nonsignificance after adjustment for maternal and childhood confounders. No associations were observed for retinal arteriolar and venular calibers and total, internalizing, and externalizing problems (Child Behavior Checklist), attention‐deficit hyperactivity disorder symptoms (Child Behavior Checklist), autism symptoms (Social Responsiveness Scale), and estimated full‐scale intelligence quotient (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children‐Fifth Edition) at ages 6, 10, and 14.ConclusionsFrom our results, we cannot conclude that retinal vessel calibers at age 6 are associated with emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes at ages 6, 10, and 14. Longitudinal studies with repeated childhood measurements are needed to further explore these associations.

Vascular health is of great importance for brain health, cognition, and behavioral outcomes. 1 Vascular risk factors, such as diabetes, hypertension, and high lipids and high cholesterol concentrations, have all been associated with stroke and cognitive impairment in adults. 2 , 3 Hypertension and vascular dysfunction in childhood are associated with lower cognitive function already in children and adolescents. 4 Cerebrovascular disease is a major cause of cognitive impairment in both children and adults and is characterized by changes to the arterioles, capillaries, and small vessels, leading to impairment of white matter in subcortical brain structure. 5 , 6 The cerebral microvasculature cannot be visualized directly in vivo. However, the embryonic origin and the function and structure of the cerebral microvasculature are similar to that of the retinal microcirculation. 5 , 7 , 8 Previous studies have shown that noninvasive visualization of the retinal microcirculation could serve as an excellent marker of cerebral microvasculature and white matter damage. 9 , 10 Changes in the retinal microvasculature are associated with neurodegenerative disease, dementia, cognitive decline, and psychopathology in adults. 9 , 11 , 12 Some previous studies suggest that in children and adolescents, an unhealthy lifestyle is associated with adverse retinal vessel calibers. 13 , 14 In preterm‐born children, smaller arteriolar diameter was associated with lower IQ at 11 years old. 7 Also, among a general population of 4‐ and 5‐year‐olds, wider retinal venular diameter was associated with lower performance on a visual short‐term memory test. 15 In comparison to 12 year old children without attention‐deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), children with ADHD were observed to have a decreased tortuosity of the retinal arteries. In another study, a denser retinal artery network was associated with higher problems score especially on the hyperactivity/inattention and conduct problem domains. 16 , 17

We hypothesized that adverse retinal microvasculature as a proxy for impaired cerebrovascular health is associated with emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes already in childhood. To test this, we assessed in a population‐based cohort study of 3866 children the associations of retinal vessel calibers in children aged 6 years old with emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes at the ages of 6, 10, and 14 years old.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

Data from the Generation R study are available upon request to the director (generationr@erasmusmc.nl), subject to local rules and regulations.

Design

This study was embedded in the Generation R Study, a population‐based prospective cohort study from early fetal life onward. 18 Children born between April 2002 and January 2006, living in Rotterdam, the Netherlands, were eligible for participation. Included in the data collection were questionnaires, physical and ultrasound examinations, biological samples, and behavioral observations, which were performed in mothers, fathers, and children. Details on response and follow‐up have been described previously. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 We had information on retinal arteriolar and venular calibers in 4151 6‐year‐old children. Analyses were restricted to a subgroup (n=3866) of children for whom we had follow‐up information on emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes at either or both 6, 10, and 14 years old. A flow chart is given in Figure S1. Because children were invited for follow‐up visits regardless of participation in previous visits, our analytical outcome sample varies between 3444 and 2600 children depending on the age at measurement. Written informed consent was provided by all parents and children. The Medical Ethics Committee of Erasmus Medical Center approved the study (MEC‐2012‐165). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines. 23

Retinal Microvasculature Assessment

As described previously, at the median age of 6.0 (range 5.0–9.1) years old, retinal photographs were taken by a well trained staff in our dedicated research center. 24 After administering cyclopentolate 1% for pupil dilation, we used a Topcon digital retinal camera (model TRC, NW300; Oakland, NJ, USA) with which we took digital retinal photographs centered on the optic disc. 24 The photographs were taken in 1 eye with image resolutions 4096 and 3072 pixels. 24 The 6 largest retinal arterioles and venules positioned at one half to 1 disc diameter from the optic nerve were measured by a semiautomatic computer‐imaging program, as recommended in previous literature. 24 , 25 Using the revised Knudtson–Parr–Hubbard formula, absolute arteriolar and venular diameters were estimated in micrometers and subsequently were summarized as central retinal arteriolar and venular equivalents, representing the average arteriolar and venular caliber of that eye, respectively. 24 , 26 Graders, masked to participant characteristics, operated the computer program and monitored all retinal measurements and grader‐specific SD scores (SDS) for both central retinal and arteriolar equivalents were constructed. 24 Intraclass correlation coefficients between graders were 0.79 for retinal arteriolar caliber and 0.89 for retinal venular caliber, which suggest adequate reproducibility. 24

Emotional, Behavioral, and Cognitive Outcomes

At the median age of 6.0 (range 5.7–8.1), 9.7 (range 9.0–10.8) and 13.5 (range 12.6–17.1) years old, the primary caregiver was asked to complete the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL)/6–18. 27 , 28 The CBCL is an internationally validated and reliable measure of emotional and behavioral problems. 27 , 28 Based on the behavior of the child in the preceding 6 months, each of the 112 items is rated on a 3‐point scale: 0 (not true), 1 (somewhat true), 2 (very true or often true). Together, the 112 items result in the total behavioral problems score, a higher score indicating more emotional and behavioral problems. This total behavioral problems sum score can be subdivided into the internalizing and externalizing problems scale. Internalizing problems consist of the subscales, emotionally reactive and anxious/depressed symptoms, as well as somatic complaints and symptoms of being withdrawn. Externalizing problems consist of the subscales rule breaking and aggressive behavior. 27 , 29 The sum scores for the internalizing and externalizing problem scale were derived by summing the raw scores for each respective subscale of which the scale consists. We used the sum score of total behavioral problems, internalizing problems, externalizing problems and of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders‐oriented ADHD symptoms. The sum scores were natural log‐transformed to deal with a skewed distribution.

The primary caregiver also completed an adapted version of the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS), consisting of 18 items, at the median age of 6.0 (range 5.7–8.1) and 13.5 (range 12.6–17.1) years old. This abbreviated version shows high correlations with the full scale. 30 , 31 The SRS is a quantitative measure of autism symptoms for children aged between 4 and 18 years old. 30 , 32 , 33 Scoring is on a 4‐point Likert scale. The shortened 18‐item SRS contained items from 3 subscales: social cognition, social communication, and autistic mannerisms. The Cronbach's alpha indicated high interitem reliability for the SRS (α=0.92). The total sum score of autism symptoms was natural log‐transformed to deal with a skewed distribution.

Children's IQ was assessed using a subset of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children‐Fifth Edition (WISC‐V) also at the age of about 14 years old. The WISC‐V is an instrument assessing individual cognitive functioning in 6‐ to 16‐year‐olds. In collaboration with Pearson (Pearson Clinical Assessment, San Antonio, TX, USA), 4 core subtests from the WISC‐V were selected to assess specific cognitive domains and to derive an estimated full‐scale intelligence quotient (FSIQ). 34 The 4 subsets consisted of vocabulary, matrix reasoning, digit span, and coding. 29 For all outcomes, we constructed SDS (observed value−mean)/SD) within the population to enable comparison of effect estimates.

Covariates

We used a directed acyclic graph to identify potential covariates (Figure S2). We obtained information on sex, birth weight, gestational age at birth, ethnicity, maternal educational level, parity, gestational hypertensive disorders, smoking, and childhood daily physical activity using questionnaires and registries. 18 At median age of 6.0 years old, children were invited to visit the research facility and their height and weight were measured. We calculated sex‐ and gestational age adjusted body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2) SDS. At the same visits, systolic and diastolic blood pressure was measured and a venous blood sample was collected and serum glucose, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, and C‐reactive protein concentrations were obtained. 18 , 35 During the 6‐year follow‐up visit we used the 12‐item reliable and validated computerized short version of Raven's Progressive Matrices to assess maternal nonverbal cognitive ability. 36

Statistical Analysis

First, we described maternal and childhood characteristics. We performed a nonresponse analysis by comparing characteristics of children with retinal measurements and with and without outcome assessments by using independent Student t test, Mann–Whitney U and χ2 tests. Second, we used linear regression models to assess the associations of retinal vessel calibers with continuous scores for the total problems, internalizing and externalizing problems and ADHD symptoms at 6, 10, and 14 years old separately. Autism symptoms were assessed at 6 and 14 years old separately and estimated FSIQ solely at age 14 years old. Third, we used generalized linear mixed effect models to examine the associations of retinal vessel calibers and repeated measurements for total, internalizing, and externalizing problems and ADHD symptoms at 6, 10, and 14 years old, and for autism symptoms at 6 and 14 years old. Missing values in these models were handled by using full‐information maximum likelihood for the outcomes. For all analyses, the basic models were adjusted for child sex and age at outcome assessment. The confounder‐adjusted model, which we considered the main model, was additionally adjusted for maternal BMI at enrollment, maternal educational level, smoking during pregnancy, hypertensive disorders during pregnancy, maternal IQ, BMI, C‐reactive protein and cholesterol, systolic and diastolic blood pressure at 6‐year follow‐up. When assessing outcomes at 14 years old we additionally adjusted for physical activity at about 14 years old. Potential confounders were identified based on previous literature and we selected those that fulfilled the graphical criteria for confounding in a directed acyclic graph. We considered 3 groups of outcomes, namely CBCL derivatives, SRS scores, and estimated FSIQ. We corrected for multiple testing by using the Bonferroni method and specified significant P values as P<0.003. Missing data in covariates (ranging from 32.% to 0.1%) were multiple imputed using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method. Ten imputed data sets were created and analyzed together. 37 Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 26.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Retinal Vessel Calibers and Emotional, Behavioral, and Cognitive Outcomes

We did not observe associations of retinal vessel calibers and emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes at any age in these main models. However, in the corresponding basic models, given in Table S3, we observed that a 1‐SDS wider retinal arteriolar calibers were associated with a −0.034 SDS (95% CI, −0.068 to −0.001; P value <0.05) lower externalizing problems (CBCL) score. DISCUSSION

In this population‐based cohort study among 3866 children we examined the associations of retinal vessel calibers with emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes at different ages in school‐age children. We observed that wider retinal arteriolar calibers were associated with lower externalizing problems scores. This association attenuated into nonsignificance after adjustment for confounders. We observed no associations of retinal arteriolar and venular calibers with total, internalizing, and externalizing problems, ADHD symptoms, autism symptoms and estimated FSIQ at 6, 10, and 14 years old in our main models.

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in both women and men. 38 Vascular health throughout the whole body is of great importance for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease. 1 , 38 Cerebrovascular disease is a major cause of cognitive impairment and is characterized by changes to the arterioles, capillaries, and small vessels, leading to impairment of white matter in subcortical brain structure but also stroke or hemorrhage. 5 , 6 Retinal vessel calibers have been recognized as an excellent marker for risk stratification in cardiovascular disease. 39 Furthermore, the retinal microvasculature has its origin in the same embryonic structure and a similar function as does the cerebral microvasculature. 5 , 7 , 8 The retinal microvasculature thereby provides a window into the cerebral microvasculature and has been proven to also be an excellent marker for cerebrovascular health. 9 , 10 Changes in the retinal microvasculature have also been associated with neurodegenerative disease, dementia, cognitive decline, and even psychopathology. 9 , 11 , 12 In adults the association of an adverse retinal microvasculature and emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes has been widely established. 40

In children the relationship between the retinal microvasculature and cardiovascular disease has been less frequently examined. However, previous research has shown an association between childhood blood pressure, adiposity, sedentary behavior, and a lack of physical activity with adverse retinal vessel calibers. 14 , 41 Because previous research has established that risk factors such as obesity, glucose intolerance, and hypertension in childhood are associated with cardiovascular disease and premature death in later life, there seems to be an indication that adverse retinal vessel calibers in childhood are associated with adverse health in later life. 42 What remains largely unknown is how much time passes exactly between having risk factors both in childhood and adulthood, and the development of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.

We examined the associations of retinal vessel calibers with emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes and observed no associations. Our results are not in line with our hypothesis and with the results from the few previous studies. In comparison to 87 controls, 93 preterm‐born children were found to have smaller retinal arteriolar calibers at about 11 years old and subsequently had lower IQ's. 7 Another study among 251 children aged 4 to 6 years old, showed that widening of the retinal venular vessel was associated with poorer performance on cognitive testing and both retinal venular and arteriolar widening was associated with more errors given on a short‐term memory test. 15 Furthermore, a previous study in 190 children between 8 to 12 years old, found children to have higher problem scores, especially in the hyperactivity/inattention scales, when they had a denser retinal vascular network. 17 However, this last study did not find an association with retinal vessel diameters and problem scores or cognitive performance. 17 Also, in a study of 182 Belgian children, retinal vessel diameters were not associated with problem behavior in 6 to 11 years old. The main differences between these studies and ours rests in the study design and population. In our study we examined a much larger number of (predominantly low‐risk) children from the general population. In comparison to studies among children born preterm or with ADHD, our general population might be relatively more healthy. Also, our measurements of retinal vessel calibers were at about 6 years old and after a follow‐up period of maximum 7 years we measured emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes.

Following existing knowledge about risk factors involved in the development of cardiovascular disease and how they track from childhood into adulthood, our follow‐up period might simply be too short. 43 Lastly, the human body favorably protects its most important organ, the brain, 44 as early as in utero, where in children with intrauterine growth restriction the fetal body will preserve energy for the brain and displays brain sparing. 45 Also, in later age the body will deploy an energy conservation mechanism to suppress lower priority processes in favor of preserving energy for the brain. 44 There is evidence that in childhood the body has protective mechanisms to shield the brain from the negative effects of risk factors for cardiovascular disease. Among these mechanisms are greater neuroplasticity, the development of collateral circulation, repair of the endothelium, vascular resilience, and the development of a greater cognitive reserve. 46 , 47 , 48 At a young age, the body might adapt and be able to protect the brain as much as possible, delaying the time to complications from cerebrovascular risk factors. Meaning, in childhood the complications of an adverse retinal microcirculation as proxy for cerebrovascular health might not be detectable because the body's compensatory mechanisms still suffice to prevent neurodevelopmental consequences. The presence of these compensatory mechanisms may mask early subclinical adaptations, making assessment of the retinal microvasculature unlikely to provide reliable predictive information on neurocognitive development. Changes in the retinal microcirculation in later life might serve as marker of cerebrovascular vulnerability. However, our results suggest that a single school‐age retinal microvasculature measurement in a general population, is unsuitable as a screening tool for later emotional, behavioral, or cognitive development up to early adolescence. Future research should focus on longer follow‐up or explore alternative retinal markers such as retinal tortuosity, the retinal ganglion cell, or nerve fiber layer that have been associated with neurocognitive performance in childhood or later life. 17 , 49 , 50 , 51

Strengths of this study include the longitudinal study design, large number of participants from a general population, and extensive report on emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes. With these strengths, this study contributes to resolving the uncertainty suggested by prior research of context‐specific effects in high‐risk populations, such as preterm‐born children. This study also has limitations. Of the 9502 singleton live births only 3866 children had data regarding retinal vessel calibers and emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcome measurements. Children not included in our analyses were more often of non‐Dutch ethnicity and had higher BMI and blood pressure measurements. Also, their mothers were less often highly educated. This potential bias toward a relatively more healthy population may have led to an underestimation and therefore attenuation toward the null, of the assessed associations. This might affect the generalizability. Our results should therefore be viewed in the light of a relatively healthy general population and should only carefully be extrapolated to other populations. We used common, well‐validated tools for assessment of neurodevelopmental outcomes in children. However, a limitation is the reliance on parent‐reported data, which may introduce subjectivity due to parental mood or expectations. We adjusted for cardiovascular and metabolic health measurements at 6 years old, including BMI, C‐reactive protein, cholesterol, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Although these covariates were considered as confounders for the association of retinal microvasculature with emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes, they may also lay in the causal pathway between exposure and outcome. Thus, these cardiovascular and metabolic health measurements could have attenuated the effects. When, however, we compared the results of the basic and fully adjusted models, we did not observe differences in associations, suggesting that adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors did not attenuate any association. We did not formally correct for multiple testing. Because our analyses were hypothesis driven and our findings were largely null, the risk of type 1 errors are limited but not absent. Our outcomes were measured after a relatively short follow‐up period, with the measurements at about 6 years old even being cross‐sectional. Although we adjusted for a large number of potential confounders, residual confounding might still be a possibility also due to the observational nature of the study.

CONCLUSIONS

From our results, we cannot conclude that retinal vessel calibers at 6 years old are associated with emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes at 6, 10, and 14 years old. Further longitudinal studies using repeated childhood measurements are necessary to assess if the retinal microvasculature in childhood is associated with neurocognitive disease and psychopathology in later life.
