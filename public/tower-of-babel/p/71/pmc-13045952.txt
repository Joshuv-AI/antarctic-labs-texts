T-tube insertion as a salvage procedure for failed primary repair in penetrating esophageal injury complicated by sepsis: a case report from low resource setting
Authors: Bienfait Mumbere Vahwere, Suzan Nabulobi, Okullo Obong, Byamugisha Frednan, Arinda Tracy, Yasa Abdullahi Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Esophageal perforation is a life-threatening emergency associated with high morbidity and mortality.Case presentation:A 28-year-old female presented with a cervical esophageal stab injury. An initial primary repair failed, and the patient developed severe sepsis. Surgical re-exploration revealed two esophageal perforations. The lateral perforation was repaired, and a T-tube was inserted through the anterior defect to create a controlled fistula. A feeding jejunostomy was also performed. The patient recovered fully following 9 weeks of T-tube drainage and enteral feeding.Clinical discussion:Cervical esophageal perforations present unique management challenges due to their proximity to vital structures and the high risk of mediastinal sepsis. Primary repair is preferred when feasible; however, failure can result in uncontrolled leakage and infection. In such cases, T-tube drainage provides a controlled fistula that allows decompression, sepsis control, and spontaneous closure once healing occurs. Compared to advanced techniques such as endoscopic stenting or vacuum-assisted closure, T-tube diversion is simple, inexpensive, and suitable for resource-limited environments. Adequate drainage, nutritional support through feeding jejunostomy, and close follow-up are critical to achieving favourable outcomes.Conclusion:T-tube drainage is a valuable salvage option in the management of complicated oesophageal perforations in resource-limited settings and nutritional support through feeding jejunostomy are key to optimizing outcomes.

Introduction

Esophageal perforation is a rare but severe surgical emergency, often resulting from penetrating trauma, iatrogenic causes, or spontaneous rupture. It carries a high-mortality rate, particularly in cases of delayed diagnosis or when initial repair fails[1,2].

In Sub-Saharan Africa, the challenge is compounded by limited access to diagnostics and intensive care support. In such settings, the use of a T-tube to create a controlled fistula offers a viable alternative for managing complex esophageal injuries, especially when primary repair fails and sepsis develops[3]. Experience from this case add new insights of adding feeding jejunostomy tube to avoid malnutrition challenges as it has been reported among complications while using T-Tube as alternative compare to total parenteral nutrition, which may have more complications and be expensive and difficult to access especially in low-resource settings.

This case report presents the successful use of a T-tube in a patient with a penetrating cervical esophageal injury, initially treated with primary repair, which failed and led to sepsis. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[4].

Case presentation

A 28-year-old female of Acholi ethnicity, para two with no known chronic illness or allergy was referred to our facility from a neighboring district 4 h after being stabbed in the neck during an altercation on July 5, 2023. She presented with bleeding, air leakage from the wound, and difficulty swallowing. Initial vital signs were: BP 110/60 mmHg, pulse 110 bpm, GCS 15/15. After resuscitation following the Advanced Life Trauma Life Support (ATLS), the patient was taken to theater. Rigid esophagoscopy confirmed a distal cervical esophageal perforation. A primary repair was performed after debridement, and a nasogastric tube was inserted. No airway injury was noted. No mediastinal drainage was left around the surgical site. Investigations done: Full blood count on 7th/7/2023, the leucocytes were 10.5 × 109/L with 81% of neutrophils and Hb 14 g.

The surgical site started leaking saliva on the second postoperative day, July 8, 2023, and revealed widespread cellulitis and necrosis of the anterior neck and upper anterior chest (Fig. 1). The patient experienced severe sepsis by day 3, July 9, 2023 (temperature: 39°C, blood pressure: 90/48 mmHg, pulse: 118 bpm, SPO2: 84%, RR 26 cycles per minute). The SIRS was used to diagnose sepsis related to surgical site infection and failure of initial closure of a traumatic penetrating esophageal injury, indicating the need for a second operation. The Clavian Dindo classification was used to classify this postoperative complication as grade IV. Figure 1.Visualisation of the esopheageal perforation after debriedment of the surgical site.

Visualisation of the esopheageal perforation after debriedment of the surgical site.

Re-exploration under general anesthesia was done following aseptic technique and revealed two perforationson the esophagus: a lateral defect (1 cm) was closed primarily; a larger anterior defect (2 cm) was fitted with a rigid T-tube (Fig. 1). The surrounding edges were refreshed and sutured to reduce the perforation over the tube. A rigid silicone T-tube (size 16 Fr) inserted through the anterior defect, secured with interrupted Vicryl suture number 2/0. Tube arms aligned longitudinally; the skin was with nylon sutures number 2/0. T-Tube was connected on urinary for drainage, which was bilious initially mixed with saliva and later cleared. Following aseptic technique, a feeding jejunostomy Tube (Using 24 G two-way Foley’s catheter) was inserted for enteral nutrition as shown in Fig. 2. Following the procedure, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for observation. There, they were given IV PISA 4.5 g every 8 hours for 5 days, IV Tramadol 100 mg every 8 hours for 7 days, IV Paracetamol 1 g every 8 hours for 7 days, IV Rabeprazole 40 mg once daily for 7 days, and IV fluids 3 L of NaCl 0.9% and Dextrose 10% in a ratio of 1.5:1.5 in a 24 hours.

We began enteral feeding on July 11 and continued antibiotics and analgesics as prescribed on July 7, 2023, full-blood count was repeated and revealed leucocytes were7.33 × 109/L and all signs of sepsis had subside. On August 19, 2023, patient had stabilized, full-blood count was repeated and revealed leucocytes were7.33 × 109/L and Hb 12 g.

The T-tube remained in situ for height weeks to allow for fistula formation. It was then removed on September 12 under general anesthesia, Since The T tube was rigid, the external part of the tube was resected then removed from the surgical site as the longitudinal arm of the tube removed was held and by a long Alligator via rigid esophagoscopy. A nasogastric tube was inserted temporarily. A stoma bag was put over the fistulato drain the fistula until it stopped draining (Fig. 3). the fistula site healed within 5 days. On September 22, Barium swallow was done which reported normal esophagus, no fistula (results accessible on request due to privacy). Oral feeding resumed gradually, and the patient was discharged with no complications. Figure 3.Surgical site convered with stoma bag after removal of the tube to drain the created fistula.

On September 29, 2023, full-blood count was repeated and revealed that the leucocytes were 5.5 × 109/L. Feeding tube was removed and wound dressing of the surgical site of feeding tube was done which healed

The patient was discharged after 9 weeks of hospitalization after fully recovery. At 2 months, she had full oral intake and normal barium swallow. At 9-month phone follow-up, she reported no dysphagia, hypertrophic scar, or keloid formation.

Discussion

Traumatic esophageal perforations are challenging due to their rarity and diagnostic difficulty. Mortality rates range between 20% and 30%, rising to over 60% in delayed presentations[5]. When treatment is started, the reported death rate from treated esophageal perforation ranges from 10% to 25%.

Primary closure of the perforation site and wide drainage of the mediastinum is recommended if perforation is detected in less than 24 hours[2]. However, in our case, this was not done which could have resulted into primary repair failure.

Treatment option for delayed or missed rupture of oesophagus is not very clear and is controversial. Recently a substantial number of patients with esophageal perforation are being managed by nonoperative measures. Patients with small perforations and minimal extraesophageal involvement may be better managed by nonoperative treatment[6].

The management strategy of penetrating oesophageal injury depends on factors such as injury location, time of diagnosis, and patient stability[7]. While primary closure is preferred in early presentations, it can fail, especially when tissue viability is compromised or sepsis sets in. In such cases, diversion techniques or T-tube drainage are warranted[8].

T-tube insertion allows for controlled external drainage, reducing mediastinal contamination and facilitating healing[3]. This method is particularly suitable in low-resource settings where advanced interventions (e.g., esophageal stenting or vacuum therapy) may not be available[9]. Treatment options for delayed or missed rupture of oesophagus is not very clear and is controversial[2]. Nonetheless, nonoperative therapy could be more effective for individuals with minor perforations and little traesophageal contact. An alternative option to the use of T-tube, esophageal stents can be used to address esophageal anastomotic leaks and perforations endoscopically and appears to be both safe and effective when used in conjunction with pleural or mediastinal drainage[10].

Nowadays, endoscopic vacuum treatment (EVT) and covered self-expanding stents are the mainstays of organ-preserving care, with good closure rates and increasing acceptance among thoracic and gastrointestinal surgeons Although more experience with the method and indications is needed to increase success rates, several recent evaluations show high technical/clinical success with EVT and stents and advocate them early, relegating T-tubes to select and delayed patients[11].

A second treatment is frequently required for removal, which many centers would want to avoid if a stent or EVT can close earlier. T-tubes typically need weeks to months in situ, with continuing fistula maintenance, skin care, and nutritional assistance[12]. Whereas T-tubes are useful in certain situations, particularly those with delayed or highly contaminated perforations, they can cause persistent fistula, local infection, and subsequent stricture. Primary repair, drainage, stenting, and EVT are highlighted in current reviews and society primers[13]; T-tube is mentioned but not given much attention, resulting in fewer surgeons being exposed again[14].

There are fewer traditional “late, friable, septic” situations where T-tubes are useful since more patients are suitable for nonoperative treatment or endoscopic therapy due to earlier detection using CT scan /contrast swallow, endoscopy[6].

Our case supports existing literature advocating for T-tube use in delayed or complicated oesophageal injuries, especially when primary repair fails and the patient develops sepsis.

Structured complication grading using Clavian–Dindo classification aids comparability. In this case, sepsis requiring ICU care was Grade IV after the first surgery, while subsequent recovery without reoperation classified as Grade I postoperative phase after Tube insertion and removal.

The low-resource environment influenced decision-making: absence of stenting, limited ICU capacity, and restricted nutrition options necessitated the T-tube plus jejunostomy strategy. The favourable outcome demonstrates that well-executed traditional techniques can yield excellent results even without advanced technology.

Conclusion

Primary repair, remains the gold standard in early-diagnosed oesophageal injuries and stenting for complex cases though it requires experienced hands and costly. However, when sepsis or repair failure occurs especially in low-resource settings T-tube drainage with feeding jejunostomy offers a safe, cost-effective salvage technique. Structured postoperative evaluation and long-term follow-up (≥6 months) are essential to identify late complications, such as stricture or dysphagia.
