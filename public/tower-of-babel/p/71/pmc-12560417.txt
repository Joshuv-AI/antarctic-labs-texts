Update on new autoinflammatory disorders from the 2024 Pediatric Rheumatology European Society Congress
Authors: Grant S. Schulert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Since the molecular characterization of periodic fever syndromes led to the concept of autoinflammation, the pace of scientific advancement in this field has been dramatic. Here, we review many of the most impactful new discoveries in autoinflammation, as presented at the 2024 Pediatric Rheumatology European Society Congress. This includes new genes and diseases, such as SHARPIN mutations and dominant-negative mutations in OTULIN as causes of disorders of ubiquitination, PMVK mutations as potential causes of a mevalonate kinase deficiency mimic, and ARF1 and REXO2 as causes of interferonopathy. Several new molecular mechanisms and mutations were also reported for older diseases including coatomer protein complex subunit alpha (COPA) syndrome, Aicardi-Goutières syndrome, PLCG2-associated immune dysregulation (PLAID), and NLRP3-AID. Finally, molecular and omics studies of STING1-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI) and haploinsufficiency of A20 (HA20) contributed to advances in underlying autoinflammatory biology.

Introduction

The concept of autoinflammation was proposed in the late 1990 s and early 2000 s, with the molecular characterization of the underlying causes of hereditary periodic fever syndromes including Familial Mediterranean Fever, TNF-receptor Associated Periodic Syndrome (TRAPS), and Cryopyrin-Associated Periodic Syndrome (CAPS) [1]. Along with defining the genetic basis of these disorders and ultimately leading to development of targeted biologic therapy for such inflammasomeopathies [2], this contributed to critical advances in our fundamental understanding of inflammation and innate immunity.

The subsequent decades have seen the identification of dozens of genetically distinct autoinflammatory disorders, with often overlapping clinical syndromes but molecularly unique disease mechanisms [3]. These discoveries have led to improved diagnosis of patients with autoinflammatory phenotypes, and the end to often long and costly diagnostic odysseys. Additionally, discovering the genetic basis of autoinflammatory disorders has led to recognition of new disease paradigms – from the inflammasomeopathies to interferonopathies [4], disorders of NF-kB [5], and disorders of ubiquitination [6]. Here, we review new discoveries in autoinflammation through the fall 2024, with a focus on new genes and diseases (Table 1), older diseases with new mechanisms and mutations, and advances in autoinflammatory biology.

New autoinflammatory disease associations

Disorders of ubiquitination: expanding the chain gang

Over the past decade, numerous inborn errors of immunity and autoinflammatory syndromes have been identified related to disorders of ubiquitination [6] (Fig. 1). Ubiquitin is a critical post-translational modification that controls proteasomal degradation in cells. Ubiquitination occurs in three steps: first, ubiquitin is activated by enzymes such as UBA1 and UBA6; second, this activated ubiquitin is transferred to one of several dozen ubiquitin E2 enzymes; and finally, these E2 enzymes together with ubiquitin E3 ligases transfer ubiquitin to specific residues on the substrate protein [7]. Critically, specific E3 ligases can target a host of proteins, while specific proteins can be modified by multiple ligases. The specific pattern of ubiquitination impacts protein function and ultimate fate. There are also deubiquitylases (DUBs) that remove or trim ubiquitin modifications from proteins. The role of ubiquitination in autoinflammation has been strikingly highlighted with the identification of vacuoles, E1 activating enzyme, X-linked autoinflammatory somatic (VEXAS) syndrome, a largely adult-onset disorder caused by somatic mutations in UBA1 [8]. Patients with VEXAS have acquired, myeloid-lineage mutations in UBA1 on the X-chromosome, leading to loss-of-function of UBA1. While the precise mechanism is not fully elucidated, VEXAS patients demonstrate activation of the unfolded protein response and interferon pathway. Clinically these patients present with multisystem inflammation with features resembling relapsing polychondritis, rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis, and myelodysplastic syndrome. Along with VEXAS, other autoinflammatory disorders linked to ubiquitination include haploinsufficiency of A20, which has both ubiquitin E3 ligase and deubiquitylate activities [9]; E3 ubiquitin ligase X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis (XIAP) deficiency [10]; and proteasome disorders including proteasome-associated autoinflammatory syndromes (PRAAS) [11] and chronic atypical neutrophilic dermatosis and lipodystrophy and elevated temperature (CANDLE) [12] among others.

New work has expanded the spectrum of disorders linked to linear ubiquitination, where ubiquitin molecules are linked together in a head-to-tail manner, using the N-terminal methionine of ubiquitin. This is mediated by the linear ubiquitin chain assembly complex (LUBAC), an E3 ligase complex of several proteins, and removed by the DUB OTULIN [13]. Rare autosomal recessive mutations in the LUBAC components haem-oxidized IRP2 ubiquitin ligase 1 (HOIL-1) and HOIL-1-interactive protein (HOIP) lead to a systemic autoinflammatory disorder [14, 15]. Interestingly, despite the dichotomous functions from LUBAC, biallelic mutations in OTULIN lead to a clinically similar condition known as OTULIN-related autoinflammatory syndrome (ORAS) [16, 17]. Oda et al. recently reported two individuals with homozygous mutations in the LUBAC component SHANK-associated RH domain interactor (SHARPIN) [18]. These children had a syndrome they termed “sharpenia” with recurrent fever, multisystem inflammation, and immune deficiency, but without dermatitis which is seen in mice lacking this enzyme. SHARPIN mutations were associated with impaired LUBAC expression and NF-kB -mediated signaling but excessive TNF-induced cell death. Mutations in both patients and mice demonstrated inflammatory arthritis, and human LUBAC deficiencies including sharpenia had defective B cell activation and germinal center formation. Sharpenia patients showed clinical and immunological improvement with TNF inhibitor treatment. This work expands both the genetic and functional understanding of LUBAC-related disorders. A critical topic of future work is to explore how mutations that cause opposing molecular functions – LUBAC and deubiquitylase deficiencies – can cause a similar clinical phenotype.

Two separate studies also reported patients with autoinflammatory phenotypes linked to heterozygous, dominant-negative mutations in OTULIN, distinct from the biallelic mutations seen in ORAS. Davidson et al. reported two patients with an identical de novo C129S mutation in the OTULIN catalytic domain [19]. The patients demonstrated fevers, systemic inflammation, severe cutaneous disease with panniculitis and pathergy, lung disease, and immune deficiency. In parallel, Takeda et al. reported a patient with s similar clinical phenotype and a de novo R306Q mutation in OTULIN (along with a P152L variant inherited from a healthy parent) [20]. Davidson et al. showed that the C129S variant affects OTULIN deubiquitinase activity, driving accumulation of linear ubiquitin chains in the presence of a wild-type protein. While the variant had minimal effects on NF-kB signaling, patient-derived fibroblasts showed excessive TNF-induced cell death and interferon-driven inflammation. Together, authors proposed that this mutation alters OTULIN into an inactive receptor without otherwise impacting the LUBAC complex. Takeda et al. further demonstrated that cells derived from their patient showed increased linear ubiquitination, and that the R306Q variant specifically reduces OTULIN deubiquitinase activity and augments TNF-induced cell death. Heterozygous expression of this OTULIN variant was sufficient to drive linear ubiquitination and augmented LUBAC-mediated NF-kB activation in a dominant-negative manner. These paired studies expand the understanding of the ORAS spectrum, and the complex role of linear ubiquitination in inflammation. Further work with complex animal model systems will provide important confirmation of the causative role of such mutations in autoinflammation.

New genes and new diseases

One of the first identified and most common monogenic autoinflammatory disorders is mevalonate kinase deficiency (MKD) [21]. This metabolic autoinflammatory disorder is caused by typically biallelic mutations in mevalonate kinase (MVK), which catalyzes the conversion of mevalonate to mevalonate-6 phosphate. This leads to a spectrum of disease severity based on residual mevalonate kinase activity, with more mild patients presented with a periodic fever syndrome initially termed Hyper IgD Syndrome (HIDS), while more severe patients have mevalonic aciduria and perinatal onset multisystem involvement including hepatic and CNS inflammation. Heterozygous mutations in MVK, along with other genes in the pathway, are also associated with porokeratosis [22]. While the mechanism of MKD is not fully understood, dysfunctional isoprenoid biosynthesis likely has multiple cellular consequences, including abnormal prenylation of small guanosine triphosphatases leading to dysregulation of the pyrin inflammasome [23]. Recently, several patients were identified with an autoinflammatory phenotype and mutations in phosphomevalonate kinase (PMVK), immediately downstream of MVK in the mevalonate pathway. Yildiz et al. reported a patient with recurrent fevers, arthritis, gastrointestinal involvement, elevated IgD, and elevated urine mevalonic acid, consistent with HIDS [24]. However, while no mutations in MVK were identified, the patient carried compound heterozygous mutations in PMVK (Arg110Gln and Val106Met, in trans). However, no functional studies or measurements of PMVK protein were reported to further explore this intriguing association. Berner et al. reported a similar patient with fever, systemic inflammation, arthritis, and mucocutaneous involvement, found to have homozygous Val131Ala variants in PMVK [25]. This team was able to demonstrate that the patient had severely diminished PMVK activity and expression levels but normal MVK levels. They also demonstrated accumulation of unprenylated RAP1A guanosine triphosphate. Lastly, Jairaman et al. reported a child with fever, recurrent arthritis, and gastrointestinal involvement, and found to carry homozygous Ala133Val variants in PMVK [26]. Patient-derived mononuclear cells showed enhanced resting and LPS-stimulated IL-1β secretion, which was rescued by simvastatin, an inhibitor of the mevalonate pathway. While not definitive, this work supports a causative role for PMVK mutations. Notably, several of these patients showed good response to IL-1 blockade. While these studies lack definitive molecular studies to prove the functional consequences of PMVK mutations, they strongly suggest that other defects in the mevalonate pathway could lead to autoinflammation that resembles MKD.

Nucleic acid sensors such as cyclic GMP-AMP synthetase (cGAMP) and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) are critical pattern recognition receptors for host defense. The consequences of excessive activation of this pathway are vividly illustrated in patients with STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI), who presented with an early onset interferonopathy with severe cutaneous and pulmonary features and autoimmunity [27]. A key regulator of cGAS-STING is through autophagocytic degradation or recycling via retrograde Golgi to endoplasmic reticulum transport. ADP-ribosylating factor 1 (ARF1) is a small GTPase which is known to have a key role in regulating such intracellular trafficking through recruiting COPI components [28]. Hirschenberger et al. recently reported a patient with a high interferon signature, chilblain lupus, and developmental delay, found to have a de novo mutation in ARF1 R99C [29]. Two additional patients were later identified with the same mutation and similar skin lesions and developmental delay, along with a patient with an R99H variant with history of cold digits without frank chilblains. Functionally, these authors demonstrated that both patient-derived cells and model cell lines expressing these mutations showed an interferon signature with increased cGAS-STING signaling. They also demonstrated that this inflammation was driven by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-induced cGAS activation, and that mutant ARF1 caused mitochondrial destabilization. Mutant ARF1 showed impaired GTPase activity, association with COPI complex, and retrograde transport, leading to trapping of STING at the Golgi-ERGIC. Their work supports a dual role of ARF1 in regulating both STING trafficking and stabilizing mitochondria, which when defective leads to interferonopathy.

Further intersection of mitochondrial stability and interferonopathy were illustrated through the identification of REXO2 mutations. REXO2 is a mitochondrial RNA exonuclease with critical roles in mtRNA degradation, and loss causes accumulation of non-coding mtRNA. Previously, mutations in this gene have been linked to malignancies, and single-nucleotide polymorphisms have been found to be risk alleles for inflammatory bowel disease [30]. Idiiatullina et al. reported a child with severe neonatal-onset dermatitis, systemic inflammation with interferon activation, and recurrent infections, found to have a de novo mutation in REXO2 (T132A) [31]. This variant was predicted pathogenic and lacked ability to cleave RNA. Expression of this variant in a mononuclear cell line caused and increased interferon response, which was dependent on melanoma differentiation associated protein 5 (MDA5) and mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) and associated with release of mtRNA into the cytosol. Finally, mutant REXO2 had a dominant-negative effect, binding to wild-type protein and inhibiting its RNA cleavage in a dose-dependent fashion. This supports a model where the absence of REXO2 drives accumulation of cellular nucleic acids and interferonopathy. These newly recognized associations would support examination of other variants in mitochondrial related genes as they relate to cellular stress and driving interferonopathy.

Old(er) diseases, new mechanisms and mutations

The coatomer protein complex subunit alpha (COPA) protein is a critical component of Golgi-derived non-clathrin-coated vesicles and is essential for intracellular vesicular traffic in cells. Dominant-negative mutations in small WD40 domain of COPA cause a syndrome characterized by autoimmunity, interstitial lung disease, arthritis, and nephropathy termed COPA syndrome [32]. However, other domains of this protein have been less well defined, including the C-terminus, but appeared to be involved in COP complex I (COPI) integrity, binding to COP subunit epsilon, and vesicle tethering to the ER. Delafontaine et al. recently reported 6 patients from 3 unrelated families with autoimmunity and autoinflammation including ILD, nephropathy, neuromyelitis optica, and transverse myelitis [33]. Patients in all three families were found to have heterozygous, c-terminal mutations in COPA that cause COPI dysfunction. These authors showed in cell lines that COPA mutations impacted COPI integrity, formation, and/or stability, leading to impaired antegrade and retrograde ER-Golgi trafficking. Several patients had enhanced type I interferon signaling, but in contrast to WD40 domain variants does not induce STING-dependent signaling. Rather, these mutations caused activation of ER stress response pathways including NF-kB activation, enhancement of the unfolded protein response and disturbed autophagy. Taken together, this work demonstrates that mutations in different regions of the same gene can cause an autoinflammatory syndrome but through a markedly distinct mechanism, with implications for treatment of patients with COPA mutations which has previously focused on classical immune suppressants rather than targeting interferon.

Aicardi-Goutières syndrome (AGS) is an autoinflammatory interferonopathy associated with systemic and CNS inflammation [34]. Mutations in several genes have been liked to AGS, but among the most common is TREX1, encoding a 3’−5’ exonuclease that degrades cytosolic DNA, thus limited activation of cGAS-STING. While many AGS-associated TREX1 mutations cause loss-of-function or a dominant-negative effect, but many other disease-associated mutations reported are outside the core catalytic domain and the mechanisms remain unclear. Shim et al. reported two patients with features of AGS and an elevated ISG score, with Pro61Gln mutations in TREX1 [35] in the poorly characterized polyproline helix (PPII) motif. Indeed this variant had no effect on in vitro or in vivo exonuclease activity or ER localization of TREX1, and it can suppress cGAS-STING signaling in cell lines. Rather these PPII mutations destabilized TREX1, leading to impaired exonuclease function, thus causing unrestrained cGAS-STING activation. Together, this proposes a novel mechanism by which TREX1 variants can lead to interferonopathy and critically highlights the importance of understanding not only the enzymatic function of a variant but the function in its cellular context.

One of the first genetically identified causes of autoinflammatory syndromes were mutations in NLRP3, encoding cryopyrin, leading to NLRP3-associated autoinflammatory diseases (NLRP3-AID, formerly the cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome, CAPS) [36]. However, NLRP3-AID is phenotypically diverse, ranging from the initially described familial cold urticaria to Muckle-Wells syndrome to the potentially life-threatening neonatal-onset multisystem inflammatory disease (NOMID). Since it was revealed that these disorders are linked through dominantly-inherited mutations in NLRP3, the spectrum of this disorder has been intensely studied, and while there is substantial genotype-phenotype correlation, this is not always absolute. In addition, there is poor functional characterization of most disease-associated variants, as well as several relatively high-frequency variants of unknown clinical significance (VUCS). Cosson et al. reported an exhaustive functional study of 34 disease-associated NLRP3 variants, which they characterized in vitro for inflammasome induction, priming, and activation signals, and response to four inhibitors, with cell death as readout [37]. Through this work, they identified 5 functionally distinct groups of mutations. Group 1 variants were identical to WT NLRP3 in that they required priming with LPS and activation with nigericin to induce cell death. Interestingly most of these variants have been classified as VUCS, and this work would support that they in fact have little functional consequence. In contrast, Group 2 variants could responded to activation signals without a requirement for priming, in clear distinction to WT. The functional role for many group 2 variants has also long been debated given their relatively high prevalence and incomplete penetrance; this work for the first time proposes a molecular mechanism by which they could trigger autoinflammation. Group 3 variants required priming but not activation signals, while group 4 variants induced cell death with either priming or activation. Finally, group 5 variants induced cell death upon protein expression alone without requirement for either priming or activation, consistent with constitutively active mutations. This meticulous study not only confirmed distinct functional mechanisms for different NLRP3 variants but helped support certain VUCS as potentially pathogenic or likely benign.

A similarly ambitious study examined the functional classes and presentations of patients with PLCG2-associated immune dysregulation (PLAID). PLCg2 is a pleotropic signaling molecular in hematopoietic cells, and dominant mutations in PLCG2 lead to highly penetrant but also highly variably expressed features including immune deficiency, autoinflammation, and autoimmunity [38, 39]. Baysac et al. examined a large collection of patients with their corresponding mutations expressed in a human B cell line [40]. While most variants showed B cell alterations, only some caused a gain-of-function phenotype that previously was described as autoinflammatory PLAID (aPLAID). The largest number of variants instead caused monoallelic loss-of-function and was often associated with the original PLAID phenotype with cold urticaria, atopy, immune deficiency, and autoimmunity. While immune deficiency and autoinflammation could be seen in association with both gain and loss of function variants, this work also identified a third, new phenotypic cluster, which included humoral immune deficiency, autoinflammation, susceptibility to infection with herpesviruses, and NK dysfunction. This symptomatology was only observed in association with certain heterozygous LOF variants, which authors propose as PLAID haploinsufficiency. Further mechanistic work using complex systems such as animal models is needed to fully elucidate the impact of this new class of variants. This work highlights the phenotypic spectrum caused by PLCG2 variants, including distinct molecular mechanisms and cellular consequences.

Advances in autoinflammatory biology

Several new studies also offered critical new functional insights into previously recognized disorders. As noted above, SAVI causes systemic inflammation, vasculopathy, cutaneous and pulmonary disease [27]. STING1 mutations induce type I interferon activation, but likely other pathways as well. de Cevins et al. performed a multiomics study of SAVI including immune profiling of PBMC using scRNA-seq as well as plasma proteomics [41]. This work found the multiple populations of immune cells displayed a type I interferon response, but also NF-kB activation and signs of cellular stress and cell death. Lymphocytes in particular were noted to show an interferon-associated hyperactivated signature with features of senescence. They also identified a population of disease-associated monocytes with hyperinflammation and an interferon signature with a UPR. Informatically predicted cellular interactions suggested that these monocytes could drive both activation and cell death in effector T cells. Additionally, by using an innovative approach of examining in vitro interferon-stimulated PBMC in parallel with SAVI patient cells, they identified a 21-gene transcriptional signature of SAVI independent of IFNb, but high in TNF and KRAS signaling pathways. Coincidentally, the authors noted that one of their SAVI patients had a distinctive transcriptional signature with much more diminished UPR. Genetic analysis of this patient identified a variant in EIF2AK3, encoding a regulator of the UPR called PERK. Together, this work maps the interferon signature in SAVI including potential immune cell crosstalk, identifies interferon-independent gene signatures, and demonstrates how the overall genetic landscape may impact the molecular phenotype of SAVI.

Another complex and clinically variable inborn error of immunity is haploinsufficiency of A20 (HA20), in which some heterozygous mutations in TNFAIP3 cause childhood onset autoinflammation with frequent Behcet’s phenotype [9]. Among the many functions of A20 is its ability to restrict proliferation of B and T lymphocytes, and interestingly somatic A20 mutations have recently been identified as a driver in non-Hodgkin lymphomas [42]. Schultheib et al. examined this overlap between HA20, immune dysregulation, and lymphoma [43], and showed that HA20 patients have skewed T and B cell repertoires, which overlap with antigen signatures of TNFAIP3-mutant lymphomas. This was then linked to a specific B cell antigen receptor IGHV4-34. Interestingly, TNF blockade, which is clinically effective for some HA20 patients, also reversed this lymphomas-like immune repertoire, through a proliferative autocrine TNF activation loop. Finally, they demonstrated that HA20 lymphocytes can transform into lymphoma cells and on a single cell level visualize that HA20 patients can have lymphoma-like transcriptional signatures. Together, this work broadens our understanding of the immunologic effects of A20, including the critical link between immune dysregulation, somatic mutations, and malignant transformation.

Conclusions

Since the articulation of the autoinflammatory paradigm, researchers around the world have identified a multitude of molecular defects that can lead to human disease. Along with transforming the care of such patients, these “experiments of nature” have contributed immeasurable to our understanding of inflammation. The recent work reviewed here shows we continue to find new genetic causes of autoinflammation that broaden known disease mechanisms (interferonopathy, ubiquitination, isoprenoid pathway). The elegant pairing of genetic and functional analysis expands the understanding of how novel mutations lead to disease – often showing different phenotypes can exist within the same gene. Finally, translational genomics approaches drive a further understanding of the immune landscape and consequences of autoinflammation. Several new concepts are also highlighted – including the critical need to examine cellular context together with molecular function, and how multiple genes and variants can interplay to drive phenotypes. Ultimately such work is expected to return to the bedside and support more specific and personalized treatments for patients with immune dysregulation and autoinflammation.
