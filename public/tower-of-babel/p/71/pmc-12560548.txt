Factors associated with postoperative rebound pain after peripheral nerve block anaesthesia for orthopedic trauma surgery: a retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Yi-Min Wang, Jia-Ni Liang, De-Cheng Liu, Bing-Cheng Zhao, Hui Zhang, Hong-Fei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative rebound pain, refers to an acute exacerbation of pain severity following the resolution of a peripheral nerve block (PNB). This transient but intense pain typically manifests within 24 h after block administration. Despite its clinical significance, rebound pain remains poorly characterized in trauma surgery populations.ObjectiveThis retrospective cohort study aimed to investigate the incidence and associated risk factors of rebound pain in orthopedic trauma surgery patients receiving PNB anaesthesia.MethodsAdult patients undergoing orthopedic trauma surgery with preoperative PNB between April 2023 and March 2025 were included. Rebound pain was operationally defined as a transition from well-controlled pain (numerical rating scale [NRS] ≤ 3) to severe pain (NRS ≥ 7) within 24 h post-block. Potential associations with rebound pain including patient characteristics, surgical and anesthetic factors were examined by using univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses.ResultsFour hundred and thirty-five (41.9%) of 1037 included patients experienced postoperative rebound pain. Multivariable analysis showed that the factors independently associated with rebound pain were younger age (OR 0.797; 95% CI [0.655–0.971]), diabetes (OR 1.439; 95% CI [1.249–2.832]), preoperative moderate-to-severe pain (OR 2.353; 95% CI [2.234–2.543]), preoperative Neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR) elevation (OR 2.034; 95% CI [1.759–2.454]), and surgery involving bone (OR 2.786; 95% CI [2.553–3.147]), but a lower risk of rebound pain was perioperative i.v. dexamethasone (OR 0.462; 95% CI [0.304–0.632]) or i.v. dexmedetomidine (OR 0.363; 95% CI [0.238–0.545]). Despite a high incidence of rebound pain, there were high rates of patient satisfaction (80.5%), and there was no statistical difference in sleep quality.ConclusionsPostoperative rebound pain showed associations with younger age, diabetes, preoperative moderate-to-severe pain, preoperative NLR elevation, bone surgery, and absence of intraoperative use of i.v. dexamethasone or dexmedetomidine. The current data suggest that preventive measures remain clinically warranted, especially for at higher risk patients, though future studies are needed to refine optimal protocols.Keywords: Orthopedic trauma surgery, Pain management, Peripheral nerve block, Postoperative rebound pain, Regional anaesthesia

Introduction

An increasing number of orthopedic surgeries are conducted in a trauma setting under peripheral nerve block (PNB), where patients often present with acute pain, and these operations can be associated with severe postoperative pain [1]. PNBs are considered an essential part of multimodal analgesia, particularly advantageous due to their ability to provide rapid, site-specific analgesia while minimizing hemodynamic instability [1, 2]. PNBs can provide targeted pain relief for the initial duration of 6–8 h, reduce reliance on systemic opioids and their associated adverse effects, and include improvement in clinical, economic, and humanistic outcomes [3–5]. Other benefits include reduction in hospital resource utilization, enhanced postoperative recovery and improvement in patient satisfaction [6].

Successful PNBs provide complete blockade of nociceptive signals originating from the surgical area, especially within 24–48 h after surgery. However, despite their efficacy, upon resolution of the block, patients may experience a rapid escalation in pain severity-a phenomenon commonly described as ‘rebound pain’. Several definitions of rebound pain have been published in many literatures [7]. The rebound pain score (RPS) is described as the difference between the highest pain score within the first 12 h after resolution of the peripheral nerve block (PNB) and the lowest pain score recorded during the 12-h period preceding block resolution by Williams and colleagues [8]. And then, Barry et al. provides a suggested cut-off score to determine the incidence of rebound pain [9]. Patricia outlined the cardinal features of rebound pain, these include severe pain, burning or dull in quality, occurring whether at rest or on movement, lasting about 2 h, and occurring within 12–24 h after the PNB has worn off [10].

Rebound pain represents a significant impediment to effective pain management, compromising efforts to maintain appropriate analgesic control. Its occurrence during PNB resolution directly contradicts therapeutic objectives aimed at enhancing patient satisfaction and preventing persistent pain through seamless transition from regional anesthesia to oral analgesics. This rebound pain not only undermines recovery but also correlates with increased opioid consumption, prolonged hospital stays, and patient dissatisfaction [7, 11]. While rebound pain has been studied in orthopedic surgeries, its prevalence and predictors in orthopedic trauma populations remain poorly characterized.

Existing literature has identified anesthetic, surgical, and patient-specific variables that may modify rebound pain risk after PNB. Among these, certain factors-including age, sex, block duration, local anesthetic type, and preoperative pain levels-are established predictors of heightened postoperative pain [8, 10, 12]. Surgical characteristics, such as procedure type and anatomical site, have also been associated with rebound pain intensity. Furthermore, as trauma patients often experience higher baseline pain scores, psychological stress, and delayed surgical interventions which may exacerbate rebound pain [1, 13]. However, extrapolating these findings to trauma surgery is problematic due to differences in patient physiology, injury acuity, and perioperative management [14]. For instance, trauma patients frequently require urgent surgery without the preoperative optimization possible, potentially altering pain modulation pathways [15]. Additionally, the inflammatory milieu of acute trauma may accelerate the metabolism of local anesthetics, shortening block duration and predisposing patients to earlier rebound pain [16]. Despite these plausible mechanisms, few studies have systematically evaluated risk factors in this population.

The primary objective of this study was to identify the incidence and factors associated with rebound pain in adult patients undergoing orthopedic trauma surgery using PNB for anesthesia. Detecting these factors could permit precision pain therapy targeting specific patient populations and operations with increased likelihood of rebound pain.

Methods

In this single center retrospective cohort study, we retrieved and analyzed the clinical data of 1797 patients between 18 and 80 years of age who underwent orthopedic trauma surgery in our hospital between 1 April 2023 and 31 March 2025, and those who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital, (approval number 2025-KY-KZ-045-01). The writing of this study adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology guidelines (STROBE).

Patients

Eligible patients received a PNB by an anaesthetist. PNB was defined as local anaesthetic being deposited at a targeted nerve, which was for analgesia primarily for orthopedic trauma surgery. The other inclusion criteria included that patients who were 18–80 years of age, and those American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification was 1–3. The exclusion criteria included (1) Patients who preoperative used opioid; (2) Patients whose peripheral nerve block was failure, defined as patients without a demonstrable sensory block or patients who experienced moderate-to-severe pain (NRS > 3) before postanesthesia care unit (PACU) discharge; (3) Patients were transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) after surgery; (4) Patients received a patient controlled analgesia (PCA) after surgery; (5) Patients were reported any residual numbness at time of 24 h; (6) Patients who refused follow-up or had incomplete follow-up data (i.e.missing NRS within 24 h).

Sample size

In this study, we referenced methodological approach of sample size calculation by Barry et al. [9], and conducted a sensitivity power analysis for each predictor variable in the multivariate analysis. We also corrected for collinearity because collinearity tends to reduce statistical power in multivariable models. Power analysis was conducted with G*Power software. The two-tailed test assumed alpha of 0.05, three levels of power (80%, 90% and 95%), and 41.9% participants with rebound pain of a sample size of 903 (accounting for exclusion criteria and list-wise deletion in multivariable analysis). A standardized (mean = 0; standard deviation [SD] = 1) normal distribution was assumed for age, body mass index (BMI), and binomial distributions for other variables.

Data collection

Data were extracted from hospital electronic medical record databases, and the daily follow-up electronic questionnaire from application program. All patients received a follow-up by the nurse anesthetist approximately 24 h after PNB as part of routine care. Data about the clinically relevant pain and outcomes were recorded. Otherwise, baseline data and potential risk factors were collected (Table 1). Some of these risk factors have been found to be associated with postoperative acute pain in previous studies. Age, gender, BMI, ASA, Surgical history, diabetes, smoking, preoperative pain severity, preoperative C-reactive protein (CRP), and preoperative NLR were included as patient factors. Surgical site (upper or lower limb) and involvement of bone, soft tissue or both were included as surgical factors. Local anaesthetic type, type of PNB, regional anaesthesia, and intraoperative co-analgesic medications were included as anaesthetic factors. Type of PNB was grouped as brachial plexus block (BPB), interscalene block (ISB), femoral compartment block (FCB), and sciatic nerve block.

Outcomes

The primary outcome variable for the study was the incidence of rebound pain. Rebound pain is defined as an increase from well-controlled to severe pain typically within 24 h of resolution of the nerve block. Severe pain (NRS ≥ 7) following the resolution of a peripheral nerve block (PNB) has been proposed as a criterion for defining RPS, a specific diagnostic cut-point has not yet been established [9]. Thus, the incidence of rebound pain was measured by a change from mild pain (NRS ≤ 3) when the block was last measured to be working (measured in PACU) to severe pain (NRS ≥ 7) within 24 h after PNB performance. All patients must have indicated no residual numbness at the 24 h follow-up. Secondary outcomes included severity (amplitude) of the RPS, overall patient satisfaction with pain control on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 2 = dissatisfied, 3 = neutral, 4 = satisfied, and 5 = very satisfied), and sleep quality on Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI) (0 = very good, 1 = fairly good, 2 = fairly bad, 4 = very bad).

Statistics

Patient characteristics data are presented as mean [SD] or number (%) unless otherwise stated. For missing perioperative data, the multiple imputation method was used to address the missing data. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov method was used to test the data distribution. Rebound pain scores followed an approximately normal sample distribution; means (95% confidence intervals [CI]) were reported for each variable subgroup. RPS means between patients with rebound pain and those without were analyzed using Welch’s t-test. Univariate comparisons for the incidence of rebound pain were analyzed using logistic regression. For the analysis of rebound pain risk factors, multivariate logistic regression was used, and the indicators with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included. Univariate linear regression was performed to analyze the association between each variable and the secondary outcome of RPS. Data were analyzed using R 4.3.1. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of 1796 cases who underwent orthopedic trauma surgery were identified at the Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital. Patients were excluded based on preoperative opioid use (N = 195), having moderate to severe pain (NRS > 3) as their last recorded pain score in PACU (N = 79), reporting residual numbness at the time of 24 h (N = 55), missing NRS within 24 h (N = 156), receiving a patient controlled analgesia (PCA) (N = 242), or missing primary outcome (N = 32). One thousand thirty-seven patients remained in the sample cohort after inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Patient characteristics features and incidence and severity of rebound pain within factors are shown in Table 1. Among the cohort, 435 (41.9%) of these patients experienced postoperative rebound pain, defined as experiencing severe postoperative pain at ward when the last PACU pain was mild. The overall mean RPS was 5.48. Those classified as without rebound pain had a mean RPS of 3.25 [1.36], whereas those classified with rebound pain had a mean RPS of 7.84 [1.25], respectively, with a mean difference 4.55 (95% CI 4.38–4.72; P < 0.001).

Discussion

PNBs are gaining popularity for orthopedic surgery with the use of ultrasound and regarded as the standard of practice in performing most peripheral nerve blocks in regional anesthesia [17, 18]. Rebound pain is defined as mechanical-surgical pain resulting from unopposed nociceptive inputs that become suddenly exposed after the resolution of analgesia from PNBs. It is reported that the incidence rate of rebound pain is approximately 35–49.6% after PNBs [9, 11]. This retrospective cohort analysis identified rebound pain in a 41.9% incidence of the orthopedic trauma surgery patients included. A significant disparity in RPS was observed between those experiencing rebound pain and those who did not, indicating this metric could potentially serve as an effective indicator for tracking rebound pain occurrence among orthopedic trauma surgery patients. The mean RPS of 5.48 highlights the transient but intense nature of rebound pain, with a stark contrast between those classified with rebound pain and without rebound pain. In addition, the findings illuminate both risk and protective factors, clinical implications of rebound pain severity, and paradoxical patient satisfaction rates despite high pain scores.

In the multivariate analysis, the study identified three non-modifiable patient and surgical risk factors for rebound pain: younger age, diabetes, and bone surgery. Preoperative moderate to severe pain and NLR elevation showed a trend towards association with rebound pain. Of the anesthetic factors, i.v. dexamethasone or dexmedetomidine administration was the modifiable factor that may be associated with a reduction in rebound pain. High patient satisfaction levels persisted notwithstanding frequent rebound pain episodes, which may indicate this phenomenon represented a transient component of patients’ overall perioperative experience. Furthermore, there was no statistical difference of sleep quality whether rebound pain happened or not.

The RPS used in this study is not completely consistent with the timing described by Barry et al., which calculated the RPS from pain scores within 12–24 h after block resolution, there maybe exist a potential limitation in their pain assessment protocol where rebound pain emerging the first 12 h post-procedure might remain unconcerned. It is noteworthy that the 24-h period selected for the study is encompassed by the 12–24 h range established by Barry and colleagues. A prior study categorized patients with preoperative moderate-to-severe pain (NRS > 3) before PACU discharge as having suboptimal pain control [19]. This classification accounted for potential causes such as block failure, premature block regression, or surgical pain in areas unaffected by the nerve block. These individuals were excluded to isolate rebound pain, defined as the progression from adequate analgesia at PACU discharge to severe pain following transfer to the ward.

Younger age independently predicted rebound pain, consistent with prior studies [9, 11], possibly because of faster local anesthetic metabolism in younger patients, leading to shorter block durations. Additionally, younger individuals may exhibit heightened pain sensitivity post-block resolution due to neuroplasticity differences. Patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathy demonstrated reduced time to onset of sensory blockade, with increased time to first opioid request when compared with patients without neuropathy, though the underlying mechanisms are not unclear [20]. In the study, diabetic patients faced a higher rebound pain risk, likely reflecting peripheral neuropathy and centralized pain sensitization. However, Tang, et.al demonstrated that nerve block duration in patients with diabetes was prolonged. Maybe it was a marked reduction in nerve conduction velocity concomitant with hyperglycemia-induced neuropathic impairment in individuals with diabetes mellitus [21]. Furthermore, microvascular dysfunction was observed to significantly compromise both perineural and intraneural vascular perfusion [22]. We observed disparities in rates of rebound pain between diabetic and non-diabetic cohorts may be attributable, in part, to the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying peripheral sensitization, particularly those mediated through chronic hyperglycemic states and associated diabetic neuropathy.

As a reliable and readily available marker of immune response to various infectious and non-infectious stimuli, NLR is widely used across various medical disciplines, such as cancer, atherosclerosis, inflammation, and stress [23–26]. Some studies have also investigated the association between NLR and acute postoperative pain [27, 28]. In addition, NLR maybe correlate with prolonged nociceptor sensitization and reduced analgesic efficacy. Trauma-induced stress activation may synergize with surgical stress to amplify pain signaling. Therefore, the elevated NLR can remarkably reflect the difference in preoperative stress or inflammation status, which was associated with the higher incidence of rebound pain in this study. Notably, preoperative elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), a biomarker of systemic inflammation, was not identified as a risk factor for rebound pain in the study. This paradoxical observation may be attributed to the predominance of stress response mechanisms over localized inflammatory processes in orthopedic trauma patients, coupled with the potential mitigating effects of anti-inflammatory therapies administered during early clinical intervention before surgery.

Preoperative pain is another patient-specific aspect and is deemed to be a determinant of acute postoperative pain. A 3.1-fold increase in RP frequency was observed in patients with preoperative NRS > 3, which results align with earlier research [29]. The location of surgery are key factors in both postoperative pain and RP. The incidence of RP after bone surgery has been reported to be 50–80%, with bone surgery increasing the risk making it a major contributing factor [9, 29]. In this study, RP occurred in 50.2% of 247 patients with bone surgery. Bone surgery elevates local proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6, explaining the heightened RP susceptibility [30]. Thus, being cognizant of patient-specific factors that could contribute to RP can help clinicians personalize perioperative pain management approaches and create treatment plans tailored to individual patient characteristics.

Trauma can induce abnormal levels of plasticity in both peripheral nociceptors and central neurons, which are responsible for pain perception and processing [31]. Prolonging the sensory blockade might reduce central sensitization and lower the incidence of rebound pain [32]. Dexamethasone alleviates pain through its anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties. It inhibits the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, reduces prostaglandin and leukotriene production by blocking phospholipase Alpha-2 stabilizes cell membranes to prevent tissue damage, and reduces swelling by decreasing capillary permeability [33]. In addition, it affects nociceptive signaling pathways, lowering pain perception [34]. Dexamethasone was associated with a reduced incidence of rebound pain in this study, and suggest that perioperative intravenous dexamethasone, when used as an adjunct, effectively reduces the incidence of rebound pain, while perineural administration may also offer some benefits [35, 36]. Adjuvant use of dexmedetomidine for brachial plexus blocks leads to a prolonged duration of analgesia of almost 5 h [37]. Maagaard and colleagues’ study showed that combined dexamethasone and dexmedetomidine probably does not prolong the duration of analgesia when compared with dexamethasone alone, but that the combination likely results in a longer duration of analgesia when compared with dexmedetomidine alone and placebo [38]. However, Huan and colleagues’ study found that dexmedetomidine as an adjuvant significantly reduced the incidence of rebound pain after ISB [39]. In the current study, i.v dexmedetomidine administration was associated with a reduced RPS and incidence of rebound pain. Furthermore, to our knowledge, dexmedetomidine given through any route has not specifically been studied for association with rebound pain after PNB. The reduction in rebound pain incidence and RPS found may be consistent with the known effect of dexmedetomidine on postoperative pain in general rather than any possible effect on PNB duration. Therefore, further prospective evidence is needed to confirm these findings and support dexmedetomidine’s broader clinical use for preventing rebound pain.

It is noteworthy that patient satisfaction remained high even in the presence of considerable rebound pain. One possible explanation is that, for many patients, this period of discomfort may have been a transient part of their broader surgical recovery experience [9]. The study showed no statistical difference from sleep quality. This contrasts with studies linking rebound pain to sleep disruption, potentially due to our cohort’s heterogeneous trauma profiles or assessment timing. As trauma surgery is usually performed in the night in this study, many blocks wear off during the daytime. In clinical practice, severe rebound pain also occurs when blocks wear off during night. Whether sleep prevents patients from sensing any early signs, or if the pain simply has a rapid onset, is unknown [40].

A particular strength of this study is its large cohort size, and the diversity of factors studied for association with rebound pain, potentially representing the largest single investigation on rebound pain to date. This study has several important limitations that should be considered. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, the generalizability of the findings is inherently limited. The patient population and perioperative management protocols from one institution may not be representative of broader clinical practices, which restricts the external validity of our conclusions. A major concern is the retrospective assessment of RPS, which relies on documentation in electronic medical records. This approach is susceptible to recall bias and inconsistencies in pain reporting, potentially affecting the accuracy of risk estimates. Furthermore, the absence of preoperative psychological assessments-such as evaluations for anxiety or depression-represents a significant unmeasured confounder. These psychological factors are well-established risk factors of acute pain experience and patient satisfaction, and their omission may have influenced the outcomes observed. Additionally, the exclusion of opioid-tolerant patients and those receiving continuous PNB catheters limits the applicability of the results to these specific subgroups. Since both factors are likely to affect the incidence of rebound pain, the findings may not extend to all clinical scenarios involving PNBs. Similarly, the fact that no perineural adjuvants were used in this cohort further narrows the generalizability of the study, as adjuvants may modify the duration and quality of analgesia, thereby influencing rebound pain. Future prospective studies should include preoperative psychological screening, standardized documentation of PNB failure, and detailed assessment of analgesic regimens-including the use of continuous catheters and adjuvants. Investigations into the impact of co-analgesics (e.g., intravenous dexmedetomidine) and the timing of preoperative analgesia would also help identify more effective strategies for mitigating rebound pain.

Conclusions

This retrospective study of 1037 orthopedic trauma surgery patients that received a PNB showed a relatively high incidence of rebound pain across a large variety of surgical procedures. Factors associated with an increased risk of rebound pain were younger age, diabetes, surgical involving bone, preoperative NLR elevation, preoperative moderate-to severe pain. Other factors with a trend towards association were the absence of perioperative i.v. dexamethasone or dexmedetomidine use. Despite a high incidence of rebound pain, most patients were satisfied with their pain management, and did not require to improve sleep quality. While patient satisfaction remains high, improving RPS through targeted interventions may further enhance recovery outcomes.
