Does spatial functional division in urban agglomerations reduce negative externalities in large cities? Evidence from urban agglomerations in China
Authors: Songlin Zhang, Jia Ding, Haoqing Zheng, Hongwei Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The spatial unit of urban agglomerations is extremely important for reducing negative externalities in large cities. Although there is a large body of literature on reducing negative externalities within a single city, few studies have focused on the important role of urban agglomerations. To offer a new method for reducing negative externalities in large cities and fill in this gap in the literature, our paper examines the effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities by applying the concept of spatial functional division to data on 19 urban agglomerations and data on negative externalities from 35 large cities in China. We first show that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations can significantly reduce negative externalities. The robustness of the results was confirmed using the instrumental variable approach, alternative indicators, and other robustness checks. Furthermore, we identified the productivity improvement mechanisms in large cities that generate the observed mitigatory effects of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities. The heterogeneous analyzes indicate that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations has a stronger mitigatory effect on negative externalities in large cities in the context of higher inter-city connectivity, greater polycentricity, and official approval regarding urban agglomerations’ regional planning. Our findings emphasize the importance of urban agglomerations for reducing negative externalities in large cities, particularly in China, where urban agglomerations have been rapidly developing and spatial functional division in urban agglomerations has been continuously improving.

Introduction

Over the past several decades, large Chinese cities have experienced dramatic population growth [1]. The concentration of population in large cities has created a unique opportunity for the development of large cities and economic growth [2]. However, the economic benefits of such concentration are not unlimited. Given that infrastructure, resources, and the environment have not been prepared for the dramatic population growth, many studies argue that the rapid concentration of people becomes a great source of negative externalities [3,4]. These negative externalities in practice include high housing costs [5], environmental pollution [6], traffic congestion [7], and the inadequate provision of public services [3]. Clearly, each of these negative externalities is an important obstacle to sustainable urban development [8]. The data from the Seventh National Census in 2020 indicates that the population of large Chinese cities, accounting for 26.31% of the national total population, has increased 4.11% since 2010 [9], reflecting an ongoing trend of individuals relocating to large cities. This trend is putting further pressure on the negative externalities in large cities, which makes it a necessity for scientists and policymakers alike to look for effective ways of dealing with them. To this end, our paper is concerned with the following question: how can negative externalities in large cities be addressed? Simultaneously, this question is of practical significance for sustainable urban development in nations and regions that resemble the Chinese context.

There are indeed substantial research endeavors being conducted on reducing negative externalities in large cities due to facts of this sort. As documented by Liu and Sun [4], restricting, prohibiting, and regulating the movements of migrants are direct ways to reduce negative externalities in large Chinese cities. Using these depopulation methods can decrease competition for land and other services and thus reduce negative externalities, but it cannot achieve the optimal allocation of resources. Furthermore, the literature presents a significant number of alternative ways, such as considering a combination of infrastructure investment and behavioral modiﬁcation [10], attempting to optimize the spatial balance between public goods supply and demand [11], and suggesting appropriate urban planning initiatives [12]. Overall, more attention has been paid to negative externalities as a result of dramatic population growth in large cities. Existing studies have tried to reduce negative externalities within a single city [4,[10], [11], [12]]. The important role of urban agglomerations, however, has been largely overlooked.

Urban agglomerations are the most dynamic and potential core areas for national economic development and global division, as a fact, and are characterized by clusters of highly networked cities anchored by one or more large cities within a certain geographic region [13]. Large cities and small and medium cities in the same urban agglomerations tend to focus on different functions, which leads to the emergence of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations. This new division in the literature can be specifically explained as large cities concentrate on producer services and small and medium cities concentrate on production [14,15]. Consistent with these studies, we define spatial functional division in urban agglomerations as the division of functions across cities within the same urban agglomeration, in which large cities are expected to specialize in producer service functions and small and medium cities are expected to specialize in production functions.

With the improvement of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations, the positive economic outcomes of such division are increasingly recognized. Broadly, the growth literature has proven the positive economic outcomes of two different spatial units: single cities and urban agglomerations. In regard to the single city level, the core view is that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations allows each of the cities to become more competitive than it would be in isolation. For example, Meijers [16] shows that functional specializations, as a result of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations, lead large cities and small and medium cities to complement each other. In addition, several scholars [15,17,18] further discuss the mechanisms through which cities benefit from spatial functional division in urban agglomerations, and they emphasize that the mechanisms are different for large cities and small and medium cities. On the one hand, large cities that specialize in producer services provide these services to small and medium cities, which improves large cities’ management level [19]. This is conducive to making large cities regional management centers [15]. A good management center, in turn, may meld large cities and small and medium cities into spatially integrated urban agglomerations with functional specializations. On the other hand, small and medium cities compensate for their lack of size by being deeply embedded and thus benefiting from spatial functional division [20]. Today, cities in the same urban agglomerations are more closely linked than ever [21], which may prompt the spatial unit of urban agglomerations to play a more important role. In this context, a new body of work has emerged that investigates how spatial functional division in urban agglomerations effects economic activities at the urban agglomeration level. In this regard, Van et al. [22] emphasize that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations is crucial for the development of urban network economies. McCann and Acs [23] find a similar result and argue that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations enhances regional productivity. Balland and Boschma [24] present the results of an empirical study using an original dataset covering 292 NUTS-2 regions in Europe. They find that complementary interregional linkages, such as spatial functional division in urban agglomerations, have a positive effect on the probability that a region will diversify.

In conclusion, previous literature has suggested a compelling link between spatial functional division in urban agglomerations and economic performance at the city level and the urban agglomeration level; however, few attempts have been made to evaluate the effects on reducing negative externalities in large cities. Besides, many methods for reducing negative externalities are highly related to economic performance, as shown in Glaeser [10]. This indicates that, to some extent, there is space for further research on the effects of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on reducing negative externalities. To suggest a new method for reducing negative externalities in large cities and fill research gaps, we discuss in detail whether spatial functional division in urban agglomerations reduces negative externalities in large cities by examining spatial functional division in data on 19 urban agglomerations and data on negative externalities for 35 large Chinese cities.

This study contributes to the existing literature in the following aspects. First, this paper for the first time investigates the impacts of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities and analyzes how to reduce negative externalities in large cities from the perspective of urban agglomerations instead of a single city. Furthermore, we explore the productivity improvement mechanisms, which help specifically interpret the impacts of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities. Second, this paper reveals the heterogeneous effects of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities across different contexts. In this way, we attempt to find the contexts that are more conducive to reducing negative externalities in large cities. Finally, this paper provides some recommendations for policymakers to reduce negative externalities in large cities and promote sustainable urban development. Our empirical results reveal that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations can significantly reduce negative externalities in large cities. This ﬁnding offers a new method that can be used in contemporary policymaking to reduce negative externalities in large cities. Moreover, in our current era, where urban agglomerations have been rapidly developing and spatial functional division in urban agglomerations has been continuously improving, this study's implications may be practically significant.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we conduct a theoretical analysis of the effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities and develop the related hypothesis. In Section 3, we present the research design, including the setting of the econometric model, the deﬁnition of variables, and the selection of data. We demonstrate the empirical results in Section 4. Section 5 concludes.

Theoretical analysis and hypothesis development

As existing studies have demonstrated, negative externalities in large cities mainly appear as high housing costs [5], environmental pollution [6], traffic congestion [7], and the inadequate provision of public services [3]. Obviously, sufficiently wealthy cities are able to address negative externalities [10], and in large cities, raising productivity can make the city wealthier. Therefore, in large cities, negative externalities can be mitigated by improving productivity. Based on this analysis, we analyze how spatial functional division in urban agglomerations influences productivity in large cities by focusing on the nature and features of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations and thus, spatial functional division in urban agglomerations may have an effect on the negative externalities in large cities.

To strengthen the comparative advantages of different cities, cities in urban agglomerations have demonstrated a clear division according to their functions (i.e., producer services versus production) [15]. This division increases urban productivity to some extent [25]. In the process of enforcing spatial functional division, large cities tend to specialize primarily in producer services functions, such as research, design, and management activities, while small and medium cities specialize mainly in production functions, such as producing, manufacturing, and processing activities [14,26]. We can observe that the specialized functions of large cities are concentrated in high-end sectors. Consequently, large cities are more successful at attracting high-skilled workers. Large cities with a higher attraction for high-skilled workers can offer higher incomes [27]. In turn, higher incomes in large cities can motivate more high-skilled workers to migrate to these cities [28,29]. This circular mechanism further improves productivity in large cities. Based on the above, we believe that as high-end functions and high-skilled workers continuously agglomerate in large cities, these cities can achieve greater advancements in productivity compared with small and medium cities.

There are three main mechanisms by which productivity improvements in large cities reduce negative externalities. First, in parallel with the rise in urban productivity, the incomes of high-skilled workers increase [30], enabling them to afford the high housing costs. Second, in the context of the continuous inflow of high-skilled workers and increasing income levels, residents in large cities may desire higher quality living conditions and environments [31]. This can pressure the government to invest more manpower and material resources in infrastructure construction, environmental governance, public services supply, and more [32]. Third, productivity improvements in large cities can directly result in economic benefits [33], thus furnishing financial backing for initiatives such as infrastructure improvements, environmental governance, public services supply, and more. Existing studies have emphasized the important role of sufficient government funds in reducing negative externalities in large cities. For example, sufficient funds are required for infrastructure projects such as subways, high-speed rail, and highways [34]. Based on the above analysis, we formulated the following hypotheses:H1Spatial functional division in urban agglomerations reduces negative externalities in large cities.H2Increasing productivity in large cities is a potential mechanism through which spatial functional division in urban agglomerations can reduce negative externalities in large cities.

Spatial functional division in urban agglomerations reduces negative externalities in large cities.

Increasing productivity in large cities is a potential mechanism through which spatial functional division in urban agglomerations can reduce negative externalities in large cities.

Research design

Model

We adopt standard panel data to empirically test the hypothesis that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations reduces negative externalities in large cities. Following a previous study [35], fixed effects and random effects models are two common approaches to panel data analysis. To justify the accuracy of the model setting and obtain valid results, we first use the Hausman test to examine whether a random effect or fixed effect model should be implemented following Zhang and Zheng [36]. If the null hypothesis of the Hausman test is accepted, the random effects model is applicable. If not, the fixed effects model is applicable. The Hausman test suggests that the fixed effects model is preferred in this study. The results of the Hausman test are reported in Section 4 as a reference. Furthermore, the fixed effects model is applicable to impose control on unobservable individual heterogeneity. This reduces the endogeneity problem caused by omitted variables [37]. Therefore, based on Ref. [5], we established the fixed effects model as follows:(1)lnNEGEit=α+βlnSFDit+∑jγjXit+μi+εitwhere i indexes cities and t indexes years. lnNEGE refers to the natural logarithm of negative externalities in large cities. lnSFD is the natural logarithm of the spatial functional division in urban agglomerations. X represents a set of control variables. μi denotes city ﬁxed effects. εit denotes the random error term. The coefficient of our research interest is β, which is the estimated effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on the negative externalities in large cities. We expect the coefficient to be negative, which would suggest that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations reduces negative externalities in large cities.

Subsequently, we used the means of principal component analysis to construct the index as a proxy for negative externalities in each large city. Key explanatory variable

The natural logarithm of the spatial functional division in urban agglomerations (lnSFD) is the key explanatory variable in Eq. (1). Based on the comparative advantages of cities, urban agglomerations serve as the spatial scale where a clear functional division system across cities emerged. Specifically, large cities mainly specialize in producer services functions, while small and medium cities mainly specialize in production functions. Therefore, following Yu et al. [15], we used the SFD index to proxy spatial functional division in urban agglomeration, and the SFD index is specified as follows:(2)SFDit=∑c=1mESIcit/∑c=1mEPIcit∑p=1nESIpit/∑p=1nEPIpitwhere ESIcit denotes the number of employees in the producer services industry in large cities, EPIcit denotes the number of employees in the production industry in large cities, ESIpit denotes the number of employees in the producer services industry in small and medium cities and EPIpit denotes the number of employees in the production industry in small and medium cities. i, t, c, p denote cities, years, large cities and small and medium cities, respectively. Following Zhang et al. [5], we classified municipalities, provincial capitals, and sub-provincial cities as large cities and other cities at the prefecture-level as small and medium cities in the empirical analysis.

Based on the industrial classification for national economic activities [39] and the analytical technique of Yu et al. [15], we defined traffic, transport, storage and post, information transmission, computer services and software, financial intermediation, real estate, leasing and business services and scientific research and technical services as producer services industries. Mining, manufacturing, electricity production and distribution, gas and water and construction were classified as production industries. A higher value for lnSFD indicated a higher level of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations.

Control variables

Following the literature, various variables, including labour scale, industrial structure, government intervention, and foreign direct investment, that may affect negative externalities in large cities are controlled. We addressed the more detailed motivations behind these control variables and their measures as follows.

① Labour scale (lnL). Existing studies show that labour scale is essential to enterprise production and economic growth, which can improve urban productivity and reduce negative externalities in large cities [40]. Based on Shao et al. [41] and data availability, we used the natural logarithm of the sum of persons employed in secondary and tertiary industry as well as persons employed in private enterprises and self-employed individuals in urban areas to proxy the labour scale.

② Industrial structure (lnIND). The upgrade of China's industrial structure has greatly contributed to the fast-growing national economy [42]. Therefore, the industrial structure is expected to reduce negative externalities in large cities through economic growth. Meanwhile, the larger the industrial structure is, the more energy consumption and environmental pollution there will be [43]. This is harmful for reducing negative externalities in large cities. Because industrial structure may affect the negative externalities in large cities, we introduced industrial structure into the regression model as a control variable. Referring to Fang et al. [44] and Tang and Li [42], we measured the industrial structure by the natural logarithm of the proportion of secondary and tertiary industry output values in GDP.

③ Government intervention (lnGOV). Government intervention, a necessary supplement to the market mechanism, plays an important role in reducing negative externalities in large cities, as documented by Liu and Sun [4]. Thus, we measured the government intervention by using the natural logarithm of the ratio of local government expenditures to GDP, following Fan et al. [40].

④ Foreign direct investment (lnFDI). In theory, the impact of foreign direct investment on the negative externalities in large cities is ambiguous. On the one hand, foreign direct investment brings superior technology and promotes technological progress [45]. Technological progress is necessary for reducing the negative externalities in large cities. On the other hand, foreign direct investment also brings environmental pressure to the region through the pollution haven effect and the pollution halo effect [42]. Consequently, foreign direct investment has a negative effect on reducing negative externalities in large cities. Referring to Zhang et al. [45], we adopted the natural logarithm of the amount of actually utilised foreign capital divided by GDP to measure foreign direct investment.

Results and discussion

Test of H1

Baseline estimates

Based on Eq. (1), we conduct an empirical study on the effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities. As shown in Table 3, the results of the Hausman test statistics significantly reject the null hypothesis. Therefore, the results of the fixed effects model used in this paper are valid. To check the robustness of the results, we gradually added control variables, following He et al. [50]. For column (1), the coefficient of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations is −0.348 and it is significant at the 1% level. This finding indicates that the spatial functional division in urban agglomerations significantly reduces negative externalities in large cities. For columns (2) through (5), we include the following controls: labour scale, industrial structure, government intervention and foreign direct investment. The estimated coefficients of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations are −0.178 (p = 0.027) in column (2), −0.171 (p = 0.021) in column (3), −0.170 (p = 0.020) in column (4), and −0.182 (p = 0.016) in column (5), which suggest that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations still reduces negative externalities in large cities even after taking into account the effects of control variables. These results denote the robustness of the estimation results. This preliminary confirmation supports H1. One possible reason is that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations contributed to productivity improvement. Large cities, as the managers and connectors of urban agglomerations, further promote spatial functional division in urban agglomerations. In light of this, productivity in large cities can be increased continuously through the entire urban agglomeration development, which is consistent with the view of Yu et al. [51]. Meanwhile, productivity improvement is the most straightforward way of reducing negative externalities in large cities. These results further imply that cities in urban agglomerations are not isolated and that negative externalities in large cities can be reduced by improving spatial functional division. Considering endogenetity

The endogeneity problem is the main challenge in estimating the effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities. Therefore, we referred to Fan et al. [40] and used the instrumental variable approach to reduce the potential bias in the empirical results possibly caused by the endogeneity problem. First, we used the reciprocal of the earth's surface slope as an instrumental variable for spatial functional division in urban agglomerations following Zhang et al. [25]. Since the raw data for the instrumental variable only varies over cities, it cannot be directly used in the empirical analysis of panel data. Thus, we used the interaction term of the reciprocal of the earth's surface slope and the corresponding year variable as the instrumental variable for spatial functional division in urban agglomerations, following the approach suggested by Nunn and Qian [52].

The results of the instrumental variable estimate are shown in column (1) of Table 4. The Kleibergen-Paap rk LM statistic is highly significant at the 1% level, which disproves the null hypothesis that the equation is underidentified. The Kleibergen-Paap rk Wald F statistic was 52.434, which is greater than the Stock-Yogo critical values, thus disproving the null hypothesis regarding weak instrumental variables. Therefore, the instrumental variable that we used for our estimates is credible. Robustness checks

Having established the robustness of the baseline estimate results through the addition of control variables and addressing endogeneity in Sections 4, 4.1.1.1.2, this section now proceeds to apply two further sets of robustness checks to affirm the reliability of our baseline estimate. In the first set of robustness tests, we first used a variety of indicators from the six factors of real estate, environment, traffic, educational resources, employment and medical resources to re-calculate negative externalities in large cities by using principal component analysis. Additionally, we used the unemployment rate2 to represent the key employment features and used the ratio of urban population to the number of doctors3 to represent key medical resource features. Column (2) of Table 4 reports the results of the estimates. The coefficient of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations is −0.185 and significant at the 5% level. Next, we considered that negative externalities in large cities are not only influenced by spatial functional division in urban agglomerations in the present but may also have been influenced by spatial functional division in urban agglomerations in the past. Thus, we used lagged spatial functional division in urban agglomerations (lnSFD_lag) to re-examine the effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities. Column (3) of Table 4 shows that the coefficient of lnSFD_lag is −0.129, which is statistically significant at the 10% level. Our main finding still holds after replacing the indicators of the key variables.

For the second set of robustness tests, to avoid the estimation bias caused by particular samples, we referred to Fan et al. [40] and eliminated the disturbance of peculiar cities. First, municipalities are different from provincial capitals and sub-provincial cities with respect to population size, public services, political structure, and more, which may affect the robustness of the findings [35]. Therefore, we excluded the four municipalities of Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, and Chongqing and re-estimated Eq. (1). The results are presented in column (4) of Table 4. Next, the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei, the Yangtze River Delta, and the Pearl River Delta urban agglomerations are the most urbanised and economically developed areas in China, following He et al. [53]. These three urban agglomerations' economic conditions are significantly different from other urban agglomerations in China, which may affect the robustness of the findings. Hence, we re-examine Eq. (1) by excluding the three most urbanised and economically developed urban agglomerations in column (5) of Table 4. In addition, given that these municipalities and large urban agglomerations are the “bellwether” of China's urbanization and economic development, the role of spatial functional division in these regions will attract much attention. Therefore, we set up a single group to analyze the effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities, and the results are reported in column (6) of Table 4. The estimated coefficients of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations are −0.149 (p = 0.055) in column (4), −0.166 (p = 0.055) in column (5), and −0.238 (p = 0.018) in column (6). The results indicate that the spatial functional division in urban agglomerations still reduces negative externalities in large cities, which is consistent with the results of our baseline estimates analysis. Furthermore, we found that the spatial functional division in urban agglomerations has a stronger effect on reducing negative externalities in large cities for these municipalities and large urban agglomerations. The findings show that the negative externalities reduction effect of spatial functional division has a higher marginal value in municipalities and large urban agglomerations, and these regions have a better advantage in promoting spatial functional division in urban agglomerations.

Heterogeneity analysis

Our empirical analyses above reflect the total effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities for all observations. However, when using the total sample, it is difficult to explore potential heterogeneous effects. To this end, we examined whether the effects of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities are heterogeneous across different contexts by using sub-samples. The results can provide additional insight into what contexts can amplify the mitigatory effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities, in turn guiding policy discussions.

Inter-city connectivity is an important factor that drives allocation within the entire urban agglomeration, which may cause the mitigatory effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities to differ between large cities. Therefore, we divided the samples into high inter-city connectivity and low inter-city connectivity based on whether the proportion of cities connected via high-speed rail lines in the same urban agglomerations was higher than the median value of this ratio in 19 urban agglomerations. Conclusions

Research findings

In this paper, we examined the link between spatial functional division in urban agglomerations and negative externalities in large cities by using a dataset covering 19 urban agglomerations and 35 large cities in China from 2010 to 2019. We presented plausible evidence that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations can significantly reduce negative externalities large cities. Furthermore, we identified the mechanisms of productivity improvements in large cities that generate the observed mitigatory impact of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities. Finally, the heterogeneous analyses indicated that spatial functional division in urban agglomerations has a stronger mitigatory effect on negative externalities in large cities in the context of higher inter-city connectivity, greater polycentricity, and official approval regarding urban agglomerations’ regional planning.

Policy recommendations

The above findings have important policy implications for reducing negative externalities in large cities. Today, with cities highly integrated in the same urban agglomerations, the urban agglomerations is becoming an important spatial scale for economic activities. Therefore, negative externalities in large cities cannot simply be considered “independently” and need to have an element of the urban agglomerations awareness. Particularly, policymakers should strive for the rational allocation of producer services and production within urban agglomerations, fostering spatial functional division to enhance productivity growth potential and mitigate negative externalities. Besides, policymakers should utilize regional planning to strengthen the effect of the spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on reducing negative externalities in large cities. The mitigatory effect of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations on negative externalities in large cities is stronger in the context of higher inter-city connectivity, greater polycentricity, and official approval regarding urban agglomerations’ regional planning. Therefore, when policymakers build a series of regional plans to drive the development of urban agglomerations, it is necessary to enhance inter-city connectivity and polycentricity and enable the relevant regional planning for urban agglomerations to gradually receive nationwide official approval.

Limitations and future research directions

While this paper is among the first to consider reducing negative externalities in large cities at the urban agglomerations level to the best of our knowledge and has provided strong theoretical implications and practical insights, there are still two limitations that should be acknowledged. First, we focus on the spatial unit of urban agglomerations to examine the effect of their spatial functional division on negative externalities in large cities. Other channels at the urban agglomerations level for reducing negative externalities in large cities should be further explored. This is because the rise of urban agglomerations is a multidimensional phenomenon, and they create various benefits for cities to a certain extent. For example, cities could potentially benefit from region-wide agglomeration economies by being well-positioned in urban agglomerations. Therefore, evaluating the impact of region-wide agglomeration economies within urban agglomerations on reducing negative externalities in large cities is an important topic, and we would level it for future research. Second, due to data limitations, the raw data used in this paper were sourced at the city level. Considering that the micro-level participants in the spatial functional division are firms, it could be more practical to analyze the spatial functional division in urban agglomerations and its role in reducing negative externalities through the lens of firm behavior. As micro-firm data becomes more comprehensively available in the future, the utilization of cross-firm data could facilitate the refinement of measurement techniques for assessing spatial functional division in urban agglomerations.
