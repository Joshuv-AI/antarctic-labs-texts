Antibody and B‐Cell Responses to PHH‐1V81 XBB.1.16‐Adapted SARS‐CoV‐2 Booster Vaccination Are Associated With Variant Neutralization
Authors: Cèlia Torres, Alfons Jiménez, Meritxell Deulofeu, Antoni Prenafeta, Carlota Dobaño, Gemma Moncunill, Laura Ferrer, Luis M Molinos‐Albert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmune escape by SARS‐CoV‐2 emerging variants jeopardizes vaccine effectiveness, necessitating new immunization strategies. Here, we assessed the antibody B‐cell response in 40 individuals receiving either the PHH‐1V81 XBB.1.16‐ or BNT162b2 XBB.1.5‐adapted vaccine boosters. Both immunizations significantly increased ancestral‐reactive B cells cross‐recognizing the vaccine variant. Notably, XBB.1.16 cross‐reactive B cells were associated with neutralizing responses, reinforcing the potential of PHH‐1V81 as a strong boosting candidate against emerging variants.Keywords: B cells, infectious diseases, neutralizing antibodies, SARS‐CoV‐2, vaccines

Introduction

Immune escape by Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) emerging variants has compromised vaccine effectiveness resulting in an increased number of breakthrough infections, making thus essential to develop updated immunization strategies to ensure strong and long‐lasting protective responses [1, 2]. In this context, the monovalent Omicron XBB.1.16‐adapted PHH‐1V81 vaccine (BIMERVAX XBB.1.16, HIPRA, Spain) consists of an adjuvanted homodimer of the spike (S) protein receptor‐binding domain (RBD) derived from this Omicron sublineage. This updated vaccine received regulatory approval throughout the European Union as a booster for the prevention of COVID‐19 in individuals aged 16 years and older [3, 4]. Similarly, the Omicron XBB.1.5‐adapted BNT162b2 (Comirnaty Omicron XBB.1.5, Pfizer‐BioNTech, New York, NY, USA) is a monovalent mRNA vaccine encoding the S protein of this variant, and has been authorized in several countries with varying vaccination schedules [5, 6]. Thus, both adapted vaccines were developed to enhance protection against the more recent SARS‐CoV‐2 Omicron variants. During the HIPRA‐HH‐14 PhaseIIb/III pivotal non‐inferiority trial, it was shown that a booster dose of PHH‐1V81 XBB.1.16 induced superior plasma neutralizing antibodies against XBB.1.16 and XBB.1.5 variants, as compared to BNT162b2 XBB.1.5‐adapted mRNA vaccine [4].

While high‐affinity antibodies are essential to protect upon infection, memory B cells (MBC) rapidly proliferate upon antigen reencounter and differentiate to produce a new wave of antigen‐secreting cells (ASC) [7]. Moreover, MBC exhibit a higher stability than plasma antibodies as well as a broader repertoire to react against heterotypic variants [8, 9]. Here, we sought to interrogate the antibody B‐cell response induced by the newly‐adapted PHH‐1V81 XBB.1.16 and BNT162b2 XBB.1.5 SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccines.

Results

We determined the kinetics of RBD IgG antibody response and SARS‐CoV‐2‐reactive B‐cell frequency and variant cross‐recognition in participants from the HIPRA‐HH‐14 clinical study [4], who received a booster dose of either the PHH‐1V81 XBB.1.16‐ (n = 27) or the BNT162b2 XBB.1.5‐ (n = 13) adapted vaccines (Figure 1A and Table 1). All participants presented detectable IgG antibody levels to ancestral RBDWT, RBDXBB.1.16 and RBDXBB.1.5 variants at baseline (D0) experiencing a significant increase 14 days (D14) after boosting, whereas a slight but significant decay within the following 3 months (M3) was observed, yet remaining above baseline (Figure 1B). Notably, a strong increase to the more evolved RBDJN.1 variant was also detected, although at lower levels as compared with the other variants (Figure 1B). Similar patterns were exhibited by both vaccine groups, which maintained equivalent antibody levels to all variants along the follow‐up period (Figure 1C). We next compared plasma antibody neutralization titers between D0 and D14 timepoints against pseudoviruses encoding the ancestral, XBB.1.16, XBB.1.5, and JN.1 variants. A strong significant increase was observed in both groups against the four variants (Figure 1D,E), suggesting the induction of potent cross‐reactive antibodies by both vaccine formulations. To evaluate the impact of booster vaccination on the B‐cell compartment, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were stained with fluorescently‐labelled tetramerized receptor RBD proteins, including the ancestral RBDWT, and RBDXBB.1.16 or RBDXBB.1.5 variants for PHH‐1V81‐ and BNT162b2‐immunized donors, respectively, and analyzed them by flow cytometry (Figures 2A and S1). We found a significant expansion of RBD+ WT B cells cross‐recognizing RBDXBB.1.16 or RBDXBB.1.5 variants (median fold‐change 1.51 [0.913–14.8] and 1.16 [0.804–2.14], respectively), as determined by D14 after PHH‐1V81 or BNT162b2 booster dose, respectively (Figure 2B). In contrast, frequencies of RBD+ WT and RBD‐variant specific B cells remained unchanged, suggesting that a booster immunization with a heterotypic RBD variant favored cross‐reactive B‐cell clone recall, consistent with ancestral immune imprinting [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Analysis of the immunoglobulin isotypes by D14 revealed that RBD+ WT B cells cross‐recognizing RBDXBB.1.16 or RBDXBB.1.5 variants were predominantly IgG, whereas RBD+ WT exhibited similar IgG and IgM fractions (Figure 2C,D), which could be reminiscent of the earliest phase of memory [15]. Contrarily, IgA represented a small fraction of RBD+ B cells (Figure 2C,D). The B‐cell compartment is highly heterogeneous and SARS‐CoV‐2 infection or vaccination can induce a transient expansion of CD27+CD21‐ activated memory B cells (AM), which are likely a germinal center‐derived population that differentiates into antibody‐secreting cells (ASC) [16]. Analysis of the B‐cell subset distribution based on the expression of CD21 and CD27 surface markers revealed that both groups of participants experienced a strong enrichment of variant cross‐reactive RBD+ CD27+CD21‐ following boosting immunization (Figure 2E,F and Supplementary Figure 2). Interestingly, we also detected an expansion of RBD+ WT CD27+CD21‐ B cells in the PHH‐1V81 group (Figure 2E), probably due to the engagement of low affinity RBD+ XBB.1.16 B cells. Consistent with total B cells, variant cross‐reactive IgG+ B cells significantly increased in both vaccinated groups (median fold‐change 1.68 [0.500–14.0] and 1.42 [0.704–2.41], for PHH‐1V81 and BNT162b2, respectively, Figure 3A). In addition, PHH‐1V81‐vaccinated individuals showed a significant increase of RBD+ WT IgG+ B cells (median fold‐change 1.18 [0.516–3.09], Figure 3A). Finally, PHH‐1V81‐vaccinated individuals exhibited a slightly higher fold‐change increase of RBD+ WT B cells cross‐reacting with the vaccine variant (median fold‐change for PHH‐1V81 1.51[14.8–0.91], and BTN162b2 1.16 [2.14–0.8] p = 0.0422, and 1.68[14.03‐0.50], and 1.42 [2.41–0.70] p = 0.0139, for total B cells and IgG+, respectively, Figure 3B). We next evaluated the interrelation between the serological and B‐cell compartments by examining correlation matrices between RBD IgG antibody levels, neutralization titers, and SARS‐CoV‐2‐reactive B cells. In the PHH‐1V81 group, we observed a strong association of the IgG antibody levels against RBDwt, RBDXBB.1.16, RBDXBB.1.5, and RBDJN.1 variants by timepoints D14 and M3 with the RBD+ WT/XBB.1.16 B‐cells by D14, indicating a strong coordination between both compartments and that high frequencies of SARS‐CoV‐2‐reactive B cells by D14 predicted high antibody levels by M3 (Figure 4A). In contrast, we found weaker and non‐statistically significant associations between both compartments in the BNT162b2 group (Figure 4B). However, the overall lack of associations between the serological and B‐cell compartments was likely due to the limited sample size of the BNT162b2 vaccine group. Finally, we detected a significant association between plasma antibody neutralization against WT, XBB.1.5, and XBB.1.16 variants and RBD+ WT/XBB.1.16 B cells in PHH‐1V81‐vaccinated individuals (Figures 2G,H, 3C,D and 4A).

Discussion

Our findings suggest a coordinated mobilization of germinal center responses on the export of B cells and plasma cells producing potent IgG antibodies against both the ancestral and the divergent variant included in the PHH‐1V81 vaccine. We demonstrate that a booster immunization with PHH‐1V81 provokes the expansion of RBD+ wt B‐cells cross‐reactive with the XBB.1.16 variant. In contrast, no de novo generation of variant‐specific B cells was observed in either vaccine group, consistent with immune imprinting induced by initial ancestral vaccination [11, 12, 13, 14, 17]. Interestingly, a previous report showed a slight but significant increase of B cells specific to the full spike of XBB.1.15 in 44 participants under hemodialysis vaccinated with BNT162b2 [18]. Nonetheless, the expanded RBD+ WT/XBB.1.16 B‐cell pool was associated with an efficient neutralizing response against XBB.1.16 variant after PHH‐1V81 booster potentially protecting upon subsequent exposures. Booster immunization with BNT162b2 also induced the expansion of RBD+ wt B cells cross‐reactive with the XBB.1.5 variant. However, the limited sample size of this vaccine group likely precluded to establish a significant association between neutralization and B‐cell responses. Moreover, the lack of significance between the serological and B‐cell compartments could also be explained by the BNT162b2 vaccine formulation containing a full spike‐based mRNA immunogen, unlike PHH‐1V81 which is based on a recombinant RBD protein [4]. While PHH‐1V81 and BNT162b2 vaccinated groups experienced an expansion of RBD+ wt B‐cells cross‐reactive with the XBB.1.16/XBB.1.5 variant, distinct previous SARS‐CoV‐2 exposure status could also explain the superior coordination between serological and B‐cell compartments observed in the former.

To counteract immune imprinting and induce optimal variant‐specific humoral responses by PHH‐1V81, additional studies with different vaccination regimens are warranted. In this regard, it was recently shown that repeated exposures to Omicron variants could mitigate ancestral immune imprinting inducing variant‐specific B cells [19]. Therefore, similar strategies would benefit the improvement of antibody breadth, considering the strong immunogenicity of PHH‐1V81. Finally, strong increases of antigen‐specific B cells post‐boost have been shown to be good predictors of a more durable memory B‐cell and neutralizing response upon subsequent immunizations [12, 20]. Long‐term follow up studies are required to evaluate the durability of the B‐cell and neutralizing antibody responses induced by PHH‐1V81 against autologous and heterologous variants. In summary, the present findings, together with those previously observed in the HIPRA‐HH‐14 clinical study [4], supports PHH‐1V81 as an efficient booster vaccine that induces robust humoral responses potentially providing an effective protection against emerging SARS‐CoV‐2 variants.

Methods

Human Samples

Paired sera and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were obtained at baseline (D0) and Day 14 (D14) from 40 subjects participating in the HIPRA HH‐14 trial, a Phase IIb/III pivotal trial [4], where adults previously vaccinated with a primary scheme and at least one booster dose of an EU‐approved mRNA vaccine randomly received a single intramuscular dose of 40 µg of PHH‐1V81 XBB.1.16 (n = 27) or 30 µg BNT162b2 XBB.1.5 (n = 13). Additionally, plasma‐only samples were collected 3 months post‐booster (M3). The trial enrolled healthy adults aged 18 or older who provided informed consent. Clinical information of the participants is depicted in Table 1. Previous SARS‐Cov‐2 exposure was reported in 70.4% (n = 27) and 46.2% (n = 13) in PHH‐1V81 XBB.1.16 and BNT162b2 XBB.1.5 vaccinated donors, respectively (Fisher exact test, p = 0.1748, Table 1). SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination status was similar between both groups of participants (Fisher exact test, p = 0.7215, Table 1). Last infection or vaccine dose must have been at least 6 months before Day 0. Participants with chronic diseases who were stable and controlled were also eligible for the study. PBMCs were isolated from venous blood samples by density‐gradient centrifugation using Ficoll‐Paque (Merck), cryopreserved in heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (HI‐FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Merck), and stored in liquid nitrogen. Samples belong to the HIPRA private collection C.0006636, and their use was approved by the ethical committee according to the Biomedical Research Study EIB‐2024‐001‐PHH1V (PM‐00151‐ID).

Luminex

Antigen‐coupled microspheres were added in multiplex to a 384‐well μClear flat bottom plate (Greiner Bio‐One) in 90 μL of Luminex Buffer (1% BSA, 0.05% Tween 20, 0.05% sodium azide in PBS) using an Integra Viaflo semi‐automatic device. Positive control pools were added to each assay plate as serially diluted titration curves for QA/QC purposes. Pre‐pandemic samples (n = 128) were used as negative controls. Technical blacks consisting in Luminex Buffer (0.05% Tween 20 in PBS) and microspheres without sample were added in each assay plate to control for background signal. Test and control plasma samples were added to the 384‐well plate using an Assist Plus Integra device. Samples were tested at 1:500 dilution. Samples and controls were incubated with the multiplex in the assay plate for 1 h at room temperature under shaking and protected from light. Then, plates were washed three times with 200 μL/well of Luminex buffer using a BioTek 405 TS. Secondary antibodies conjugated with phycoerythrin (PE), goat anti‐human IgG (GTIG‐001, Moss Bio, 1:400), IgA (GTIA‐001, Moss Bio, 1:200) or IgM (GTIM‐001, Moss Bio, 1:200) were added to each well and incubated for 30 min. Plates were washed and microspheres resuspended in 80 μL of Luminex Buffer to be acquired on a Flexmap 3Dreader. At least 50 microspheres per analyte per well were acquired. Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) was reported for each antigen. RBDXBB.1.16 (#SPD‐C82Q4) and RBDXBB.1.5 antigens were obtained from Acro Biosystems. Recombinant RBDWT and RBDJN.1 proteins were produced by transient transfection of exponentially growing Freestyle 293‐F suspension cells as previously described [21].

Flow Cytometry Immunophenotyping

Immunophenotyping of lymphocyte populations was performed on cryopreserved PBMC samples of immunized individuals. Recombinant biotinylated SARS‐CoV‐2 RBD antigens were used for tetramer preparation with fluorophore‐conjugated streptavidin (SA) to detect RBD‐specific B cells. Biotinylated Wuhan‐Hu‐1 RBD (Acro Biosystems, Cat# SPD‐C82E8) and XBB.1.16 RBD (Acro Biosystems, Cat# SPD‐C82Q4) or XBB.1.5 (Acro Biosystems, Cat# SPD‐C82Q3) were mixed in 4:1 molar ratio with PE SA (ThermoFisher, Cat# SA10041) or APC SA (ThermoFisher, Cat# S868), respectively, and incubated for 30 min on ice in the dark. Unbound SA sites were subsequently quenched using 0.8 mM biotin (Cat# 29129). Each tetramer preparation contained 7.97 µM RBD and 16 µM biotin. PBMCs were incubated with fluorescently labelled RBD tetramers (RBD‐Wu‐PE, all samples; RBD‐XBB.1.5‐APC or RBD‐XBB.1.16‐APC, samples from BNT162b2 or PHH‐1V81 groups, respectively), for 20 min at 4°C. Cells were then washed once with 1% FBS‐PBS (FACS buffer), and incubated for 30 min at 4°C with the following B‐cell staining panel antibodies: CD19 AF700 (HIB19, 1:50 dilution), CD21 BV421 (B‐Iy4, 1:50 dilution), CD27 PE‐CF594 (M‐T271, 1:100 dilution), IgM BV605 (G20‐127, 1: 50 dilution), IgG BV786 (G18‐145, 1:60 dilution), IgD APC‐H7 (IA6‐2, 1:100 dilution) (BD Biosciences) and IgA FITC (IS11‐8E10, 1:62.5 dilution) (Miltenyi Biotec). A LIVE/DEAD fixable dead cell stain kit (405 nm excitation) (Molecular Probes, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was also included. Finally, cells were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer, and then fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde‐PBS. Following a lymphocyte and single cell gating, dead cells were excluded. Flow cytometric analyses of stained cells were performed using a Cytek Aurora instrument (Cytek Biosciences), and the FlowJo software (v10.6, FlowJo LLC).

Neutralization Assay

The neutralization titre against Omicron XBB.1.5 and XBB.1.16 variants were determined by inhibitory dilution 50 (ID50) by a pseudovirion‐based neutralisation assay (PBNA) and reported as reciprocal dilution for each individual sample and geometric mean titre (GMT) for treatment group comparison. HIV reporter pseudoviruses expressing SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein and Luciferase were generated as previously described [22, 23]. Infectivity was verified for each stock produced being optimal at 20 000–25 000 Relative Light Unit (RLU). Pseudovirus was preincubated with serial dilutions of heat‐inactivated serum samples for 1 h at 37°C and then added onto ACE2 overexpressing HEK293T cells. After 48 h, cells were lysed with britelite plus luciferase reagent (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Luminescence was measured for 0·2 s with an EnSight multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer). Neutralization was calculated by comparing the experimental RLUs calculated from infected cells treated with each serum to the max RLUs (maximal infectivity calculated from untreated infected cells) and min RLUs (minimal infectivity calculated from uninfected cells) and expressed as the neutralization percentage: Neutralization (%) = (RLUmax–RLUexperimental)/(RLUmax–RLUmin) *100.

ID50 were calculated by plotting and fitting neutralization values and the log of serum dilution to a 4‐parameters equation in Prism 9.0.2 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Wilcoxon matched‐paired signed rank test was used for longitudinal analysis to compare paired measurements between timepoints D0, D14 and M3 within the same individual. Unpaired Mann–Whitney test was used for group comparison. Spearman rank correlation coefficients were calculated with two‐tailed p‐values with 95% confidence intervals. Correlograms were performed using R (v.4.3.1), employing the Hmisc package (v.5.2.2) to calculate Spearman correlations and the corrplot package (v.0.92) for visualization. p‐values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini and Hochberg (BH) method to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR) when appropriate. For the isotype and phenotype analysis of RBD+ B cells, samples with less than 10 cell counts in the parental population were excluded. Statistics are described in figure legends.
