Association between clinical pulmonary infection score and mortality in patients with severe pneumonia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Daxiu Lin, Yihua Zeng, Mei Huang, Yan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association of clinical pulmonary infection score (CPIS) with risk of mortality in patients with severe pneumonia remains unclear now. This study aimed to identify the prognostic role of CPIS for mortality among severe pneumonia patients based on available evidence.MethodsPubMed, Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Web of Science (WOS) databases were searched up to August 30, 2025. Observational studies evaluating the association between CPIS and mortality in patients with severe pneumonia were included. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were combined and subgroup by age, combination of multiple organ failure (MOF), and type of CPIS variable were performed.ResultsTen observational studies involving 1,048 patients were included with the mortality rate of 34.3% (359/1,048). Pooled results demonstrated that higher CPIS was significantly related to increased risk of mortality in patients with severe pneumonia (OR =3.19, 95% CI: 2.28–4.45, P<0.001). Then subgroup analyses by the age (elderly: OR =5.90, P<0.001; mixed: OR =2.23, P<0.001), combination of MOF (yes: OR =5.90, P<0.001; no: OR =1.89, P=0.002; nor reported: OR =3.60, P=0.023) and type of CPIS variable (categorical: OR =5.90, P<0.001; continuous: OR =2.23, P<0.001) manifested consistent results.ConclusionsCPIS was significantly associated with the risk of mortality in severe pneumonia and patients with higher CPIS were exposed to the increased risk of death.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This meta-analysis of 10 retrospective studies involving 1,048 patients showed that a higher clinical pulmonary infection score (CPIS) was significantly associated with increased mortality in patients with severe pneumonia.

• The association remained significant across subgroup analyses stratified by age, multiple organ failure status, and CPIS variable type.

What is known and what is new?

• Severe pneumonia is associated with high mortality, and timely risk stratification is essential for guiding treatment and improving outcomes. CPIS is a simple and widely used clinical tool for assessing pulmonary infection, but its prognostic value in severe pneumonia has remained uncertain.

• This study is the first meta-analysis, to our knowledge, to systematically demonstrate that elevated CPIS is significantly associated with a higher risk of death in severe pneumonia patients, supporting its potential role as a prognostic indicator.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• CPIS may serve as a practical bedside tool for early mortality risk assessment in severe pneumonia and may help clinicians identify high-risk patients who require closer monitoring and more aggressive management.

• However, given that all included studies were retrospective and conducted in China, large prospective multicenter studies are still needed before CPIS can be more broadly adopted as a robust prognostic marker in routine clinical practice.

Introduction

Severe pneumonia remains a major cause of hospitalization and mortality worldwide. In 2019, lower respiratory tract infections—including pneumonia—accounted for approximately 2.49 million deaths globally, ranking as the fourth leading cause of death after neonatal conditions, ischemic heart disease, and stroke (1). Among critically ill patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs), mortality rates for severe pneumonia range from 20% to over 50% (1,2). Streptococcus pneumoniae continues to be the most frequently identified pathogen in adult community-acquired pneumonia; however, advances in molecular diagnostics have revealed an increasing role of respiratory viruses (2-4). To evaluate prognosis in pneumonia, several clinical scoring systems are routinely applied, including the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI), Confusion, Urea, Respiratory rate, Blood pressure, age 65 years (CURB-65), and Infectious Diseases Society of America/American Thoracic Society (IDSA/ATS) guideline criteria, as well as ICU-based severity scores such as Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA). These models generally demonstrate good predictive performance, with the PSI and IDSA/ATS criteria achieving area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) of 0.86–0.87, while APACHE II, SOFA, and quick SOFA (qSOFA) show AUCs of approximately 0.85, 0.83, and 0.81, respectively (5,6).

The clinical pulmonary infection score (CPIS) was originally developed to support the diagnosis of ventilator-associated pneumonia by integrating clinical variables such as body temperature, white blood cell count, tracheal secretions, oxygenation index, and chest radiographic findings (7,8). CPIS is simple to calculate and noninvasive, which has promoted its use in guiding antimicrobial therapy decisions. However, its prognostic utility remains insufficiently explored. For example, one study reported that CPIS >7 predicted 30-day mortality in ventilator-associated pneumonia patients with 74.4% sensitivity but only 49.0% specificity, yielding an AUC of just 0.612—much lower than that of APACHE II (9). In a retrospective observational study of 92 patients with ventilator-associated pneumonia, 50% had a CPIS <6 on day 1 and 73% had a CPIS ≥6 on day 3. The 28-day mortality was 31.5%, but there was no statistically significant association between CPIS on day 1 or 3 and mortality (9). However, the value of CPIS in prognostic assessment among patients with severe pneumonia remains limited and inconsistent. Nevertheless, no systematic meta-analysis has synthesized the available evidence.

Therefore, this study was designed and conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis statement and aimed to investigate the association between CPIS and mortality in patients with severe pneumonia, thereby clarifying its potential role as a prognostic tool. Importantly, prognostic association and predictive discrimination represent distinct methodological concepts. The present study focuses on the association between CPIS and mortality risk, rather than on the discriminatory performance of CPIS as a prediction model. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2715/rc) (10).

Methods

Literature search

The PubMed, Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Web of Science (WOS) databases were searched up to August 30, 2025 with the following terms: “clinical pulmonary infection score”, “CPIS”, “severe pneumonia”, “mortality”, and “death”. Detailed literature search strategies in individual databases are shown in Appendix 1. References of included studies were also reviewed.

Criteria for inclusion

Studies that met the following criteria were included: (I) patients were diagnoses with severe pneumonia according to the international or domestic guidelines (11,12); (II) the CPIS was evaluated according to previously published formula (13,14); (III) the association between CPIS and mortality rate was explored; (IV) odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were provided or enough data were reported to calculate them; and (V) available full texts.

Criteria for exclusion

Studies that met the following criteria were excluded: (I) duplicated, overlapped, or had insufficient data; and (II) reviews, case reports, meeting abstracts, or animal trials.

Data extraction

The following information was collected from the included studies: the first author, country, year, sample size, number of deaths, age, combination of multiple organ failure (MOF), type of CPIS variable, OR, and 95% CI.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates studies across three domains: Selection (0–4 stars), Comparability (0–2 stars), and Outcome (0–3 stars). Studies with an NOS score ≥6 were considered high quality, consistent with commonly accepted thresholds in observational meta-analyses. Particular attention was paid to the Comparability domain, which reflects whether studies adjusted for key confounders such as age, baseline disease severity, or comorbidities (15).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted by STATA version 15.0 software. Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated by Q test and I2 statistics. When significant heterogeneity was detected representing as I2>50% and/or P<0.10, the random effects model was used; or fixed effects model was applied (16). ORs with 95% CIs were combined to evaluate the relationship of CPIS with the risk of mortality. Subgroup analysis based on age (elderly vs. mixed), combination of MOF (yes vs. no), and type of CPIS variable (categorical vs. continuous). Sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the stability of pooled results. Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test were performed to detect publication bias, and significant publication bias was defined as P<0.05 (17,18).

Results

A total of 2,497 publications were searched from databases and 213 duplicated records were excluded. After reviewing titles, abstracts, and full texts, ten available studies were eventually included in this meta-analysis. The specific process was presented in Figure 1.

All 10 studies were retrospective and from China. A total of 1,048 patients were enrolled with the mortality rate of 34.3% (359/1,048). Four studies focused on elderly patients combined with MOF and applied the CPIS of 6 as the optimal critical value to clarify the association between CPIS and mortality among severe pneumonia patients. Association between CPIS and mortality in patients with severe pneumonia

After combining the 10 included studies, pooled results demonstrated that CPIS was significantly related to the risk of mortality among severe pneumonia patients and patients with higher CPIS were at increased risk of death (OR =3.19, 95% CI: 2.28–4.45, P<0.001; I2=92.6%, P<0.001) (Figure 2).

Then subgroup analyses stratified by the age (age: OR =5.90, 95% CI: 4.01–8.70, P<0.001; mixed: OR =2.23, 95% CI: 1.53–3.25, P<0.001) (Figure S1), combination of MOF (yes: OR =5.90, 95% CI: 4.01–8.70, P<0.001; no: OR =1.89, 95% CI: 1.26–2.84, P=0.002; not reported: OR =3.60, 95% CI: 1.19–10.88, P=0.02) (Figure S2) and type of CPIS (categorical: OR =5.90, 95% CI: 4.01–8.70, P<0.001; continuous: OR =2.23, 95% CI: 1.53–3.25, P<0.001) (Figure S3) were performed, which further identified the significant association of CPIS with mortality in severe pneumonia patients. Discussion

This study identified the significant association between CPIS and risk of mortality in patients with severe pneumonia for the first time and higher CPIS was related to increased risk of death. Furthermore, we performed the subgroup analyses based on age, combination of MOF, and type of CPIS variable, and further demonstrated the prognostic role of CPIS for patients with severe pneumonia. However, importantly, this study focuses on the association between CPIS and mortality risk rather than on the development or validation of a prognostic prediction model. Meanwhile, there might be some limitations in this meta-analysis; thus, more high-quality studies are still needed to prove the above conclusion.

The CPIS serves as a composite measure encompassing several key clinical and laboratory parameters—such as body temperature, leukocyte count, tracheal secretions, oxygenation status [partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2)], radiographic findings, and microbiological culture results. Elevated CPIS values indicate a higher burden of infection, impaired gas exchange, and systemic inflammatory response, all of which contribute to the development of sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and multi-organ dysfunction, leading to increased mortality. A prospective study demonstrated that rising CPIS from baseline through day 5 strongly predicted worse outcomes in ventilator-associated pneumonia, underscoring its ability to reflect disease trajectory and prognosis (29). Furthermore, the CPIS has been shown to be a reliable tool for diagnosing ventilator-associated pneumonia. In a study involving 135 patients with ventilator-associated pneumonia, the CPIS was significantly higher in non-survivors compared to survivors, indicating its potential as a prognostic indicator (9,30). Additionally, integrating CPIS with biomarkers such as procalcitonin (PCT) or C-reactive protein (CRP) has been found to improve early detection and risk stratification, potentially augmenting predictive accuracy over CPIS alone. In summary, the CPIS provides a comprehensive assessment of infection severity and systemic involvement, making it a valuable tool in predicting mortality risk in patients with severe pneumonia.

In clinical practice, CPIS offers a pragmatic and inexpensive bedside tool to monitor therapy effectiveness and guide antibiotic management. Serial CPIS measurements can help detect inadequate response to treatment—persistent elevated scores may prompt clinicians to reassess antimicrobial regimes or escalate support, while consistently low or decreasing scores (e.g., CPIS <6 by day 3) may aid in safely discontinuing antibiotics, reducing unnecessary exposure. However, caution is warranted: in complex patients such as those with trauma, the components of CPIS may overlap with non-infectious systemic inflammation, potentially prolonging unnecessary antibiotic courses.

To enhance the prognostic value of CPIS, future studies should explore several complementary approaches. First, combining CPIS with biomarkers such as PCT or CRP may improve early detection of high-risk patients and enhance risk stratification, potentially increasing predictive accuracy compared to CPIS alone. Second, large-scale prospective studies are needed to refine CPIS thresholds and examine serial trends over time, such as persistent elevation vs. decreasing patterns, in order to better predict outcomes across different pneumonia subtypes, including community-acquired pneumonia, hospital-acquired pneumonia, and ventilator-associated pneumonia. Finally, integrating CPIS into predictive models alongside validated severity scores such as APACHE II or SOFA, and leveraging machine learning techniques, may facilitate personalized risk prediction, enable earlier interventions, and inform the development of clinical decision-support systems for critically ill pneumonia patients.

There are several limitations in our study. First, all the included studies were conducted in China and the sample size is relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare systems and populations. Second, more subgroup analyses by other important parameters, such as the cause of pneumonia and treatment strategy, are unable to be performed due to lack of original data. Third, in this meta-analysis, we only focused on the risk of mortality, and the association of CPIS with other clinical outcomes, such as the quality of life and symptoms, could be investigated in future studies. Four, head-to-head comparisons between CPIS and other established severity scores (e.g., APACHE II or SOFA) could not be performed due to the lack of individual-level comparative data in the included studies.

Conclusions

CPIS was significantly associated with the risk of mortality in severe pneumonia patients and patients with higher CPIS were exposed to the increased risk of death. However, due to limitations of included studies, more prospective studies are still needed to verify our findings.
