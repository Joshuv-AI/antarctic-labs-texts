Association between COVID-19 vaccination status and mortality in hospitalised COVID-19 patients during the Omicron period: a retrospective cohort study in Taiwan
Authors: Ming-Hung Chang, Shian-Chin Ko, Kuang-Ming Liao, Chung-Han Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThe Omicron variant of SARS-CoV-2 has emerged as the predominant strain of COVID-19 since 2022. Its association with prior vaccines remained under investigation.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingClinical data for patients, from 1 May 2022 to 31 January 2023, were extracted from the Chi-Mei Medical Center, Chiali electronic medical record databases.ParticipantsHospitalised COVID-19 patients in dedicated wards were enrolled in the study. Cases of COVID-19 reinfection and relapse were also included. Patients who did not have COVID-19, those with PCR repositivity, or those with incomplete laboratory data were excluded.InterventionsVarious doses of vaccines included primary series, additional dose and booster. Introduction

The emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic, stemming from the SARS-CoV-2, has presented a significant global health challenge. Epidemiological investigations have underscored key risk factors for severe disease and mortality, including advanced age, racial disparities and underlying health conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic lung disease and obesity.1 Efforts to combat the spread of the virus have centred on vaccine development, which has demonstrated efficacy in preventing symptomatic illness and reducing the risk of severe outcomes such as hospitalisation and death.2,5

As vaccination efforts have gained momentum, discussions have shifted towards transitioning from strict Zero-COVID policy towards coexistence with the virus in a postpandemic landscape. However, the emergence of the Omicron variant, characterised by heightened transmissibility, has rapidly become the dominant variant of concern since its identification in November 2021. Taiwan, in particular, has grappled with a significant surge in COVID-19 cases since the spring of 2022.6 In this study, we sought to examine the relationship between vaccination status and the incidence of hospitalisation among COVID-19 patients admitted to dedicated wards.

Methods

Case collection

This study retrospectively enrolled hospitalised COVID-19 patients isolated in dedicated wards from May 2022 to January 2023 by Chi-Mei Medical Center, Chiali electronic medical record databases. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research. Diagnosis confirmation was based on a positive result from nucleic acid amplification test (PCR) or rapid antigen test for SARS-CoV-2. COVID-19 reinfection and relapse were included, while PCR repositivity was excluded.7

Baseline comorbidities were evaluated using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), with a range of conditions including malignancy, cerebrovascular disease, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, peptic ulcer disease, liver disease, chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, connective tissue disease, dementia, hemiplegia and AIDS.8 The Quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score (qSOFA) based on altered mental status, respiratory rate and systolic blood pressure was calculated on admission or transfer to dedicated wards.9 Laboratory parameters, encompassing white cell count, absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count, platelet count and haemoglobin levels, were also recorded.

COVID-19 vaccination

The study investigated various doses of COVID-19 vaccines, which were categorised as the primary series (first two doses), additional doses and booster doses (third dose onward). Approved vaccines by the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration encompassed ChAdOx1-S (Oxford/AstraZeneca), messenger RNA-based vaccines such as BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech), mRNA-1273 and mRNA-1273.214 (Moderna), as well as recombinant protein vaccines like NVX-CoV2373 (Novavax) and MVC-COV1901 (Medigen). The interval between the date of last vaccination and the date of confirmed COVID-19 diagnosis was evaluated.

Specific therapies for COVID-19

The potential targets of COVID-19 therapies included inhibition of viral replication and a decrease in inflammation. Our study recorded the administration of antiviral agents such as nirmatrelvir+ritonavir (Paxlovid), molnupiravir (Lagevrio) and remdesivir (Veklury), alongside corticosteroids like dexamethasone.10 Nirmatrelvir+ritonavir, molnupiravir and a 3-day regimen of remdesivir were typically prescribed for COVID-19 patients exhibiting risk factors for severe disease classified by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention,1 but not requiring supplemental oxygen. Conversely, a 5-day regimen of remdesivir was indicated for cases classified as severe, characterised by oxygen saturation levels ≤94% under ambient air conditions or necessitating oxygen supplementation without endotracheal tube intubation. Dexamethasone, on the other hand, was reserved for patients with severe disease requiring oxygen supplementation, high-flow nasal oxygen, non-invasive ventilation, intubation or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was mortality in 30 days, which was defined as the duration from admission or acquiring a nosocomial infection onset to the date of death from any cause, within a 30-day observation period. The secondary outcomes were mortality in 90 days and severe disease of COVID-19. The presence of hypoxaemia (oxygen saturation ≤94% on room air) or the need for oxygenation support indicated severe disease.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.24.0 software (SPSS). Categorical variables were assessed using the χ2 test, except for those with expected frequencies of less than 5, for which Fisher’s exact test was employed. Mean comparisons of continuous variables between two groups were performed using the independent sample t-test. 30 and 90 days survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test or generalised Wilcoxon test. Multivariable analysis calculated HR by Cox proportional hazard model, which assessed the association between potential risk factors and mortality in 30 days. All p values were derived from a two-tailed hypothesis, with statistical significance established at p<0.05.

Clinical outcomes

There were 44.1% of hospitalised COVID-19 patients exhibiting severe disease. The overall mortality rate was 18.6% in 30 days and 21.1% in 90 days. The 30-day mortality rates were 15.9% in the vaccination group and 23.8% in the non-vaccination group, showing a significant difference (p=0.037). Secondary outcomes between vaccination and non-vaccination are shown in table 1. The survival curve plotted by the Kaplan-Meier method is shown in figure 3.

30-day (a) and 90-day (b) survival curves of hospitalised COVID-19 patients with vaccination (green line) and without vaccination (blue line).

Multivariable analysis for 30-day survival by Cox regression, shown in table 2, identified age, CCI, qSOFA and administration of dexamethasone as independent and strong prognostic indicators for survival. Patients aged over 75 exhibited a hazard of death approximately 2.2 times higher than their counterparts under the age of 75 (adjust HR (aHR) 2.188; 95% CI 1.237 to 3.872; p=0.007). Individuals with a CCI of 3 or above had a hazard of death 2.6 times higher than those with a CCI below 3 (aHR 2.584; 95% CI 1.343 to 4.969; p=0.004). The presence of a qSOFA score of 2 or more revealed 3.5 times higher hazard of death than that of 1 or below (aHR 3.520; 95% CI 2.035 to 6.089; p<0.001). Patients with administration of dexamethasone showed about 2 times higher hazard of death than patients without dexamethasone (aHR 1.975; 95% CI 1.172 to 3.328; p=0.011).

The acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccine reduced the risk of 30-day mortality, but this did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (aHR 0.831; 95% CI 0.541 to 1.277; p=0.398). In table 3, we evaluated various vaccine doses, type of primary series and the intervals between last vaccination date and diagnosis date of COVID-19. Our analysis revealed a significant reduction in the crude HR (cHR) with each extra vaccine dose. In addition, a notable decrease in cHR was observed among patients receiving three or more doses, those administered mRNA-based or recombinant protein vaccine as their primary series, and those with an interval of less than 12 weeks between their last vaccination date and the diagnosis date of COVID-19. After adjusting for confounding variables, only the group with an interval of less than 12 weeks retained marginal statistical significance (aHR 0.423; 95% CI 0.165 to 1.086; p=0.074). In the subgroup analysis shown in figure 4, vaccination demonstrated significantly lower adjusted HR among patients aged <75 years (aHR 0.297; 95% CI 0.090 to 0.979; p=0.046) and those with CCI <3 (aHR 0.122; 95% CI 0.026 to 0.566; p=0.007).

Discussion

Extensive research into SARS-CoV-2 drove the rapid development of COVID-19 vaccines. These vaccines primarily target the spike protein, a key element of the virus that attaches to the ACE2 receptor on human cells. They employed diverse technologies, including adenovirus vector vaccines, messenger RNA-based vaccines and recombinant protein vaccines. Studies showed the remarkable efficacy and effectiveness of these vaccines in reducing infection rates, disease severity, hospitalisations and mortality associated with COVID-19.2,5

However, with the process of adaptive mutations, the Omicron variant, along with its numerous subvariants, notably BA.1, BA.2, BA.3, BA.4, BA.5, BF.7, BQ.1.1, XBB and descendant lineages, gained dominance worldwide in 2022 and influenced vaccine effectiveness with its potential of immune escape.11 12 Research by Andrews et al suggested that the initial two doses of primary series offered limited defence against symptomatic disease caused by the Omicron variant.13 Consequently, the administration of an additional dose or booster became imperative during the Omicron era. Although the mRNA booster’s effectiveness in preventing infection wanes over time, it provides durable protection against hospitalisation and mortality.13,16

Our study population predominantly consisted of individuals of advanced age and a notable prevalence of comorbidities. Despite the Omicron variant showing reduced virulence compared with earlier variants such as Delta, reviewed by Relan et al,17 vulnerable populations remained susceptible. After the initial diagnosis of COVID-19, the vaccination group exhibited a 40.1% incidence of severe disease, whereas the non-vaccination group showed a significantly higher prevalence of 51.9% for severe disease. The mortality rate in 30 days was 15.9% vs 23.8% between vaccination and non-vaccination groups, with statistical significance. However, the patients with vaccination demonstrated a relatively low CCI and qSOFA, which could potentially influence the outcomes.

In multivariable analysis, we analysed potential predictors affecting 30-day survival. The association between vaccination and survival became less apparent after adjusting for multiple variables, even when considering different doses or types of primary series. The interval between the last vaccination date and the diagnosis date of COVID-19 might play a role in 30-day survival. Populations with an interval of less than 12 weeks had a lower hazard of death, with marginal statistical significance. The correlation of the vaccine decreased as the interval increased. Associations between other clinical factors and survival were also observed in our study. Consistent with our findings, advancing age emerged as the most significant risk factor for severe COVID-19 outcomes, with the risk escalating notably with increasing age.1 The underlying diseases identified by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention as contributing to severe COVID-19 contained malignancy, cerebrovascular disease, heart conditions, chronic lung disease (limited to interstitial lung disease, pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension, bronchiectasis, asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), tuberculosis, chronic liver disease (limited to cirrhosis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, alcoholic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis), chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, disabilities, dementia, mental health disorder, obesity, immunodeficiency and pregnancy.1 Despite the variation from above comorbidities, CCI was still useful to predict outcome. Tuty Kuswardhani et al reviewed that a higher mean CCI score was associated with mortality and disease severity of COVID-19,18 and our multivariable analysis demonstrated a significantly increased risk within the high CCI group. The qSOFA also dominated the outcome of 30-day survival in our study. Heydari et al reported that the qSOFA score could be considered a simple and rapid screening tool for identifying high-risk COVID-19 patients due to the excellent area under a receiver operating characteristic curve.19

Absolute lymphocyte count was recognised as a useful and easily obtained indicator in determining the severity of hospitalised COVID-19 patients. Lymphopenia or decrease in absolute lymphocyte count was considered to be associated with severe disease of COVID-19.20,22 Nevertheless, the patients with an absolute lymphocyte count lower than 800 per μL did not experience a higher risk of mortality in our study. A 5-day regimen of remdesivir was the only antiviral medicine indicated for severe disease of COVID-19. Other antiviral agents, such as nirmatrelvir+ritonavir, molnupiravir and 3-day regimen of remdesivir, were approved for mild disease. Interestingly, the adjusted HR for 5-day remdesivir treatment did not reach statistical significance. This phenomenon was attributed to the possibility that some patients with severe disease might no longer meet the indications for medication, either due to endotracheal tube intubation or immediate death. Corticosteroid was known as an anti-inflammatory drug in severe and critical COVID-19. It explained the significantly higher HR in the administration of dexamethasone in our study.

In our subgroup analysis, vaccination was associated with lower mortality among relatively young populations and low-CCI group after adjustment. This observation could potentially imply that the efficacy of current vaccines might be comparatively diminished among individuals with advanced age and multiple comorbidities. Jo et al demonstrated the characteristics of immune responses to the two doses of mRNA vaccine in older individuals, revealing a lower induction and early contraction of antigen-specific T cells.23 Huang et al illustrated that subjects with more comorbidities had a poor serological response to three doses of COVID-19 vaccination.24 Besides, when comparing different primary series, the multivariable Cox regression analysis showed no significant difference in 30-day mortality between mRNA vaccines and ChAdOx1-S recipients, suggesting that baseline characteristics might explain the crude differences. Similarly, a nationwide cohort study by Bouillon et al reported that two doses of ChAdOx1-S were as effective as two doses of mRNA vaccines against severe outcomes during the Alpha and Delta periods,25 and our findings indicated that such differences also appeared minimal in the Omicron era.

Limitations

Some limitations were noted in our study. First, this study was retrospective and conducted at a single centre, which might introduce biases such as selection bias, information bias and misclassification. Second, the evaluation of vaccination effectiveness was limited by the absence of a community population, including uninfected individuals and those with mild disease managed at home. Third, some patients in the dedicated ward had other principal diagnoses, and COVID-19 was not their main illness. Lastly, the absence of a dedicated intensive care unit at the district hospital in southern Taiwan resulted in most critical COVID-19 patients accepting Do-Not-Resuscitate. Those opting for aggressive medical interventions would be transferred to other facilities.

Conclusions

The strong indicators including age, CCI and qSOFA dominated the clinical outcomes of COVID-19. In subgroup analysis, a statistically significant association with better 30-day survival was observed among relatively young patients and those with low CCI. No statistically significant association was observed among patients with advanced age or higher comorbidity burden.
