Deep Learning‐Based Prediction of Final Infarct Core from CT Perfusion Data: A Comparison to the Clinical Standard
Authors: Freda Werdiger, Milanka Visser, Chushuang Chen, Christina Lam, James Kolacz, Mark W. Parsons, Longting Lin, Christopher Levi, Andrew Bivard, the INSPIRE (International Stroke Perfusion Imaging REgistry) Study Group
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the management of acute ischemic stroke, computed tomography perfusion (CTP) is used to define the ischemic core and penumbra to estimate tissue fate after reperfusion therapy. The core/penumbra dichotomy uses single‐value thresholds, which potentially discards valuable data and oversimplifies the complexity of core and penumbral estimation. Advancing the dichotomous CTP output to a probability model has several advantages such as more sophisticated modeling of pathophysiology, supporting reader interpretation, and assessing a greater range of available data to estimate tissue fate.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we developed a CTP probability model to move away from single perfusion thresholds to estimate tissue fate. All patients from the International Stroke Perfusion Imaging Registry database had baseline CTP and were included in the current study if they had a large vessel occlusion that recanalized fully after thrombectomy and had follow‐up diffusion‐weighted imaging. Data were split into training, validation, and testing groups. Training and validation cohorts were used to develop a deep learning model in project MONAI (Medical Open Network for Artificial Intelligence) and performance metrics were derived from the testing set.ResultsIn total, 243 patients were included in the study. The Attention U‐Net was the best performing deep learning model, producing the best prediction of follow‐up infarct core on the test set (n = 48): mean diverse counterfactual explanations score = 0.430±0.213, mean area under the curve = 0.765±0.095; better than the single‐value thresholding with a diverse counterfactual explanations scoreof 0.247±0.167 (paired t‐test, P<0.0001) and area under the curveof 0.604±0.074 (P<0.0001).ConclusionThe deep learning probabilistic CTP model outperformed the current clinical standard, providing a more accurate core estimate than single‐threshold‐based measures.

Clinical Perspective

Computed tomography perfusion imaging is commonly used to guide treatment in the course of acute ischemic stroke care. This typically involves the use of automated software to measure lesion volumes. The mathematical models underpinning these are >30 years old and there have been no advances since.This study introduces a new model that improves on the current standard of a single threshold by adopting a deep learning structure capable of modeling complex relationships between computed tomography perfusion imaging data and treatment outcome and expressing the result as a probability of tissue infarction rather than a binary prediction of infarct or healthy tissue.The new model is shown here to significantly outperform the current imaging standards and with further development represents an improvement on the tools currently used by clinicians, offering more accurate identification of tissue destined to infarct versus salvageable tissue, and thus more accurately guiding reperfusion treatment decisions.

In the hyperacute phase of acute ischemic stroke, computed tomography perfusion (CTP) imaging is often acquired to assist in decision‐making. CTP‐based decision making underpins a tissue‐based approach to selecting patients for reperfusion therapies 1 where patients with a smaller ischemic core and large penumbra have been shown to have excellent clinical outcomes with complete reperfusion. Additionally, patients with a large ischemic core are unlikely to have an excellent clinical outcome but may still benefit from treatment. 2 CTP has also been used to extend the treatment time window for treatment with thrombolysis and thrombectomy. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 The value of CTP is in the support of treatment decision‐making by assisting clinicians to predict treatment benefit in an individual patient. Used in this manner, CTP constitutes a form of personalized medicine.

In a clinical setting, hemodynamic maps are derived from the CTP source data using commercial software. 8 Typically, these maps are cerebral blood volume (CBV), cerebral blood flow (CBF), mean transit time, and Tmax or delay time (DT). Single‐value thresholds are then applied to respective CTP maps to distinguish the volume of tissue that is ischemic but salvageable (penumbra) from tissue is the unsalvageable (ischemic core). For example, regions of the CBF map where blood flow is reduced by >30% (CBF<30%) has been well validated as an approximation of the ischemic core, and regions where DT is >3 seconds as the penumbra. 9 , 10 Therefore, in a clinical setting, CBF<30% may be used to estimate the volume of tissue that will infarct even after successful recanalization. The actual extent of tissue infarction is then typically established by performing magnetic resonance diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) 24 to72 hours after stroke onset. However, this method of extracting the ischemic core volume from CBF alone discards the value of combining data from several maps to make a prediction on the final infarct but is not accessible under the single‐value‐threshold model.

Limitations of single value thresholding (eg, CBF<30%) to predict tissue infarction have been noted. First, the dichotomy of core/penumbra, or more broadly, predicting ischemic tissue as either dead or alive, may be an oversimplification. Instances of so‐called ghost infarct, or tissue reversal, have been reported in the literature; regions designated as ischemic core may later revert to healthy tissue. 11 Laredo et al 12 have noted that single‐value thresholding accuracy in predicting final infarct varies with time. Depending on the timing of perfusion imaging, minute‐to‐minute fluctuations in blood flow arising from dynamic changes in collateral perfusion may transition tissue from ``core’’ to ``penumbra’’ (and vice versa). Chen et al 13 noted that accurate threshold values vary between grey and white matter. The most likely explanation for these observations is that a single threshold approximating infarcted tissue fails to account for the significant variation that is seen in individual stroke patients due to factors such as age, comorbidities, disease severity, lesion topography, or impact of treatment (i.e., partial versus complete recanalization).

In recent years, the development of artificial intelligence and deep learning (DL) applications for medicine has accelerated. DL models are capable of modeling complex systems such as the relationship between acute CTP data and final tissue fate, due to their mathematical sophistication. 14 They are also particularly applicable to medical imaging problems. For analysis of perfusion maps, information such as the location of the perfusion deficit within the brain can be considered alongside voxel intensity, whereas single‐value thresholding considers local voxel intensity only. Finally, DL models have the flexibility to include several CTP maps, in addition to other data, in making predictions and are not limited, as single‐value thresholding is, by one map at a time.

In this study, we have used DL to predict ischemic core from acute CTP data in a cohort of patients who had full recanalization. We used the follow‐up DWI infarct as the basis, or ground truth, to develop the DL model. In doing so, we aimed to move away from ischemic core estimations based on the thresholding of a single CTP map and improve on the current clinical standard with an updated, DL‐based model capable of more accurate predictions of tissue fate.

Methods

Data were taken from the INSPIRE database and can be made available upon a reasonable request to the corresponding author at Freda.Werdiger@unimelb.edu.au. All code is available at github.com/FredaWerdiger/CTPDeepLearningPrediction.

Study Design

This was a study using data from the INSPIRE (International Stroke Perfusion Imaging Registry) database consisting of patients who presented to hospital with a large vessel occlusion and underwent baseline CTP imaging within 24 hours of symptom onset. All patients underwent successful endovascular clot retrieval (ECR) with complete/near‐complete reperfusion restored to occluded vessels. Patient then underwent follow‐up magnetic resonance DWI to assess the extent of tissue injury. The goal of this study was to predict the follow‐up DWI infarct from acute CTP data.

All data were sourced from a subset of the INSPIRE, known as ATLAS. ATLAS data were in a structured format that was designed for use in artificial intelligence platforms. Subjects presented to 1 of 16 participating sites across Australia, China, Canada, and the United Kingdom between 2011 and 2022 and were recruited into the registry prospectively (Hunter New England Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee Reference No. 11/08/17/4.01).

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

For this study, large vessel occlusion was defined as an occlusion of either the internal carotid artery or the M1 branch of the middle cerebral artery. Patients were required to have a complete occlusion of the M1 or internal carotid artery at the time of acute imaging. The acute thrombolysis in myocardial infraction score was used to select patients with a complete occlusion (thrombolysis in myocardial infarction score = 0). Images and clinical data were reviewed by A.B., M.V., and F.W. and patients excluded if it was apparent that the occlusion spontaneously reperfused prior to CTP acquisition, or if a clot migrated to another location before follow‐up imaging. Patient were included if they received ECR treatment, with or without intravenous thrombolysis. Successful recanalization was determined via the modified thrombolysis in cerebral infarction score. A modified thrombolysis in cerebral infarction score score of 2c or 3 was considered an important inclusion criterion (all thrombolysis in cerebral infarction score2b cases were initially included and reviewed by C.C. for potential reclassification to 2c according to Goyal et al 15 and Almekhlafi et al. 16 ).

Complete datasets for acute CTP and follow‐up DWI imaging in INSPIRE were required for inclusion in this study; patients with missing imaging data were excluded. Cases with severe noise, streak artifact, or excessive motion were also excluded. Patients were excluded if midline shift or major hemorrhagic transformations (parenchymal hematoma type 2 in the Heidelberg bleeding classification) were present on follow‐up DWI imaging and led to structural deformation that excluded successful registration to acute CTP.

Image Acquisition

CTP studies were acquired with a wide range of scanners from different manufacturers (Toshiba, Siemens, Phillips, GE Medical Systems) with different voxel thickness (0.5–10 mm) and axial coverage (8–16 cm whole brain). A contrast bolus was administered to the patient for vessel enhancement. The acquisitions were either a single volume or multiple (17–24) separate volumes through the duration of the bolus passage. The scan duration varied from 53 to 62 seconds.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging scans were acquired with a wide range of scanners from different manufacturers (Siemens, Phillips, GE Medical Systems, United Imaging Healthcare) with different field strength (1.5 or 3 T) and voxel thickness (2–7 mm). DWI acquisition (b v = 1000 s/mm2) was used as a gold standard measure of the ischemic core. The boundaries of the stroke infarct needed to be demarcated on the DWI image for the patient to be included in the study.

Preprocessing

Source CTP data were processed automatically using commercial software (MIStar, Apollo Imaging, Melbourne, Australia); CBV, CBF, mean transit time, and DT were derived for each subject using single‐value deconvolution with delay and dispersion correction. 17 In addition, the mean of all contrast‐free frames (mean baseline [MBL] image) was automatically derived for each subject. This image was used to enrich the DL model with structural information from the same imaging dataset (as patients were INSPIRE participants, all had CTP acquired but not always noncontrast CT). Motion correction was performed automatically. Finally, the ischemic core map was also derived through MIStar using previously validated thresholds of ischemic core CBF<30% of normal tissue to perform a comparison to the DL‐predicted infarct. 9

For DWI images, stroke infarct was first segmented by an in‐house tool (github.com/FredaWerdiger/dwi_inference), 18 and results were carefully reviewed by 2 experts (J.K. and D.L.). Corrections were made if necessary. A third expert (F.W.) reviewed final segmentations to ensure faithful ground truth maps. Reviewers had access to all patient imaging and clinical data registered to INSPIRE. Finally, the segmentations were registered to the CTP images, 19 registrations were inspected visually, and corrections were made where necessary. Then all images included in the final dataset were inspected by authors F.W., M.V., and A.B. Further details on data processing are available in the Supplementary Materials.

Data Stratification

All subjects remaining in the study after quality checks were divided into training (60%), validation (20%), and testing (20%). Further details of data stratification can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Deep Learning Models

The DL models were developed and tested in the Project MONAI (Medical Open Network for Artificial Intelligence) framework. 20 Two out‐of‐the‐box DL architectures (U‐Net 21 and Attention U‐Net 22 ) were tested, along with 1 in‐house model, the DenseNet (https://github.com/FredaWerdiger/DenseNetFCN3D‐pytorch, after Jégou et al. 23 ). All networks were designed to process whole images, performing 3‐dimensional ``convolutions’’ to extract relevant information during the training process.

The CTP input data (CBF, CBV, mean transit time, DT, and MBL image volumes) were combined (or concatenated) into a 4‐dimensional multichannel input feature image. The intensity was then normalized between 0 and 1, separately for each channel. All images were resized to 128×128×128 voxels (however, performance was also tested with images sized to 256×256×256 voxels).

To determine which combination of features provided the best predictive value, a hierarchical feature selection process was used: first, the model was trained on each of the features, alone, and the strongest performing feature was selected; second, the model was trained on the winning feature in combination each of the remaining 4 features, to select a second feature that most improved performance in combination with the first; and so on until the model no longer improved with the addition of new features.

The resulting concatenated feature image was then used to train the DL models to predict the registered follow‐up DWI infarct. The DL model returned a probability of infarct. From this, we created probability band maps: 50% probability and higher, 70% probability and higher, and 90% probability and higher. Further details on implementation and probability band maps can be found in the Supplementary Material.

Performance Metrics and Statistical Analysis

The test cohort was used to derive performance metrics; mean values were derived across the entire testing cohort. Predicted infarct was compared to the ground truth DWI infarct to derive positive predictive value, negative predictive value, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver‐operating curve (AUC) for each image, using each voxel as an instance. Due to the large imbalance between infarct and noninfarct voxels, only background voxels in the affected brain hemisphere were considered. The diverse counterfactual explanations (Dice) metric was also used to compare model performance and determine the highest performing model. 24 The Dice score (or Dice similarity coefficient) measures the overlap between 2 regions based on both location and shape, returning a value between 0 and 1, with 0 representing no overlap and 1 perfect overlap. For the DL model, performance metrics were calculated for the 50%, 70%, and 90% probability bands. These values were compared to the same metrics for ischemic core regions derived through single‐value thresholding (CBF<30%).

Paired t‐tests were performed to compare DL and single‐value threshold results, with P<0.05 denoting a significant difference between groups. The correlation and agreement of predicted volumes compared to the follow‐up DWI lesion volumes were assessed by Spearman correlation and Bland–Altman analysis. Analysis was performed for the volumes predicted by the 50% probability band of the DL model and single‐value thresholds.

Patient demographics were summarized with median values and interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Results

Study Population

In total, 243 patients were included in the study. Figure 1 shows the selection criteria for the study population.

Selection criteria. CTP indicates CT perfusion; DWI, diffusion weighted imaging; ECR, endovascular clot retrieval; HT, hemorrhagic transformation; INSPIRE, International Stroke Perfusion Imaging Registry; LVO, large vessel occlusion; and TICI, thrombolysis in cerebral infarction.

The median age at onset of the study population was 69 (IQR: 60–77), with 38% being female. The median baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score was 13 (IQR: 8–18), and 95% (n = 232) of subjects had a reported premorbid modified Rankin scale score of 0–2. Six patients (2.5%) had an unknown time of onset. Of those remaining, the median time from either onset (175 subjects) or wake‐up (63 subjects) until CT imaging was acquired was 5.4 hours (IQR: 2.5–10.2). All patients received ECR, with 59 (24%) receiving intravenous thrombolysis before and the median time to ECR was 7.7 hours (IQR: 4.5–13.7). The median time of follow‐up DWI imaging was 3 days (IQR: 1–5, max: 15). The median volume of the follow‐up infarct on DWI was 22.5 mL (IQR: 8.1–59.3, min–max: 0.1–312.4). There were 37 (15%) subjects with lesions under 5 mL and 53 (22%) over 70 mL. The final cohort included 35 (14%) patients with evidence of a prior stroke in a different hemisphere or vascular territory to the index stroke, and 76 (31%) patients with hemorrhagic transformation HI1, HI2, or PH1 according to the Heidelberg bleeding classification (ie, without substantial mass effect attributed to it).

Model Performance

The results of hierarchical feature selection are shown in Table S1. The Attention U‐Net, with the input images of CBF, DT, CBV, and MBL, predicting the ischemic core with a 50% probability, resulted in the highest overall performance (mean Dice = 0.430±0.213, AUC = 0.766±0.095, Table 2, Figure 2), which was significantly higher than the single‐value threshold method comparator (mean Dice = 0.247±0.167; P<0.0001; AUC = 0.604±0.074, P<0.0001). The U‐Net and DenseNet models with the same input images (CBF, DT, CBV, and MBL) showed reduced Dice and AUC scores (U‐Net: Dice = 0.387±0.207, AUC = 0.766±0.111, DenseNet: Dice = 0.402±0.200, AUC = 0.751±0.124). Figure 2 summarizes individual Dice and AUC values for the DL probability bands and the single‐value threshold outputs. The DL model outperformed single‐value thresholding in 45 of 47 test cases (see the Table S3 for detailed results). Of the cases where DL outperformed the threshold model, the 50% probability band was the most accurate in 27 cases, 12 were most accurate in the 70% probability band, and 6 in the 90% probability band.

Results of the volume analysis are shown in Figure 3. There was strong correlation between the predicted and actual DWI infarct core for all models. Spearman correlation coefficient (r) for volumes predicted by the DL 50%, 70%, and 90% probability band were r = 0.692 (P<0.0001, 95% CI: [0.468–0.806]), 0.724 (P<0.0001, 95% CI: [0.545–0.840]), and 0.771 (P<0.0001, 95% CI: [0.617–0.869]), respectively. Figure 3A shows the correlation between the infarct predicted by the 50% probability band map for the DL model (based on CBF, CT, CBV, and MBL) and the actual follow‐up infarct. The threshold‐predicted volume was also highly correlated with the DWI infarct, as shown in 3b: P<0.0001, r = 0.692, 95% CI: [0.499–0.820]. As compared with Figure 3A, B shows a trend toward underestimation of the size of the core for the threshold model. This trend is further demonstrated by the Bland–Altman analysis: Figure 3C,D shows results of Bland–Altman analysis for DL‐predicted volumes (50% probability band) and threshold‐predicted volume, both against follow‐up DWI infarct, respectively. The mean difference of the DL model was 9.24 mL (SD 27.7; 95% CI: [−45 to 63.5]), smaller in magnitude than −13.6 mL for thresholding model (SD 22.8; 95% CI: [−58.4 to 31.1]).

Figure 4 shows images of 8 subjects; corresponding Dice scores are shown in Table 4. DL outperformed single‐value thresholding in 5 cases. In 4 of these (50, 240, 230, and 194) the DWI infarct was, via visual assessment, far larger than CBF<30%, suggesting patients experienced infarct growth despite full recanalization. However, DL predicted the larger core, whereas the threshold‐based estimation did not. Also shown in Figure 4 are 3 challenging cases: the sole case where thresholding outperformed DL (subject 201); a case (subject 127) with a poor thresholding result (Dice = 0.08) but a strong DL result (0.455 in >70% band); and a case (subject 72) where both models returned low Dice scores (thresholding:0.023, DL = 0.066). However, in this case the final infarct could be considered small (9.7 mL) even though it did not fall into the definition used in this paper to define a small lesion (<5 mL).

Discussion

We developed a DL model that employed acute (prethrombectomy) CTP hemodynamic maps and contrast‐free baseline image to predict the final infarct core after successful ECR. Whereas the clinical standard CTP methodology for estimating core is to apply single‐value thresholds to the CBF and DT parametric maps, we combined multiple CTP feature maps, together with the MB structural CT images to predict the probability of infarct for each image voxel: structural imaging was extracted from the same imaging dataset and a second, noncontrast CT acquisition was not required. The DL model consistently outperformed the standard threshold‐based model and, although both groups correlated strongly with the ground truth follow‐up infarct, single‐value thresholding was far more likely to underestimate the size of the lesion. In the cases where subjects appeared to experience infarct growth (as well as can be estimated from acute CTP and follow‐up DWI) despite successful treatment, the DL method was more likely to predict this than the threshold‐based method. Further studies will be undertaken to determine whether DL can predict infarct growth in a suitable cohort.

The Dice score was also used to measure performance on the test set, with 50%, 70%, and 90% probability bands. It is notable that the 50% probability band was the most accurate for the DL model even though the Bland–Altman analysis showed a trend toward overestimation, and the correlation coefficient for predicted and actual core volumes was stronger for the 90% probability band map than the 50% probability band map. This reflects the fact that the Dice score evaluates a prediction based on both volume and location, whereas the correlation and Bland–Altman tests only consider volume. The 90% probability band may result in a more precise volume measurement but suffers a penalty from the Dice as the region is smaller and less likely to overlap with the ground truth location. Slight shifts in lesion location that may result from coregistration of acute and follow‐up imaging may have also contributed to this effect.

A major advantage of this study is the direct comparison to the current clinical CTP standard. It is important to note that the MIStar ischemic core and penumbra thresholds have been arguably the most well validated of all deconvolution software, and are very useful in clinical practice. 9 , 10 , 17 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 However, our study shows the limitations of restricting core and penumbra estimation to only 2 thresholds (regardless of deconvolution software used). In addition, we have provided a novel method of expressing the CTP imaging output. Rather than adopting the dichotomy of core and penumbra, we used probability band mapping, expressing the likelihood of infarction based on the given data. Our probability band map delineates regions with 50%, 70%, and 90% or higher probability of infarct after successful treatment. We suggest supplementing the current standard core/penumbra maps with our more intuitive and accurate DL model. Whereas the absolute nature of a ``hard’’ threshold leaves little room for interpretation, this new model enables clinicians to apply some interpretation based on circumstances. Indeed, there is significant variation across individual patients and images, some of which—acquisition time, stroke location—directly affect evolution of ischemia. These variations may cause the DL probability model to be more or less confident in predicting infarct, which is reflected in the distribution across probability bands and exemplified by Figure 4, especially with subject 72. Our new probability model leaves more room for clinicians to interpret CTP data in the context of these circumstances.

Prior studies have found that combining CTP maps in a probability model may enable more accurate determination of the ischemic core than a single‐value threshold. 29 , 30 Peerlings et al 29 have done so with a logistic regression model to calculate the probability of infarction. Here, we have used a DL model that surpasses logistic regression in its ability to model complex nonlinear interaction between features, resulting in a more accurate prediction than with a single‐value threshold. In addition, a DL model can improve with the addition of new data points. It is notable that although the DenseNet outperformed the U‐Net when more CTP data were available (CBF, DT, CBV, MBL), it did less well based upon CBF alone (see the Supplementary Section on Additional Results). We have noted in our previous work 18 that the DenseNet shows marked improvement when given more data, as compared with the U‐Net, which improved to a lesser degree.

Other studies have been carried out on this topic, many under the banner of the ISLES2018 challenge. 31 These data contain 94 labeled 2‐dimensional training images (from 63 patients) and 62 unlabeled 2‐dimensional testing images (from 40 patients). Our results are comparable to these studies; however, the dataset used in our study is much larger (243 versus 103 subjects) and has images acquired at more sites (16 versus 4). For ISLES2018 (Ischemic Stroke Lesion Segmentation 2018), both raw (native) CTP data, noncontrast CT, and processed CTP maps were supplied for predicting final infarct. Anand et al 32 used only CTP maps as input and achieved a Dice score of 0.43 (SD 0.31) on the test set. Adding on the noncontrast CT scan (analogous to our MBL), Clèrigues et al 33 achieved a score of 0.49 (SD 0.31). Liu 34 concatenated 4 CTP maps together with the maximum value for the native 4‐dimensional (3‐dimensional + time) CTP data—the CT volume with peak contrast along the time dimension—as input and achieved a mean Dice of 0.49 (SD 31). These lower Dice scores (compared with challenge such as BraTS2020 where the top ranking result was 0.89 35 ) are an indication of the difficult nature of predicting the outcome of ischemic stroke therapy.

This study has some limitations, the foremost being the size of the dataset. Although the INSPIRE dataset is considered quite large by conventional standards, and the dataset presented here reflects the variability seen in real‐world medical imaging acquisition, given the vast range and variety in stroke presentation, more cases are needed to train the DL model to consistently make accurate predictions. A larger dataset would enable us to train more complex and deeper models, and to include more data for each subject (such as clinical information). We continue to enrich ATLAS with more INSPIRE cases, and follow‐up studies will reflect this. Furthermore, this study uses postprocessed CTP maps. Studies have shown that using predeconvolution native CTP data may improve prediction of follow‐up infarct. 36 , 37 , 38 This may be due to the preservation of valuable data, which is compromised after deconvolution. Future studies will extract features from native CTP data and perhaps circumvent deconvolution. Finally, there is variability in the timing of the follow‐up DWI acquisitions, spanning the acute and subacute stages, as we aimed to retain as many subjects as possible. When using acute or subacute DWI infarct as a surrogate for final infarct, the presence of hemorrhagic transformation or formation of vasogenic edema may affect the infarct demarcation. Although we relied on expert assessment to ensure faithful ground truth maps, further studies are required to understand how the length of the follow‐up period affects the interpretation of results.

Conclusion

We have presented a DL model that uses multiple inputs from acute stroke CTP data to make a prediction of follow‐up infarct after successful endovascular therapy. The model returns the probability of infarct in the form of probability band maps. When compared with the follow‐up infarct manually segmented from DWI images, the model achieved a mean AUC score of 0.766 and a mean Dice score of 0.430 across the test set of 47 images. This outperformed the current clinical standard, which is based on a single‐value threshold. With further development, the model presented here may supplement the use of CTP imaging in the current standard of acute stroke care, providing physicians with additional information useful in the process of decision‐making.
