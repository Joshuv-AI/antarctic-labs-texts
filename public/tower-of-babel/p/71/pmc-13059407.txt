Can the addition of “Black Bone” sequence improve diagnosis of skull fractures after traumatic brain injury in children?
Authors: Junwei Li, Ke Jin, Xiaoming Li, Xiamei Zhuang, Yan Yin, Huiting Zhang, Hong Liu, Meitao Liu, Haolin Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate whether three-dimensional (3D) Black Bone imaging enhances diagnostic accuracy in detecting skull fractures in children with traumatic brain injury (TBI), using computed tomography (CT) as the reference standard.MaterialsData were collected between January 2022 and August 2024 from 50 pediatric TBI patients (27 boys, 23 girls; mean age: 4.76 ± 3.39 years) who underwent CT (gold standard) and conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with or without the Black Bone sequence, for fracture diagnosis. Patients were categorized by age (< 3 years, ≧3 years) and fracture type (linear, depressed, sutural disconnection, comminuted). The presence or absence of skull fracture was assessed using conventional MRI with and without Black Bone images. Conen’s kappa, McNemar’s test, area under curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, negative predictive values (NPV) and positive predictive values (NPV) were employed to compare inter-reader agreement and fracture detection performance between image types.ResultsFor all cases of skull fracture, conventional MRI with Black Bone images demonstrated an AUC of 0.947 (95% CI: 0.937–0.990) compared to 0.711 (95% CI: 0.650–0.766) for conventional MRI without Black Bone. In addition, conventional MRI with Black Bone images achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 76.0%, sensitivity of 94.7%, NPV of 85.7%, and agreement (ĸ) of 0.896, all higher than those obtained by conventional MRI without Black Bone. However, both imaging modalities showed lower accuracy, sensitivity, and agreement in detecting fractures among patients aged 3 years, as well as in cases of linear and sutural disconnection fractures, compared to patients aged 3 years and cases of depressed and comminuted fractures.ConclusionsBlack Bone imaging could improve MRI detection of pediatric skull fractures in TBI, especially in children aged 3 years old.Keywords: Volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination, Skull fracture, Magnetic resonance imaging, Three-dimensional, Radiation, Pediatric

Background

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a common cause of pediatric emergency visits. Non-contrast head computed tomography (CT) is typically the first imaging technique for TBI due to its broad availability, high sensitivity for fractures and acute hemorrhages, and rapid acquisition time. However, CT imaging has some limitations. The sensitivity of CT is lower for detecting microhemorrhages, acute ischemia, and axonal injury compared to that of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [1]. Additionally, CT exposes patients to ionizing radiation, increasing the risk of radiation-induced cancer, particularly in children [2, 3]. Consequently, MRI is recommended as an alternative neuroimaging technique in certain cases, especially when CT findings do not explain patients’ neuroimaging symptoms [4]. Despite its advantages, MRI has limited capability in evaluating cortical bone. Bone tissues with ultrashort T2 relaxation time ( 1 ms) are not reliably captured by conventional MRI sequences due to echo time (TE) constraints [5]. Conventional MRI demonstrates low sensitivity in detecting skull fractures [6], which are prevalent in children with TBI.

“Black Bone” imaging is an MRI technique designed to evaluate structural bony abnormalities [7, 8]. This technique utilizes a three-dimensional (3D) volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination (VIBE), which is a T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) gradient echo (GRE) sequence with short TE and repetition time (TR). However, its application in diagnosing fractures in children remains underreported, and the sensitivity and specificity of Black Bone imaging for fracture detection still debated [9–12]. The aim of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of “Black Bone” MRI in detecting fractures in children with TBI.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Board (NO. 2021 − 304). Pediatric children clinical diagnosed with TBI were identified. General anesthesia was administered if necessary. Patients were excluded if they had progressive neurological deterioration, contraindications for CT and/or MRI, or severe motion artifacts in imaging studies. Between January 2022 and August 2024, 50 pediatric TBI patients underwent head CT, 3D Black Bone MRI, and conventional MRI within 8 h to 3 days following trauma.

CT protocol

CT scans were acquired using a 256-row Revolution CT scanner (GE Healthcare, USA) with the following parameters: tube voltage, 120 kV; tube current, 200 mA/s; pitch, 0.992:1; detector width, 80 mm; and speed, 158.75 mm/s. Transverse, sagittal and coronal images were reconstructed using the multi-plane reconstruction tool with a slice thickness of 1.0 mm, aligned with the orientation of the MRI protocol described above.

MRI protocol

All MRI examinations were conducted using 3 T MRI scanners (MAGNETOM Skyra; MAGNETOM Prisma; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with 16-channel head coils. The institutional pediatric head trauma conventional MRI protocol included T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) sequence (TE/TR: 9ms/1800ms; flip angle, 150; slice thickness, 5.0 mm; scan time, 1 min 16s), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) sequence (TE/TR: 109ms/2200ms; flip angle, 150; slice thickness, 5.0 mm; scan time, 57s), T2-fluid attenuation inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR) sequence (TE/TR: 90ms/9000ms; flip angle, 150; slice thickness, 5.0 mm; scan time, 1 min 48s), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (TE/TR: 3800ms/79ms; flip angle, 180; b-value 1, 0; b-value 2, 1000; slice thickness, 5.0 mm; scan time, 1 min 43s), and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) (TE/TR: 27ms/70ms; flip angle, 15; slice thickness, 2.0 mm; scan time, 1 min 11s). The Black Bone sequence utilized sagittal acquisition based on the VIBE protocol (TE/TR: 2.46ms/5.8ms; flip angle, 4; slice thickness, 0.4 mm; scan time, 3 min 58s). Black Bone images underwent 3D reconstruction and gray level inversion on a Siemens post-processing workstation. Subsequently, multiplanar images in the coronal and axial planes with 1 mm slice thickness were generated.

Image analysis

CT and MR images were evaluated for presence or absence of skull fractures. For detected fractures, the type (linear, sutural disconnection, depressed or comminuted) and location were recorded. CT was used as the gold standard. Two independent readers, (M.T.L., a pediatric radiologist with five years of experience; Y.Y., a pediatric radiologist with 12 years of experience) evaluated the CT images without blinding to trauma status. All MRI sequences were anonymized and randomized. Another two experienced pediatric radiologists (X.M.L. and J.W.L.), with 35 and 13 years of experience respectively, independently assessed skull fractures using two sets of MRI images (conventional without and with Black Bone images). The conventional set of images, including T1WI, T2WI, T2-FLAIR, DWI and SWI, were reviewed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 23.0 (International Business Machines Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA).

Skull fractures were recorded as binary variables (presence or absence). Pediatric patients were categorized by age (< 3 years, ≧3 years) and fracture type (linear, depressed, sutural disconnection, or comminuted). For consensus interpretation of both sets of images, diagnostic performance parameters, including area under curve (AUC), specificity, sensitivity, accuracy, negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV), were calculated using CT as the reference standard. To compare the AUC values between the different groups, we employed DeLong’s test for paired ROC curves. Patients with one or more missed fractures were classified as having missed diagnoses.

McNemar’s test was used to compare the accuracy of CT and MRI data in detecting skull fracture. Conen’s kappa test was employed to evaluate interrater reliability between two MRI sets and the CT for positive findings. Cohen’s kappa test was also applied to assess inter-reader agreement, with the level of agreement categorized as follows: <0.20 (slight), 0.21–0.40 (fair), 0.41–0.60 (moderate), 0.61–0.80 (substantial), and > 0.81 (almost perfect) [13]. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

Fifty children (27 boys and 23 girls) with TBI met the inclusion criteria for this study. The mean age at the first neuroimaging study was 4.76 ± 3.39 years (range: 7 months to 11 years and 9 months). Sedation was necessary for MRI examinations in eight children, who were administered oral chloral hydrate at a dose of 10 mg/kg, 30 min before scanning. The average time interval between head CT and MRI was 1.2 days.

Imaging analysis

Using the established reference criteria for diagnosing skull fracture, it was confirmed that 38 out of 50 patients sustained skull fractures, yielding an incidence rate of 76%. Among these 38 patients, 13 (34.2%) experienced fractures involving multiple skull bones, such as parietal and temporal bones or parietal and frontal bones. Notably, the parietal bone was the most commonly fractured, accounting for 50% of cases. Additionally, multiple fracture types were observed in individual patients. A total of 55 skull fractures were identified and categorized by type and location (Table 1).

Discussion

MRI, a nonionizing imaging technique, has demonstrated superiority over CT in contrast resolution, sensitivity, and specificity for detecting parenchymal lesions [14, 15]. However, traditional MRI exhibits low sensitivity for skull fractures. Skull fractures, predominantly linear, are common in children with TBI [16]. While isolated linear, nondisplaced, and sutural disconnection fractures may have limited clinical significance, precise diagnosis of skull fractures in pediatric TBI remains essential for medicolegal purposes. Consequently, improved diagnostic performance in fracture detection is necessary.

The Black Bone sequence, first reported in 2012, minimizes soft tissue contrast and bone signal, enabling segmentation of bone from surrounding soft tissues [17, 18]. Previous studies demonstrated that Black Bone imaging utilizing GRE parameters, or ultrashort echo time, or zero echo time parameters optimized to enhance the bone-soft tissue boundary [9, 19]. In this study, the VIBE sequence was modified with a low flip angle to further reduce TE/TR while maintaining bone contrast. In addition, fat signal suppression was employed to optimize the visualization of the bone-soft tissue margin, and gray level inversion was applied to transform hypointense skull regions into hyperintense areas in the 3D Black Bone images, creating CT-like visualizations to improve the assessment of skull fractures. Besides, maximum intensity projection was used to reconstruct 3D Black Bone images, providing axial, sagittal, and coronal views essential for clinical skull fracture diagnosis.

Our study demonstrated that skull fractures in children with TBI can be visualized using the Black Bone imaging utilizing a VIBE sequence. Morphologically, skull fractures are classified as linear, depressed, sutural disconnection, and comminuted. In this study, most fractures were linear (68.42%), consistent with previous research [20]. Additionally, sutural disconnection fractures were included for the first time, accounting for 50.00% of cases. The 3-year age cutoff was selected for its clinical significance, as it reflects cranial suture development, distinct fracture patterns, and age-specific injury mechanisms. This threshold aligns with pediatric trauma classifications and improves clinical applicability. Conventional MRI with Black Bone showed high diagnostic accuracy and agreement in detecting skull fractures across various groups. Meanwhile, lower accuracy, sensitivity, and agreement were observed for Black Bone imaging in children younger than three years compared to older children. Similarly, lower diagnostic performance was noted for linear and sutural disconnection fractures compared to depressed and comminuted fractures. This difference is likely due to the wider and more flexible cranial sutural in younger children, which complicates the distinction between normal anatomical structures and fractures, particularly linear fractures without displacement [21]. Consequently, even with the addition of Black Bone images, standard MRI imaging techniques may not clearly identify fractures in younger children. In our study, two false-negative findings occurred, and both cases involved children younger than three years. One case was a linear fracture in the occipital bone, misinterpreted as a transosseous vascular channel due to its small size and lack of separation or dislocation (Fig. 5). The other case was a sutural disconnection fracture, misinterpreted as an incomplete cranial sutural fusion (Fig. 4). These two missed fractures do not necessarily require neurosurgical intervention as part of head trauma management [22].

No false-positive cases were observed in either image set, possibly because Black Bone imaging can depict unfused sutures and vascular channels, reducing diagnostic uncertainty. Radiologists also tend to exercise caution when diagnosing skull fractures with MRI in clinical practice. In children, unfused sutural and vascular channels can mimic skull fractures in acute settings, as noted in previous head CT studies [18].

Fractures at air-bone interfaces, such as the mastoid region of the temporal bone and the paranasal sinuses, present significant diagnostic challenges due to minimal signal, as reported in prior studies [11, 12, 23, 24]. In this study, two mastoid fractures were clearly identified on Black Bone images without misinterpretation (Fig. 1), likely due to the shorter TE, which minimizes signal loss caused by T2 decay and magnetic field heterogeneity. Additionally, postoperative CT and MRI were performed in a case of depressed fracture. While fractures in the operative area were obscured by metal artifacts on traditional MRI sequences, Black Bone images successfully demonstrated these fractures (Fig. 2). The reason might be that VIBE sequence in Black Bone imaging can achieve the shortest TE, resulting to reduce signal loss caused by magnetic field heterogeneity around metal.

We acknowledge the limitations of our study. First, the small sample size limits the generalizability our findings, and large sample studies will be performed in the future. Second, brain MRI data were obtained based on clinical indications, specifically in cases where head CT revealed abnormalities or patients exhibited unexplained neurological symptoms. This selective imaging approach may introduce selection bias by focusing on patients with pronounced clinical findings, potentially underrepresenting the full spectrum of pediatric TBI cases. Nonetheless, our findings highlight the potential utility of Black Bone imaging, warranting further investigation in prospective studies. Third, although Black Bone imaging demonstrated diagnostic accuracy for skull fracture diagnosis, some fractures, such as isolated linear, non-displaced, and sutural disconnection fracture, were still missed. While these fractures often have low clinical significance, accurate diagnosis is crucial for medicolegal purposes and may necessitate the development of more optimized detection techniques. Finally, MRI in young or uncooperative children frequently necessitates sedation to ensure high-quality imaging, which poses risks such as respiratory, neurological, and cardiovascular complications [25]. Future efforts will focus on improve the Black Bone imaging using VIBE sequence combined with compressed sensing or deep learning techniques to reduce scanning time, potentially minimizing the sedation requirements.

Conclusions

In pediatric TBI patients, the integration of Black Bone imaging, utilizing a 3D VIBE sequence with gray level inversion, demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared to conventional MRI alone, significantly increasing sensitivity, accuracy, and agreement for skull fracture detection, particularly in children aged 3 years. Additionally, Black Bone imaging addressed challenges such as metal artifacts and signal loss at the air-bone interface, which often obscure fracture visualization. While these findings indicate that conventional MRI with Black Bone imaging improved the detection sensitivity of specific fractures subtypes (e.g., depressed and comminuted fractures), its potential as a standalone alternative to CT requires validation through a lot of prospective studies.
