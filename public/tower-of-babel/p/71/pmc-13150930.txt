Three-Dimensional Ultrafast Ultrasound Reveals Dynamic Vascular Alterations During Intraocular Melanoma Progression in Mice
Authors: Yu Xia, Tengbo Rao, Renxin Luo, Jinyu Yang, Changguan Wang, Xuemin Li, Jiarui Yang, Jiabin Zhang, Heping Cheng, Jue Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to investigate the feasibility of three-dimensional contrast-free ultrafast Doppler ultrasound for longitudinal assessment of vascular remodeling in an experimental intraocular melanoma model, and to characterize dynamic microvascular alterations during tumor progression.MethodsIntraocular melanoma was induced in a mouse model and monitored over 12 days using three-dimensional ultrafast Doppler imaging. Ocular as well as tumor vasculature were segmented from serial volumetric datasets, and multiparametric analysis of vascular volume occupied (VVO), fractional moving blood volume (FMBV), average blood velocity (ABV), and blood velocity variance (BVV) was performed. Wide-field optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) was also performed at each time point.ResultsUltrafast Doppler imaging enabled consistent visualization of intraocular tumor vasculature and surrounding blood flow. In ocular structures, VVO and FMBV in both the retina and retrobulbar regions progressively increased during early to mid-stage uveal melanoma (UM), whereas ABV and BVV showed a transient rise in the retina but not in retrobulbar regions. Within the tumor, VVO and FMBV increased significantly during early to mid-stage, but declined in advanced-stage (day 10 vs. day 3, P < 0.01). ABV peaked at day 7 before decreasing, whereas BVV showed a continuous rise, with maximum values at day 7 (P < 0.01 vs. day 3). These findings indicate spatiotemporal heterogeneity of vascular remodeling during intraocular melanoma progression.ConclusionsThree-dimensional ultrafast Doppler imaging provides a noninvasive and quantitative approach to monitor dynamic vascular changes in intraocular melanoma. This method reveals distinct temporal patterns of angiogenesis and perfusion heterogeneity, highlighting its potential value for early diagnosis and longitudinal evaluation of intraocular tumors.

Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most common primary intraocular malignant tumor in adults, characterized by highly aggressive local behavior and a strong tendency to systemic metastasis.1 The disease typically affects individuals between the age of 50 and 70 years, with an incidence ranging from less than 1 to 10 cases per million population per year.2 Despite advances in therapeutic approaches combining radiotherapy, phototherapy, local resection, and enucleation, the prognosis of UM remains poor, with a high rate of disease-related mortality occurring within a relatively short period following initial diagnosis.3 Metastasis is the primary cause of mortality, occurring in approximately 50% of patients. Once metastases are present, particularly to the liver, the median survival time is usually less than 1 year.4,5 Current management strategies highlight the need for improved screening and diagnostic modalities that can detect UM at an earlier, more treatable stage.

The standard diagnostic modality for UM relies on a combination of comprehensive ophthalmological examination and imaging techniques, of which ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are most frequently applied in clinical practice.6 On B-scan ultrasonography, UM typically appears as a collar-button–shaped mass with acoustic hollowness, whereas an MRI often reveals hyperintensity on T1-weighted images and hypointensity on T2-weighted images, which are considered significant features for UM diagnosis and differential diagnosis.7,8 Recent advances in optical imaging technologies, such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCTA), have further enabled the monitoring of tumor growth and retinal involvement in clinical and experimental settings.9,10 However, these imaging techniques only provide structural information, with limited ability to record tumor vascularization or hemodynamic parameters. Conventional color Doppler imaging (CDI) based on focused ultrasound beams provides noninvasive quantification of blood flow velocity and has been used to assess tumor perfusion and monitor treatment response in UM since the last century.11,12 Nevertheless, traditional CDI lacks sufficient spatial resolution and sensitivity to visualize microvascular structures and fails to offer comprehensive insights into tumor angiogenesis. Therefore, there is a critical need for novel imaging techniques capable of capturing vascular hemodynamics throughout UM progression with high spatiotemporal resolution.

Advances in ultrasound technology have enabled ultrafast plane-wave imaging, dramatically increasing frame rates and sensitivity.13 Ultrafast Doppler imaging (uDI) allows high-resolution microvascular flow visualization and has been applied in various fields,14–18 including ocular imaging.19 With ultrasound contrast agent injection, ultrasound localization microscopy (ULM) can achieve super-resolution vascular mapping, visualizing retinal and choroidal microvasculature.20 However, prior uDI studies of the eye were mostly limited to 2D, restricting comprehensive 3D mapping of the ocular vascular system. Contrast-enhanced ULM require prolonged agent infusion and complex processing, making them impractical for longitudinal use. By contrast, contrast-free uDI eliminates exogenous contrast, making it better suited for dynamic 3D monitoring of the entire eye. To our knowledge, 3D uDI has not yet been validated for evaluating microvascular hemodynamic changes in intraocular melanoma.

This study proposes that angiogenesis and vascular remodeling may serve as hallmarks of intraocular melanoma progression, and hemodynamic parameters of both the tumor and surrounding ocular tissues provide valuable indicators for monitoring disease dynamics. To address this hypothesis, we developed and validated a contrast-free 3D ocular uDI system capable of visualizing vascular structures encompassing the entire eye and retrobulbar vasculature. We applied this technique for longitudinal monitoring over 2 weeks in an intraocular melanoma mouse model. Throughout pathological progression, quantitative assessments of ocular and tumor-associated vascular architecture and hemodynamics were performed. The superior performance of 3D uDI was further validated against concurrently acquired OCTA images. Additionally, histopathological examination of terminal tumors confirmed intraocular melanoma development. Collectively, our study establishes a noninvasive imaging platform for long-term assessment of intraocular melanoma progression, potentially offering novel insights into tumor vascular biology and identifying new therapeutic targets.

Methods

Cell Line

B16-F10 mouse melanoma cells (Meisen CTCC, Zhejiang, China) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle's medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in 5% CO. For the animal studies, cells were detached from the dishes, counted, washed once, and then re-suspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at the desired concentration.

Animals

Five male 12-week-old C57BL/6 mice (Charles River, Beijing, China) were used under standard housing conditions. All animal procedures were approved by the Peking University Animal Ethics Committee (approval: AAIS-ZhangJ-9) and adhered to the ARVO guidelines for the use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Intraocular Melanoma Model

A mouse intraocular melanoma model was established by subchoroidal implantation of cutaneous melanoma cells, as previously described10 with minor modifications. Under anesthesia, a small conjunctival peritomy was made in the temporal region of the left eye. A 30-gauge needle was inserted approximately 1.5 mm posterior to the limbus, and 1 × 105 B16-F10 cells in 1 µL PBS were injected into the subretinal space. Mice underwent ultrafast ultrasound and OCTA imaging before tumor implantation and on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 12 post-injection. After the final imaging on day 12, the mice were euthanized for histological confirmation of the tumors. The experimental workflow is illustrated in Figure 1a.

(a) Timeline of the intraocular melanoma mouse model: subretinal injection of melanoma cells on day 0 followed by serial imaging (3D ultrafast Doppler imaging [uDI] and OCTA) on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 12, concluding with histological analysis. (b) Schematic of the 3D uDI acquisition and processing pipeline: motorized multi-slice scanning for uPDI and uCDI, three-dimensional reconstruction, and computation of vascular flow indices (VVO, FMBV, ABV, and BVV).

Common Pre-Imaging Protocol

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% for induction, and then 1.5%–2% via nasal mask for maintenance) and placed supine on a heated platform to maintain body temperature. Periocular fur was removed, and the head was positioned to keep the cornea horizontal. The mouse was secured with surgical tape (Micropore, 3M, St. Paul, MN, USA) to minimize movement. Transverse 3D ultrasound scans of the left eye were then performed from superior to inferior, with probe position adjustments to cover the entire eye and tumor.

3D Ultrafast Ultrasound Data Acquisition

Ultrasound imaging was performed using a Verasonics Vantage 256 system with a 28 MHz linear array transducer (L35-16vX). For 3D data acquisition, the transducer was mounted on a motorized linear stage and translated across the eye. At each slice position, 1000 frames were acquired using plane-wave transmissions with 7-angle compounding (0° and ±2°, ±4°, and ±6°) at a pulse repetition frequency of 3500 hertz (Hz). The transducer was stepped 0.2 mm between slices to scan the entire eye volume.

Ultrasound Image Reconstruction and Processing

Acquired radio frequency (RF) data were IQ-demodulated and beamformed (delay-and-sum). Respiratory motion artifacts were removed by discarding frames identified via B-mode cross-correlation analysis.17 Blood flow signals were then isolated using a spatiotemporal singular value decomposition (SVD) filter (removing the first 10% of singular values). Power Doppler (PD) and color Doppler (CD) images were generated for each slice. Vascular regions were segmented by thresholding the PD images; the resulting mask was applied to CD images to suppress noise. Finally, multi-slice images were reconstructed into 3D volumes (ImageJ 3D Viewer) for visualization.

Multiparametric 3D uDI Analysis

For quantitative analysis, the eye was segmented into distinct regions (retina/choroid, retrobulbar, and tumor) based on B-mode and PD images. Within each region, we calculated vessel volume occupancy (VVO) and fractional moving blood volume (FMBV) from PD data, and average blood velocity (ABV) and blood velocity variance (BVV) from CD data. Detailed imaging and quantitative analysis process are shown in Figure 1b.

According to the definition in previous work, VVO reflects vascular density within a region.21 PD images underwent adaptive threshold binarization processing to generate vascular masks. VVO was then calculated as the ratio of the vascular mask volume VVessel to the region of interest (ROI) volume VROI: VVO=VVesselVROI

According to Rubin et al.’s original definition, FMBV represents the relative blood perfusion level normalized by high blood signals in large vessels. The intensity of the PD image was linearly scaled to obtain the relative PD (rPD). FMBV was then calculated as the ratio of the summed rPD intensity within the ROI to the VROI: FMBV=∑rPDVROI

The binary vascular mask obtained based on the PD image was applied to the CD image to calculate ABV and BVV. ABV was calculated as the ratio of the summed absolute values of the velocity in the vascular mask to VVessel: ABV=∑VelVesselVVessel

Similarly, BVV was calculated as the variance of the absolute values of flow velocities in the vascular mask: BVV=∑VelVessel-ABV2VVessel

OCTA Imaging

Four hours after each ultrasound imaging, the mice were anesthetized as described above and the pupils were dilated with 1% Tropicamide. Then, the mice were manually positioned in front of the lens of full-range swept-source OCT (SS-OCT; TowardPi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Tumor growth and structural damages of the retina and choroid were assessed in OCTA using both B-scan and en face mode.

Image Quality Assessment and Grading

For OCTA imaging, baseline image quality was evaluated using the device's built-in quality scoring system (maximum score = 10). En face OCTA images with a quality score >8 were considered acceptable for analysis. In addition, all OCTA datasets underwent independent manual review to verify correct retinal layer segmentation; datasets with segmentation errors were manually corrected prior to analysis. Following intraocular melanoma implantation, the built-in OCTA quality metric was no longer applicable due to progressive degradation of optical transparency caused by tumor pigmentation, retinal elevation, and shadowing. In this context, OCTA was primarily used for structural assessment on B-scan images, consistent with previous reports,10 and only B-scans deemed sufficiently clear on manual inspection were retained.

For ultrafast Doppler ultrasound imaging, image quality was ensured using an explicit, quantitative motion-screening criterion.17 Respiratory motion was identified at the frame level based on inter-frame similarity analysis of beamformed B-mode sequences, and motion-contaminated frames were excluded prior to Doppler processing. At the slice level, datasets were excluded if more than 50% of the frames were rejected due to motion.

Histology

Mice were euthanized and their eyes were enucleated, rinsed in PBS, and kept in 4% formalin buffer at 4°C. Tissue samples were placed in histology cassettes, using the LEICA ASP200S for the embedding process. This included dehydration by passing through increasing percentages of ethanol (from 70% to 100%) and finally processing by xylene until adding paraffin. Paraffin sections with a thickness of 3 to 5 µm were prepared with a Leica RM2135 microtome and sections were loaded on Citotest adhesion microscope slides for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.3

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the tidyplots package. Data are presented as median values with interquartile range (IQR). For comparisons across multiple time points within the same animals (n = 5), the Friedman test was applied. When a significant overall effect was observed, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Dynamic In Vivo Visualization of Intraocular Melanoma Progression

Figure 2 compares ultrafast power Doppler imaging (uPDI) with OCTA en face and b-scan results at different stages of an intraocular melanoma model. OCTA en face imaging clearly visualized the retinal vasculature before tumor induction, but the signal deteriorated by days 1 to 3 post-induction (due to inflammation) and was nearly absent by days 5 to 12 (due to tumor pigmentation and structural changes obscuring flow). OCT b-scan images showed a subtle subretinal elevation with hyper-reflective deposits by day 1 (arrow) and retinal thickening/infiltration by days 3 to 7 (asterisk), but provided limited structural information; by days 10 to 12, media opacity from the tumor largely obscured the retinal layers. Similar OCTA signal degradation after tumor induction was consistently observed in an independent mouse (Supplementary Fig. S1), supporting that this pattern is reproducible rather than incidental. In contrast, uPDI maximum-intensity projections consistently detected intraocular blood flow throughout all stages. Early after tumor implantation (days 3–5), uPDI revealed focal microvascular sprouting, and at later stages (days 7–12) it showed the tumor extending beyond the sclera with abnormal vasculature. Unlike OCT/OCTA, the ultrasound-based imaging was not hindered by intraocular pigmentation or opacity, enabling continuous visualization of tumor-associated vessels.

Three-Dimensional Reconstruction of Ocular and Intraocular Melanoma Vasculature

Figures 3 and 4 illustrate tumor growth and invasion of ocular tissues in the intraocular melanoma model as assessed by 3D uDI. Tumor regions were segmented from 3D ultrasound data to calculate volume, allowing classification into no tumor (before and day 1 after cell injection), early to mid-stage of tumor development (days 3–7), and advanced-stage of tumor development (days 10–12; see Fig. 3). During the no tumor period, no tumor-related flow signals were detected and tumor volume remained zero. In the early to mid-stage of tumor development, localized choroidal masses emerged and gradually enlarged, with steadily increasing volume. In the advanced-stage of tumor development, tumor volume markedly increased and breached the ocular wall. Quantitative analysis showed that advanced-stage tumor volume was one to two orders of magnitude greater than in the early–mid stage, reflecting the aggressive proliferative characteristics of B16F10 melanoma cells.

Tumor volume (mm³) measured longitudinally in the intraocular melanoma mouse model, demonstrating progressive enlargement over time and a marked increase during the advanced stage when the tumor breached the sclera (n = 5).

Representative 3D ultrasound Doppler renderings illustrating the temporal evolution of tumor morphology and vascularization at different stages. All images were obtained from the same mouse. Representative volumetric uPDI and uCDI images at different stages (see Fig. 4, Supplementary Movies S1–S7) further illustrate these changes. At day 3, a small confined lesion appeared at the injection site, limited to the choroid without significant intraocular disruption. At days 5 and 7, the tumor rapidly expanded into a dome-shaped mass with evident neovascularization, elevating and even detaching the overlying retina, while simultaneously extending outward to invade the sclera. By days 10 and 12, the tumor reached its maximum size and protruded in a mushroom-like shape beyond the globe contour, indicating that the tumor has penetrated the wall of the eyeball and grown outside the eye. At this stage, the vascular architecture appeared irregular and sparse, a hallmark of malignant tumor progression.

Changes in Hemodynamics of Fundus and Retrobulbar Vessels

Figure 5 and Supplementary Tables S1 to S3 summarize the cohort-level (n = 5) temporal evolution of multiparametric hemodynamic indices across different ocular vascular regions during intraocular melanoma progression. Based on 3D uPDI, ocular vasculature was segmented into the iris, retina, and retrobulbar compartments (see Fig. 5a). We focused on the retinal and retrobulbar regions most affected by intraocular melanoma. Because intraocular melanoma progression in the advanced stage occupied the entire globe and disrupted native vascular architecture, analysis was terminated on day 7. The time courses of hemodynamic parameters are shown in Figure 5b. In the retina, VVO increased significantly from preoperative 0.535 (0.017) to day 3 (0.621 [0.047], P = 0.044), day 5 (0.648 [0.068], P = 0.009), and day 7 (0.639 [0.043], P = 0.026). FMBV also rose steadily, from preoperative 0.356 (0.081) to day 5 (0.505 [0.055], P = 0.008) and day 7 (0.578 [0.125], P = 0.006). ABV transiently increased at day 3 (3.508 [0.345] vs. pre 2.971 [0.241], P = 0.018) before returning near baseline at day 7 (2.855 [0.199], P = 0.900). BVV exhibited a marked increase at day 3 (4.092 [0.205] vs. pre 2.537 [0.152], P < 0.0001), followed by a partial decrease on day 7 (2.865 [0.619], P = 0.084). In the retrobulbar region, VVO declined slightly at day 1 (0.311 [0.042] vs. pre 0.337 [0.029], P = 0.141) but subsequently increased, reaching significance by day 7 (0.469 [0.009], P = 0.001). FMBV also decreased on day 1 (0.281 [0.043] vs. pre 0.387 [0.078], P = 0.030) but rebounded to higher levels at day 7 (0.576 [0.067], P = 0.012). In contrast, ABV and BVV remained relatively stable across time points without significant differences. When retina and retrobulbar regions were analyzed together, the trends of hemodynamic changes were in accordance with the changes in the retina. Taken together, these results demonstrate that blood volume-related indices (VVO and FMBV) in both the retina and retrobulbar regions progressively increased during early to mid-stage intraocular melanoma, whereas flow velocity indices (ABV and BVV) showed a transient rise in the retina but not in the retrobulbar regions, indicating that both local circulations were more perturbed from the early phase of tumor development.

Changes in Hemodynamics of Intraocular Melanoma Vessels

Figure 6 and Supplementary Table S4 summarize the cohort-level temporal evolution of intratumoral microvascular parameters during intraocular melanoma progression. Tumor vasculature was segmented from 3D uPDI datasets acquired between days 3 and 12 (see Fig. 6a), and multiparametric hemodynamic analyses were performed across all animals (n = 5). As shown in Figure 6b, all indices exhibited marked temporal changes, reflecting angiogenesis and vascular remodeling within the tumor. As shown in Figure 6b, VVO increased significantly from day 3 (0.469 [0.033]) to day 7 (0.561 [0.046], P = 0.048), but then declined sharply by day 10 (0.281 [0.090], P = 0.002 vs. day 7) and day 12 (0.267 [0.060], P = 0.009 vs. day 7). FMBV presented a similar trend, rising from day 3 (0.263 [0.017]) to day 7 (0.324 [0.017], P = 0.010), and then falling significantly at day 10 (0.188 [0.016], P = 0.0005 vs. day 7) and day 12 (0.168 [0.044], P = 0.013 vs. day 7). For ABV, values increased from day 3 (2.241 [0.166]) to a peak at day 7 (2.614 [0.177], P = 0.007), before declining at day 10 (2.165 [0.092], P = 0.002 vs. day 7) and day 12 (2.060 [0.211], P = 0.001 vs. day 7). BVV showed the most prominent increase, rising from day 3 (2.521 [0.186]) to day 7 (2.916 [0.105], P = 0.008), before decreasing markedly at day 10 (2.404 [0.289], P = 0.002 vs. day 7) and day 12 (2.284 [0.104], P = 0.0002 vs. day 7). In summary, during tumor growth, blood volume and velocity first increase and then decrease, and the significant increase in flow velocity heterogeneity suggests that there are heterogeneous changes within the tumor during development, which may be related to vascular modeling of the tumor.

Histological Validation of the Intraocular Melanoma Model

Histological validation further confirmed the establishment and progression of the intraocular melanoma model. Gross examination of enucleated eyes revealed a pigmented intraocular mass, whereas H&E staining demonstrated a dome-shaped tumor originating from the uveal tract and infiltrating intraocular tissues. Notably, the tumor had already breached the scleral wall and extended beyond the globe, consistent with the advanced-stage features observed in ultrasound imaging (Supplementary Fig. S2). These findings provide pathological confirmation of successful tumor induction and progression.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a novel 3D contrast-free ultrafast ultrasound technique for ocular imaging, enabling in vivo visualization and quantitative analysis of blood flow in the iris, retina, choroid, and retrobulbar regions, and applied it for longitudinal monitoring in an intraocular melanoma mouse model. In specific, fundus and retrobulbar blood flow density increased significantly, whereas flow velocity rose initially and then declined. Within the tumor, both blood flow density and velocity followed a similar rise-and-fall pattern, accompanied by a marked increase in velocity variability. These findings demonstrate the clinical value of 3D uPDI for monitoring intraocular melanoma growth and changes in tumor vasculature, and provide new insights into hemodynamic mechanisms underlying intraocular melanoma progression, with potential implications for identifying novel therapeutic targets.

This 3D uDI approach offers several methodological advantages for imaging intraocular tumors. It provides true volumetric coverage of the eye, capturing blood flow from the iris and retina to the choroid and retrobulbar space (see Fig. 2)—capabilities beyond those of OCT/OCTA.18 In this study, ultrasound visualized intratumoral and posterior vessels even when OCTA was impeded by tumor pigmentation or media opacities. Moreover, uDI requires no exogenous contrast agents, unlike super-resolution ultrasound techniques that rely on microbubble injections and prolonged acquisitions.22 Its ultrahigh frame rate enables detection of weak microcirculation signals with relative tolerance to mild motion, whereas the resulting 3D datasets provide quantitative metrics (vascular volume, perfusion, and velocity) for longitudinal functional assessment. Together, these features make 3D uDI a robust tool for noninvasive, real-time monitoring of ocular tumor vascular dynamics.

This study also compared 3D ultrafast ultrasound imaging with OCTA, a widely used modality for ocular microvascular imaging. Compared with OCTA, 3D ultrasound has lower spatial resolution (approximately 100 µm vs. a few microns)23 but substantially greater penetration depth and field of view. OCTA finely resolves retinal capillaries but is limited to superficial layers and is highly susceptible to attenuation from pigmentation or media opacity. In contrast, ultrafast ultrasound can image the entire globe and retrobulbar region and is unaffected by optical opacities. In this study, OCTA detected subtle early changes after tumor implantation (e.g. subretinal cell deposits on day 1 and minor vessel displacement), but its signals became severely attenuated as the tumor grew. By comparison, uDI continuously visualized intratumoral and retrobulbar blood flow throughout tumor progression, enabling sustained visualization of tumor neovascularization.

Angiogenesis, a hallmark of cancer, refers to the formation of new blood vessels from the pre-existing vascular network to meet the nutritional and metabolic demands of tumors,24 and highly vascularized UM tumors are reported to be more aggressive and exhibit a worse prognosis.2 This study documented changes in microvascular parameters during intraocular melanoma progression and revealed dynamic vascular remodeling throughout tumor growth. In the early stage after implantation, the predominant feature was angiogenesis. The tumor initially grew within the globe with relatively slow volumetric expansion, yet both VVO and FMBV increased significantly, reflecting robust induction of neovascularization and marked increases in microvascular density and perfusion. This finding is consistent with the general understanding of the pathogenesis of malignant tumors involving endothelial proliferation and recruitment of host vessels, thereby enhancing blood supply.25 In the mid to advanced-stage, functional impairment and remodeling of the microvasculature became evident. Once the tumor breached the scleral wall, rapid volumetric expansion was accompanied by loss of central vascular structures, resulting in significant declines in VVO and FMBV, indicative of reduced vascular density and perfusion. Concurrently, BVV increased markedly while ABV decreased, suggesting highly heterogeneous flow velocities and the coexistence of ischemic and hyperperfused regions within the tumor. This may reflect physiological aspects of intraocular melanoma growth. On the one hand, direct shunting arteries may supply excessive flow to focal regions, producing localized hyperemia and high velocity.26 On the other, disorganized vascular networks may cause perfusion deficits through stagnation or leakage.27 In addition, vasculogenic mimicry (VM)—tumor cell-derived vascular-like channels independent of endothelial cells—may contribute.28 Although VM can transport blood, the flow is often slow and irregular, potentially undetectable by Doppler imaging, leading to apparent ischemic zones.29 By enabling visualization and quantification of vascular heterogeneity, our technique provides a foundation for future evaluation of hypoxia microenvironment and therapeutic efficacy in intraocular melanoma.

As the eye contains a highly vascularized network, investigating the impact of tumor growth on normal ocular circulation carries important clinical significance. Our results showed that during the early to mid-stage of tumor growth, VVO and FMBV in the fundus exhibited an overall upward trend. In the early stage, this rise may be partly attributable to inflammatory hyperemia of the retinal vasculature following surgical induction, as well as increased oxygen demand from localized proliferation of intraocular melanoma cells.2 In the mid stage, the sustained elevation of VVO and FMBV more directly reflected the influence of tumor-induced neovascularization on adjacent tissues, potentially linked to compensatory increases in host vessel recruitment.24 The retrobulbar vasculature followed a similar overall trend, with VVO and FMBV showing a modest decline on day 1—likely due to mechanical injury from the surgical procedure—followed by a gradual increase but the difference did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that in the early to mid-stage, intraocular melanoma exerts stronger effects on adjacent fundus vasculature than on the more distant retrobulbar vessels.5 In the fundus, ABV and BVV were significantly elevated on day 3 before returning near baseline, likely associated with heterogeneous tumor infiltration. OCTA revealed that early intraocular melanoma invasion of the retina was uneven, predominantly affecting the injected hemisphere. This may explain the localized increase in retinal flow velocity and heterogeneity. By day 5 and beyond, as tumor infiltration extended across the fundus, heterogeneity diminished, and the subsequent decline in flow velocity may be attributable to expansion of total vascular cross-sectional area from neovascularization, leading to velocity dilution. In the retrobulbar region, ABV and BVV displayed a similar temporal pattern but without statistical significance, reinforcing that intraretinal hemodynamic alterations were more dominant than retrobulbar changes in the early to mid-stage. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of quantitative intraocular flow analysis for monitoring early tumor progression.

Our technique proves its value in UM monitoring, whereas it is still worth noting that each imaging modality contributes unique information, and a multimodal approach could enhance UM management. MRI provides characteristic T1- and T2-weighted signals for tumor detection and differentiation, but it is expensive, low in spatial resolution, and lacks hemodynamic data. OCTA offers noninvasive, high-resolution views of superficial microvasculature, yet covers only a limited area and is prone to motion artifacts. Ultrafast ultrasound is contact-based but is portable, relatively motion-tolerant, and delivers real-time functional information on both tumor and ocular blood flow. In practice, these modalities are complementary: MRI establishes the diagnosis and anatomy, ultrasound provides global hemodynamic assessment, and OCTA details fine capillary changes. Integrating these methods could give a more comprehensive picture of tumor vascular anatomy and function, improving diagnostic and monitoring capabilities.

Beyond the present mouse intraocular melanoma model, the methodological framework established in this study is not inherently model-specific. Owing to its contrast-free nature, volumetric coverage, and relative robustness to optical degradation, the proposed 3D ultrafast Doppler approach is, in principle, applicable to a broad range of ocular and periocular vascular conditions. Building on the technical feasibility and longitudinal stability demonstrated here, we also conducted extended research on a rat model of pachychoroid spectrum diseases (PSDs) via vortex vein ligation (Supplementary Fig. S3). In this model, both qualitative visualization and quantitative hemodynamic metrics consistently revealed early-stage blood flow congestion following ligation, followed by progressive flow attenuation at later stages. These ongoing efforts aim to further evaluate the generalizability of the technique across different species, pathological mechanisms, and imaging contexts.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a mouse model with a much smaller eye (approximately 3 mm diameter) than humans (approximately 24 mm). Achieving comparable resolution in the human eye may require advanced high-frequency probe technology (e.g. 2D matrix arrays) that is still in development.30,31 Second, 3D volumes were acquired via mechanical scanning over a few seconds, making the method susceptible to motion artifacts in patients; faster volumetric imaging using high-frequency matrix transducers could address this in the future. Third, our quantitative analyses relied on manual segmentation of tumors and ocular regions, which may introduce observer bias—automation with machine learning could improve objectivity. Fourth, the B16F10 tumor model used in this study exhibits rapid and aggressive growth (large tumors within approximately 2 weeks),10 with relatively limited stromal complexity compared with human UM. Such rapid progression may be accompanied by pronounced tumor-associated inflammation, which could amplify angiogenic responses and hemodynamic changes relative to those observed in human tumors or less aggressive models. Therefore, the magnitude and temporal patterns of vascular remodeling reported here may not directly translate to human UM, and caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings clinically. Finally, our sample size was limited and we did not investigate therapeutic interventions or alternative tumor models. Accordingly, our findings primarily demonstrate the technical feasibility of 3D ultrafast Doppler imaging for longitudinal whole-eye hemodynamic assessment. Future studies incorporating slower-growing models, genetically engineered systems, or larger cohorts with longer-term follow-up will be essential to validate the clinical relevance of ultrasound-derived vascular parameters, including their relationship to outcomes such as metastasis.
