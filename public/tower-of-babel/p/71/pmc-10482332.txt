Fluorescence-guided photoimmunotherapy using targeted nanotechnology and ML7710 to manage peritoneal carcinomatosis
Authors: Barry J. Liang, Sumiao Pang, Robert Perttila, Chen-Hua Ma, Payal Srivastava, Brandon Gaitan, Aaron J. Sorrin, Nada Fadul, Idrisa Rahman, Zoe Ylo¨niemi, Dana M. Roque, Tayyaba Hasan, Petteri Uusimaa, Huang-Chiao Huang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Fluorescence-guided intervention can bolster standard therapies by detecting and treating microscopic tumors before lethal recurrence. Tremendous progress in photoimmunotherapy and nanotechnology has been made to treat metastasis. However, many are lost in translation due to heterogeneous treatment effects. Here, we integrate three technological advances in targeted photo-activable multi-agent liposome (TPMAL), fluorescence-guided intervention, and laser endoscopy (ML7710) to improve photoimmunotherapy. TPMAL consists of a nanoliposome chemotherapy labeled with fluorophores for tracking and photosensitizer immunoconjugates for photoimmunotherapy. ML7710 is connected to Modulight Cloud to capture and analyze multispectral emission from TPMAL for fluorescence-guided drug delivery (FGDD) and fluorescence-guided light dosimetry (FGLD) in peritoneal carcinomatosis mouse models. FGDD revealed that TPMAL enhances drug delivery to metastases by 14-fold. ML7710 captured interpatient variability in TPMAL uptake and prompted FGLD in >50% of animals. By combining TPMAL, ML7710, and fluorescence-guided intervention, variation in treatment response was substantially reduced and tumor control improved without side effects.

INTRODUCTION

Peritoneal metastasis, incomplete resection, and drug resistance render advanced-stage ovarian cancer virtually incurable with current surgical and chemotherapy approaches. Tumor recurrence is nearly universal, and the 5-year survival rate of around 30% or less has not changed significantly in the past 30 years (1). Up to 70% of these patients are in advanced stages with peritoneal metastasis at diagnosis (2). The primary mechanism of high-grade serous carcinoma metastasis, transcoelomic spread, involves the deposition of numerous cancer nodules throughout the abdominal cavity (3). Although nearly half of the women with advanced ovarian cancer achieve complete remission after surgery and chemotherapy, many patients will relapse due largely to residual submillimeter lesions (4, 5). These residual micrometastases are difficult to detect and often develop resistance to standard treatments (6). Radical new approaches that combine targeted therapy, imaging, and monitoring of treatment responses are needed to address drug-resistant micrometastases.

New approaches to treating peritoneal metastases are emerging with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of molecular imaging agents to help identify additional lesions during surgery (7). Another major advance has been the development of photosensitizer immunoconjugates (PICs) for targeted photodynamic therapy (PDT) [e.g., photoimmunotherapy (PIT)] of tumors (8–10). PIT is increasingly used to treat deep-seated, unresectable metastases through direct photochemical ablation and priming to sensitize the tumor to chemotherapy (11, 12). However, tools to improve the effectiveness, response monitoring, and consistency of PIT in gynecologic oncology have been lacking. The efficacy of PIT depends on the accumulation of PICs in diseased tissue and the precise delivery of harmless near-infrared light to generate cytotoxic reactive oxygen species, such as singlet oxygen (1O2) (13). While PICs improve tumor selectivity, their delivery to cancer cells is limited by the finite number of surface expression markers in cancers. Furthermore, variability in the expression of cancer biomarkers could also contribute to the heterogeneity of treatment effects.

Addressing challenges associated with detecting and treating peritoneal metastasis will require (i) efforts to advance targeted co-delivery and monitoring of PICs and chemotherapy, (ii) treatment personalization through drug delivery monitoring and light dosimetry optimization in real time, and (iii) a medical laser endoscopy system capable of simultaneously performing and monitoring intraoperative treatment in the peritoneal cavity. Here, we satisfy these needs by developing a targeted photo-activable multi-agent liposome platform with cancer cell targeting, PIT, chemotherapy, and fluorescence-emitting agents. Targeted photo-activable multi-agent liposome (TPMAL) co-assembles the following components: (i) PICs to enable PIT and fluorescence-guided intervention, (ii) targeted irinotecan chemotherapy delivery to induce cancer cell cytotoxicity, and (iii) dye labeling to facilitate fluorescence-based assessment of the nanoplatform biodistribution. The TPMAL formulation is engineered by combining PICs on the surface of a liposome, irinotecan chemotherapy in the liposomal aqueous core, or carboxyfluorescein (CF) dye in the phospholipid bilayer. Advancement in the co-assembly and co-delivery of PICs and chemotherapies into a single targeted nanoplatform sets the foundation for improved outcomes. In addition to cancer treatment, the photosensitizer fluorescence signal produced from TPMAL could enable image-guided treatment delivery and planning, thus further enhancing treatment outcomes and reducing normal tissue exposures.

Intraoperative PDT of peritoneal carcinomatosis using nontargeted photosensitizers and a fixed light dose combination is already safe and feasible in the clinic (14–16). Still, it has yet to achieve complete responses or long-term tumor control, mainly due to the heterogeneity and lack of tumor specificity in photosensitizer uptake (16). Additionally, PDT and irinotecan have been demonstrated to be effective for treatment of primary tumors by us (17, 18) and others (19), but their selective co-delivery to metastasis and imaging have been challenging. While targeted nanoplatforms, like TPMAL, are being designed to improve tumor specificity for photosensitizer uptake, efforts to address heterogeneity in treatment response continue to fall behind. This study utilizes TPMAL for photo-chemotherapy that is engineered with molecular targeting and fluorescence tracking features, which have unique capabilities to improve therapeutic delivery and monitoring of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)–positive metastases in difficult-to-treat locations within the body. In addition, this is the first report to demonstrate that PDT and irinotecan (in TPMAL) can be safely combined with cisplatin-paclitaxel cocktail to further reduce metastatic burden. Using a laser endoscopy system (ML7710), we also advanced a two-pronged approach to ensure that TPMAL-assisted PDT and irinotecan delivery are safe, predictable, and customizable (Fig. 1).

Workflow schematic.

This shows the overall workflow of integrating targeted nanomedicine, medical laser endoscopy system, and fluorescence-guided intervention to improve the safety, efficacy, and consistency of photoimmunotherapy (PIT) for peritoneal metastasis. The targeted photo-activable multi-agent nanoliposome (TPMAL) co-delivers fluorophores, photosensitizer immunoconjugates (PICs), and chemotherapy drugs at high payloads for combined imaging, PIT, and chemotherapy of peritoneal tumors. A medical laser endoscopy system (ML7710) coupled with Modulight Cloud was engineered to perform PIT and fluorescence-guided intervention in the abdominal cavity of the patient. Specifically, fluorescence-guided drug delivery (FGDD) can reveal interpatient variability in PIC uptake, which prompts the need for intraoperative fluorescence-guided light dosimetry (FGLD) adjustment in study subjects.

First, we developed a minimally invasive, fluorescence-guided strategy to inform the intraperitoneal delivery of TPMAL. The intraperitoneal delivery route is clinically relevant for peritoneal metastasis, and dual-fluorescence imaging allows us to obtain the distribution and levels of TPMAL in the tumor. Second, we leverage ML7710 to monitor the photobleaching of TPMAL during PDT for fluorescence-guided interventions. To demonstrate the full potential of this strategy, ML7710 is connected to the Modulight Cloud not only to capture and analyze multispectral fluorescence emission from TPMAL for real-time fluorescence-guided drug delivery (FGDD) and fluorescence-guided light dosimetry (FGLD) but also to perform PIT in the peritoneal cavity of mice. Modulight Cloud enables real-time connectivity for simultaneous treatment monitoring, remote protocol development, and fluent transfer/viewing of data. This combined strategy provides the means to improve the consistency of PIT responses through FGDD and FGLD. FGDD and FGLD are developed to inform the timing and dosing of light activation, respectively. First, due to idiosyncratic pharmacokinetics, the optimal TPMAL accumulation in tumors may occur at different times for different patients. FGDD identifies the time when the TPMAL fluorescence signal is near peak levels in tumors, thereby informing the initiation of PIT for best outcomes. Second, the amount of TPMAL in tumors could display intersubject variability. FGLD monitors the photobleaching of photosensitizers during PIT so that the light dose can be adjusted accordingly in real time to improve the consistency of treatment effects. Our strategy allows us to customize light dose and improve the consistency of PDT and irinotecan combination, an important consideration for heterogeneous cancers such as peritoneal metastasis. The results from this study suggest that performing real-time longitudinal monitoring of TPMAL delivery and photosensitizer photobleaching can guide treatment decisions to improve the safety, efficacy, and consistency of PIT, thereby reducing metastatic burden in vivo.

RESULTS

Monodispersed TPMAL (PIC-Nal-CF) is dual fluorescent and photochemical responsive

To assemble PIC, five benzoporphyrin derivative (BPD) photosensitizer molecules were covalently attached to each anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) monoclonal antibody (cetuximab, also known as Erbitux) via carbodiimide chemistry at a conjugation efficiency of 53.8 ± 5.3% (Fig. 2A and table S1). Monodispersed CF nanoliposomes (Nal-CF) were prepared using the extrusion method and had an average size of 120.7 ± 8.5 nm [Polydispersity Index (PdI) < 0.1] (Fig. 2B and table S2). To synthesize TPMAL (PIC-Nal-CF), click chemistry was used to covalently link the PIC to the Nal-CF (Fig. 2C). The resulting PIC-Nal-CF has an average size of 131.2 ± 9.4 nm (PdI < 0.1), a zeta potential of around −15 mV, and approximately 30 PICs per PIC-Nal-CF at a conjugation efficiency of 29.1 ± 6.0% (Fig. 2D and table S2). The number of CF per PIC-Nal-CF was determined using the molar extinction coefficient of CF (80,447 M−1 cm−1, 490 nm) (fig. S1 and table S3). Nal-CF and PIC-Nal-CF contain ~287 and ~92 CF molecules per liposome, respectively (table S2). Stability studies showed minimal changes in the overall hydrodynamic size and PdI of PIC-Nal-CF and Nal-CF for up to 18 weeks of storage at 4°C (Fig. 2E and fig. S2).

Conjugation of PIC to Nal-CF did not alter the absorption and emission spectra of BPD and CF. The maximum absorption wavelength of CF (λmax = 490 nm) was maintained in both PIC-Nal-CF and Nal-CF (Fig. 2F). Similarly, conjugation of five BPD photosensitizers to cetuximab to form PIC or PIC-Nal-CF did not alter the Soret (435 nm) and Q bands (690 nm) of BPD (Fig. 2G). A single excitation wavelength at 455 nm was used to activate both CF and BPD in PIC-Nal-CF for fluorescence detection. We examined the fluorescence spectra of PIC-Nal-CF using ML7710 (Fig. 2H) and a plate reader (Fig. 2I). Upon 445-nm excitation, PIC-Nal-CF displayed a strong CF fluorescence signal between 500 and 600 nm, and BPD fluorescence spectrum was observed around 700 nm. As expected, Nal-CF and CF alone only displayed CF fluorescence signal between 500 and 600 nm. Little to no BPD fluorescence was detected from BPD alone or PIC samples in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). This is due to aggregation and self-quenching of BPD molecules in aqueous solution as shown in our studies (20–22). When PIC and BPD are fully dissolved in organic solvent dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), the BPD fluorescence signal around 700 nm can be restored (fig. S3).

Furthermore, using ML7710 and a plate reader, we observed a stronger BPD fluorescence signal at 700 nm from PIC-Nal-CF compared to PIC or BPD. This is consistent with our previous work showing that the conjugation of PIC to PEGylated liposomes can help dequench BPD in PBS, partially restoring BPD fluorescence signal (21, 22). This was further confirmed by showing that the BPD photoactivity of PIC-Nal-CF is nearly five times higher compared to free BPD and PIC, and similar to the clinically relevant nanoliposomal BPD formulation (Nal-BPD) (Fig. 2J). The enhanced photoactivity implies that PIC-Nal-CF could be more readily activated for single oxygen production. Upon 690-nm red light activation, the singlet oxygen yield of PIC-Nal-CF is ~200% higher than that of PIC (Fig. 2K). No singlet oxygen generation was detected upon 690-nm activation of Nal-CF. In summary, using ML7710, we showed that a single 455-nm laser can activate both BPD and CF for dual-fluorescent detection of PIC-Nal-CF, and the 690-nm laser can be used to excite the BPD of PIC-Nal-CF for singlet oxygen production. In contrast to the 445-nm wavelength, light excitation using 488 nm only led to CF fluorescence emission but not BPD fluorescence emission (fig. S4).

FGDD of TPMAL (PIC-Nal-CF) can be performed in both liquid phantom and peritoneal carcinomatosis mouse model using ML7710

Intralipid (0.01 to 0.1%) is a light-scattering agent used in the clinic for intraperitoneal PDT of peritoneal carcinomatosis (14, 16, 23). Using ML7710, we examined whether Intralipid alters the fluorescence emission spectra of PIC-Nal-CF in liquid phantoms containing 0.03% ink and Intralipid (0 to 0.1%). The 0.03% ink was used as a light absorber to mimic the turbid environment of serosanguinous fluid (i.e., blood and ascites) of peritoneal carcinomatosis. As expected, the addition of 0.03% ink without Intralipid significantly reduced the fluorescence signal from CF and BPD (fig. S5). The addition of 0.1% Intralipid significantly restored the CF and BPD fluorescence, by ~39% and ~59%, respectively. This suggested that clinically relevant 0.1% Intralipid could aid fluorescence monitoring of PIC-Nal-CF.

Using ML7710, the biodistribution of PIC-Nal-CF and Nal-CF was longitudinally monitored in OVCAR-5 peritoneal carcinomatosis mouse model (fig. S6). Figure 3A shows representative fluorescence emission spectra of PIC-Nal-CF and Nal-CF in different quadrants (Q1 to Q4) of the mouse peritoneal cavity (Fig. 3B). In agreement with in vitro results (Fig. 2), distinct CF (500 to 600 nm) and BPD (700 nm) fluorescence signals from PIC-Nal-CF can be detected in the peritoneal cavity of diseased mice (Fig. 3A). ML7710 showed that the CF fluorescence signal from PIC-Nal-CF and Nal-CF decreased over 24 hours. At 6 hours, an average of 22% and 69% loss of CF fluorescence signal was observed in animals treated with PIC-Nal-CF and Nal-CF, respectively (Fig. 3C). At 24 hours, >80% of the CF fluorescence signal diminished in mice treated with Nal-CF, while 60% loss of signal was observed in the PIC-Nal-CF group. These results demonstrate that PIC-Nal-CF has a higher and prolonged retention than Nal-CF in the peritoneal carcinomatosis mouse model.

To evaluate the cancer selectivity of PIC-Nal-CF, tumor and normal tissues were collected at different time points after injection of PIC-Nal-CF or Nal-CF (Fig. 3D), and then the CF levels were determined using an established standard curve and excised tissues (figs. S7 and S8, respectively). At 12 hours after injection, PIC-Nal-CF has a higher accumulation in diaphragmatic tumor [D(T)], pancreatic tumor [P(T)], splenic tumor [Sp(T)], bowel mesenteric tumor [M(T)], subgastric omentum tumor [SgO(T)], uterine tumor [U(T)], and pelvic omentum tumor [PO(T)] by 5.9-, 7.5-, 6.4-, 14.1-, 6.2-, 7.8-, and 3.9-fold, respectively, compared to Nal-CF (Fig. 3E). Minimal to no PIC-Nal-CF and Nal-CF were detected in normal tissues (heart, lung, liver, kidney, and skin) at 12 hours (Fig. 3F). Further analysis shows that the area under the curve (AUC) of PIC-Nal-CF in tumors is up to 3.4-fold higher than that of Nal-CF (Fig. 3G). Together, the study demonstrates enhanced tumor selectivity and retention of TPMAL (PIC-Nal-CF) platform compared to its “nontargeted” control.

FGDD reveals that TPMAL can “lock in” cargo ratios and improve PIC delivery to peritoneal metastases

Effective combination treatment relies on optimal co-delivery, release, and monitoring of theranostic agents at target sites. Here, we evaluated the biodistribution of PIC-Nal-CF by tracking both BPD and CF fluorescence signals. The PIC-Nal-CF used in this study delivered BPD (0.9 mg/kg) and CF (2.9 mg/kg). Therefore, the BPD-to-CF mass ratio can be calculated to be ~1:3. At 2 hours after intraperitoneal injection of PIC-Nal-CF, we were able to detect similar BPD-to-CF mass ratios in tumors, ranging from 1:2.5 to 1:3.7 (Fig. 4A). These mass ratios are comparable to the original PIC-Nal-CF mass ratios and suggest that PIC-Nal-CF can “lock in” and “co-deliver” the designed drug ratios to target sites. Compared to the normal tissues (heart, lungs, liver, and kidneys) (Fig. 4B), we observed a higher PIC-Nal-CF accumulation in metastatic tumors (Fig. 4C). At 2, 6, 12, and 24 hours after PIC-Nal-CF injection, the overall accumulation of CF was 15, 8, 23, and 11 times higher in tumors than in normal tissues. There was also a higher BPD accumulation in metastatic tumors compared to the normal tissues (heart, lungs, liver, and kidneys). The BPD accumulation in all tumor tissues peaked at 12 hours after PIC-Nal-CF injection and started to decrease at 24 hours.

One of the challenges to PIT is the poor uptake of photosensitizers by tumor cells. Here, we tested if PIC-Nal-irinotecan (IRI) can improve BPD delivery to metastatic tumors compared to PIC alone or the clinically relevant Nal-BPD. At 24 hours after intraperitoneal injection, animals treated with PIC-Nal-IRI demonstrated the strongest BPD fluorescence signal in diaphragmatic, pancreatic, mesenteric, and subgastric omental tumors (Fig. 5A). The BPD fluorescence signal was found to be low in all healthy tissues (e.g., heart, lung, liver, and kidney) (fig. S9). Further analysis confirmed that PIC-Nal-IRI significantly enhances BPD delivery to diaphragmatic tumor, pancreatic tumor, mesenteric tumor, subgastric omental tumor, and uterus tumor by up to 4.2-, 2.8-, 4.2-, 5.7-, and 3.5-fold, respectively, compared to PIC and Nal-BPD (Fig. 5B). Finally, our results also show that PIC-Nal-IRI has the highest tumor-to-normal tissue (lung, liver, kidney) ratios compared to PIC and Nal-BPD (fig. S10). The tumor-to-skin ratio was found to be similar between PIC-Nal-IRI, PIC, and Nal-BPD.

Intraoperative FGLD allows customization of PIT in peritoneal carcinomatosis mouse model

Photosensitizer photobleaching refers to the irreversible photochemical alteration of a photosensitizer such that it is permanently unable to generate fluorescence signal or singlet oxygen. It has been shown that BPD photobleaching patterns correlate with cytotoxic response in three-dimensional tumor nodules (24). Here, we showed that ML7710 can not only perform intraperitoneal 690 nm light activation of PIC-Nal-IRI for PIT in vivo but also concurrently collect BPD photobleaching patterns for treatment monitoring and data storage in Modulight Cloud, allowing easy access and real-time analysis. Before FGLD, we assessed this unique function of ML7710 for evaluation of photobleaching in liquid phantoms. We showed that ML7710 can rapidly and longitudinally measure BPD photobleaching. Figure S11 shows that light activation (690 nm, 100 mW/cm, 0 to 60 J/cm) of TPMAL (PIC-Nal-CF) resulted in ~50% BPD photobleaching in liquid phantoms.

We next investigated the safety and feasibility of using ML7710 for FGDD, FGLD, and personalized PIT in OVCAR-5 peritoneal carcinomatosis mouse models (Fig. 6A). At 24 hours after injection of PIC-Nal-IRI (1 mg/kg BPD), FGDD was performed to measure the baseline BPD fluorescence signal in each peritoneal quadrant of the mouse using ML7710 (Fig. 6B). Before PIT, BPD fluorescence measurements (FGDD) revealed large interpatient variability in tumoral uptake of PIC-Nal-IRI across individual animals (Fig. 6C). To perform PIT, red light (690 nm, 150 mW/cm, 25 J/cm) was used to photoactivate PIC-Nal-IRI in each abdominal quadrant of the mouse to achieve a total light dose of 100 J/cm per mouse (Fig. 6B). Immediately after PIT, the residual BPD fluorescence signal in each peritoneal quadrant of the mouse was collected using ML7710 and the level of BPD photobleaching was intraoperatively analyzed in Modulight Cloud. Figure 6D shows that ~47% of the animals show ≥70% photosensitizer photobleaching across the different quadrants after one round of PIT at 100 J/cm. To perform FGLD, the remaining 53% of animals, with <70% photosensitizer photobleaching after the first round of PIT, received additional incremental rounds of PIT at 25 J/cm until ≥70% photosensitizer photobleaching has been reached (690 nm, 150 mW/cm, 6.25 J/cm per quadrant) (Fig. 6, C and D). In our study, various degrees of photosensitizer photobleaching (15.5 to 93.3%) were observed in different animals after the initial fixed light dose of 100 J/cm. For example, Fig. 6E shows one mouse subject with ~70 to 80% of photosensitizer photobleaching across the different quadrants after one light dose of 100 J/cm. In another subject (Fig. 6F), only around 30% photosensitizer photobleaching was observed across the quadrants that received the same 100 J/cm light dose. Leveraging FGLD, two additional rounds of light activation (25 J/cm each) were given to this animal to achieve a final 70% or higher photosensitizer photobleaching. These results demonstrate that ML7710 coupled with Modulight Cloud can safely perform FGDD to capture interpatient variability in PIC-Nal-IRI uptake across individual animals. This also prompted the need for up to three additional rounds of FGLD PIT in more than half of the animals.

FGLD improves the consistency and efficacy of TPMAL (PIC-Nal-IRI)–assisted PIT with standard chemotherapy

Heterogeneity in treatment response is another problem limiting successful PIT for peritoneal metastases. We next tested if fluorescence-guided intervention with PIC-Nal-IRI–assisted PIT and its combination with taxane-platinum–based chemotherapy cocktail can further improve PIT outcomes in OVCAR-5 peritoneal carcinomatosis mouse model (Fig. 7A). Without treatment, mice with OVCAR-5 peritoneal carcinomatosis have an average total tumor burden of 1378.8 ± 326.3 mg at 16 days after tumor implantation (Fig. 7B). We showed that a single cycle of PIT using PIC-Nal-IRI (no FGLD) only slightly reduced the overall tumor burden by ~26%, compared to the no treatment (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, PIT without FGLD showed no statistically significant (P > 0.05) metastatic burden reduction given the large variation in treatment response (1012.5 ± 474.0 mg). Using ML7710, FGLD PIT significantly reduced the total metastatic burden by 41% (807.5 ± 202.8 mg; P < 0.05), lowering the variability in endpoint tumor burden from 47% to 25% (Fig. 7B). To achieve further reduction in metastatic burden, we studied if FGLD PIT can be combined with cisplatin and paclitaxel chemotherapy, which are commonly given as adjuvant chemotherapy for patients with peritoneal carcinomatosis. After PIT or FGLD PIT, two cycles of chemotherapy (5 mg/kg of cisplatin and 3 mg/kg of paclitaxel) were intraperitoneally administered to animals on days 9 and 14 following tumor inoculation. Two cycles of chemotherapy alone reduced the total metastatic burden by ~57% (591.0 ± 171.1 mg). In contrast, the combination of FGLD and chemotherapy further reduced the total metastatic burden by around 86% (195.3 ± 65.84 mg), compared to no treatment. This reduction of tumor burden was found to be uniform across all disease sites in the peritoneal cavity (Fig. 7C). There were also minimal to no changes in animal weights across all treatment groups, indicating that the proposed regimens do not appreciably add to the systemic toxicity (fig. S12).

Correlations between endpoint tumor burden (mg) and the BPD fluorescence intensity before PIT (i.e., pretreatment BPD fluorescence signal) (Fig. 7D) or the percent fluorescence signal change after PIT (i.e., photosensitizer photobleaching) (Fig. 7E) were also carried out. In the PIT group, we observed a strong negative correlation [blue slope: −0.03 endpoint tumor burden mg/initial BPD relative fluorescence unit (RFU)], where the pretreatment BPD fluorescence increases as the endpoint tumor burden reduces (Fig. 7D). In mice treated with FGLD PIT, a moderate negative correlation (green slope: −0.015 mg/initial BPD RFU) was observed, indicating that when FGLD is implemented, the endpoint tumor burden is less dependent on the pretreatment BPD fluorescence signal. The combination groups (PIT + chemotherapy or FGLD PIT + chemotherapy) show the weakest negative correlation (cyan and red slopes: −0.005 and −0.002 mg/initial BPD RFU, respectively), suggesting that effective tumor burden reduction can be achieved consistently, despite large interpatient variability in pretreatment BPD fluorescence across individual animals. Figure 6E shows that FGLD can ensure that the degree of BPD photobleaching is above a certain preassigned threshold (≥70%), improving both the consistency and efficacy of PIT or its combination with chemotherapy.

DISCUSSION

Ovarian cancer continues to be the most lethal gynecologic malignancy in the United States. While the 5-year survival rate for stage I disease is 92%, over 75% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages (III to IV) with limited treatment options, bringing the overall 5-year survival rate below 50% (1). PDT is a promising treatment for peritoneal carcinomatosis. Glatstein, Hahn, Cengel, and others have demonstrated the feasibility of intraperitoneal PDT for women with peritoneal metastases (14–16) and in canines (25). In a phase II trial, patients with peritoneal carcinomatosis received Photofrin photosensitizer injection 48 hours before laparotomy (16). At the end of surgery, patients with disease <5 mm received PDT. While laser light was delivered safely to peritoneal surfaces of the abdomen and pelvis to activate photosensitizers, PDT did not improve the overall survival. The authors reported that the heterogeneity and lack of tumor specificity in photosensitizer uptake may be the reasons for the lack of greater efficacy. The team further suggested that a single treatment modality is unlikely to lead to a sustained complete response. The separated light delivery and fluorescence monitoring systems could also prolong operating room time. On the basis of the lessons learned from three decades of clinical studies, we demonstrated that the efficacy, safety, and consistency of PIT can be significantly improved by successful integration of three technological advances: (i) PIC nanotechnology, (ii) fluorescence-guided treatment planning, and (iii) a medical laser endoscopy system.

PIT is a targeted version of PDT introduced by Levy in 1983 (8). PIT involves conjugating one or more photosensitizers to an antibody (or other targeting moiety) to enhance tumor selectivity in complex sites, such as the peritoneal cavity (11, 12). It has been reported that PIT not only produces cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (e.g., singlet oxygen; Fig. 2K) to directly kill cancer cells but also induces activation of local and systemic antitumor immune responses when appropriate light and photosensitizer doses are delivered (26). Specifically, immunogenic cell death (ICD) can occur, leading to release of tumor-associated antigens and damaged-associated molecular patterns, thus promoting maturation of dendritic cells. Previous work from Nakajima et al. (27) and Zhou et al. (28) investigated the mechanisms behind near infrared (NIR)–PIT, which led to the elucidation that PIT resulted in minute membrane damage–inducing ICD effects including increased levels of calreticulin, heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), and HSP90, as well as rapid discharge of HMGB1 and adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Then, in 2020, a cetuximab-IRDye700 conjugate (known as RM-1929 or ASP-1929) was approved for PIT of head and neck cancer in Japan (10).

In ovarian cancer mouse models, we have demonstrated that PIT reduces metastatic burden and minimizes bowel tissue damage (11, 29). This is achieved by 690-nm light activation of PIC, which is made of FDA-approved BPD photosensitizers and anti-EGFR antibody cetuximab. Our PIC is “cancer-activatable,” meaning that BPD photosensitizers are quenched (inactivated) when conjugated to an antibody, and they can be unquenched (activated) by cancer cells upon receptor-mediated endocytosis and lysosomal proteolysis (20, 21). Furthermore, it has been shown that PIT synergizes with platinum agents and reverses chemoresistance in ovarian tumors in vitro and in vivo (29–31). Many of the photosensitizers (e.g., BPD and indocyanine green) can also generate fluorescence signals for imaging and fluorescence-guided intervention. In 2021, the FDA approved CYTALUX (a targeted fluorescent imaging agent that conjugates folic acid to an indocyanine green–like dye) for fluorescence-guided surgery of disseminated ovarian cancer lesions (7). While these successes suggest that PIT and fluorescence imaging could be a cancer theranostic package, there are still several hurdles to be addressed before integrating PIT and imaging into the standard of care for peritoneal metastases. First, our previous work has shown that PIC delivery can be limited by the heterogeneous and finite number of receptors on cancer cells. Second, a single modality is unlikely to result in a sustained complete response. Therefore, the combination of PIT with standard chemotherapy (e.g., irinotecan, taxanes, and platinum agents) is needed and, ideally, should be co-delivered and longitudinally monitored. Last, to give the right treatment dose to the right patient at the right time, it is most critical to develop a user-friendly medical laser system along with image-guided strategies to inform the dosimetry and timing of PIT.

Our TPMAL platform technology was developed to co-deliver PIC and CF dyes (PIC-Nal-CF) or irinotecan chemotherapy (PIC-Nal-IRI). Biodistribution studies and treatment response studies were carried out independently using these two formulations, respectively. Biodistribution studies leveraged the co-assembly of PIC and CF (PIC-Nal-CF) to enable dual-fluorescence imaging. We identified a single excitation wavelength (445 nm) that can be used to activate both BPD and CF dyes for fluorescence tracking of PIC-Nal-CF biodistribution in metastatic tumors. We showed that PIC-Nal-CF can lock in and co-deliver the designed cargo mass ratio (BPD-to-CF, 1:3) to tumor sites at 2 hours after injection (Fig. 4A). The changes in BPD-to-CF mass ratio observed at later time points (6, 12, 24 and hours) after injection (Fig. 4C) suggest the disassembly of nanoconstructs, which could provide a unique way to monitor TPMAL stability in cancer cells in future studies. Our biodistribution analysis further revealed that PIC-Nal-CF is cancer-selective with improved accumulation and retention in peritoneal tumors by up to ~14 times, compared to Nal-CF. Similarly, PIC-Nal-IRI also enhanced BPD photosensitizer delivery to peritoneal tumors by up to 5.7-fold, compared to PIC. Thus, treatment response studies using PIC and irinotecan chemotherapy were co-delivered in TPMAL (PIC-Nal-IRI). This is because salvage chemotherapy with irinotecan has been used to treat patients with advanced ovarian cancer, and there has been some enthusiasm with the combination of irinotecan and bevacizumab (or etoposide) showing activity and a modest improvement of overall survival to 13.8 to 19 months in heavily pretreated patients with recurrent ovarian cancer (32–34). Furthermore, the mechanism underlying synergistic cytotoxicity between PDT, using FDA-approved BPD photosensitizer, and irinotecan has already been established in our previous studies (17, 18).

An ideal cancer treatment should be combined with longitudinal monitoring of drug delivery and treatment adjustment to further improve patient outcomes. Here, we have developed a single ML7710 system that can simultaneously activate PIT, capture multispectral fluorescence emissions of the delivered TPMAL, and customize PIT dosimetry in real time. Our two-pronged FGDD and FGLD approach is based on (i) the detection of fluorescence signal of TPMAL in the tissue (to verify the presence and distribution of BPD photosensitizer in treated areas) and (ii) the monitoring of photosensitizer photobleaching during PIT (to adjust dosimetry for individual patients). In peritoneal carcinomatosis mouse models, FGDD revealed a high interpatient heterogeneity of photosensitizer uptake in tumors, and FGLD informs light dose escalation to improve overall treatment consistency. While our data suggest that pretreatment photosensitizer uptake and photobleaching are critical predictors for improving the therapeutic efficacy, other factors such as tissue oxygenation and metabolism can also affect treatment outcomes (35). The next generation of laser endoscopy systems could include functions to monitor changes in hemoglobin oxygenation level and/or optical redox ratio that are known predictors of photodynamic treatment response. The use of a single ML7710 also eliminates the need for two separate laser treatment and intraoperative fluorescence monitoring systems. Because of the complex anatomy of the peritoneal cavity, simple fluorescence spectral scans and acquisition may reduce the operative time compared to wide-field fluorescence imaging and processing. However, one possible limitation of the current setup of ML7710 is the small tissue area coverage for fluorescence measurement using 1-cm cylindrical diffusing fibers. To address this issue, ML7710 could be configured with different light delivery optics, such as balloon and light-emitting fabric (36), to deliver light more uniformly and acquire fluorescence spectra in larger animals or humans. Intraoperative catheters or balloons have already been shown to be safe and feasible for PDT and brachytherapy of diffuse tumors in the clinic (37–41).

Microscopic peritoneal metastasis is regularly present after complete cytoreductive surgery in advanced ovarian cancer patients (4). Recent reports suggested that complete cytoreductive surgery followed by locoregional therapies such as hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) (42, 43) or molecular PDT (44) could improve survival outcomes for patients with peritoneal metastasis. Additionally, our previous study has already shown that combination of PDT and EGFR-targeted therapy can enhance survival in human ovarian carcinomatosis mouse models (45). Therefore, the combination of conventional cytoreductive surgery followed by EGFR-targeted PDT and chemotherapy should be studied next. Our long-term goal is to treat patients whose peritoneal disease has been resected to a thickness of <0.5- to 1-cm tumors during open surgery. Furthermore, based on previous clinical work, we believe that the most advantageous and safe initial application of FGLD PIT is following ovarian tumor resection while patients are in the operating room. In this context, patients will receive intraperitoneal injection of TPMAL in 0.1% Intralipid solution that enhances light scattering and delivery to peritoneal surfaces for PIT. Following FGLD PIT, patients will likely resume standard platinum-taxane chemotherapy. Using ML7710 and TPMAL, this study showed that FGLD PIT can be safely combined with standard platinum-taxane chemotherapy to reduce interpatient variability in drug uptake, as well as reduce metastatic tumor burden. Future directions include the study of immune modulation and abscopal effects in a syngeneic mouse model, as well as ICD analyses to provide additional insights into the underlying mechanisms of antitumor immune responses to PIT and chemotherapy compounds.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis and characterization of PICs

Carbodiimide chemistry was used to conjugate BPD photosensitizer to anti-EGFR monoclonal cetuximab antibody (21, 22). Briefly, cetuximab (2 mg/ml) was PEGylated with mPEG-NHS (40 kDa; 16 mg/ml) at a molar ratio of 1:3 overnight at room temperature. The resulting PEGylated cetuximab was reacted with N-hydroxysuccinimidyl (NHS) esters of BPD and azide at a 9:2.5:1 molar ratio for 20 hours before purification with a Zeba spin desalting column [7-kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO)] that has been preequilibrated with 30% DMSO in PBS. The resulting PIC was washed with 5% DMSO in PBS and concentrated with an Amicon centrifugal filter tube (30-kDa MWCO). The BPD concentration was determined using ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy using established molar extinction coefficients of 80,500 cm−1 M−1 and 34,895 cm−1 M−1 at 435 and 690 nm, respectively. The final protein concentration was evaluated with a bicinchoninic acid assay.

Preparation and characterization of nanoliposomes

CF-labeled nanoliposomes (Nal-CF), nanoliposomal irinotecan (Nal-IRI), and nanoliposomal BPD (Nal-BPD) were formed following the freeze-thaw extrusion method (22, 46). Briefly, dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC), distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine-methoxy polyethylene glycol (DSPE-mPEG2000), dioleoylglycerophosphoglycerol (DOPG; Avanti Polar Lipids), and cholesterol were mixed at a molar ratio of 6:0.4:0.6:2.8. Distearoylglycerophosphoethanolamine-N-dibenzocyclooctyl polyethylene glycol (DSPE-mPEG2000-DBCO) at 0.2 mol % was added to nanoliposomes for DBCO functionalization. To prepare Nal-CF, 0.2 to 1 mol % of 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(carboxyfluorescein) (18:1 PE CF) were added for CF labeling. For Nal-BPD, 50 nmol of BPD was co-dissolved with lipids at a BPD–to–total lipid ratio of ~0.6 mol %. Chloroform from all lipid mixtures was removed by rotary evaporation to create a thin film. The resulting dried film was hydrated with UltraPure water before freeze-thaw cycling alternating between 4°C and 45°C. For Nal-IRI, dried lipid film was hydrated with 3 mM irinotecan followed by freeze-thaw cycle. The lipid suspensions of Nal-IRI or Nal-BPD were extruded through polycarbonate membrane (Nuclepore, Whatman, 0.1 μm) at 45°C. Nal-CF was extruded sequentially through polycarbonate membrane from 0.2 μm then to 0.1 μm. Extruded liposomes were dialyzed against PBS to remove unencapsulated agents (Spectra/Por, MWCO 300 kDa, Spectrum Laboratories Inc.). NanoBrook Omni (Brookhaven) was used to determine particle size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential. The BPD and irinotecan concentrations were determined using UV-Vis spectroscopy as described previously (17, 18).

Synthesis and characterization of TPMAL

TPMAL was synthesized via copper-free click chemistry by combining azide-containing PIC and DBCO-containing Nal-CF (or Nal-IRI) at 90:1 PIC-to-nanoliposome molar ratio for overnight conjugation. Sepharose CL-4B size exclusion chromatography (Sigma) was used to purify the TPMAL. The concentrations of BPD and irinotecan were measured based on established molar extinction coefficients (22). Concentration of CF was calculated based on determined molar extinction coefficients in PBS (table S3). Absorbance and fluorescence spectra were obtained from micro-plate reader (Cytation 5, BioTek) at a fixed concentration (1 μM CF and 2 μM BPD). Singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG; 5 μM) was used to detect singlet oxygen yield upon light activation (690 nm, 50 mW/cm, 10 J/cm) of TPMAL with a fixed BPD concentration (4 μM). NanoBrook Omni (Brookhaven) was used to determine particle size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential at different storage conditions.

Photobleaching study of TPMAL (PIC-Nal-CF) in saline buffer and liquid phantom

The photobleaching of TPMAL was first evaluated in vitro using the ML7710 system (Modulight Corp., Tampere, Finland). Briefly, TPMAL (6.9 μM BPD, 1 ml of PBS) was placed into a 1.5-ml test tube and light-activated (690 nm, 100 mW/cm) with the ML7710 system via a cylindrical light–diffusing fiber (DCYL 210, 1 cm) for up to 10 min. During light activation, the BPD and CF fluorescence spectra (excitation: 445 or 488 nm; emission: 500 to 800 nm; integration time: 1000 ms) generated from TPMAL were collected using the multi-channel setup offered by ML7710 (i.e., two 1-cm cylindrical light–diffusing fibers, one for excitation and one for collection at a fixed 2-mm distance; DCYL 210). Fluorescence measurements of TPMAL (1.4 μM BPD) were also carried out in liquid phantom (18 ml; aqueous solution containing 0.03% ink with 0 to 0.1% Intralipid, Baxter Healthcare, IL) using ML7710 as described above. Fluorescence measurements were taken in complete darkness to minimize any background.

Fluorescence measurement of TPMAL (PIC-Nal-CF) in orthotopic mouse models of ovarian peritoneal carcinomatosis

The orthotopic xenograft mouse model of ovarian carcinomatosis was established by us as described previously (11). Female athymic nude mice (18 to 20 g and 4 to 6 weeks old, The Jackson Laboratory) were injected intraperitoneally with 3.2 × 107 human OVCAR-5 ovarian cancer cells suspended in 2 ml of PBS. All animal studies were carried out according to the protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Maryland, College Park. In vivo fluorescence monitoring of TPMAL was carried out using ML7710. Briefly, TPMAL or control CF-labeled nanoliposomes (2.9 mg/kg CF, 1 ml) were intraperitoneally injected into animals 2 weeks after tumor inoculation. Fluorescence measurements were taken at 2, 6, 12, and 24 hours after TPMAL injection. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane before and during fluorescence measurements. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 1.5 ml of 0.1% Intralipid solution (Baxter Healthcare) to enhance light scattering. Two 1-cm cylindrical light–diffusing fibers (RD10, Medlight) were placed into the peritoneal cavity of a dorsally recumbent anesthetized animal via two 14-gauge catheters through the abdominal wall (distance between the catheters was fixed at 4 to 5 mm). Fluorescence excitation was performed with one fiber (445 nm, 50 mW/cm), while the fluorescence signal (emission: 500 to 800 nm) from TPMAL was collected using the other fiber (integration time: 2500 ms). TPMAL fluorescence spectra were collected by moving both fibers simultaneously in a clockwise direction in the peritoneal cavity from quadrant 1 (top left) to quadrant 4 (bottom left). Animals were euthanized, and tissues were excised for ex vivo fluorescence imaging validation. Animal protocols were approved by the University of Maryland, College Park IACUC, which has been fully accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC) since 2011 with PHS Assurance Number D16-00172 and USDA Certificate Number 51-R-0095.

Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of TPMAL (PIC-Nal-CF and PIC-Nal-IRI)

Fluorescence images of TPMAL in excised tissues were acquired using a reflectance fluorescent microscope, with the lasers and filters adjusted to either image CF or BPD. A 450-nm laser (PL450B, Thorlabs, Newton, NJ) at a power of 1 mW and a 685-nm laser diode (HL6750MG, Thorlabs, Newton, NJ) at a power of 10 mW were used to excite the CF and BPD, respectively. The light from the laser was then collimated and projected on the tissue of interest. The emitted fluorescence was then collected through another objective lens. The fluorescent light then passed through a 520-nm (FF01-520/35-25, Semrock, West Henrietta, NY) and 735-nm (FF01-735/28-25, Semrock, West Henrietta, NY) filter to capture the light emitted by CF and BPD, respectively. The remaining filtered fluorescent light was collected by a 12-bit charge-coupled device camera (EM-CCD, PCO, Kelheim, Lower Bavaria, Germany). All images were processed using ImageJ [National Institutes of Health (NIH)] and MATLAB (Natick, MA).

Intraperitoneal PIT using TPMAL (PIC-Nal-IRI) and chemotherapy in peritoneal carcinomatosis mouse models

Intraperitoneal PIT was performed using the ML7710 system on day 6 after tumor inoculation. Tumor-bearing animals were randomized into the following groups: (i) no treatment, (ii) PIT, (iii) FGLD PIT, (iv) chemotherapy (cisplatin-paclitaxel cocktail), (v) PIT + chemotherapy, and (vi) FGLD PIT + chemotherapy. Animals were intraperitoneally injected with TPMAL (1 mg/kg BPD, 5 mg/kg irinotecan) 24 hours before treatment. Before PIT, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and secured in a dorsally recumbent position. Two 14-gauge catheters, fixed at 4- to 5-mm distance apart, were inserted through the abdominal wall. Two 1-cm cylindrical light–diffusing fibers (RD10, Medlight) were inserted into the peritoneal cavity through the catheters for fluorescence measurement of TPMAL and PIT immediately following intraperitoneal administration of 0.1% Intralipid. Fluorescence measurements of TPMAL (excitation: 445 nm, 50 mW/cm; emission: 500 to 800 nm, integration time: 1000 ms) were carried out before PIT (to establish a baseline TPMAL delivery) and after PIT (to assess the degree of photosensitizer photobleaching and inform FGLD) as described above. To perform PIT, red light (690 nm, 150 mW/cm, 25 J/cm) was used to photoactivate TPMAL in each abdominal quadrant of the mouse to achieve a total light dose of 100 J/cm per mouse. Animals randomized in the FGLD PIT group received an additional 25 J/cm (for total cavity irradiation) light dose escalation(s) and repeated fluorescence measurements until at least 70% photosensitizer photobleaching had been achieved. Mice received two cycles of intraperitoneal chemotherapy (5 mg/kg cisplatin and 3 mg/kg paclitaxel) 3 and 8 days after PIT treatment. At 16 days after tumor inoculation, tissues were resected and weighed at the time of necropsy. The same tissues were collected from healthy animals. Metastatic burden was determined by subtracting the weight of organs from the diseased animals by the weight of the organs from the healthy animals.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out at least in triplicate. Specific tests and number of repeats are indicated in the figure captions. Results are shown with mean ± the standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Reported P values are two-tailed. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) statistical tests and appropriate post hoc analyses were applied to avoid type I errors.
