Antithrombotic Monotherapy or Combination Therapy for Atrial Fibrillation in Stable Coronary Artery Disease: A Meta‐Analysis of Randomized Trials
Authors: Rohin K Reddy, David Koeckerling, Gregory Y H Lip, James P Howard, Yousif Ahmad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn patients with atrial fibrillation and stable coronary artery disease, guidelines recommend oral anticoagulation monotherapy after periods on dual antithrombotic therapy. These recommendations are based on evidence from prematurely terminated randomized controlled trials that were underpowered for bleeding and mortality end points. New data are available, so we conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis to pool the totality of randomized evidence.MethodsThis investigation was preregistered on International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024590457). Systematic searches and data extraction were performed independently and in duplicate for randomized controlled trials enrolling patients with atrial fibrillation and stable coronary artery disease that reported clinical end points. The prespecified coprimary end points were major bleeding and all‐cause death. Prespecified secondary outcomes included all bleeding, cardiovascular death, noncardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, the composite of net adverse cardiovascular events and coronary revascularization. End point data were pooled using fixed and random effect models, to generate hazard ratios (HR) and relative risks and corresponding 95% CIs.ResultsFour randomized controlled trials enrolling 4092 patients were eligible, with 2049 randomized to monotherapy and 2043 randomized to dual therapy. Monotherapy reduced the hazard of major bleeding by 41% (HR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.44–0.79], P<0.001, I 2=29%). There was no statistically significant difference in hazard of all‐cause death (HR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.45–1.74], P=0.73, I 2=72%), or other secondary outcomes.ConclusionsIn patients with stable coronary artery disease and atrial fibrillation, oral anticoagulation monotherapy reduces major bleeding compared with dual antithrombotic therapy. There were no significant differences in all‐cause and cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, adverse cardiovascular events, and coronary revascularization.Keywords: anticoagulation, atrial fibrillation, bleeding, coronary artery disease, randomized controlled trial, meta‐analysisSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation, Percutaneous Coronary Intervention, Coronary Artery Disease, Thrombosis

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

This systematic review and meta‐analysis of 4 randomized trials, including approximately 4000 patients with atrial fibrillation and stable coronary artery disease, demonstrated that single‐agent anticoagulation therapy significantly reduced major bleeding compared with dual antithrombotic therapy, without a statistically significant increase in ischemic events or mortality.

This systematic review and meta‐analysis of 4 randomized trials, including approximately 4000 patients with atrial fibrillation and stable coronary artery disease, demonstrated that single‐agent anticoagulation therapy significantly reduced major bleeding compared with dual antithrombotic therapy, without a statistically significant increase in ischemic events or mortality.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

For most patients with atrial fibrillation and stable coronary artery disease, long‐term anticoagulant monotherapy should be favored over combination therapy with an antiplatelet, in the absence of high ischemic risk or other acute indications.

Patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) presenting with acute coronary syndromes have clear indications for dual antithrombotic therapy (DAT) with oral anticoagulation and an antiplatelet based on data from large, randomized controlled trials (RCTs). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 For patients with AF and stable coronary artery disease (CAD), the evidence is less certain. Pursuing a strategy of single‐agent anticoagulation therapy (SAT) without continuing antiplatelets may reduce bleeding without worse performance with regards to ischemic end points.

International guidelines currently provide varying recommendations for managing antithrombotic therapy in patients with AF and stable CAD, based on moderate levels of evidence. The 2024 European Society of Cardiology guideline for the management of stable CAD provides a Class I, level of evidence B recommendation that patients with long‐term indications for oral anticoagulation (ie, AF) receive lifelong therapeutic warfarin, or preferably a direct oral anticoagulant, in the absence of contraindications. 5 The 2023 joint North American societies’ guideline gives a Class 2b, level of evidence C‐Limited Data recommendation for direct oral anticoagulant monotherapy for patients with stable CAD requiring anticoagulation, if there is no acute indication for concomitant antiplatelet therapy. 6

This discrepancy in recommendations reflect a prior paucity of randomized data, with the majority composed of RCTs that were terminated prematurely. One trial evaluating rivaroxaban was halted due to higher mortality in the DAT arm, 7 and another largely evaluating warfarin suffered from slow enrollment. 8 These trials were individually underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences in individual secondary end points. Similarly, prior data may not extend to other commonly used anticoagulants in clinical practice.

New randomized data are available. 9 In this context, we performed a systematic review and meta‐analysis of RCTs testing the efficacy and safety of SAT versus DAT for patients with AF and stable CAD.

METHODS

Data collected from this article may be made available on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Ethics and Study Conduct

This study was performed and reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidance (Table S1), 7 with preregistration on International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024590457). Ethical approval or patient consent was not required as this was a study‐level systematic review and meta‐analysis.

Literature Searches and Inclusion Criteria

MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane databases were systematically searched using terms related to “antiplatelets” OR “anticoagulation” OR “monotherapy” OR “combination therapy” AND “atrial fibrillation” AND “coronary artery disease” with no language restrictions from inception through August 1, 2025 for RCTs reporting clinical end points that compared SAT and DAT in patients with AF and stable CAD. Gray literature was not searched. CAD was considered stable if patients had received coronary revascularization at least 6 months before enrollment or had an angiographically confirmed epicardial stenosis that was managed conservatively. No trials were excluded based on sample size or follow‐up duration. We manually searched bibliographies of previous meta‐analyses, reviews, and conference abstracts from major scientific meetings. Searches and data extraction for study characteristics and end point data were performed by 2 independent, blinded investigators. Disputes were resolved by consensus.

Study End Points

The prespecified coprimary end points were major bleeding and all‐cause death. Major bleeding was defined according to International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis criteria: fatal bleeding; or symptomatic bleeding in a critical area or organ (intracranial, intraspinal, intraocular, retroperitoneal, intra‐articular or pericardial, or intramuscular with compartment syndrome; or bleeding causing a fall in hemoglobin levels of ≥1.24 mmol/L [20 g/L]) or leading to a transfusion of ≥2 units of whole blood or red cells. 10 Prespecified secondary end points included any bleeding, cardiovascular death, noncardiovascular death, myocardial infarction (MI), all stroke, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, systemic embolism, coronary revascularization, and a composite of net adverse cardiovascular events as defined by constituent trials.

Risk of Bias and Certainty of Evidence

Risk of bias was assessed at the study‐level using Cochrane’s Risk of Bias 2 Tool. 11 The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach 12 was applied to assess the certainty of the evidence for the coprimary end points.

Statistical Analysis

Prespecified conventional pairwise meta‐analyses were performed. End points were assessed as hazard ratios (HRs) over the entire follow‐up by extracting study‐level HRs and corresponding 95% CIs. HRs were synthesized by employing inverse‐variance weighted random effects models fitted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation to calculate the heterogeneity variance, τ 2 13 , 14 In sensitivity analyses, fixed effect models were fitted with inverse variance weighting. Separately, event counts reported in 2‐by‐2 tables were extracted and used to calculate pooled relative risks (RR) with corresponding 95% CI at the longest follow‐up, because HRs were not available for all end points. Absolute risks, absolute risk differences, and numbers needed to treat or harm were calculated based on cumulative event rates reported at the end of follow‐up in each trial by transforming pooled relative effects across a range of assumed baseline risks. 15

In additional sensitivity analyses, because several end points had sparse events, we complemented inverse variance weighted RR meta‐analyses with exact methods: an exact Mantel–Haenszel estimator (without continuity correction) and logistic regression (generalized linear mixed model). These approaches do not rely on large‐sample normal approximations, which can be unstable when event rates are low, and therefore provide more robust estimates under sparse data conditions.

To investigate the influence of each trial on the coprimary end points, leave‐one‐out sensitivity analyses were performed. The I 2 statistic was calculated to assess the proportion of variance not explained by chance alone. To directly quantify the presence of interstudy heterogeneity, Cochrane’s Q test was performed and τ 2 was reported.

The presence of small‐study effects was assessed graphically using funnel plots and statistically using linear regression. 16 All analyses were performed according to the intention‐to‐treat principle. Hypothesis testing was performed at the 2‐tailed 5% significance level. R (version 4.3.1) was used for all analyses.

RESULTS

Initial searches identified 902 records, of which 826 were excluded following abstract screening (Figure S1). Out of these records, 76 full‐text records were evaluated, and 72 were not RCTs that fulfilled the prespecified inclusion criteria. The 4 RCTs eligible for this meta‐analysis were OAC‐ALONE (Optimizing Antithrombotic Care in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation and Coronary Stent), 8 AFIRE (Atrial Fibrillation and Ischemic Events With Rivaroxaban in Patients With Stable Coronary Artery Disease), 7 PRAEDO‐AF (Prospective Randomized Study of Safety Outcomes Treated With Edoxaban in Patients With Stable CAD and Atrial Fibrillation), 17 and EPIC‐CAD (Edoxaban Versus Edoxaban With Antiplatelet Agent in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation and Chronic Stable Coronary Artery Disease). 9 These trials enrolled 4092 patients, with 2049 randomized to SAT and 2043 randomized to DAT. Across the trials, the mean age of participants ranged from 71.7 to 75.2 years and in each trial, at least three quarters were male. Risk of Bias

Three trials were at low risk of bias, and the open‐label PRAEDO‐AF 17 was judged to have some concerns, as it was unclear whether outcome adjudication was performed by blinded assessors (Figure S2).

GRADE Assessment

The certainty of the evidence for major bleeding was high and moderate for all‐cause death, due to inconsistency.

Primary End Points

SAT reduced the hazard of major bleeding by 41% (HR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.44–0.79], P<0.001, I 2=29%; RR, 0.57 [95% CI, 0.38–0.87], P=0.007, I 2=50%). There were 24 fewer major bleeding events per 1000 with SAT (95% CI, 35 fewer to 7 fewer) (Table S2). Forty‐two (95% CI, 29–143) was the number of participants needed to treat with SAT in order to prevent 1 major bleeding event. There was no difference in the hazard of all‐cause death (HR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.45–1.74], P=0.73, I 2=72%; RR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.50–1.77], P=0.84, I 2=68%). Forest plots are presented in Figure 1.

Secondary End Points

SAT reduced the hazard of any bleeding by 45% compared with DAT (HR, 0.55 [95% CI, 0.46–0.65], P<0.001, I 2=0%). There were no statistically significant differences in cardiovascular death (RR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.49–1.65], P=0.74, I 2=38%), noncardiovascular death (RR, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.40–1.86], P=0.70, I 2=50.7), MI (RR, 1.57 [95% CI, 0.79–3.12], P=0.20, I 2=0%), ischemic stroke (RR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.57–1.36], P=0.57, I 2=0%), hemorrhagic stroke (RR, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.23–1.14], P=0.10, I 2=1.6%), systemic embolism (RR, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.24–4.23], P=1.00, I 2=0%), net adverse cardiovascular events (HR, 0.68 [95% CI, 0.40–1.17], P=0.16, I 2=86%), or coronary revascularization (RR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.46–1.50], P=0.54, I 2=0%). Small‐Study Effects

There was no strong visual evidence of funnel plot asymmetry (Figure S5) or statistical evidence for small‐study effects (P Egger=0.36 for major bleeding, P Egger=0.57 for all‐cause death) that would indicate bias on the coprimary end points. These tests, however, have limited power when the number of studies included in a meta‐analysis is <10, so should not be taken as conclusive evidence supporting the absence of small‐study effects.

Sensitivity Analyses

Fixed effect meta‐analyses yielded similar point estimates to the random effects models, but CIs were wider in the random effects models, suggesting greater statistical uncertainty after heterogeneity of the treatment effect was incorporated (Figures S3 and S4). Leave‐one‐out analyses were similar in direction and magnitude. The results of the analyses employing exact methods to calculate pooled relative risks were similar to the main analyses and are reported in Table S3.

DISCUSSION

This meta‐analysis of 4 RCTs enrolling 4092 participants with stable CAD and AF shows that SAT reduces the hazard of major bleeding by 41% compared with DAT, with no statistically significant difference in the hazard of all‐cause death. All secondary end points including cardiovascular and noncardiovascular death, MI, stroke‐related end points, net adverse cardiovascular events, and coronary revascularization were statistically similar between the 2 therapeutic strategies, although the number of patients and events was relatively low compared with previous trials including mixtures of participants with acute coronary syndromes and stable CAD that tested different antithrombotic regimens. 18 This resulted in CIs that included clinically important effects with respect to both benefit and harm with SAT, compared with DAT.

The present study has clinical relevance. Major bleeding in patients with AF portends a poor outcome and is associated with death. 19 In clinical practice, physicians often withhold anticoagulation in patients with bleeding events due to the perception of increased future bleeding risk. Discontinuation in this scenario is associated with high mortality, though resuming anticoagulation post bleed greatly lowers this risk. 18 The results of this study should provide reassurance that anticoagulant monotherapy is safe and should probably represent the standard of care for most patients with AF and stable CAD, in the absence of contraindications or in those with high ischemic risk, who were not well represented among constituent trials. Similarly, the average CHA₂DS₂‐VASc for enrolled participants was ~4 across the trials, so results may not apply to those with higher risk of stroke. In the absence of high‐quality evidence for these patients, shared clinical decision‐making between patients and their physicians is still required.

The initial European Society of Cardiology 2010 guideline 20 recommending SAT long term after percutaneous coronary intervention in patients with AF was based on RCTs from the pre‐stent era that demonstrated warfarin alone, or warfarin alongside aspirin, provided hard cardiovascular outcome benefit following MI, relative to aspirin alone. 21 , 22 A large observational nationwide cohort study from Denmark provided further evidence supporting these recommendations, demonstrating that in patients with AF and stable CAD, adding antiplatelet therapy to warfarin increased bleeding, without reduction in risk of recurrent coronary or thromboembolic events. 23 Around the same time, direct oral anticoagulants were introduced into the treatment armamentarium recommended for SAT by subsequent clinical guidelines due to RCTs showing similar protection against MI with direct oral anticoagulants and warfarin. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

The concept of deescalating antithrombotic therapies to reduce bleeding while preserving protection against ischemic events was first introduced by large RCTs that tested the efficacy and safety of triple therapy (oral anticoagulation, P2Y12 inhibition, and aspirin) versus DAT up to 1 year following percutaneous coronary intervention. 1 , 4 , 29 These 3 studies consistently demonstrated that DAT reduced bleeding and was comparably efficacious to triple therapy. The trials included in this meta‐analysis now similarly show a reduction in major bleeding with SAT, with equivalent efficacy end points, which provides further evidence in support of class I recommendations for SAT in patients with stable CAD and AF. 7 , 8 , 9 , 17

This analysis has limitations, some of which suggest further avenues for future clinical investigation. All trials enrolled patients with CAD, and although most strokes associated with AF are presumed to be cardioembolic, competing causes of stroke exist, including carotid artery disease and intracranial vessel stenosis, which are more common in Asian populations. Whether these data translate to patients with stable disease in other vascular territories such as the peripheral, aortic, or carotid circulations is unknown. 30 There also remain no randomized data regarding the impact of SAT therapy in non‐East Asian populations with stable CAD and AF. This is important as differences exist in bleeding and ischemic risk between East Asian and non‐East Asians and results are not necessarily readily generalizable across populations. 31 , 32 Another important aim for future trials in this space is to ensure more female participants are enrolled, as the majority included in this analysis were male. Additionally, there were only 2 stent thrombosis events included in this meta‐analysis, likely secondary to formal exclusion of patients with prior stent thromboses in 2 constituent trials. 7 , 8 Consequently, this analysis is underpowered to draw any firm conclusions regarding increased risk of stent thrombosis with SAT following withdrawal of antiplatelet medication and future trials enrolling higher ischemic risk populations are required to better understand this phenomenon. Similarly, the safety and efficacy of prescribing apixaban or dabigatran as SAT remain unknown and require further study.

The AQUATIC (Assessment of Quitting Versus Using Aspirin Therapy in Patients Treated With Oral Anticoagulation for Atrial Fibrillation or Other Indication With Stabilized Coronary Artery Disease) trial is an ongoing double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, multicenter RCT testing SAT versus DAT in patients with stable CAD and AF at high residual risk of recurrent coronary and vascular events. 33 AQUATIC is aiming to recruit 2000 patients who are at least 12 months out from a previous acute coronary syndromes or percutaneous coronary intervention across 51 sites in France, and the choice of anticoagulation is at the discretion of the investigator, which should result in inclusion of participants randomly allocated to apixaban and dabigatran SAT. This study may go some way toward addressing some of the remaining open questions in this area.

Finally, regarding limitations, as an aggregate data meta‐analysis, there is inherent variability between constituent trials with regard to demographics, geography, outcome definitions and follow‐up, which can introduce heterogeneity into pooled analyses. Specifically, although leave‐one‐out sensitivity analyses did not identify undue influence of a single trial that drove the pooled estimates, differences in follow‐up duration (which ranged between 1 and 2.5 years) across the included trials could have affected our results. Trials with shorter follow‐up are less likely to capture late events such as death or recurrent ischemic complications, potentially underestimating these risks, whereas early hazards such as periprocedural bleeding may be disproportionately represented. Conversely, longer trials naturally accumulate more bleeding and mortality events, which inflates absolute risk estimates even if relative hazards are comparable. Such variability complicates interpretation of pooled absolute risks and numbers needed to treat or harm and may also contribute to time‐dependent heterogeneity due to evolution of treatment effects over follow‐up. An individual participant data meta‐analysis would help to harmonize end points and follow‐up duration, enable detailed investigation of time‐varying effects of randomization and identification of baseline predictors associated with end points of interest. Importantly, this would allow for a deeper understanding of heterogeneity across trials through careful, prespecified testing for treatment‐by‐covariate interactions to assess for biologically plausible effect modification. Importantly, the prespecified coprimary end points in this study were not affected by outcome heterogeneity: major bleeding was defined according to International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis criteria 8 and all‐cause death is resistant to misclassification. Finally, some end points had 0 or low event counts, which may limit the large‐scale approximations used with traditional synthesis techniques used in meta‐analysis. To ensure our pooled estimates were reliable, we did additional analyses to ensure reliability in the context of sparse outcomes data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in patients with AF and stable CAD receiving anticoagulation monotherapy, the totality of the current randomized evidence base shows statistically significant reductions in major bleeding with low heterogeneity. Although there were no significant differences in hazard of death or adverse cardiovascular events across 4 RCTs, CIs encompassed potentially important treatment effects with respect to both benefit and harm.
