Multi-scale fractures and faults in tight reservoirs: from characterization to engineering applications
Authors: Hu Li, Qingchao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMulti-scale fractures and fault systems in tight reservoirs are key controls on hydrocarbon accumulation and migration. They also provide the geological basis for effective hydraulic fracturing. The seven papers in this collection address cutting-edge topics. These include high-precision fracture and fault characterization, geomechanical response mechanisms, and natural–hydraulic fracture coupling. The studies employ multi-scale methods, from 3D reconstruction of micron-scale fractures to stress perturbation modeling of regional faults. Together, they highlight the need for multidisciplinary integration in multi-scale fracture and fault research. This editorial summarizes recent advances in the field and outlines future directions.Keywords: Tight reservoirs, Multi-scale fractures, Fault analysis, GeomechanicsSubject terms: Geology, Tectonics

Introduction

Tight reservoirs, such as shales, tight sandstones, and carbonates, are a major focus of global hydrocarbon exploration and development1–3. These reservoirs have extremely low porosity and permeability. As a result, hydrocarbon accumulation and flow depend heavily on natural fractures and faults4,5. Natural fractures often form connected networks. They not only provide additional storage space but also greatly enhance reservoir conductivity6. More importantly, the characteristics of natural fractures directly affect hydraulic fracturing performance. They act as the main pathways for hydraulic fracture propagation and determine the stimulated reservoir volume and its uniformity7–10. Therefore, a systematic understanding of multi-scale fractures and faults is essential. It is the foundation for reservoir geological modeling and for achieving efficient development of tight reservoirs.

Research on fractures and faults in tight reservoirs is evolving in two key ways. First, it is moving from “single identification” to “systematic characterization.” Second, it is shifting from “static description” to “dynamic coupling”11,12. In terms of fracture characterization, traditional methods for identifying and predicting fractures continue to improve. Their accuracy has been significantly enhanced by geomechanical modeling and seismic attribute analysis. In addition, the coupling mechanism between natural and hydraulic fractures has become a central scientific issue in fracturing optimization13.

Collection overview

The seven papers published in this collection showcase recent advances in multi-scale fracture research. In the area of fracture characterization and fault stress analysis, Xue et al. developed an evaluation model for CO2 geological storage in deep coal seams. Their model is based on high-pressure CO2 isothermal adsorption experiments. It reveals that free-phase storage dominates in the supercritical region14. Zhang et al. addressed the challenge of identifying fractures in micron-scale computer tomography images of volcanic reservoirs. They developed a hybrid learning framework that integrates random forest with a 2.5D U‑Net + + architecture. This approach enables high-precision 3D reconstruction of micro-fractures and quantitative evaluation of connectivity15. Zhang et al. systematically analyzed how pore–microfracture structures respond to pressure changes across different lithofacies. They found that siliceous lithofacies are more resistant to compaction under overpressure conditions16. Zheng et al. quantitatively revealed how fault dip angle affects in-situ stress under different stress regimes17. In the field of natural–hydraulic fracture coupling and fracability evaluation, Liu et al. used a coupled fluid–solid finite element–discrete fracture model. They simulated hydraulic fracture propagation in naturally fractured reservoirs. Their results show that fracture behavior, whether crossing or being arrested, is controlled by multiple factors. These include the mechanical contrast between the rock matrix and natural fractures, in-situ stress, fracturing fluid viscosity, and injection rate18. Cheng et al. developed a quantitative fracability evaluation model for continental shales. Their model integrates the brittleness index, horizontal principal stress difference coefficient, and fracture toughness. It enables systematic assessment of the fracability of Jurassic shales19. In addition, Leng et al. used grey relational analysis. They revealed a segmented co-evolutionary pattern between organic matter maturation and pore systems20.

Based on the analysis of the seven papers collected in this issue, we believe several common themes and approaches in multi‑scale fracture research deserve special attention.

First, multi‑method integration is emerging as a research paradigm for fracture characterization and evaluation. A single technique can hardly capture the full complexity of fracture systems. The studies in this collection show that researchers are actively combining two families of methods: experimental techniques (e.g., scanning electron microscopy, X‑ray diffraction, nuclear magnetic resonance, on‑site desorption, low‑temperature N2 adsorption, field‑emission scanning electron microscopy) and artificial intelligence methods21,22 (including classical machine learning, deep learning, unsupervised and semi‑supervised learning, computer vision, and ensemble learning). By leveraging the complementary strengths of these approaches, researchers can better address the complexity of fracture systems.

Second, multi‑scale characterization is gradually forming a systematic technical framework from nano‑ and micro‑scales up to the kilometer scale. Fracture development is inherently scale‑dependent, and single‑scale studies cannot reveal the full picture. Together, the papers in this collection illustrate a multi‑scale characterization chain from “point” (sample scale) to “volume” (basin scale). This chain covers nano‑ to micro‑scale pore structure analysis, micro‑CT 3D reconstruction, sample‑to‑basin cross‑scale reservoir evaluation, and regional fault‑in‑situ stress modeling. Such a systematic framework lays the methodological foundation for cross‑scale parameter transfer and coupled fracture simulations.

Third, geomechanical mechanisms are becoming a core driving logic in fracture research. Incorporating mechanical principles into fracture analysis is key to improving prediction reliability. Several studies in this collection share this common thread: using fluid‑solid coupled models to reveal the interaction between hydraulic and natural fractures, quantitatively analyzing the relationship between fault geometry and the in‑situ stress field, and building fracability evaluation models that integrate brittleness index, horizontal principal stress difference coefficient, and fracture toughness. Together, these studies show that a mechanism‑driven approach is essential for moving fracture research from statistical description toward causal prediction.

Fourth, cross‑domain method sharing is extending the application boundaries of fracture research. Fracture characterization methods are not only central to hydrocarbon exploration but are also serving broader objectives such as carbon storage. The methodological framework established in this collection for assessing CO2 geological storage potential in deep coal seams integrates high‑pressure isothermal adsorption experiments with multiple adsorption models and reveals the dominant role of free‑phase storage in the supercritical region. Notably, the fracture and pore characterization methods underpinning this framework are highly consistent with those used in shale gas and tight gas reservoir evaluation, demonstrating the cross‑domain transferability of fracture research methodologies. As the global “dual carbon” goals advance, fracture characterization techniques will find growing applications in CO2 storage, geothermal development, nuclear waste disposal, and other fields.

Future perspectives and conclusions

Despite recent advances, several challenges still exist in multi-scale fracture research of tight reservoirs. First, integrating fracture information across scales and constructing multi-scale models remain technical bottlenecks. Fractures exhibit strong heterogeneity and scale dependence, from microns (grain boundaries) to kilometers (seismic fault zones). Future work should focus on multi‑scale modeling via multi‑source data assimilation. Integrating core, log, seismic, and dynamic monitoring data will enable a “point‑to‑volume” technical framework23,24.

Second, AI and ML techniques hold great promise for fracture research, but they urgently require high-quality training data and physical constraints25. Current deep learning models for fracture identification rely on limited annotated samples, restricting their generalizability and geological interpretability. Future efforts should prioritize physics‑constrained methods such as physics informed neural networks (PINNs) and the construction of open‑access fracture databases.

Third, the dynamic coupling mechanisms between natural and hydraulic fractures require further investigation26–30. Future work should develop full‑process fracture propagation simulations incorporating thermal‑hydraulic‑mechanical‑chemical coupling. Combined with true triaxial experiments, these approaches can reveal natural–hydraulic fracture interaction mechanisms and support fracturing parameter optimization.

Furthermore, as deep and ultra-deep tight reservoirs become new frontiers in hydrocarbon exploration, the evolution characteristics and engineering responses of fracture systems under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions urgently need systematic investigation31–33. Deep reservoirs undergo complex diagenetic and tectonic evolution. The effectiveness of fractures, in terms of aperture, connectivity, and conductivity, exhibits increasingly complex relationships with the present-day in-situ stress field. Future research should focus on developing in-situ characterization technologies for deep fracture systems, advancing experimental methods for rock mechanics and fracture flow under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions, and establishing fracture effectiveness evaluation and fracability prediction models tailored to deep reservoir conditions.

In summary, multi-scale fractures and faults are a core research topic in tight reservoirs. Their detailed characterization and coupled mechanisms are fundamentally important for understanding hydrocarbon accumulation and guiding development design. The contributions in this Collection present the technological frontiers and multidisciplinary integration trends in this field from various perspectives. We hope these studies will inspire further interdisciplinary collaboration and investigation. Together, they can advance fracture and fault research in tight reservoirs toward greater precision, systematization, and engineering relevance.
