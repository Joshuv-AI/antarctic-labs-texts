Persistent semiquinone radicals enable efficient near-infrared-driven H2O2 photosynthesis
Authors: Shuai Dou, Yaning Zhang, Jing Xu, Ying Zhang, Yang Lou, Jiawei Zhang, Yuming Dong, Yongfa Zhu, Chengsi Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photosynthesis of H2O2 under sunlight is a sustainable method; however, most developed photocatalysts utilize limited near-infrared light, which accounts for 52% of the solar spectrum. In typical near-infrared photocatalysts, excited electrons fall into low-energy sub-gap states, reducing the driving force for H2O2 generation. Here, a polydopamine-loaded porphyrin supramolecular photocatalyst efficiently utilizes near-infrared light for H2O2 production from H2O and O2, achieving an apparent quantum yield of 2.8% at 1020 nm. This substantial near-infrared utilization significantly boosts activity under full-spectrum irradiation, with an H2O2 generation rate of 3.37 mM/h and solar-to-chemical conversion efficiency of 2.2%. Persistent semiquinone radicals in polydopamine are demonstrated to enable ultrafast sub-gap electron transfer (ca. 79 fs) from porphyrin to polydopamine and facilitate near-infrared-driven •OOH radical generation, thereby accelerating H2O2 production. This study sheds light on the potential of near-infrared-responsive photocatalysts and offers insights into optimizing their performance for sustainable H2O2 synthesis.

Introduction

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) has emerged as a prominent green oxidizing agent with widespread applications in medicine, bleaching, and environmental remediation1,2. The current global market for H2O2 is valued at ca. 6 billion dollars, with projections indicating growth to ca. 10 billion dollars by 20303. Traditionally, H2O2 is synthesized industrially via the anthraquinone process, which relies on high-pressure conditions, costly palladium catalysts, and explosive hydrogen gas4. In recent years, photocatalytic methods have gained attention as a sustainable alternative for producing H2O2 using water (H2O), oxygen gas (O2), and sunlight in the presence of suitable photocatalysts. To maximize the utilization of solar energy, the photocatalysts are expected to efficiently harness near-Infrared (NIR, λ > 800 nm) light, which constitutes approximately 52% of the solar spectrum. However, most developed NIR-responsive photocatalysts suffer from significant limitations5–8, including low H2O2 production rate (e.g., ~ 100 μM/h), a narrow NIR-responsive range (λ < 900 nm), and minimal contribution from NIR light (< 10 %) to overall activity under full-spectrum illumination.

Tetrakis(4-carboxyphenyl) porphyrin (TCPP) supramolecule exhibits remarkable NIR photocatalytic activity for H2O2 production through a novel dual-pathway mechanism9. Unlike previously reported photocatalysts, which rely solely on oxygen reduction (ORR) for H2O2 generation, the TCPP supramolecule demonstrates both ORR and water oxidation reduction (WOR) to produce H2O2. Specifically, ORR occurs on the pyrrole ring, while WOR is driven by photo-thermal transitions involving the carboxylic groups and peroxy acid intermediates (Fig. 1a). Notably, a key feature of the TCPP supramolecular photocatalyst is its light absorption capability extending into the NIR region (up to 1100 nm). Under monochromatic NIR light (940 ± 10 nm), SA-TCPP achieves a H2O2 production rate of 0.15 mM h⁻¹, with an apparent quantum yield (AQY) of up to 1.1%. Despite these advancements, the contribution of NIR-driven activity to the overall photocatalytic performance remains limited. In most NIR-responsive photocatalysts, electrons excited by NIR light primarily transition to sub-gap energy levels (trap states)10,11, which possess lower energy than the conduction band, reducing the potential driving force for effective H₂O₂ production (Fig. 1b). Consequently, this inherent energy limitation may hinder the efficient utilization of NIR light in TCPP-based systems. Radicals generated within the photocatalyst framework have been reported to smoothly mediate ORR to H2O2 by facilitating the utilization of electrons in the conduction band12. However, the generation of such reactive radicals typically requires UV or visible excitation. Besides the inherent energy limitation, the high energy barrier for the ORR to H2O2 further constrains the efficient utilization of sub-gap NIR-excited electrons. Thermodynamically, effective ORR requires that the conduction band edge potentials be more negative than the reduction potential of O2 to H2O2 (0.68 V vs. RHE)13. During the actual ORR process, O2 is initially reduced to free or adsorbed •OOH intermediates, followed by the subsequent reduction of these intermediates to H2O2. While the further reduction of •OOH intermediates to H2O2 occurs at potentials around 1 V, their initial formation requires a more negative potential (approximately 0.30 V vs. RHE, based on the average of reported data)14–16. Accordingly, the ability to form •OOH intermediates using NIR-excited electrons occupying sub-gap states plays a crucial role in the efficient utilization of NIR light for H2O2 production.

This study addresses the challenge of utilizing NIR light for H2O2 photosynthesis by integrating polydopamine (PDA), a well-established full-spectrum absorption material17,18, onto self-assembled TCPP porphyrin supermolecules (SA-TCPP). Although PDA has been employed to promote the photocatalytic H2O2 production19, its use to enhance NIR-driven H2O2 generation remains rare, despite its broad solar-spectrum absorption. Moreover, while radical-mediated fast electron transfer has been reported12, the utilization of the persistent semiquinone radicals intrinsically present in PDA to mediate the transfer of low-energy, NIR-excited sub-bandgap electrons remains poorly understood.

Here, we investigate H2O2 generation from only H2O and O2 using PDA/SA-TCPP composite photocatalysts, demonstrating that NIR-driven ORR contributes nearly 30% to the overall activity. By enhancing NIR utilization, the overall H2O2 generation rate reaches 3.37 mM h−1, with a solar-to-chemical conversion efficiency (SCC) of 2.2%. We elucidate the mechanism by which persistent semiquinone radicals in PDA facilitate rapid transfer of NIR-excited sub-gap electrons in SA-TCPP, promoting the formation of •OOH radicals and H2O2. This work establishes persistent semiquinone radicals as effective electron shuttles for driving NIR-excited sub-gap ORR-to-H2O2 conversion, providing guidance for the design of efficient organic photocatalysts for scalable solar H2O2 production under sunlight.

Results

Theoretical feasibility of semiquinone-mediated sub-gap ORR of SA-TCPP under NIR light

PDA is synthesized via a self-polymerization method using dopamine monomers. The structural composition of the prepared PDA, shown in Fig. 1c, includes dihydroxyindole (hydroquinone-type, QH2) and indoledione (quinone-type, Q) units with varying degrees of (un)saturation. This structural characterization is confirmed through the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), Raman, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Figs. 1–3) measurements, consistent with findings reported in the literature19,20. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images in Supplementary Fig. 4 reveal that the prepared PDA exhibits a nanoparticle morphology, with particle sizes ranging from 50 to 250 nm. Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) is further employed to elucidate the PDA structure. A distinct signal at g = 2.0035 with a line width of 5.5 G is observed for the PDA powder. (Supplementary Fig. 5) This is attributed to the presence of substantial persistent semiquinone radicals (SQ•), consistent with previous reports21,22.

Ultraviolet-Visible-Near Infrared diffuse reflection spectrum (UV-Vis-NIR DRS) in Supplementary Fig. 6 shows the absorption band edge of the prepared PDA extends up to 1400 nm, attributed to the stacking of indole conjugated structural units. This absorption profile together with the band structure analyses (Supplementary Fig. 6) demonstrates a large overlap with the solar spectrum, encompassing not only the visible light region but also the NIR region, indicating the strong potential of PDA for NIR-light responsiveness.

To further explore the theoretical ORR pathway for H2O2 production on PDA and verify the NIR utilization, Gibbs free energy calculations are performed (Fig. 1c). The formation energy of SQ• radicals from QH2 and Q is only 0.38 eV, indicating that the generation of SQ• radicals is highly favorable and supports their persistent presence within the PDA structure. Comparable formation energies (0.2–0.5 eV) have also been reported for other SQ• systems23. Both the reaction barrier for SQ• radical generation from QH2/Q and the subsequent O2 addition to SQ• to form adsorbed Q*OOH are lower than the energy provided by NIR photons(energy < 1.55 eV), indicating that these steps are energetically accessible under NIR excitation. However, the formation of H2O2 requires the desorption of the *OOH intermediate followed by its reaction with a neighboring SQ• or QH2 unit. In contrast, the subsequent desorption of the *OOH intermediate and its conversion to H₂O₂ via reaction with neighboring SQ• or QH2 units involves substantially higher activation barriers (TS3-1,2) that exceed the energy accessible under NIR excitation, rendering these steps kinetically unfavorable. EPR measurements are conducted for PDA to examine its NIR-light driven •OOH generation from ORR. The experiment is conducted in the dark and under NIR light irradiation within an anoxic environment, using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as the •OOH radical trapper (Fig. 1d). The anoxic environment is specifically used to slow down the O2 reduction on PDA, allowing the accumulation of intermediates. In the dark, PDA exhibits negligible signals of •OOH radicals, indicating that O2 is not spontaneously reduced on PDA. In contrast, a distinct semiquinone radical (SQ•) is observed, consistent with the EPR signals for powders (Supplementary Fig. 5). With NIR irradiation, adduct EPR signals are observed. The signals consist of the SQ• radical adduct, with an intensity 2.6 times as high as that in the dark, along with newly emerged •OOH radicals, as confirmed by simulation results (details in Supplementary Fig. 7). The result suggests •OOH radicals are generated from 1 electron transfer from SQ• radicals to O2. Previous studies have demonstrated that SQ• radicals exhibit high reaction rate constants with O2 (106 ~ 108)24,25, further supporting our observations.

In situ attenuated total reflectance infrared absorption (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy is employed to investigate the NIR-light response of the prepared PDA. The experimental setup is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 8, incorporating a silicon (Si) prism as the IR-transparent element. The absorption changes, determined by subtracting the baseline signal of the pristine PDA surface, provide insights into the surface reactions of PDA with H2O and O2 under NIR irradiation. Under NIR light irradiation (λ > 800 nm), distinct vibrational peaks at 1112 cm⁻¹ and 842 cm⁻¹ are observed. They are attributed to stretching vibrations of the adsorbed •OOH radicals and O-O in the radicals (Supplementary Table S1). Additional in-situ FTIR observations with 18O2 isotopic labeling further confirm that the observed peak changes belong to •OOH radicals generated onto the PDA surface (Supplementary Fig. 9). Control experiments with different light wavelength cutoff filters verify that this PDA-induced generation of •OOH radicals enables substantial NIR utilization with the light wavelength extending up to 1020 nm (Supplementary Fig. 10).

With increasing irradiation time from 0 to 30 min (Fig. 1e), the intensity of the two peaks at 1112 cm⁻¹ and 842 cm⁻¹ increases. However, after the light is turned off, the intensity of these peaks gradually diminishes to nearly negligible levels, indicating that NIR light-induced •OOH generation is highly reversible. Furthermore, the prepared PDA exhibits negligible H2O2 generation under NIR irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 11), suggesting that the generated •OOH radicals cannot be further reduced, reinforcing the high reversibility of •OOH generation under NIR light. Under NIR light irradiation, the QH2 and Q moieties may initially interact to increase the amount of persistent SQ• radicals, which subsequently react with O2 to form •OOH radicals and regenerate Q moieties (as supported by FTIR and 13C solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) results in Supplementary Figs. 12, 13).

While prior studies reported the H2O2 generation on PDA alone (0.5-1.0 mM h−1 g−1) via •OOH intermediates under sunlight irradiation26, the current work highlights the facile generation of •OOH radicals on PDA under NIR irradiation. Although PDA cannot directly utilize NIR light to produce H2O2, the efficient generation of •OOH radicals on it under NIR irradiation remains crucial for enabling NIR-driven H2O2 production. In particular, in the case of NIR-driven H₂O₂ generation on SA-TCPP, •OOH radical formation with the excited sub-gap electrons represents a key rate-limiting step (Fig. 1b). Therefore, the facile generation of •OOH radicals presents a promising strategy for enhancing the efficiency of NIR-driven sub-gap H2O2 production on SA-TCPP.

The band structure of SA-TCPP is investigated using UV-Vis-NIR DRS to assess the potential for charge transfer from its sub-bandgap states to PDA. As shown in Fig. 1f, SA-TCPP displays a strong absorption band around 730 nm and a weak Urbach tail extending from 730 to 1400 nm. These features correspond to a bandgap energy (Eg) of 1.77 eV and an Urbach sub-bandgap energy (EUrbach) of 1.21 eV (Supplementary Fig. 14). The Urbach energy is typically attributed to trap states within the SA-TCPP structure, which may arise from structural distortions, impurities, and lattice imperfections associated with its amorphous nature (as supported by the XRD results in Supplementary Fig. 15). The relationship between Eg and EUrbach is illustrated in the inset of Fig. 1f. These trap states are generally distributed both above the valence band maximum (VBM) and below the conduction band minimum (CBM). Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements determine the VBM and the trap state level above the VBM to be 1.79 eV and 1.51 eV, respectively. By subtracting Eg and EUrbach from these values, the CBM and the trap state level below the CBM are calculated to be 0.02 eV and 0.30 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 14).

It is noteworthy that in SA-TCPP, the electrons excited by NIR light predominantly accumulate at the trap state level located below the conduction band minimum (CBM), with a potential of 0.30 V vs. RHE. This value is only 0.02 V lower than the reduction potential of O2 to •OOH radicals (0.32 V), indicating a minimal driving force and, consequently, limited utilization of NIR light for •OOH radical generation. In contrast, PDA exhibits a more favorable energetic alignment: the photon energy of NIR light (< 1.55 eV) is substantially higher than the energy barrier (0.51 eV) required for •OOH radical generation (Fig. 1c), enabling efficient 1-electron transfer from persistent semiquinone (SQ•) radicals to O2. Furthermore, the quinone moieties formed in PDA during this process possess a redox potential (Q/SQ•) of 0.52 V vs. RHE (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17), which is 0.22 V positive relative to the trap state level in SA-TCPP (0.30 V vs. RHE). This favorable potential difference suggests that the transfer of sub-gap electrons from SA-TCPP to PDA is thermodynamically viable. Therefore, coupling SA-TCPP with PDA may enhance NIR light utilization through efficient sub-gap electron transfer, as illustrated in Fig. 1g.

Boosting H2O2 Generation under NIR Light by loading PDA on SA-TCPP Photocatalysts

To prepare PDA and SA-TCPP composite photocatalysts (PDA/SA-TCPP), an appropriate amount of the prepared PDA nanoparticles is introduced during the self-assembly process used for fabricating TCPP nanosheets.

In the composite structure, small PDA nanoparticles ranging in size from 50 nm to 250 nm with a thickness of ca. 20–80 nm (Supplementary Fig. 18) are predominantly dispersed onto SA-TCPP nanosheets (Supplementary Fig. 19). Figure 2c presents the FTIR spectrum of PDA/SA-TCPP, alongside the spectra of SA-TCPP and PDA recorded as controls to investigate the formation of PDA loading onto the SA-TCPP nanosheet. Owing to the presence of similar functional groups in both compounds, most peaks in the spectra of PDA and SA-TCPP overlap. Nevertheless, three distinct peaks at 1693 cm⁻¹, 1399 cm⁻¹, and 1176 cm⁻¹ are exclusively attributed to SA-TCPP with minimal interference from PDA. Specifically, the peaks at 1693 cm⁻¹ and 1176 cm⁻¹ correspond to the stretching vibrations of C = O and C-O in the carboxyl groups27, respectively, while the peak at 1399 cm⁻¹ is assigned to C = C stretching vibrations in the pyrrole ring of the porphyrin structure28. Upon PDA loading, the peaks associated with the carboxyl groups shift by 17 cm⁻¹ and 9 cm⁻¹ to lower wavenumbers, whereas the peaks associated with the porphyrin core remain unchanged. This shift indicates the formation of hydrogen bonds between the SA-TCPP and PDA29.

The formation of hydrogen bonds between PDA and SA-TCPP is further supported by the UV-Vis-NIR DRS spectra. As shown in Fig. 2d, pure PDA has a strong and broad absorption spectrum up to 1500 nm, and the Soret band (corresponding to the S0 → S2 transition) of SA-TCPP appears at 357 nm and redshifts to 385 nm after PDA loading. This redshift is likely attributed to the formation of edge-to-edge hydrogen bonds between PDA and SA-TCPP, which enhance the transition dipole moment, facilitate exciton splitting, and lower the energy of the S2 state30,31. In addition, isotope-labeled FTIR, solid-state 13C NMR, and XPS measurements further confirm the presence of hydrogen-bond interactions between PDA and SA-TCPP (Supplementary Figs. 20, 21). The hydrogen-bonding is expected to contribute to the stabilization of PDA on the SA-TCPP nanosheet during the catalytic reaction

Under the NIR light (λ > 800 nm) irradiation, PDA/SA-TCPP still achieves a substantial H2O2 generation rate of 0.99 ± 0.30 mM/h in the first hour, with 3.30 ± 0.26 mM accumulated after 4 h. The H2O2 generation continues to increase with reaction time, which is different from the observations for PDA alone where negligible H2O2 generation is detected (Supplementary Fig. 12). In addition, loading PDA onto pre-assembled SA-TCPP nanosheets yielded a photocatalytic H2O2 generation activity comparable to that of the in-situ self-assembled PDA/SA-TCPP system (Supplementary Fig. 23), indicating that the enhanced activity does not originate from the incorporation of PDA during the self-assembly process. By contrast, a physical mixture of PDA and SA-TCPP is prepared, which exhibited less than half the activity of PDA/SA-TCPP (Supplementary Fig. 24). These results clearly indicate that the integration of SA-TCPP with PDA enables efficient NIR photon utilization for H2O2 production in this photocatalytic system.

Figure 3b depicts the AQY of PDA/SA-TCPP measured at different wavelengths (400–1020 nm). In the visible light region (400–800 nm), the average AQY of PDA/SA-TCPP is 22.3 ± 0.2% at 420 nm (Supplementary Fig. 25), which is 1.9 times higher than that of SA-TCPP and competitive among most reported values for H2O2 generation from H2O and O2 (Supplementary Table S2). In the NIR region, the AQY values of PDA/SA-TCPP at 850 nm, 940 nm, and 1020 nm are 7.1 ± 0.1%, 4.8 ± 0.1%, and 2.8 ± 0.1%, respectively, (Supplementary Figs. 26, 27), approximately 3.0–4.0 times as high as those of SA-TCPP (1.6% at 850 nm, 0.87% at 940 nm, and N/A at 1020 nm), which are competitive among most reported NIR light-responsive photocatalysts (Supplementary Table S3). The AQY results confirm that the enhanced photocatalytic activity is attributed to the improved NIR photon utilization enabled by PDA loading.

Figure 3c compares the H2O2 generation rates of PDA/SA-TCPP and SA-TCPP under Xe lamp irradiation with different cutoff filters (λ > 420 nm, 420 nm < λ < 800 nm, and λ > 800 nm) to better understand the contribution of NIR light utilization to overall H2O2 generation. Under visible and NIR irradiation (λ > 420 nm), the H2O2 generation rate of the PDA/SA-TCPP (3.37 ± 0.12 mM/h) is ca. 2.5 times higher than that of the SA-TCPP alone (1.35 ± 0.08 mM/h). Under NIR-only irradiation (λ > 800 nm), PDA/SA-TCPP achieves a rate of 0.99 ± 0.06 mM/h, nearly 7.1 times higher than SA-TCPP (0.14 ± 0.03 mM/h), and contributes about 29% to the total activity under λ > 420 nm irradiation, indicating a substantial enhancement in NIR photon conversion efficiency. By contrast, the enhancement under visible light alone (420 nm < λ < 800 nm) is relatively modest at 1.9 times.

Figure 3d compares the long-term H2O2 accumulation between PDA/SA-TCPP, SA-TCPP, and PDA photocatalysts. Leveraging its superior NIR utilization, which also helps minimize H2O2 decomposition, PDA/SA-TCPP accumulates 52.2 mM H2O2 (0.16 wt%) after 24 h irradiation, which is 2.6 times higher than SA-TCPP alone and 130 times relative to pure PDA. It is worth noting that higher H2O2 generation activity on PDA through chemical (thermal-driven) or photochemical pathways has been reported26. However, such reactions predominantly occur under neutral or alkaline conditions. Under the acidic conditions used in this study, the H2O2 production via chemical and photochemical pathways are determined to be negligible compared with the H2O2 production observed for PDA/SA-TCPP (Supplementary Figs. 28−31). According to the above, the integration of PDA with SA-TCPP significantly enhances overall photocatalytic H2O2 production under broad-spectrum light, primarily due to improved NIR light utilization.

Rapid sub-gap electron transfer enabling efficient NIR Utilization

The NIR photocatalytic activity enhancement mechanism for PDA/SA-TCPP is studied next. In the PDA/SA-TCPP system, the peroxy acid intermediate—similar to that in SA-TCPP—is still observed under irradiation by comparative 13C NMR, 1H NMR, TOF-MS, and FTIR analyses of PDA/SA-TCPP and SA-TCPP after room temperature reaction. (Supplementary Fig. 32) The similarity of the spectra excludes hole transfer from SA-TCPP to PDA, which indicates that holes remain on SA-TCPP. Further scavenger experiments (Supplementary Fig. 33) suggest the contribution of holes to the NIR-driven H2O2 production is nearly half similar as we reported in the SA-TCPP. KPFM measurements (Supplementary Fig. 34) of PDA/SA-TCPP before and after light irradiation further indicate that the photogenerated holes remain predominantly on SA-TCPP rather than transferring to PDA. These accumulated holes may oxidize the carboxyl groups on SA-TCPP to form peroxy acid intermediates (-COOH + 2 h+ + H2O → -CO3H + 2H+). Subsequently, the generated -CO3H undergoes thermal hydrolysis—the primary role of heat throughout the photothermal catalytic process, as supported by the data in Supplementary Figs. 30, 31, thereby regenerating -COOH and yielding H2O2 (-CO3H + H3O → -COOH + H2O2)9. Moreover, PDA loading onto Zn-TCPP, with a similar structure to SA-TCPP, enhances H2O2 generation by eightfold (Supplementary Fig. 35). In-situ XPS shows the electron transfer from SA-TCPP to PDA on light. (Supplementary Fig. 36) The results show that PDA loading promotes electron transfer to accelerate the ORR to H2O2 under visible light, which is consistent with previous studies32,33. However, while this electron transfer explains the activity boost, it does not account for the much greater enhancement under NIR light (7.4 times) compared to visible light (1.9 times).

We then conduct control experiments to elucidate the effect of PDA on the NIR activity enhancement. We employ capacitance measurements, specifically utilizing Mott-Schottky (M-S) curves (Supplementary Fig. S37), both in the dark and under NIR irradiation, to investigate the transfer of NIR light-excited electrons from SA-TCPP to PDA. Generally speaking, in the M-S curves, the slope at low bias reflects the charge density at defect levels, while the one at high bias reflects the charge density in the band34. Supplementary Fig. 37a shows that in SA-TCPP, the slope of the curve at a low bias (−0.1-0.2 V) remains unchanged before and under irradiation, suggesting most NIR-excited electrons at defect levels recombine with photogenerated holes. In contrast, Supplementary Fig. 37b shows that in PDA/SA-TCPP, the slope decreases significantly, accounting for the defect electron density estimated to be 8.1 × 1016 cm-3 and 1.3 × 1017 cm−3 before and under NIR irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 37c). Note that for PDA-alone, little contribution to this variation is observed after NIR irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 37d). The increase of defect electron density for PDA/SA-TCPP after NIR light irradiation indicates more NIR light-excited electrons transfer from trap levels to PDA rather than bulk recombination.

To gain further insight into the charge transfer, femtosecond transient absorption spectra (fs-TAS) of PDA/SA-TCPP and SA-TCPP are recorded. (Fig. 4a, b) The pump wavelength is set at 660 nm (S0 → S1 transition) to more accurately probe electron relaxation and transfer at the trap levels35,36. For SA-TCPP, a ground-state bleaching (GSB) signal appears at 779 nm at 20 fs. At longer delay times ( > 170 fs), SA-TCPP exhibits an excitonic-state absorption (ESA) signal at 792 nm, and by 550 fs, two additional ESA signals emerge at 824 nm and 858 nm through an intersystem crossing (ISC) process37,38. The signals at 779 nm and 792 nm are attributed to the 1T states of SA-TCPP coupling with the electron spin. In contrast, PDA/SA-TCPP displays distinct decay behavior compared to SA-TCPP. The GSB signal shifts to 792 nm. Although trap-state signals also appear at 560 fs, as observed in SA-TCPP, their intensity decreases with increasing delay time. At 640 fs, the peak at 824 nm diminishes, accompanied by the emergence of a new peak at 899 nm. As the delay time further increases to 690 fs, 730 fs, and 790 fs, the trap-state signals continue to decrease, while the 899 nm peak shifts progressively to 944 nm and 1026 nm. This rapid decay likely reflects efficient interfacial electron transfer from SA-TCPP to PDA39. Meanwhile, the overall signal intensity is an order of magnitude stronger than that of SA-TCPP, highlighting the efficient utilization of trap-state electrons enabled by PDA loading.

Transient absorption kinetics at 824 nm are then compared for SA-TCPP and PDA/SA-TCPP to analyze exciton decay dynamics at trap states with and without PDA loading (Fig. 4c). Lifetimes and amplitudes at the fitted wavelengths are summarized in Supplementary Table S4. A bi-exponential model is employed to fit the decay curves, where τ₁ corresponds to polaron formation from excitons and τ₂ to polaron separation, with an additional constant term accounting for recombination processes beyond the measurement window40. For SA-TCPP, τ₁ and τ₂ are determined as 380 fs and 225 ps, respectively. In contrast, PDA/SA-TCPP exhibits much faster dynamics, with τ₁ of 79 fs and τ₂ of 7.14 ps. These results demonstrate that PDA loading drives excitons at the trap states of SA-TCPP to form interfacial polarons instead of bulk polarons. Compared with bulk polaron formation without PDA, interfacial polaron formation is 5-fold faster, and their subsequent dissociation into free charges is 30-fold more efficient. The TRPL results further confirm that the acceleration of charge separation after PDA loading (Supplementary Fig. 38).

A schematic summary of the exciton processes in SA-TCPP and PDA/SA-TCPP together with the calculated absolute energy levels are shown in Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 39. The presence of PDA clearly enhances interfacial charge transfer by forming interfacial polarons. Notably, the transferred electrons in PDA/SA-TCPP exhibit a longer recombination lifetime (> 8 ns) compared with those in SA-TCPP (> 2 ns), as indicated by the offset constants of the TAS signals at 1026 nm and 824 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 40). In PDA/SA-TCPP, the transferred electrons on PDA eventually occupy the LUMO states, where they can be further captured. Since the TAS spectra display sequential ESA features at 899 nm, 944 nm, and finally 1026 nm after PDA loading, the 1026 nm peak is attributed to electrons localized in the PDA LUMO states, while the 899 nm signal likely corresponds to the LUMO + 2 orbitals. These LUMO + 2 states, originating mainly from persistent SQ• radicals (the orange orbital mapping shown in Fig. 4d), facilitate electron transfer from trap states of SA-TCPP probably by providing additional electron-pair energy. The acceleration of charge transfer mediated by the SQ•-related LUMO + 2 states was further supported by subsequent experimental evidence. The involvement of SQ• in ultrafast sub-gap electron transfer is further supported by TAS measurements performed after suppressing SQ• (Supplementary Fig. 41). Upon SQ• suppression, the interfacial trap-electron transfer process is prolonged, ranging from 570 fs to 820 fs, whereas with SQ•, it ranges from 590 fs to 690 fs. The TAS spectra further demonstrate that the trap-electron transfer proceeds via hydrogen-bonding interactions (Supplementary Fig. 42). Moreover, analyses of the interfacial electron density difference, Bader charge, and density of states are consistent with these observations (Supplementary Fig. 43).

To further verify that the trap electrons in SA-TCPP can be more effectively utilized for •OOH radical generation upon PDA loading and elucidate the role of SQ• radicals on H2O2 production, EPR measurements are carried out in air and anoxic conditions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 44, PDA/SA-TCPP exhibits a pronounced •OOH radical signal under NIR irradiation, verified by radical-quenching experiments and isotope-labeling FTIR results (supplementary Figs. 45–47), markedly stronger compared with pristine SA-TCPP. The EPR fitting results together with quantitative H2O2 analysis further demonstrate the effective utilization of trap electrons for •OOH radical and H2O2 production is mediated by SQ• (supplementary Fig. 48). In addition, transient photocurrent responses and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 49 and Table S7) also reveal that PDA loading enhances the transfer of NIR-excited trap electrons from SA-TCPP to PDA. The transferred trap electrons subsequently facilitate the further reduction of •OOH radicals adsorbed on Q moieties of PDA to form C-OOH intermediates (TS-1 in Fig. 1c) and then spontaneously hydrolyze to form H2O2, as confirmed by in situ FTIR and in situ Raman analyses (Supplementary Fig. 50). Because this pathway avoids the desorption of •OOH radicals from Q moieties (Fig. 1c), the electron transfer for their further reduction will be more favorable than the direct reduction of •OOH radicals in PDA alone. Also note that although the formation of •OOH and H2O2 may consume SQ• by converting it to Q, subsequent electron transfer from SA-TCPP efficiently regenerates SQ•, as illustrated by the quantitative EPR analysis before and after the extended reaction in Supplementary Fig. 51. According to the above discussions, the complete H2O2 production process mediate by SQ• radicals is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 52.

The enhancement of NIR utilization by combining PDA with persistent SQ• radicals is also verified in additional systems. Four NIR-adsorption photocatalysts41,42, including Bi12CoO20, Black-TiO2, (Tetracarboxyphthalocyaninato) cobalt (II), and Zn-TCPP, are selected as model systems. Upon PDA loading, each material exhibits an approximately 2 ~ 8-fold increase in photocatalytic activity compared with its pristine counterpart, demonstrating the general applicability of the PDA modification strategy. (Supplementary Fig. 53).

H2O2 generation on PDA/SA-TCPP photocatalysts under simulated and real sunlight

Encouraged by the high NIR-driven H2O2 production originating from rapid charge transfer from the trap states of SA-TCPP to PDA, we further evaluated the performance of PDA/SA-TCPP under both simulated and real sunlight. Figure 5a shows the H2O2 generation activity of PDA/SA-TCPP when the reaction suspension is irradiated and heated solely under simulated sunlight (AM 1.5 G). The stability of PDA/SA-TCPP is then examined. In ten consecutive cycles (40 h in total), an average of 3.76 mM H2O2 is produced per cycle without any observable activity decay. (Fig. 5b) FTIR spectra and SEM images after the cycling reaction confirm negligible morphological and structural change in PDA/SA-TCPP before and after the stability tests (Supplementary Fig. 56). Long-term operation under AM 1.5 G illumination for three extended cycles (72 h, Supplementary Fig. 57) also demonstrated stable performance, with each cycle yielding 14.67 mM H₂O₂. Motivated by this durability and efficiency, we further evaluated H₂O₂ generation using a lab-built reaction–evaporation device (Supplementary Fig. 58). Remarkably, the device produced H2O2 at a concentration of 1.6 wt% within 10 h, reaching a commercially relevant level.

Next, we perform outdoor tests under natural sunlight for H2O2 generation in the air to further confirm its robustness. For three consecutive days using the same photocatalyst, the PDA/SA-TCPP powder achieves a SCC efficiency of 1.81% in average, slightly lower than that obtained under laboratory conditions due to solar intensity and solution temperature (Supplementary Fig. 59 and Table 8). Moreover, the photocatalyst film is cast onto a Petri dish with a diameter of 10.8 cm. In particular, the PDA/SA-TCPP powder is ultrasonically dispersed in ethanol, and the resulting suspension is poured into the Petri dish and dried at room temperature to obtain a film. The film exhibits good adhesion to the Petri dish substrate and doesn’t peel off during the outdoor tests. After 1 h reaction under ambient air with simultaneous heating and irradiation by the real sunlight, the H2O2 generation amount still reaches above 1 mM, and the corresponding SCC efficiency is ca. 0.12% for this large area film (Fig. 5c). The obtained activity remains relatively high among reported supported systems (Supplementary Table 9). The accumulation amount reaches ca. 4 mM, and the SCC efficiency remains essentially unchanged during an additional 3 h of operation, with the solution temperature stabilizing at ca. 333.15 K. The SCC efficiency also surpasses the average solar-to-biomass conversion efficiency of natural photosynthesis in plants (0.1-1.0%), highlighting the potential of PDA/SA-TCPP for practical solar-driven H2O2 production43–45.

Given the excellent photocatalytic H2O2 production activity of PDA/SA-TCPP, an integrated system for in-situ H2O2 generation and pollutant degradation is designed, as illustrated in Fig. 5d. Representative pollutants, including methylene blue (MB) and ciprofloxacin (CIP), are employed to evaluate the removal efficiency of the system. In the device, the concentration of in-situ produced H2O2 reaches a stable level of 2.1 ± 0.2 mM during the reaction at a flow speed of 2 mL/min. Within 12 min, nearly 95% of both pollutants are removed, corresponding to a degradation rate of 0.23 min−1 and 0.21 min−1, respectively. Discussion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that loading PDA nanoparticles onto SA-TCPP supramolecular photocatalysts dramatically enhances the activity for photosynthesis of H2O2 from only H2O and O2. The integration of PDA improves the conversion of NIR photons to H2O2, achieving the AQY values of 7.1%, 4.8% and 2.8% at 850 nm, 940 nm, and 1020 nm, respectively. The advantage contributes to a remarkable H2O2 generation rate of 3.37 mM/h and an SCC of 2.2% under full-spectrum irradiation. Persistent SQ• radicals in PDA are demonstrated to enable the rapid transfer (79 fs) of sub-gap electrons from SA-TCPP to PDA and facilitate the generation of •OOH radicals under NIR light irradiation. This work not only offers a simple approach to designing effective H2O2 generating photocatalysts that harness NIR energy but also holds potential for application across a broader range of materials.

Methods

Materials

Ammonia solution (NH3·H2O, 28–30.0%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.); ethanol, anhydrous (EtOH, ≥ 99.7%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.); hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36–38.0%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.); sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 96.0%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.); isopropanol (IPA, ≥ 99.7%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.); sodium sulfate (Na2SO4, 99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.); 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine 1-oxyl (TEMPO, 98%, Innochem); nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT, 98%, Innochem); formic acid (HCOOH, 88%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.). All commercial chemicals were used without further purification. Ultrapure water (18.20 MΩ) was used in all experiments.

Preparation of polydopamine (PDA)

PDA is prepared by a self-polymerization method from dopamine hydrochloride (98%, Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Corp., Ltd., China)46. In a typical process, 2.0 mL ammonia aqueous solution (NH3·H2O, 28-30%) is added to a 130 mL ethanol solution (30 vol%). The solution is stirred at room temperature for 30 min, and then, 10.0 mL dopamine hydrochloride solution (50.0 mg/mL) is added to the solution. The mixture solution becomes pale brown at first. During stirring, the mixture solution converts into dark brown and then gradually turns into black, indicating the formation of PDA particles. After 48 h of reaction, the PDA is collected by centrifugation (5440 × g) and washed with water three times. The collected solids are dried in a vacuum box at 333.15 K for 12 h to obtain the final product.

Preparation of SA-TCPP supermolecule photocatalysts9

Typically, 500.0 mg of meso-Tetra(4-carboxyphenyl) porphyrin (TCPP, 97%, Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Co, Ltd, China) is fully dissolved with 10.0 mL of 0.5 M KOH aqueous solution. 0.1 M HCl is gradually introduced using a constant-pressure funnel until the pH of the solution reaches 4.0. Subsequently, the suspension is stirred for 72 h to obtain the self-assembled TCPP (SA-TCPP). The resulting precipitate is isolated by centrifugation (5440 × g) and repeatedly washed with ultrapure water until pH ≈ 7.0. The collected SA-TCPP is finally dried under vacuum at 313.15 K prior to further use. Note that to be consistent with the preparation of PDA/SA-TCPP composites, the preparation of SA-TCPP photocatalysts is carried out at room temperature, rather than at 353.15 K in an oil bath as previously reported by us. Though the preparation temperature change leads to different crystallization of SA-TCPP, the H2O2 generation activity of SA-TCPP is similar, probably due to the same short-range ordering (J-aggregation) as verified by the PL spectra (Supplementary Fig. 61).

Preparation of PDA/SA-TCPP composites

Typically, PDA/SA-TCPP composites are prepared by a self-assembly precipitation method at room temperature. The prepared 220.0 mg of PDA and 200.0 mg of TCPP are added into 10.0 mL of 0.5 M NaOH solution, with then 100.0 mL of water is added to the mixture, and the mixture is stirred 2 h to form a transparent dark purple solution.

0.1 M HCl aqueous solution is dropped into the solution by a constant pressure dropping funnel until the pH of the solution decreases to 4.0. Then the black precipitants are formed in the solution, and the final products (PDA/SA-TCPP composites) are centrifuged (5440 × g) and washed with water three times until the pH is 7.0. The obtained PDA/SA-TCPP is dried in a vacuum box at 353.15 K overnight. It is worth noting that the term “PDA/SA-TCPP” mentioned in the article specifically refers to the photocatalyst with an optimal loading amount ratio (mPDA: mSA-TCPP = 1.1: 1). In addition to the optimal PDA loading amount, other mass ratios between PDA and SA-TCPP photocatalysts are prepared, and including the mass ratios of PDA: TCPP are 0.5, 1, 1.2, and 1.5 (Supplementary Figs. 62–64). The sample is denoted as x-PDA/SA-TCPP. Note that the PDA/SA-TCPP are prepared from commercially available chemicals, easily manipulated methods, and common laboratory equipment, indicating good potential for scale-up preparation.

Characterization

The lattice structure is recorded by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a D8 diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (Bruker AXS, Germany). The sample morphology is characterized using a transmission electron microscope (TEM JEM-2100, JEOL, Japan) operating at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV, and a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). Optical absorption properties in the ultraviolet-visible-near-infrared region are analyzed with a UV-3600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The bonding information is analyzed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy with a Nicolet iS5 instrument (Thermo, USA) employing the KBr pellet method. The band structure is investigated by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) using an ESCALAB Xi+ system (Thermo, USA). The related radicals are characterized by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) at X-band frequency (9.8 GHz) on a Bruker EMXplus-6/1 spectrometer (Germany).

Photocatalytic H2O2 production tests

The photocatalytic performance of PDA/SA-TCPP for H2O2 production is evaluated under continuous O2 bubbling (100 mL min-1). The light irradiation is achieved by using a 300 W Xe lamp equipped with a 420 nm cut-off filter (Beijing Perfectlight Technology Co., Ltd, China). In a typical experiment, 0.2000 g photocatalyst is ultrasonically dispersed in 50 mL of water (pH ≈ 4, adjusted by adding 2-3 drops of 0.1 M HCl) in a 100 mL glass bottle. A rubber stopper is capped onto the glass bottle. The rubber stopper is pinned with a PTFE pipe and a needle, which serve as the gas inlet and outlet, respectively47. The H₂O₂ activity is evaluated at 353.15 K unless otherwise specified, the temperature shown to cause negligible decomposition of the generated H2O2 (Supplementary Figs. 65–67). Note that despite the decrease in O2 solubility at 353.15 K, the elevated temperature simultaneously enhances the diffusivity of the dissolved O2 and the volume-mass transfer rate of O2 is estimated as ca. 33.8-91.3 mM/h, far exceeding the O2 consumption (1.6 mM/h) based on the H2O2 production rate of PDA/SA-TCPP. (Supplementary Note 1)

Hydrogen peroxide concentrations were determined using the well-established potassium titanium(IV) oxalate spectrophotometric method48,49. In acidic medium, H₂O₂ reacts with Ti(IV) oxalate to form a yellow Ti-peroxo complex, which exhibits a characteristic absorption at 400 nm. After the desired reaction time, 1.0 mL of solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter and mixed with 1.0 mL of 0.02 M Ti(IV) oxalate solution and 3.0 mL of water. The resulting yellow complex was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 400 nm using a UV-3600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The calibration curve is linear over 0.5–2.0 × 10-3 M (absorbance 0.5–2.0, 1 cm cells), with a precision of ~ 1% (relative mean deviation 1.1%)50. Although this method can be affected by fluoride ions or colored species, in our system, this interference was eliminated by using ultrapure water (18.20 MΩ). The method shows comparable accuracy to the conventional iodometric method (Supplementary Fig. 65).

Electrochemical and PEC measurements

The rotating disk electrode test

The rotating disk electrode (RDE) test is performed on a CHI-750E electrochemical workstation, operated via computer control and a conventional three-electrode setup. The glassy carbon disk coated with the sample serves as the working electrode, while an Ag/AgCl electrode and a platinum mesh electrode are employed as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively9. DFT calculation

The DFT calculations are performed on Material Studio with the Dmol3 package58. The geometry optimization is performed first to relax the reactants and products, respectively, and then, the transition state calculations are carried out to obtain the energy barriers for SQ⋅ with O2 to produce H2O2. For the transition state calculations, the linear synchronous transit/quadratic synchronous transit (LST/QST) method is used. The exchange and correlation functions are treated by the Becke three-parameter Lee−Yang−Parr (B3LYP) level of theory, taking into account the bulk solvent effects of water. The tolerances for geometry optimization and TS calculations are set with the total energy of 5 × 10−5 Ha/atom, the maximum force of 0.002 Ha/Å, the maximum ionic displacement of 5 × 10−3 Å, and the maximum root-mean-square (RMS) forces of 0.01 Ha/Å. To calculate the LUMO orbitals of PDA, a structural model consisting of nine moieties containing Q, SQ•, and QH₂ units with varying saturation degrees of the indole is constructed. The exchange and correlation functions are also treated by the B3LYP. Geometry optimization is performed with convergence criteria of 1 × 10⁻⁴ Ha/atom for total energy, 0.02 Ha/Å for maximum force, and 0.05 Å for maximum ionic displacement. Note that, considering the inherent challenges in accurately modeling the exact material under realistic catalytic conditions, the DFT calculations in this work are intended primarily to provide supportive insight for the experimental results.
