Cross‐Cultural Adaptation and Psychometric Properties of Low Back Pain Core Outcome Measures: A Systematic Review
Authors: Nora Bakaa, Ayda Babakhamseh, Natalie Chan, Marianne de Vera, Julie Finnigan, Rebecca Ramdelall, Brianna Tang, Lee‐Ran Goodman, Lisandra Ameida de Oliveira, Paulo Carvalho do Nascimento, Yuva Venkata Raghava Neelapala, Stephanie Di Pelino, Luciana G Macedo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe purpose of this systematic review was to identify and evaluate the quality of cross‐cultural adaptations of the six core outcome measures (NRS, SF‐12, PROMIS, ODI, RMDQ and VAS) for low back pain (LBP) and assess their psychometric properties.MethodsMedline, EMBASE, Emcare and CINAHL databases were searched from inception to August 2024. Eligible studies included those assessing core LBP outcome measures in adults with LBP. The COSMIN checklist was used to assess the quality of cross‐cultural adaptations and evaluate the psychometric properties. Additionally, the COSMIN risk of bias tool and GRADE approach were employed to assess study quality and provide levels of evidence.ResultsA total of 82 studies were included. Among them, 35 studies examined the cross‐cultural adaptations of the ODI, 25 for the RMDQ, 4 for PROMIS, 1 for SF‐12 and 2 for NRS/VAS. The quality of cross‐cultural adaptations was generally poor or fair due to inadequate reporting of pre‐testing processes and small sample sizes. Most psychometric properties were rated as having an inadequate risk of bias, with evidence quality ranging from very low to low.ConclusionThis review highlights that the cross‐cultural adaptations of the five core LBP outcome measures have generally been conducted with low quality. To improve the adaptation process, studies should employ larger sample sizes and standardised pre‐testing procedures. Additional research is needed to explore a broader range of psychometric properties of cross‐cultural adaptations, ensuring better validity, reliability and comparability across adapted versions.Significance StatementImproving the quality of cross‐cultural adaptations of LBP outcome measures is crucial to ensuring that assessments are culturally relevant and accurate across diverse populations, leading to better‐informed clinical decisions and equitable care for patients with LBP globally.Trial RegistrationCRD42023413077

Introduction

Outcome measures are essential for clinical practice and research to assess status, inform decisions about treatment and monitor progression over time (Andrade 2015; Ayers and Bozic 2013; Chow et al. 2009; Johnson 2008). Core set outcome measures are a consensus‐derived collection of outcome measures that allow clinicians and researchers to standardise the assessment of endpoints for a specific health condition (Guillemin et al. 1993). In low back pain (LBP), a leading worldwide cause of disability (Hoy et al. 2012, 2014; Bergman 2007; Yao et al. 2016; Koes et al. 2006), five core patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) have been recommended (Chiarotto, Boers, et al. 2018). This core set of outcome measures includes the Short Form‐12 (SF‐12) and Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Global Health Form (PROMIS GH‐10) for health‐related quality of life (HRQoL), the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) for physical functioning and the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for pain (Chiarotto, Boers, et al. 2018). Although the measurement properties of these instruments have been rigorously evaluated in their original language versions, the increasing trend of multicultural countries and international research projects underscores the need for appropriate cross‐cultural adaptation of these core measures to enhance the inclusion of more culturally diverse populations within research studies and to facilitate assessment in clinical practice.

Language discordance of clinical outcome measures in cross‐cultural healthcare systems and global research creates an insurmountable barrier for patients accessing resources and equitable care (Ahmed et al. 2016; Bowen 2001). When translated to other languages without consideration of cultural nuances, questionnaires developed in English may convey ethnocentric concepts that do not consider differing beliefs around patient experience and care (Guillemin et al. 1993; Sperber 2004; Ramada‐Rodilla et al. 2013; Connor‐Smith and Flachsbart 2007). Cross‐cultural adaptation of an outcome measure involves an intensive process with multiple translations, synthesis of translations, back translations to the original language, expert committee review and pre‐testing the measure (Beaton et al. 2000a; Terwee et al. 2012). However, these standards are rarely met for outcome measures, including the core outcome measures identified for LBP (Yao et al. 2016; Maneesriwongul and Dixon 2004; Epstein et al. 2015; Sousa and Rojjanasrirat 2011). As a result, LBP research is often bound by the exclusion of participants due to a lack of proficiency in the language of the original tool (Chen et al. 2022; Chapman et al. 2011; Ramasamy et al. 2017; Chiarotto et al. 2016; Chiarotto, Maxwell, et al. 2019). Alternatively, when tools are translated without undergoing a complete cross‐cultural adaptation, the resulting instrument may lack cultural sensitivity, leading to inaccurate assessments that bias research findings and misinform clinical decisions (Sperber 2004; Maneesriwongul and Dixon 2004).

Evaluation of cross‐cultural adaptation of PROMs requires an assessment of the psychometric properties of the adapted tool and a comparison with the measurement properties of the original tool (Mokkink et al. 2016). Although there are some reviews of individual outcome measures (Yao et al. 2016; Chiarotto et al. 2016), these reviews predate recent updates to the COSMIN methodology and therefore need to be updated with more comprehensive search strategies and evaluated using the evolving COSMIN framework, which places greater emphasis on content validity, transparent reporting and cross‐cultural equivalence (Mokkink et al. 2016). To our knowledge, no studies have systematically evaluated a core set of low back pain (LBP) outcome measures and their cross‐cultural adaptations. This review addresses that gap by focusing on five core outcome measures—ODI, RMDQ, SF‐12, NRS and PROMIS—which are consistently recommended in LBP core outcome sets, widely used in clinical trials, and frequently referenced in international guidelines. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to identify cross‐cultural adaptations of these five outcome measures and evaluate their psychometric properties.

Methods

This systematic review followed the Consensus‐based Standards for selecting health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) reporting guidelines and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Mokkink et al. 2016; Moher et al. 2010). The protocol was registered on Prospero under CRD42023413077.

Role of Funding Source

The funders played no role in the study's design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, or reporting.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed in consultation with a Health Sciences librarian. Electronic database searches were conducted in Medline, EMBASE, Emcare and CINAHL from inception to 28 February 2023, and updated on 2 August 2024. Citation tracking was completed on the Web of Science portal, and reference lists of included studies were manually searched to obtain any other relevant studies. Articles assessing the psychometric properties without an associated cross‐cultural adaptation article were hand‐searched for an adaptation study. If one was not located, the psychometric study was excluded. The search strategy included key terms for back pain, cross‐cultural adaptation and the core set outcome measures. The search strategy was adapted accordingly for each database (see Appendix A).

Eligibility Criteria

Population

This review defined LBP as pain or discomfort in the lower back region attributed to any known or unknown pathoanatomical cause. We included studies of adults aged 18 years or older with specific and/or non‐specific LBP of any duration, but excluded emergent or serious causes such as malignancy, infection, or fracture, as these represent distinct clinical pathways.

Outcome Measures

Studies conducting cross‐cultural adaptation of the following core outcome measures for low back pain were included: ODI, RMDQ, pain NRS or VAS, SF‐12 and PROMIS (Chiarotto, Boers, et al. 2018). Subsequently, studies assessing the cross‐cultural validity using cognitive interviews and psychometric properties of the questionnaires in different languages were included.

Study Design

No language restriction or publication date was imposed. Selection criteria were limited to cross‐cultural adaptation and psychometric study designs. Studies in languages other than English must have had an English abstract to be eligible. Grey literature, randomised controlled trials, cohort studies, cross‐sectional studies, observational studies and systematic reviews were excluded.

Beaton et al. (2000b) identified guidelines for cross‐cultural adaptation of self‐reported measures. The included components are at least two translations, synthesis of translations, back translations to the original language, expert committee review and pre‐testing of the measure. Cross‐cultural adaptations of outcome measures were included in this review if they comprised at least two translations, a back translation and a review from an expert panel. As per the COSMIN criteria, psychometric studies were included if they had any properties relevant to evaluating outcome measures: reliability, internal consistency, measurement error, hypothesis testing for construct validity, cross‐cultural validity, content and face validity, structural validity, criterion validity and responsiveness (Mokkink et al. 2016).

Selection Process

Titles and abstracts were systematically screened for inclusion using Covidence by pairs of independent reviewers, following the eligibility criteria outlined previously. A pilot screening of titles, abstracts and full texts was conducted on the first 10 potentially eligible studies. All reviewers independently applied the eligibility criteria and then met to compare decisions, discuss discrepancies and refine the screening process to ensure consistent application across the review. Inter‐rater agreement was 80% during this pilot phase, after which the screening procedure was finalised. Studies written in languages other than English selected through abstract screening were translated into English before full‐text screening through the DeepL translator software (Kutylowski 2017). Full texts of selected abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers. Disagreements on all states were resolved through discussion and a third reviewer when consensus could not be reached.

Data Extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted data from eligible studies, and the extracted data were reviewed for accuracy. The information from data extraction was used to inform decisions about data synthesis. Data extraction included information about study characteristics such as aim, study design, original and adapted language of PROM and the country where the study was conducted.

The quality of the cross‐cultural adaptations was assessed using the COSMIN 15‐item checklist criteria relevant for determining the quality of cross‐cultural adaptations (Terwee et al. 2012). The checklist includes information about the percentage of missing items, a description of how missing items were handled, if the sample size was adequate, if the translation process was described, the expertise of translators, if the translators worked independently, if items were translated forward and backward, how differences between the original and translated versions were resolved, the use of pre‐test, the adequacy of the sample size and characteristics in the pre‐test, any important flaws in study design and statistical methods (e.g., confirmatory factor analysis, or differential item function).

Results of studies evaluating the psychometric properties of included studies were extracted for information on sample demographic age and sex, type of validity and corresponding values, type of reliability and corresponding values and responsiveness and corresponding values.

Data Synthesis

Each of the 15 data items for cross‐cultural adaptation was synthesised and assigned a rating of ‘excellent’, ‘good’, ‘fair’, or ‘poor’ based on adapted recommendations from Terwee et al. (2012) (see Appendix B). As per the recommendations, a ‘poor’ score was applied only when there was an irredeemable flaw in the methods. Items not reported were left blank unless another rating was specified for uncertainty in the recommendations.

We rated methodological quality with the 2018 COSMIN Risk‐of‐Bias checklist and classified each measurement property as ‘sufficient’ (+), ‘insufficient’ (−), or ‘indeterminate’ (?) (see Appendix C) (Prinsen et al. 2018). We used a streamlined COSMIN‐adapted GRADE process that began at ‘high’ certainty and applied downgrades of (i) risk of bias (doubtful (−1)/inadequate (−2)), (ii) imprecision (total N < 100 (−1); N < 50 (−2)) and (iii) indirectness (population (−1)). Inconsistency could not be judged with single studies, and publication bias was not assessed per COSMIN guidance. Resulting evidence was labelled high, moderate, low, or very low, while properties rated ‘indeterminate’ or not examined were left blank. We followed the 2018 COSMIN manual specified in our 2023 protocol. An updated guideline (v2.0, August 2024) was released after data extraction and quality assessment were completed. As v2.0 builds on the same framework, applying it would not have changed individual study ratings; post hoc adoption would have compromised protocol fidelity. We acknowledge this newer guidance as a limitation and recommend its adoption in future reviews.

Results

Study Selection

A total of 3597 studies were identified for title and abstract screening and an additional 697 after the updated review, which was reduced to 2135 and 610 after duplicate removal (Figure 1). Following title and abstract screening, 160 studies were eligible for full‐text review, and an additional 7 after the updated review. After the full‐text screening, 71 studies were excluded for reasons such as not meeting the cross‐cultural adaptation definition (n = 44), being grey literature or systematic review (n = 13) and having no associated cross‐cultural adaptation study identified for the psychometric study (n = 9). A complete list of reasons for exclusion can be found in Appendix D. Ultimately, 82 studies were included in the final analysis. Of these, 48 studies were identified for the ODI, 30 for the RMDQ, 6 for PROMIS, 1 for SF‐12 and 2 for pain NRS/VAS (Table 1 for study characteristics).

The most common reasons for these ratings were a lack of description expertise of translators, a description of how resolutions were resolved and a small sample size.

Discussion

This review highlights the growing body of research on cross‐cultural adaptations and psychometric studies of the five core LBP outcome measures. Overall, all cross‐cultural adaptations for the core outcome measures were rated poor or fair. It is important to note that even the original‐language versions of several core LBP outcome measures have shown gaps in psychometric evidence, particularly in relation to content validity and responsiveness (Chiarotto, Ostelo, et al. 2018; Terwee et al. 2018). The main reasons for low or indeterminate ratings in adapted versions were small sample sizes, inadequate reporting of pretest procedures and limited information on translator qualifications (Beaton et al. 2000a). Many studies also received ‘indeterminate’ ratings, particularly for content validity, structural validity and cross‐cultural validity. These ratings often reflected insufficient reporting rather than poor conduct, but this lack of transparency prevents reliable judgment. Thus, our conclusions should be interpreted cautiously: absence of evidence does not equal evidence of poor quality. To address this, systematic evaluation of all key psychometric properties, including content validity, structural validity, cross‐cultural validity, responsiveness and criterion validity, should be embedded in the study design. This would reduce the frequency of indeterminate ratings and build a stronger evidence base for evaluating adapted outcome measures.

Our findings differ from Sheahan et al. (2015), who concluded most ODI adaptations followed best practices, but align more closely with Yao et al. (2016), who found that only one of 27 versions fully completed all recommended steps. The likely explanation for these differences lies in methodological rigor. Earlier reviews applied less stringent criteria, whereas our review used COSMIN guidelines and stricter inclusion criteria, such as requiring two forward and one backward translation. As a result, adaptations that were acceptable under earlier standards do not necessarily comply with the more rigorous COSMIN framework (Mokkink et al. 2016). These evolving standards highlight the need to prioritise content validity and transparent reporting, so that future adaptations can be evaluated against current best practices.

The underreporting of content, structural and cross‐cultural validity likely reflects both historical and practical factors, including limited awareness of evolving COSMIN priorities, small study samples and reporting conventions that emphasised reliability over validity. Future research should systematically address these gaps by reporting translation processes transparently and prioritising partner (e.g., clinicians and patients) involvement and content validity assessment. While no adaptation fully met COSMIN criteria, certain versions demonstrated relatively stronger evidence for specific psychometric properties. For example, ODI adaptations in Danish, Dutch, Italian, Portuguese and Norwegian, and RMDQ adaptations in Norwegian, Portuguese, Spanish, Korean and Hungarian populations represent the best available tools for use in those linguistic contexts, provided that their limitations are clearly acknowledged. By contrast, PROMIS, SF‐12 and NRS/VAS were supported by only a small number of studies, with incomplete assessment of psychometric properties. The limited evidence prevents strong conclusions regarding their suitability, highlighting the urgent need for additional high‐quality, well‐designed, cross‐cultural validation research on these core outcome measures.

Cultural differences can directly impact the validity of adapted outcome measures when items are based on ethnocentric assumptions embedded in the original English versions. For example, references to ‘walking several blocks’ or ‘climbing stairs’ may not be meaningful in settings without comparable urban infrastructure, while items addressing ‘household chores’ or ‘work responsibilities’ may differ substantially across cultural contexts, particularly where gender roles or informal labour dominate (Acquadro et al. 2008). Without careful adaptation, such items risk reduced content validity and misinterpretation (Beaton et al. 2000a, 2000b; Gjersing et al. 2010; Wild et al. 2005). Previous methodological reviews have highlighted that many translation processes insufficiently account for experiential and conceptual equivalence, leading to inconsistent validity across cultures (Maneesriwongul and Dixon 2004; Epstein et al. 2015). Future cross‐cultural adaptations should therefore incorporate local patient and clinician input, use qualitative methods such as cognitive interviewing or focus groups, and explicitly report modifications made to address cultural differences.

Several steps are critical for strengthening the cross‐cultural adaptation of LBP outcome measures. Researchers should prioritise the assessment of content and cross‐cultural validity to capture cultural relevance during the adaptation process. Future work is particularly needed in underrepresented populations such as African, South Asian, West Asian and Indigenous communities. In practice, clinicians and researchers may need to adopt these tools cautiously, ensuring that their limitations are recognised, transparently reported and mitigated where possible (e.g., supplementing with additional outcomes or qualitative data). This more pragmatic approach acknowledges the current evidence gaps while allowing for continued clinical and research activity in diverse cultural settings. Policymakers and professional organizations should invest in developing high‐quality adaptations to reduce inequities in outcome measurement.

Limitations

We required ≥ 2 forward translations, one backward translation and expert review, reflecting minimum standards for cross‐cultural adaptation (Beaton et al. 2000a; Epstein et al. 2015; Acquadro et al. 2008; Gjersing et al. 2010). This may have excluded some adaptations, but since they did not follow minimum methodological standards for adaptations, their inclusion could be misleading. Additionally, by limiting inclusion to studies with an English abstract, we may have introduced language bias and overlooked valuable evidence from non‐English‐speaking regions, which we acknowledge as a limitation with implications for global health equity. While this review focused on the five core LBP outcome measures, the PROMIS, SF‐12 and pain NRS/VAS had relatively few cross‐cultural adaptations available in the literature, which limited the depth of our evaluation. In contrast, other widely used PROMs, such as the SF‐36, have been more extensively adapted and validated across languages and cultural contexts. These instruments were not included because they were not part of the pre‐identified ‘core’ outcome measures for LBP, but their exclusion may limit the practical utility of our findings. Future systematic reviews could consider including additional outcome measures like the SF‐36 to provide a more comprehensive overview of available adaptations. Patient and clinician partners were not involved in interpreting the results or developing recommendations. This is a limitation, as partner involvement could have strengthened the contextual relevance and applicability of our findings to clinical practice and policy.

Conclusion

Cross‐cultural adaptations of the five core LBP outcome measures were mostly rated poor or fair, with frequent gaps in content and cross‐cultural validity, responsiveness and reporting. PROMIS, SF‐12 and NRS/VAS had few adaptations, while ODI and RMDQ were more common; some versions (e.g., Danish, Dutch, Portuguese, Spanish, Italian, Norwegian, Korean, Hungarian) showed relatively stronger evidence, but none fully met COSMIN standards. Clinicians and researchers may cautiously use the best available adaptations while acknowledging limitations. Future work should focus on rigorous methods, transparent reporting and adaptations in underrepresented populations to strengthen the evidence base.
