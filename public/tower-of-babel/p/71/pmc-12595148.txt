Radiation exposure to families of patients following administration of 177Lu-DOTATATE for the treatment of metastatic neuroendocrine tumours
Authors: D Levart, S Wicks, N Heraghty, E Kalogianni, N Mulholland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe administration of 177Lu-DOTATATE peptide receptor radionuclide therapy to patients for the treatment of well-differentiated, metastatic neuroendocrine tumours poses an external radiation hazard due to gamma emissions of lutetium-177. Patients are provided with standardised precautions to follow in the first 16 days following therapy, distancing themselves from family members and to limit their radiation exposure to less than 5mSv in 5 years in compliance with UK legislation. The purpose of this study was to measure the radiation exposure of adult family members of patients undergoing 177Lu-DOTATATE radionuclide therapy using thermoluminescent dosimeters (TLDs) worn continuously for 4 weeks following therapy administration, and to establish whether existing radiation protection precautions are adequately protecting patients’ family members.ResultsParticipating family members (n = 12) received a median of 0.038 mSv effective dose over 4 weeks from patients administered a median of 7515 MBq 177Lu-DOTATATE. Patients remained in the Nuclear Medicine department for an average of 6.2 h post-administration and at the time of discharge the median dose rates were 150 µSv/h at 0.1 m and 15 µSv/h at 1 m from the patient’s anterior abdomen, corresponding to a median lutetium-177 retention of 35.8% of the administered activity at the time of discharge measured using quantitative SPECT-CT imaging. Family members spent a median total time over the 4 week measurement period of 39.5 h at 1 m and 19.9 h at less than 1 m from the patient.ConclusionImplementing standardised contact restrictions for patients and their family members following 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT limits the dose received by family members to less than 5 mSv in 5 years, ensuring sufficient protection and compliance with the UK legislation.Keywords: Radiation Protection, Radionuclide Therapy, 177Lu-DOTATATE

Background

Peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) using Lutetium-177 labelled somatostatin analogues such as 177Lu-DOTATATE (Lutathera®, Novartis Pharmaceuticals) is an established procedure for the treatment of well-differentiated, metastatic neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), offering improved response rates and progression-free survival with fewer adverse side effects compared to alternative treatments [1–3]. Following the results of the phase 3 NETTER-1 trial [3] and subsequent approval of the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in 2017 [4] and the National Institute of Clinical Excellence (NICE) in 2018 [5] the use of Lutathera PRRT has increased dramatically in the UK [6].

Patient treatment typically comprises four therapy cycles of 7.4 GBq 177Lu-DOTATATE administered at 6–12 week intervals [7]. Lutetium-177 (physical half-life 6.73 days) emits β-particles (maximum energy 0.498 MeV and 1.7 mm soft-tissue penetration depth) that provide a therapeutic effect through the local deposition of ionising radiation when the radiopharmaceutical binds to somatostatin receptors expressed on the surface of tumour cells [7]. Gamma radiation at 113 keV and 208 keV with low relative abundance (6.2% and 10.4% respectively) are also emitted [8], which provide a means of post-therapy imaging and dosimetry measurements. These γ-emissions present an external radiation hazard to those who come into contact with the patient after administration. Biological clearance of radioactivity occurs predominantly through the urine, the majority of which takes place in the first 48 h after treatment [7, 9], and poses a further potential radiation hazard to others. For these reasons the careful consideration of patient contact restrictions and radiation protection precautions is required to limit the exposure of hospital staff, family and members of the public.

Depending on the local regulatory framework, some countries necessitate inpatient isolation of patients following 177Lu-DOTATATE administration. Conversely, the safety and feasibility of an outpatient therapy protocol has been demonstrated by centres in Australia, Canada, UK, US [10–13], provided appropriate contact restrictions are in place. At King’s College Hospital (London, UK) patients undergoing 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT on an outpatient basis are required to avoid contact with other adults at a distance of less than 1 m for 6 days and not to share a bed with their partner for 16 days post-treatment. For children the restriction periods are age-dependent and patients are asked to avoid close contact at less than 1 m for 17, 13 and 10 days for a child less than 2 years, 2–5 years and 5–11 years respectively. These standardised precautions are based on a previous study [12] that combined patient retention and dose-rate measurements with models of social contact time to determine the period over which contact should be restricted to maintain a 1 mSv effective dose constraint per treatment cycle for patients’ family members and 0.3 mSv constraint for members of the public. This dose constraint ensures that the dose received by patients’ family members is restricted to less than 5mSv in 5 years and members of the public to less than 1mSv per year in line with current UK legislation (Ionising Radiations Regulations 2017 (IRR17)) [14]. In the UK similar precautions are advised in the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) approved Lutathera® Treatment Procedure Guide provided by Novartis Pharmaceuticals [15], which recommends that patients keep 1 m from other adults and sleep in a separate bedroom to their partner for the first 7 days post-treatment, as well as the Medical and Dental Guidance Notes [16], which gives a more conservative recommendation of restricting contact with other adults to 1 m for 15 days and to avoid sleeping in the same bed as their partner for 20 days post-therapy.

Standardised contact restrictions based on the extrapolation of dose-rate decay curves and models of social contact time are useful to maintain compliance with the legislation, however they may not be appropriate for all individuals. The radiation exposure received from a patient will depend on their individual disease burden, somatostatin avidity, radiopharmaceutical biodistribution and renal function, as well as the differing patterns of time spent in close contact with others. Direct measurement of the cumulative effective dose received over time by patients’ family members provides a better indication of the radiation exposure and well-established contact restrictions derived on this basis have been employed for the safe outpatient 131I-NaI treatment for hyperthyroidism for many years [16, 17]. Few studies exist in the literature that have used personal dosimeters to measure the dose to patients’ relatives post-177Lu-DOTATATE administration [10, 18]. Reported measurements are for persons that would be considered ‘Carers and Comforters’ under the Ionising Radiation (Medical Exposure) Regulations 2017 (IR(ME)R17) legislation [19] and as such are not subject to the dose limits imposed by IRR17. Therefore, typical contact restrictions have not been applied for these persons and measurements taken for a limited time of up to 5 days post-treatment.

The aim of the study was to determine the radiation exposure of family members of patients undergoing 177Lu-DOTATATE radionuclide therapy by the measurement of the effective dose, and to establish whether existing radiation protection precautions are adequately protecting patients’ family members in compliance with UK legislation. The results may also be used as an indication as to whether the existing advice is overly cautious and if there is any scope to reduce the length of the current contact restriction period.

Method

Effective dose to the family members of patients receiving 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT as an outpatient at King’s College Hospital was estimated using lithium fluoride thermoluminescent dosimeters (TLDs) (UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) Personal Dosimetry Service) [20]. Family members who live with the patient were approached with the permission of the patient and a total of 12 participants were recruited (11 adult partners, 1 adolescent child). Dosimeters were sent by post to the family members prior to the day of treatment, along with instructions on how to wear them and a return envelope. Participants were required to wear a TLD in the form of a pin badge at waist height continuously for a period of 4 weeks after the patient’s treatment. If the family member attended the therapy appointment with the patient then they were asked to bring their badge dosimeter with them and wear it from their first contact with the patient post-administration. Participants who did not attend the therapy appointment were to wear the dosimeter as soon as the patient returned home. A participant diary was also provided to family members in order to record the estimated length of time (in hours) per day spent at 1 m and less than 1 m from the patient during the 4 week measurement period as well as any deviations from the wearing instructions.

The patient treatment protocol remained unchanged from the standard of care and involved the administration of 7.4 GBq 177Lu-DOTATATE (Lutathera®) in 23–25 ml over 30 min, with co-infusion of 50 g amino acids for renal protection (25 g Lysine/25 g Arginine) (Preston Pharmaceuticals, Preston, UK) diluted in 1 l of saline and starting 30 min prior to 177Lu-DOTATATE and maintained for 4 h. Treatments were undertaken in the designated outpatient therapy suite where the patient is asked to remain until discharge at approximately 6 h after the infusion in order to limit contact with others immediately post-administration. Dose rate measurements were made using a G-M probe (Series 900 mini-monitor probe type D) at 0.1 and 1 m from the anterior mid-abdomen immediately post-administration (before the patient empties their bladder) and prior to discharge. 3-bed-position SPECT-CT imaging from vertex to mid-thighs, using a Siemens Symbia T16/Intevo Bold gamma camera (Siemens Healthineers, Forcheim, Germany) was performed prior to discharge as per standard practice and images were reconstructed quantitatively using Hermia Hybrid Recon software (Hermes Medical Solutions, Stockholm, Sweden) to estimate the retention of Lutetium-177 in the patient at the time of discharge.

Prior to discharge the following radiation protection advice and contact restrictions were provided to the patient verbally and in written form as per standard protocol (where ‘close contact’ means contact at a distance of less than 1 m):

Any deviations from these contact restrictions were also noted in the participant diary. At the end of the measurement period the dosimeter and participant diary were returned to the Nuclear Medicine Department. TLDs were sent to be analysed by an Approved Dosimetry Service (UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) Personal Dosimetry Service) to give a measure of effective dose received by the family member. It was requested that these results were supplied with no standard correction applied for background radiation due to the fact that the periods of time TLDs were kept in the Nuclear Medicine Department before and after being issued to participants was highly variable. Previous environmental monitoring had shown a lower local background measurement within the department than standard UK background levels applied by UKHSA due to its location underground and shielding within walls. It was assumed that TLDs were on issue to participants for 5 weeks (4 weeks measurement period and 1 week to allow for postage time either side of this) during which time the standard UKHSA background level of 2.47 µSv per day was applied, while the local background level of 1.00 µSv per day was applied for the time the TLD was kept in the Nuclear Medicine Department.

The patient contact time at 1 m and less than 1 m recorded in the participant diaries was used to establish the level of compliance with contact restrictions. It was also used to provide an indication of the suitability of the published data on social contact times that were used to generate the standardised restrictions [12, 21], where it was assumed that the patient will spend 6 h per day at 1 m from their adult partner, followed by 8 h sleeping at 0.1 m. After day 16 there is no longer any restriction on contact with other adults advised, therefore the contact times recorded in participant diaries from week 4 could be compared those used in the model to determine its suitability in establishing contact restrictions. Additionally, the number of hours recorded per day were summed to give a total contact time in the first 2 days post-radiopharmaceutical administration when the retained activity and therefore external dose rate from the patient will be greatest as well as during the first, second, third and fourth weeks post-treatment to determine the relationship with the measured effective dose to family members.

Results

The median effective dose received by the family members was 0.038 mSv (range 0.014–0.111 mSv) from patients who were administered a median of 7515 MBq 177Lu-DOTATATE (range 7313–7911 MBq). Detailed records of the time spent in contact with the patient were returned by 11 participants. The median total time over the 4 week measurement period spent in contact with the patient was 39.5 h (range 0.0–194.3 h) at 1 m and 19.9 h (range 0.0–229.8 h) at less than 1 m. The median number of hours recorded in the first 2 days and each week in first, second, third and fourth weeks post-treatment are shown in Table 1. Participant diaries from week 4, when all contact restrictions are lifted, recorded a median of 1.6 h per day (range 0.0–7.0 h/d) spent at 1 m and 0.5 h per day (range 0.0–14.6 h/d) at < 1 m from the patient. Dose rate measurements were recorded for 10 patients at both post-administration and pre-discharge time points. The median dose rates at 0.1 m and 1 m from the anterior abdomen of patients immediately after completion of 177Lu-DOTATATE infusion were 300 µSv/h (range 150–500 µSv/h) and 35 µSv/h (range 20–60 µSv/h) respectively. Patients stayed in the Nuclear Medicine department for a median of 6.2 h (range 4.5–8.1 h) and at the time of discharge the median dose rates were 150 µSv/h (range 60–500 µSv/h) at 0.1 m and 15 µSv/h (range 6–30 µSv/h) at 1 m from the patient’s anterior abdomen. This corresponds to a median lutetium-177 retention of 35.8% (range 19.7–48.8%) of the administered activity at the time of discharge measured using quantitative SPECT-CT imaging, which was acquired for all 12 patients. Discussion

The effective dose to the family members of patients undergoing 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT were measured using TLDs for 4 weeks after treatment. All participants in this study received a dose that was lower than the 1 mSv dose constraint applied per treatment cycle by a factor of 10 or more. With the assumption that all patients undergo a typical 4 cycle treatment regime, and the dose received by family members is the same at each cycle would result in a median total effective dose to family members of 0.21 mSv over the whole course of treatment, which is well within the 5mSv in 5 years dose limit for family members as well as the 1 mSv annual dose limit for members of the public. This demonstrates that the current radiation protection advice provided to patients at our centre affords sufficient protection to their family members and maintains compliance with the UK legislation [14]. It also suggests the potential for dramatic reduction in restriction duration if an individualised method of defining radiation precautions was implemented. This outcome was to be expected since the previous study on which the standardised precautions are based took a conservative approach when determining the length of contact restrictions, by selecting the upper 95th percentile of data [12], resulting in an overestimation for the majority of patients as has been shown here. From Figs. 1 and 2 it can be noted that of the two participants with the highest measured effective dose, one had the greatest percentage radiopharmaceutical retention and dose rate at 0.1 m at the time of discharge and the other spent the longest time at 1 m and < 1 m from the patient within the first two days post-treatment. These observations follow what would logically be expected to increase the external dose rate and total accumulated dose, however the number of participants in this study is insufficient to draw any conclusions on the relationship of these variables.

Calais and Turner have previously measured the median radiation exposure to an accompanying ‘carer’ (family member or friend) of patients undergoing 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT to be 0.04 mSv (range 0.01–0.47 mSv) for a period of 1–5 days post-treatment [10], which is consistent with the 0.038 mSv median effective dose measured in this study. Polito et al. have published personal dosimetry measurements of 0.02 mSv (range 0.01–0.06 mSv) for adult carers staying with a paediatric 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT inpatient who were hospitalised for 3 days post-administration [18]. Whilst direct comparison of results is difficult due to the fact that it is not clear what patient contact restrictions were in place and the relatively short time period of dose measurement post-therapy, these studies demonstrate a minimal radiation dose received in the period of highest radiopharmaceutical retention and therefore highest external dose rate, even for paediatric patients who require more care at close contact than self-caring adult patients.

It should be noted that one limitation of this study exists in the estimation of effective dose using TLDs. The personal dosimetry service used in this study provides a measurement of effective dose (Hp(10)) with a minimum reporting level of 0.02 mSv and measurement uncertainties of less than 30% in compliance with the European Commission report Radiation Protection 160 [20, 22]. Additional uncertainties arise from the use of the relatively small measurement area provided by a TLD, which will only register the dose received by regions of the body in close proximity to where the dosimeter is worn as well as the significant uncertainty introduced by the possible variation in TLD positioning between participants as demonstrated by S. Mosley and G. Currie [23].

The standardised radiation protection precautions advise patients to limit close contact (at less than 1 m) with other adults for 6 days and avoid sharing a bed with their partner for 16 days after treatment. Published data on social contact times were used in generating the standard contact restrictions [12, 21], where it was assumed that the patient will spend 6 h per day at 1 m from their adult partner, followed by 8 h sleeping at 0.1 m. Participant diary records from week 4, when all contact restrictions are lifted, show that on average participants spent less than half the time assumed at each relevant distance in contact with patients when compared with the published social contact times, whereas some individual participants spent longer periods in contact with the patient. While this data relies on the accurate recording by participants and there may remain some non-typical cautionary distancing as a result of the advised precautions, it highlights the fact that social contact times will vary dramatically from patient to patient and as a result the standardised contact restrictions may be overly conservative for many.

This study has demonstrated the patient-dependent variability in radiopharmaceutical retention, dose rate measurement at discharge and social contact times, highlighting the potential for reducing the burden imposed by standardised precautions through the implementation of personalised contact restriction times. This could be achieved using the method described in Levart et al. [12] that involves post-therapy imaging at multiple time points to determine activity retention curves and would require the patient to return to the hospital on numerous occasions, which may not be practical for neither the patient nor the Nuclear Medicine department. Monserrat Fuertes et al. [24] describe a means of using 6 h and 24 h dose-rate measurements to produce individualised radiation protection recommendations for 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT patients and demonstrates the benefits compared to standardised advice. This is a practical method for inpatient therapy procedures as both measurement time points fall during the patient’s stay in hospital, however it is less straightforward for outpatient treatments where the patient would have to return for the 24 h measurement. Broggio et al. [25] provide a spreadsheet for calculation of precautions for a range of radionuclide therapies, including 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT, which provide a degree of personalisation by taking into account the dose-rate measurement at the time the patient leaves the hospital, though the estimation of whole-body retention function and social contact times used in the model remain conservative. These studies show that individualised contact restrictions are achievable, however implementation of these methods requires careful consideration to ensure they remain practical for both the patient and hospital. Further work to determine whether patient-specific indicators such as total disease burden or glomerular filtration rate (GFR) can be used as a predictor of retained 177Lu-DOTATATE at discharge would be valuable as a means of identifying, prior to treatment, the patients who may benefit most from individualisation of contact restrictions.

Whilst this study demonstrates the potential for a reduction in the duration of radiation precautions following 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT through the implementation of individualised contact restrictions, it also provides confirmation that following current standardised precautions the dose received by patients’ adult family members is well within that required to maintain compliance with the UK legislation. This will be of particular importance for patients considered for non-standard treatment regimens such as those receiving more than 4 treatment cycles. Additionally further work is required to extend this study to include patients’ household members who are children, as it is likely that social contact times and therefore radiation exposure will differ dramatically for this demographic, especially very young children.

Conclusion

Implementing standardised advice for patients to limit contact with their family members following 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT restricts the dose received by family members to less than 5 mSv in 5 years, ensuring sufficient protection and compliance with the UK legislation. Individualised advice based on the patients’ personal circumstances would reduce the burden that overly conservative restrictions places on patients, however application of this to the routine clinical setting may not be practical.
