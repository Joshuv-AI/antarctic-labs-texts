Changes in General Thoracic Surgery Volume during the Medical-Political Conflict in South Korea by Institutional Type and Surgical Complexity: An Analysis of National Health Insurance Claims
Authors: Yeong Min Lee, Geun Dong Lee, Sung-Hoon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn February 2024, South Korea experienced a major healthcare disruption following the mass resignation of approximately 86% of resident physicians in protest of a government-led medical reform. In response, the government introduced a policy that centralized high-complexity operations in tertiary hospitals and redirected low-complexity procedures to general hospitals and clinics. However, the nationwide impact of this policy on surgical distribution and volume remains unclear.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed using nationwide claims data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service, accessed via the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA). General thoracic surgery volumes from February to July 2023 (pre-crisis) were compared to the same period in 2024 (post-crisis) across tertiary hospitals (n=47), general hospitals (n=331), and smaller hospitals/clinics (n=37,888). Surgical complexity was categorized using relative value units (RVUs), which reflect procedural intensity and resource requirements.ResultsOverall thoracic surgery volume declined by 15% during the crisis. Tertiary hospitals reported a 22% reduction in procedures, while general hospitals and clinics recorded increases of 9% and 92%, respectively. High-complexity operations (≥30,000 RVUs) at tertiary hospitals fell by 22%, with only partial compensation by general hospitals. Low-complexity procedures (<30,000 RVUs) also decreased at tertiary hospitals but were not adequately redistributed.ConclusionThe 2024 healthcare crisis caused significant disruption to surgical capacity in South Korea. Although some redistribution occurred, the government’s reallocation strategy did not fully achieve its intended goals. Recovery of pre-crisis surgical capacity, especially for high-complexity procedures, remains incomplete.Keywords: Thorax, General surgery, Delivery of health care

Introduction

In February 2024, South Korea faced a major healthcare crisis triggered by the government’s unilateral announcement of a medical reform policy, which included a substantial increase in medical school admission quotas. This announcement provoked collective action among medical professionals, particularly resident (trainee) physicians [1]. Key concerns centered on the shortage of qualified faculty and clinical training infrastructure needed to maintain medical education standards, as well as the lack of concrete plans to support essential specialties, including surgical disciplines directly tied to patient care [2].

By October 2024, approximately 86.7% of resident physicians had resigned. To mitigate the resulting disruption, the government attempted to reorganize hospital operations by relying more heavily on board-certified specialists. Some departments responded by expanding the number of full-time hospitalists to address care gaps [3]. However, in general thoracic surgery, where procedures require highly trained surgeons, recruitment was particularly difficult. The withdrawal of residents, who had played a critical role in daily hospital operations, caused severe disruptions in care delivery, especially in tertiary hospitals.

At the same time, the government launched a healthcare delivery reform initiative to concentrate high-acuity, complex care in tertiary hospitals while redirecting low-acuity cases to primary and secondary institutions. As part of this initiative, a revised reimbursement system was introduced in November 2024 [4]. Under the new system, tertiary hospitals received additional incentives for performing high- complexity operations, while financial support was reduced for those with a high proportion of low-complexity procedures. Despite these changes, the impact of this policy remains largely unexplored in the academic literature.

To date, most studies have examined single institutions, limiting the ability to evaluate nationwide effects of the healthcare crisis. Moreover, no empirical research has specifically examined general thoracic surgery, a field marked by a large proportion of complex operations. Therefore, this study aims to analyze changes in general thoracic surgery volumes before and during the healthcare crisis using national health insurance claims data. Surgical trends are assessed by healthcare facility level, organ system, and procedural complexity based on relative value units (RVUs). In particular, this study focuses on institutional-level changes in surgical volume to determine whether the government’s intended redistribution of care and surgical complexity was effectively achieved during this period of systemic disruption.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study used national health insurance claims data provided by the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) of South Korea [5]. The dataset included both inpatient and outpatient services reimbursed under the Korean National Health Insurance program and encompassed data from tertiary hospitals (n=47), general hospitals (n=331), hospitals (n=1,410), and clinics (n=36,478) nationwide. Surgical volume was defined as the number of procedures billed, rather than the number of individual patients, with a focus on operations performed under general anesthesia and high-complexity procedures. To evaluate the impact of the healthcare workforce crisis that began on February 20, 2024, claims data from a 6-month post-crisis period (February to July 2024) were compared with the same period in the previous year (February to July 2023).

A total of 95 procedure codes were included in the analysis. Eligible procedures in the HIRA database involved the lung, esophagus, mediastinum, trachea, bronchus, chest wall, or diaphragm. Procedures classified under interventional categories (M codes) or marked as “inquiry period limitation” were excluded. Surgical complexity was categorized primarily using RVUs, which capture estimated time, effort, resources (personnel, facilities, equipment), and risk for each procedure. This classification was supplemented by the Ministry of Health and Welfare’s Guidelines for Supporting the Structural Reform of Tertiary Hospitals released in November 2024 [4]. Thoracic procedures were further stratified by anatomical organ to allow detailed analysis by organ system. Institutional review board approval was waived because the study used de-identified public data.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome was the change in surgical volumes before and during the healthcare crisis, stratified by institutional level. Data were collected using the HIRA Open Data platform [6]. National insurance claim counts were obtained for February to July 2023 and compared with February to July 2024. Medical institutions were categorized as tertiary hospitals, general hospitals, or hospitals/clinics. Tertiary hospitals are advanced referral centers with at least 300 inpatient beds, offering high-complexity medical services, including major surgery. General hospitals are facilities with 100 to 300 beds and at least 7 multidisciplinary departments, including surgery. Hospitals are defined as institutions with a minimum of 30 beds, providing inpatient and outpatient services to meet a broader range of local healthcare needs compared with clinics. Clinics serve primarily as outpatient facilities, treating patients with mild conditions or chronic diseases that do not require hospitalization.

Secondary outcomes were stratified by anatomical site and procedural complexity. Organ-specific categories included the lungs, esophagus, mediastinum, trachea/bronchi, chest wall, and diaphragm. For complexity-based analysis, procedures were divided into 3 equally distributed groups according to RVUs provided by the HIRA Open Data platform. RVUs reflect resource requirements, technical difficulty, and procedural intensity. The 3 categories represented low-, intermediate-, and high-complexity procedures, each encompassing one-third of the total surgical volume. The RVUs for all thoracic surgical procedures used in this study are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Additionally, we aimed to indirectly evaluate the outcomes of regional redistribution of thoracic surgery by examining regional trends in lung cancer surgery using the HIRA Open Data platform. The number of claims related to lung cancer surgery was calculated as the sum of lung resections and mediastinal lymph node dissections.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were applied to compare surgical claim volumes before and during the healthcare crisis using data from the National Health Insurance System. Claim volumes were aggregated and analyzed by healthcare facility type, thoracic organ, and procedural complexity. Although institutions in South Korea are formally classified into 4 categories by bed capacity and scope of services, hospitals and clinics were grouped together in this study. This grouping ensured statistical validity in the analysis of thoracic and esophageal surgery claims, given the relatively small claim numbers from individual lower-level institutions.

We also examined changes in the proportional distribution of surgical claims by facility type to evaluate whether the surgical burden shifted across institutional levels. Absolute differences in surgical claim volumes between the 2 time periods were calculated and expressed as percentage changes. To assess statistical significance, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported for mean percentage differences. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 25.0 (IBM Corp.).

Results

During the healthcare crisis, the total number of surgical claims in general thoracic surgery decreased by 15%, from 19,457 cases before the crisis to 16,513 cases during the crisis. Table 1 summarizes the overall distribution of surgical claims stratified by institution type, thoracic organ, and RVU category. By institution type, tertiary hospitals reported a 22% decrease in claims, from 15,134 to 11,773 cases. In contrast, general hospitals showed a 9% increase, from 4,285 to 4,667 cases. Although the absolute number of claims from hospitals/clinics remained small, it increased by 92%, from 38 to 73 cases.

When stratified by organ, surgical claims decreased consistently across all thoracic categories, though the magnitude varied. Notably, for lung transplantation—a highly complex procedure performed almost exclusively at tertiary hospitals—there was no significant difference, with 88 cases before and 90 cases during the crisis (2.3% increase). A similar downward trend was observed across all procedural complexity levels defined by RVUs.

Table 2 presents changes in surgical claim volumes by thoracic organ and institution type. At tertiary hospitals, surgical claims decreased across all thoracic organs without exception. Among these, high-complexity procedures such as esophageal and pulmonary operations declined markedly, by 14% and 23%, respectively. Although general hospitals recorded proportional increases in these procedures, the absolute numbers did not offset the reductions observed at tertiary hospitals. For instance, mediastinal operations—the most frequently claimed thoracic procedures—fell by 25% at tertiary hospitals but rose by only 15% at general hospitals. Claims from hospitals and clinics were too few to generate statistically meaningful comparisons.

Table 3 displays institutional changes in surgical claims stratified by RVU-defined procedural complexity. For high-complexity procedures (≥30,000 RVUs), tertiary hospitals experienced a 22% decrease, while general hospitals showed a 19% increase. For procedures of moderate complexity (10,000–30,000 RVUs) and low complexity (<10,000 RVUs), tertiary hospitals demonstrated uniform 22% declines in both categories. In contrast, general hospitals reported increases of 2% and 12%, respectively. As with Table 2, claims from hospitals and clinics were too limited to yield meaningful findings. Importantly, no high-complexity procedures (≥30,000 RVUs) were claimed at hospitals/clinics either before or during the crisis.

Discussion

The healthcare crisis triggered by the 2024 physician- government conflict created a severe care gap across all sectors of the South Korean healthcare system. Because general thoracic surgery, which predominantly involves high- complexity procedures, has traditionally been concentrated in tertiary hospitals that rely heavily on specialized equipment and personnel, this specialty was particularly vulnerable to disruption. In this study, we observed declines in insurance claims for all organ-specific thoracic procedures at tertiary hospitals, regardless of type. Increases at general hospitals and hospitals/clinics did not offset the reductions at tertiary institutions when absolute numbers were compared. These findings suggest that redistribution of general thoracic procedures from tertiary hospitals to lower-tier institutions was limited, underscoring the challenges of maintaining flexible and continuous surgical care during healthcare crises. The overall decline in claims further highlights the field’s dependence on residents (trainees), as seen in other surgical specialties [7].

Our results indicate that the delivery system for general thoracic surgery in South Korea remains highly centralized within tertiary hospitals, with limited redistribution of surgical volume according to case complexity. Following the collective resignation of residents, tertiary hospitals—where high-complexity procedures are concentrated—experienced disproportionate reductions in claims, irrespective of severity. This finding reflects the profound direct and indirect effects of the healthcare crisis on high-level institutions.

General hospitals absorbed some of the high-complexity cases formerly managed by tertiary hospitals, contrary to the government’s intent that they primarily manage low- complexity procedures. Although the proportion of low- complexity operations increased in general hospitals, the absolute number did not compensate for the decline at tertiary hospitals. Hospitals and clinics, constrained by limited resources, inadequate infrastructure, and shortages of specialists, were largely unable to accommodate general thoracic procedures. Their minimal volume change across complexity levels may also reflect the ongoing nationwide shortage of board-certified cardiothoracic surgeons, which has complicated recruitment.

These patterns were mirrored in the organ-specific distribution of surgical claims. For high-complexity operations such as esophageal and pulmonary surgery, increases at lower-tier institutions did not match reductions at tertiary hospitals. For mediastinal procedures, many of which are classified as lower-complexity, decreases at tertiary hospitals were not sufficiently offset elsewhere. Diaphragmatic operations continued to decline across all facility levels.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate a nationwide reduction in thoracic surgical volume, with the steepest declines in high-complexity procedures at tertiary centers. Although prior studies found no significant difference in mortality before and during the crisis, outcomes may differ when analyses are limited to thoracic surgery [8]. This was especially evident in lung cancer surgery, where claims declined sharply in Seoul, home to most tertiary hospitals. The reduction appears linked to disruptions in hospital operations following the resident exodus and compounded by challenges in recruiting specialists [9]. The workforce crisis is further underscored by the dramatic fall in applicants for the 2025 thoracic surgery board examination—down to one-fifth of the previous year—with only 2 residents applying nationwide for cardiothoracic training [10].

Despite reductions in surgical volume, tertiary hospitals continued to perform the majority of thoracic procedures. This persistence reflects structural features of South Korea’s single-payer healthcare system with universal coverage [11]. Weak referral pathways and high out-of-pocket costs encourage many patients to bypass lower-tier facilities and seek care directly at tertiary hospitals, perpetuating overcrowding and procedural concentration even during crisis [12]. This observation contrasts with the Ministry of Health and Welfare’s April 2025 statement that tertiary hospitals were shifting toward a critical and rare-disease-centered model, with increases in major procedures reported since late 2024.

This study has several limitations. First, surgical complexity was defined using RVUs, which may not perfectly represent real-world difficulty. Complexity was categorized into 3 groups based on equal distribution of claim volumes, which may have introduced misclassification. Moreover, the study relied on the 995 high-complexity procedures defined in the Ministry of Health and Welfare’s November 2024 tertiary hospital reform guidelines. Thus, clinically complex procedures may have been excluded if they carried low RVUs or lacked classification. Nevertheless, the focus of the study was to identify broad patterns of surgical change, rather than provide a precise categorization framework.

Second, the analysis was restricted to the February–July 2024 period due to data availability, limiting the ability to capture longer-term changes in surgical volume and redistribution. Future studies should investigate medium- and long-term outcomes. In addition, while previous reports found no mortality increase attributable to the physician-government conflict, this study did not directly assess causal relationships between the crisis and mortality in thoracic surgery. Longitudinal research should explore this issue further, where feasible.

Third, the findings were based solely on National Health Insurance claims data. Claims may not fully capture clinical realities, including unmet surgical demand or hospital capacity. Some procedures may also require multiple claims for a single operation, potentially leading to discrepancies, particularly for high-complexity cases.

In summary, the 2024 medical crisis in South Korea led to a sharp reduction in thoracic surgical capacity, most prominently at tertiary hospitals. Redistribution to lower-tier institutions was insufficient, and the intended reallocation of high- and low-complexity procedures across hospital levels was largely ineffective. Surgical volumes did not return to pre-crisis levels, and the system’s reliance on tertiary hospitals and resident labor was exposed. These findings reveal structural vulnerabilities in the healthcare system and highlight the urgent need for reforms to strengthen resilience and ensure continuity of surgical care during systemic disruptions.
