Advanced Technology for Detection of Wound Infection
Authors: Colton Slabe, Gregory L Kearns, James D Marshall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly detection of infection in superficial skin wounds remains a critical determinant of healing outcomes, particularly in paediatric populations, where developmental and behavioural factors often limit clinical assessment. Traditional diagnostic approaches, including visual inspection and culture‐based microbiology, are constrained by subjectivity and diagnostic delay. Over the past decade, several innovative technologies have emerged that enable earlier, more objective identification of pathogenic activity in wounds. This focused mini‐review summarizes recent advances in novel imaging, biosensor‐enabled smart dressings, and molecular diagnostics, with an emphasis on the mechanisms, clinical evidence, and applicability to superficial wound care. Collectively, these technologies represent a shift toward precision wound diagnostics that may facilitate earlier intervention, reduce unnecessary antimicrobial exposure, and improve healing outcomes.Keywords: biosensors, detection, fluorescence imaging, infection detection, paediatric, PCR, superficial, superficial wounds

Introduction

In paediatric populations, superficial skin wounds are among the most common conditions seen in outpatient, urgent care, and emergency settings. Superficial skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs) have increased significantly in this group, with bacterial skin diseases rising 49% over the past 30 years [1]. Young children with SSTIs are particularly vulnerable due to their physiological immaturity, which accounts for the disproportionate and growing number of hospitalizations for SSTIs in this age group [2, 3]. While many superficial skin wounds heal without issues, delayed recognition of infection can cause longer recovery times, inappropriate use of antibiotics, and progression to deeper tissue involvement. Clinical signs such as erythema, warmth, pain, or pus often only identify infection after bacterial levels have reached points associated with impaired healing and poorer outcomes.

Similarly, SSTIs and the consequences of delayed wound detection are not limited to paediatric populations. They pose a significant public health issue for adults as well. In the first decade of the 21st century, paediatric SSTIs experienced the highest increase in incidence (305%) among all age groups, coinciding with more than a doubling of SSTI‐related hospital admissions during the same period [3, 4]. As a result, paediatric populations face the highest risk of hospitalization, even though the impact of SSTIs on adults has grown in recent years. Consequently, technological breakthroughs have mainly focused on adult populations, with limited validation in paediatric groups.

Nonetheless, technological advancements have enabled new methods for detecting infection‐related biological signals earlier in the wound‐healing process. Advanced imaging, biosensor‐integrated dressings, and nucleic acid–based diagnostics now offer additional means to detect pathogenic activity before clinical deterioration becomes apparent. This focused, rapid review highlights emerging technologies relevant to modern care of superficial skin wounds, with particular emphasis on innovations pertinent to paediatric populations, an essential yet understudied area of wound care.

Methods

A structured literature review was conducted using PubMed and Embase, employing controlled keywords and phrases with MeSH and Emtree, respectively. The refined search terms included “pediatric superficial wounds,” “wound infections,” “wound healing,” “infection prevention,” “early detection,” “clinical assessment,” “diagnostic technologies,” and “outcomes.” The inclusion criteria covered studies that were directly relevant to superficial wounds in infants and children, infection prevention strategies, and innovations in clinical or technological methods for early detection of wound infections. The search was further refined with filters to focus on articles published in the last 25 years, ensuring the retrieval of current, relevant information. Priority was given to peer‐reviewed studies involving paediatric patients aged 1–18 years that provided original data on wound care or diagnostic strategies.

Traditional Clinical Methods of Early Wound Detection

Traditionally, evaluating acute superficial wounds in paediatric patients has involved visual inspection and clinical examination. Visual inspection includes identifying typical signs of infection, such as erythema, purulence, swelling, pain, changes in granulation tissue, foul odour, skin breakdown, and discoloration [5]. While visual examination is useful for assessing the presence and severity of potential wound infection, more precise diagnostic tools may expedite recognition and enable more appropriate initiation of anti‐infective treatment [6]. To reduce variability among individual provider evaluations, grading systems have proven helpful. The Bates‐Jensen Wound Assessment Tool (BWAT, Figure 1) is among the most widely used methods for determining wound severity and monitoring progress. It includes 15 metrics that describe wound severity and comprises both subjective and objective elements. It applies to many wound types, including superficial skin wounds, and can be useful in adult populations [7]. However, the BWAT lacks sufficient published evidence to support its use in paediatric populations, as it was developed and validated almost exclusively in adults [7, 8]. Additionally, the BWAT has demonstrated inconsistency across wounds of different anatomic locations and of individuals with darker skin tones [7]. Although traditional observation methods and scoring systems remain valuable, emerging technologies could improve detection accuracy, quantify healing, and reduce observer variability.

Technological Approaches for Early Wound Infection Detection

Technological advancements in wound imaging have allowed for more precise and objective evaluation of wound features. Techniques such as traditional photography, ultrasound, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, laser Doppler imaging, autofluorescence imaging, and thermography have greatly enhanced the ability to monitor superficial skin wounds [5]. By enabling accurate detection of early or clinically evident wound infections, these tools facilitate early identification and therapeutic intervention.

Photography and Telemedicine Evaluation

When used correctly, high‐quality digital images enable clinicians to remotely assess the relative severity of superficial wounds, facilitating rapid evaluation and reducing the need for immediate in‐person visits. In paediatric populations, telemedicine is especially valuable for overcoming challenges such as travel logistics and caregiving responsibilities [9]. However, this approach is limited because digital images provide only a one‐dimensional view, which does not reveal the wound's depth. Additionally, factors such as lighting, image resolution, patient positioning, and camera angle can introduce variability and potentially compromise diagnostic accuracy and provider assessment. Despite these limitations, due to the increasing social and financial burdens of acute and chronic wound care, as well as shortages of healthcare providers, telemedicine has become a vital tool for early detection and evaluation of superficial skin wounds and associated infectious pathologies [10]. Nonetheless, while telemedicine can facilitate early detection and triage, its effectiveness depends on standardizing imaging protocols, integrating with complementary assessment tools, and maintaining careful clinical oversight to avoid misdiagnosis or delayed intervention.

X‐Ray, Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Both X‐ray and computed tomography (CT) are common imaging methods used to evaluate potential skin wound infections. While X‐ray imaging is traditionally employed to assess skeletal trauma, it has also proven useful for early detection of wounds due to its widespread availability, low cost, and ease of use. Plain films are particularly effective in identifying radiopaque foreign bodies and signs of subcutaneous emphysema, which can indicate early infection. Consequently, X‐ray imaging is suitable for initial assessment of deeper infections, such as osteomyelitis and concomitant skeletal injuries, as well as expected necrotizing fasciitis, a rare but life‐threatening soft tissue infection [11]. CT imaging provides more detailed, three‐dimensional images that better display the extent of a wound and can detect non‐radiopaque foreign bodies, abscesses, necrosis, and involvement of underlying structures [11, 12]. The benefits of CT are especially significant when there is a risk of a superficial infection spreading into deeper tissues and are indicated in paediatric patients to identify more diffuse involvement or more dangerous conditions like pyomyositis [13]. However, the use of both CT and X‐ray in paediatric populations must be carefully considered for each wound, as children are more sensitive to ionizing radiation than adults [14]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), while used far less frequently for wound assessment, can be valuable for detecting complications such as extension into deep tissue structures [6]. The clinical utility of MRI is limited due to its high cost, limited accessibility, and the time needed to obtain and interpret each scan. Additionally, all these procedures require extra scheduling, unlike routine visual assessments of skin wounds. They are also considerably more resource‐intensive and costly than clinical wound observation or point‐of‐care wound assessment devices. As a result, more readily available alternatives like ultrasonography are often preferred.

Ultrasonography

Ultrasonography is a valuable, non‐invasive technique for wound assessment that poses little risk and is routinely available in many clinical settings, including urgent care centers and emergency departments. Although this method of wound examination is relatively understudied, it can provide unique insights into the development and progression of superficial wounds [15]. Ultrasonography can detect subtle differences in tissue composition and density, thereby allowing visualization of specific cutaneous structures. It also enables detection of skin changes before they become visibly apparent on the surface by distinguishing normal physiology from foreign bodies and signs of infection, such as inflammation and necrosis [16]. When evaluating common skin and soft tissue infections in children, including abscesses and cellulitis, ultrasonography demonstrates higher specificity and sensitivity compared to physical examination alone [16]. In a recent study, bedside ultrasound improved management in 10.3% of patients with abscesses [17]. Therefore, ultrasonography is often the first‐line imaging modality used for the evaluation of paediatric cutaneous infections, from abscesses and cellulitis to foreign bodies and even intra‐procedural management of SSTIs. Consequently, ultrasonography can play a crucial role in the real‐time detection of tissue changes associated with wound infection, thereby supporting prompt therapeutic intervention [18].

Line‐Field Confocal Optical Coherence Tomography (LC‐OCT)

In recent years, line‐field confocal optical coherence tomography (LC‐OCT) has emerged as a noninvasive imaging technique with significant utility for assessing superficial wounds and soft‐tissue injuries. LC‐OCT combines principles of optical coherence tomography and reflectance confocal microscopy, employing broadband, spatially coherent light sources to visualize skin structures down to the cellular level by measuring the echo‐time delay and amplitude of backscattered light [19]. High‐resolution images, comparable to histological slides, with a resolution of about 1 μm, enable detailed examination of the dermis and epidermis in real time [20]. Although this technology has been studied for detecting neoplastic changes in superficial skin structures, it is increasingly used for early diagnosis of various skin diseases [19, 21]. For superficial skin lesions, this method can assist in identifying wounds and monitoring healing processes by tracking vascular and structural changes as they happen [22]. In cases of infections in superficial skin wounds, LC‐OCT has the potential to evaluate viral infections like herpesvirus, where detecting intraepidermal vesicles in the appropriate clinical context can serve as a valuable diagnostic tool [23]. While preliminary studies are promising, its role in diagnosing bacterial infections remains largely unexplored [24]. However, high costs and limited availability hinder widespread clinical use and evaluation, which in turn restricts the growth of evidence supporting this technology. Additionally, its limited penetration depth of 500 μm allows for visualization mainly of the epidermis and parts of the dermis [21]. Nonetheless, this tool is among the most promising advances in paediatric wound imaging, reducing pain, procedural anxiety, and the risk of infection or secondary complications [21].

Thermography

Thermography uses infrared technology to detect changes in skin temperature, which can indicate underlying inflammation, infection, poor circulation, or stress. This non‐invasive, radiation‐free method provides real‐time assessment, making it useful for early infection detection and monitoring wound healing [25]. While this imaging modality has traditionally been used for neonatal monitoring and burn wound assessment, it has shown potential for tracking and guiding treatment interventions during wound healing in various SSTIs. By identifying thermal changes associated with either infection progression or resolution (e.g., a return to baseline skin and soft‐tissue temperature), this technology enables continuous assessment and can detect early signs of inflammation and wound progression [26, 27]. Although research on its application is limited, thermography has shown promise for monitoring superficial wounds in adult patients, including pressure ulcers and diabetic foot wounds [28]. However, its limitations include an inability to penetrate beyond the skin surface, a lack of specificity, and an absence of paediatric reference values. As a result, paediatric‐specific studies are scarce, and further evaluation of thermography in this population is needed before routine clinical use [26].

Biomarker‐Based Methods

The use of biomarkers in serum or tissue can detect physiological changes before visible signs of infection develop. Needle aspiration, biopsy, or swabbing of the wound surface allows for molecular‐level evaluation. Measuring levels of inflammatory markers and cytokines (e.g., interleukin‐1, interleukin‐6, matrix metalloproteinases) can not only identify infection but also monitor its progression [29]. Toll‐like receptors (TLR) are a family of pattern recognition receptors that detect different pathogen components and play a crucial role in the development of SSTIs. When TLRs are activated, they trigger a signalling cascade that promotes the production of inflammatory and antiviral cytokines [30]. By identifying specific and novel biomarkers in small tissue samples, superficial cutaneous wounds may be diagnosed earlier. While using specific biomarkers can be highly valuable, high costs, technical challenges, and longer processing times limit their practical use compared to directly examining the wound for signs of inflammation.

Serum biomarkers such as C‐reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), serum amyloid A, and fibrinogen also play a role in the early detection of superficial wounds. They are generally more time‐efficient than wound biomarkers due to their widespread availability and routine use in clinical practice. However, they also present challenges, including limited specificity, variable sensitivity in paediatric patients, and the influence of comorbidities. Children without serious systemic illnesses may not show elevated serum biomarkers, which can delay the detection of infections, while children with chronic inflammatory conditions like inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) may have chronically elevated biomarkers, affecting diagnostic accuracy [31]. Clinically, a complete blood count (CBC) with differential, along with the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), can also help with early wound assessment and evaluate the risk of local infection becoming systemic [5]. Although biomarker analysis has traditionally been used after clinical diagnosis, it is increasingly useful for diagnosing and managing superficial skin wounds.

DNA‐Based Assessment

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–based diagnostics offer rapid, culture‐independent detection of wound‐associated microbes by amplifying pathogen nucleic acids directly from a wound specimen (e.g., swab material or, preferably, deep tissue obtained after cleansing and debridement). In superficial wounds, biofilms, polymicrobial communities (including anaerobes), and prior antibiotic exposure can decrease culture yield. In this context, targeted real‐time PCR (qPCR) assays and multiplex PCR panels can identify specific organisms (including antimicrobial resistance markers, depending on the assay) within hours, enabling earlier and more precise antimicrobial selection and de‐escalation [5, 32, 33]. Broad‐range bacterial PCR methods, most often 16S rRNA gene PCR with sequencing and panbacterial qPCR, can further improve etiologic yield in selected cases (including culture‐negative infections), but require careful contamination control and clinical correlation because PCR detects nucleic acids (not necessarily viable organisms) and may also identify colonizing flora [5, 34, 35]. To address this issue, whole genome sequencing (WGS) can be used alongside 16S rRNA genome sequencing to provide a more comprehensive, genus‐level identification of skin pathogens [36]. WGS has been shown to excel in identifying rare and fastidious pathogens that may be missed by traditional wound culture. In samples from skin and soft tissue infections, WGS can detect pathogens at a significantly higher rate than culture methods, providing more accurate detection of many atypical organisms to which children may be particularly susceptible [37]. Major barriers to the widespread adoption of this innovative approach include the lengthy turnaround time, cost, and the need for specialized bioinformatics expertise, with only a limited number of academic medical centers currently offering WGS.

PCR can also be used to detect fungal pathogens in wounds when yeast or mould infections are suspected, such as in atypical or nonhealing wounds with necrotic tissue or in immunocompromised patients, or when microscopy suggests fungal elements but culture is negative or slow. Broad‐range (“panfungal”) PCR assays usually target conserved ribosomal loci near the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions, allowing amplification across various fungi and subsequent sequencing for genus‐ or species‐level identification. Targeted fungal qPCR assays are useful when a specific pathogen is strongly suspected [38, 39]. Studies evaluating broad‐range fungal PCR on tissue specimens support its role as a complementary test to microscopy and culture, providing a meaningful increase in diagnostic yield and faster organism identification in select patients [40, 41]. Although PCR is often employed after diagnosing a superficial wound, its main benefit is enabling early detection of infection and allowing prompt use of antimicrobial or antifungal treatments targeted to a specific pathogen [42].

Smart Dressings and Biometric Sensors

Recent innovations in smart dressings and sensor‐based technologies are leading the way in wound care, providing real‐time, noninvasive monitoring of skin wounds with potential applications in paediatrics. By detecting subtle physiological changes associated with wound infection and healing, these devices can deliver real‐time, continuous data that improves infection detection, guides prompt treatment, and promotes better healing outcomes. While many recent advances in smart dressings can release antimicrobial agents and healing factors, they primarily measure changes in pH, temperature, and oxygen levels [43]. Smart bandages and biosensors still in development primarily track wounds using electrochemical and/or colorimetric systems. These tools monitor local signs of inflammation and infection [44]. Electrochemical sensors offer the benefit of wireless connectivity and higher sensitivity than colorimetric sensors but require a power source, which can be inconvenient for patients. This challenge has spurred increased interest in fluorometric methods [45].

Clinically used fluorometric methods employ a device that emits ultraviolet light at approximately 405 nm, which penetrates superficial tissues to a depth of about 1.5 mm. The signal reflects surface and near‐surface bioburden. Several clinical studies have shown that red/cyan fluorescence correlates with elevated bacterial loads (commonly defined as ≥ 104 CFU/g), a threshold associated with delayed healing [46]. A currently marketed device used clinically is the MolecuLightR i:X and DX (MolecuLight, Pittsburgh, PA). This point‐of‐care device detects and locates increased bacterial burden in a wound. Additionally, the MolecuLight‐DXR includes thermal imaging for non‐contact skin temperature assessment (https://moleculight.com). While there is limited published preclinical work specifically validating the MolecuLight device, as opposed to its precursor (PRODIGI), this technology transitioned from the benchtop to clinical trials relatively quickly. The preclinical trials for the PRODIGI device demonstrated that fluorescence imaging improves wound sampling and treatment guidance, serving as the foundation for both 510(k) FDA approval as well as future MolecuLight studies [47]. In the FLAAG trial conducted by the manufacturer of the MolecuLight device, 350 patients' wounds were evaluated [46]. When combined with clinical assessment, this imaging technique improved the detection of ≥ 104 CFU/g across various wound types and prompted changes in care. A recent systematic review of 16 clinical studies using the MolecuLight device concluded it is clinically useful for identifying elevated bacterial load, guiding treatment, and enhancing long‐term outcomes [48]. Nonetheless, this device offers the benefit of rapid and real‐time bacterial load detection with high diagnostic accuracy, improving sampling guidance and treatment optimization. However, it has several limitations, including dependency on ambient light (requiring a dark environment), inability to specify the infecting bacteria (except for P. aeruginosa ), difficulty distinguishing biofilm infection from planktonic infection, and potential interference from artifacts like signals generated by sloughed tissue, ointments, or blood. It also requires metabolically active bacteria to produce a reliable signal [46]. Additionally, the device is costly, with an approximate price of $9000.00 USD per unit [49]. Studies have also shown that it is not superior to clinically used methods for detecting wound infection due to its relatively low sensitivity and specificity [50].

The newest device currently in clinical development for detecting bacteria or fungi in a skin wound is the WoundCueR biosensor (ParaNano Wound Care LLC, Oklahoma City, OK). This “smart bandage” is a flexible nanofiber membrane embedded in a wound dressing that changes colour when a critical level of bacterial or fungal lipase activity is detected. The threshold for detection has not been publicly disclosed but is likely approximately 104 to 105 CFU/g equivalent. The sensor is placed on or within the primary wound dressing and is designed to integrate seamlessly with existing dressings. Once applied, the “smart bandage” is sealed with a transparent adhesive backing (Figure 2). The sensor detects bacterial or fungal lipase, which triggers the nanofiber membrane to produce a visible colour change from yellow to green. Its main benefit is the very early detection of pathogens capable of causing infection, thus allowing for earlier intervention. A comparison of the two devices mentioned earlier (i.e., MolecuLight‐DXR vs. WoundCueR biosensor) is shown in Table 1. The WoundCueR device offers a faster, noninvasive, and cost‐effective alternative to traditional diagnostic methods, which can be time‐consuming and delay clinical decision making. To date, the WoundCueR biosensor has not yet received FDA marketing authorization and the device is reported by the manufacturer to be in early clinical trials. Consequently, the preclinical body of evidence supporting the use of this device remains proprietary and is not yet publicly available. Nevertheless, the growing understanding of wound biosensors represents significant progress in wound care as they can provide rapid detection of potential or actual wound infections [57].

Theragnostic Device Development

Theragnostic device development for paediatric SSTIs is an emerging field focused on early wound detection and management, combining real‐time diagnostic capabilities with on‐demand therapeutic interventions. One popular theragnostic approach for wound detection and management is point‐of‐care ultrasound (POCUS). POCUS utilizes non‐ionizing imaging to monitor real‐time anatomical changes while guiding therapeutic procedures such as incision and drainage, simultaneously detecting wounds and providing actionable treatment decisions [16]. However, this approach requires patients to be physically present in a hospital or clinic, along with extensive provider training and significant resource investment. Alternatively, at the forefront of wound detection are theragnostic hydrogels that incorporate pH‐sensitive indicators to directly report infection levels to a smartphone or device from home. These hydrogels can also deliver photodynamic therapy using reactive oxygen species (ROS) to achieve bacterial clearance. Notably, light‐triggered hydrogels have shown significant improvements in wound monitoring and healing in preclinical models of Staphylococcus aureus ‐infected abscesses [58]. Similarly, other emerging theragnostic strategies focus on integrating responsive biomaterials, such as hydrogels, with real‐time sensing to monitor wound pH, glucose, and temperature while simultaneously delivering targeted therapeutic interventions like ROS and antibiotics [59]. Although some aspects, like antibiotic delivery, have demonstrated broad effectiveness, these platforms are still largely untested in paediatric human populations, underscoring crucial translational gaps.

Future Directions

Given the limited standardization of wound care practices, there is a pressing need for additional longitudinal studies to evaluate the effectiveness of available technologies for early detection of wound infections. As summarized in Table 2, which compares current and emerging wound detection modalities, this evidence is essential to guiding prompt institution of local and, in some cases, systemic treatments. Quick intervention is necessary not only to prevent the spread of a wound infection but also to improve treatment outcomes. While methods used to detect wound infections in adults can be adapted for paediatric patients, they often require careful modification and the use of additional strategies to address developmental, physiological and psychosocial differences [60]. Therefore, developing age‐specific wound‐monitoring tools designed for paediatric use presents unique challenges that demand customized assessment strategies [61]. Fortunately, advances in wound care are growing, and combining clinical assessment with new and emerging technologies through multimodal detection approaches has the potential to considerably enhance wound management.

Conclusion

Effective wound management starts with early detection and recognition of active infections. Emerging technologies, namely biosensors, have the potential to improve clinical outcomes for patients with superficial wound infections. These innovations could transform wound care by enabling earlier detection of infections, better clinical outcomes, and reduced burden on patients and caregivers. However, more research, including randomized controlled trials, is necessary to fully assess the effectiveness of each new advancement in wound infection detection and to define its role in wound care.
