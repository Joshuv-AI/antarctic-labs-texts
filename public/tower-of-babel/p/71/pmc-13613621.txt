Targeting the cancer metabolism—immunity interface: update and perspectives
Authors: Emma Di Carlo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic crosstalk between cancer cells and immune cells is now recognized as a major determinant of immune escape and resistance to anticancer treatments. Cancer cells profoundly reshape the metabolic landscape of the tumor microenvironment, driving nutrient competition, hypoxia, and the accumulation of immunosuppressive oncometabolites that collectively blunt antitumor immunity. Effector T cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells are exposed to nutrient deprivation and suppressive metabolites, including lactate, adenosine, and kynurenine, resulting in impaired T cell proliferation and cytotoxic function and expansion of metabolically adapted regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells. Cancer-associated fibroblasts further reinforce this metabolic reprogramming through extracellular matrix remodeling, secretion of immunosuppressive metabolites, and nutrient recycling that supports tumor growth. Abnormal tumor vasculature sustains metabolic stress by causing uneven perfusion, hypoxia, and acidosis, thereby limiting immune cell infiltration, and promoting immune exhaustion. In addition, diet- and microbiome-driven metabolic cues dynamically shape cancer–immunity interactions and therapeutic responses. Targeting key metabolic checkpoints, including glycolysis, adenosine signaling, tryptophan metabolism, fatty acid oxidation, and lactate production, has emerged as a promising strategy to restore antitumor immunity. Nevertheless, metabolic heterogeneity, context-dependent immune responses, and safety concerns pose persistent challenges to its successful implementation. Recent advances in biomarker development, patient stratification, and rational combination strategies underpin the clinical translation of metabolic–immune vulnerabilities in cancer therapy. Integrating metabolic interventions with immune checkpoint blockade or adoptive cell therapies has demonstrated synergistic effects in preclinical and early clinical studies, enhancing T cell persistence and cytotoxic function within metabolically hostile tumor microenvironments. This review addresses these issues and delineates the mechanistic basis of the dynamic interplay between cancer metabolism and immune regulation. It discusses how anti-cancer therapies affect metabolic and immune pathways and highlights next-generation, metabolically targeted therapies that leverage newly uncovered, tumor-specific rewiring of glycolysis, mitochondrial function, and nutrient uptake. Special emphasis is given to the development of first-in-class inhibitors targeting glutaminase, lipid biosynthesis, one-carbon pathways, and redox homeostasis, which, when paired with immunotherapy or conventional treatments, offer unprecedented opportunities to overcome metabolic barriers, abrogate resistance, and achieve durable immune control of cancer.Keywords: Tumor metabolism, Oncometabolites, Immune cell metabolism, Immunotherapy, Therapy resistance, Microbiome and cancer, Diet and cancer, Tumor microenvironment, Cancer-associated fibroblasts

Background

Recent advances in cancer metabolomics have positioned metabolic regulation as a key determinant of tumor behavior and patient prognosis, highlighting the importance of defining the underlying molecular mechanisms to enable personalized and durable cancer therapies [1]. Metabolomics is a branch of systems biology that focuses on profiling metabolites, such as sugars, amino acids, lipids, and nucleotides, the end products of cellular processes. Because metabolism is highly sensitive to genetic changes, environment, and disease states, metabolomics provides a "snapshot" of cellular activity [2, 3]. Tumor metabolism refers to the way cancer cells process nutrients and energy to support their rapid proliferation, survival under stress, immune evasion and spread. In healthy cells, metabolism is usually balanced, and oxidative phosphorylation efficiently generates energy, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) from glucose, fats, and proteins [4, 5]. By contrast, tumor cells reprogram their metabolism and exploit unusual molecular pathways due to mitochondrial dysfunction [6]. Mitochondrial dysfunction is central to how cancer cells reprogram metabolism [7] and is caused by (1) mutations in mitochondrial (mt)DNA (mutations in genes encoding respiratory chain proteins, Complex I–V, that impair oxidative phosphorylation, OXPHOS), leading to reduced ATP production and increased reliance on glycolysis [8]; (2) altered tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle enzymes (mutations in succinate dehydrogenase, SDH, fumarate hydratase, FH, or isocitrate dehydrogenase, IDH) [9, 10], leading to accumulation of “oncometabolites” such as, succinate, fumarate, and 2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG). Oncometabolites inhibit α-ketoglutarate–dependent dioxygenases, altering epigenetics (DNA/histone methylation) and HIF stabilization (pseudo-hypoxia activates angiogenesis and glycolysis genes) [6].

Aberrant mitochondrial dynamics orchestrate cancer-associated metabolic reprogramming, fundamentally encompassing: (a) Shift toward aerobic glycolysis, known as the Warburg effect, which refers to the tendency of cancer cells to preferentially use aerobic glycolysis (the breakdown of glucose into lactate) with rapid ATP generation, and increase in lactate secretion, even when oxygen is plentiful and oxidative phosphorylation (the more efficient ATP-generating process in mitochondria) is available [11]. This condition falls within the concept of, (b) Biosynthesis priority, whereby tumor metabolic reprogramming shifts from ATP generation toward the production of nucleotides, amino acids, and lipids required for cell proliferation. By using these building blocks, mitochondrial dysfunction redirects metabolic flux toward anabolic pathways, prioritizing biosynthesis over energetic efficiency. (c) Increased nutrient uptake, characterized by elevated consumption of glucose, glutamine, and other nutrients to fuel malignant growth [12]. (d) Glutamine addiction, reflecting the reliance of cancer cells on glutaminolysis to sustain proliferation and viability. In this pathway, glutamine is converted to glutamate by glutaminase and subsequently to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), which feeds into the TCA cycle (Krebs cycle), to replenish intermediates via anaplerosis [13, 14]. Glutaminolysis provides nitrogen for nucleotide and amino acid biosynthesis and contributes to NADPH production for antioxidant defence and biosynthetic reactions (14). (e) Redox imbalance, the equilibrium between oxidants (reactive oxygen species, ROS) and antioxidants (glutathione, thioredoxin, superoxide dismutase), is subverted in cancer cells [15, 16], which maintain elevated ROS levels compared to normal cells, but also upregulate antioxidant defences to survive under oxidative stress. Dysfunctional mitochondria struggle to maintain the NAD + /NADH balance [17]. Cancer cells adapt by upregulating lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) to regenerate NAD + [18, 19]. (f) Adaptation to, and reshaping of, the microenvironment, which means that cancer cells can switch metabolic pathways depending on stress, such as hypoxia or nutrient deprivation, but they also reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) and influence stromal and immune cells by altering nutrient availability and producing metabolites that regulate extracellular matrix (ECM) composition, angiogenesis, and immune responses. Cancer cells, in turn, are conditioned by the metabolic reprogramming they forced on the surrounding microenvironmental components [20, 21]. Therefore, tumor metabolism is reprogrammed to use multiple unusual pathways, orchestrated by interactions with key stromal and immune cells, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune populations [22]. This interplay contributes to pronounced metabolic heterogeneity among cancer cell populations, driven by differences in genetic background, microenvironmental conditions, and functional state. Highly proliferative cells often rely on aerobic glycolysis, enabling rapid ATP generation and diversion of intermediates into biosynthetic pathways. In contrast, quiescent or stem-like cancer cells preferentially engage oxidative phosphorylation, supporting long-term survival and resistance to stress. Hypoxic tumor regions further promote glycolytic flux and lactate production, whereas well-oxygenated areas maintain mitochondrial respiration and fatty acid oxidation. Additionally, some subpopulations display metabolic flexibility, switching between glycolysis and oxidative metabolism in response to nutrient availability or therapeutic pressure. This metabolic diversity not only drives treatment resistance, immune evasion, and tumor progression, but also directly constrains the efficacy of immunotherapies [23, 24]. While checkpoint blockade in effector immune cells has revolutionized cancer treatment, the functional impairment of these cells, shaped by the rewired metabolism of cancer populations, significantly limits therapeutic success [25]. Integration of immunotherapy with strategies targeting cancer metabolism and its interplay with the immune system has therefore become a primary challenge.

This review highlights the mechanisms driving metabolic reprogramming in cancer, with emphasis on the cancer-immune response interplay, and the roles played by other microenvironmental components, while discussing effective tools for monitoring and re-educating cancer metabolism through approaches tailored to the specific patient’s tumor. Preclinical studies and ongoing clinical trials focused on developing “metabolism-targeted” cancer therapies will be highlighted, along with the prospects and limitations for their future developments and clinical translatability.

Exposure to abnormal lipid levels and metabolic competition with tumor cells substantially affects immune cell metabolism and functions [44].

T cells. In the lipid-rich, nutrient-poor TME, T cells switch to FAO. Chronic FAO is less efficient for effector T cell function, promoting T cell dysfunction. Excess lipids cause lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress [45]. T cells rely on oxidative phosphorylation for memory and effector functions, but mitochondrial dysfunction (excessive FAO increases electron flux through the ETC resulting in electron leakage) reduces energy production leading to reduced cytotoxicity and IFN-γ secretion. In fact, CD8⁺ T cells increase expression of PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3, hallmarks of exhaustion [46, 47], while CD4⁺ T cells skew toward Tregs, which thrive in lipid-rich environments [48].

4.Hypoxia and related metabolites. Tumors often outgrow their blood supply with resulting low oxygen levels. Hypoxia stabilizes hypoxia inducible factor (HIF)-1α in both tumor and immune cells, upregulates CD39/CD73 (critical ectoenzymes involved in generating extracellular adenosine, a potent immunosuppressive metabolite in the TME) and causes the accumulation of metabolites, such as lactate (from anaerobic glycolysis), adenosine (from ATP degradation), ROS, succinate, and other TCA intermediates, which contribute to the reshaping of immune cell metabolism and functions [55]. Indeed, hypoxia forces Tand NK cells into inefficient glycolysis, limiting ATP production, while Tregs and M2 macrophages thrive on FAO and OXPHOS. HIF-1α-driven changes enhance Treg stability, DC tolerogenicity, and M2 polarization. Effector T and NK cells experience exhaustion and functional impairment [55]. Lactate and adenosine suppress cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory functions [56]. ROS and succinate modulate signaling, promoting tolerogenic phenotypes [57]. ROS damage mitochondria and impair oxidative metabolism, leading to T cell exhaustion, while blocking the metabolic remodelling required for antigen processing in DCs. ROS trigger ferroptosis (a cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation), apoptosis, and signaling dysfunction in effector cells [58]. Hypoxia-related oncometabolites, such as succinate, fumarate and 2-Hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), stabilize HIF and cause epigenetic changes that drive cancer progression. 2-HG plays a major role in cancer metabolism. While mutant isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH1 or IDH2) is the major pathological source of (R)-2-HG in cancers, non-mutant cells can also accumulate (S)-2-HG under conditions such as hypoxia, acidosis, or redox stress. 2-HG alters epigenetic regulation of genes governing immune cell metabolism, and blocks differentiation into metabolically active effector cells. It also reprograms metabolic pathways in CD8+ T cells and impairs their cytotoxicity and IFN-γ signaling, compromising the antitumor response [59, 60].

Hypoxia and related metabolites. Tumors often outgrow their blood supply with resulting low oxygen levels. Hypoxia stabilizes hypoxia inducible factor (HIF)-1α in both tumor and immune cells, upregulates CD39/CD73 (critical ectoenzymes involved in generating extracellular adenosine, a potent immunosuppressive metabolite in the TME) and causes the accumulation of metabolites, such as lactate (from anaerobic glycolysis), adenosine (from ATP degradation), ROS, succinate, and other TCA intermediates, which contribute to the reshaping of immune cell metabolism and functions [55]. Indeed, hypoxia forces Tand NK cells into inefficient glycolysis, limiting ATP production, while Tregs and M2 macrophages thrive on FAO and OXPHOS. HIF-1α-driven changes enhance Treg stability, DC tolerogenicity, and M2 polarization. Effector T and NK cells experience exhaustion and functional impairment [55]. Lactate and adenosine suppress cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory functions [56]. ROS and succinate modulate signaling, promoting tolerogenic phenotypes [57]. ROS damage mitochondria and impair oxidative metabolism, leading to T cell exhaustion, while blocking the metabolic remodelling required for antigen processing in DCs. ROS trigger ferroptosis (a cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation), apoptosis, and signaling dysfunction in effector cells [58]. Hypoxia-related oncometabolites, such as succinate, fumarate and 2-Hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), stabilize HIF and cause epigenetic changes that drive cancer progression. 2-HG plays a major role in cancer metabolism. While mutant isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH1 or IDH2) is the major pathological source of (R)-2-HG in cancers, non-mutant cells can also accumulate (S)-2-HG under conditions such as hypoxia, acidosis, or redox stress. 2-HG alters epigenetic regulation of genes governing immune cell metabolism, and blocks differentiation into metabolically active effector cells. It also reprograms metabolic pathways in CD8+ T cells and impairs their cytotoxicity and IFN-γ signaling, compromising the antitumor response [59, 60].

Effects of the microbiome on cancer and immune cell metabolic programs

The TME is a complex and dynamic system composed of cancer, immune, and stromal cells, along with diverse extracellular factors [61]. Increasing evidence identifies the microbiome, both gut and intra-tumoral, as a key regulator of this environment, influencing not only immune responses but also cellular metabolism [62]. Through the production of bioactive metabolites and activation of specific signaling pathways, microbes can reprogram tumor and immune cell metabolism in a context-dependent manner, either promoting tumor progression or enhancing antitumor immunity. This section examines microbiome-driven effects on tumor and immune cell metabolism, their integrated crosstalk, and related therapeutic implications.

Microbiome effects on tumor cell metabolism

Nutrient competition and metabolic reprogramming. Microbes can consume or modify nutrients (glucose, amino acids, lipids), limiting their availability for tumor cells. For example, bacterial metabolism of tryptophan into indole derivatives can shift tumor cell metabolic pathways primarily through the activation of the AhR and by modulating the host's immune response to create a more permissive TME [63, 64].

For example, bacterial metabolism of tryptophan into indole derivatives can shift tumor cell metabolic pathways primarily through the activation of the AhR and by modulating the host's immune response to create a more permissive TME [63, 64].

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Butyrate, propionate, and acetate from gut commensals can regulate histone acetylation in tumor cells, affecting gene expression linked to glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation. Butyrate can act as both an energy source and a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, sometimes suppressing tumor growth [65]. Due to the Warburg effect, cancerous cells rely on glucose as their primary energy source, so butyrate accumulates and functions as an HDAC inhibitor.

Bile acid metabolites. Microbial transformation of bile acids can activate the nuclear receptor Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR) and the cell surface receptor, Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) in tumor cells, altering lipid and mitochondrial metabolism and potentially promoting carcinogenesis, notably in hepatobiliary and colorectal cancers [66, 67].

Polyamines and oncometabolites. Bacteria produce polyamines, such as putrescine, and spermidine, which promote tumor proliferation and DNA stability. Some microbes produce lactate (e.g., Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Enterococcus) or succinate (e.g., Bacteroides, Prevotella, Propionibacterium), feeding tumor cell metabolic pathways and supporting the Warburg effect. Lactate, a glycolytic end product, fuels TCA cycle, serves as a building block for macromolecules, and modulates the TME to support immunosuppression and angiogenesis [68]. Succinate, a TCA cycle intermediate, also accumulates in tumors and contributes to pro-tumoral functions, including epigenetic alterations and oxidative stress, further fostering cancer progression [69].

Microbiome effects on immune cell metabolism

Regulation of T cell function through metabolic cues. SCFAs, most abundant microbial metabolites, promote memory and effector T cell differentiation by enhancing mitochondrial metabolism and acetyl-CoA availability. Tryptophan catabolites (via indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, IDO, or microbial kynurenine pathways) suppress T cell proliferation and favour Treg differentiation. Gut bacteria-derived metabolites, such as butyrate, induce Treg cells through inhibition of HDACs [70, 71].

Impact on myeloid cells (macrophages, DCs, MDSCs). Bacterial metabolites (like glutamine, succinate, and itaconate) can polarize macrophages toward M1 (anti-tumor, glycolysis-driven) [72, 73] or M2 (pro-tumor, oxidative metabolism-driven, heavily depending on OXPHOS and FAO) [74] phenotypes. The large bowel microbial fermentation product, butyrate, facilitates M2 macrophage polarization [75], increasing activation of the H3K9/STAT6 signaling pathway [76]. Microbiome-driven lactate and adenosine production in the TME can skew DCs toward a tolerogenic state, dampening antigen presentation [56].

NK cells and innate lymphoid cell (ILC) metabolism. Microbial-derived butyrate enhances NK cell cytotoxicity by increasing glycolytic flux [77]. Conversely, certain microbial metabolites dampen NK metabolism, reducing cytotoxic activity. SCFAs, such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate, at high concentrations, can suppress NK activity, lowering levels of activating receptors (TRAIL, NKp30, NKp44) and production of cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-22, granzyme B, granzyme A, perforin) [78]. The effects of microbial-derived metabolites on ILCs are subset-specific, since ILC1, ILC2, and ILC3 respond differently. Bacterial fermentation of dietary fibres (e.g., by Bacteroides, Firmicutes) produce SCFAs, which modulate IFN-γ production in NK and ILC1 cells, inhibit ILC2 activation and IL-5/IL-13 secretion dampening allergic inflammation, while promote IL-22 production in ILC3 strengthening epithelial barrier. SCFAs generally promote barrier protection (ILC3) and limit excessive inflammation (ILC2) [79, 80].

Microbiome–immune–tumor interactions

Immune checkpoint therapy sensitivity. Microbiome composition (e.g., Akkermansia muciniphila, Bifidobacterium spp.) correlates with response to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [81, 82]. Bifidobacterium spp. and Akkermansia muciniphila enhance DC activation and CD8⁺ T cell priming. Improved T cell metabolic fitness, which means better mitochondrial respiration, and less exhaustion, improves antitumor responses and checkpoint inhibitor efficacy. Specific bacteria, such as A. muciniphila in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and Bifidobacterium species in advanced melanoma, are associated with better responses [83, 84]. Specific bacteria can be positively or negatively associated with response to treatment, progression-free survival, and overall survival. [84].

Metabolic crosstalk loops. Tumor-derived lactate suppresses local T cell glycolysis [56], but microbiome derived SCFAs can partially rescue T cell metabolic activity [85]. By producing bioactive metabolites, such as SCFAs, secondary bile acids, and indole derivatives, that enter the circulation and signal through host metabolic receptors, including the bile acid–sensing receptors FXR and the G-protein–coupled receptor TGR5 (in the liver, intestine, and adipose tissue), the microbiome can modulate the host’s systemic metabolism, regulating glucose homeostasis and lipid utilization, which in turn determine the availability of glucose, lipids, and amino acids within the TME, influencing tumor cell metabolism and immune cell function [86]. Accordingly, microbiome-targeted interventions represent a powerful complement to metabolic and immunotherapeutic strategies, capable of reshaping the TME’s metabolic landscape and of overcoming nutrient and signaling barriers to markedly enhance treatment efficacy.

Therapeutic implications. Emerging strategies to modulate the microbiome and thereby reprogram the TME are uncovering promising therapeutic opportunities to establish a metabolically favorable and immunologically supportive niche, ultimately enhancing antitumor immune responses.

Microbiome modulation strategies include, probiotics, prebiotics, and postbiotics to supply beneficial metabolites (SCFAs, inosine); fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) to restore a favourable microbiome for immunotherapy response [87, 88]; engineered bacteria designed to produce metabolites (such as butyrate, propionate, acetate, lactate and lactate analogues, succinate, fumarate, arginine or arginine analogues), that can shift the TME from immunosuppressive and nutrient-deprived to immune-permissive and metabolically supportive for effector cells, improving responses to immunotherapy or chemotherapy [89].

In conclusion, the microbiome alters the metabolic landscape of the TME by producing metabolites (SCFAs, bile acids, tryptophan derivatives, polyamines) that can either fuel tumor cells or reprogram immune cells [92]. These interactions critically determine whether the balance tips toward tumor progression or effective anti-tumor immunity. Dietary factors significantly influence the gut microbiota [94], which in turn interacts with the host and the TME to affect tumor development.

Effects of diet on cancer and immune cell metabolic programs

There’s a growing and fast-moving literature on how what we eat changes the nutrient landscape inside tumors and thereby reprograms both cancer and immune cell metabolism [92–96]. Diet alters systemic nutrient and hormone levels, glucose, insulin/IGF, lipids, amino acids, ketone bodies, and therefore the composition of the TME. Tumor cells and infiltrating immune cells compete for the same nutrients, such as glucose and amino acids within the TME. This metabolic competition, coupled with the TME's acidic and hypoxic conditions, impairs immune cell function, and promotes tumor survival, creating a suppressive environment for anti-tumor immunity [97, 98]. Metabolic conditions that favor tumor growth, such as lipid abundance, hyperglycemia, enhanced insulin signaling, and obesity, can suppress anti-tumor immunity. Conversely, interventions that limit tumor-preferred fuel sources may restore immune function [99, 100], although these effects are strongly context-dependent. These diet- and metabolism-driven outcomes reflect tightly coupled interactions among nutrient availability, tumor metabolic programming, and immune cell activity within the TME. To clarify these relationships, the following sections examine (i) how dietary changes reshape tumor cell metabolism, (ii) how they alter immune-cell metabolic fitness and effector function, (iii) evidence from dietary intervention studies highlighting both benefits and risks, and (iv) the resulting translational implications for cancer prevention and therapy.

Mechanisms underlying how diet changes tumor cell metabolism.

Glucose/insulin axis. High-carbohydrate intake triggers higher glucose/insulin/IGF signaling, which can promote glycolysis, anabolic growth and mTOR activity in many tumors. Conversely, carbohydrate restriction or lowered insulin can reduce glycolytic flux and mTOR signaling in some cancers [101, 102]. Many solid tumors, including glioblastoma, some breast, colon, pancreatic cancers, rely on aerobic glycolysis for ATP and biosynthesis. In these cancers, restricting carbohydrate intake reduces substrate availability for glycolysis leading to a decreased glycolytic flux. Preclinical studies show glioblastomas (which rely heavily on aerobic glycolysis) are especially sensitive to glucose restriction or ketogenic diets (reduced carbohydrate intake and increased fat consumption), which can reduce angiogenesis, inflammation, and tumor growth, while improving sensitivity to radiation or chemotherapy and prolonging survival [103].

Tumors can use extracellular lipids for beta-oxidation and membrane synthesis; certain lipid mixes (and obesity) promote immunosuppressive myeloid programs and impair CD8+ T cells [104]. Obesity and high-fat diets alter circulating lipid levels (free fatty acids, cholesterol, triglycerides, oxidized lipids). TAMs and MDSCs can take up excess lipids, via scavenger receptors (CD36, Fatty Acid Transport Proteins, FATPs), and reprogram towards an immunosuppressive state [105, 106]. This condition implies PPARγ and sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP-1) activation, leading to lipid droplet biogenesis; secretion of IL-10, TGF-β, and arginase-1, which suppress T cell activation and cytotoxic functions, while enhance checkpoint ligand (PD-L1, VISTA) expression in cancer and immune cells [107–110]. In obesity-driven breast cancer models, lipid-loaded myeloid cells are more suppressive and promote tumor progression [111]. Recent work shows the source of fat (saturated versus mono/polyunsaturated) matters for tumor growth and anti-tumor immunity [112, 113].

Tumor-expressed enzymes, such as IDO, can deplete tryptophan locally and suppress T cells [37]. Manipulating amino acid supply or enzymes can shift tumor/immune phenotypes [116, 117].

Mechanisms underlying how diet alters immune-cell metabolism and function

Nutrient competition. Activated T cells need glucose and amino acids to sustain glycolysis and effector function. Tumors that heavily consume glucose or glutamine can starve T cells, leading to functional exhaustion. Likewise, lipid laden TMEs promote FAO programs that can reduce T cell effector activity [118]. Targeted interventions aimed at modulating lipid metabolism in Tregs have been recognized as an innovative and promising approach to enhance the effectiveness of tumor immunotherapy [48, 119].

Nutrient/energy sensing pathways like AMPK and mTOR (which are essentially the cell’s metabolic “decision-makers”), hypoxia, and metabolic byproducts, such as lactate, adenosine, and kynurenine, act as immunosuppressive checkpoints in that they directly reprogram immune cells into a suppressive or exhausted state [120]. Many of these metabolites are responsive to diet or to the systemic metabolic state. Targeting metabolic checkpoints can reinvigorate anti-tumor immunity [121].

Dietary intervention studies. What’s promising and what’s risky

Caloric restriction (CR), fasting and fasting-mimicking diets. Preclinical models often show that CR or fasting can reduce tumor growth, sensitize tumors to chemotherapy orradiotherapy, and sometimes improve T cell responses [122]. Human clinical trials are still early and show mixed results; oberved benefits appear context-dependent and can vary according to tumor type and patient nutritional status [123]. CR may not be safe or appropriate for all patients, since it can worsen cachexia, which affects 50–80% of patients with advanced cancer [124], leading to fatigue, immune suppression, and decreased tolerance to therapy.

Several early-phase clinical trials are testing fasting/KD or metabolic drugs alongside standard therapy. Evidence is promising, but not conclusive. Translation into the clinical practice requires addressing key issues such as, careful tumor-type stratification, patient selection, and safety monitoring.

Role of stromal fibroblasts in the tumor metabolism—immune system relationship

Stromal fibroblasts, especially cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), play a central role in coordinating the interplay between tumor metabolism and the immune microenvironment. Their function extends well beyond structural support; they dynamically regulate nutrient availability, metabolic signaling pathways, and immune cell behavior within the TME [129].

The metabolic contributions of stromal fibroblasts in the TME include: (a) Reverse Warburg Effect. CAFs often undergo aerobic glycolysis, producing lactate and pyruvate [130]. Tumor cells can then use these metabolites through oxidative phosphorylation, enhancing their growth. (b) Nutrient Supply. CAFs secrete amino acids, such as alanine and glutamine, and lipids that fuel cancer cell metabolism [131]. (c) ECM Remodelling. Increased ECM stiffness and altered composition impair nutrient and oxygen diffusion, driving tumor hypoxia and metabolic stress. (d) Mitochondrial Transfer. CAFs can transfer mitochondria to tumor cells via tunnelling nanotubes or extracellular vesicles, boosting cancer cell bioenergetics [132, 133].

Cancer-associated reprogramming of fibroblast metabolism influences immune regulation through multiple mechanisms, including (a) Nutrient Competition with Immune Cells. CAF glycolysis depletes glucose, reducing availability for effector T cells and NK cells, weakening their cytotoxic functions. High lactate production acidifies the TME, suppressing CD8⁺ T cell and NK cell activity, while fostering Treg recruitment and activation, and favouring MDSC proliferation and immunosuppressive functions [134, 135]. (b) Cytokine and Chemokine Secretion. CAFs release TGF-β, IL-6, and CXCL12, which suppress cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) recruitment and function, and promote immunosuppressive cell populations (Tregs, MDSCs, TAMs). (c) Immune Checkpoint Modulation. CAFs can upregulate PD-L1/PD-L2 on immune and stromal cells indirectly through cytokine signaling, reinforcing T cell exhaustion [136]. (d) Physical Barrier to Immune Cells. Dense, collagen-rich ECM produced by CAFs hinders CD8⁺ T cell infiltration into tumor nests, creating an “immune-excluded” tumor phenotype and contributing to resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) across multiple solid tumor types [137, 138].

CAF metabolic activity reshapes the immune microenvironment and influences cancer metabolism–immunity crosstalk through several mechanisms, including (a) Lactate accumulation, which promotes immunosuppressive niches; (b) Amino acid metabolism (e.g., arginine and tryptophan), which regulates T cell activation versus exhaustion. CAF-derived metabolites inhibit CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, in part by impairing glycolysis, and promote Treg expansion, as Tregs better tolerate acidic, lactate-rich conditions; (c) Lipid metabolism, in CAFs is characterized by enhanced lipid synthesis, uptake, and secretion, which enriches the TME with fatty acids and other lipids that promote macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype. CAFs also modulate macrophage expression of enzymes involved in FAO and lipid storage, thereby enhancing oxidative metabolism, a hallmark of M2 polarization [105]; (d) CAF-derived metabolites (lactate, kynurenine, PGE2, etc.), which skew DC metabolism away from oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis, leading to impaired maturation and reduced costimulatory molecule expression, so that they become poor antigen-presenters and may even promote Treg induction [139].

Immune cells, in turn, markedly modulate the metabolic activity of CAFs [61]. Activated CD8⁺ T cells secrete IFN-γ and TNF-α, which can drive CAFs toward a less pro-tumorigenic, more inflammatory phenotype, often referred to as “immune-activating CAFs,” although in many tumors CAFs remain resistant to immune-mediated clearance. In contrast, Th2 and Treg cells promote fibroblast differentiation into immunosuppressive CAFs through the secretion of IL-4, IL-13, and IL-10, thereby enhancing extracellular matrix deposition and fostering immune exclusion. Additionally, TAMs release TGF-β, IL-6, PDGF, and VEGF, which stimulate CAF proliferation, drive their differentiation into myofibroblast-like cells, and increase the production of extracellular matrix proteins. TAM-derived exosomes and microvesicles carrying microRNAs or proteins can also reprogram fibroblasts into CAFs. Activated CAFs, in turn, feedback by producing signals, such as CCL2, CXCL12, IL-1β, and matrix metalloproteinases, which attract more macrophages, promote their M2-like immunosuppressive polarization, and remodel the extracellular matrix to favour tumor growth and invasion [140].

Overall, by promoting tumor progression via metabolite supply, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immunosuppressive network formation in the TME, CAFs represent key therapeutic targets to improve the effectiveness of conventional and immunotherapy treatments.

Therapeutic implications. Current strategies aimed at disrupting bidirectional signaling loops between CAFs and immune cells are focused on: (a) targeting CAF metabolism. Inhibiting glycolysis or lactate export from CAFs could improve T cell function [141], or (b) reprogramming CAFs. Converting activated CAFs back to a quiescent state reduces their immunosuppressive and metabolic supportive roles. CAF-directed therapies, combined with ICIs or metabolic inhibitors, may synergize to restore antitumor immunity [142].

In summary, CAFs function as metabolic “feeders” for tumor cells, while simultaneously starving and suppressing immune effector cells. Their ability to remodel the metabolic landscape of the TME directly influences immune evasion and cancer progression [143, 144].

Role of vascularization in the cancer metabolism—immune system relationship

Newly formed vessels provide oxygen and nutrients, such as glucose, amino acids, and lipids, which tumors need for rapid proliferation. However, tumor-driven angiogenesis is usually abnormal, i.e. disorganized, leaky, and uneven, leading to regional hypoxia, forcing tumor cells to reprogram their metabolism. In poorly perfused regions, cells switch to aerobic glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation. Hypoxia stabilizes HIF-1α, which increases glycolytic enzymes (increase in glucose uptake, and lactate production), and upregulates VEGF, creating a feedback loop that promotes further angiogenesis [143]. Accordingly, tumors preferentially rely on glycolysis despite the presence of oxygen, leading to lactate production and acidification of the microenvironment [144].

In well-vascularized regions, cancer cells primarily depend on oxidative phosphorylation, whereas in poorly vascularized regions, cancer cells rely on glycolysis, FAO, or glutamine metabolism. This condition creates metabolic symbiosis, in which hypoxic cells produce lactate while well-oxygenated cells use lactate as a fuel, thereby promoting efficient resource sharing within the tumor.

Angiogenesis requires endothelial cells (ECs) to switch from a quiescent to an activated state via proangiogenic factors or hypoxia in a tightly regulated process, which results in EC metabolic reprogramming [145, 146]. Angiogenic ECs primarily utilize glycolysis for ATP production, while quiescent ECs upregulate FAO and downregulate other metabolic pathways to reduce oxidative stress. In tumor ECs, the glycolytic activator 6-Phosphofructo-2-kinase/Fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) is upregulated to drive tumor angiogenesis, and the increased phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) expression diverts 3-phosphoglycerate (a glycolytic intermediate) into the serine synthesis pathway (SSP), leading to nucleotide and protein synthesis during angiogenic sprouting. Glutamine and glutamine-derived aspartate are also critical metabolites supporting EC migration and neo-vessel formation [147, 148].

While functional vessels help clear lactate, CO₂, and other byproducts, abnormal angiogenesis determines waste build up leading to acidosis, which selects for aggressive, stress-resistant tumor clones [149]. Tumor vascularization plays a critical role in shaping both cancer metabolism and the immune system’s ability to interact with cancer cells. Connections between tumor metabolism—vascularization—immunity can be summarized in the following key points.

Tumor vascularization and metabolism. Blood vessels provide glucose, amino acids, lipids, and oxygen, which are required for rapid tumor growth. Poorly formed tumor vasculature, however, often leads to nutrient deprivation and hypoxia, forcing tumor cells to switch from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis [150]. Tumor cells adapt metabolically (e.g., using lactate, fatty acids, or autophagy) to nutrient deprivation, which creates diverse metabolic niches. Inefficient vascular clearance causes lactate accumulation, acidifying the microenvironment. This metabolic byproduct directly affects immune cell activity [25].

Beyond the influence of vascularization and other components of the complex TME, anti-cancer therapies, tailored to tumor type and stage, are critical drivers of metabolic regulation in cancer and immune cells.

Impact of anti-cancer therapies on cancer metabolism

Anti-cancer therapies invariably impact tumor metabolism, either as a primary mechanism of action or as a downstream consequence of treatment. A central question, therefore, is how major treatment modalities, such as immunotherapy, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, anti-angiogenic agents and targeted therapies, reshape tumor metabolic programs.

• Targeted therapies are designed to inhibit specific oncogenic signaling pathways that regulate both proliferation and metabolism. The main categories are monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting extracellular receptors or ligands, and kinase inhibitors .

Monoclonal Abs targeting extracellular receptors or ligands. Their mechanism of action consists in receptor blockade, ligand neutralization, Ab-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), complement activation, or delivering toxins/radioisotopes. Examples are Trastuzumab (HER2), Cetuximab (EGFR), and Bevacizumab (VEGF-A) [163].

Monoclonal Abs targeting extracellular receptors or ligands. Their mechanism of action consists in receptor blockade, ligand neutralization, Ab-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), complement activation, or delivering toxins/radioisotopes. Examples are Trastuzumab (HER2), Cetuximab (EGFR), and Bevacizumab (VEGF-A) [163].

Adaptive metabolic responses and resistance mechanisms of cancer cells to anti-receptor or ligand mAbs include mechanisms such as:

Alteration of target antigen, through, (a) downregulation or loss of receptor expression, as in the case of the loss of HER2, which reduces trastuzumab efficacy; (b) mutation of genes encoding for receptors or ligands, which leads to changes in the epitope, thus preventing mAb binding, as in the case of EGFR mutations, which lead to cetuximab resistance; c) shedding of the extracellular domain, which results in decoy receptors soaking up Abs in the circulation, as it occurs in the case of HER2 extracellular domain release [164].

Kinase inhibitors (EGFR, PI3K, AKT, mTOR, BRAF inhibitors). These are small molecules that inhibit the activity of tyrosine kinases, typically by blocking their ATP-binding site and preventing phosphorylation of target protein. Examples are Erlotinib (EGFR inhibitor, primarily used to treat cancers characterized by aberrant EGFR signaling, particularly NSCLC, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and CRC), Imatinib (BCR-ABL inhibitor, primarily used to target chronic myeloid leukemia), Sorafenib (multi-kinase inhibitor targeting RAF, VEGFR, PDGFR, and c-KIT, is approved for advanced HCC, RCC, and radioiodine-refractory thyroid carcinoma). Kinase signaling pathways, including EGFR, PI3K, AKT, mTOR, and BRAF, act as central regulators of cancer cell metabolism [167]. These pathways promote aerobic glycolysis, lipid biosynthesis, and nucleotide synthesis, while limiting mitochondrial reliance. Pharmacologic inhibition of these kinases suppresses glucose uptake, glycolytic flux, and anabolic metabolism. For example, PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibitors reduce Glucose Transporter 1 (GLUT1) expression and suppress protein and lipid synthesis, though the extent depends on cell type, inhibitor and context [168].

Adaptive metabolic responses and resistance mechanisms of cancer cells to kinase inhibitors include mechanisms, such as upregulation of (a) Hexokinase 2 (HK2), which catalyses the first step of glycolysis, (b) GLUT1, which facilitates glucose uptake across the plasma membrane, and can be overexpressed following MYC, RAS, and PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway activation [169–171], loss of tumor suppressors like p53, which normally represses glycolysis [172], or by HIF-1α under low oxygen conditions [173], and (c) PFKFB3, which regulates the synthesis of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP), a potent activator of phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1, the rate-limiting enzyme of glycolysis), and increases lactate production to maintain ATP, thus providing energy supply despite inhibited signaling pathways [174].

In addition, because kinase inhibitors can trigger a metabolic shift from glycolysis to OXPHOS (metabolic rewiring), cancer cells adapt by enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis via PGC1α, increasing TCA cycle activity and NADPH production, and elevating mitochondrial membrane potential to resist apoptosis, ultimately maintaining energy production and redox balance, as observed in BRAF inhibitor–resistant melanoma [175]. Cancer cell adaptation to kinase inhibitors also includes activation of FAO to supply ATP, and increased lipid droplet formation to buffer ROS, thus supporting mitochondrial function and energy supply. Resistant cells may increase glutathione (GSH) synthesis and activate nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling, resulting in oxidative stress neutralization and apoptosis prevention [172, 176].

• Anti-angiogenic agents (VEGF inhibitors). VEGF inhibitors, such as bevacizumab, impair tumor angiogenesis, reducing oxygen and nutrient supply. The resulting hypoxia and nutrient-poor microenvironment drive tumor cells toward glycolysis and other adaptive metabolic states [177]. Aerobic glycolysis results in lactate accumulation and acidification of the TME, which promotes invasion and immune evasion (acid suppresses T cell activation and cytotoxicity, while promoting Treg stability and suppressive activity) [178–181]. Lactate is not just a waste product, since it stabilizes HIF-1α, reinforcing glycolysis and angiogenesis [182, 183]. Lactate serves as a carbon and energy source for oxidative cancer cells and stromal cells (reinforcing the tumor–stroma metabolic symbiosis), through the “lactate shuttle” (which indicates how lactate moves between tissues to serve as a fuel, signaling molecule, and metabolic intermediate), and modulates epigenetics by fuelling histone lactylation [174, 184].

Hypoxia plus VEGF blockade impair the Electron Transport Chain (ETC) (Complexes I–IV) function, leading to ROS buildup. Electron “leakage” from ETC is increased, and leaked electrons react with oxygen (even at low levels) to form superoxide (O₂•⁻) [185]. O₂•⁻ is converted to other ROS, such as H₂O₂, and hydroxyl radicals. ROS stabilize HIF-1α, enhancing glycolysis, angiogenesis signals, and survival programs; activate pathways like NF-κB and MAPK, promoting proliferation and invasion; cause mtDNA mutations and ETC protein damage, worsening mitochondrial dysfunction [186].

Adaptive metabolic responses and resistance mechanisms of cancer cells to VEGF inhibitors, which induce hypoxia and nutrient deprivation [187], include: (a) upregulation of GLUT1, HK2, and LDHA, with increased lactate production that acidifies the TME, thereby providing ATP and supporting survival under low oxygen conditions; (b) in tumor cells that switch back to OXPHOS under nutrient limitation, adaptations include increased mitochondrial mass and respiration, as well as enhanced FAO, which supports ATP production under glucose-limited conditions [188]. Increased FAO helps maintain energy levels under hypoxia and limited glucose, while lipid droplet accumulation buffers oxidative stress; (c) hypoxic, nutrient-deprived tumors rely on glutamine for TCA cycle anaplerosis, with glutamine supporting biosynthesis and redox balance via GSH; (d) VEGF inhibition induces metabolic stress, and activation of autophagy recycles macromolecules to supply energy and biosynthetic precursors, thereby supporting survival in hypoxic regions [189, 190].

• Chemotherapy and cytotoxic drugs interfere with DNA, RNA, or protein synthesis, but also target cellular metabolism, which is a key factor in both drug efficacy and side effects [191].These therapies include a broad range of agents, such as antimetabolites, alkylating agents, topoisomerase inhibitors, and mitotic inhibitors that target microtubules.

Antimetabolites (e.g., methotrexate, 5-FU, gemcitabine) directly block nucleotide biosynthesis, starving cells of DNA/RNA precursors. Their effects on cancer cell metabolism include: (a) Nucleotide pool depletion. Reduced dNTPs lead to stalled DNA replication and repair. Cells attempt to reroute glucose and glutamine toward nucleotide biosynthesis via the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). (b) Increased energy demand. DNA repair and stress responses consume ATP and NADPH, creating metabolic stress. (c) Indirect induction of oxidative stress by an increased ROS production during stalled DNA repair. Consequently, cancer cells activate compensatory pathways, (i.e. Glycolysis upregulation, PPP activation, Glutamine metabolism), and enhance antioxidant systems (glutathione, thioredoxin) [192].

• Radiotherapy damages DNA, but also perturbs tumor metabolism through oxidative stress, by means of (a) ROS generation. Radiotherapy increases ROS, damaging cellular structures. Cancer cells adapt by boosting antioxidant systems and increasing GSH synthesis, increasing NADPH from PPP and malic enzyme, and increasing the Nrf2 pathway (master regulator of antioxidant response) activation. This buffering reduces radiation-induced oxidative damage. (b) Hypoxia adaptation. Radiotherapy can exacerbate tumor hypoxia by damaging vasculature. Hypoxia stabilizes HIF-1α and reprograms energy metabolism toward glycolysis by inducing pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1, which phosphorylates and inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase), reducing mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation and suppressing oxidative phosphorylation. In clear RCC, HIF1α mediates altered tumor metabolism and promotes tumorigenesis, activating the transcription of genes that cause an increase in glucose uptake, glycolysis, and lactate production, a decrease in the flux of pyruvate entering the TCA cycle, and a decrease in oxidative phosphorylation [202]. (c) Metabolic rewiring. To survive oxidative damage, tumor cells often upregulate glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and fatty acid metabolism, with the effect of selecting for tumors with antioxidant and glycolytic resilience [203, 204].

• Immunotherapy can alter tumor metabolism, and this is one of the main ways it exerts its anti-cancer effects. Tumors exist in a metabolically competitive microenvironment where cancer cells, immune cells, and stromal cells all compete for nutrients, such as glucose, amino acids, and oxygen. Immunotherapies can reshape this balance in several ways:

1. Checkpoint Blockade. ICIs (anti–PD-1, anti–PD-L1, and anti–CTLA-4 Abs) relieve suppression of T cells, allowing them to proliferate and become metabolically active. Activated T cells switch toward aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect, as it occurs in cancer cells), increasing nutrient competition with cancer cells [205]. This condition can starve tumor cells of glucose and amino acids, while enhancing T cell effector function. Tumors can develop primary resistance (never respond) or acquired resistance (initially respond, then relapse).

The primary mechanisms underlying cancer cell adaptation to ICIs are as follows.

Tumor-intrinsic adaptations, which include: (1) Loss of antigen presentation due to downregulation/mutation of MHC-I molecules, loss of β2-microglobulin leading to impaired antigen display, or defects in the antigen processing machinery (TAP1/2, ERAAP); (2) Altered interferon signaling due to mutations in JAK1/JAK2 resulting in insensitivity to IFN-γ, blocking anti-tumor immune signals and preventing upregulation of PD-L1 and antigen presentation; (3) Checkpoint upregulation, which means that even if PD-1/PD-L1 is blocked, tumor cells may upregulate other inhibitory ligands (such as, TIM-3, LAG-3, TIGIT, VISTA); (4) Metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells, characterized by increased IDO/Tryptophan 2,3-Dioxygenase (TDO) expression, leading to tryptophan depletion, and kynurenine accumulation, upregulation of the adenosine pathway (CD39/CD73 axis leads to the production of adenosine, which bind to A2A receptor on immune cells, impairing T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, and enhancing Treg stability and function), and hypoxia (via HIF-1α), which promotes metabolic suppression of effector T cells [206, 207].

2. Chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T) therapy and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapies are powerful adoptive cell transfer approaches [218], which activate T cells to release cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α) that can alter tumor metabolism (e.g., reduce tumor glycolysis or glutamine utilization).

CAR-T cells versus tumor metabolism. CAR-T cells need glucose and oxidative phosphorylation to sustain proliferation and killing. In tumors with high glycolysis, cancer cells outcompete CAR-T cells for glucose, and lactate accumulation impairs T cell effector functions. Tumor hypoxia reduces CAR-T cell cytotoxicity, whereas tumor adenosine, kynurenine, and low arginine all inhibit CAR-T persistence [219–221].

The solutions under study include the following approaches

Preconditioning CAR-T cells with metabolic regulators. Pre-treating CAR‑T cells with a combination of the AMPK activator, metformin, and the mTOR inhibitor, rapamycin, enhances mitochondrial function, increases spare respiratory capacity, improves cytotoxic persistence under hypoxia, and prolongs survival in a glioma model, demonstrating how metabolic modulation can improve CAR-T efficacy in a hostile TME [222].

Engineering CAR-T cells with enhanced metabolic fitness, which involves modifying T cells to optimize their energy production, nutrient utilization, and resistance to metabolic stress. This can be achieved through strategies, such as upregulating mitochondrial biogenesis, promoting oxidative phosphorylation, enhancing glycolytic flexibility, modulating key metabolic regulators, includingAMPK or mTOR, as well as increasing PGC-1α expression. It was found that CAR-T cells incorporating a 4—1BB costimulatory domain develop enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, respiratory capacity, and FAO, which collectively promote a central-memory differentiation state and durable antitumor activity, whereas CD28 costimulation drives glycolytic metabolism and effector-memory phenotypes, enabling rapid effector function, but limited persistence [223].

TIL therapy versus tumor metabolism. TILs are extracted from tumors, expanded, and reinfused. In the TME [228, 229], TILs are exposed to nutrient-deprived, hypoxic, and acidic environments, leading to exhaustion. Kynurenine (via IDO/TDO), adenosine, and lactic acid collectively suppress TIL function by creating an immunosuppressive metabolic microenvironment. In this context, TILs often express high levels of exhaustion markers, such as PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3, partly due to metabolic stress.

The solutions under study include the following approaches

Pre-treating TILs with metabolic reprogramming agents (e.g., PI3K inhibitors, metabolic modulators) before infusion [230, 231]. Both CAR-T and TIL therapies are highly effective in haematological cancers, where the metabolic environment is less hostile [237, 238]. In solid tumors, their efficacy is constrained by both, physical barriers, including thecomplex stromal architecture and composition, and metabolic barriers in the TME. While direct clinical studies combining metabolic inhibitors with TIL therapy are still emerging, several preclinical adoptive cell therapy models (particularly CAR-T) demonstrate that metabolic modulation (e.g., AMPK/mTOR targeting, AKT inhibition, glycolysis restriction) can favourably reprogram T cell metabolism, improving persistence, memory, and antitumor efficacy in hostile TMEs [239]. Future advances in cancer immunotherapy will require coordinated strategies that rewire T cell metabolism to enhance their effector function [240], while co-targeting tumor-specific metabolic adaptations that sustain malignancy [241].

Therapeutic implications. Immunotherapy does not just “unleash” T cells, it reshapes tumor metabolism, both by making immune cells more metabolically competitive and by directly pushing tumor cells into less favourable metabolic states. This metabolic tug-of-war is central to the success or failure of cancer immunotherapies. Tumor resistance to immunotherapy often arises from metabolic competition between cancer and immune effector T cells [242]. Accordingly, there is growing interest in combining immunotherapy with metabolic interventions, such as IDO inhibitors (to restore tryptophan availability); adenosine receptor blockers; drugs targeting tumor glycolysis or lactate transport. Metabolic drivers of tumor immunotherapy resistance and treatment strategies

Clinical considerations:

• Combination approaches are key: metabolic-targeting agents are often most effective when paired with ICIs or adoptive cell therapies, because they relieve metabolic suppression and let immune cells respond to checkpoint blockade

• Cell engineering (CAR-T/TIL metabolic reprogramming) is a high-value strategy: examples include enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, knocking out suppressive receptors (A2AR), or expressing cytokines (IL-12/IL-15) to improve persistence in hostile TMEs

• Biomarkers and patient selection matter: not all tumors use the same dominant metabolic escape; profiling (e.g., IDO expression, hypoxia markers, CD73 levels) can guide rational combinations

• Many interventions are investigational or in early clinical development: clinical efficacy depends on tumor type, dosing, and combination partners

Metabolic targeted therapies in preclinical studies and clinical trials

A critical appraisal of metabolism-targeted cancer therapies requires assessment of their clinical relevance, by distinguishing established treatments from newly approved agents and emerging strategies under evaluation in preclinical and clinical settings, while also identifying approaches that have yet to demonstrate meaningful benefit.

What’s clinically established

Conventional antimetabolites targetingDNA/RNA biosynthesis, such as 5-FU/capecitabine, gemcitabine, methotrexate, pemetrexed, cytidine analogs (gemcitabine), remain cornerstones across gastrointestinal, breast, colorectal, lung, hematologic and other cancers; they act primarily through substrate mimicry and/or inhibition of key enzimes in nucleotide biosyntesis and folate-dependent one-carbon metabolism [243].

In 2024, it also gained a U.S. approval for metastatic/unresectable pheochromocytoma andparaganglioma [257, 258]. Class-related adverse effects include anemia andhypoxia.

Strategies that look promising versus disappointing

Arginine deprivation (ADI-PEG20/pegargiminase). Exploits the metabolic vulnerability of certain tumors that cannot synthesize arginine due to deficiency of argininosuccinate synthetase 1 (ASS1). Positive Phase III (ATOMIC-Meso) in non-epithelioid pleural mesothelioma: overal survival (OS) 9.3 versus 7.7 months when added to chemo; not yet broadly approved. Biomarker: ASS1 loss [259].

Glutaminase inhibition (Telaglenastat/CB-839 programs) [260]. Exploits glutamine dependence of "glutamine addicted" tumors (with high glutaminase expression or c-Myc-driven glutamine addiction), which rely heavily on glutamine as a carbon and nitrogen source. Multiple Phase II/III trials negative in patients with metastatic melanoma, NSCLC and RCC. Despite very strong preclinical rationale, given the glutamine dependence observed in many tumors, large randomized clinical trials have been negative or failed to demonstrate clear benefit when added to standard therapies (e.g., no improvement in metastatic RCC with cabozantinib in a randomized trial). Subsequent combination studies reported limited consistent efficacy. Thus, preclinical metabolic dependency doesn’t always translate into durable clinical benefit [261].

Mitochondrial “lipoate” analog (Devimistat/CPI-613) [262]. The Phase III AVENGER 500 trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of devimistat (CPI-613) in combination with modified FOLFIRINOX (mFFX) versus standard FOLFIRINOX in treatment-naïve patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer. The primary endpoint of OS was not met. The median OS was 11.10 months for the devimistat plus mFFX arm and 11.73 months for the FOLFIRINOX arm (hazard ratio 0.95; P = 0.655). Median progression-free survival (PFS) was 7.8 months in both arms (hazard ratio 0.99; P = 0.94) [257]. Devimistat in combination with mFFX did not improve long- and short-term metastatic pancreatic cancer patient outcomes compared with standard FFX. In addition, there were no new safety concerns identified with the addition of devimistat [263]. These findings suggest that targeting mitochondrial metabolism with devimistat does not provide a clinical benefit over standard chemotherapy in metastatic pancreatic cancer.

Monocarboxylate transporter (MCT1) blockade (AZD3965) [264]. Exploits a metabolic vulnerability of cancer cells, that rely on glycolysis for energy and need to export lactate to survive, by preventing lactate efflux. Phase I Dose-Escalation Study: A first-in-human, multicenter, open-label study in patients with advanced solid tumors or lymphoma and no standard therapy options showed target engagement and tolerability. In dose expansion cohort enrolled patients with R/R diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and Burkitt lymphoma, efficacy signals are early and tumor-selective (responses correlated with MCT1 expression levels in tumor samples).Currently not practice-changing based on available evidence [265].

Clinical takeaways

Biomarker-first strategy: IDH mutation testing (hematologic malignancies, cholangiocarcinoma; IDH1/2 for diffuse glioma) [269, 270] and assessment of VHL/HIF pathway status (RCC, VHL-associated tumors) [271] are essential to determine eligibility for approved targeted therapies.

Given metabolic plasticity, most emerging agents are being paired with chemo, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy, e.g., in the ATOMIC-Meso Randomized Clinical Trial, the treatment with ADI-PEG20 (pegargiminase) combined with chemotherapy prolonged survival beyond standard chemotherapy with a favourable safety profile in patients with non-epithelioid pleural mesothelioma [277]. Combination and biomarker-enriched designs are expected to dominate in near-term clinical trials.

Challenges, open questions, and perspectives

Despite compelling preclinical evidence, cancer metabolism–targeting therapies have demonstrated limited clinical efficacy. This failure is largely attributed to the extensive metabolic heterogeneity within and between tumors, which enables cancer cells to bypass inhibited pathways through metabolic plasticity. Importantly, the lack of robust predictive biomarkers hampers patient stratification and target validation, leading to suboptimal trial design. Additionally, metabolic dependencies often overlap with those of immune and normal tissues, constraining therapeutic windows. Adaptive resistance mechanisms, pathway redundancy, and patient-specific metabolic contexts further diminish efficacy, highlighting the need for more precise, context-dependent, and combinatorial treatment strategies. Open questions include identifying predictive biomarkers, optimizing therapeutic timing, and designing safe, synergistic combinations with immunotherapy to balance metabolic intervention, while preserving immune function and ensuring durable clinical benefit. Major translational and biological challenges, and open questions are detailed in the following key points.

Key challenges

High intra- and inter-tumor metabolic heterogeneity and plasticity. Tumors,and their associated stromal and immune cells, switch fuel sources (glycolysis, FAO, glutaminolysis) in response to therapy and environment, so targeting one pathway often produces compensatory rewiring. This plasticity undermines single-pathway metabolic inhibitors.

Top open questions

Which metabolic nodes are truly tumor-selective in specific histology and spare anti-tumor immune cells? Key uncertainity remain around identifying enzimes and transporters that can be targeted with sufficient tumor specificity to avoid impairing immune function, for example distinguishing isoform-selective dependencies, such as LDH isoforms or the differential roles of MCT4 versus MCT1 in tumor compared to immune cell metabolism.

MCT4 blockade prevents lactate export, causing intracellular acid accumulation in CAFs or tumor cells, thereby disrupting tumor metabolism and potentially enhancing immune cell activity. In preclinical CRC models, selective inhibition of MCT4 reduced lactate efflux, increased intratumoral pH, improved immune cell infiltration and T cell activation, and enhanced the efficacy of ICB, providing proof-of-concept for targeting MCT4 to reverse lactate-mediated immunosuppression in the TME [278].

MTHFD2 is highly overexpressed in many cancers (AML, ovarian, colorectal, some lung and solid tumors) and is a promising tumor-selective target [286]. In preclinical studies pharmacologic MTHFD2 inhibitors (LY345899, DS18561882) have proven anti-proliferative effects in AML and solid tumors [287].

To what extent does post-translational lactylation of histones and proteins in immune cells drive durable immunosuppression (M2-like macrophages, PD-L1 upregulation, T cell exhaustion signatures), and to what degree is this process therapeutically reversible?

Key recent studies show lactylation controls immunosuppressive genes (Arg1, NUPR1, PD-L1, etc.) and can stabilize suppressive programs, but durable repression across cell divisions or after lactate removal is not yet fully proven [288]. Some reports describe feedback loops (e.g., lactylation upregulating metabolic genes or factors like Arg1, NUPR1, PD-L1, etc. that reinforce suppressive states), suggesting potential persistence, while the loop is intact, but demonstrations that histone lysine lactylation, Kla, alone creates long-term, heritable immunosuppression are limited and still emerging. New tools (site-specific incorporation, genetic-code expansion) now permit direct tests of specific Kla sites; these are being used to probe function [289].

Can temporal sequencing, such as transient tumor glycolysis inhibition before T cell infusion, improve adoptive cell therapy persistence and function?

Temporal sequencing of metabolic interventions is a cutting-edge strategy in adoptive cell therapy (ACT), aiming to improve T cell persistence and function. For example, tumor glycolysis inhibition (LDHA, MCT4, PDK1), before T cell infusion, allows T cells enter a more favourable metabolic environment, resist exhaustion, and persist longer [239]. T cell metabolic reprogramming (e.g., 4-1BB co-stimulation, PI3K/AKT modulation) during expansion, allows T cells sustain effector function even in nutrient-poor TME [290].

What robust imaging and liquid biomarkers can reliably predict metabolic immunosuppression and effectively monitor patient responses to metabolic-targeted therapies?

Imaging (FDG-PET, [18F]-FLT PET, Lactate-sensitive MRI, pH-sensitive MRI/PET tracers) provides non-invasive, longitudinal, and spatially resolved metrics of metabolic immunosuppression. Liquid biopsies allow dynamic monitoring of biomarkers (Plasma lactate, kynurenine/tryptophan ratio, glutamine/glutamate ratio, circulating cytokines, exosomes/EV metabolites) and are less invasive than tissue biopsies. Lactic acidosis of Warburg tumors is associated with ICB resistance, and targeting both lactate transporters, MCT1 and MCT4, augments ICB efficacy [278]. Modulation of T cell metabolism has been determined by using mitochondrial OCR in PBMCs / infused T cells, and higher OXPHOS predicted better T cell persistence [291].

Which combinations of metabolic inhibitors with ICI or ACT therapies are synergistic versus antagonistic, and what molecular mechanisms drive these antagonistic effects?

How does tumor metabolic plasticity, assessed by single-cell metabolomics and longitudinal metabolomics, evolve under therapy pressure in patients?

How do systemic factors, such as age, diet, or microbiome, modify the TME metabolism and therapy outcome, and can these be manipulated clinically?

For translational trials, which safety biomarkers can reliaby predict immune suppression or systemic metabolic toxicity early to avoid harming host immunity?

Develop predictive preclinical models by using humanized and organotypic systems, that better recapitulate human TME metabolism, before clinical testing to reduce risks of clinical failure. Rather than relying on tumor cell–intrinsic metabolism alone, these models incorporate cellular heterogeneity, spatial organization, and nutrient gradients that shape metabolic behavior in vivo. Such models include 3D co-culture systems and organoids that integrate tumor cells with CAFs, immune cells, and endothelial components, enabling the study of metabolic crosstalk, including lactate shuttling, nutrient competition, and extracellular acidification. Microfluidic “tumor-on-a-chip” platforms further enhance predictive power by recreating dynamic gradients of oxygen, glucose, and metabolites, thereby mimicking hypoxia and acidosis characteristic of the TME [292]. Patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) [293, 294] and genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) [295] provide metabolically relevant in vivo models by preserving stromal architecture and immune–metabolic interactions, particularly when combined with metabolic tracing, spatial metabolomics, and single-cell analyses [294]. Collectively, these predictive preclinical models enable more accurate evaluation of metabolic interventions, such as targeting lactate transport or glycolysis, and improve the translational relevance of therapies aimed at modulating TME metabolism.

Microfluidic “tumor-on-a-chip” platforms further enhance predictive power by recreating dynamic gradients of oxygen, glucose, and metabolites, thereby mimicking hypoxia and acidosis characteristic of the TME [292]. Patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) [293, 294] and genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) [295] provide metabolically relevant in vivo models by preserving stromal architecture and immune–metabolic interactions, particularly when combined with metabolic tracing, spatial metabolomics, and single-cell analyses [294]. Collectively, these predictive preclinical models enable more accurate evaluation of metabolic interventions, such as targeting lactate transport or glycolysis, and improve the translational relevance of therapies aimed at modulating TME metabolism.

• Most clinically established: FDG-PET (standard), MRSI (limited clinical use), LC–MS in biofluid diagnostics.

Targeting the metabolism–immunity interface is a high-reward, but high-complexity approach. Recent mechanistic insights plus advances in CAR-T metabolic engineering and targeted delivery create multiple translational avenues.

Conclusions

Cancer metabolism and immune regulation are intricately linked, forming a dynamic interface that critically shapes tumor progression and therapeutic response. The metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells, characterized by enhanced glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and lipid synthesis, creates a nutrient-deprived and immunosuppressive TME. This metabolic competition between malignant and immune cells disrupts immune surveillance by impairing effector T cell activation, proliferation and cytotoxic activity, while promoting Treg and MDSC expansion, and facilitating immune escape. Furthermore, metabolites such as lactate, adenosine, and kynurenine act as potent immunomodulators, shaping the inflammatory milieu and dictating therapeutic outcomes (Fig. 1). Recent advances in metabolomics and single-cell technologies have elucidated how key metabolic nodes, such as the mTOR, AMPK, and HIF-1α pathways, orchestrate the bidirectional communication between tumor and immune cells [25–27].

Emerging evidence underscores the potential of targeting metabolic checkpoints to restore antitumor immunity. Strategies include pharmacological inhibition of glycolytic enzymes, such as LDHA, and PFKFB3, blockade of adenosine signaling, in particular CD39/CD73–A2A axis, and modulation of tryptophan catabolism via IDO/TDO inhibitors. Integrating metabolic intervention with ICIs and ACT has demonstrated synergistic effects [92]. However, metabolic heterogeneity across tumors, along with systemic metabolic adaptations, presents significant translational challenges [21], underscoring the need for precision strategies informed by metabolic and immune profiling (Fig. 2).

Combination Design Workflow for Metabolic and Immunomodulatory Therapies. The algorithm illustrates rational combination strategies integrating IDO and arginase inhibition, blockade of the adenosine pathway (A2A receptor and CD73), modulation of TGF-β and VEGF signaling, and immune checkpoint inhibition. The framework emphasizes mechanistic complementarity, tumor microenvironmental context, and safety considerations. High-level decision flow: 1. Classify the tumor immune context. a. If inflamed: consider checkpoint backbone + metabolic target(s) that relieve local suppression. b. If excluded: prioritize stroma/vascular normalization (TGF-β or VEGF modulation) to enable T cell entry, then add checkpoint ± metabolic agents. c. If desert: focus on induction strategies (e.g., VEGF inhibition to normalize vessels, cytokine strategies, tumor-directed therapies) before checkpoint ± metabolic modulators. 2. Map dominant suppressive mechanisms. a. Amino-acid restriction: Elevated IDO or ARG; consider adding an IDO or ARG inhibitor. b. Adenosine/hypoxia-driven suppression: High CD73/adenosine signature or hypoxic conditions; consider adding an A2A antagonist or CD73 inhibitor. c. TGF-β–driven immune exclusion: High TGF-β levels and desmoplasia; consider a TGF-β pathway modulator or a bifunctional molecule. d. Vascular/hypoxia-mediated suppression: High VEGF, hypoxia, or abnormal vasculature; consider anti-VEGF therapy or vascular normalization strategies. 3. Select a checkpoint backbone. a. Use anti-PD-1/PD-L1 (or anti-CTLA-4 when rationale supports) as the immune-rescue backbone, unless contraindicated. 4. Check mechanistic complementarity and sequencing. a. Prefer combination strategies in which: i. one agent enhances immune cell infiltration or function (e.g., via TGF-β or VEGF modulation); ii. a second agent alleviates local metabolic suppression (e.g., through IDO/arginase inhibition or adenosine blockade); and iii. a checkpoint inhibitor restores the activity of exhausted T cells. b. Sequencing heuristics: i. When immune exclusion is dominant, start with a brief lead-in phase (1–3 cycles) to normalize vasculature or stroma; then add checkpoint blockade and metabolic modulators. ii. Use concurrent dosing only when preclinical PK/PD and safety data support it, especially if rapid synergistic effects are expected. iii. Use staggered dosing when there is a meaningful risk of overlapping immune-related toxicities. 5. Evaluate safety and Pharmacokinetics (PK) and Pharmacodynamics (PD): a. Eliminate combinations with high overlapping organ toxicities (e.g., severe hepatic, cardiac). b. Check for PK interactions that alter exposure of immunotherapy or small molecules. c. If the toxicity risk is moderate, implement a staggered (sentinel) dose-escalation design. 6. Preclinical prioritization: a. Require at least one in vivo model that recapitulates the target TME and shows: improved effector T cell infiltration and function, reduced suppressive cells and metabolites, and tumor control versus monotherapy. b. Biological readouts: intratumoral amino-acid levels, adenosine concentration, pSMAD/TGF-β signaling, vessel perfusion. 7. Design early clinical evaluation (phase I/II): a. Start with a safety lead-in cohort (sentinel patients) if novel combo. b. Built-in translational endpoints: serial biopsies (baseline + on-treatment), peripheral immune profiling, metabolite assays (kynurenine/tryptophan, arginine/ornithine), adenosine pathway markers, imaging for perfusion and hypoxia. c. Prespecify biomarker-driven expansion cohorts (e.g., high-IDO expressing, high CD73, high TGF-β signature)

Future directions should focus on integrating multiomics data, advancing spatiotemporal metabolic imaging, and developing personalized strategies that co-target metabolic and immune pathways. Optimizing therapeutic efficacy will require biomarker-driven patient selection based on kynurenine-to-tryptophan ratios and transcriptional signatures indicative of pathway activation. A deeper understanding of the cancer metabolism–immunity axis will not only refine current immunotherapeutic paradigms, but also unveil novel metabolic vulnerabilities exploitable for durable cancer control. The convergence of immunometabolism and systems biology thus represents a promising frontier for next-generation oncologic interventions.
