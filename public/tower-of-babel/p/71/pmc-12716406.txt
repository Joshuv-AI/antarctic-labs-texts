Giant pulmonary artery masquerading as pneumonia
Authors: Jessica Blank, André Martin Mansoor, Steven Elias Mansoor
Published: 2025

Description

A woman in her early 70s with a history of hypertension and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was admitted to the hospital with refractory epistaxis, several months of progressive weight gain and dyspnoea. On physical examination, haemoglobin oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry was 82% on room air. The jugular venous pressure was elevated to 20 cm H20 with prominent a-waves. Cardiac auscultation revealed a prominent loud pulmonic component of the second heart sound (P2), a 3/6 holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border and an early, high-pitched 2/4 decrescendo diastolic murmur at the left upper sternal border. Both murmurs augmented with inspiration. There were no focal findings on pulmonary examination.

A chest radiograph showed extensive opacification over the left upper and lower lobes (figure 1), which was initially interpreted in the radiology report as consistent with pneumonia or haemorrhage. However, given the absence of fever and cough, non-focal pulmonary exam and the presence of cardiovascular physical findings, alternative causes for the thoracic opacification were considered. These included atelectasis, lymphadenopathy, intra- and extrapulmonary masses and an enlarged mediastinal structure.

Anteroposterior (AP) chest radiograph demonstrating extensive left thoracic opacification.

Subsequent CT cross-sectional imaging revealed a markedly dilated left pulmonary artery (PA), measuring 6.5 cm in diameter (figure 2; for additional CT scan images, please seeonline supplemental video 1). The dilated PA was determined to be the source of the opacification seen on the chest radiograph and the patient’s clinical presentation raised suspicion for pulmonary hypertension (PH). On re-evaluation of the chest radiograph, in addition to the globular appearance of the cardiac silhouette, there is prominent dilated pulmonary vasculature involving the right hemithorax, which is supportive of PA dilatation. The differential diagnosis for a dilated PA is broad and includes conditions such as pulmonary hypertension, left-to-right shunt (eg, patent ductus arteriosus, atrial septal defect and ventricular septal defect), valvular disease (eg, pulmonic stenosis), rheumatologic conditions (eg, Behçet’s disease and Takayasu’s arteritis), connective tissue disease (eg, Marfan’s, Ehlers-Danlos and Loeys-Dietz syndromes) and infection (eg, tuberculosis and syphilis).1

Right-heart catheterisation was performed, confirming the diagnosis of severe pulmonary hypertension with the following findings: right ventricular systolic pressure of 117.5 mm Hg, mean PA pressure of 45 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 11 mm Hg and pulmonary vascular resistance of 10 woods units. Although vasoactive testing was not performed, had it been done, it would have been expected to show a negative response (mean PA pressure that does not decrease by at least 10 mm Hg to reach an absolute value of <40 mm Hg), consistent with severe, fixed pulmonary vascular changes. A negative vasoreactivity response is common in cases of severe PH that are non-reversible and typically require advanced treatment modalities with combination vasodilator therapies, including an endothelin receptor antagonist and phosphodiesterase five inhibitor, with the potential addition of a parenteral prostanoid if escalated therapy is required for symptom management.

The evaluation to identify the cause of PH included a transthoracic echocardiogram, ventilation-perfusion scan, liver function studies, rheumatoid factor, antinuclear antibody testing and HIV serologies, but all were unremarkable. As a result, the aetiology was presumed to be idiopathic (Group I PAH) and/or secondary to underlying COPD (Group III PAH).

In terms of treatment, the patient and her family opted to focus on symptom management. Despite a trial of diuretic therapy, non-invasive ventilation and vasodilator therapy, the patient’s condition did not improve. The family ultimately decided to discontinue further treatments and pursue comfort measures, leading her to discharge to a hospice facility, where she passed away shortly thereafter.

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge when the radiographic interpretation does not align with the clinical presentation, as the initial reading suggested pneumonia, while further evaluation revealed that the opacification was due to a dilated PA. This emphasises the importance of differential diagnosis when encountering thoracic opacification in the absence of typical pulmonary symptoms. Additionally, the management of PH in this patient was limited by her preference for a palliative approach. Despite the presence of a dilated PA and confirmed PH, advanced therapies, such as dual pulmonary vasodilation with prostanoids, were not pursued due to the patient’s wishes. This case underscores the importance of aligning clinical decision-making with patient preferences, especially when the underlying disease is advanced and progressive.

Learning points

Thoracic opacification on chest radiography must be correlated with clinical data. If history, physical examination and laboratory evaluation are incongruent with pneumonia, alternative aetiologies should be explored.
