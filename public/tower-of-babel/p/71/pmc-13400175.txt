Core gastric microbiota linked to pathogenesis and preserved across age-stratified cohorts
Authors: Weidong Huang, Qiujin Chen, Xuejie Gao, Gaocheng Yi, Yongyuan Su, Jiayi Shen, Tao Zhang, Hao Zhang, Yanbin Chen, Yingying Yu, Wen Wang, Hewei Jiang, Xu Lin, Zhaowei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe stomach harbors a complex microbial niche, yet the persistence and disease relevance of its core microbiota remain unclear. Using full-length 16S and ITS sequencing of 255 pediatric samples, this study identifies 11 core bacterial genera organized into two antagonistic communities, CST1A and CST1B. CST1A is enriched in non-ulcer controls, whereas CST1B is associated with gastroduodenal disease, with limited fungal involvement. Stratified analyses show that Helicobacter pylori infection depletes CST1A taxa. Functional profiling indicates that CST1A is linked to motility-related processes, while CST1B favors nucleotide and peptidoglycan biosynthesis. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that CST1A-derived Achromobacter spanius alleviates gastric mucosal injury by modulating inflammatory responses. Cross-cohort validation across eight datasets (n = 1,299) confirms the persistent relevance of the CST1A-CST1B structure across disease stages. Together, these findings define an early-life core gastric microbiota linked to disease risk and conserved across the lifespan.Keywords: core gastric microbiota, early life, pediatric peptic ulcers, H. pylori, ecological preservation

Introduction

The human stomach was conceptualized as a physiologically hostile microenvironment for microbial persistence, characterized by its highly acidic luminal environment (pH 1.5–3.5) and digestive activity mediated by pepsinogen activation and gastric lipase.1 With advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies, however, the stomach has been increasingly recognized as a dynamic microbial habitat with stratified ecological organization.2,3,4,5,6,7 Notably, Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori, HP) remains the prototypical gastric pathogen,8 and emerging studies have identified Streptococcus anginosus as a gastric pathobiont capable of establishing persistent colonization with carcinogenic effects,9 suggesting a complicated microbial interaction in gastropathology. However, the functional interplay among commensal members of the gastric microbiota remains unclear.

As the principal etiopathogenic driver of gastric adenocarcinoma and peptic ulcer disease, H. pylori can induce chronic inflammation and genomic instability in gastric epithelia.10 Paradoxically, although the global seroprevalence of H. pylori reaches 43.1%,11 only 1%–3% of colonized individuals develop gastric malignancy, suggesting that H. pylori alone is insufficient to explain malignant progression. Intriguingly, longitudinal cohort studies have shown that patients remain at a 2.8-fold higher risk of gastric cancer even after eradication, compared with uninfected controls.12 Furthermore, growing evidence supports shifting the focus from single pathogens to theories of microbial imbalance in the intestine.13,14 Whether gastric diseases likewise arise from disruption of a microbiota network, rather than from single invaders alone, remains unclear.

Despite recent progress in gastric microbiome research, current profiling approaches remain limited in their ability to capture early developmental or physiologically normal states of the gastric microbiota, which are often obscured by confounding factors such as advanced age, polypharmacy, and metabolic comorbidities.15 Of note, precancerous lesions induce profound histopathological changes, including glandular atrophy16 and chronic inflammatory infiltration,17 that reshape microbial niches and complicate causal inference in microbiome-disease associations.18 These challenges underscore the need for a developmental life-course framework. Studying early-life microbiome dynamics may help identify stable core taxa that are stable across cohorts and constitute the foundation of the gastric ecosystem.

Pediatric populations provide a strategic model for identifying autochthonous gastric microbiota.19 Reduced environmental heterogeneity, limited cumulative antibiotic exposure, and relatively homogeneous dietary patterns during childhood collectively minimize ecological confounding. Moreover, childhood represents a critical ontogenetic window of host-microbe coevolution,20 during which microbial communities may retain ancestral ecological signatures through stable vertical transmission.21 This synchronicity between host mucosal maturation and microbial colonization suggests that childhood microbiota may serve as informative markers of gastric ecosystem assembly.

In this study, we performed full-length 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing on gastric brushing specimens from 255 children undergoing upper endoscopy, complemented by exploratory internal transcribed spacer (ITS) profiling of the gastric mycobiome, and systematically delineated the structure of the core gastric microbiota and its links to disease. Our analyses revealed a preserved microbiota network that emerges in early life and persists into adulthood. Within this network, two functionally antagonistic subclusters form a seesaw configuration whose dysbiosis is tightly coupled to pediatric peptic ulcer pathology. H. pylori colonization suppresses the core gastric microbiota, with persistent disruption of this ecosystem propagating across the gastric cancer continuum. Together, these discoveries support a life-course ecological framework for gastric microbial stability and indicate specific network elements that forecast distinct disease trajectories.

Results

Identification of gastric microbial network in H. pylori-negative pediatric patients

A total of 255 children younger than 18 years who underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy were enrolled in this study, including 161 males and 94 females. Among them, 177 were delivered vaginally and 78 via cesarean section. To identify the core gastric microbiota, we employed full-length 16S rRNA and ITS amplicon sequencing on samples from the pediatric endoscopic cohort (n = 255) using a third-generation long-read platform (Figure 1A). Sequencing quality-control metrics and rarefaction curves indicated sufficient sequencing depth and coverage across samples, ensuring that most of the gastric microbial diversity was captured for downstream analyses (Figure S1A). Based on endoscopic diagnoses, 187 were identified with superficial gastritis and 68 with duodenal ulcer (Table 1). To assess H. pylori infection status, we performed high-throughput sequencing and compared the results with conventional diagnostic methods, including the 13C-urea breath test, immunohistochemistry, and rapid urease test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis yielded an optimal read-based cutoff value of 0.01, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.927 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.855–1.000) (Figure S1B). Based on this threshold and endoscopic findings, participants were classified into four groups: Hp-negative non-ulcer controls (HpNNC, n = 149), Hp-negative duodenal ulcer (HpNDU, n = 29), Hp-positive non-ulcer controls (HpPNC, n = 38), and Hp-positive duodenal ulcer (HpPDU, n = 28).

Given the hematological markers for duodenal ulcer (Table S1), we compared blood parameters between ulcer and non-ulcer groups, adjusting for age, sex, and delivery mode. Children in the control group exhibited significantly higher mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), hematocrit (HCT), red blood cell count (RBC), and hemoglobin (HGB), while those in the ulcer group showed elevated immature granulocyte counts, red cell distribution width (RDW), and RDW coefficient of variation (Figure S1C). Because of the imbalance in sample size between the non-ulcer and duodenal ulcer (DU) groups, we further performed 1,000-iteration bootstrap resampling; the resampled estimates were highly consistent with the original results, supporting the stability of these hematological differences (Table S2). These clinical distinctions validated the disease stratification and provided phenotypic references for subsequent microbial correlation analyses.

The microbial abundance matrices were then denoised by removing genera with relative abundance <0.005 and retaining only those present in ≥10% of samples (Figure S1D). Genus-level co-occurrence networks were independently constructed for HpNNC and HpNDU groups using Spearman correlations (ρ > 0.5, q < 0.05). The HpNNC network comprised 24 nodes and 83 edges, while the HpNDU network expanded to 27 nodes and 133 edges (Figure 1B). Across both networks, 67 stable genus-genus correlations (45 positive and 22 negative) were identified, involving 23 genera (Figure 1C). Hierarchical clustering of these genera revealed two major clusters, CST1 and CST2. Notably, CST1 comprised 21 genera and could be further divided into two opposing submodules: CST1A and CST1B, characterized by strong positive intra-submodule correlations and negative inter-submodule associations (Figure 1D). To validate the clinical relevance of this network, multivariate regression analyses (adjusted for age, sex, and delivery mode) were conducted. Only the CST1 cluster showed significant correlations with host hematological parameters: CST1A was positively associated with both HGB and HCT (q < 0.001), whereas CST1B exhibited negative associations. No significant correlations were observed for CST2 (Figure 1E). These results collectively support the robustness and host relevance of the CST1 microbial cluster.

Cross-validation of the core gastric microbiota in H. pylori-infected pediatric ulcer

To assess whether the preserved core microbial network identified in H. pylori-negative children remained stable under H. pylori infection, we constructed genus-level co-occurrence networks for the HpPNC and HpPDU groups. The HpPNC network comprised 20 nodes and 49 edges, whereas the HpPDU network expanded to 33 nodes and 144 edges. We observed that Helicobacter exhibited distinct antagonistic patterns across disease states: in HpPNC, it showed negative correlations primarily with Streptococcus-dominated taxa, while in HpPDU, its antagonism extended to a Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia (BCP)-dominated group (Figure 2A). Tracking 15 genera across the HpNNC-HpPNC-HpPDU continuum revealed 26 stable positive correlations (Figure 2B), all of which belonged to the previously defined 23 genera from the H. pylori-negative core network (Figure 2C). These results indicate that, despite the presence of H. pylori, a subset of core bacterial interactions remains preserved, albeit with altered antagonistic targets.

To further delineate the clinical relevance of the core taxa, we examined associations between the 23 genera and hematological parameters that differed significantly between DU and control groups. Multivariate linear regression (adjusted for age, sex, and delivery mode) identified 11 genera significantly correlated with at least one clinical parameter (q < 0.05), including Leifsonia, BCP, Achromobacter, unclassified Alcaligenaceae, unclassified Burkholderiales, Pedobacter, Gemella, Actinomyces, Rothia, Streptococcus, and Granulicatella. CST1A genera (e.g., Leifsonia, BCP, and Achromobacter) were positively associated with HGB, with BCP and Achromobacter also linked to MCHC. In contrast, CST1B genera (e.g., Streptococcus and Rothia) were negatively associated with both HGB and MCHC (Figure 2D). Using these 11 clinically relevant genera, we reconstructed a refined core microbial network, which preserved the original modular layout: strong positive correlations within each submodule (CST1A and CST1B) and negative correlations between them (Figure 2E). This stable core architecture was maintained in both the HpPNC and HpPDU groups, despite the consistent negative correlation between H. pylori and the core microbiota in both networks. The number of correlation edges within the core microbiota increased specifically in the HpPDU network (Figure S2A). These refined modules reinforce the clinical relevance of the original CST1 structure and support its robustness as a functionally organized microbial hub.

To further validate the structure of the core gastric microbiota using an independent sequencing approach, we randomly selected 10 samples from the cohort for shotgun metagenomic sequencing. Correlation analysis among the core genera based on metagenomic relative abundances showed that the direction of pairwise associations was largely concordant with that inferred from amplicon data (Figure S2B). This validation supports the conclusion that the identified core network is not an artifact of the amplicon-based pipeline. To assess the reliability of 16S rRNA gene sequencing-based abundance estimates in low-biomass gastric mucosal samples, we performed an independent validation using quantitative PCR (qPCR) for a subset of bacterial taxa. Based on the availability of previously published and experimentally validated primers, we focused on four bacterial targets: three genera (Streptococcus,22 Leifsonia,23 and BCP24) and the species H. pylori,25 whereas other genera lacked suitable primers and were, therefore, not included. For each target genus or species, 10 gastric brushing specimens were randomly selected for qPCR assays. To account for potential differences in total bacterial load across samples, taxon-specific qPCR signals were normalized to universal 16S rRNA gene amplification. The normalized abundances were subsequently log2-transformed and compared with the corresponding relative abundances obtained from 16S sequencing. Spearman correlation analysis indicated moderate to strong concordance between qPCR-derived and sequencing-based abundances for the three tested targets (Spearman’s ρ = 0.648–0.927), with consistent trends observed across all of them (Figure S2C). Although only a limited number of taxa could be evaluated, these results support that, for bacterial groups with available and reliable primer sets, 16S rRNA gene sequencing provides reasonably robust abundance estimates in gastric mucosal samples.

As an exploratory analysis, we also profiled the gastric fungal community in the HpNNC group. The most detected fungal genera included Malassezia (38.1%), Candida (14.9%), Cutaneotrichosporon (5.47%), Aspergillus, and Trametes (3.44%). After applying arbitrary abundance (>0.006) and prevalence (>0.08) thresholds for visualization purposes, we constructed preliminary fungal co-occurrence networks (Spearman ρ > 0.2, false discovery rate [FDR]-adjusted q < 0.05) across different clinical subgroups. These exploratory networks were sparse and highly variable: HpPDU showed no significant correlations, whereas HpNNC (4 nodes and 2 edges), HpPNC (17 nodes and 6 edges), and HpNDU (10 nodes and 33 edges) exhibited inconsistent topology and limited connectivity.

Given the low biomass, high compositional variability, and lack of reproducible interaction patterns, these preliminary observations do not support the existence of stable, structured fungal communities in the gastric mucosa. In contrast to the robust and conserved bacterial core network described above, the fungal component appears unstructured and stochastic under the current detection limits. We, therefore, consider fungal profiling in this study as purely exploratory and do not draw biological conclusions from these data.

Modular subtyping reveals microbial distribution among superficial gastritis patients

To explore inter-individual variability in the gastric microbiota, we stratified the HpNNC group, defined as H. pylori-negative individuals diagnosed with superficial gastritis, based on features derived from the previously defined 11 genus core microbial network. Microbial composition within the HpNNC group exhibited significant clustering patterns (Figure 3A). Partitioning Around Medoids clustering identified two optimal clusters (K = 2), as supported by silhouette width analysis (Figure 3B; S3A). Cluster robustness was confirmed using k-means clustering, yielding a high adjusted Rand index (= 0.84; Figure S3B). Based on this, the HpNNC group was divided into two subgroups: HpNNC1 (n = 71) and HpNNC2 (n = 78). We next compared hematological profiles between each HpNNC subtype and the H. pylori-negative duodenal ulcer group (HpNDU), adjusting for age, sex, and delivery mode. Although the differences were not statistically significant across all measures, HpNNC1 tended to show greater divergence from HpNDU compared with HpNNC2, with relatively higher HGB (Estimate = +11.37, q < 0.01), increased RBC (Estimate = +0.29, q < 0.05), and reduced RDW (Estimate = −2.51, q < 0.05) (Figure 3C). Compositional analysis revealed that HpNNC1 was enriched in CST1A-associated genera, such as Achromobacter and BCP, whereas HpNNC2 was dominated by CST1B genera, including Streptococcus and Rothia (Figure 3D). Based on these taxonomic differences, we designated HpNNC1 as the AB subtype (CST1A-enriched) and HpNNC2 as the S subtype (CST1B-enriched). All signature genera showed >25% prevalence within their respective subtypes (Figure 3E). These observations suggest that the AB and S microbial subtypes exhibit distinct ecological and clinical trends, although further validation is needed to establish their potential clinical significance.

Disruption of the CST1A-CST1B network balance in ulceration and its interplay with H. pylori

To investigate the disruption of the core gastric microbiota under ulcerative conditions, we used HpNNC1 (CST1A, n = 71) as an optimized reference group and compared microbial profiles across disease states. Members of the CST1A module, such as Achromobacter and BCP exhibited significantly lower abundance and prevalence in both HpNDU and HpPDU groups compared with HpNNC1 (Figure 4A). The CST1A/CST1B abundance ratio was also significantly reduced in both HpNDU and HpPDU (p = 0.013), indicating a consistent shift in microbial balance under ulcerative conditions (Figure 4B). Given the marked difference in sample size between HpNNC1 and the ulcer groups, we further performed 1,000-iteration bootstrap resampling when comparing HpNNC1 with HpNDU and HpPDU; the bootstrap-based differential analyses yielded highly similar effect sizes and significance patterns, supporting the robustness of these findings despite sample size imbalance (Table S3). Although no individual genera were significantly different between HpPNC and HpPDU, core genera from the CST1 network still effectively distinguished these groups in classification models (AUC = 0.937, 95% CI: 0.886–0.987; Figures S3C–S3E). These findings indicate a consistent depletion of CST1A taxa and disruption of the CST1A/CST1B modular balance in ulcer states.

To explore whether this perturbation of the core network is reversible during ulcer healing, we further examined the CST1-centered interactions across the acute ulcer stage and the recovery stage. We first quantified the similarity of core co-occurrence networks between HpNNC1 and each ulcer stage based on pairwise correlation patterns among core genera. The recovery-stage network showed a higher similarity to HpNNC1 (recovery stage vs. HpNNC1, r = 0.80, p = 0.009) than the acute-stage network (acute stage vs. HpNNC1, r = 0.72, p = 0.019), suggesting a partial reversion of the core network toward the non-ulcer reference during recovery (Figure S4A). We then calculated, for each core genus, its mean absolute correlation with all other CST1 members as a proxy of relative interaction strength. Overall interaction strength within the core network was increased in the acute stage and partially attenuated in the recovery stage (Figure S4B), and the global pattern of interaction strengths in the recovery stage more closely resembled that of HpNNC1 than that of the acute stage (Figures S4C and S4D). Together, these findings support a dynamic but partially reversible disruption of the CST1A-CST1B network during ulceration and subsequent healing.

To further examine the regulatory role of H. pylori, mediation analysis showed that H. pylori had a negative effect on both CST1A (−0.64, 95% CI: −0.707 to −0.574) and CST1B (−0.05, 95% CI: −0.0839 to −0.0375). Bidirectional indirect effects were observed, whereby H. pylori influenced CST1B via CST1A (0.0435, 95% CI: 0.0003–0.086) and CST1A via CST1B (0.1587, 95% CI: 0.099–0.22), suggesting multi-pathway modulation of microbial equilibrium (Figure 4C). To assess the stability of the core microbial modules, we next evaluated the associations between CST1A/CST1B abundances and host factors in the H. pylori-negative reference group. No statistically significant correlations with age were observed for CST1A abundance (R = 0.073), CST1B abundance (R = 0.023), or the CST1A/CST1B ratio (R = 0.065), with all p values exceeding 0.05 (Figure 4D). Similarly, no significant differences were observed by sex or delivery mode (q > 0.05; Table S4). Together, these results highlight the CST1A-CST1B dynamic balance in pediatric ulcer and its interplay with H. pylori.

For each fungal genus, pairwise comparisons were performed between HpNDU, HpPNC, and HpPDU groups against the reference group HpNNC1. While several genera showed moderate coefficients—such as Malasseziaceae_gen_Incertae_sedis and Aspergillus in the HpPDU group, many associations did not reach statistical significance after multiple-testing correction.

Phylo-functional divergence of CST1A and CST1B reveals a modular gastric ecosystem

To characterize the ecological structure of the core gastric microbiota, we constructed co-occurrence networks spanning genus, species, and operational taxonomic unit (OTU) levels. The 23 core genera were taxonomically affiliated with four major phyla, Actinobacteriota, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, and Proteobacteria, and exhibited clear differentiation between CST1A and CST1B at the order level (Figure S5A). Correlation analysis across taxonomic hierarchies (ρ > 0.5, p < 0.05) revealed a structurally preserved pattern, which exhibited positive intra-group correlations but negative inter-group correlations (Figure S5B). At the species level, CST1A was primarily composed of Pedobacter nutrimenti (14%), Achromobacter spanius (8%), and unclassified BCP (7%). In contrast, CST1B was dominated by Rothia mucilaginosa (14%), unclassified Streptococcus (12%), and Streptococcus salivarius (8%) (Figure S5C). Predicted functional profiling suggested distinct metabolic potentials between the two modules: CST1A was associated with cell motility-related functions (q = 2.1 × 10−5) and fatty acid degradation pathways (q < 0.001), whereas CST1B was linked to amino sugar metabolism and peptidoglycan biosynthesis (both q < 0.001) (Figure S5D). These results imply that CST1A and CST1B showed differences in taxonomic composition and metabolic capabilities, supporting their ecological complementarity within the gastric environment.

To complement the 16S-based functional predictions, we performed exploratory shotgun metagenomic profiling of gastric brushing specimens. However, because of the intrinsically low microbial biomass of the stomach and stringent host DNA depletion, microbial read recovery remained extremely limited: even at ∼20-Gb sequencing depth, only 0.03%–3.4% (0.0003–0.034) of reads could be assigned to microbial genes after host filtering (Table S6). Ultra-deep sequencing (∼100 Gb) of a subset of samples showed that gene family accumulation curves remained unsaturated, indicating inadequate coverage for robust functional profiling (Figure S6A). Accordingly, these metagenomic analyses were insufficient for reliable module-level comparisons and should be interpreted as exploratory.

Protective effects of A. spanius in ethanol- and indomethacin-induced gastric injury models

Because major CST1B members have been linked to adverse gastric outcomes, whereas the role of CST1A remains undefined, we selected A. spanius, a CST1A species with relatively high abundance, for functional validation. In the ethanol-induced gastric epithelial injury model, ethanol stimulation was associated with activation of cytokine-related pathways, as well as MAPK, JAK-STAT, TNF signaling, complement cascade, and apoptosis pathways (Figure 5A; Table S7). In contrast, pretreatment with A. spanius was associated with reduced activity of inflammation-, immune-, and apoptosis-related pathways, together with altered growth factor signaling (Figures 5B and 5C). This reversal pattern was not observed with A. spanius conditioned medium or heat-killed bacteria, suggesting that the protective effect depends on live bacteria (Figure 5C). In the indomethacin-induced mouse model, A. spanius was detected in gastric tissues of treated mice (Figures 5D and 5E). Histopathological analysis showed that A. spanius pretreatment was associated with less-severe gastric mucosal injury than in the model group (Figure 5F). Consistently, IL-6 expression showed a decreasing trend in the A. spanius group (Figure 5G; Table S8). Together, these findings support a protective effect of A. spanius against gastric epithelial injury and mucosal inflammation and provide functional evidence that CST1A may exert host-modulatory effects distinct from those of CST1B.

Cross-cohort validation reveals structural and predictive conservation of the core gastric microbiota

To evaluate the conservation of the core gastric microbial network across age groups, geographic regions, and disease spectra, we performed cross-cohort validation in eight independent 16S rRNA datasets, including one pediatric cohort with duodenal ulcer (n = 51) and seven adult cohorts with gastric cancer or advanced gastric mucosal lesions (combined n = 1,248) (Figure 6A). For each cohort, genus-level abundance profiles of the previously defined core network members were extracted and used as input features for XGBoost classifiers to distinguish disease from cohort-specific reference states (Figure 6B). The core-based models demonstrated consistently high predictive performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.694 to 0.944 across cohorts (Figure 6C), underscoring the cross-cohort predictive relevance of the core microbial network. These results underscore the predictive performance of the core microbial network across multiple cohorts, encompassing varying ages, geographies, and disease progression. To control for feature selection bias, we additionally constructed two sets of control models. In the first control setting (other high-abundance genera feature set), we identified the 15 most abundant genera in each dataset and then removed all genera belonging to the predefined core microbiota as well as Helicobacter; the remaining high-abundance genera were used as predictors. In the second control setting (Hp-only feature set), models were built using only the relative abundance of Helicobacter as a single predictor. For both control feature sets, we applied the same train-test split, algorithms, and hyperparameter tuning procedures as for the core microbiota models. ROC analyses showed that the core-based classifier consistently outperformed both alternatives (Figure S6B). The selected features demonstrated consistently favorable AUC values across various machine learning models, indicating their robust predictive performance (Figure S6C). In parallel, these antagonistic network patterns remained consistent across all eight independent cohorts: the CST1B subgroup demonstrated robust intra-subgroup positive correlations, whereas CST1A-CST1B inter-subgroup correlations were predominantly negative. This preserved topological architecture collectively supports their role as an evolutionarily maintained homeostatic regulatory module (Figure 6D).

We further assessed the risk associations of CST1A and CST1B across validation cohorts. In large Asian gastric cancer cohorts, higher CST1A abundance consistently correlated with lower cancer risk (odds ratio [OR] < 1), while CST1B was associated with higher risk (OR > 1), reproducing the discovery cohort pattern. Smaller Asian cohorts showed consistent directional trends but wider confidence intervals. In South American/European cohorts, CST1B remained robustly risk associated, whereas CST1A detection was markedly lower with unstable effect estimates (Figure S6D). These patterns suggest that CST1B functions as a conserved risk module across populations, while CST1A shows greater geographic specificity. Together, these findings confirm that the gastric core microbial network exhibits both structural stability and predictive generalizability across independent cohorts, highlighting its potential as a robust framework for gastric disease stratification.

Discussion

In this study, we constructed and validated a core gastric microbiota network that emerges in childhood and remains stable across different age groups and disease stages. Using full-length 16 S rRNA sequencing, exploratory ITS amplicon sequencing, and ecological co-occurrence modeling on gastric brushing specimens from 255 children, we identified an antagonistic modular network composed of two antagonistic and functionally distinct components, CST1A and CST1B. This network was consistently detectable in superficial gastritis, duodenal ulcer, and H. pylori infection, indicating that it constitutes an ecological framework essential for maintaining gastric homeostasis.

CST1A was enriched in BCP, Achromobacter, and Leifsonia. Previous studies have shown that BCP is negatively associated with BMI during early gut development26 and is involved in bile acid metabolism.27 In patients with cirrhosis, a condition frequently accompanied by gastric mucosal injury, the abundance of Achromobacter is reduced, suggesting that this taxon may contribute to mucosal homeostasis.28 Leifsonia, commonly found on healthy skin and oral mucosa, may possess ecological dispersal capacity that facilitates gastric mucosal repair.29,30 We further validated the protective role of the CST1A module through in vitro and in vivo experiments. In the in vitro experiment, A. spanius pre-treatment alleviated ethanol-induced damage to GES-1 gastric epithelial cells. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that A. spanius regulated multiple pathways related to inflammation, immunity, apoptosis, and growth factor signaling, which is consistent with the functional characteristics of CST1A. In contrast, CST1B was enriched in opportunistic oral-derived pathogens. Previous studies have shown that these genera act synergistically with H. pylori to activate inflammatory MAPK signaling pathways and disrupt cell-cycle regulation, thereby promoting precancerous transformation.31 Similar to the gastric microbiome profiles reported in adults, the taxa associated with the CST1B module were predominantly of oral origin, as evidenced by the frequent detection of Streptococcus, Neisseria, and Fusobacterium in earlier studies.32 Of note, Streptococcus anginosus has been closely linked to the histopathological progression of gastric cancer.9 The enhanced motility and fatty acid degradation capacity of CST1A suggests its role as a primary colonizer and mucosal maintainer. High motility facilitates effective colonization and dissemination of bacteria on the gastric mucosal surface,33 while fatty acid degradation may help maintain gastric environmental homeostasis.34 In contrast, the reinforced amino sugar metabolism and peptidoglycan biosynthesis pathways in CST1B support its characteristic as an opportunistic proliferator. These metabolic capabilities provide the material basis for rapid proliferation and cell wall synthesis,35 enabling explosive growth in the impaired mucosal microenvironment, which is rich in glucose and mucus degradation products.36,37 The divergence in metabolic and ecological strategies between these two modules collectively shapes the antagonistic balance of the gastric mucosa. However, under strong perturbations, a systemic shift from homeostasis to ecological dysbiosis may occur.

The stomach is a dynamic environment that is continuously subjected to perturbations such as gastric acid, diet, and pathogen infections. The relative abundance of CST1A and CST1B exhibits a dynamic balance of reciprocal fluctuations, constituting the characteristic network of reciprocal antagonism. This structure may result from long-term co-evolution and mutual selection between the host and microorganisms and may represent a stable ecological configuration adapted to the dynamic gastric environment. The protective function of CST1A and the potential pathogenicity of CST1B are rooted in their distinct genomes and metabolic pathways. From a systems perspective, a fundamental shift in the functions of these two modules would signal a qualitative change in the entire gastric microbiome, shifting from homeostasis to sustained ecological dysbiosis. Furthermore, we found that the microbial network state during ulcer healing was more similar to the network characteristics of individuals without overt mucosal lesions, providing important evidence for the dynamic reversibility of the gastric microbiome ecosystem. Notably, this study, focused on children, captured the assembly process of the gastric microbiome in early life under nearly physiological conditions, minimizing confounding factors and enabling the identification of protective modules. In addition to the recognized enrichment of gastric pathogens, we also identified and characterized a microbial module (CST1A), whose members maintain ecological stability in the pediatric stomach but significantly decrease during the acute phase of duodenal ulcer.

It should be noted that the seesaw is used to summarize the antagonistic relationship between CST1A and CST1B, a structure that, based on network visualization, is also supported to some extent by quantitative analyses. On the network level, CST1A and CST1B exhibit a stable positive correlation within their respective submodules, while inter-module correlations are predominantly negative. This topological feature was reproducible in the discovery cohort as well as in eight independent validation cohorts. On the population level, we calculated the OR corresponding to a one-standard deviation change in CST1A/CST1B abundance using logistic regression. In the large cohort, the directions were highly consistent, with CST1A consistently showing OR < 1 and CST1B consistently showing OR > 1. A model constructed with core taxa (including CST1A/CST1B) achieved an AUC of 0.694–0.944 across multiple cohorts, systematically outperforming models based on high-abundance non-core taxa or a single Helicobacter feature, indicating that this imbalance pattern has quantifiable predictive significance. However, the standardization of this model requires further research, mainly because different experimental protocols were used for microbiome analysis across the cohorts and sequencing and analysis could only achieve relative quantification. Therefore, the standardized analysis of core microbiota is still in the developmental stage under the current conditions.

The clinical relevance of the CST1A-CST1B network balance is underscored by its predictive value across populations and its independence from H. pylori. Geographically, CST1B functions as a conserved, risk-associated module, and its association with gastric dysplasia remains significant across Asian, South American, and European cohorts. In contrast, CST1A exhibits stronger regional specificity, showing consistent protective effects in Asian cohorts but markedly lower detection and unstable risk estimates elsewhere. Critically, even in H. pylori-negative individuals, the distinction between AB and S subtypes reveals a distinct, pathogen-independent disease pathway and establishes the intrinsic equilibrium of the core network as a fundamental indicator of gastric health. This confirms that microbial network imbalance itself, not just a specific pathogen, underlies gastric disease risk.

In summary, we propose that the gastric core microbiome, composed of the antagonistic CST1A and CST1B modules, forms a persistently maintained ecological scaffold. These findings support a network-imbalance framework for interpreting gastric microbial dysbiosis, particularly in H. pylori-negative contexts.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered. First, although full-length 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing improved taxonomic resolution, it cannot reliably distinguish closely related species within conserved genera such as Streptococcus, Actinomyces, and Achromobacter. A. spanius was selected as a representative CST1A-associated candidate for functional validation based on its high sequence-matching confidence, rather than as the only functionally relevant CST1A member. Other core microbiota members require further investigation through strain-level metagenomics, bacterial isolation, and functional validation, particularly in longitudinal cohorts assessing their responses to clinical interventions and associations with disease outcomes or prognosis. Second, the indomethacin-induced gastritis model captures general inflammatory injury but does not fully reproduce the pathological microenvironment shaped by H. pylori. Future studies should establish H. pylori-A. spanius co-infection models, particularly in human gastric tissue organoids, to examine bacteria-bacteria interactions and bacteria-host crosstalk under more physiologically relevant conditions. Third, functional inference was limited by 16S-based prediction and by the difficulty of metagenomic sequencing in low-biomass gastric samples, where host DNA contamination can reduce microbial read depth even after deep sequencing.38 Fungal analysis was also constrained by low biomass and possible transient colonization, highlighting the need for fungal profiling methods optimized for gastric mucosal samples.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human pediatric cohort

A total of 255 pediatric participants were recruited from Fujian Children’s Hospital, including 187 children with no endoscopic signs of gastric disease and 68 children diagnosed with duodenal ulcers. Gastric mucosal samples were collected using a gastric cell brush during clinically indicated upper endoscopy.

Inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) children younger than 18 years of age; (2) ability to provide a complete and reliable recent medication history; and (3) provision of written informed consent by their legal guardians. Exclusion criteria were: (1) receipt of any systemic antibiotic therapy (oral or intravenous) within 4 weeks prior to sample collection; (2) use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) or H2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs) within 2 weeks prior to sampling; (3) use of bismuth agents, mucosal protectants, or any probiotic/prebiotic preparations within 4 weeks prior to sampling; (4) presence of known severe comorbidities that could affect gastrointestinal function, such as inflammatory bowel disease or immunodeficiency disorders; and (5) inability to provide a clear medication history or refusal to provide written informed consent.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (Approval No. 2024–232) and the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Children’s Hospital (Approval No. 2024ETKLRK12011). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians prior to enrollment.

Mice

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fujian Medical University (IACUC number: IACUCFJMU2025-Y-0449). A total of 24 male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing 18–20 g) were purchased from SLAC ANIMAL (Shanghai, China). To eliminate the potential confounding influence of estrogen cycles on the gastric inflammatory response, only male mice were utilized in this study. Animals were drug and test naive prior to the start of the experiments. Mice were group-housed (6 per cage) under standard conditions of temperature, humidity, and a regular light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups.

Cell

The human gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 was purchased from IMMOCELL. The cell line was authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based detection kit (IMMOCELL, Cat# IMC-802-20 T) and were confirmed to be negative prior to all experiments. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 Medium Modified supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (penicillin, streptomycin, and amphotericin B). Cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with an atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air. For co-culture experiments with A. spanius, the medium was replaced with RPMI Medium Modified supplemented with 10% FBS.

Microbe strains

Achromobacter spanius (standard strain CICC 22011) was obtained from the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC). The strain was routinely cultured and passaged on Nutrient Broth Agar solid medium. For experimental expansion, the bacteria were cultured in Nutrient Broth liquid medium at 30°C with shaking at 200 rpm.

Method details

Gastric mucosal sample collection

Gastric mucosal samples were collected using sterile cytology brushes during upper endoscopy. After sampling, the brush head was aseptically cut with autoclaved scissors and transferred into sterile cryovials. Samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after collection and transferred to −80°C within 1 h for long-term storage.

DNA extraction from gastric mucosal samples

To ensure efficient lysis of both bacterial and fungal cells, frozen mucosal samples were first subjected to mechanical disruption using a zirconia (Servicebio, G0201-150G) bead-beating procedure. This was followed by enzymatic digestion with lysozyme (Sangon, A610308-0005) in a lysis buffer containing 1 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), EDTA (0.75 mg/mL) and Triton X-100 (1.2%, v/v); the lysozyme solution was sterilized by ultrafiltration using Amicon Ultra-4 50 kDa MWCO centrifugal filters (Millipore, UFC8050) prior to use.

Total genomic DNA was extracted using the Magnetic Universal Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, China,DP705-2) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and only samples with an OD260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were retained for downstream amplification.

Amplicon library preparation and PacBio revio sequencing

Amplicon libraries were constructed using a two-step PCR amplification strategy. In the first step, the full-length 16 S rRNA gene and ITS regions were amplified separately from genomic DNA in independent reaction systems. Amplification was performed using the Kinnex 16 S rRNA Kit and Kinnex PCR 12-fold Kit in combination with barcoded primers.

Barcoded primer sequences were:16 S Forward: CTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCTGATCGAGTCAAGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG, 16 S Reverse: AAGCAGTGGTATCAACGCAGAGTCATCGACGTRGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT, ITS Forward: CTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCTTATCGGTGCACTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA, ITS Reverse: AAGCAGTGGTATCAACGCAGAGCTGCGTAACTCCGTGTTTCAAGACGGG.

PCR products were examined by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm successful amplification and expected fragment sizes. Amplicons were purified using AMPure XP beads to remove primers and residual impurities and then quantified by fluorescence-based methods. Based on the quantification results, purified amplicons from each sample were normalized and pooled at equimolar ratios to generate final 16 S rRNA and ITS libraries.

The pooled libraries were evaluated on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer to assess fragment size distribution and integrity, followed by qPCR-based quantification. Final libraries were sequenced on the PacBio Revio platform, generating HiFi reads with a target sequencing depth of approximately 5×104 circular consensus (CCS) reads per sample for both bacterial 16 S rRNA and fungal ITS amplicons.

qPCR validation of selected taxa

To assess the reliability of 16 S rRNA gene sequencing-based abundance estimates in low-biomass gastric mucosal samples and to account for variability in microbial biomass across samples, we performed qPCR validation for a subset of bacterial taxa with available, experimentally validated primer sets; primer sequences and references are listed in the Key Resources Table. Among the taxa detected in our 16 S rRNA gene amplicon data, three genera (Streptococcus, Leifsonia and BCP) and the species Helicobacter pylori were selected for validation. Taxon-specific qPCR assays were performed on DNA extracted from the same gastric brushing specimens used for 16 S amplicon sequencing, with ten samples randomly selected for each target genus or species. In parallel, universal bacterial 16 S rRNA gene qPCR was performed and used to normalize taxon-specific signals to correct for differences in total bacterial load. Normalized abundances were log2-transformed and compared with the corresponding 16 S sequencing-derived relative abundances, and concordance was evaluated using Spearman correlation. Only these four taxa were tested, as other genera in the core network lacked suitable, reliably validated primer sets. This analysis was used to evaluate the robustness of sequencing-based abundance estimates and to help exclude substantial false-positive signals potentially introduced by the low microbial biomass of gastric samples.

Shotgun metagenomic library preparation and sequencing

For a subset of pediatric gastric mucosal samples, shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed in parallel with amplicon sequencing. Metagenomic libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, genomic DNA was fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to Illumina-compatible adapters with unique index barcodes to enable multiplexing. Library concentrations were quantified on a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the Invitrogen High Sensitivity DNA Assay, and fragment size distribution and integrity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Indexed libraries were then pooled at equimolar ratios and sequenced on an Illumina platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

Cell culture and bacterial pretreatment

The human gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 was used in this study and routinely cultured in Cytiva RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and triple antibiotics. The candidate probiotic A. spanius was inoculated into liquid medium and cultured at 30°C until reaching the logarithmic growth phase, after which it was prepared into three different treatments. For the live bacterial suspension (AS-Live), the culture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to collect the bacterial pellet, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in antibiotic-free RPMI 1640 medium. The bacterial supernatant (AS-CM) was obtained by filtering the aforementioned centrifugation supernatant through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove bacteria, followed by proportional dilution. The heat-killed bacterial suspension (AS-HK) was prepared by treating the live bacterial suspension in a 95°C water bath for 30 min, cooling, and diluting proportionally for subsequent use.

Establishment of the ethanol-induced cellular inflammatory injury model

GES-1 cells were seeded into culture plates at a density of 3–4 × 105 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator to allow for adherence. The old medium was then discarded, and the cells were washed once with sterile PBS. Subsequently, the cells were pretreated for 3 h with antibiotic-free medium containing the different bacterial treatments (AS-L, AS-S, or AS-H) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50. After pretreatment, the supernatant was discarded, and antibiotic-free RPMI 1640 medium containing a final ethanol concentration of 3% was added. The culture plates were sealed with Parafilm to prevent ethanol evaporation and subsequent concentration gradient changes. Following stimulation for 3–6 h, the medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed twice with cold PBS in preparation for sample collection.

Total RNA extraction

For total RNA extraction, adherent cells were directly lysed without digestion by adding TRIzol reagent at a ratio of 1 mL per 10 cm2. The lysate was collected, mixed with chloroform equal to 1/5 of the total volume, vigorously shaken for 15 s, incubated at room temperature for 2–3 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10–15 min at 4°C. The upper colorless aqueous phase was carefully transferred to a new centrifuge tube and mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol by inversion. After standing at room temperature for 10 min, the mixture was centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C to precipitate the RNA. The supernatant was discarded, and the RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol prepared with DEPC water, centrifuged, and air-dried at room temperature. Finally, the pellet was dissolved in an appropriate volume of RNase-free water; a small aliquot was taken for quality assessment, and the remaining samples were stored at −80°C for subsequent analyses.

Transcriptome library construction and sequencing

After the RNA samples passed integrity assessment via AATI and effective concentration quantification via qPCR, mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT)-attached magnetic beads and fragmented into short pieces using fragmentation buffer. Subsequently, first-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis with the addition of dNTPs, DNA polymerase I, and RNase H. The double-stranded cDNA was purified using AMPure XP beads, followed by end repair, A-tailing, sequencing adapter ligation, fragment size selection, and PCR amplification to construct the final sequencing library. Based on specific requirements, the libraries were pooled, 5’-phosphorylated, and circularized. Rolling circle amplification was then performed to form DNA nanoballs (DNBs). The DNBs were loaded onto a flow cell and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 high-throughput platform by Novogene. The raw FASTQ data generated were rigorously filtered to remove paired reads containing >10% unknown bases (N), reads with >50% low-quality bases (Q ≤ 5), and reads containing adapter sequences, thereby yielding high-quality clean data for downstream analyses.

Experimental animals and strain colonization

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–20 g were purchased and acclimatized for one week under a standard 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Indomethacin (Sigma-Aldrich, 17378) used for modeling was completely dissolved in a 5% sodium bicarbonate solution prior to the experiment. The candidate strain A. spanius was inoculated into nutrient agar (NA) or nutrient broth (NB) medium and cultured at 37°C until reaching the logarithmic growth phase. The bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in NB to the desired concentration. Following acclimatization, the mice were randomly divided into four experimental groups based on body weight: the normal control (NC) group, the model (M) group, the A. spanius protection (AS) group, and the A. spanius-only (AS- only) group. Mice in the AS and AS-only groups were orally gavaged with the A. spanius suspension every two days for a total of four doses, while mice in the NC and M groups were synchronously gavaged with the corresponding NB. Subsequently, all mice entered a one-week intervention-free period to allow for the observation of bacterial colonization in the stomach.

Indomethacin-induced gastric injury model and histopathology

Following the one-week intervention-free period, all mice were fasted for 18 h with free access to water prior to modeling. Subsequently, mice in the M and AS groups were orally gavaged with 30 mg/kg indomethacin to induce mild gastric mucosal inflammatory injury, whereas the NC and AS-only groups received the corresponding solvent. Six hours after the indomethacin administration, the mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation. The stomachs were rapidly excised, opened along the greater curvature, and gently rinsed with cold PBS to remove contents. A portion of the glandular stomach tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24–48 h, sequentially dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks were sectioned and subjected to routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Pathological features, including mucosal erosion, epithelial desquamation and necrosis, submucosal edema, and the infiltration of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and plasma cells, were evaluated under a light microscope.

Verification of strain colonization and qPCR analysis

Mucosal tissues containing the ulcer lesions and surrounding areas were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent use. To verify bacterial colonization, genomic DNA was extracted from the gastric tissues and subjected to 16 S rRNA PCR amplification to confirm the presence of AS. For the detection of inflammatory cytokine expression, total RNA was extracted from the homogenized tissues using the RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (R701, Vazyme). Following the determination of RNA concentration and purity, cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (R323-01, Vazyme), which includes a genomic DNA removal module. The primers used (forward primer as1-f: GCCCTTATGGGTAGGGCTTC; reverse primer as1-r: CAGACTGCGATCCGGACTAC) were designed using the NCBI BLAST tool. Subsequently, real-time quantitative PCR was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme) on a real-time PCR system. The primers used in this study, including the target genes (Tnf QM05522S, Il1b QM03422S, Il6 QM03482S) and the internal reference gene (Gapdh QM00014S), were all purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology. The relative mRNA expression levels of the inflammatory cytokines were calculated following normalization to the internal reference gene, Gapdh.

Shotgun metagenomic data processing and quality control

Raw metagenomic reads generated on the Illumina platform were subjected to a standardized quality-control pipeline to remove low-quality and non-target sequences prior to downstream analysis. Initial quality filtering was performed using fastp (v0.19.7). The following criteria were applied: (1) reads were discarded if more than 40% of bases had a Phred quality score ≤20; (2) reads containing more than 10% ambiguous (N) bases were removed; (3) reads with adapter-insert overlaps exceeding 15 bp were trimmed or discarded; and (4) obvious PCR duplicates were removed to reduce duplication artifacts.

Host-derived reads were then removed in two steps. First, reads were processed with KneadData (v0.7.4) to perform initial host screening. Second, the remaining reads were aligned against the human reference genome hg38 using Bowtie2 with the “--very-sensitive” preset, followed by additional filtering with samtools (using flags -f 12 -F 256) to remove residual host-mapped pairs. The resulting host-depleted, high-quality reads were defined as clean data and used for downstream taxonomic and functional profiling. For metagenomic functional profiling, gene family abundances were quantified using HUMAnN (v4.0.0.a1) with default parameters. For taxonomic profiling, clean host-depleted reads were aligned against the NCBI non-redundant (NR) protein database using DIAMOND blastx (v2.1.8) with the “-very-sensitive” mode. Taxonomic assignments were generated based on the best-hit scheme, and read counts were aggregated to produce species-level abundance tables. Only hits with an e-value ≤ 1e−5 and alignment length ≥50 amino acids were retained to ensure taxonomic specificity. The resulting species abundance matrices were used for downstream community composition analyses.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Clinical and hematological parameter analysis

To evaluate group-level differences while adjusting for potential confounders, we applied multivariate linear regression models to compare superficial gastritis controls and duodenal ulcer patients. Hematological parameters were treated as dependent variables, with disease status as the primary independent variable. Models were adjusted for age, sex, and delivery mode (vaginal vs. cesarean).

To assess potential statistical bias arising from substantial sample differences, we employed a bootstrap resampling approach for robustness validation. Specifically, 1000 bootstrap samples were generated by randomly sampling with replacement from the original dataset, each having the same size as the original dataset. For each bootstrap sample, the multivariate linear regression model, adjusted for age, sex, and delivery mode, was refitted, and the regression coefficient for disease status was recorded. The mean and 95% confidence interval of the 1000 bootstrap estimates were then computed and compared with the original model estimates to evaluate the stability of the key parameter estimates.

Regression coefficients and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for each parameter. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided q-value <0.05. All clinical and statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.2.1).

Microbial differential abundance analysis

Microbial abundance data at the genus level were analyzed using MaAsLin246 (Multivariate Association with Linear Models) R package. Age, sex, and delivery mode were included as covariates to control for potential confounding effects.

To evaluate the complex multivariate associations between clinical hematological parameters and microbial features, we utilized the Maaslin2 R package. Linear mixed models were constructed with disease status and targeted clinical variables (e.g., RBC, HGB, MCHC) as primary fixed effects, while strictly adjusting for potential confounding covariates including age, sex, and delivery mode. Significance was defined at an FDR-adjusted q < 0.05.

To evaluate the potential bias in statistical associations caused by substantial sample differences, a bootstrap resampling procedure (1000 iterations) was implemented for robustness validation. For each bootstrap sample, generated by random sampling with replacement from the original dataset, the MaAsLin2 analysis was repeated. The stability of each significant bacterial genus was then assessed by calculating the proportion of bootstrap iterations in which it remained significantly associated.

Bacterial genera were considered significantly associated with disease status or other clinical factors when they met both criteria.

Microbial association network analysis

To minimize noise and retain biologically meaningful signals, we applied prevalence and abundance filters to the microbial abundance matrices. The optimal thresholds for each dataset (e.g., bacterial and fungal) were determined separately through an iterative procedure. Specifically, we assessed the effect of a gradient of abundance and prevalence thresholds on the rate of taxa filtering. The final thresholds were selected at the point where the rate of taxa loss began to plateau, ensuring the removal of low-confidence taxa while preserving most of the community structure for downstream network analysis.

Genus-level co-occurrence correlation matrices were calculated using the Hmisc R package (utilizing Spearman’s rank correlation), retaining edges with |ρ| ≥ 0.5 and FDR <0.05.

Furthermore, to quantify the structural similarity of these networks across different disease stages (e.g., acute vs. recovery), Mantel tests were systematically performed using the vegan package with 999 permutations.

Both positive and negative significant correlations were included. Networks were visualized using Gephi (v0.9.2), where nodes represented genera and edges represented significant correlations. Edge weight was set proportional to the magnitude of the correlation coefficient (|ρ|). Network properties were used to characterize core taxa and their topological roles.

Microbial community typing

Microbial community types were identified using Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) clustering.47 Community typing was performed based on Jensen-Shannon divergence and Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices derived from genus-level relative abundance profiles.

Clustering analyses were conducted in R using the cluster and factoextra packages. The optimal number of clusters was determined by silhouette width. To evaluate robustness, clustering results were further validated using k-means clustering and Rand index-based similarity analysis.

Representative genera for each community type were defined based on high relative abundance and cluster specificity and were used in downstream functional and clinical association analyses.

Structural Equation Modeling and mediation analysis

To evaluate the bidirectional antagonistic mediation effects between H. pylori and the core microbial clusters (CST1A and CST1B), Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was performed using the lavaan R package. The models were fitted using Maximum Likelihood estimation with Robust standard errors (MLR) to account for potential non-normality in the abundance distributions. Mediation effects and their 95% confidence intervals were rigorously evaluated using a 1,000-iteration bootstrap resampling procedure.

Prediction of microbial functional potential

Functional profiles of 16 S rRNA gene data were predicted using PICRUSt2, with inferred gene families mapped to KEGG Orthology (KO) terms. KO abundances were collapsed into pathway-level functional categories and then aggregated by microbial community type.

Inter-group comparisons of functional pathway abundances were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and pathways with FDR <0.05 were considered significantly different.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and pathway recovery assessment

To comprehensively evaluate transcriptomic alterations and changes in the activity of specific biological pathways, the raw sequencing expression matrix was first rigorously preprocessed. Genes were retained for subsequent analyses only if they were expressed in more than half of the samples with an average expression count greater than 10. Differential gene expression analysis was initially conducted utilizing the DESeq2 R package. Subsequently, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was employed to explore the differences in molecular mechanisms among the various intervention groups. The human C2 KEGG canonical pathway gene sets were imported, and the GSVA package was used to calculate the pathway enrichment scores for each sample (n = 3 biological replicates per treatment group) under unsupervised conditions. Furthermore, standard GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses for differentially expressed genes were executed using the clusterProfiler package.

To elucidate the targeted mechanisms, the study focused on extracting the GSVA scores of core pathological pathways associated with apoptosis (e.g., Fas-JNK signaling), inflammation and immunity (e.g., complement cascades, IL-6-JAK-STAT pathway), MAPK, and metabolic stress. Inter-group GSVA score boxplots, dynamic trend line charts, and Z score normalized hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated using the ggplot2 and pheatmap packages to visualize the differences in pathway activation patterns among the normal control (NC), model (M), and intervention (AS) groups. To determine the specific functional form of AS, we simultaneously compared the transcriptomic effects among the live AS group, the AS conditioned medium (AS-CM) group, and the heat-killed AS (AS-HK) group. Finally, a Recovery Index (calculated as [AS - M]/[NC - M]) was computed for each targeted pathway to quantitatively evaluate the reversal and restorative efficacy of live AS on the pathological transcriptional signatures of the model group.

Machine learning-based classification

To assess the classification performance of core microbiota features, we constructed seven machine-learning models using their respective R packages: Extreme Gradient Boosting (xgboost), Random Forest (randomForest), AdaBoost (adabag), Neural Networks (nnet), Gradient Boosting Machine (gbm), GBMBoost (mboost), and Support Vector Machines (e1071). All models were trained using a 7:3 train-test split of the dataset, with input features standardized prior to modeling. Algorithm-specific hyperparameters were tuned based on standard recommendations. Notably, a 10-fold cross-validation was applied to the XGBoost training set to optimize boosting iterations and prevent overfitting.

Model performance was evaluated on the independent test set using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). 95% CIs for AUC were estimated via bootstrap resampling.

To test whether the predictive performance of the core microbiota reflected their specific community composition rather than simply high relative abundance, we additionally constructed two sets of control models. In the first control setting (other high-abundance genera feature set), we identified the 15 most abundant genera in each dataset and then removed all genera belonging to the predefined core microbiota as well as Helicobacter; the remaining high-abundance genera were used as predictors. In the second control setting (Hp-only feature set), models were built using only the relative abundance of Helicobacter as a single predictor. For both control feature sets, we applied the same train-test split, algorithms, and hyperparameter tuning procedures as for the core microbiota models, and compared performance using AUC and corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

All machine-learning analyses were implemented in R using standard packages for classification and model evaluation.

Cross-cohort association analysis of core submodules CST1A and CST1B

Core gastric microbiota submodules CST1A and CST1B were defined in the discovery cohort based on genus-level co-occurrence patterns, and the same genus-submodule assignments were used for all external validation cohorts (d1-d8). For each 16 S rRNA gene amplicon dataset, we imported the genus-level relative abundance matrix, the sample metadata, and the pre-defined list of core genera with their submodule labels (CST1A or CST1B). Samples were restricted to those with complete group annotations (e.g., noncancer [NC] vs. gastric cancer [GC], NC vs. duodenal ulcer [DU], or low-vs. high gastric cancer risk).

Genus-level abundances were first subset to the intersection between each cohort and the core genus list. For each sample, submodule-level abundances were obtained by summing the relative abundances of all genera assigned to CST1A or CST1B, respectively, yielding one abundance value per submodule per sample. Submodule prevalence was quantified as the proportion of samples with non-zero abundance (prevalence_gt0, reported in Figure S6D).

To assess the association between submodule abundance and disease status within each cohort, we performed logistic regression with disease status as the binary outcome (0 = noncase, 1 = case). For each dataset, the noncase category (e.g., NC or low-risk group) was used as the reference level. Effect directions were summarized as “Negative (protective)” for OR < 1 and “Positive (risk)” for OR > 1 (Figure S6D). All analyses were implemented in R using custom scripts.

Table S6. Summary of shotgun metagenomic sequencing quality metrics and host read depletion for brushing specimens, related to STAR Methods (shotgun metagenomic sequencing)

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed on gastric brushing specimens from selected participants. For each sample, Raw reads and Raw bases denote the total number of sequencing reads and bases generated, whereas Clean reads and Clean bases represent the read and base counts remaining after standard quality control filtering. GC(%) indicates the GC content of the clean reads, and Q20(%) and Q30(%) report the proportions of bases with Phred quality scores ≥20 and ≥30, respectively. To reduce host contamination, reads were mapped to the human reference genome and removed; Host removed reads and Host removed bases denote the remaining non-host (putative microbial) reads and bases after this filtering step. Microbiome ratio and Host ratio indicate the proportions of microbial vs. host bases among the clean reads, respectively, highlighting that only approximately 0.01%–3.5% of the sequenced bases could be confidently retained as microbial signal despite deep sequencing and stringent host depletion
