Is there a role of radiotherapy for steroid-resistant thyroid eye disease?
Authors: Jae Myoung Noh
Published: 2025

Thyroid eye disease (TED), also known as Graves’ orbitopathy, is the most common extrathyroidal manifestation of Graves’ disease [1]. Autoimmune reactions against thyroid-stimulating hormone receptors expressed in the orbital tissues result in fibroblast proliferation and overproduction of glycosaminoglycans [2,3]. Affected patients may present with clinical manifestations including periorbital edema, lid retraction, proptosis, and diplopia. Owing to the anti-inflammatory effects and radiosensitivity of lymphocytes infiltrating the orbit, orbital radiotherapy (RT) in combination with glucocorticoids is considered an effective second-line treatment for active moderate-to-severe TED, particularly in the presence of progressive diplopia [4,5]. The efficacy of RT for TED has been debated in clinical studies [6-9], largely due to several confounding factors and the use of diverse assessment criteria [5,10].

In the recent issue of Radiation Oncology Journal, Park and Lee [11] evaluated the efficacy of RT in patients with steroid-resistant TED by assessing the clinical outcomes of orbital inflammation and exophthalmos. This study retrospectively analyzed 62 patients with TED who were resistant to intravenous steroid therapy and received RT. The clinical activity score (CAS) was used to evaluate the severity of inflammation, and the posterior scleral distance (PSD) was used to measure exophthalmos. The estimated marginal means of the CAS demonstrated a progressive decline from 3.309 at baseline to 2.841 after steroid treatment and then to 1.387, 0.518, and 0.187 at 1, 6, and 12 months after RT. This finding suggests that the therapeutic response after RT was sustained over 1 year. In addition, PSD also improved after RT despite initial worsening after steroid therapy. In this context, the present study is valuable, as RT may serve as an effective salvage therapy for patients with steroid-resistant disease.

Although this study demonstrated the long-term efficacy of orbital RT, extended follow-up is required to establish its safety. Although this study lacked information on diabetes, RT should be used with caution in diabetic patients to minimize the risk of retinopathy [5,12]. Another study on the long-term safety of orbital RT for Graves’ ophthalmopathy reported no secondary tumors after a median follow-up of 11 years [13]. However, the authors recommend restricting orbital RT to patients older than 35 years, given the long latency of radiation-induced tumors. Park and Lee [11] included a 19-year-old patient who demonstrated sustained improvement over 7 years; however, caution is warranted regarding the potential risk of secondary malignancies.

In conclusion, RT plays a meaningful role in the management of steroid-resistant TED, providing a sustained therapeutic response beyond 1 year. With the advent of newer biologic agents, such as teprotumumab, rituximab, and tocilizumab, are available for the treatment of moderate-to-severe and active TED [4,5], future studies are needed to determine the optimal therapeutic approach—RT or biologic agents—for individual patients, particularly when the response to first-line treatment is inadequate and TED remains moderate to severe and active.
