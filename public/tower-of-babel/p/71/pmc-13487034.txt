Evaluation of surgical costs associated with isolated distal fibula fractures
Authors: Michael Amick, Tim Wardrop, Taylor Mendenhall, Chong Zhang, Angela P Presson, Devon Nixon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Operative fixation of distal fibula fractures is a common surgical procedure within orthopaedics. Understanding the variables that influence cost for surgical management of such injuries is increasingly important. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the cost differences in isolated distal fibula fixation.Methods:In total, 394 patients were identified who underwent isolated distal fibula fracture fixation between 2018 and 2025 based on Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) coding (27792). Patient demographics, fixation strategy, implant type, and surgical setting were analyzed. Multivariable generalized linear models with inverse Gaussian distribution were used to estimate adjusted cost ratios. Cost data were obtained using the Value-Drive Outcomes (VDO) tool, an instrument at our institution that aggregates itemized costs for each surgical encounter. Costs are reported as relative cost ratios rather than raw dollar amounts.Results:Of the 394 surgeries, 244 were performed at a specialty surgery center (SSC) and 150 were completed at an academic medical center (AMC). Total direct costs were higher for fibula fracture surgery performed at an AMC vs an SSC, resulting in a 42% increase in total direct costs for AMC procedures (cost ratio 1.42, 95% CI 1.32-1.52). Further, AMC facility costs were 84% higher than SSC (cost ratio 1.84; 95% CI 1.75-1.94), with the difference driven primarily by non-OR facility costs whereas OR facility costs were comparable between sites. Implant choice for fibula fixation did not appear to meaningfully influence cost. Surgeon specialty type did not differ in facility cost after controlling for surgery setting (facility cost ratio 1.00, 95% CI 0.95-1.06; P = .97).Conclusion:Distal fibular fracture fixation is significantly more costly when performed at an academic medical center than at a specialty surgery center, largely due to differences in non-OR facility costs. When medically appropriate, operative management of fibula fractures in an outpatient setting may lead to significant cost reductions.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative study.Keywords: distal fibula fracture, cost analysis, value-based care

Introduction

Ankle fractures—including isolated distal fibula injuries—are among the most common orthopaedic issue requiring operative fixation, with an annual incidence of 107 to 184 per 100 000 individuals across the United States.1,2 These injuries represent a major source of health care utilization and cost, with direct expenditures exceeding $1.2 billion annually. 1 Although surgical fixation through open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) remains the standard of care for unstable or displaced fibular fractures, the financial burden of treatment varies.

Recent trends in health care delivery have underscored the importance of value-based care, where outcomes are optimized in relation to costs. Specialty surgery centers (SSCs) have emerged as an attractive site for orthopaedic procedures, including ankle fracture fixation, as a lower-cost, streamlined surgical option with the potential to reduce overall expenditures by 30% to 60% compared with larger, academic medical centers (AMCs).3,4 However, true cost differential is nuanced and involves many subtle factors influenced by patient selection, fixation technique, implant type, and postoperative course.5,6

Comprehensive cost analyses on operative treatment of isolated distal fibula fractures remain limited. Existing literature has demonstrated variability in implant selection, surgeon preference, and care pathways, each of which can impact not only total cost but also the contribution margin for health care systems.7-9 Few studies have systematically compared costs between SSC and AMC settings. The present study evaluated costs associated with operative treatment of isolated distal fibula fractures. This analysis focused on the impact of fixation strategy, implant type, and facility setting (SSC vs AMC) on costs at a single institution. Through this study, we aimed to provide a more detailed assessment of cost factors in isolated distal fibular fracture management.

Methods

Data

Following institutional review board (IRB) approval, we conducted a retrospective chart review of patients who underwent operative fixation of an isolated distal fibula fracture between 2018 and 2025 based on Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code 27792. At our institution, isolated fibular treatment is typically performed through a standard lateral or posterior-lateral approach with a one-third tubular plate. Syndesmotic and/or deltoid stabilization are included depending on injury-specific factors and intraoperative testing following fibula stabilization. Patients with concomitant injuries, polytrauma, or incomplete cost data, as well as bimalleolar and trimalleolar fractures, were excluded. We individually reviewed all charts to ensure that the patient sustained an isolated fibula fracture.

Collected demographic variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification. Operative variables included additional construct components (syndesmotic screw and/or deltoid ligament repair), implant type (lateral locking plate vs one-third tubular plate), and surgeon specialty (orthopaedic foot and ankle surgeon vs orthopaedic trauma surgeon). Procedures were further categorized by facility location as either the university-affiliated academic medical center (AMC: site of service 22) or the university-affiliated specialty surgical center (SSC: site of service 19).

Using the institutional Value Driven Outcome (VDO) tool, 10 cost data for total direct costs and facility costs, along with pharmacy, implant, and supply costs, were analyzed. Facility costs were further separated into operating room (OR) and non-OR facility cost components, and per-hour OR facility cost was computed as the OR facility cost divided by total OR time. All costs were inflation-adjusted to 2022 US dollars using the Price Indexes for Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE). 11 Then, to de-identify the costs (as required for publishing our institutional cost data), observations within each cost type were divided by the sample median of that cost type. Implant costs represent the hardware used for fibula fixation and syndesmotic/deltoid repair. Pharmacy costs include medications administered including anesthesia. Supply costs represent all disposable supplies. Because patients did not typically accrue laboratory or imaging costs, these categories were excluded from the final analysis.

The VDO tool serves as a procedure-level database, aggregating itemized expenditure data to enable the evaluation of institutional costs at the level of individual operations.10,12,13 Costs associated with individual procedures are derived from both direct resource consumption and physician relative value units, producing a comprehensive estimate of patient care expenses. 10 This approach has been applied in prior orthopaedic foot and ankle cost analyses, including studies of total ankle replacements, Achilles tendon repair, and ankle arthrodesis.14-16 In accordance with institutional agreements and contractual limitations, costs are not reported in raw dollar figures; instead, findings are expressed as cost ratios.

Statistical Methods

Patient demographics, surgical characteristics, and costs were summarized by procedure location (AMC and SSC). It is important to note that the SSC mentioned in this article is not a true ambulatory surgery center (ASC)—it is an orthopaedic specialty facility within a larger academic medical system. Of note, these analyses are exploratory, hypothesis-generating, but not confirmatory.

For continuous variables, we report the mean (SD), median (IQR), and range; between-group differences were assessed using t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 or Fisher exact tests. No a priori sample size calculation was performed because of the study being a retrospective analysis. Subjects with missing data were excluded from statistical evaluation.

We evaluated patient and surgical variables for their association with total cost and facility cost using univariable and multivariable generalized linear models (GLMs). Multivariable models included age, year of surgery, location, surgeon specialty (orthopaedic trauma and orthopaedic foot and ankle surgery), and fixation type. Trainee involvement and total operating room time were also included to address any potential influence on costs. Multicollinearity was assessed using the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) scaled as GVIF1/(2df), 17 which is comparable across continuous and categorical predictors with multiple levels and equivalent to √VIF for single-coefficient predictors. All values were below 2.24, corresponding to a conventional VIF threshold of 5. The selection of the appropriate GLM family was guided by 2 steps: first, visual inspection of the cost distributions via histograms, and second, evaluation of the mean-variance relationship relative to the exposure variables using the Tweedie profile.18,19 Based on these assessments, we determined that an inverse Gaussian distribution with a log link best fits the data. Goodness-of-fit was examined visually using simulated residuals. 20 We reported cost ratios with 95% CIs. Analyses were conducted in R 21 with a 2-sided significance level set at P <.05.

Results

In total, 394 patients met inclusion criteria. Of the 394 patients, 150 underwent surgical fixation at an AMC and 244 at an SSC. Baseline characteristics were broadly similar across both groups (Table 1). With the numbers available, no statistically significant differences could be detected between groups for age (P = .58) or sex (P = .50). The type of surgeon varied significantly by facility: orthopaedic trauma surgeons performed 82% of the AMC cases, whereas orthopaedic foot and ankle specialists performed 91% of SSC cases (P < .001).

Adjunctive fixation methods (syndesmosis fixation and/or deltoid repair) differed significantly between sites (P = .004). Isolated fibula-only fixation was performed in 47% of AMC and 40% of SSC cases. Fibula and syndesmotic fixation were more frequent at an AMC (47% vs 42%), while combined fixation (fibula, deltoid, and syndesmosis) was performed more frequently at the SSC (11% vs 3%). Locking plate utilization was rare, with most constructs fixed using one-third tubular plates (Table 1). Given the rarity of locking plate use, we were underpowered to study implant type on cost (ie, cost differences between lateral locking plates and tubular plates).

Cost Outcomes

Mean total direct costs were nearly double at the AMC cohort compared to the SSC group (Table 1; Figure 1). Figure 2 shows total direct costs by surgical year, highlighting a persistent separation between cohorts throughout the study period, with AMC cases demonstrating greater costs. Adjusting for age, year of surgery, surgeon type, fixation construct, trainee involvement, and total OR time, surgical fixation at an AMC was associated with 42% higher total direct costs (cost ratio, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.32-1.52; P <.001; Table 2).

To better characterize the source of the facility cost difference, we further separated facility costs into OR and non-OR components (Table 1). OR facility costs were similar between settings (median 1.1 at AMC vs 0.97 at SSC, P = .051), and per-hour OR facility costs were slightly higher at the AMC than the SSC (median 0.69 vs 0.63, P < .001). In contrast, non-OR facility costs were substantially higher at the AMC (median 15 vs 0.46; P < .001), accounting for most of the facility cost difference between sites. Regression analyses confirmed that syndesmotic and combined fixation constructs were independent predictors of higher cost compared with fibula-only fixation. After controlling for surgery site, there were no differences in facility costs between surgeon types (orthopaedic trauma vs orthopaedic foot and ankle) (Tables 2 and 3). The facility cost ratio was consistent between univariable analysis (1.77, 95% CI: 1.64, 1.92) and multivariable analysis (1.84, 95% CI: 1.75, 1.94), indicating that the facility cost difference is not substantially attributable to differences in patient characteristics, surgeon specialty, trainee involvement, or case duration.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive comparison of costs associated with isolated distal fibula fracture fixation performed at an SSC vs an AMC. Our findings demonstrate that, even at the same institution, total direct costs were nearly twice as high for procedures performed at an AMC, with non-OR facility charges accounting for the bulk of the difference. Even after adjusting for patient demographics, ASA classification, surgeon specialty, fixation construct, trainee involvement, and total OR time, procedures performed at AMCs were associated with a 42% increase in total direct costs and 84% increase in facility costs.

Facility charges accounted for the single largest component of expenditure for both AMC and SSC (Figure 1), with significantly lower facility costs at SSC compared with AMC. This aligns with prior research showing that ambulatory centers achieve substantial cost savings through increased efficiency and lower overhead, without compromising patient outcomes.22,23 Allen et al 24 further demonstrated that ankle ORIF procedures performed in SSCs benefit from shorter turnover times and streamlined scheduling, with no increase in complication rates. Our results reinforce these findings. It is possible that cost differences would be further amplified using our VDO tool if we could, in fact, compare a true ASC to an AMC.

Although facility type was a dominant cost driver, fixation strategy also significantly affected costs. Procedures involving syndesmotic stabilization or combined fixation with deltoid repair were more costly than fibula-only constructs. These findings are consistent with previous studies by Okelana et al 8 and Freking et al, 5 which show that procedural complexity and implant selection contribute to cost variability in ankle fracture care. Implant and supply costs were also higher in AMC cases. Although locking lateral plates were rarely used in our cohort, prior studies have consistently shown that they increase implant costs by 5- to 8-fold.8,25 These findings may highlight opportunities for further cost optimization.

Surgeon specialty was strongly correlated with facility location: orthopaedic trauma surgeons performed many AMC cases, and orthopaedic foot and ankle surgeons treated most SSC patients (Table 1). Although trauma surgeons were associated with higher facility costs in univariable analysis, this effect was fully attenuated after adjusting for surgery location, although the facility cost ratio itself was largely unchanged from univariable (1.77) to the adjusted analysis (1.84) including surgeon specialty, trainee involvement, and total OR time. Decomposition of facility costs into OR and non-OR components further clarified the mechanism: non-OR facility costs were higher at the AMC and accounted for nearly the entire facility cost difference. These findings indicate that the cost difference reflects properties of the care setting itself, including pre-operative holding, post-anesthesia recovery, and other non-OR resources (rather than differences in surgeon specialty or case duration). Patients treated at AMCs also had higher ASA classifications, reflecting greater medical complexity. While this likely contributed to increased perioperative resource utilization, our adjusted analysis showed that facility-related costs remained significantly elevated even after accounting for ASA class. This is consistent with findings by Pasic et al 26 who reported similar cost disparities between inpatient and outpatient ankle ORIF in Canada, even after adjusting for comorbidities.

There is a growing body of evidence that SSCs can deliver surgery at substantially lower cost; our findings support this cost savings.3,4,27 In the context of surgery for isolated distal fibula fracture, SSC-based care resulted in reduced total direct costs, primarily because of lower facility costs. Category-specific cost breakdowns (Figure 1) also highlight modifiable drivers beyond the site of care. Prior studies have demonstrated that standardizing implants and minimizing unnecessary fixation can enhance value without compromising stability or healing.5,25 These represent actionable targets for orthopaedic teams aiming to align care with value-based principles.

This study has several notable limitations. As a retrospective, single-hospital system design, the nature of the study introduces risk of bias and issues with reproducibility. Although multivariable modeling adjusted for ASA class, fixation construct, and surgeon specialty, residual differences in comorbidities, socioeconomic status, or insurance coverage, as examples, may still influence both facility assignment and cost. Cost differences highlighted in this study center on the episode of care (surgery itself) and do not account for other potential cost differences, including physical therapy, nursing, or rehabilitation care. We were unable to comment on cost differences between implant choices, as 98% of patients were treated with tubular plates regardless of surgery location and surgeon specialty. This study does not address the cost implications of secondary operations, such as elective hardware removal. As a single-institution analysis, practice and cost structures may vary across regions.

Conclusion

Operative fixation of isolated distal fibula fractures is significantly more costly when performed at an academic medical center compared with a specialty surgery center, primarily because of higher non-OR facility–related expenses. For providers and health systems, these findings support efforts to optimize site-of-care selection and align resource use with value-based care principles. Overall, directing eligible patients to outpatient specialty centers represents a practical strategy to lower costs while maintaining high-quality surgical outcomes.
