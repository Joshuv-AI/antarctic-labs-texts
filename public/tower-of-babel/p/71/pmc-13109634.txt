Sustainable Oxidation of Azoles: Structure‐Reactivity Controls and Reactive Species Dynamics in UV‐Activated Persulfate Processes
Authors: Heejin Yang, Ahyeon Jo, Sungju Yu, Chang‐Gu Lee, Gyu Leem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the increasing use of azoles, comparative studies on their degradation and mineralization across oxidative systems remain limited. This study systematically examined the degradation and mineralization behaviors of four representative azoles, benzotriazole (BTA), 5‐methylbenzotriazole (5MBTA), triazole (TA), and pyrazole (PR), in UV‐activated persulfate processes. Although all azoles were rapidly degraded, mineralization efficiencies differed markedly between peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and peroxydisulfate (PDS) and were strongly influenced by molecular structure. PR and TA achieved near‐complete mineralization within 30 min, whereas BTA and 5MBTA showed limited total organic carbon removal, particularly in UV/PMS. Scavenger and probe tests indicated that BTA degradation was dominated by singlet oxygen, while 5MBTA proceeded through both singlet oxygen and electron‐transfer pathways. Electrochemical analysis confirmed that PMS facilitated electron transfer more effectively than PDS, explaining differences in oxidation behavior. pH tests showed that alkalinity reduced mineralization in UV/PDS but enhanced it in UV/PMS through SO5 2−‐mediated electron transfer. Both systems maintained strong performance under varying pollutant levels, oxidant dosages, and realistic water matrices. Overall, degradation followed PR > TA > 5MBTA > BTA. These findings clarify structure–reactivity relationships in azole oxidation and highlight UV/PMS and UV/PDS as promising and sustainable advanced treatment technologies for azole‐contaminated wastewater.

Introduction

Azole compounds, including benzotriazole (BTA), 5‐methylbenzotriazole (5MBTA), triazole (TA), and pyrazole (PR), are widely used in industrial and consumer applications owing to their chemical stability and versatile functional properties [1, 2]. BTA and 5MBTA are widely used as corrosion inhibitors in cooling systems, antifreeze formulations, and metal‐finishing processes [3]. Additionally, triazole‐ and pyrazole‐based compounds such as TA and PR are widely utilized not only as catalysts but also across diverse applications, including corrosion inhibition, fluorescent sensing, and drug delivery systems [4, 5]. However, a major environmental concern associated with azoles is their persistence during conventional wastewater treatment [6]. Their aromatic heterocyclic structures, high electron densities, and nitrogen substitutions confer strong resistance to microbial degradation and oxidative breakdown, leading to their continuous release into aquatic environments [7]. Numerous studies have detected BTA and 5MBTA in wastewater effluents, raising concerns regarding their environmental persistence and potential ecotoxicological impacts [8, 9]. Moreover, several azoles exert toxic and endocrine‐disrupting effects on aquatic organisms, highlighting the urgent need for effective and reliable advanced treatment strategies [10].

Despite their structural similarities, azoles exhibit significantly different degradation behaviors depending on their molecular compositions [11]. BTA and 5MBTA have fused benzene‐triazole ring systems that enhance stability, whereas TA and PR are composed of smaller, less‐conjugated heterocycles that are relatively more susceptible to oxidation [11, 12]. These structural changes have a decisive effect on the way each compound interacts with reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced during advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), affecting both degradation kinetics and mineralization efficiency [13]. Thus, comparative studies involving multiple azole types are essential to reveal ROS selectivity for different heterocyclic structures and to understand how specific molecular features regulate the persistence or degradation of these contaminants [14, 15, 16]. These insights are essential for optimizing AOP design and mitigating the environmental risks posed by azoles in water systems.

AOPs are recognized as effective treatment technologies that remove refractory organic pollutants via the generation of highly reactive species, notably hydroxyl radicals (OH•) [17, 18]. Commonly applied processes include UV/H2O2‐ and O3‐based processes, Fenton and photo‐Fenton reactions, and persulfate‐based oxidation using peroxymonosulfate (PMS) or peroxydisulfate (PDS). Among these, UV‐activated persulfate processes (UV/PMS and UV/PDS) have attracted increasing attention owing to their strong oxidative capacity, broad applicability, and operational robustness [19]. UV exposure activates PMS (HSO5 −) and PDS (S2O8 2−), leading to the formation of sulfate radicals (SO4 •−), which are more persistent and possess higher redox potential (2.5–3.1 V) than OH•, thereby enhancing oxidation efficiency [20]. Additionally, secondary ROS such as superoxide radicals (O2 •−) and singlet oxygen (1O2) are formed, enabling the selective oxidation of target pollutants [21, 22]. Furthermore, UV/persulfate processes maintain high efficiency across wide pH ranges and show reduced interference from background matrix components such as natural organic matter (NOM), bicarbonates, and anions [23]. Their high selectivity, minimal secondary pollution, and adaptability make them promising candidates for treating persistent and emerging contaminants.

Ensuring access to clean water and improving wastewater treatment efficiency are central priorities of Sustainable Development Goal 6 (SDG 6) [24]. Among emerging contaminants, azoles are increasingly detected in aquatic environments and are challenging to remove using conventional treatment methods due to their structural stability and resistance to degradation. Advanced oxidation strategies that enable both rapid decomposition and effective mineralization are therefore critical for mitigating their environmental persistence and supporting sustainable water management [25]. Mineralization, defined as the complete conversion of organic pollutants into water and carbon dioxide, serves as a key indicator of the practical performance of advanced oxidation processes, as incomplete mineralization can lead to toxicity or the continued accumulation of harmful intermediates [26]. In UV‐activated persulfate systems, the decomposition pathways of azoles vary according to their physicochemical properties, which strongly influence mineralization efficiency and result in distinct differences in total organic carbon removal between UV/PDS and UV/PMS processes [27, 28]. Consequently, a comparative evaluation of azole mineralization across these systems is essential for elucidating structure‐dependent reactivity, identifying the governing degradation mechanisms, and assessing their applicability under realistic water treatment conditions, ultimately supporting the development of sustainable and robust technologies aligned with the objectives of SDG 6.

However, few studies have systematically compared the decomposition and mineralization behaviors of structurally diverse azoles under the UV/PMS and UV/PDS conditions. To fill this research gap, we systematically evaluated the removal and mineralization efficiencies of BTA, 5MBTA, TA, and PR using both processes. Degradation performance was assessed through changes in azole concentration, whereas mineralization was determined by TOC removal. To better understand why the TOC removal efficiency differed between the two processes, we carried out scavenger and probe experiments to reveal and compare the key ROS contributing to azole degradation. The azole degradation mechanism was analyzed by identifying the predominant ROS and their reaction pathways. Furthermore, the role of pH was explored, showing that changes in solution acidity led to markedly different degradation and mineralization behaviors in the UV‐activated persulfate processes. Finally, the impact of key water quality indicators, such as the initial pollutant concentration, oxidant capacity, background anions, and NOM, was investigated to evaluate the robustness and practical applicability of the UV/PMS and UV/PDS processes for azole‐contaminated water treatment.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Chemicals

Analytical‐grade BTA (1H‐Benzotriazole, C6H5N3), 5MBTA (5‐Methyl‐1H‐benzotriazole, C7H7N3), TR (1,2,4‐Triazole, C2H3N3), PR (1,2‐Diazole, C3H4N2), nitrobenzene, nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT), furfuryl alcohol (FFA), potassium peroxymonosulfate (PMS, KHSO5·0.5KHSO4·0.5K2SO4), and technical‐grade sigma humic acid (SHA) were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich. Samchun Chemical Co. supplied tert‐butanol, sodium thiosulfate pentahydrate (Na2S2O3 · 5H2O), p‐benzoquinone (BQ), sodium azide (NaN3), ethanol (EtOH), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), and hydrochloric acid (HCl). Potassium persulfate (PDS, K2S2O8) was purchased from Daejung Chemicals. The high‐purity methanol and acetonitrile used for HPLC analysis were obtained from Fisher Scientific. The Aladdin humic acid sodium salt (AHA) was purchased from Aladdin Scientific.

Experimental Procedure

A UV irradiation reactor consisting of a continuous 254 nm wavelength mercury lamp was used in the experiment. The UV irradiation intensity on the surface of the reaction solution was set to 3.82 mJ·cm−2, and a circular quartz container with a volume of 50 mL was used as the reaction vessel. The initial concentration range of pollutants was set to 0.2–0.8 mM, the concentration of PDS and PMS was set to 5–20 mM, and the pH was set to 3–12. Scavengers that selectively react with specific ROS are commonly used to identify dominant ROS and assess their relative contributions in advanced oxidation processes. Accordingly, EtOH, TBA, BQ, and L‐histidine were employed to quench OH•/SO4 •−, OH•, O2 •−, and 1O2, respectively. The influence of each reactive species was evaluated based on the extent to which azole decomposition was inhibited by each scavenger. In addition, 50 mM NBT and FFA probes were used to confirm the effects of the active species. In the coexistence ion experiment, 5 mM of Cl−, SO4 2−, and NO3 − ions were added to the solution matrix. For the HA experiment, HA was added to ultrapure water, filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter, and used in the experiment at a concentration of 10 mg L−1. Analytical Method

Residual pollutants were quantified using HPLC with a C18 column (SunFire C18 5 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm). Mobile phase compositions and detection wavelengths were optimized for each analyte: BTA was analyzed using ACN (ratio 0.65) and 0.1% phosphoric acid (ratio 0.35) at 210 nm; 5MBTA was analyzed using ACN (ratio 0.5) and water DI (ratio 0.5) at 280 nm; TA was analyzed using ACN (ratio 0.65) and water DI (ratio 0.35) at 200 nm; PR was analyzed using ACN (ratio 0.55) and water DI (ratio 0.45) at 215 nm. The flow rate for all chromatographic runs was maintained at 1.0 mL min−1. To ensure quantitative accuracy, experiments and analyses were conducted in duplicate, calibration curves were generated from the analysis values, and the method detection limits were determined. To investigate the potential changes induced by the addition of oxidizing agents and azoles, an open‐circuit potential (OCP) analysis was conducted using a CompactStat, Ivium. An Ag/AgCl reference electrode, platinum counter electrode, and glassy carbon working electrode were used in the electrochemical analysis. The electrodes were immersed in 50 mL of an electrolyte solution containing 0.1 M Na2SO4. During the measurements, oxidizing agents (50 mM) and azoles (10 mM) were sequentially introduced into the electrolyte at controlled time intervals.

Results and Discussion

Variation in Azole Degradation Efficiency Depending on the Process

The changes in the degradation performance of azoles across the different processes are presented in Figures 1 and S1. The experimental results revealed that BTA and 5MBTA exhibited the lowest removal efficiencies when only PMS or PDS was applied, whereas their degradation significantly improved under UVC irradiation. In contrast, TA and PR showed relatively higher removal rates with PMS or PDS alone compared to BTA and 5MBTA but displayed the lowest degradation efficiency when subjected only to UVC light. These differences can be attributed to the distinct structural characteristics of the compounds (Table 1). In BTA and 5MBTA, the benzene ring strongly absorbs UV radiation, facilitating intersystem crossing to the excited triplet state and enabling subsequent electron transfer [33]. However, the overall removal efficiency of these compounds remains below 40%, likely because of the short lifetime of the UV‐excited triplet state, which limits effective electron transfer reactions [1]. Although all azoles tested contained C‐H and N‐H bonds, differences in degradation behavior are mainly related to changes in heterocyclic ring structure and electronic properties. In particular, TA and PR containing triazole or pyrazole rings show relatively higher electron‐donating capacity, but the direct electron transfer reaction with PMS or PDS proceeds slowly, resulting in limited degradation under oxidant‐only conditions (Equations (2)–(3)) [34]. When UVC irradiation was combined with PMS or PDS, the removal efficiencies of all four azoles increased significantly regardless of structural differences. Correspondingly, the consumption of PMS and PDS also increased markedly under UV irradiation (Figure S2). This synergistic enhancement arises from the photolytic activation of PMS and PDS by UVC light, producing reactive radicals such as SO4 •−, OH•, and O2 •− [35]. Consequently, both the UV/PDS and UV/PMS processes achieved consistently high degradation efficiencies, demonstrating superior performance over single‐oxidant or UV treatments, and underscoring the advantages of UV‐assisted sulfate radical oxidation for azole removal.

Variation in Azole Mineralization Efficiency Depending on the Process

As shown in Figure S1, the processes involving UV irradiation, PDS, and PMS exhibited excellent azole decomposition within 30 min. Nevertheless, to compare the variation in mineralization efficiency among these processes, the TOC removal efficiency for each treatment was further analyzed (Figures 2 and S3). The results revealed that TOC removal was negligible when UV or oxidant was applied individually, whereas significant mineralization occurred only in the combined UV/PDS and UV/PMS processes [36]. Notably, BTA and 5MBTA exhibited lower TOC removal efficiencies in UV/PMS than in UV/PDS, whereas TA and PR showed substantial TOC removal in both processes. These findings indicate that although azoles were effectively decomposed by the generated reactive species, their intermediate and by‐product compounds were not readily mineralized [37]. These differences can be explained by two mechanisms. First, variations in the dominant ROS between the UV/PDS and UV/PMS processes influence the degradation efficiency owing to differences in their oxidation potentials. When ROS with high oxidation potential dominate, both pollutant and intermediate decomposition are enhanced. Conversely, the dominance of ROS with lower potential reduces the mineralization efficiency. Second, the degradation efficiency may depend on the reactivity and selectivity of the generated ROS. If ROS exhibit high selectivity toward azole structures, the parent compound may decompose rapidly, hindering the mineralization of intermediates [13]. To elucidate these mechanisms, scavenger experiments were conducted to identify the major ROS involved in the UV/PDS and UV/PMS processes.

Mineralization of azole compounds in UV persulfate activation. (a) BTA, (b) 5MBTA, (c) TA, and (d) PR (azoles = 0.4 mM; PMS and PDS = 10 mM; UV254 nm = 3.82 J·cm−2, Time = 30 min).

Analysis of the TOC removal rates revealed that BTA exhibited significant variations in mineralization efficiency depending on the applied process. To further elucidate these differences, LC‐MS analysis was conducted to identify and compare the intermediate products formed during the BTA degradation under each condition (Figure S4 and Table S1). The results showed that In the UV/PDS process, several intermediate products were detected during the initial reaction period but gradually disappeared within 30 min, indicating that both BTA and its intermediates were completely decomposed. In contrast, in the UV/PMS process, intermediate peaks emerged progressively over time and persisted even after 30 min of reaction, suggesting that although BTA underwent rapid primary degradation, the resulting intermediates were more resistant to further degradation. Previous studies have reported that BTA decomposes through the cleavage of its benzene and triazole rings, generating various intermediate products [38, 39]. In this study, BTA was degraded in both processes, producing intermediates with m/z values ranging from 52 to 111.6 (Table S1). This indicates that BTA rapidly breaks down into smaller molecular fragments during decomposition [40].

Verification of Dominant ROS via Scavenger Experiments

The dominant reactive species involved in azole degradation were identified using EtOH, TBA, BQ, and L‐His as quenchers, and changes in the degradation rates were evaluated through scavenger experiments (Figures 3 and S5). The results showed that TA and PR exhibited the greatest L‐His inhibition. In contrast, BTA and 5MBTA were most suppressed by BQ, indicating that TA and PR were primarily degraded by 1O2, while BTA and 5MBTA were degraded mainly by O2 •−. Although both BTA and 5MBTA appeared to be decomposed by O2 •− irrespective of the oxidant type (PMS or PDS), their TOC removal efficiencies differed markedly, suggesting additional ROS contributions. To verify this, probe experiments using NBT for O2 •− and FFA for 1O2 were performed (Figure S6). Both probes exhibited time‐dependent degradation, confirming the formation of both ROS species. However, FFA significantly inhibited BTA degradation, indicating that 1O2 played a dominant role in BTA oxidation (Figure S6a), whereas 5MBTA degradation was barely affected by FFA (Figure S6c). In contrast, the addition of NBT had little effect on either compound (Figure S6b,d). These results demonstrate that BTA degradation proceeds mainly through 1O2 oxidation, regardless of the oxidant type, whereas 5MBTA decomposition involves both 1O2 oxidation and electron transfer pathways, which is consistent with its distinct TOC removal behavior.

Effect of ROS on UV persulfate activation. (a) BTA, (b) 5MBTA, (c) TA, and (d) PR (azoles = 0.4 mM; PMS and PDS = 10 mM; EtOH, TBA, BQ, and L‐histidine = 10 mM; UV254 nm = 3.82 J·cm−2, Time = 30 min).

Azole compounds are susceptible to oxidation by 1O2 because they contain relatively electron‐rich N—H and C—H bonds, which are highly vulnerable to electrophilic attack [41]. Consequently, 1O2 generated through PDS or PMS activation plays a significant role in azole degradation. However, 1O2 has a lower oxidation potential than other ROS and exhibits selective reactivity, effectively oxidizing certain compounds [21]. Thus, 1O2 alone has limited ability to fully mineralize azoles and their intermediates. In contrast, O2 •−, with higher oxidative strength, can further degrade these intermediates, enhancing the overall mineralization efficiency. In the UV/PDS process, 1O2 is mainly produced via secondary reactions involving O2 •− (Equations (4)– (7)) [42]. Therefore, quenching O2 •− suppresses subsequent 1O2 generation. Conversely, in the UV/PMS process, 1O2 can also form through PMS self‐decomposition, although O2 •− production proceeds more slowly (Equations (8)–(13)) [43, 44]. Particularly for 5MBTA, O2 •− generation in UV/PMS was very slow (Figure S6d), as 5MBTA activates PMS through direct electron transfer, favoring ROS formation pathways other than O2 •− production (Equation (14)) [45].

Electrochemical analysis was performed to confirm that 5MBTA, unlike BTA, was degraded by an electron transfer reaction (Figure 4). The addition of PMS and PDS shifts the electrode potential to a more positive value, indicating that the oxidant accepts electrons at the electrode surface [38]. The magnitude of the potential increase was different for PMS and PDS, which could be attributed to the low reduction potential of PMS [46]. This facilitates the activation of PMS via electron transfer, thereby facilitating the generation of reactive species [47, 48]. Subsequently, when BTA was introduced into the PMS process, the potential briefly decreased and then stabilized at equilibrium, whereas for 5MBTA, the potential shifted in the negative direction (Figure 4a). These observations suggest that BTA does not participate significantly in electron transfer reactions under these conditions [49], whereas 5MBTA contributes electrons to the oxidative species and direct electron transfer [50, 51]. However, during the PDS process, both BTA and 5MBTA increased in potential and then remained stable until equilibrium was reached (Figure 4b). This behavior implies that azole compounds increase the oxidation potential of PDS through the formation of PDS‐azole complexes [52]. Overall, these results show that 5MBTA exhibits improved electron‐donating capacity compared to BTA, which supports its higher reactivity and more efficient degradation in the electron transfer induction process.

pH Influencing Azole Degradation in the Process

pH is a critical factor that strongly governs the performance of AOPs because it influences both radical generation pathways and pollutant reactivity [53]. Depending on the solution pH, the relative production of SO4 •− and OH• changes significantly, while the ionization state of organic contaminants also shifts, altering their susceptibility to oxidative attack [54]. As a result, pH affects not only the degradation rate of parent pollutants, but also the extent of TOC removal, which reflects the overall mineralization efficiency. To assess this effect, TOC removal was analyzed across a wide range of pH values (Figure 5). The results showed that, for BTA, TOC removal in the UV/PMS process was negligible across the entire pH range, whereas in the UV/PDS process, the TOC removal efficiency decreased markedly with increasing pH. This difference can be attributed to changes in the generation of ROS under alkaline conditions [55]. In the UV/PDS process, SO4 •− formed by PDS activation readily reacts with OH− ions to produce OH• [56]. More importantly, the formation of OH• occurs at the expense of SO4 •− and reduces the secondary generation of O2 •−, which plays a dominant role in BTA degradation and mineralization [57]. Collectively, these effects result in a marked decline in TOC removal efficiency in the UV/PDS process at elevated pH levels. In the case of 5MBTA, the TOC removal rate in the UV/PDS process also decreased with increasing pH. Similar to BTA, this decline can be attributed to the suppression of O2 •− generation as SO4 •− is increasingly converted into OH• under alkaline conditions, thereby lowering the overall removal efficiency. In contrast, the UV/PMS process exhibits the opposite trend, showing enhanced TOC removal with increasing pH. This improvement can be explained by the higher molar absorption coefficient of PMS at elevated pH levels, which results in enhanced photolytic activation and increased production of reactive radicals [19]. Moreover, under alkaline conditions, PMS predominantly exists as SO5 2−, and the SO5 •− species generated via reactions between PMS and OH• can further react with water to produce additional O2 •−, thereby amplifying the overall oxidative capacity [42, 44]. Consequently, the UV/PMS process demonstrated superior efficiency under alkaline conditions, suggesting its potential advantage over UV/PDS for pollutant mineralization in high‐pH environments.

General Factors Influencing Azole Degradation in the Process

To investigate the influence of process conditions on azole removal, the removal efficiency was evaluated with respect to additional factors beyond pH. The primary variables considered were the initial azole and oxidant concentrations [58]. The experimental range was set at 0.2–0.8 mM for the initial azole concentration and 5–20 mM for the oxidant concentration, showing the change in process efficiency for each condition (Figures 6–7). The results showed that BTA and 5MBTA exhibited similar trends. Specifically, as the initial azole concentration increased, the decomposition efficiency decreased, likely due to a higher pollutant‐to‐radical ratio that limited the availability of reactive species per molecule [59]. Conversely, increasing the oxidant concentration enhanced the decomposition efficiency, reflecting the greater generation of reactive species at higher oxidant dosages [60].

Assessment of Suppression by Common Backgrounds in the Water Matrix

As shown in the previous sections, azoles were effectively degraded within a short time under the UV‐PDS and UV‐PMS processes. However, the presence of co‐existing ions such as Cl−, NO3 −, SO4 2−, and CO3 2−, as well as HA, can reduce decomposition efficiency by competing with azoles for reactive species. To evaluate the extent of this suppression, efficiency was tested in the presence of two types of HA along with Cl−, NO3 −, CO3 2−, and SO4 2−, which are commonly found in natural water matrices (Figure 8). The results indicated that in the UV/PMS process, the degradation of both BTA and 5MBTA was only marginally affected by coexisting ions or HA. Similarly, in the UV/PDS process, most of the background components did not significantly suppress the removal efficiency. However, a noticeable decrease in azole degradation was observed in the presence of AHA during UV/PDS. This effect can be attributed to the high reactivity of NOM toward radicals, leading to ROS scavenging [61]. Despite the presence of NOM, both the UV/PMS and UV/PDS processes achieved complete azole removal within 30 min, similar to the blank conditions. This result indicates that the effect of background organic matter is minimal, suggesting that both processes can maintain high removal efficiencies, even in real wastewater environments [62].

Overall Comparison for Azole Decomposition and Removal With UV/Persulfate

The UV‐activated PS processes were applied as a strategy for effective azole removal, and the removal efficiencies were compared and evaluated (Table S2). Overall, all four azole compounds showed rapid degradation in the UV/PS process, achieving > 97% removal within 30 min. Among them, PR showed the fastest degradation rate, with almost complete mineralization under both UV/PMS and UV/PDS conditions. TA also showed high reactivity and a slightly slower degradation rate than PR, but maintained a similarly strong mineralization efficiency in both processes. BTA and 5MBTA degraded rapidly at the molecular level; however, the mineralization efficiency was significantly lower in the UV/PMS process. When comparing the overall degradation and mineralization performances, the removal efficiency followed the order: PR > TA > 5MBTA > BTA. This was attributed to the rapid elimination of PR and TA resulting from their high susceptibility to ROS attack and electron transfer pathways, thereby achieving high mineralization efficiency. Consistent with these findings, evaluation of the process‐specific EE/O values (Text S1) further confirmed that the energy consumption for the removal of TA and PR was lower than that for the other compounds (Table S3). In contrast, BTA and 5MBTA require stronger oxidation conditions for complete mineralization owing to their more stable aromatic structures. Therefore, UV/PDS is more suitable for achieving deeper degradation of these compounds. In addition, consistent removal performance was observed under various water quality conditions, including the presence of background anions and natural organic matter. This indicated that both UV/PMS and UV/PDS exhibited strong matrix resistance and robustness under realistic environmental conditions. Collectively, these findings highlight the practical applicability and reliability of UV‐activated persulfate processes for treating azole‐contaminated wastewater, while demonstrating the importance of selecting suitable persulfate species to achieve complete mineralization according to the azole structure.

Conclusions

This study systematically compared the degradation and mineralization behaviors of four representative azoles, BTA, 5MBTA, TA, and PR, in UV/PMS and UV/PDS advanced oxidation processes. The experiments showed that all azoles were effectively degraded in the UV/PMS and UV/PDS processes, whereas their removal by a single oxidant or UV treatment alone was limited. PR and TR exhibited the fastest degradation rates and achieved near‐complete mineralization within 30 min in both processes, whereas BTA and 5‐MBTA showed incomplete mineralization due to significantly lower TOC removal in the UV/PMS system, despite rapid first‐order degradation. Scavenger and probe experiments identified the major ROS. TA and PR were primarily oxidized by 1O2, whereas BTA degradation proceeded via 1O2 oxidation and the electron transfer pathway. These mechanical insights explain the difference in TOC removal between the azoles and highlight the selective reactivity of ROS with the azole structure. pH played an important role in mineralization efficiency. In the UV/PDS system, both BTA and 5MBTA exhibited reduced TOC removal under alkaline conditions due to inhibited O2 •− production. In contrast, in the UV/PMS system, enhanced PMS photolysis and SO5 •− formation at high pH promoted O2 •− generation, resulting in increased TOC removal for 5MBTA only. Furthermore, the presence of common background ions and natural organic matter demonstrated that both the UV/PDS and UV/PMS processes were robust and adaptable in realistic water matrices, with minimal inhibitory effects. In addition, although detailed theoretical calculations were not performed in this study, the experimentally identified reactive oxygen species selectivity and electron‐transfer behavior provide substantial mechanistic evidence supporting the distinct degradation patterns observed among the four azoles. Future work incorporating density functional theory‐based descriptors will enable quantitative correlation between azole electronic structures and degradation reactivity, thereby further strengthening mechanistic understanding. Overall, the degradation efficiency followed the order PR > TA > 5MBTA > BTA, and complete mineralization of BTA and 5MBTA could only be achieved with UV/PDS. Collectively, these findings provide a strong basis for optimizing UV‐activated persulfate processes by clarifying structure–reactivity relationships and reactive oxygen species pathways governing azole transformation. The demonstrated robustness and flexibility of these systems highlight their potential for sustainable water treatment and continuous microcontaminant removal, contributing to the advancement of SDG 6 through improved water quality and resilient water management.
