A Metabolomics and Transcriptomics Resource for Identifying Candidate Genes in the Biosynthesis of Specialised Metabolites in Populus tremula
Authors: Sara M. Rydman, Jenna Lihavainen, Kathryn M. Robinson, Stefan Jansson, Benedicte R. Albrectsen, Nathaniel R. Street
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aims to identify candidate genes involved in the biosynthesis of salicinoid phenolic glycosides (SPGs), a group of specialised metabolites characteristic of the Salicaceae family. While the integration of multi‐omics data represents a powerful approach to link genes encoding enzymes and their regulatory factors to metabolite biosynthesis, suitable multi‐omics data resources are scarce. We present a comprehensive dataset comprising untargeted liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) and mRNA‐sequencing data from various organs of European aspen ( Populus tremula L.) and from genotypes that produce contrasting sets of SPGs. We present a reproducible pipeline for the analysis of the LC–MS data, including predicted annotation of potential novel SPGs. We demonstrate the utility of the resource by identifying candidate genes involved in the biosynthesis of SPGs with a cinnamoyl moiety. By integrating gene and metabolite differential analyses with a gene co‐expression network, we identified two HXXXD‐type acyltransferase genes and one UDP‐glucosyltransferase gene as candidates for future downstream characterisation. The combined gene expression and metabolomics resource is integrated into PlantGenIE.org to facilitate easy access and data mining. All raw data are available in public databases, and all data and results files are available at an associated Figshare repository.

Introduction

Plants are metabolic factories, with all species sharing a general (primary) metabolism, tied to essential functions and a specialised (secondary) metabolism that distinguishes taxonomic groups. Many specialised metabolites are involved in interactions with other species, for example, as defence or signalling compounds (Huang and Dudareva 2023), and many are used for nutrition, flavour, energy or pharmaceuticals due to their specific properties (Kallscheuer et al. 2019; Zhou et al. 2022; Kocaturk et al. 2023; Mewalal et al. 2017). Despite their importance to plant survival and function and the broad range of beneficial uses, most biosynthetic pathways producing specialised metabolites are not fully characterised (Delli‐Ponti et al. 2021; Muhich et al. 2022). One limitation to elucidating the biosynthetic pathways involved is the paucity of resources comprising metabolomics and gene expression data to enable analysis approaches to place candidate genes within pathways.

Ongoing technological advancements have not only made the generation of omics data economically viable but have also broadened the horizons of omics research. This enables data generation across different levels: genome, transcriptome, proteome, metabolome and epigenome (Krassowski et al. 2020). There is also a growing recognition of the importance of making omics data publicly available, with Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable (FAIR) principles providing guidelines (Wilkinson et al. 2016) to facilitate re‐analysis of existing data or to use such data to address new biological hypotheses. Although resources exist for exploring genomics and transcriptomics data, few resources include metabolomics data, despite the central role that metabolites play in determining organism function. The integration of metabolomics and transcriptomics data presents a challenge due to the lack of a one‐to‐one relationship between most metabolites and genes (Jendoubi 2021). Furthermore, the biosynthesis of many specialised metabolites is tightly regulated, necessitating the collection of appropriate samples (Delli‐Ponti et al. 2021). Compared to transcriptomics, reproducible analysis methods and public data repositories for metabolomics data are also less mature. Analytical challenges, such as instrument drift and metabolite saturation (Märtens et al. 2023), proprietary software and subjective decisions made during peak integration (Steuer et al. 2022), limit reproducibility. However, integrated analyses with other omics remain imperative to characterise biosynthetic pathways more comprehensively (Cavill et al. 2016).

Despite these challenges, several studies have generated metabolomics and transcriptomics data. For example, transcriptomics (Sundell et al. 2017), proteomics (Obudulu et al. 2016) and metabolomics (Abreu et al. 2020) data were generated from high spatial resolution samples of developing wood in Populus tremula and research on medicinal plants such as Ferula assafoetida (Amini et al. 2019) and Trapa bispinosa (Yin et al. 2022) have aimed to identify the genes behind bioactive substances. Integrated studies of plants subjected to stress have also provided insights into resistance mechanisms. For example, Zhang et al. (2023) demonstrated the important role of flavonoid metabolism in the resistance of rice to nematodes, and flavonoids have also been shown to be important for salt‐tolerance in Sophora alopecuroides (Zhu et al. 2021). Furthermore, recent advances in multi‐omic single‐cell data integration have given new insights into cell‐type‐specific regulation of metabolic pathways, including the monoterpene indole alkaloid (MIA) pathway in Catharanthus roseus (Li et al. 2023), flavonoid biosynthesis in Arabidopsis (Zhang et al. 2025), and somatic embryo development in cotton (Gossypium spp.; Ge et al. 2025). Notably, Kang et al. (2025) generated single‐cell LC–MS and RNA‐Seq data from the same plant cell, enabling direct gene‐metabolite correlations at single‐cell resolution. Unfortunately, the lack of integration in public resources limits the exploration of most multi‐omics datasets, severely limiting the potential to reuse these resources to make new discoveries.

Access to raw data is now the de facto standard, while data presentation in an explorable and immediately usable form for those without advanced bioinformatics skills and powerful computational resources is not. The power of explorative resources is exemplified by tools such as the electronic fluorescent pictograph (eFP) browser (Winter et al. 2007) at the Bio‐Analytic Resource (BAR; https://bar.utoronto.ca/; Toufighi et al. 2005), which offers gene expression visualisation and data analysis tools that have become an essential resource for the plant research community. The BAR was originally developed for Arabidopsis thaliana but has been extended to many other species. Additional explorative resources include the Plant Expression Omnibus (PEO; https://expression.plant.tools/) containing RNA‐Seq data for 103 species (Koh et al. 2023); Phytozome (https://phytozome‐next.jgi.doe.gov/), a genomics resource for 169 species (Goodstein et al. 2012); and the PLAZA platform (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/plaza/), facilitating comparative, evolutionary and functional analyses of plant genomes (Van Bel et al. 2022). There are also resources dedicated to forest tree species (Wegrzyn et al. 2019; https://treegenesdb.org/), including P. tremula (Sundell et al. 2015; https://plantgenie.org/).

To address the scarcity of integrated multi‐omics public resources enabling integrative approaches, we generated a resource—AspGen exAtlas (Aspen Genotype expression Atlas)—comprising transcriptomic (RNA‐Seq) and untargeted metabolomic (LC–MS) data from multiple organs and leaf development stages of nine P. tremula genotypes. Our aim was to identify candidate genes for the biosynthesis of salicinoid phenolic glycosides (SPGs), a class of specialised metabolites synthesised via the phenylpropanoid pathway (Zenk 1967; Ruuhola and Julkunen‐Tiitto 2003; Babst et al. 2010). The SPGs are a group of phenolic compounds characteristic of the Salicaceae, previously variably referred to as phenolic glycosides, salicinoids or salicylates. SPGs serve as a constitutive defence in Populus (Philippe and Bohlmann 2007), typically constituting 8%–16% of the leaf dry weight (Fellenberg et al. 2020), although levels up to 30% have been reported (Donaldson et al. 2006). There are more than 20 known SPG structures, built around a salicin molecule that can be adorned with various functional groups, for example, benzoyl, hydroxy‐6‐oxo‐2‐cyclohexene (HCH), acetyl (AC) and cinnamoyl (CN) moieties (Boeckler et al. 2011). Here, we utilised the natural variation among Swedish P. tremula genotypes that produces varying levels of distinct SPGs (Abreu et al. 2011; Keefover‐Ring, Ahnlund, et al. 2014). This variation has been previously used to define ‘chemotypes’ (Keefover‐Ring, Ahnlund, et al. 2014) based on the presence of AC, benzoyl and CN moieties on defence‐related SPG skeletons (Lindroth et al. 1987a; Nichols‐Orians et al. 1992; Reichardt et al. 1992; Binns et al. 1968; Palo 1984; Julkunen‐Tiitto 1986). While our motivation for developing the resource was to study SPG biosynthesis, the resource is equally applicable to the study of any metabolite represented in the LC–MS data.

While several studies have increased the understanding of SPG biosynthesis (Babst et al. 2010; Ruuhola and Julkunen‐Tiitto 2003; Zenk 1967), most of the identified SPGs have not yet been associated with enzymes responsible for their biosynthesis. Chedgy et al. (2015) functionally characterised two BAHD‐type acyltransferases in Populus trichocarpa (salicyl alcohol O‐benzoyltransferase [SABT] and benzyl alcohol O‐benzoyltransferase [BEBT]), which were hypothesised to catalyse the formation of two potential intermediates in the SPG pathway. Recent studies exploring the genetic architecture of SPGs have identified several candidate genes (Riehl et al. 2023; Barker et al. 2019; Bresadola et al. 2019). However, to date, only two genes within the SPG pathway have been functionally validated both in vitro and in vivo: a UDP‐glucose‐dependent glycosyltransferase (UGT71L1; Fellenberg et al. 2020; Gordon et al. 2022) and a sulfotransferase (SOT1; Lackus et al. 2020).

Materials and Methods

Plant Material and Experimental Design

Clonal replicates from the Swedish Aspen (SwAsp) collection (P. tremula; Luquez et al. 2008) were used in this study. We selected nine genotypes based on previously reported SPG chemotypes in mature, pre‐formed leaves of the SwAsp collection (Keefover‐Ring, Ahnlund, et al. 2014; Abreu et al. 2011), defined by the high abundance of AC‐SPGs (genotypes 47, 56 and 60) or CN‐SPGs (genotypes 14, 34 and 76) and low abundance of AC and CN SPGs (AC−/CN−; genotypes 41, 45 and 51). The AC−/CN− chemotype corresponds to the TL (tremuloides‐like) chemotype in Keefover‐Ring, Ahnlund, et al. (2014). The trees were propagated from tissue culture (18/6 h light and 22°C/18°C day/night). On 1 October 2020, the plants were transferred to soil (K‐jord, Hasselfors Garden, Sweden) in 1 L pots and maintained in greenhouse conditions under day/night 18/6 h (LED light FL300 Sunlight, Senmatic), 20°C/15°C and 60% relative humidity and 80% light intensity. The plants were fertilised weekly with 10 mL L−1 Rika‐S (610411; SW Horto AB).

Sampling was conducted on 14 and 15 December 2020 from 12:30 to 17:30, when the plants were 10 weeks old. Two biological replicate plants per genotype were sampled on each day (n = 4), except genotype 76, which had only three plants. Seven sample types (shoot tips, leaves of three developmental stages, bark, axillary buds and fine roots) were collected from each plant using scissors and scalpels. The leaf developmental ages comprised young leaf (YL; 3rd leaf from the top), expanding leaf (EL; 5th–6th leaf) and older mature leaf (ML; 12th–13th leaf). The shoot tip sample contained the apical meristem and the youngest emerging leaves. Stem pieces were collected by sampling the internode between the ML (12th–13th leaf) and the following leaf (13th–14th leaf). Bark (including periderm, cortex, phloem and cambium) was then sampled by peeling the outer green layer from the stem piece, pre‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. Axillary buds were collected from the top shoot (approximately 25–30 buds). Young fine roots were collected by selecting five to six white lateral roots. The roots were gently cleaned with water and dried with lint‐free tissues. All samples were collected in paper bags and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Shoot tips, leaf and root samples were coarsely crushed in the paper bags. All samples were then divided into two batches (i.e., employing a split sample study design), one for RNA‐Seq and the other for LC–MS analysis. The samples for RNA‐Seq were stored at −80°C, while the samples for LC–MS were freeze‐dried.

Metabolite Extraction

Metabolites were extracted from four biological replicates of each genotype, except genotype 76, which had three replicates. Freeze‐dried plant material (approx. 3 mg) was extracted with 1 mL of cold extraction buffer (methanol:chloroform:water = 3:1:1) containing a mix of labelled internal standards used for system monitoring and quality control (QC) purposes. The internal standards are listed in Table S1. Samples were extracted in a bead mill for 3 min (30 Hz, Retsch GmHB) and centrifuged for 10 min at 4°C at 18,000 × g. The upper, aqueous phase was recovered, and an aliquot (100 μL) was dried in a vacuum and stored at −80°C prior to LC–MS analysis. In addition, QC samples were produced by combining aliquots from each genotype per sample type (organ QC) and by combining aliquots of organ QCs (master QC). QC samples were included in each LC–MS analysis batch.

UPLC‐ESI‐MS/MS Analysis

Before injection, the samples were reconstituted with 10 μL of methanol and 10 μL of water. The analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC system and Agilent 6546 Q‐TOF mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies). Chromatographic separation was performed with an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 μm particle size) combined with a VanGuard precolumn (2.1 mm × 5 mm, 1.8 μm Waters Corporation). The column temperature was maintained at 40°C, and the injection volume was 0.75 μL in negative ion mode and 1 μL in positive ion mode.

The mobile phases were water (A) and 75:25 acetonitrile:isopropanol (B), both containing 0.1% formic acid. Metabolites were eluted with the following linear gradient with a flow rate of 500 μL min−1: from 0.1% to 10% B over 2 min; to 99% B over the next 5 min; held at 99% B for 2 min; after which the mobile phase returned to initial conditions of 0.1% B for 0.3 min and the flow rate was increased to 800 μL min−1 for 0.5 min. These conditions were held for 0.9 min before returning to a flow rate of 500 μL min−1 for 0.1 min in preparation for the next injection.

Electrospray ionisation (ESI) was used in positive and negative modes. To uphold mass accuracy, reference ions purine (4 μM) and HP‐0921 (Hezakis (1H, 1H, 3H‐tetrafluoropropoxy) phosphazine; 1 mM) were introduced into the MS at a flow rate of 50 mL min−1. The respective m/z values for these ions were m/z 121.05 (ESI+) and m/z 119.03632 (ESI−) for purine, and m/z 922.0098 (ESI+) and m/z 966.000725 (ESI−) for HP‐0921.

Mass spectra were acquired in centroid mode across the m/z range of 70–1700, with an acquisition rate of 4 scans s−1. Drying gas temperature was 150°C with a flow of 8 L min−1; nebuliser pressure was set at 35 psig; sheath gas temperature was set at 35°C with a flow of 11 L min−1. Capillary voltage remained at 4000 V for both positive and negative mode, and the nozzle voltage was set to 300 V. The fragmentor was set to 120 V, the skimmer to 65, and the OCT 1 RF Vpp to 750 V. The QC samples (both master and organ‐specific) were analysed over multiple collision energies (10, 20 and 40 V) to obtain MS/MS spectra. Moreover, the QC master sample was injected at the beginning of the LC–MS run to stabilise the instrument and subsequently at regular intervals (every ~23 study samples).

In this study, we used the data collected in negative ion mode as the formic acid adduct [M + HCOO]− and molecular ions [M − H]− of SPGs were detected with higher intensity than in positive ion mode. Raw data files of positive ion mode are available at the Figshare repository, offering a complementary data set for further exploration.

UPLC‐ESI/MS/MS Data Preprocessing and Untargeted Metabolomics Analysis

Raw data files (MassHunter .d) were converted to .mzML using MSConvert (ProteoWizard; Chambers et al. 2012). The .mzML files were then processed in R using XCMS version 3.19.0 (Smith et al. 2006). Briefly, CentWave was used for feature detection. For fine‐tuning the CentWave parameters in XCMS, Autotuner (McLean and Kujawinski 2020) was employed. Data were further refined by removing low‐intensity peaks (intensity below 3000), since these are unlikely to be validated by MS/MS. Peak matching (or correspondence) was subsequently performed to group peaks across all samples, presumably originating from the same metabolite. Sample type was used for peak matching, with peaks present in at least two samples and/or within 60% of a sample group to be considered a feature. Gap filling was used to ensure that missing values (NA) are only reported if no detectable signal was obtained from the original sample data. The XCMS peak matching and gap filling generated 32,039 features in negative ion mode for all samples.

Feature clustering was performed using RAMClustR (Broeckling et al. 2014) to merge features (such as fragments, adducts and isotopes) originating from the same metabolite. From 32,039 features, RAMClustR generated 4125 feature clusters (containing more than one feature) and 3212 singletons (i.e., ungrouped features). Singletons and feature clusters collected before 30 s and after 540 s were removed from the data, giving a total of 3620 feature clusters (hereafter referred to as metabolites), each given an individual RC‐ID (i.e., RamCluster ID). RC‐ID intensities were generated as described in Broeckling et al. (2014) by calculating the weighted mean, using the sum of feature area across samples as a weight. The spectra intensities from RAMClustR were then normalised with the sample dry weight (mg). The intensities were subsequently normalised using variance stabilising normalisation (vsn) using MetaboDiff v.0.9.5 (Mock et al. 2018) in R. All scripts and parameters used to create the pre‐processed negative ion mode data are available at https://github.com/sarawestman/exAtlas.

Metabolite Annotation

To validate SPGs previously described by Abreu et al. (2011), Keefover‐Ring, Ahnlund, et al. (2014) and Keefover‐Ring, Carlsson, and Albrectsen (2014), we extracted the MS/MS of reported compounds (based on m/z and retention time) from the QC samples. SPGs with validated spectra were included in downstream analyses (Table S2).

Sirius version 5.6.3 (Dührkop et al. 2019) was used to analyse MS/MS data. The raw data MS/MS file (MassHunter .d) of a master QC sample was converted to .mgf using MSConvert (ProteoWizard; Chambers et al. 2012) and analysed using Sirius, encompassing: (1) identifying the molecular formula of measured ions using fragmentation trees and isotope patterns, enforcing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen as elements (Böcker and Dührkop 2016), and (2) predicting molecular fingerprint using CSI:FingerID (Dührkop et al. 2015). CANOPUS was used to obtain compound class prediction (Dührkop et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2021). Metabolites with a Natural Product Classes (NPC) superclass probability above 0.8 were classified as having an associated hypothetical annotation. Further manual screening of the MS/MS spectra was not conducted in this study. Tentatively annotated (MS/MS and high‐resolution mass) phenylpropanoids and proanthocyanidins from P. tremula leaves are listed in Table S2.

RNA Isolation

RNA was isolated from three of the four available biological replicates (randomly selected) of all genotypes (except genotype 76, where only two replicates were available). Frozen samples, approx. 100 mg fresh weight, were transferred to 2 mL Eppendorf tubes containing three pre‐cooled 3 mm tungsten carbide beads. The samples were then ground at 30 Hz for 30 s using a Retsch MM 400 Mixer Mill, frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground for an additional 30 s.

To optimise the RNA yield, different extraction procedures were used, tailored to the plant material being processed. RNeasy Mini Kit (74104; Qiagen) was used for RNA extraction from leaves following the manufacturer's instructions for plants. Modifications were made for non‐leaf materials using a modified version of the Chang et al. (1993) protocol. In short, 2% PVP‐40 was added to 10 mL of extraction buffer placed in a heating block. When completely dissolved, 20 μL of 2‐Mercaptoethanol was added to the buffer. Subsequently, 700 μL buffer was added to each sample, followed by the addition of 700 μL of chloroform:IAA (24:1). The tubes were inverted ca 30 times and centrifuged (9600 × g, 10 min). The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and the process was repeated, starting from the addition of 700 μL chloroform:IAA (24:1). The supernatant was then transferred to a new tube, mixed with 1.4 mL ethanol (100%) and kept at 4°C for 1 h. The samples were then centrifuged at 20,800 × g (20 min, at 4°C). Pellets were dissolved in 350 μL RLT buffer (RNeasy Mini Kit), vortexed and diluted with 350 μL ethanol (70%). After vortexing, the solution was transferred to a RNeasy spin column (RNeasy Mini Kit) and processed according to the RNeasy manufacturer's protocol. All samples were treated with RNase‐Free DNase Set (79254; Qiagen) as described by the manufacturer. RNA integrity and purity from the two extraction procedures were assessed using a 2100 BioAnalyzer (Plant RNA Nano) and NanoDrop.

RNA Sequencing and Analysis

Library construction, quality assessment and RNA sequencing were performed by Novogene. Briefly, messenger RNA was purified from total RNA using poly‐T oligo‐attached magnetic beads. After fragmentation, the first strand cDNA was synthesised using random hexamer primers, and the second strand cDNA was synthesised using dUTP instead of dTTP. The directional library was ready after end repair, A‐tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, USER enzyme digestion, amplification and purification. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the Novogene NGS Stranded RNA Library Prep Set (PT044). Libraries were checked using a Qubit and real‐time PCR for quantification and Bioanalyzer for size distribution detection. Quantified libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, according to effective library concentration and data amount. In total, 3 Gb of 2 × 150 bp paired‐end reads were generated. Raw read counts per sample ranged between 4,460,822 and 12,668,396, with a median of 8,863,370.

The raw read FASTQ files underwent RNA‐Seq preprocessing through version 0.4.2 of the pipeline available at https://github.com/UPSCb/UPSCb‐common. The preprocessing involved removing rRNA with SortMeRna v.4.3.4 (Kopylova et al. 2012) and subsequent trimming of adapters using Trimmomatic v.0.39 (Bolger et al. 2014). Adapter sequences were removed using TruSeq3‐PE.fa (https://github.com/timflutre/trimmomatic/blob/master/adapters/TruSeq3‐PE.fa), applying a sliding window of five bases, ensuring a minimum quality score of 30 and retaining sequences with a minimal length of 50 bases. Following this, reads were mapped to the P. tremula v.2 transcriptome (Robinson et al. 2024) and quantified using Salmon v.1.8.0 (Patro et al. 2017). Each step underwent QC assessment using FastQC v.0.11.9 (Andrews 2010). Unless otherwise specified, default parameter settings were used for each tool. The expression was aggregated to the gene level using the tximport package in R (Soneson et al. 2015) and subsequently normalised via variance stabilising transformation (VST), available in the R package DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014).

Scripts and parameters for preparing the data can be found at https://github.com/sarawestman/exAtlas/tree/main/src/RNAseq.

We obtained functional gene annotations and best DIAMOND hits (highest sequence similarity matches) to A. thaliana from the PlantGenIE resource (Sundell et al. 2015).

Statistical Analyses of RNA‐Seq and LC–MS Data

Differential Expression/Abundance Analysis

Differential expression analysis of genes was performed with DESeq2 v.1.42.0 (Love et al. 2014). Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a log fold change cut‐off of 0.5 and an adjusted p value (false discovery rate, FDR) threshold of 0.001.

To identify metabolites differentially abundant among SPG chemotypes, a differential abundance analysis on SPG chemotype groups was performed using MetaboDiff v.0.9.5 (Mock et al. 2018), employing a difference in mean (dm, representing the difference of the group means obtained from the Student's t‐test), a cut‐off of 0.5 and an adjusted p value threshold set at 0.001. Separate tests were performed for each sample type, with chemotype used as the design formula in both DESeq2 and MetaboDiff (~Chemotype).

The MS/MS spectra of compounds identified from the differential abundance test were extracted for further tentative annotation. Compounds were assessed based on the presence of mass fragments matching known SPGs, including salicyl alcohol (SA; C7H8O2, m/z 124.0524), HCH (C7H8O4, m/z 156.0423), CN (C9H8O2, m/z 148.0524), benzoic acid (BA; C7H6O2, m/z 122.0368) and AC (C2H3O2, m/z 59.0133) groups. The annotation was thus based on high‐resolution mass and MS/MS. Chemical formulae were calculated using https://www.chemcalc.org/mf‐finder (Patiny and Borel 2013), with a range setting of C0‐100, H0‐200 and O0‐50 and 5 ppm as a threshold.

We also performed differential expression analysis of genes and differential abundance analysis of metabolites across various sample types to identify entities specific to each sample type. This involved performing pairwise comparisons of genes and metabolites from each sample type against all other sample types using MetaboDiff and DESeq2, employing the aforementioned cut‐off criteria and a design formula specifying the sample type (e.g., ~ isRoot, which could be either Yes or No). To pinpoint genes and metabolites specific to each sample type, we excluded those differentially expressed/abundant in at least two sample types. Entities were characterised based on their direction of expression, meaning that a gene or metabolite specific to one sample type could be upregulated in one type and downregulated in another (see Figure S1 for further clarification).

Gene Co‐Expression Network Analysis

A gene co‐expression network was generated using the R package weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA; Langfelder and Horvath 2008). Following the instructions from a WGCNA tutorial (see https://fuzzyatelin.github.io/bioanth‐stats/module‐F21‐Group1/module‐F21‐Group1.html#Module_Construction), separate networks were created for the shoot tip and each leaf stage. In short, outlier samples and genes were identified and removed. Based on pairwise similarity measures between genes, an unsigned weighted network adjacency matrix was generated, using a soft threshold of 4 for the shoot tip, 5 for YL and EL and 6 for ML. Modules were identified by first transforming the adjacency network into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM) dissimilarity matrix, followed by hierarchical clustering analysis (with a minimum cluster size set to 30). The standardised gene expression profile for each module was identified as the Module Eigengene. Modules with similar expression profiles (Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.75) were merged. This process resulted in the identification of 58, 53, 53 and 46 modules for the shoot tip, YL, EL and ML, respectively. The eigengene patterns of each module are available at the Figshare data repository. Module eigengene expression profiles were correlated with SPG abundance using Pearson correlation to identify modules potentially associated with SPG biosynthesis.

Functional Enrichment Analysis of RNA‐Seq Data

Gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis was conducted on WGCNA modules and significantly DEGs using topGO v.2.54.0 in R (Alexa and Rahnenfuhrer 2023). Significantly enriched GO terms (FDR < 0.05) were subsequently analysed using rrvgo v.1.14.0 in R (Sayols 2023), grouping GO sets considering their semantic similarity, thereby simplifying the interpretation of the results. The GO terms representing biological processes for individual WGCNA modules were visualised with a bubble plot. For DEGs, parental GO terms were displayed in a dot plot using the ggplot2 package version 3.5.2 in R.

Resource Overview

We generated RNA‐Seq transcriptomics and untargeted LC–MS metabolomics data from multiple sample types from a set of nine aspen genotypes known to produce contrasting sets of SPGs. The raw RNA‐Seq data are deposited at the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) as accession PRJEB73507, and the raw metabolomics data are deposited in a Figshare repository (DOI: 10.17044/scilifelab.27336156). All processed and normalised data used to perform the statistical analyses presented here, including the co‐expression network, are available at the Figshare resource. The Figshare resource includes .RData files to allow exploration of the data and results. For exploration and visualisation, the metabolite and gene expression data have been integrated into the expression visualisation tools at the PlantGenIE.org resource. All analysis scripts, including the reproducible and automated LC–MS preprocessing pipeline, are available from the Git repository https://github.com/sarawestman/exAtlas.

Results and Discussion

Software and pipelines for preprocessing metabolomic data are continuously being developed. Many of the new tools are open‐source and are being developed to be computationally efficient, fast and to comply with the FAIR principles (Misra 2021). While there are now well‐established tools for processing and analysing RNA‐Seq data, this is not the case for metabolomics data. Detailed and transparent documentation of data handling steps, especially of metabolite preprocessing parameters, is of importance to allow reproducibility since the selection of settings can have a major impact on the outcome (Hohrenk et al. 2020). In this paper, we implemented an R‐based workflow of open‐source tools to preprocess the LC–MS data. The workflow includes XCMS for peak integration and peak correspondence (Smith et al. 2006), AutoTuner for XCMS dataset parameter optimisation (McLean and Kujawinski 2020), and RAMClustR for feature clustering (Broeckling et al. 2014). The scripts and workflow are available for reuse.

Organs and Leaf Developmental Stages Have Distinct Transcriptome and Metabolome Compositions

To provide an overview of variation among organs and genotypes, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) of the transcriptome and metabolome separately, which revealed similar distinct clustering patterns across sample types in both datasets (Figure 1). Notably, photosynthesising samples exhibited a sequential arrangement along principal component (PC) 2 in both the transcriptome (Figure 1a) and metabolome (Figure 1b), in agreement with their developmental stages. Leaves of the sampled developmental stages exhibited greater differentiation at the transcriptome level compared to the metabolome, with clear separation of the shoot tip and youngest leaf age from that of later leaf development stages. The opposite pattern was observed for bark and axillary buds (hereafter referred to as buds), which were more distinct from each other based on their metabolomes. For all samples, the differences in sample types were far greater than any variation among genotypes, as indicated by the tight clustering of samples from all genotypes for a given sample type.

Organ diversity exceeds genotype variation in aspen. Principal component analysis (PCA; first two axes) of (a) the transcriptome, and (b) the metabolome from seven sample types, originating from nine aspen genotypes (three biological replicates per genotype, except genotype 76 with two biological replicates). Each sample type is represented by its own colour and label. The transcriptome constitutes normalised gene counts (variance stabilised transformed [VST], n = 33,376 genes). The metabolome was generated from liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC–MS) profiling in negative ion mode (variance stabilising normalisation [VSN], n = 3620 metabolites).

To investigate transcriptome and metabolome differences among sample types, as indicated by their separation in the PCA, we identified DEGs and differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs). Roots and buds had the highest number of sample‐specific genes with higher or lower expression (referred to as up‐ and downregulated genes; Table 1). We used untargeted metabolomics to capture a wide range of metabolites in the samples, avoiding determination biases associated with targeted analyses. Additionally, SPG metabolism was specifically targeted to support the use of the RNA‐Seq data for the investigation of unknown enzymes potentially contributing to SPG biosynthesis. This approach resulted in a limited number of identified (annotated) DAMs, as shown in Table 1. The annotations were made using in‐house libraries and the Sirius software, which was used to generate hypothetical annotations. In contrast, a large portion of the DEGs was associated with at least one GO term, suggesting the genes were involved in a particular function or pathway (Table 1). We performed enrichment tests of GO terms using the set of sample‐specific DEGs to identify biological processes specific to each sample type. As the gene expression and metabolite data originated from the same sample, and as both data types showed similar patterns of among‐sample similarity (Figure 1), we considered that validation using gene expression is a useful proxy for validating that the metabolite results will reflect meaningful biological differences among samples. Enrichment analysis for biological processes was conducted on sample‐specific up‐ and downregulated genes for each sample type, respectively (Figure 2 and Table S3). These results reflected known biological differences among the samples. For example, the upregulated genes in the shoot tip were enriched in genes involved in cell cycle and development, and genes upregulated in buds were involved in RNA biosynthetic processes and developmental processes. Bark samples, which included multiple cell types, were enriched in genes involved in plant‐type cell wall organisation or biogenesis, cell differentiation and polysaccharide biosynthetic process. GO term enrichments of the leaf samples were indicative of the development in foliar gene activities: YL were enriched for processes related to RNA modification, plastid organisation and pigment metabolic processes; EL for processes related to photosynthesis, indicating a developmental shift from sink to source; and ML for protein phosphorylation and processes related to interspecies interaction between organisms (Figure 2). Roots were represented by upregulated genes involved in negative regulation of catalytic activity, response to chemical and transport (Figure 2). Hence, the dataset captured known biological differences among the samples and ontological differences during leaf development.

A Multi‐Omics Approach for Linking Genes to Metabolites

Many studies have shown that leaves of Populus and Salix are particularly rich in SPGs, with the highest concentration often found in younger leaves (Lindroth et al. 1987b; Bingaman and Hart 1993; Kleiner et al. 1999; Ruuhola and Julkunen‐Tiitto 2000; Gould et al. 2007; Yepes‐Vivas et al. 2025). Consistent with these findings, shoot tip and YL in our dataset also exhibited the highest total SPG abundance among all sample types analysed (Table S4). Another layer of variability in this dataset stems from the SPG chemotype categorisation introduced by Abreu et al. (2011) and Keefover‐Ring, Ahnlund, et al. (2014). As each genotype is assigned a specific SPG chemotype, exploiting the differences between chemotypes enables the identification of genes and metabolites with distinct expression/abundance, potentially linking DEGs to DAMs. The chemotype categorisations by Abreu et al. (2011) and Keefover‐Ring, Ahnlund, et al. (2014) have been based on leaf material collected from SwAsp trees growing in the greenhouse and/or field. As such, we aim to utilise the ontological changes in SPG composition and abundance within the leaf samples (i.e., shoot tip, YL, EL and ML) to link genes with SPG biosynthesis in this study.

To validate the chemotype classifications reported by Keefover‐Ring, Ahnlund, et al. (2014) for the nine genotypes and different sample types, we generated a 3‐dimensional Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualisation of the metabolome data derived from shoot tip and leaf samples. Most genotypes clustered according to the previously reported chemotypes, as indicated by the clustering of samples coloured according to the previously defined chemotype classes of high CN, high AC and low acetyl/cinnamoyl (AC−/CN−, called TL [tremuloides‐like] in Keefover‐Ring, Ahnlund, et al. 2014) moieties (Figure S2a). However, in the current study, genotype 76 was grouped with the AC−/CN− chemotype samples and was reclassified as AC−/CN−. Notably, the 3D UMAP projection exhibited two prominent clusters, segregating shoot tips and YL from EL and ML (Figure S2b), suggesting distinct ontological shifts in metabolome composition.

A PCA plot, colouring samples based on either sample type or chemotype, revealed that sample type was the primary source of variation contributing to PC1 (Figure 3a), while PC2 and PC3 distinguished the CN chemotype from the AC and AC−/CN− chemotypes (Figures 3b and S3a, including genotype labels). Additionally, PC4 and PC5 differentiated the AC from CN and AC−/CN− chemotypes (Figure S3b). Given the distinct separation of CN from the remaining chemotypes, we focused on the CN chemotype tested against AC−/CN−.

Identifying Novel Putative SPG Metabolites

Identifying unknown/unidentified metabolites with differential abundance between SPG chemotypes can potentially uncover previously undiscovered SPGs (Gertsman and Barshop 2018). However, as the location of SPG biosynthesis remains unresolved, and as gene expression varied between leaf ages (Figure 1a), we performed differential expression analysis of the metabolome and transcriptome between chemotypes (CN compared to AC−/CN−) separately for each sampled leaf development stage.

The highest number of DAMs (FDR < 0.001 and dm > 0.5) between chemotypes was identified in YL (n = 123 DAMs) and the lowest in ML (n = 63 DAMs; Table S5), indicating that differences between the chemotypes were most distinct in younger leaf developmental stages, further emphasising the need to consider ontology when sampling for metabolite analyses. Across all developmental stages, we identified 81 DAMs with higher and 88 with lower abundance in the CN chemotype (Figure 3c). Among these, seven were known SPGs: acetyl‐cinnamoyl‐salicortin (metabolite ID RC1038), cinnamoyl‐salicortin (RC77 and RC1037), HCH‐cinnamoyl‐salicortin (RC465), acetyl‐tremulacin (RC212), HCH‐tremulacin (RC84) and tremuloidin (RC185). HCH‐cinnamoyl‐salicortin (RC465) had the largest influence on chemotype separation in the PCA, followed by cinnamoyl‐salicortin (RC77) and acetyl‐cinnamoyl‐salicortin (RC1038), in addition to many unknown metabolites.

To determine whether the unknown metabolites that contributed to chemotype separation could be unannotated SPGs, we inspected their MS/MS spectra. Mass fragments matching known SPGs, such as SA, HCH, CN, BA and AC groups were present in 13 of these putative SPGs with higher abundance (comparing CN and AC−/CN−) and 14 SPGs with lower abundance (Table S2). There was a strong correlation between SPGs carrying the same type of moiety, including the candidate SPGs (Figure S4, showing the shoot tip samples). In addition, all CN‐SPGs were abundant in CN, whereas most of the benzoyl‐SPGs were found in lower concentrations. Determining the molecular structures of these candidate SPGs was beyond the scope of this study, but four potential isomers of tremulacin (metabolite ID RC222, RC413, RC1024 and RC2221), one salicortin (RC1607) and one cinnamoyl‐salicortin (RC2370) were identified.

Comparing the transcriptomes of CN and AC−/CN− chemotypes, shoot tip samples had the highest and EL the lowest number of DEGs (Table S5), with 11 DEGs common for all leaf stages (Table S6 and Figure S5). Most of the 11 common DEGs lacked a description or Pfam annotation, but included a disease resistance gene (Potra2n19c34221), an ankyrin repeat (Potra2n81s34522) and an LRR‐repeat protein (Potra2n14c27816). Ankyrin repeats and LRR‐repeat proteins have previously been linked to various biological processes, including resistance against pathogens (Sharma and Pandey 2016), suggesting a potential link between disease resistance and chemotype.

Gene Expression Network Analysis Identifies Sets of Genes Linked to SPG Composition

Gene co‐expression network analysis was performed separately for each developmental leaf stage to identify gene modules (i.e., modules/clusters/communities), with each module assigned a colour‐coded identifier. Networks were constructed independently to account for the fact that leaf developmental progression, rather than chemotype, contributed most to the variance in the data (Figures 3 and S2) and to ensure that expression variation among genotypes was the primary contributor to co‐expression patterns. We then correlated the eigengene value of each module to the abundance of known and candidate SPGs, here using the shoot tip network as an example (Figure 4). To identify network modules containing genes potentially involved in the biosynthesis of CN‐SPGs, we focused on the module showing the most statistically significant correlation with HCH‐CN‐salicortin (the single most influential CN‐metabolite in Figure 3c) and the module containing the highest proportion of genes classified as differentially expressed between the CN and AC−/CN− chemotypes. In each leaf stage network, the module with the most significant correlation with HCH‐CN‐salicortin also displayed the highest proportion of DEGs for the CN chemotype (Figure 5a). Subsequently, GO enrichment analysis was performed on each of these ‘top modules’ to explore potential connections to phenylpropanoid biosynthesis or secondary metabolism. Notably, only the ‘salmon’ gene module in the shoot tip network showed significant enrichment in biological processes, whose categories include cell wall development, phenylpropanoid metabolic processes and polysaccharide biosynthesis (Figure 5b).

Closer examination of the Pfam annotations and gene descriptions of the upregulated DEGs in the salmon‐module (comprising 112 out of 469 genes) supports the observed GO enrichment, highlighting several cell wall‐related genes as well as phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthetic genes and regulators (Table S7). Among the cell wall‐related genes, we identified three annotated as cellulose synthases (CESAs; Potra2n6c13749, Potra2n4c8952 and Potra2n2c4078) and one COBRA‐like protein (Potra2n15c28689). CESAs produce cellulose, a polysaccharide vital for the load‐bearing properties of the plant cell wall (McFarlane et al. 2014). In turn, the deposition and organisation of cellulose are mediated by COBRA (and COBRA‐like) proteins (Xue et al. 2024).

Key enzymes in the central phenylpropanoid pathway were also identified, specifically Phenylalanine ammonia‐lyase (PAL; Potra2n10c20411) and cinnamate‐4 hydroxylase (C4H; Potra2n13c24959; Table S7). PAL initiates the pathway by deaminating phenylalanine to produce cinnamic acid, which is hydroxylated to p‐coumaric acid by C4H. Subsequently, 4‐coumaroyl CoA ligase (4CL) converts p‐coumaric acid into p‐coumaroyl‐CoA, which is fed into various phenylpropanoid branches, including flavonoids, lignin and phenolic acid biosynthesis (Deng and Lu 2017). The salmon‐module included several lignin‐related genes, such as cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD; Potra2n9c19275), ferulic acid 5‐hydroxylases (F5H; Potra2n5c11765 and Potra2n7c16495), O‐methyltransferases (OMTs; Potra2n12c23766, Potra2n13c25235 and Potra2n1c2649) and laccases (Potra2n11c22797, Potra2n16c30273 and Potra2n10c20714). The enzymatic actions of CAD, F5H, Caffeic acid OMT (COMT) and Caffeoyl‐CoA OMT (CCoAOMT) are required to synthesise monolignols, which can be further polymerised into lignin by laccases and peroxidases (Deng and Lu 2017).

Additionally, several transcription factors were identified among the upregulated genes in the salmon‐module: one WRKY (Potra2n2c5891), two NAC proteins (Potra2n11c22498 and Potra2n16c29948) and four v‐myb myeloblastosis viral oncogene homologue (MYB) domain proteins (Potra2n15c28118, Potra2n15c28915, Potra2n1c829 and Potra2n3c7305; Table S7). WRKYs are among the largest transcription factor families, influencing a myriad of plant processes (Rushton et al. 2010), with documented effects on phenylpropanoids, terpenes and alkaloids (Schluttenhofer and Yuan 2015). NACs, a plant‐specific transcription factor family, have been associated with disease resistance, as well as plant growth and development (Zheng et al. 2025). A number of NACs and MYBs have been proposed to be part of a transcription factor network that regulates secondary wall biosynthesis (Zhong and Ye 2007). All upregulated MYBs in the salmon‐module have Arabidopsis homologues (MYB42, MYB52 and MYB103). Their expression is downregulated in a secondary cell wall NAC RNA interference (RNAi) mutant (SDN1/NST1), and, in the case of MYB103, is directly targeted by SDN1 (Zhong et al. 2008), suggesting a potential involvement in secondary wall synthesis. As one of the largest plant transcription factor families, MYBs regulate a vast number of plant processes (Wu et al. 2024) and are considered key regulators in the phenylpropanoid pathway (Liu et al. 2015). Furthermore, overexpression of a MYB regulating tannin production led to a reduction of SPG abundance (Mellway et al. 2009), which subsequently led to the identification of two genes hypothesised to synthesise precursors of SPG moieties (Chedgy et al. 2015).

Each module in Figure 5a was subsequently examined for genes previously reported in the recent ‘SPG literature’: Tsai et al. (2006), Touminen et al. (2011), Chedgy et al. (2015), Woolbright et al. (2018), Bresadola et al. (2019), Fellenberg et al. (2020), Lackus et al. (2020), Gordon et al. (2022), Riehl et al. (2023) and Saint‐Vincent et al. (2023). In cases where the study system was not P. tremula, the most similar gene in terms of primary sequence was identified using the best DIAMOND hit provided at PlantGenIE.org. If the gene model reported in the literature was not available at PlantGenIE.org, we performed a BLASTp search using the P. tremula genome version 2.2 as a template (Table S8). Notably, the ‘salmon’ module contained eight genes previously associated with the phenylpropanoid pathway in Tsai et al. (2006): PAL (Potra2n10c20411), CCoAOMT (Potra2n9c19246), 4‐Coumarate‐CoA ligase (4CL; Potra2n1c307 and Potra2n3c6783), F5H (Potra2n5c11765), COMT (Potra2n12c23766), Flavonoid O‐methyltransferase (FOMT; Potra2n13c25235) and trans‐Cinnamate 4‐hydroxylase (C4H; Potra2n13c24959; Table S7). One of the 4CL genes, Potra2n3c6783, has also been reported by Gordon et al. (2022). Additionally, three acyltransferases (Potra2n8c18294, Potra2n10c20569 and Potra2n14c26512) identified by Touminen et al. (2011) and two other ‘salmon’ module genes with the following Arabidopsis descriptions were previously highlighted by Riehl et al. (2023): trichome birefringence‐like 33 (Potra2n10c20801) and Leucine‐rich repeat protein kinase family protein (Potra2n13c24940; Table S7). Taken together, the ‘salmon’ module of the shoot tip represents a strong candidate for uncovering genes involved in the SPG pathway.

None of the previously functionally validated SPG genes (Chedgy et al. 2015; Fellenberg et al. 2020; Lackus et al. 2020; Gordon et al. 2022) were present in the salmon‐module. However, salmon‐module genes belonging to the same enzyme families, especially acyltransferases and glycosyltransferases, represent attractive SPG candidates. Acyltransferases play an important role in diversifying phenylpropanoids by transferring acyl groups from an activated donor to an acceptor molecule, with phenylpropanoids functioning as both donors and acceptors. Two families of acyltransferases known to utilise phenolic metabolites are BAHD‐acyltransferases and SCPL‐acyltransferases (Bontpart et al. 2015). BAHD members have multiple conserved domains, including the HXXXDG domain, common for transferases utilising coenzyme A thioesters as acyl donors. The acyl‐CoA donors include AC, benzoyl, malonyl and hydroxy‐CN (D'Auria 2006). Glycosyltransferases facilitate the transfer of sugars between molecules, potentially altering their solubility, toxicity, activity, stability and compartmentalisation. These acceptor molecules encompass sugars, proteins, lipids and phenylpropanoids (Le Roy et al. 2016). Upon examining the annotation information for upregulated DEGs within the ‘salmon’ module of the shoot tip network, we identified six glycosyltransferases, one UDP‐glycosyltransferase, one acyltransferase and three other transferases (Table 2). Further insights from the best DIAMOND A. thaliana hits suggest that these three additional transferase genes are probably HXXXD‐type acyltransferases (Table S7), representing compelling SPG candidates.

The most significantly DEG in the ‘salmon’ module of the shoot tip network (Potra2n1c3102) was annotated as a UDP‐N‐acetylglucosamine transferase subunit ALG14‐like protein (Table 2), a nucleotide sugar transferase involved in N‐glycosylation in eukaryotes (Lu et al. 2012), in particular the biosynthesis of glycans (Niemann et al. 2015; Ebert et al. 2018) and oligosaccharides that can affect cell wall properties (Chen and Cheng 2024). It is therefore an interesting hypothesis to explore whether the levels of UDP‐N‐acetylglucosamine in the cell wall influence the availability of sugar molecules for salicinoid biosynthesis.

Two HXXXD‐Type Acyltransferases and One UDP‐Glycosyltransferase Are Novel Candidate Genes in SPG Biosynthesis

In this study, younger leaves exhibited a higher proportion of DEGs (29% and 18%) within network modules compared to older leaves (5% and 6%). The shoot tip module also showed an enrichment of genes associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, some of which are in common with prior descriptions by Tsai et al. (2006). Previous studies on SPG turnover indicate that immature leaf tissues are active sites of SPG biosynthesis. In P. trichocarpa , carbon allocation and turnover of compounds such as salicin, salicortin and sugars (tracked using 13CO2) suggested that SPGs are primarily synthesised in immature leaves (Massad et al. 2014). Furthermore, immature leaves also had higher turnover of SPGs following damage early in the growing season and a rapid increase in glucose, the core carbohydrate of SPGs, within 24 h post‐damage, consistent with SPG synthesis mainly occurring in young leaves. However, the authors also acknowledged the possibility of SPG translocation from other tissues to young leaves. In another study, Gould et al. (2007) found salicin in the phloem sap of Populus deltoides Bartr., with the highest concentration in the youngest leaves.

In another study on Salix myrsinifolia plantlets, Ruuhola and Julkunen‐Tiitto (2000) inhibited the key enzyme Phe ammonia‐lyase (PAL) in the phenylpropanoid pathway by exposing the plants to 2‐aminoindan‐2‐phosphonic acid (AIP), leading to a drastic accumulation of phenylalanine and a reduction of SPGs in the shoot tips. In contrast, mature leaves exhibited the lowest phenylalanine accumulation, and SPG levels remained stable with no significant turnover, suggesting that the plant prioritises defending the vulnerable shoot tip. Their results align with the findings of Subramaniam et al. (1993), who studied PAL1/2 expression across various organs of P. trichocarpa × P. deltoides. They reported the highest expression levels in young stems, young leaves and apical buds, while mature leaves and older stems showed low expression. Subsequent studies on PAL gene expression in Populus have shown that PAL1 and PAL2 have distinct expression patterns, being associated with cell types rich in condensed tannins and lignins, respectively (Kao et al. 2002). Since then, three additional PAL genes have been identified in Populus, segregating them into two distinct phylogenetic clades (Tsai et al. 2006; Hamberger et al. 2007).

Our results revealed a significant correlation between SPG abundance and gene expression of genes encoding enzymes potentially involved in SPG biosynthesis, supporting young leaves as the location of SPG biosynthesis, as opposed to transport into young leaves. Assuming that genes involved in SPG biosynthesis are highly active in immature leaves but not in mature leaves, two HXXXD‐type acyltransferases (Potra2n10c20569 and Potra2n8c16912) and one UDP‐glycosyltransferase (Potra2n15c28969) from the gene set in Table 2 showed clear separation between young and mature leaves, as well as between CN and AC−/CN− chemotypes (Figure S6; see remaining genes in Scatterplots/File2 at the Figshare repository). We consider these genes to be promising candidates for further in vitro validation studies.

Use of PlantGenIE Tools to Explore the Variation of an UDP‐N‐Acetylglucosamine Transferase Potentially Conferring a Growth‐Defence Trade‐Off

To demonstrate the utility of the AspGen exAtlas dataset within the PlantGenIE.org resource, we explored a gene within the ‘salmon’ module of the shoot tip network that is annotated as a UDP‐N‐acetylglucosamine transferase subunit ALG14 (Potra2n1c3102; Table 2). This gene was highly significantly differentially expressed between chemotype groups, as visualised in the Chemotypes data set using the exImage tool (Figure 6a), as well as exhibiting clear expression differences in the AspGen exAtlas dataset (Figure 6b). While the aim of this study was to discover genes involved in the biosynthesis of SPGs, the significance of this gene in the study suggests a role in processes other than SPG biosynthesis. We speculate that this might indicate a trade‐off between SPG and cell wall biosynthesis. We took advantage of the resources at PlantGenIE.org to further explore the expression characteristics of this gene. The first‐degree neighbours of Potra2n1c3102 in the shoot tip network (available from the Figshare repository) were visualised in the AspLeaf data set; using the exHeatmap tool, two distinct clades were revealed, corresponding to either high expression through developmental stages towards the shoot apex (Apical clade) or fully expanded leaves (‘Expanded clade’; Figure 6c). The enrichment tool showed an over‐enrichment of GO terms for ‘cell wall organisation or biogenesis’ for both clades and ‘secondary metabolic process’, ‘xylan acetylation’ and ‘acyltransferase activity’ in the Apical clade (Figure 6d), indicating functional involvement in both cell wall development and specialised metabolism. This led us to explore the ‘Expanded clade’ in the AspWood dataset, which revealed a clear pattern of expression for 13 of the genes within expanding xylem (Figure 6e). Taken together, gene expression patterns implicate Potra2n1c3102 as a compelling candidate for investigating cell wall development in addition to its putative function in influencing specialised metabolism. Moreover, in the AspGen exAtlas dataset, Potra2n1c3102 showed a strong negative correlation with HCH‐cinnamoyl‐salicortin (r = −0.72) in the shoot tip among genotypes expressing the gene (vst > 0). The differential expression of Potra2n1c3102 may mirror a growth‐defence trade‐off mechanism (Abreu et al. 2020), such that available carbon is alternatively channelled to cell wall development or specialised metabolism and warrants further examination.

Conclusions

Our study illustrates the synergistic value of integrating metabolomic and transcriptomic data, providing a resource for identifying candidate genes involved in metabolite biosynthesis. In our pursuit to enhance understanding of SPG biosynthesis, we compared genes exhibiting differential expression between genotypes producing distinct SPGs, leading to the identification of two transferases and one UDP‐glucosyltransferase as candidate genes for future characterisation. The availability of untargeted LC–MS data with associated MS/MS data additionally enables the identification of novel metabolites. Our study successfully identified 27 putative SPG metabolites by exploring contrasting SPG genotypes.

Beyond exploring natural genetic variation for genotypes with contrasting SPG compositions, the resource also provides exploration opportunities across diverse tree organs and leaf developmental stages, offering valuable insight into ontogenetic variation in SPGs. Young leaves emerged as the most likely location of SPG biosynthesis, as they were the most informative for identifying candidate genes involved in SPG biosynthesis.

The AspGen exAtlas resource is openly available for public exploration at PlantGenIE.org, encompassing multiple sample types, enabling early investigation into potential metabolite biosynthesis sites.
