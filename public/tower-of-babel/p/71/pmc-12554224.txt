Selenium-functionalized hyaluronic acid dissolvable microneedle patch synergistic herbal shikonin-loaded tetrahedral framework nucleic acids for psoriasis therapy
Authors: Mengting Zhuo, Ni Cheng, Jiaqi Du, Tao Wang, Chen Sun, Shanglin Lu, Jun Wang, Dejun Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis is a complex, chronic inflammatory skin disease for which current therapies are limited by systemic toxicity, rapid relapse, and incomplete resolution. Despite advances in microneedle (MN)-assisted transdermal drug delivery, conventional systems often suffer from suboptimal pharmacokinetics and limited therapeutic versatility. Here, we engineered a dissolvable MN patch composed of selenium-functionalized hyaluronic acid (SeHA) integrated with shikonin-loaded tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (SFN) to enable sustained drug release while modulating the reactive oxygen species (ROS)-rich psoriatic microenvironment. The SeHA-SFN MNs exhibited robust mechanical properties, permitting efficient penetration of hyperkeratotic epidermis, followed by rapid dissolution and targeted SFN delivery. In vitro, SFN enhanced shikonin uptake, suppressing keratinocyte hyperproliferation and promoting apoptosis, while SeHA scavenged ROS, mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation. In vivo, the system ameliorated psoriatic phenotypes in an imiquimod-induced murine model, attenuating epidermal thickening, systemic inflammation, and pathological microenvironmental cues. Biosafety assessments confirmed excellent biocompatibility. By synergizing antioxidative SeHA with SFN-mediated shikonin delivery, this platform presents a promising and versatile approach for localized and targeted management of psoriasis, potentially extending to other inflammatory skin disorders.Keywords: Psoriasis, Selenium-functionalized hyaluronic acid, Dissolvable microneedle patch, Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids, Shikonin

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, multifactorial immune-mediated skin disorder, affecting approximately 125 million people worldwide [[1], [2], [3]]. It is characterized by erythema, scaling, itching, and pain, and can significantly impair patients' quality of life, imposing a substantial psychosocial burden [4]. The World Health Organization recognizes psoriasis as a serious non-communicable disease. Available treatment options include topical therapies, phototherapy, conventional systemic treatments, and biologics [5]. However, these therapies are often associated with adverse side effects, such as liver damage, renal toxicity, hypertension, and skin irritation or atrophy, which can become more pronounced with prolonged use [6]. Furthermore, long-term use of biologics, including TNF-α inhibitors and anti-IL-12/23 drugs, has been linked to an increased risk of several inflammatory conditions and cancer [7]. Despite the variety of treatment approaches, an ideal therapeutic strategy for psoriasis remains elusive, highlighting the urgent need for novel treatment options [4,7].

As a typical trans-dermal medical device with a minimally invasive approach and controlled/sustained drug release pattern, microneedles (MNs) have gained momentum in the diagnosis and treatment of psoriasis [[8], [9], [10]]. MNs can efficiently penetrate the protective stratum corneum (SC) layer and painlessly deliver drugs to the skin, providing patients with better compliance. Due to pain-free, minimally invasive, and highly efficient nature, MNs have demonstrated their suitability as carriers for a broad range of active agents, including small molecules, biological agents, and nanomaterials [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. For example, MNs patches loaded with methotrexate (MTX) [15,16], IL-17 monoclonal antibodies (mAb) [13,17], and regulatory T cells [14,18] have been explored for psoriasis treatment, showing successful drug delivery to the dermis and improved therapeutic outcomes. However, as small molecular drugs have a short effective duration in skins, the fast release of molecular drugs from dissolving MNs is not ideal for treating of psoriasis [19], which can hinder the drug utilization because of the short effective skin retention and lack of target ability.

To overcome the challenges, researchers have established nano-carrier loaded MNs systems, such as micelles [20,21], nanoparticles [22,23], and liposomes [24,25] were implied as a drug delivery tool. Drugs can be stored in micropores or be released directly into the dermis after penetrating the epidermis to produce local or systemic therapeutic effects through the vascular network in the dermis. However, despite the above achievements, it is still a challenge to design MNs that can overcome the cell membrane barriers to increase drug utilization because of the short effective skin retention and lack of target ability [10]. Therefore, a more efficient MN-based transdermal delivery system is demanded. Unlike conventional carriers, tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) have emerged as promising drug delivery platforms for its unique tetrahedral structure and nucleic-acid-derived biocompatibility, which enables superior skin penetration [26,27]. Previous studies revealed that tFNAs can be efficiently endocytosed by mammalian cells in a caveolin-dependent manner to accurately release loaded medicinal molecules into cells [28]. tFNAs also reported have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant indicating that tFNAs may act synergistically with MNs and enhance its properties for psoriasis treatment [27].

Moreover, the complex inflammatory microenvironment of psoriasis, such as activated inflammatory pathways and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines from infiltrated immune cells, was associated with oxidative stress caused by the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [[29], [30], [31]]. Consequently, scavenging ROS has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for managing psoriasis [32]. As a potent antioxidant, selenium has shown considerable potential in mitigating ROS accumulation, protecting keratinocytes from oxidative damage, and could exert alleviating psoriatic inflammation [[33], [34], [35]]. Against this background, we hypothesized that selenocystamine-functionalized hyaluronic acid (SeHA) incorporated as the scaffold for the dissolvable microneedle patch, enabling regulation of the ROS microenvironment in psoriasis lesions, could synergistic for psoriasis therapy.

Herein, we propose a novel SeHA dissolvable microneedle patch synergistic herbal Shikonin (SHI)-loaded tFNAs with the features of effective drug delivery and modulate the local ROS microenvironment for synergistic psoriasis treatment (see Scheme 1). SHI, a bioactive compound derived from Arnebiae Radix, exhibits potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties, widely applied in the research of psoriasis [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. As drug delivery carriers, tFNAs provide efficient loading of herbal SHI due to their unique spatial structure, offering advantages in biocompatibility, structural stability, cellular uptake, and tissue permeability [27,[40], [41], [42]]. Moreover, as a potent antioxidant, SeHA can reduce ROS accumulation, protect skin cells from oxidative damage, and attenuate skin inflammation in psoriasis [[33], [34], [35]]. Therefore, by integrating SeHA with SHI-loaded tFNAs, we propose the development of an innovative multifunctional transdermal drug delivery system with both targeted drug release and microenvironment-modulating capabilities for improved psoriasis treatment. Upon penetration into psoriatic skin, the MNs rapidly dissolved, facilitating the efficient release of SFN into the dermis, further modulating the ROS microenvironment via antioxidant effect of the SeHA. Owing to the strong transmembrane penetration ability of tFNAs, SFN facilitated enhanced cellular uptake of SHI, effectively inhibiting keratinocyte hyperproliferation and inducing apoptosis, thereby achieving targeted psoriasis therapy. Based on these capabilities, the obtained system significantly ameliorated psoriatic symptoms in an imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis model, by markedly reducing epidermal thickness and attenuating inflammatory responses, demonstrating promising in vivo therapeutic potential. Overall, we believe that the innovative multifunctional transdermal drug delivery system with both targeted drug release and microenvironment-modulating capabilities, holds substantial potential for the treatment of psoriasis and other inflammatory skin disorders.

Materials and methods

Materials

Imiquimod cream [5 % (wt %)] was purchased from Sichuan MED-SHINE Pharmaceutical Co. Penicillin-streptomycin and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA. Hyaluronic acid (HA) (Mw = 330 kDa) was obtained from Bloomage Freda Biopharm Co., Ltd (China). Selenocystamine dihydrochloride (51,044 B) was purchased from Admas (USA) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), Shikonin, N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) (99 %) and 1-(3-dimethylamino) propyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide (EDC) (97 %) were purchased from Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co. Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was obtained from Beijing Solepol Technology Co. Single-stranded DNAs were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shang hai) Co. All other reagents were of analytical grade. 4 % paraformaldehyde universal tissue fixative was obtained from Hefei White Shark Biotechnology Co. Deionized water was used throughout the experiment.

Synthesis and characterization of SFN

Four distinct single-stranded DNAs (ssDNAs), as listed in Table S1, were used for the synthesis of tFNAs following a previously reported self-assembly method [43]. Briefly, each ssDNA was dissolved in enzyme-free water at an equal molar concentration (10 μM). Equimolar amounts of the four ssDNAs were mixed in TM buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM MgCl2, pH 8.0), heated at 95 °C for 10 min, and then immediately cooled at 4 °C for at least 20 min to form tFNAs.

For the preparation of SFN, SHI was first dissolved in DMSO (500 μM) and then added dropwise to the freshly prepared tFNA solution at predetermined molar ratios (SHI:tFNA = 1:100, 1:200, 1:400, or 1:500). The mixture was gently shaken at room temperature for 6 h to allow SHI encapsulation. The unbound SHI was removed by centrifugation through a 30 kDa ultrafiltration tube at 12,000×g for 20 min. The retentate (SFN complexes) was washed twice with enzyme-free water, lyophilized to dryness, and accurately weighed using an analytical microbalance. The dried SFN was then reconstituted in enzyme-free water to a defined concentration for subsequent experiments.

The zeta potential of the dispersion at 25 °C was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) at a 90° scattering angle. For tFNAs visualization, samples were stained with 2 % phosphotungstic acid for 2 min, allowed to dry naturally, and then imaged using transmission electron microscope (HT-7700, Hitachi, Japan). Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was used to assess the self-assembly of the tFNAs and SFN.

To characterize the formulation's stability, the particle size distribution of the SFN complex was systematically analyzed by dynamic light scattering following storage intervals of 0, 2, 4, and 6 days. Investigation into the drug release profile was conducted using a standard dialysis method. Specifically, the SFN formulation (comprising tFNAs and SHI at a molar ratio of 1:200) was introduced into a dialysis chamber (30 kDa MWCO, Solarbio) and dialyzed against 50 mL of PBS receptor medium under sink conditions at a constant physiological temperature of 37 °C. Aliquots (2 mL) of the external medium were withdrawn at predetermined time points, and the concentration of released SHI was quantified spectrophotometrically at 492 nm to construct the release kinetic profile.

Synthesis of selenocysteamine-modified hyaluronic acid

The SeHA was synthesized based on previously reported literature [44]. Briefly, a total of 100 mg of hyaluronic acid (330 kDa) was dissolved in 50 mL of DMSO. Subsequently, 251 mg of 1-[3-dimethylaminopropyl]-3-ethylcarbodiimide (EDC) and 151 mg of N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were added, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 6 h. Selenocystamine dihydrochloride was then gradually introduced into the reaction solution, which was maintained in the dark under an inert gas atmosphere for 48 h. Upon completion, the product was dialyzed against distilled water using a dialysis membrane (molecular weight cutoff: 5000 Da) and then lyophilized. The resulting SeHA was characterized using 1HNMR and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy.

Preparation of SeHA-SFN MNs patches

The preparation of SeHA-SFN MNs patches were based on previous reports in the literature with some modifications [45]. Specifically, 0.3 g of SeHA was precisely weighed and dissolved in three separate batches into a predetermined volume (2 mL) of SFN (125 nM) drug solution to form a 15 % aqueous solution. This solution was then subjected to ultrasonication to form the needle layer gel solution. PVA was dissolved in deionized water to prepare a 20 % (w/v) backing layer gel solution. Both solutions were degassed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 3 min. A defined volume (typically 300–400 μL per mold, corresponding to the measured cavity volume) of the needle-layer gel solution was loaded into the microneedle molds using a positive-displacement pipette. The filled molds were subjected to vacuum (0.09–0.1 MPa) for 2 min to remove trapped air bubbles and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 3 min to drive the solution into the microcavities. Any residual solution on the mold surface was gently removed using a sterile, lint-free swab to prevent uneven distribution. This filling/centrifugation step was repeated 1–2 times until all cavities were completely filled. Supernatants and removed residues were collected and stored under sterile conditions for reuse to minimize drug material loss. For the backing layer, the PVA solution was added on top of the filled mold and spread evenly to cover the cavities. The molds were then dried under static conditions at 37 °C for 72 h. After drying, the SeHA-SFN MN patches were carefully demolded and stored in a desiccator until use. To ensure batch-to-batch consistency, the initial weight of the needle-layer solution per mold was standardized, and the drug content in randomly selected patches from each batch was quantified by dissolving the MNs in water and measuring the SHI absorbance at 492 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The morphology of the microneedles was examined by scanning electron microscopy. Dissolution behavior was assessed using an optical microscope, release kinetics were measured using an intelligent transdermal tester, and mechanical strength was evaluated by force–displacement curves obtained using an electronic universal testing machine.

Solvation kinetics of SHI in MNs

The dissolution kinetics of SHI in MNs were assessed using a transdermal drug delivery testing instrument (TP-2A, Yuezhong Instrument & Equipment Co., Ltd., China). SHI and SFN MN were inserted into fresh mouse skin, with the stratum corneum exposed to ambient conditions and the dermis facing the receptor chamber. The chamber was filled with PBS (pH 7.4), maintained at 37 °C, and magnetically stirred at 250 rpm. Samples were taken periodically and analyzed using a UV spectrophotometer to determine SHI release. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

In vitro skin penetration test

Fresh whole porcine skin was prepared to assess the skin penetration capability of MNs. MNs was prepared by replacing SFN with SHI@tFNA-Cy5. The porcine skin was fixed on microscope slides, and MNs patches loaded with tFNA-Cy5 were rapidly inserted into the skin and pressed for 2 min. After removing the MNs patches, fluorescence signals within the skin layers were visualized using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica, Germany). Dye penetration was measured by scanning fluorescence intensity along the Z-axis at 24 μm intervals.

Cell culture, cellular uptake, cell viability and apoptosis assay

HaCaT and RAW264.7 cell lines were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (H-DMEM) supplemented with 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. The culture medium was refreshed twice a week.

For cellular uptake analysis, 1 × 105 cells were seeded into confocal dishes and incubated overnight in complete medium. The following day, the medium was replaced with H-DMEM containing 1 % FBS and either SHI (5 μM) or SHI (5 μM)-loaded tFNA (SFN). After 8 h of incubation, the cells were fixed with 4 % (w/v) paraformaldehyde for at least 15 min. The cytoskeleton was stained using a FITC-labeled microfilament probe (C2201S) for 40 min, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) for 10 min. Between each staining step, the samples were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The samples were then imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

For cell viability, HaCaT cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with various concentrations of SHI and SFN (SHI = 0.5, 1, 2, 5, and 8 μM). Cells were incubated for 24 h. After treatment, 10 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h. Subsequently, 100 μL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm.

To assess apoptosis, HaCaT cells were treated with SHI and SFN for 24 h. Apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V-FITC Early Apoptosis Detection Kit (from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), following the manufacturers’ protocols. Apoptotic cell populations were analyzed via flow cytometry.

Intracellular ROS and oxidative indices and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

RAW 264.7 and HaCat cells were treated with HA or SeHA (400 μg/mL) for 12 h and subsequently stimulated with 100 μM H2O2 for 2 h. ROS levels were assessed using the ROS Fluorescence Probe (S0053, Beyotime). Intracellular ROS were detected using the ROS fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, BC0175, Solarbio) and analyzed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany), following the manufacturer's protocol.

To evaluate changes in oxidative and cytokine levels in vitro, RAW 264.7 cells were treated with HA or SeHA (0, 6.25, 25, and 100 μg/mL) for 12 h and then stimulated with 100 μM H2O2 for 6 h. Afterward, the cultured cells were lysed, and oxidative index levels were analyzed using the SOD kit (BC0175, Solarbio) and the GSH assay kit (S0053, Beyotime), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The culture medium was also collected for IL-6 and TNF-α assays.

Anti-psoriasis effect in IMQ-induced psoriasis model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong Second Medical University (Ethics No. 2025SDL451). A total of 25 male C57BL/6 mice (7–8 weeks old) were acclimated under standard conditions for one week and then randomly assigned into five groups (n = 5 per group): (1) Blank control group, (2) IMQ model group, (3) SHI cream group, (4) SeHA MNs group, and (5) SeHA-SFN MNs group. Following one day of normal feeding to restore the stratum corneum, each mouse (except those in the blank control group) received a topical application of 62.5 mg of imiquimod cream on the shaved dorsal skin once every 24 h. Starting from the second day, one microneedle patch was administered daily according to the respective treatment group. Skin lesion progression was recorded daily.

The Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scoring system was used to evaluate erythema, scaling, and epidermal thickening. A composite score was calculated to assess the therapeutic efficacy of each treatment in alleviating psoriasis-like symptoms. On the eighth day, blood samples were collected, and the affected dorsal skin was excised post-euthanasia. Skin tissue samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed, and stained sections were analyzed on the same day. For each sample, three random fields of view were selected per slide, and the epidermal thickness was measured and averaged.

In vivo ROS measurements

To determine the level of ROS at the site of disease, fresh skin tissue was washed in 4 °C PBS (containing 10 % FBS) to remove adipose tissue. A single-cell suspension was prepared by adding 1 mL of skin dissociation solution (1 mg/mL collagenase IV, 50 mg/mL DNase, 10 % FBS) in a centrifuge tube and digested at 37 °C for 90 min. Digestion was terminated by adding 10 mL of complete medium and the collected mixture was passed through a 200-mesh cell sieve. Cells in the digested mixture were collected by centrifugation at 250 g for 10 min and washed once. Intracellular ROS were detected by flow cytometry analysis using the ROS fluorescent probe DCFH-DA.

Detection of immune cells in spleens by flow cytometry

Mouse spleens were washed in PBS (containing 10 % FBS), crushed by instrumental grinding, and filtered through a 200-mesh sieve. The cells were then lysed by adding erythrocyte lysate (Solarbio) on ice for 5 min. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 1500 rpm, resuspended, and stained with antibodies. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a flow cytometer (C6plus, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Independent Student's t-test was used for comparison of two groups, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparison of multiple groups, followed by Tukey's HSD test. p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of SFN

SHI, a naturally occurring naphthoquinone-based bioactive molecule, is widely recognized for its potent anti-inflammatory properties [36,46]. However, its inherent hydrophobicity significantly limits its solubility in aqueous media and hinders its transdermal delivery efficiency [47]. MNs, known for their pain-free, minimally invasive, and highly efficient drug delivery capabilities, present a promising strategy for SHI administration [[48], [49], [50]]. Nevertheless, conventional MNs often lead to rapid drug release, reducing their therapeutic effectiveness [19]. To overcome this limitation, we encapsulated herbal SHI within tFNAs prior to integrating them into MNs, thereby enhancing controlled release and bioavailability. The tFNAs were prepared through a self-assembly strategy based on precise Watson–Crick base pairing (Fig. 1a). Four single-stranded DNAs (ssDNAs) (Table S1, Supporting Information) were designed to self-assemble into triangular subunits, which subsequently paired with complementary strands to form a stable tetrahedral structure [43]. SHI was then loaded into tFNAs via oscillation, facilitated by its affinity for DNA grooves [41]. Specifically, the naphthoquinone core of SHI intercalated into the double-helix groove region of tFNAs through π-π stacking interactions, while its hydroxyl side chains formed hydrogen bonds with the phosphate backbone, enhancing the structural integrity and bioavailability of SFN.

The successful self-assembly of tFNA was verified by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). As shown in Fig. 1b, the four individual ssDNAs (Lanes 2–5) exhibited the fastest migration, while partial assemblies containing two (Lane 6) or three (Lane 7) strands showed progressively slower migration due to increased molecular size. Fully assembled tFNAs (Lane 8) displayed the slowest mobility, which is consistent with their larger molecular weight and compact tetrahedral structure, confirming successful formation. After SHI loading, the resulting SFN complex (Lane 9) exhibited a band at the same position as native tFNAs, indicating that SHI incorporation did not alter the integrity or structure of the tFNAs. This gradual shift in band mobility with increasing strand number provides direct evidence of stepwise self-assembly of the tFNAs. To determine the proportion of SHI-carrying tFNAs, the loading amount (Fig. 1c) and encapsulation efficiency (Fig. 1d) were systematically investigated at different molar ratios (1:100–1:500). The results showed that the encapsulation efficiency tended to decrease with increasing ratio, while the drug loading tended to increase. Considering the loading efficiency and utilization of SHI, 1:200 (tFNA: SHI) was determined as the optimal ratio, which resulted in an encapsulation efficiency of 94.2 ± 16 % and a drug loading of 54.4 ± 4.3 %. Furthermore, the difference between synthesized SFN and free SHI was clearly visible to the naked eye, as shown in Fig. S1. The SFN solution appeared as a clear purple liquid, whereas free SHI displayed a lighter purple color, with visible precipitation of insoluble SHI. Notably, unlike the obvious precipitation observed in the free SHI sample due to its poor water solubility, no solid matter was observed in the SFN solution, indicating that SFN was well dispersed in the aqueous medium.

The particle size (Fig. 1e) and zeta potential (Fig. 1f) of SHI, tFNA and SFN were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS). With the loading of SHI, the average particle size of SFN increased from 12.28 ± 1.36 nm to 24.75 ± 1.43 nm. The average zeta potential of SFN changed from −8.78 ± 0.49 mV to −21.9 ± 0.55 mV, which was attributed to the loading of tFNA by SHI and the deprotonation of hydroxyl groups in tFNA, a property which enhances the stability of the complexes, and further confirmed the successful synthesis of SFN. Photophysical properties were then determined to confirm the successful encapsulation and binding of SHI in tFNAs. As shown in Fig. 1g, by scanning the UV–vis absorption spectrum of SHI, SHI showed a clear high absorption peak in DMSO solution near 492 nm. The absorbance peak of SFN was close to that of natural SHI, which confirmed the successful encapsulation of SHI in tFNAs. As shown in Fig. S2a, the particle size of SFN remained stable (∼26 nm) over 6 days in aqueous solution, confirming good structural stability. Furthermore, the drug release study (Fig. S2b) demonstrated a sustained release profile, with gradual release over 36 h and approximately 90 % cumulative release at 72 h. These findings confirm that tFNAs effectively stabilize SHI and provide controlled release, thereby prolonging its bioavailability for therapeutic action.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1h) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Fig. 1i) were further characterized the morphological features of SFN. According to the TEM images, tFNA typically appear in TEM and AFM images as well-dispersed, uniform (∼10 nm), and clearly defined nanostructures, reflecting their stable hydrophilic and negatively charged DNA frameworks. After incorporation of SHI, however, their morphology becomes altered, often showing larger (∼20 nm), irregular, or aggregated particles, which is consistent with previous reports [43]. These changes arise because hydrophobic drugs tend to associate with one another and embed within or onto the DNA framework, which disturbs the native base-stacking and hydrogen-bonding interactions and partially deforms or compacts the tetrahedral structure.

Cellular uptake and biological effect of SFN in vitro

To evaluate the regulatory effects of the SFN complex on human immortalized keratinocytes (HaCaT), a key cell type implicated in the pathogenesis of psoriasis, we systematically assessed its cellular uptake, antiproliferative activity, and ability to induce programmed cell death. Previous studies have demonstrated that tFNAs enhance the stability and bioavailability of encapsulated molecules, facilitating increased cellular uptake [28]. Additionally, the unique and highly ordered structure of tFNAs offers protection against enzymatic degradation, thereby further stabilizing small molecules [43]. In this study, we used confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) to assess the cellular uptake of the SFN in HaCaT cells. As shown in Fig. 2a, the red fluorescence intensity of SHI (Fig. S3) for SFN-treated cells was significantly stronger than that observed in the free SHI group, indicating enhanced intracellular accumulation of SHI.

Considering that excessive keratinocyte proliferation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of psoriasis [51], we investigated the antiproliferative effects of the SFN on HaCaT cells in vitro. Previous studies have demonstrated that SHI can induce apoptosis and inhibit keratinocyte growth through the upregulation of CEBP-δ in psoriatic skin lesions [39]. To assess the antiproliferative potential of SFN, we conducted an MTT assay using HaCaT cells. As shown in Fig. 2b, free SHI exhibited typical dose-dependent cytotoxicity, reducing the proliferative activity of HaCaT cells. Notably, the inhibition of cell viability was significantly more pronounced in the SFN-treated group, indicating that tFNAs effectively enhance the bioavailability and antiproliferative efficacy of SHI. To directly visualize the impact on cell viability, we employed Calcein-AM/PI staining to distinguish live and dead cells. As shown in Fig. 2c, a substantial increase in PI-stained (dead) cells was observed in the SFN-treated group, further demonstrating the enhanced cytotoxic effect achieved through the combination of tFNA and SHI. Importantly, there was no statistically significant difference in cell viability between the blank tFNA group (400 nM) and the untreated control, confirming the excellent biocompatibility of tFNAs (Fig. 2d).

To further elucidate the mechanism of cell death, we assessed apoptosis rates using Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining combined with flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 2e and Fig. 2f, the proportion of early apoptotic cells (24.1 % ± 1.55 %) and late apoptotic/necrotic cells (6.6 % ± 0.42 %) in the SFN-treated group (8 μM) was significantly higher than that observed in the free SHI group at the same concentration (16.1 % ± 0.88 % and 3.8 % ± 0.58 %, respectively; p < 0.01). These findings underscore the enhanced pro-apoptotic effect of SFN, corroborating the results obtained from the cell viability assays.

Synthesis and biological effect of SeHA

Increasing evidence suggests that excessive ROS are not merely a hallmark of psoriasis but actively contribute to its pathogenesis by amplifying inflammatory signaling pathways [[29], [30], [31]]. Consequently, scavenging ROS has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for managing psoriasis [32]. As a potent antioxidant, selenium has shown considerable potential in mitigating ROS accumulation, protecting keratinocytes from oxidative damage, and alleviating psoriatic inflammation [[33], [34], [35]]. In this context, we synthesized SeHA through a condensation reaction (Fig. 3a) to effectively modulate the ROS-rich microenvironment present in psoriatic lesions. Specifically, the carboxylic acid groups of HA were activated using EDC/NHS coupling agents [44], enabling conjugation with selenocystamine, a molecule containing two primary amine groups. The successful synthesis of SeHA was confirmed through 1HNMR and Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. As illustrated in Fig. S4, distinct peaks corresponding to the methylene groups of selenocystamine (2H, -(CH2)2-Se, labeled as “γ” in Figure S4 a,c) and the methylene (2H, -CH2-) and glycosidic (H) protons of HA (“α” in Figure S4 b,c) appeared at 3.1 and 3.2–4.5 ppm, respectively, indicating successful modification of HA with selenocystamine [44]. Furthermore, the characteristic peaks at 740 cm−1 (Se–C) and 545 cm−1 (Se–Se) observed in the FT-IR spectrum (Fig. 3b) of SeHA provided additional confirmation of successful selenization [44].

To evaluate the potential cytotoxicity and antioxidant capacity of SeHA in vitro, HaCaT cells and RAW 264.7 macrophages were selected as representative models of keratinocytes and immune cells, respectively. Cell viability following SeHA treatment was assessed using an MTT assay, revealing that concentrations up to 400 μg/mL exhibited no significant cytotoxicity in either cell line (Fig. S5). This result indicates the favorable biocompatibility of SeHA and its suitability for further biological applications. To investigate the ability of SeHA to scavenge ROS, we employed an H2O2-induced oxidative stress model in both cell types. As depicted in Fig. 3c, exposure to 100 μM H2O2 markedly reduced cell viability, indicating that excessive oxidative stress led to irreversible cell damage. However, treatment with SeHA (100 μg/mL) significantly improved the survival rates of RAW 264.7 macrophages and HaCaT keratinocytes by approximately 46.6 % and 42.2 %, respectively, compared to the PBS and HA-treated groups. These findings demonstrate that SeHA effectively mitigates ROS-induced cytotoxicity, underscoring its protective potential against oxidative stress.

Further elucidation of the ROS-scavenging capacity of SeHA was achieved by measuring intracellular ROS levels using dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) as a fluorescent probe [52]. As illustrated in Fig. 3d, cells exposed to H2O2 exhibited a significant increase in green fluorescence intensity, reflecting elevated intracellular ROS levels. Remarkably, SeHA treatment significantly decreased the fluorescence intensity to 50.3 % in RAW 264.7 cells and 53.4 % in HaCaT cells compared to the H2O2-only group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3e). In contrast, no significant reduction in fluorescence was observed in the HA-treated group, indicating that the antioxidant capability is attributed to the selenium component of SeHA. These findings confirm that SeHA exhibits strong antioxidant activity and significant protective effects against ROS-induced cellular damage, highlighting the potential of SeHA in modulating the oxidative microenvironment associated with psoriasis.

Selenium-containing compounds are widely recognized for their potent antioxidant properties and pivotal roles in cellular redox regulation [[33], [34], [35]]. These effects are primarily mediated through the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway, which subsequently enhances the expression of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH), and glutathione S-transferase (GST) [44]. To explore whether SeHA could similarly influence the expression of these antioxidative defense mechanisms, we assessed the levels of SOD and GSH in RAW 264.7 cells following SeHA treatment. As demonstrated in Fig. 3f and Fig. 3g, SeHA treatment significantly elevated the activity of SOD and the concentration of GSH compared to untreated controls. Specifically, the SOD activity in RAW 264.7 cells increased from (1.54 ± 0.08) μg/106 cells to (2.24 ± 0.32) μg/106 cells (p < 0.01), while the GSH level rose from (0.87 ± 0.19) μg/106 cells to (2.98 ± 1.11) μg/106 cells. These marked increases suggest that SeHA effectively activates intracellular antioxidant defense systems.

Excessive ROS production within the inflammatory microenvironment of psoriasis is known to activate inflammatory signaling pathways, leading to the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [29,44]. These cytokines play critical roles in the perpetuation of chronic inflammation and the exacerbation of psoriatic lesions. To assess the anti-inflammatory potential of SeHA, we employed an H2O2-induced RAW 264.7 macrophage model and evaluated cytokine levels in the culture supernatant using ELISA. Our findings revealed that SeHA significantly suppressed the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines in a dose-dependent manner under H2O2-induced oxidative stress. Specifically, IL-6 levels were markedly reduced from 226.0 ± 11.9 ng/mL to 146.4 ± 9.4 ng/mL following treatment with 25 μ g/mL SeHA, while TNF-α secretion was decreased by 20.7 % at a concentration of 6.25 μg/mL (Fig. 3h). These data indicate the potent anti-inflammatory capability of SeHA in mitigating ROS-induced inflammatory responses. Notably, no statistically significant reduction in cytokine levels was observed in cells treated with HA alone, underscoring the critical role of the selenium component in enhancing the anti-inflammatory efficacy of the material. Taken together, we presume that the dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of SeHA may contribute synergistically to its therapeutic potential in psoriasis.

Fabrication and characterization of SeHA-SFN MNs patches

Encouraged by the above results, we hypothesized that integrating SFN with SeHA-based dissolving MNs could offer a sustained-release transdermal delivery system while effectively modulating the ROS-rich microenvironment of psoriasis. The SeHA-SFN MNs Patches were fabricated through a two-step casting process, with the needle tips composed of SFN and SeHA solution and the supporting arrays made from polyvinyl alcohol (PVA). The morphology and structural integrity of the MNs were validated via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Fig. 4a, the MNs exhibited a complete and well-defined needle body with a sharp, intact tip, indicating successful fabrication without structural defects. The SeHA-SFN MN patches measured 14.5 × 14.5 mm, comprising a 15 × 15 array of positive quadrangular cone-shaped needles. Each needle had a base edge length of 360 μm, a height of 800 μm, and a tip spacing of 720 μm. This geometry was intentionally designed to confer sufficient mechanical strength for skin insertion while minimizing insertion force, as the sharp tips enable easy penetration through the stratum corneum and the robust needle bodies resist deformation or fracture during application. These parameters were meticulously designed to achieve an optimal balance between mechanical strength and adequate penetration depth through the SC, ensuring efficient transdermal drug delivery. To visualize the distribution of SFN within the MNs, SFN was labeled with Cy5 and examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy. As depicted in Fig. 4b, the SFN exhibited a uniform and homogeneous distribution throughout the MN matrix, indicating effective encapsulation. Elemental mapping analysis revealed the presence of C, N, O, and Se at the needle tips, verifying the successful incorporation of selenium into the MNs (Fig. 4c).

The dissolution properties of the MNs were evaluated by immersing them in PBS, demonstrating that the needle tips dissolved completely within 2 min (Fig. 4d), underscoring their rapid and efficient dissolution characteristics. In addition, the diselenium bond energy is approximately 176 kJ/mol [53], which is lower than that of the disulfide bond, indicating that the diselenium bond is more sensitive to oxidation–reduction leading to disintegration of the hydrogel under psoriasis microenvironment. To assess the mechanical strength of the SeHA-SFN MNs, a texture analyzer was employed. The results indicated that the MNs exhibited a compressive strength of 0.311 N per needle, substantially exceeding the minimum threshold of 0.058 N required for successful penetration of the SC (Fig. 4e). These findings suggest that the SeHA-SFN MNs possess sufficient mechanical robustness for effective transdermal application.

To examine the controlled drug release capacity of the SeHA-SFN MNs, in vitro transdermal release assays were conducted. As depicted in Fig. 4f, SHI encapsulated within the dissolving SeHA MNs displayed a rapid release, reaching a plateau of 85 % within 15 min due to the fast dissolution of SeHA. In contrast, the SeHA-SFN MNs exhibited a biphasic release profile (Fig. 4g): an initial burst phase with approximately 40 % of SHI released within the first 12 h, followed by a sustained release phase, achieving a cumulative release of around 80 % at 72 h. This prolonged release behavior is likely attributed to the protective encapsulation of SHI within tFNAs, mitigating the issue of premature drug release commonly observed in conventional MNs systems. In addition, the transdermal efficiency of MNs was verified in an ex vivo skin puncture assay using fresh pig skin with Cy5-loaded tFNA as an indicator. CLSM depth scanning revealed continuous fluorescence signals, indicating the effective penetration of the MNs through the skin layers. The gradual decrease in fluorescence intensity with increasing scanning depth confirmed the controlled diffusion of the encapsulated cargo into the deeper layers of the skin (Fig. 4h and i). Notably, the diffusion pattern suggested that the MNs patches not only penetrated the SC effectively but also facilitated the sustained release of active agents into the surrounding skin tissue.

The in vivo therapeutic effect of SeHA-SFN MNs patches

To assess the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the SeHA-SFN MN-based drug delivery system, we employed an IMQ-induced psoriasis-like murine model, a well-established approach for replicating the pathological features of psoriasis. IMQ was topically administered to the dorsal skin of mice for seven consecutive days to induce psoriatic lesions (Fig. 5a). Following successful model establishment, the psoriatic mice were randomly assigned to five treatment groups: SHI cream, SeHA MNs, and SeHA-SFN MNs, with comparisons made against a normal control group (healthy mice) and an untreated model group (psoriatic mice without treatment). Treatment with MNs patches or SHI cream commenced on the second day after IMQ application and was administered every day. Fig. 5b illustrates the skin condition of the mice on the eighth day of IMQ-induced psoriasis modeling. The untreated model group exhibited pronounced erythema, scaling, and thickening, characteristic of severe psoriatic inflammation. In contrast, the treatment groups showed varying degrees of symptom alleviation. Notably, the SeHA-SFN MN-treated group demonstrated the most substantial improvement, with visibly reduced erythema, minimized scaling. The Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), which includes assessments of erythema, epidermal thickening, and scaling, was used to evaluate the severity of skin lesions across different treatment groups. As shown in Fig. 5c, after 7 days of treatment, the SeHA-SFN MNs group achieved a mean PASI score of 2.5 ± 0.5, representing a 73 % reduction compared with the IMQ model group (9.4 ± 0.4) and a 67 % reduction compared with the SeHA-MNs group (7.5 ± 0.5). These visual observations underscore the synergistic benefits of the SFN and the antioxidative effects of SeHA in mitigating IMQ-induced psoriasiform dermatitis.

Histopathological analysis was performed to evaluate epidermal and dermal structures, key indicators of psoriatic pathology. Fig. 5d illustrates that the IMQ-induced model group exhibited marked pathological changes, including significant epidermal and dermal thickening, pronounced elongation of epidermal rete ridges, and excessive stratum corneum hyperplasia leading to desquamation and scale formation, underscoring the successful establishment of the psoriatic model. The SHI cream and SeHA MNs groups exhibited moderate alleviation of these pathological abnormalities; however, the epidermal and dermal thicknesses remained elevated compared to the normal control group. In contrast, the SeHA-SFN MNs-treated group exhibited the most effective therapeutic outcome, as evidenced by markedly reduced epidermal hyperplasia, thinner epidermal layers, and a more intact skin architecture. Quantitative measurements of epidermal thickness (Fig. 5f) were in agreement with the PASI score assessment (Fig. 5c). Specifically, the epidermal thickness in the normal control group was approximately 21.16 ± 3.83 μm, whereas the untreated model group exhibited a substantial increase to 205.87 ± 29.31 μm, approximately 9.73-fold higher than the control. Following treatment, epidermal thickness was significantly reduced across all groups, measuring 138.07 ± 27.87 μm in the SeHA MNs group, 94.89 ± 4.09 μm in the SHI cream group, and a remarkable 47.64 ± 12.57 μm in the SeHA-SFN MNs-treated. These findings underscore the superior efficacy of SeHA-SFN MNs in ameliorating psoriatic skin pathology. To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy, the expression of Ki67, a proliferation marker associated with keratinocyte hyperproliferation and increased psoriasis severity, was assessed through immunohistochemical analysis. As shown in Fig. 5e and 5g, all treatment groups demonstrated a reduction in keratinocyte proliferation compared to the model group, with the SeHA-SFN MNs-treated group exhibiting the most pronounced decrease, suggesting that SeHA-SFN MNs effectively mitigate both the phenotypic and histopathological characteristics of psoriatic lesions.

The in vivo antioxidative and anti-inflammatory efficacy effect of SeHA-SFN MNs patches

To further validate the antioxidative effects of SeHA, ROS levels in skin tissues were quantitatively assessed using DCF-DA fluorescence via flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 6a and b, ROS fluorescence intensity in the IMQ-induced model group was elevated by approximately 2.45-fold compared to the normal control group (p < 0.001), confirming the presence of an oxidative stress-rich microenvironment. Treatment with SeHA MNs led to a significant reduction in ROS levels, decreasing by approximately 1.31-fold (p < 0.01), aligning with the observed protective effects against oxidative damage. Notably, the SFN-SeHA MN-treated group exhibited a synergistic ROS-scavenging effect, with ROS levels further reduced to 1.08-fold (p < 0.001). In addition, key antioxidant enzyme activity was examined. As depicted in Fig. 6c, SeHA treatment significantly elevated the levels of SOD, an essential antioxidant enzyme involved in ROS neutralization. This increase in SOD activity was consistent with the ROS scavenging efficiency determined by flow cytometry, further substantiating the antioxidant potential of SeHA in modulating oxidative stress.

(b) Quantitative analysis of ROS levels in skin tissues of different treatment groups. (c) Measurement of SOD levels in skin using a total SOD activity assay kit. (d) Representative spleen naked eye images. (e) Statistical graph of spleen weight to body weight ratio. Given the systemic nature of psoriasis, it is crucial to evaluate whether localized drug delivery via MNs can modulate systemic inflammation. Psoriasis is often associated with splenomegaly, reflective of systemic immune dysregulation, characterized by cytokine overproduction and immune cell infiltration. Therefore, we evaluated the degree of splenomegaly across different treatment groups by calculating the spleen index (the ratio of spleen weight to body weight) on day 8. IMQ application led to a significant increase in the spleen index in the untreated model group (1.0 ± 0.07), approximately three times higher than that of the normal control group. Importantly, the SeHA-SFN MNs group exhibited the lowest spleen index among all treatment groups, closely approximating the values observed in healthy controls (Fig. 6d and Fig. 6e). This significant reduction in splenomegaly further supports the systemic anti-inflammatory effects of the SeHA-SFN MN platform, reinforcing its potential for comprehensive psoriasis management.

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory disease with a complex pathogenesis primarily driven by dysregulated immune responses and the overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-23 (Fig. 6f and Fig. 6g). These cytokines not only promote keratinocyte hyperproliferation but also perpetuate local and systemic inflammation. To assess the impact of SeHA-SF MNs on inflammatory regulation, we conducted immunohistochemical analysis to examine the expression of TNF-α in psoriatic lesions. As expected, the untreated IMQ-induced model exhibited significantly elevated TNF-α expression. However, treatment with the SeHA-SFN MNs resulted in a notable downregulation of TNF-α, indicating a potent anti-inflammatory response. Additionally, the IL-23/IL-17 axis, a critical contributor to psoriasis pathogenesis, was markedly activated in the IMQ model group, with significantly higher IL-23 and IL-17 levels compared to the normal group (p < 0.001). Although slight reductions in these cytokines were observed in the SHI cream and SeHA MNs groups, the SeHA-SFN MNs group demonstrated a more pronounced suppression, achieving cytokine levels comparable to those of healthy mice. SHI, a naphthoquinone derivative from Arnebiae Radix, possesses notable anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties [36,37]. Its therapeutic efficacy in psoriasis is largely attributed to modulation of the IL-23/IL-17 A axis, achieved through inhibition of IL-23 secretion, suppression of Th17 differentiation, antagonism of IL-17 A signaling, and restoration of skin barrier function [38,39]. Moreover, SHI attenuates activation of the JAK/STAT3 pathway, thereby reducing proinflammatory cytokine production, keratinocyte hyperproliferation, and immune cell infiltration, mitigating the pathological features of psoriasis [46]. The anti-inflammatory effects were evaluated by measuring serum inflammatory cytokines (Fig. S6). The levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were quantified using ELISA kits. In the IMQ-induced model group, serum cytokine levels were markedly elevated compared to the control group. However, treatment with SeHA-SFN MNs significantly reduced the expression of these inflammatory markers. Mechanistically, this synergistic anti-inflammatory effect can be attributed to the dual actions of SeHA and SFN. SeHA attenuates oxidative stress by scavenging excessive ROS and activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway [44]. Moreover, selenium supplement not only enhances the cellular antioxidant defense system but also suppresses ROS-dependent activation of NF-κB, a key pro-inflammatory transcription factor as reported [54]. Meanwhile, SHI further contributes by inhibiting the JAK/STAT3 pathway [[37], [38], [39]], thereby reducing inflammatory cell infiltration and cytokine release. Together, these findings elucidate the cooperative mechanism through which the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities of SeHA-SFN MNs synergistically ameliorate psoriatic inflammation.

We next characterized the influence of SeHA-SFN MNs treatment on immune cells from IMQ-induced psoriasis mice using flow cytometry in the spleen. As reported, the levels of CD2+, CD4+, CD8+ cells were found to be high in psoriasis with a markedly increased ratio of CD4+/CD8+ cells [55]. Compared to the IMQ model, SeHA-SFN MNs treatment significantly reduced the proportion of CD4+ T cells in the spleen and draining lymph nodes, suggesting a reversal of the inflammatory immune imbalance observed in untreated mice (Fig. 7a and Fig. 7b). Additionally, the role of macrophages, particularly CD11c + dendritic cells, in driving psoriatic inflammation was evaluated (Fig. 7c and Fig. 7d). The proportion of CD11c + dendritic cells was markedly elevated in the model group (25.5 %) compared to the normal group (11.6 %), signifying substantial macrophage activation. Notably, the SeHA-SFN MNs markedly suppressed this elevation, reducing the percentage of CD11c + cells to 14.2 %, highlighting its capacity to modulate innate immune responses and attenuate systemic inflammation.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that SeHA-SFN MNs exert a synergistic therapeutic effect on psoriasis through multi-level regulation. Histologically, they markedly reduced epidermal hyperplasia and restored skin architecture (Fig. 5b–g). At the molecular level, they attenuated oxidative stress by lowering ROS and elevating SOD, while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-23 (Fig. 6a–g). Immunologically, SeHA-SFN MNs rebalanced systemic immune responses by reducing CD4+ T cells and CD11c+ dendritic cells (Fig. 7a–c). This coordinated improvement across structural, molecular, and cellular dimensions underscores their robust potential for comprehensive psoriasis management.

The safety evaluation of SeHA-SFN MNs patches

To comprehensively assess the in vivo biosafety of the proposed treatment strategies, multiple evaluations were conducted to ensure their safe and effective application. An initial indicator of skin recovery following MNs administration was the monitoring of micropore closure on the skin surface. As shown in Fig. 8a, distinct puncture marks were observed immediately after MNs insertion. However, progressive healing was evident, with the majority of the micropores closing within 2 h, and complete epidermal restoration occurring within 36 h post-administration, indicating minimal skin disruption and a favorable healing profile. Systemic biosafety was further evaluated by monitoring the body weight of mice across all experimental groups throughout the study. As illustrated in Fig. 8b, all groups, except the healthy control, exhibited a reduction in body weight starting from day 3, attributed to the IMQ-induced inflammatory response. Notably, no significant differences in body weight were observed among the treatment groups, suggesting that SeHA-SFN MNs did not exacerbate disease-associated weight loss or induce additional adverse systemic effects. Furthermore, histopathological analysis of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, revealed no detectable abnormalities in the SeHA-SFN MN-treated group compared to the control group (Fig. 8c), further confirming the absence of treatment-related organ toxicity. To further assess the systemic biosafety of the treatment, comprehensive hematological analyses were conducted. As illustrated in Fig. 8d, psoriasis is often accompanied by erythema, scaling, and varying degrees of vascular endothelial damage, which are typically reflected in abnormal blood parameters, including elevated white blood cell (WBC) count, altered red blood cell (RBC) levels, decreased hemoglobin (HGB), and fluctuations in mean platelet volume (MPV). Notably, treatment with SeHA-SFN MNs effectively alleviated these hematological abnormalities, restoring the parameters toward normal physiological ranges, thereby confirming the systemic safety and therapeutic benefit of the microneedle-based intervention.

Safety assessment of SeHA-SFN MNs patches. (a) Closure of micropores on the skin surface after application of MN. (b) Body weight of mice in each group (c) Histopathological analysis of heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney organs. (d) Hematological analyses of whole blood of mice.

Our findings suggest that SeHA-SFN MNs exert therapeutic effects through multidimensional regulation of the psoriatic microenvironment. SeHA enhances Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defenses, reducing ROS accumulation and oxidative stress [44], which indirectly suppresses downstream inflammatory signaling. Meanwhile, tFNA-mediated delivery of SHI augments its anti-inflammatory efficacy and promotes apoptosis of hyperproliferative keratinocytes by inhibiting the IL-23/IL-17 axis and JAK/STAT3 pathway [[37], [38], [39]]. These coordinated actions reshape both the keratinocyte and immune-inflammatory microenvironments, attenuating epidermal hyperplasia, reducing cytokine overproduction, and restoring immune balance. Such synergistic regulation underlies the potent and comprehensive therapeutic effects of SeHA-SFN MNs against psoriasis. Taken together, these findings strongly demonstrate that SeHA-SFN MNs have good biocompatibility and biosafety, supporting their potential for safe and effective transdermal drug delivery in psoriasis treatment.

Conclusion

In summary, we successfully developed a SeHA dissolvable MNs patches loaded with SHI -encapsulated tFNAs for the targeted treatment of psoriasis. The biocompatible SeHA matrix not only facilitated effective transdermal penetration through the abnormally thickened psoriatic epidermis but also exhibited strong antioxidative effects, alleviating oxidative stress and minimizing inflammation. The SHI-loaded tFNAs demonstrated enhanced cellular uptake, effectively inhibiting keratinocyte hyperproliferation and inducing apoptosis, thereby addressing the hyperproliferative and inflammatory features of psoriasis. The SFN-SeHA MNs showed significant therapeutic efficacy in an imiquimod-induced psoriasis-like mouse model, reducing both phenotypic and histopathological manifestations, as well as downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-23, IL-17, and IL-6. Importantly, the biosafety assessment confirmed that the SeHA-SFN MNs did not induce systemic toxicity or adverse effects on major organs, supporting their suitability for clinical applications. Given its ability to deliver bioactive compounds while modulating the psoriatic microenvironment, this platform may serve as a foundation for the development of advanced therapeutic strategies for other inflammatory skin disorders. Future research could further explore the potential of this advanced system for broader dermatological applications.
