Influence of graded levels of glucosamine sulfate on growth performance, hematological and biochemical health biomarkers, carcass traits, and economic efficiency in broiler chickens
Authors: Ayman H Abd El-Aziz, Nagwa I El-Kasrawy, Islam M Youssef, Mohamed S El-Kholy, Mahmoud Moustafa, Mohammed Al-Shehri, Mohammad MH Khan, Mohamed E Abd El-Hack, Mahmoud M Abo Ghanima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of graded dietary levels of glucosamine sulfate (GS) on growth performance, hematological and biochemical parameters, carcass features, mineral profile, and economic efficiency in broiler chickens reared under Egyptian conditions. A total of 300 one-day-old, unsexed Cobb 500 chicks (average weight 41.8 ± 0.2 g) were randomly distributed into four groups: a control group receiving a basal diet without GS (GS0), and three treatment groups supplemented with 0.1 % (GS1), 0.2 % (GS2), or 0.3 % (GS3) GS. Each group had five replicates of 15 birds and was monitored for 42 days. Final body weight and total weight gain increased linearly (P < 0.001) with GS level, while feed intake decreased (P = 0.002) and feed conversion ratio improved (P < 0.001). Carcass yields, including breast, thigh, fillet, and internal organs, improved significantly (P < 0.05), while abdominal fat was unaffected (P = 0.093). GS supplementation also enhanced hematological indices, notably white blood cell count, lymphocyte and heterophil percentages, phagocytic activity, and phagocytic index. Serum biochemical analysis showed reduced levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), uric acid, and creatinine, and increased gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) activity (P < 0.01), indicating improved liver and kidney function. Additionally, GS increased serum globulin, ionized calcium, and phosphorus levels while reducing albumin, cholesterol, triglycerides, sodium, and chloride. No significant changes (P > 0.01) were observed in total protein, total calcium, potassium, or alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity. Economically, although feed costs increased, the highest net profit and economic efficiency were achieved in the GS3 group. These findings support the use of 0.3 % GS as a beneficial additive to enhance performance, health, and profitability in broiler production.Keywords: Broiler chickens, Glucosamine sulfate, Hematology, Carcass traits, Economic performance

Introduction

In intensive livestock and poultry farming, antibiotics have been widely used to stop diseases, cure and stimulate growth, which has improved production efficacy (Deeb et al., 2025; Abd El-Hack et al., 2025). Antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) have demonstrated effectiveness against inflammation-induced reductions in feed intake and productivity, but their long-term use has controlled the appearance of antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains and residues in animal products, thus raising concerns about food safety (Attia et al., 2024; Mohamed et al., 2025; Abou-Kassem et al., 2025). Accordingly, the search for sustainable, non-antibiotic AGP alternatives that can promote productivity as well as food safety is needed, especially those driven by regulatory constraints on AGP in animal feed (Kairalla et al., 2022a, b; Cufadar et al., 2024; Dosoky et al., 2024). Currently, widely exploited antibiotic alternatives include antimicrobial peptides, phytogenic extracts and probiotic supplementation (Youssef et al., 2023a, b, c; Kairalla et al., 2023; Rehman et al., 2025; Kairalla and Alshelmani, 2025). However, these alternatives are limited, e.g., the hemolytic characteristics of some antimicrobial peptides, the susceptibility of probiotics to inactivation, and the nonuniformity of bioactive compounds present in plant extracts for large-scale commercial applicability (El-Abasy et al., 2025).

Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) are a broad family of structurally complex polysaccharides characterized by the repetition of a disaccharide unit consisting of a hexosamine (glucosamine or galactosamine) and uronic acid (glucuronic or iduronic acid) or a galactose (Sgavioli et al., 2017). The high anionic nature of these compounds is responsible for some of their important biological activities in the control of inflammation, joint homeostasis and prevention of bone resorption. Among these sugars, D-glucosamine sulfate (GS), a natural amino sugar, represents the major precursor of glycosylated proteins and lipids. It is widely used as a food supplement, as it has been shown to protect the cartilaginous architecture and maintain the viscosity of synovial fluid, and as a drug in the treatment of osteoarthritis (Bruyère et al., 2016).

Glucosamine and its acetylated derivative N-acetyl glucosamine are endogenous amino sugars included in glycoproteins, proteoglycans and GAGs. In addition to its chondroprotective properties. Similarly, GS has antioxidative and anti-inflammatory consequences, suggesting that it may have wider therapeutic applications (Dalirfardouei et al., 2016). The addition of GS in the diet and its application in poultry have proven to be beneficial for broiler performance and gut morphology (Martins et al., 2020a). However, in layers, Wang et al. (2024) noted that feeding GS (0.2–0.6 %) to laying hens would have a beneficial effect on feed intake, egg production and shell quality through increasing shell strength, thickness and mineral deposition and increasing the expression of necessary matrix protein genes.

Moreover, GS has antibacterial growth-promoting potential and does not develop resistance; therefore, it is an environmental and economic alternate to antibiotics (Martins et al., 2020b; Saravanan et al., 2023; Soni et al., 2023). It enhances the elasticity of joint fluid, suppresses enzymes that affect cartilage, and supports proteoglycan synthesis, which indicates its ability to promote bone health (Henrotin et al., 2005; Wen et al., 2010). Furthermore, GS is a precursor of chondroitin sulfate, thus promoting GAG synthesis in connective tissues (Chan et al., 2006; Martins et al., 2023). The potential beneficial effects on bone and improvements in locomotor disorders have been described in some studies on different species, including broilers (Sgavioli et al., 2017; Santos et al., 2019; Martins et al., 2020a; Martins et al., 2023). Moreover humans, similar effects have been reported in more species, such as cattle (de Mattei et al., 2002), rabbits (Kamarul et al., 2011), as well as in in vitro studies. Notably, in addition to the membranes lining the eggshell, glycosaminoglycans have been recovered from such membranes (Nakano et al., 2001) and appear to also be involved in the shell structure (Liu et al., 2016). On the basis of these findings, dietary GS supplementation might increase eggshell quality by influencing the calcium metabolism and bone calcium deposition of laying hens.

GS is a commonly used feed ingredient in the biomedical, pharmaceutical, feed and nutraceutical industries (Soni et al., 2023). However, knowledge regarding its impact on broiler growth, carcass features, serum minerals and economic efficiency in the Egyptian poultry production industry is scarce. Hence, the current investigation was aimed to assess the possible consequences of graded levels of GS (1, 2, and 3 kg/ton) on performance, carcass features, and blood biochemical factors; to determine mineral retention and economic efficiency; and to benefit Egyptian poultry producers from the use of GS in broiler rations. The uniqueness of this work lies in the use of glucosamine sulfate (GS), a naturally occurring amino sugar known for promoting skeletal health and metabolic function, as a dietary supplement in broiler production. While its benefits are well documented in other contexts, its effectiveness in increasing growth performance and economic efficiency in broilers produced under Egyptian conditions has not been extensively examined. Furthermore, these chemicals are considered promising nutraceuticals for increasing performance, intestinal health and overall well-being in broiler in Egypt.

Materials and methods

Experimental, design, birds, management, and diets

All treatments and animal care procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval number: DMU/VetMed-2025/032) at Damanhour University, Egypt, and the bird management routine was conducted in accordance with the rules of the Animal Wealth Development Department’s Animal Care and Ethics Committee, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Damanhour University.

A total of 300 unsexed one-day-old healthy commercial Cobb500 broiler chicks were acquired from a local hatchery. Chicks were individually weighed, with an average initial body weight of 41.82±0.20 grams, and randomly assigned to four groups, with 75 birds per group. Each group was subdivided into five replicates, with 15 birds per replicate, in accordance with a completely randomized design (CRD). The control group was fed a basal diet (GS0) without any additive, whereas the second (GS1), third (GS2) and fourth (GS3) groups received 0.1 %, 0.2 % and 0.3 % glucosamine sulfate, respectively. The birds were kept in stainless steel cages that measured 100 × 90 × 45 cm (length × width × height) per replicate and included hand feeds and nipple water drinkers. All of the birds were reared utilizing similar management and immunization schedules, and they were given free access to food and water. To record and confirm the uniformity of the indoor temperature and relative humidity, manual thermometers and a hygrometer were positioned in the middle of the house. The dietary supplement glucosamine sulfate was obtained from Nutrakey Industries Inc. (Shangdong, China). The initial temperature was 33°C±1 throughout the first week and subsequently reduced till it reached 25°C±1 by the sixth week. The feed was formulated and provided according to the Cobb500 standards (Table 1). For the first three weeks, the birds were given a commercial starter diet, after that a commercial grower feed for additional three weeks. For the first three weeks, a commercial starter diet consisting of 23 % crude protein and 3000 kcal of ME was provided to the birds, which was then replaced by a grower diet of 20 % crude protein and 3200 kcal of ME for another three weeks.

Growth performance

During the experimental duration (1–42 days), birds were weighed weekly, and alterations in their live body weights (LBW) were documented. A record of bird feed consumption was maintained throughout the study. The feed conversion ratio (FCR) was computed as the feed intake (FI) alienated by body weight gain (BWG), and the body weight gain was also determined. Five birds were selected at random from each group at the end of the trial (one per replication), weighed, and then euthanized, plucked, and eviscerated before the dressed carcass was weighed. The dressing percentage was calculated as a percentage of the preslaughter weight. The liver, gizzard, spleen, breast, thigh, shoulder, left fillet and abdominal fat were also weighed, and their percentages were calculated relative to the live bird's weight.

Blood parameters

Five blood samples were taken from each group's wing vein at the 42nd day of the trial (one bird per replication) and placed in distinct, labeled centrifuge tubes. In the first, white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), differential leucocyte (DL), and hemoglobin value

(Hb) were determined utilizing 3.2 % sodium citrate solutions. Indicators and phagocytic activity were determined utilizing the techniques of Kawakhara et al. (1991). After letting the other tube clot, it was centrifuged for 15 minutes at 4500 × g prior to analysis, the serum was collected and stored at -20°C in a deep freezer. Following the manufacturer's instructions, the levels of the following parameters were measured using spectrophotometry (Spectronic 1201, Milton Roy, Ivyland, PA, USA): total protein, albumin, globulin, triglyceride, total cholesterol, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), urea, creatinine, uric acid, total calcium (Ca), ionized calcium, phosphorus, chloride, sodium, potassium, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP).

Economic efficiency

The prices of one kilogram of feed in the control, GS1 (0.1 % glucosamine sulfate), GS2 (0.2 % glucosamine sulfate) and GS3 (0.3 % glucosamine sulfate) groups were 22, 22.43, 22.86 and 23.29 EGP, respectively. The costs of birds, housing, vet care, utilities (water and electricity), and depreciation of buildings and equipment were classified as total fixed costs (TFC) and were consistent across all groups, as reported by Amarapurkar et al. (2014); therefore, they were excluded from our calculations. The total feed cost is the sum of the cost of the consumed diet, including the cost of the supplement. Feed cost per kg gain = total feed cost per bird (EGP)/total weight gain per bird (g). Total revenue per bird (EGP) = average live body weight × price of one kg. Net revenue per bird (EGP) = Total revenue per bird - Total feed cost per bird. Economic efficiency = Net revenue per bird/Total feed cost per bird (EGP), according to (Khayyal et al., 2017).

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 20 was used for data analysis. The influence of supplementing the birds' diet with different amounts of glucosamine sulfate (GS) was assessed using a one-way ANOVA. Tukey's b test was used to establish the statistical significance between the various supplemented groups at a P value of less than 0.05 or 0.001. Additionally, linear and quadratic responses to rising dietary GS levels were evaluated using orthogonal polynomial contrasts. The mean ± standard error of the mean is how the findings are displayed. Results

Growth performance

In a controlled feeding study, the broiler chicks' initial body weights in the various treatment groups were comparable (41–42 g, P=0.434), further supporting the idea of a uniform starting system (Table 2). The final body weight (FW) and total BWG, on the other hand, were significantly increased by dietary treatments (P < 0.001). They also increased linearly with increasing GS supplementation (P < 0.001), whereas the quadratic effect was not significant (P = 0. 339 and 0.345, respectively). At the maximum inclusion level (GS3; 3 kg/ton), broilers had a final weight of 2438.6 g and weight gain of 2396.67 g in approximately 8 % increments compared with control birds (GS0). Moreover, feed intake was significantly affected by dietary treatments (P = 0.002). It decreased linearly (P < 0.001) with increasing GS supplementation from 3469 g in the control to 3364 g at 3 kg/ton, while the quadratic effect was not significant (P = 0.319). However, FCR was significantly improved by dietary treatments (P < 0.001). It decreased linearly (P < 0.001) with increasing GS supplementation (from 1.57 for the control group to 1.40 at the highest GS dose). On the other hand, the quadratic effect was not significant (P = 0.326) for FCR.

Carcass features

The results in Table 3 showed that dietary GS inclusion had marked effects on most carcass yield attributes, indicating that GS can exert a favorable effect on carcass traits. Dressing percentage was significantly affected by dietary treatments (P < 0.001). It increased linearly with GS supplementation (P < 0.001) and also showed a quadratic response (P = 0.047), with the highest value recorded in GS3 (72.40 %). Also, liver, gizzard, and spleen percentages increased significantly with GS supplementation (P < 0.001). These traits demonstrated highly significant linear responses (P < 0.001 each), while quadratic effects were not significant (P = 0.161, 1.000, and 0.283, respectively). Similarly, breast yield (P < 0.001) and thigh percentage (P = 0.006) increased significantly across treatments. Both traits exhibited significant linear increases (P < 0.001 and P = 0.001, respectively), while quadratic effects were not significant (P = 0. 464 and P = 0. 622, respectively). Shoulder percentage also improved significantly (P < 0.001), showing a clear linear increase (P < 0.001) without a quadratic response (P = 1.000). Left fillet yield followed the same pattern, being significantly affected by treatments (P = 0.020) with a linear increase (P = 0.003), but no quadratic effect (P = 0.378).

Blood parameters

The influences of graded levels of GS on the hematological parameters of broilers are shown in Table 4. WBC counts were significantly affected by dietary treatments (P < 0.001), with a progressive linear increase (P < 0.001) and a tendency toward quadratic response (P = 0.051). Similarly, lymphocyte and heterophil percentages increased markedly across treatments (P < 0.001), showing a strong linear effect (P < 0.001) but no quadratic response (P = 0.989 and 0.164, respectively). Eosinophil percentage was increased by treatments (P = 0.025) and also increased linearly (P = 0.003), while no quadratic effect was detected (P = 0.789). Red blood cell count was not significantly affected by dietary GS supplementation (P = 0.536), with no meaningful linear (P = 0.190) or quadratic (P = 0.978) trends. In addition, hemoglobin concentration showed a tendency toward increase (P = 0.093), with a significant linear effect (P = 0.014) but no quadratic response (P = 0.800). The phagocytic activity (PA) was significantly influenced by dietary treatments (P = 0.043), increasing linearly (P = 0.008) without a quadratic effect (P = 0.327). Similarly, the phagocytic index (PI) was significantly improved (P < 0.001), showing a strong linear trend (P < 0.001), while the quadratic component was not significant (P = 0.607).

Liver and kidney functions

The liver and kidney function parameters of birds and the effects of dietary GS supplementation are revealed in Table 5. The values demonstrated that dietary GS influenced various serum biochemical hepatic and renal indicators. With respect to liver function, both AST (P = 0.003) and ALT (P = 0.004) activities decreased significantly, showing strong linear reductions (P < 0.001 and P = 0.001, respectively) without quadratic responses to GS administration (P = 0.738 and 0.639, respectively). Similarly, LDH activity was significantly lowered (P = 0.006) in GS-treated groups, following a linear decline (P = 0.001) with no quadratic effect (P = 0.440). In contrast, GGT activity increased significantly (P < 0.001), showing a clear linear rise (P < 0.001) without a quadratic response (P = 0.426) to GS treatments. Kidney function parameters also responded to GS dietary treatments. Both creatinine (P = 0.006) and uric acid (P < 0.001) concentrations were significantly decreased, with linear reductions (P = 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively) and no quadratic influences (P = 0.392 and 0.918, respectively). Meanwhile, urea concentration was not significantly affected (P = 0.069), though it showed a minor linear tendency toward reduction (P = 0.015) without quadratic effect (P = 0.658) of dietary GS levels.

Serum protein and lipid profiles

In the current trail, dietary GS supplements significantly influenced the serum ALB, globulin and lipid metabolism of broiler chickens (Table 6). Albumin concentration decreased significantly (P = 0.036), with a clear linear reduction (P = 0.004) and no quadratic response (P = 0.917). Conversely, globulin concentration increased markedly (P < 0.001) in GS-treated groups, following a strong linear trend (P < 0.001) and only a marginal quadratic tendency (P = 0.075). However, total protein was not affected by treatments (P = 0.698), with neither linear nor quadratic effects of GS levels (P = 0.337 and 0.505, respectively). Serum lipid profile was strongly improved by GS supplementation. Both cholesterol (P < 0.001) and triglycerides (P = 0.001) concentrations decreased significantly by GS treatments, showing consistent linear reductions (P < 0.001) without quadratic influences (P = 0.358 and 0.272, respectively).

Discussion

The poultry industry is looking for alternatives to AGPs increasingly to enhance the production and well-being of broilers. This tendency is propelled by controlling modifications and increasing apprehensions regarding antibiotic resistance (Abd El-Hack et al., 2024; Youssef et al., 2024a, b). Most importantly, preserving the structural and functional integrity of the gastrointestinal tract is essential for optimal growth performance and efficient utilization of dietary nutrients in broilers (Abdulla et al., 2019). Among the nutritional approaches explored for promoting gut health, polysulfated glycosaminoglycans, such as glucosamine sulfate or chondroitin sulfate, have gained attention for their ability to enhance intestinal integrity and physiological functions (Boleli and Morita, 2017; Martins et al., 2020a; Martins et al., 2020b; Martins et al., 2023). These substances, which are reported to have chondroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects (Salvatore et al., 2000; Santos et al., 2018; Santos et al., 2019), might also support better nutrient utilization and growth performance. Nevertheless, few studies have investigated their effects on broiler performance and economic parameters, especially in practical production environments in Egypt. Thus, this study's goal was to ascertain whether adding dietary glucosamine sulfate (GS) at varying doses (1–3 kg/t diet) would improve broiler growth performance, carcass features, hematological and biochemical parameters, and serum mineral contents—with a focus on the financial return. These compounds are considered promising nutraceuticals for enhancing gut health and overall well-being.

The results agree with other studies showing improved body weight performance in broilers in response to supplementation with glucosamine sulfate (0.3 %, 3 kg/ton diet), which led to significant weight gain increases from 1 to 42 d (P = 0.039) (Martins et al., 2020a). In contrast, supplementation with 5 % chitosan or 5 % glucosamine hydrochloride (GS-HCl) did not influence the growth performance or breast muscle yield of broilers. Although they might affect lipid metabolism, both chitosan and glucosamine HCl had no effect on body weight gain or feed efficiency during the experimental period (Kobayashi et al., 2006). Glucosamine sulfate supplementation also resulted in reduced inclusion levels in the diets of the layers and improved chemical nutrient absorption and utilization (Sgavioli et al., 2017). These findings are similar to the previous observations of an increased FCR with glucosamine supplementation (quadratic effect, P = 0.0123 at 0.08 % chondroitin inclusion) (Sgavioli et al., 2017). Conferring to Wang et al. (2024), the addition of glutamine sulfate linearly increased FI, laying rate and egg mass, and the egg weight, yolk quality and FCR.

Since glucosamine sulfate (GS) is the source of glycosaminoglycans, it could influence the health of the intestinal mucosa, thereby improving villus length and nutrient absorption. Minerals and other nutrients obtained from villi are central to growth and nutrient efficiency. According to Martins et al. (2020a), increased hepatic uridine diphosphate-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), which is a glycosylation substrate obtained from GS, also improves metabolic efficiency. The consequences of a previous study showed that supplementation with GS increased the villus height in the jejunum. This may be attributed to the effect of glucosamine on the ability of the epithelium to protect the body, improving intestinal structural function. On the other hand, in layers, 0.2–0.6 % glucosamine sulfate sodium improved early laying. Glucosamine improved the FI, laying rate, and egg mass, whereas the feed conversion ratio during the trial improved. The egg weight of hens that received GS also increased, as indicated in a previous study (Wang et al., 2024). Glucosamine improved the overall productive performance of the layers because it improved hen egg production and quality. Thus, glucosamine sulfate at 1–3 kg/ton improves the performance of birds without adverse effects and is thus effective.

Similarly, Martins et al. (2023) reported better carcass yield for broilers that received the supplementation of glucosamine and chondroitin sulfates, which is most likely a result of greater muscle deposition and, therefore, nutrient utilization. Higher liver and spleen weights could also indicate an increase in metabolic activity and immune development, since it was previously reported that glycosaminoglycan supplementation improves hepatic function and lymphoid organ development in poultry (Santos et al., 2018). Additionally, the heavier gizzard in GS may indicate better bowel development, which has been shown to be associated with higher feed use efficiency and nutrient digestibility. Although only a few experiments have reported standard carcass yield, glucosamine (often with chondroitin) is definitively helpful for maintaining skeletal integrity (Sgavioli et al., 2017). In broilers, glucosamine supplementation resulted in greater relative tibia weight and hypertrophied the growth plate cartilage in broiler males (Sgavioli et al., 2017), and supplementation of broilers with glucosamine (0.18 %) significantly increased the number of chondrocytes in tibial cartilage. These alterations indicate better bone growth, indirectly affecting carcass yield because of lower values for leg abnormalities. These results indicate that dietary GS supplementation at the 3 kg/ton level (GS3) is beneficial for increasing carcass yield and muscle growth in broiler chickens. Increases in these traits are associated with GS and the well‐established roles of GS in bone formation, nutrient utilization and muscle growth.

The results agree with the outcomes of Martins et al. (2023). According to the results, dietary GS was able to modulate several immune-related hematological parameters without causing damage to erythrocytes, as previously reported (Melillo, 2013). The WBC count significantly varied due to an increase in the level of GS inclusion, these increases in WBCs and lymphocytes may indicate enhancement of the birds’ immune state, which can be attributed to the immunomodulatory effects of GS, as previously discussed in the poultry and mammalian literature (Martins et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024).

Increased phagocytosis competence indicates better functionality of macrophages and heterophils, which are known to be essential mediators of pathogen elimination and immune protection. Glucosamine also has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. In fact, one recent review concluded that glucosamine has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and immune-stimulating properties in experimental animal models. In vitro, GS can chelated free Fe²⁺ and scavenge reactive oxygen species (including decreasing malondialdehyde formation), which suggests that it might be able to reduce oxidative stress (Chen et al., 2025). These properties may indicate the potential of glucosamine to support antioxidant status in poultry, but trials measuring SOD, CAT, GSH or MDA in glucosamine-supplemented birds have not been reported thus far. Moreover, the anti-inflammatory mechanism of action of GS is suggested to occur via the reserve of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), a main transcription factor linked to inflammation (Yomogida et al., 2008; Bak et al., 2014). GS diminishes IL-8, IL-1β, and TNF-α in synovial fibroblasts by inhibiting NF-κB activation, as previously shown (Bak et al., 2014). These anti-inflammatory effects might be due to the sulfate and carboxyl groups in glucosamine binding to the NF-κB protein structure (Campo et al., 2009). The dampening of intestinal inflammation by glucosamine likely leads to protection of mucosal integrity, increased cell proliferation in the crypts of the intestine, and preservation of the villus structure. Taken together, these results suggest that dietary GS supplementation at concentrations as high as 3 kg/ton (GS3) has beneficial immunomodulatory effects on broiler chickens by increasing cellular immune responses and innate immune function but has no negative effects on broiler health.

The values demonstrated that dietary GS had an influence on various serum biochemical hepatic and renal indicators, which is consistent with findings in broilers fed glycosaminoglycans (Martins et al., 2023). These results agree with similar observations of liver safety in (Martins et al., 2023), where an absence of hepatotoxicity was evidenced by unchanging or decreasing serum levels of AST and GGT found to remain within a normal range. Even though GGT is frequently referred to as a marker of cholestasis, Martins et al. (2023) reported that the changes in GGT activity were within acceptable physiological ranges and could not represent lesions of the liver. In contrast, this mild increase was considered to result from potential changes in bile metabolism rather than the presence of a disease. Additionally, a significant reduction in serum LDH activity, an additional indicator of tissue integrity, occurred in birds fed GS-containing diets, indicating that GS may be involved in cellular stability and tissue protection. Moreover, the decrease in uric acid (the main nitrogenous waste in poultry) may indicate better renal excretion or even minor protein catabolism, which is consistent with the potential nephroprotective role of glycosaminoglycans described by (Martins et al., 2023). However, the decrease in the numerical values emphasized that GS could help improve renal function. In conclusion, the outcomes of this investigation revealed that the addition of up to 0.3 % glucosamine sulfate did not have unfavorable effects on liver or kidney function in broilers. However, the decreases in major hepatic enzymes and nitrogenous waste products obtained indicate that glucosamine might have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant protective effects on hepatic and renal physiology (Calamia et al., 2014). Collectively, these results confirm the safety of dietary GS supplementation and highlight its potential role in improving metabolic health in broiler chickens.

As reported in Martins et al. (2023), GS promoted a quadratic effect in albumin at 42 days, with the smallest amount being estimated when 0.06 % glucosamine sulfate was added. These findings are consistent with earlier reports emphasizing that changes in specific serum proteins, particularly globulins, are often reflective of an enhanced immune response rather than pathological alterations (Melillo, 2013). Despite the decrease in albumin, total serum protein concentrations were within the physiological range for broilers (2.5–4.5 g/dL) reported in Thrall (2007), Bak et al. (2014), Martins et al. (2023), indicating that the alterations were mainly a shift among proteins rather than a sign of reduced protein synthesis or poor health status. Previous studies have suggested that glucosamine may have a positive effect on lipid metabolism through lipid absorption or hepatic lipid processing. These hypolipidemic effects are desirable in poultry production, where they may lead to increased metabolic efficiency and carcass quality. In a previous study Kobayashi et al. (2006), 14-day-old broilers were fed a 5 % chitosan or glucosamine hydrochloride (G-HCl) diet for three weeks. There was no enhancement in growth or breast muscle yield in response to any treatment, but chitosan and G-HCl reduced the hepatic lipid and total glyceride (TG) contents, and G-HCl also decreased the plasma VLDL concentration and abdominal fat pad yield. Only chitosan suppressed fat digestibility, indicating that it exerts its antilipogenic effect through inhibiting the intestinal absorption and hepatic synthesis of lipids, whereas G-HCl inhibits lipogenesis mainly by inhibiting hepatic TG secretion. Furthermore, consistent serum concentrations of hepatic markers, such as AST and GGT, correlate with hepatic well-being (Thrall, 2007). Slight differences in these enzymes were observed in this study, but the values were still within the physiological range tested, which is evidence that the doses of glucosamine sulfate used do not cause either liver compromise or hepatotoxicity. Taken together, immune protein fractions, lipid profile improvement, and unaltered liver enzymes indicate that 0.3 % GS supplementation is safe and beneficial to the metabolic status, immune response and productive performance of broilers.

In addition to the literature on mineral digestibility and bone development, these findings substantiate the function of GS as a functional addition to enhance mineral utilization and skeletal health in chickens (Martins et al., 2023). Nutritional supplementation with glucosamine in poultry has also been shown to influence the metabolism of minerals, especially calcium and phosphorus, by facilitating the absorption and employment of these minerals for skeletal advancement as well as egg shell quality. The addition of glucosamine to the diets of broiler and laying hens reportedly rises the deposition of Ca and phosphorus into the tibia and eggshell, respectively. These enhancements are believed to be due to the positive effects of glucosamine on intestinal integrity and mineral absorption as well as its potential role in organizing the structure of bone and eggshell matrices (Martins et al., 2020a). Total calcium was statistically similar across the GS groups, which is consistent with previous reports where dietary GAGs did not consistently affect bulk serum calcium (Martins et al., 2023). Conversely, ionic calcium increased linearly with increasing GS concentration. These findings are consistent with those of calcium Martins et al. (2023), who described that ionic calcium was considerably greater in the serum of broilers fed glucosamine sulfate. A significant dose-dependent increase in the serum phosphorus concentration was also noted. This finding is consistent with reported improvements in mineral metabolism in broilers supplemented with glucosamine, in which phosphorus increased, and calcium increased. Also, according to Martins et al. (2023), a decrease in chloride content on day 21 and an increase in sodium content on day 42 were reported according to the glucosamine level. These variations could represent changes in renal handling or electrolyte‒water balance during metabolic adaptation.

There were no significant changes in potassium or alkaline phosphatase activity. This implies that GS altered the metabolism of certain ions and not general electrolytes or bone turnover markers, as also observed in other studies where AP activity continued to be unaltered during similar treatments. This mineral deposition seems to be specifically due to an elevation in ionic calcium and phosphorus without a change in total calcium and suggests an increase in the availability of bioactive minerals, possibly indirectly via improved absorption or mobilization. This finding is in line with indications that glycosaminoglycans improve mineral digestibility and serum mineral levels in broilers, as reported previously (Martins et al., 2020b). Moreover, the electrolyte changes (decreased Cl⁻, Na⁺) are within the physiological range and could reflect transient homeostatic perturbations rather than toxic effects. In addition, the stability of potassium and alkaline phosphatase suggests that dietary GS does not cause systemic toxicity or dysregulation of bone turnover at the doses utilized in the current experiment. Furthermore, to affect gut health, glucosamine, a glycosaminoglycan, has chemical properties that are positively related to mineral metabolism. These elements possess sulfate and carboxylic groups that provide high negative charge densities, which help them interact with positively charged ions like Ca and Na, allowing materials to be immobilized (Hernandez et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2017). This ion-binding capacity is thought to improve mineral accessibility in the gastrointestinal system. According to Hernandez et al. (2015), these interactions transpire via functional groups, with glucosamine sulfate predominantly employing sulfate groups for cation binding, where the binding efficiency is contingent upon aspects such as the molecular structure and concentration. Thus, supplementation of glucosamine sulfate in the diet of broilers might contribute to enhancing mineral utilization, particularly that of calcium, due to the formation of complexes of complex ions, which are essential. Overall, as minerals are involved in bone development, metabolic control and enzymatic reactions (Silva and Pascoal, 2014), increased mineral digestibility probably underpins the improvement in body weight gain and growth observed performance. These results harmonize with earlier findings, which described that improved mineral absorption led to improved growth performance in broilers (Islam et al., 2012; Abdulla et al., 2016; Gautier et al., 2018). In addition to its function in mineral utilization, glucosamine might act as a task in promoting growth performance across its consequence on nutrient transport and key metabolic pathways. Once in the cell, glucosamine is phosphorylated and engaged in the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, which increases the synthesis of uridine diphosphate-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), a vital substrate for protein glycosylation (Ryczko et al., 2016). Correct protein glycosylation is critical for normal cellular function and metabolism, and growth is a function of metabolism.

Feed cost per kilogram gain decreased at the highest GS concentration, indicating improved biological and economic efficiency. As far as we are aware, no findings have explicitly calculated the cost–benefit or profitability of glucosamine sulfate in broiler chickens’ diets. However, the performance benefits imply potential economic gains. Improved weight gain and egg production can increase revenue, whereas better skeletal health may reduce culling and losses. Sgavioli et al. (2017), for instance, pointed out that glucosamine combined with chondroitin may improve leg problems and reduce losses in money in the broiler business.

Conclusions

Glucosamine sulfate, particularly at a dietary level of 0.3 %, is a scientifically validated nutraceutical that promotes broiler growth, improves carcass attributes, optimizes blood biochemical parameters, enhances immunity, facilitates bone mineralization, and regulates mineral metabolism while also reducing cholesterol and triglycerides, increasing the economic value of broiler chickens (Cobb 500) reared under the Egyptian production system. Existing studies are limited to broilers, pigs and laying hens, with future research warranted in ruminants and other livestock species. Furthermore, studies should extend beyond production performance to elucidate the regulatory effects of GS on animal health. The precise mechanisms by which glucosamine improves animal production remain unclear, necessitating further investigation to determine its mode of action and optimal dosing, thereby promoting its broader application in animal production.
