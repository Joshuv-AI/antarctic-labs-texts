Prognostic factors for long-term survival in Wilms tumor: a retrospective study
Authors: Kongfeng Shao, Haojie Zhu, Xijin Lin, Zhuhong Chen, Hui Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWilms tumor (WT) is the most common renal tumor in children, with prognosis varying widely due to multiple factors. This study aims to identify key prognostic factors in WT, with a particular focus on pathological grade.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 186 WT patients diagnosed between 2000 and 2021 using SEER database. Clinical and pathological factors such as age, tumor grade, and SEER stage were assessed for their impact on overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival using Cox regression models. The SEER stage was categorized as localized, regional, or distant. A nomogram was developed based on these factors to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, and its predictive accuracy was evaluated.ResultsOf the total, 94 patients had grade I-III tumors, while 92 had grade IV tumors. Univariate analysis revealed that older age (p < 0.001), higher grade (p = 0.011), distant metastasis (p < 0.001), and larger tumor size (p = 0.025) were associated with poorer prognosis. Multivariate analysis confirmed age at diagnosis (p < 0.001), tumor grade (p = 0.008), and stage (p = 0.004) as independent prognostic factors for OS. In the grade IV subgroup, patients had a higher rate of distant metastasis (37.0%), which significantly worsened their prognosis (p = 0.004). The nomogram demonstrated a strong predictive accuracy, with a C-index of 0.796 and AUC values of 0.886, 0.790, and 0.835 for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival, respectively. Calibration and decision curve analyses further validated the model’s reliability.ConclusionWT prognosis is closely associated with age, tumor grade, and SEER stage. The nomogram developed in this study serves as a reliable tool for predicting long-term survival in WT patients, aiding in clinical management and personalized treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06289-x.Keywords: Wilms tumor, Pathological grade, Overall survival, SEER

Introduction

Wilms tumor (WT), also known as nephroblastoma, is the most common renal tumor in children, accounting for over 90% of pediatric cases [1]. In contrast, it constitutes less than 1% of adult renal tumors [2]. The prognosis for most WT patients is favorable, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate exceeding 90% [3], however, certain subgroups, such as diffuse anaplastic Wilms tumor (DAWT) and relapsed or refractory WT, are considered high-risk and have significantly worse long-term outcomes [4]. Despite advancements in modern medical treatments, the long-term OS for patients with stage IV or DAWT remains suboptimal with less than 50% OS rate [5, 6].

The previous prognostic models for WT have been developed using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, primarily focusing on pediatric populations. A Cox regression analysis by Tan et al. identified older age (hazard ratio, HR: 1.119, 95% CI: 1.039–1.206), higher lymph node density (LND) (HR: 4.465, 95% CI: 1.99–10.017), and advanced stage (HR: 1.673, 95% CI: 0.661–4.239; HR: 3.603, 95% CI: 1.455–8.923) as adverse prognostic factors in children with WT [7]. Walker et al. focused on lymph node-positive patients, finding that tumor size and LND are significant predictors of OS [8]. Additionally, Elgenidy et al. emphasized that age and tumor stage are independent prognostic factors for WT [9]. However, a critical and consistently neglected component in these SEER-based models is pathological grade.

The data from the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) have demonstrated that anaplastic histology has a distinct poor prognosis compared to favorable histology (4-year OS: 73.7% vs. >90%) [3]. Anaplastic WT has a poorer prognosis [4–6] and may also be associated with higher rates of TP53 mutations and copy number alterations (CNA), leading to chemotherapeutic resistance [10, 11]. Therefore, incorporating pathological grade is crucial for developing a more comprehensive prognostic model.

We here conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical and pathological data on WT using the SEER database. Our objectives were to identify key factors influencing OS and cancer-specific survival (CSS) and to create a prognostic prediction tool based on a multivariate model. By enrolling variables such as age at diagnosis, tumor stage, with a particular emphasis on pathological grade, we further elucidated their roles in WT prognosis, aiming to develop a more accurate tool for clinical management.

Methods

Data sources

Data for this study were obtained from the SEER database. The data agreement form was submitted to SEER, and data extraction was performed using SEER*Stat version 8.4.3. We accessed the SEER Research Plus Data, which includes information from 17 cancer registries covering diagnoses from 2000 to 2021, with the latest update in November 2023. Utilizing the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology 3 (ICD-O-3) histology code 8960 which denotes nephroblastoma, and requiring definitive pathological grade, we identified a total of 204 cases.

From the initial dataset, patients were excluded based on the following criteria: non-kidney sites (n = 4), unknown SEER stage (n = 3), multiple tumors (n = 1), and unknown lymph node status (n = 10). After applying these exclusions, 186 patients remained for further analysis. A flowchart detailing the patient selection process is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Collected variables included age at diagnosis, sex, race, tumor grade, laterality, SEER stage, treatment type (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy), tumor size, and the number of regional lymph nodes examined and positive.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and tumor characteristics. Optimal cut-off values for continuous variables (tumor size: 160 mm; lymph node density: 0.08) were determined using the ‘survminer’ R package by maximizing the log-rank statistic. Comparisons between grade I-III and grade IV were conducted using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to identify significant prognostic factors for OS and CSS, with p-values < 0.05 considered significant.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to visually represent survival outcomes across different patient characteristics. These variables were further utilized in a Cox regression model to create forest plots and nomograms. The nomograms’ discriminatory ability was assessed using the concordance index (C-index), and model performance was evaluated with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Calibration curves compared predicted probabilities with actual outcomes, and decision curve analysis (DCA) assessed the clinical utility of the nomogram.

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and data visualization was performed using R software version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Demographic and tumor characteristics

Between 2000 and 2021, a total 186 patients with WT were included in this study. The demographic and clinicopathological characteristics were summarized in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 4 years (inter-quartile range, IQR: 2–6 years), with 119 patients (64.0%) aged 3 to 17 years. Among them, 101 were male (54.3%) and 147 were white (79.0%). Of the total, 94 patients (50.5%) had low-grade tumors (grade I-III), while 92 (49.5%) had high-grade (grade IV). Tumors were located on the right side in 82 patients (44.1%), on the left side in 95 (51.1%), and were bilateral in 9 (4.8%). Distant metastasis was observed in 50 patients (26.9%). Treatment modalities included surgery in 184 patients (98.9%), chemotherapy in 172 (92.5%), and radiotherapy in 101 (54.3%). The median tumor size was 108.50 mm (IQR: 75.25–139.75 mm). An average of 3 lymph nodes were examined per patient, with a positivity rate of 22.0%.

Comparison of characteristics by pathological grade

We compared the characteristics of 94 patients with grade I-III WT to those of 92 with grade IV. As shown in Table 2, high-grade tumors were more common among older children, with 74 patients (80.4%) aged 3 to 17 years, a significantly higher proportion than in the low-grade group (p < 0.001). SEER stage also differed between the groups, with half of the low-grade tumors being localized, while high-grade tumors had a substantially higher incidence of distant metastasis (37.0% vs. 17%, p = 0.001). Additionally, a significantly higher proportion of patients in the high-grade group received radiotherapy compared to the low-grade group (77.2% vs. 31.9%, p < 0.001). Although the rate of regional lymph node positivity was higher in patients with high-grade tumors (27.2% vs. 17.0%), this difference was not statistically significant.

Survival analysis of the Wilms tumor cohort

To further investigate the impact of clinical variables on WT prognosis, we performed a Cox regression analysis in Table 3. In the univariate analysis for OS, age, grade, SEER stage, and tumor size all influenced OS significantly. Tumor risk increased with age, with adults showing an HR of 12.555 (95% CI: 3.661–43.049, p < 0.001). High-grade tumors were associated with a markedly higher risk compared to low-grade tumors (HR = 4.734, 95% CI: 1.421–15.776, p = 0.011), and distant metastasis also presented a greater risk (HR = 5.206, 95% CI: 2.034–13.324, p < 0.001). In the multivariate analysis for OS, age (p < 0.001), grade (p = 0.008), and SEER stage (p = 0.006) remained significant, while tumor size (p = 0.592) did not. Similar findings were observed in the multivariate analysis for CSS, where age (p < 0.001), grade (p = 0.010), and tumor stage (p = 0.005) remained relevant, but tumor size (p = 0.280) did not.

Survival trends via Kaplan-Meier curves

The 1-year, 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year OS rates for the entire cohort were 93.76%, 86.15%, 83.07%, and 81.57%, respectively, while CSS were 94.86%, 88.30%, 85.75%, and 84.21%, showing a similar trend (Fig. 1 A). We generated survival curves for OS based on variables such as age, grade, SEER stage, and tumor size (Figs. 1B-E). Adults (p < 0.001), high-grade tumors (p = 0.030), distant metastasis (p < 0.001), and tumors > 160 mm (p = 0.020) all indicated poorer prognosis, with survival curves leveling off after five years of follow-up. Compared to the whole cohort, the survival rate for high-grade WT was the poorest differences based on SEER stage (Fig. 1 F, p = 0.004).

For the entire Wilms Tumor cohort, the 1-, 3-, 5-, and 10-year OS were 93.76%, 86.15%, 83.07%, and 81.57%, respectively, while CSS were 94.86%, 88.30%, 85.75%, and 84.21%, reflecting a similar pattern (A). Kaplan-Meier curves showed that older age, higher tumor grade, advanced SEER stage, and larger tumor size were associated with poorer OS (B-E). In high-grade tumors, distant metastasis significantly worsened prognosis (F)

Nomogram construction and validation

Based on the results from the Cox multivariate analysis in Table 3, we developed a refined Cox model that includes age, grade, and SEER stage, excluding tumor size. The forest plot (Fig. 2 A) demonstrated that age (p < 0.001), grade (p = 0.008), and distant metastasis (p = 0.004) serve as independent prognostic factors. Utilizing these factors, we constructed a nomogram to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS as shown in Fig. 2B. The nomogram achieved a C-index of 0.796 in the entire cohort. The model’s accuracy was further evidenced by the area under the curve (AUC) values (1-year OS: 0.886, 95% CI: 0.830–0.942, 3-year OS: 0.790, 95% CI: 0.694–0.887, and 5-year OS: 0.835, 95% CI: 0.752–0.918) displayed in Fig. 2C. Calibration curves in Fig. 2D demonstrated a strong consistence between the predicted and observed survival rates for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS. Additionally, the DCA demonstrated that the model provided clinical net benefit at threshold probabilities of 0.6–19.3%, 1.6–86.2%, and 2.0–92.4.0.4% for 1-, 3-, and 5-year predictions, with corresponding maximum net benefits of 0.057, 0.127, and 0.155 (Fig. 2E), suggesting the improved performance with longer follow-up duration.

Discussion

WT predominantly affects children. The treatment strategies by the COG and the International Society of Paediatric Oncology (SIOP) were well acknowledged. Both organizations report similar survival outcomes [12]. SIOP further classifies WT into intermediate-risk tumors with focal anaplasia and high-risk with diffuse anaplasia [13], emphasizing the prognostic importance of histological components. This study incorporates pathological grade into the model to develop a more comprehensive and rational prognostic tool, an aspect not considered in previous studies [7–9, 14].

Our results indicate that higher pathological grade is linked to worse prognosis (Fig. 1 C), with high-grade tumors carrying a significantly higher risk compared to low-grade tumors (HR: 6.228, 95% CI: 1.615–24.022, p = 0.008). The COG-led AREN0532 study reported that stage I favorable histology WT had a 4-year event-free survival (EFS) of 89.7% (95% CI: 84.1–95.2%) and an OS of 100% [15], while stage III favorable histology WT had a 4-year EFS of 88% (95% CI: 85–91%) and an OS of 97% (95% CI: 95–98%) [16]. In comparison, a combined retrospective analysis of the UK SIOP-WT-2001 and IMPORT study cohorts found that DAWT had a 4-year EFS of 51% (95% CI: 41–63%) and an OS of 58% (95% CI: 48–70%), whereas focal anaplastic Wilms tumor (FAWT) had a 4-year EFS of 88% (95% CI: 76–100%) and an OS of 95% (95% CI: 86–100%), highlighting the poorer prognosis within diffuse anaplasia subtype [17]. Pathological features are significant prognostic indicators for WT, although the SEER database provides less detailed grading, with grade IV encompassing both undifferentiated and anaplastic categories.

Age is a particularly intriguing factor, with prognosis worsening as the age of onset increases [7, 9, 14, 18]. In our study, the 5-year OS for adult patients was only 36.36%, with an HR of 20.362 (95% CI: 5.199–79.745, p < 0.001). The prognosis for adult WT is significantly worse than for pediatric patients (5-year OS: 69% vs. 88%, p < 0.001) [19]. This discrepancy may be due to the rarity of adult WT and the lack of evidence-based treatment protocols, which often rely on pediatric experience [20, 21].

Most tumors have a worse prognosis in later stages, particularly with distant metastases, and WT is no exception [7, 9]. Distant metastasis is a poor prognostic factor (HR: 4.306, 95% CI: 1.579–11.742, p = 0.004), with a 5-year OS of 65.84% across the entire cohort. In the high-grade WT subgroup, the prognosis further declines to 56.77%, with long-term survival rates falling below 50%. The study from Nakata et al. also supports that metastatic tumors (p = 0.0004) are an adverse prognostic factor for OS[18]. The fifth National Wilms’ Tumor Study (NWTS-5) also reported a higher rate of distant metastasis in anaplastic WT, with long-term survival for DAWT at only 30%, consistent with our findings [22]. Consistently, a pooled analysis of COG trials (AREN0533, AREN03B2) and NWTS-5 reported that patients with favorable histology WT and extrapulmonary metastases had a reduced 4-year EFS of 77.3% and an OS of 88.9% [23], comparing to non-metastatic cases [15–17].

Other factors have also been mentioned as potentially influencing tumor prognosis, such as tumor size [8] and LND [7, 8, 24]. Tumor size was significant in univariate analysis (p = 0.025), but not in multivariate analysis (OS, p = 0.592; CSS, p = 0.280). In contrast to previous studies [7, 8, 25], our analysis did not identify LND as a significant prognostic factor (p = 0.219). Since neither factor follows a normal distribution, we calculated optimal cutoff values for classification analysis. However, tumor size and LND may interact with pathological grade and tumor stage, which might explain why they did not show predictive value for prognosis.

At the molecular level, WT also warrants attention. The NWTS-5 revealed that patients with loss of heterozygosity at chromosomes 1p and 16q had poorer outcomes [26]. In the context of anaplastic WT, TP53 mutations, which are significantly associated with DAWT histology, play a critical role [27–30]. These mutations or the accompanying loss of heterozygosity are typically linked to increased CNA load and more DNA double-strand breaks [10, 11]. Furthermore, a study by Su et al. revealed that DAWT, which accounts for one-third of diffuse anaplastic cases, is characterized by immune/stromal cell depletion, TP53 alterations, and cGAS-STING pathway downregulation, suggesting that these tumors may be treated with HDAC and/or WEE1 inhibitors [31]. Considering the limited improvement in prognosis for anaplastic WT, exploring alternative therapeutic approaches seems necessary.

The prognostic tool developed in this study, based on a multivariable Cox model, demonstrated high predictive accuracy with strong discriminative performance at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals, as reflected by the C-index and AUC values. Compared with previously reported SEER-based models, our study highlights the prognostic value of pathological grade, and the model shows greater applicability for long-term follow-up. This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design and use of the SEER database. The generalizability of our findings may be affected by the missing data, for example, pathological grade was documented in only 204 of the 2,813 patients. In addition, data on molecular markers and patient height were not available, precluding more analysis on adjusted survival and tumor size analysis. While representing a meaningful advancement in prognostic prediction, the lack of external validation should be acknowledged, and further assessment in larger, multicenter cohorts is warranted to confirm the model’s stability and clinical utility.

In conclusion, this study established age, tumor grade, and SEER stage as key prognostic factors for WT and presented a reliable long-term prognostic prediction tool. Future research should focus on developing more personalized treatment strategies, particularly for high-risk patients, to further improve long-term survival outcomes.
