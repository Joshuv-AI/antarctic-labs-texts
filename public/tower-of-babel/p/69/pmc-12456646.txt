Thoracic epidural anesthesia combined with pecs 2 block for modified radical mastectomy: A safe and effective alternative to general anesthesia in high-risk patients
Authors: Raed S. Altowairki, Muzackir Abubaker Mohammed, Mohammed I. Aljalsi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among Saudi women, with Modified Radical Mastectomy (MRM) as a standard treatment, typically performed under general anesthesia (GA). However, GA poses significant risks to patients with multiple comorbidities. We present a 66-year-old female with invasive ductal carcinoma, complicated by scleroderma with interstitial lung disease, chemotherapy-induced heart failure, and hypertension, making GA a high-risk option. To optimize perioperative safety, thoracic epidural anesthesia (TEA) combined with a pectoralis nerve block (PECS2) was used instead. This approach provided effective surgical anesthesia, hemodynamic stability, and minimized opioid use. The patient tolerated the procedure well, with intraoperative hypotension managed by vasopressor support. She resumed oral intake within 6 hours, required minimal postoperative opioids, and was discharged on the third postoperative day without complications. This case highlights the feasibility and advantages of TEA with PECS2 block as a safe and effective alternative to GA in high-risk MRM patients. By reducing GA-related pulmonary and cardiac risks, improving postoperative pain control, and shortening hospital stays, this technique may enhance perioperative outcomes in select patients. Further studies are warranted to support the wider adoption of regional anesthesia in oncologic breast surgeries.

Introduction

In 2020, Saudi Arabia reported 17,631 cancer cases, with 14,235 among Saudi nationals. Females had a higher incidence (9,575 cases, 54.3%), particularly in the 45–59 age group (35.0%). Breast cancer was the most common malignancy in women, accounting for 2,459 cases (31.4% of female cancers, 17.8% of all cancers), with an age-standardized rate (ASR) of 28.4 per 100,000.[1] Modified Radical Mastectomy (MRM) is the most common treatment that accounts for 70.8% of mastectomies,[2] typically performed under general anesthesia.

Case Presentation

A 66-year-old female with left-sided invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC, grade 2) and multiple axillary lymph nodes was referred for a modified radical mastectomy (MRM). Her history included scleroderma with interstitial lung disease (diagnosed 2 years ago), dyslipidemia, and hypertension. She previously underwent cholecystectomy and hysterectomy without complications. She received weekly neoadjuvant chemotherapy, but radiotherapy was avoided due to active scleroderma. She later developed chemotherapy-induced heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (NYHA Class II) over the past year.

On the morning of the day of the surgery, her heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), respiratory rate (RR), and oxygen saturation (SpO2) were 91 beats/min, 99/65 mmHg, 20/min, and 96%, respectively. The patient’s height was 159 cm, and she weighed 90.5 kg. Her investigations were within normal limits except for echocardiography, which was suggestive of left ventricular hypertrophy, preserved ejection fraction of more than 55%, abnormal left ventricular relaxation, and a dilated right atrium. Her chest radiograph showed bilateral interstitial vascular marking, mid to lower lung zone peripheral ground-glass opacities, and mild cardiomegaly. Her airway examination revealed Mallampati Class III, adequate mouth opening, and full neck range of motion. Due to her condition, she was counselled for thoracic epidural anesthesia (TEA) and pectoralis nerve block (PECS2), and written consent was taken.

In the operating room, Standard american society of anesthesiologists (ASA) monitors applied to the patient include a pulse oximeter, electrocardiography (ECG), and a noninvasive blood pressure cuff. Baseline values were as follows: HR: 118/min, BP: 130/54 mmHg, and SpO2: 97%. Under full aseptic and antiseptic techniques, an arterial line was inserted into the right brachial artery. A dose of 50 mcg of fentanyl and 2 mg of midazolam was given to the patient before inserting the 7fr triple lumen central line into the right internal jugular vein. A midline thoracic epidural was performed in a sitting position at the T6/T7 intervertebral space using an 18G Tuohy epidural needle. The targeted space was identified by loss of resistance to saline. The skin-to-epidural depth was 6 cm. Around 12 cm of the epidural catheter was fixed at the skin and adhered to the back.

After a test dose of 3 ml of 5% lidocaine, a loading dose of 5 ml of 0.5% bupivacaine was given slowly. Under Ultrasound guidance, a PECS2 block was performed. A dose of 20 ml, one shot of 0.25% bupivacaine, and 15 mcg of dexmedetomidine were injected. Oxygen was administered via a face mask at 8 L/min throughout the surgery. A sensory block height of T1 to T7 was achieved bilaterally (assessed by pinprick), which allowed the surgery to start. A top-up of 5 ml of 0.5% bupivacaine and 100 mcg of fentanyl were given by epidural catheter. A sedation infusion of dexmedetomidine at a rate of 0.9 mcg/kg/h was started before the skin incision.

The left breast tissues and tumor were excised, including the left axilla lymph node. Towards the end of the surgery, she complained of mild pain around the mid-clavicular line, which was effectively managed with an additional 5 ml of 0.5% bupivacaine, and the epidural removed at the end. As part of multimodal analgesia, the patient received 60 mg of ketamine, 30 mg of ketorolac, 1 g of paracetamol, and 2.5 g of magnesium throughout the surgery. The duration of the surgery was around 120 min. The estimated blood loss was 250 ml, and no blood transfusion was required. The patient remained hemodynamically stable, with episodes of hypotension managed with norepinephrine infusion at 0.7 mcg/kg/min and phenylephrine boluses of 100 mcg.

The patient was monitored in the ICU due to episodes of hypotension (MAP 63), requiring low-dose norepinephrine infusion. Pain was well controlled with fentanyl patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) and intravenous paracetamol. She resumed oral intake within six hours postoperatively and required minimal PCA use (30 mcg total), reporting a pain score of 2/10. PCA was discontinued on postoperative day 2, and she was discharged without complications on day 3.

Discussion

This report highlights TEA with PECS2 block as a safe and effective alternative to general anesthesia (GA) for MRM, particularly in high-risk patients. Interscalene block was avoided due to its potential 20% reduction in respiratory function. TEA with PECS2 provided hemodynamic stability, reduced opioid use, superior analgesia, lower postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and faster recovery, minimizing cardiac and pulmonary complications while promoting early mobility and a shorter hospital stay. The infrequent use of TEA in oncologic breast surgeries is often due to concerns about potential complications such as spinal cord injury, hematoma, intravascular migration, and respiratory compromise. However, these risks are rare with proper technique and experience. The breast, axillary nodes, and pectoral muscles receive innervation from multiple nerve sources, including branches from the intercostal nerves (T1–T6), brachial plexus, intercostal brachial nerve (T2–T3), supraclavicular nerve, and pectoral nerves from the cervical plexus.[3] TEA combined with interscalene block has been shown to enhance pain control and reduce opioid use in the first 24 hours postoperatively.[4] Local anesthetic infiltration has also been used with TEA for axillary lymph node dissection.[5] Paravertebral blocks offer another alternative to GA for mastectomies but may cause patient discomfort intraoperatively. Ono K, Danura found that the combined use of paravertebral block with GA may be a preferable choice for major breast cancer surgery.[6]

Conclusion

TEA combined with the PECS2 block is a safe and effective alternative to general anesthesia for mastectomy, particularly in high-risk patients. This technique improves postoperative outcomes, shortens hospital stays, and minimizes complications, making it a valuable approach in breast surgery anesthesia management.
