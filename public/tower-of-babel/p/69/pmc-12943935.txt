A Low-Cost and Easy Approach to Optimize Cellulose-Based Triboelectric Nanogenerators via Toner Printing
Authors: Amanda Stolpe, Erfan Jafarpour, Alireza Eivazi, Renyun Zhang, Christina Dahlström, Magnus Norgren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rising demand for wireless electronics and sustainable energy solutions drives the search for alternatives to conventional batteries. Triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) offer a promising route by converting mechanical energy into electricity via frictional events between two different material surfaces. Here, a simple and scalable surface modification method using conventional laser printing was applied to investigate the effect on triboelectric performance of cellulose-based materials against polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). Regenerated cellulose (RC) and cellulose acetate (CA) films were print patterned with black toner in a conventional laser printer at different surface coverages from 0% to 100%. The measured power output for RC films against PTFE showed minimal response from the patterning over the whole range and could be considered as constant with an average of 52 ± 2 W m−2. On the other hand, the CA sample films showed a significant and gradual increase in power output from 45 to 65 W m−2 as the toner print coverage increased from 0% to 100%. These results demonstrate that synergistic interactions between the printed toner and the substrate can strongly influence TENG performance and are highly dependent on the physical and chemical properties of the underlying material. In CA, toner–substrate intermixing enabled by laser printing temperatures exceeding the glass transition temperature provides a proof-of-concept for enhancing triboelectric performance through controlled surface–bulk interactions.

1. Introduction

The demand for wireless, electricity-driven applications and gadgets is constantly increasing. At the same time, there is a dire need to switch to a more sustainable way of living and to use clean energy technologies. The last decades have shown a trend of increasing interest in finding off-grid energy solutions for small- and large-scale applications [1,2,3,4,5,6]. Batteries are the most common source of energy in many wireless applications due to their high energy density. However, for certain applications, batteries have many drawbacks, including high cost, limited life span, and waste of valuable or harmful metals when not recycled [2,7,8,9]. The triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) is a promising candidate for replacing batteries in small-scale wireless applications, since the TENG mechanism converts dissipating mechanical energy directly into electricity. The working principle of TENGs arises from the frictional events generating a potential difference, which occurs when two different materials, one electron donor and one electron acceptor, are put into mechanical contact repeatedly. The combined effects of triboelectrification and electrostatic induction enable the conversion of mechanical energy into electrical energy, harnessed as electricity via a connecting circuit. As long as frictional events occur, the TENG continues to charge, enabling it to function as a self-sufficient power generator [10,11,12]. Dissipating mechanical energy is pervasive in everyday environments, and TENGs can harvest and convert it into electricity with high efficiency, even at low frequencies and amplitudes. This enables harvesting of, e.g., human motion and vibrations [1,13,14,15]. The simplicity of the TENG makes it cheap and easy to fabricate, creating possibilities for efficient electricity generation in various applications, including different kinds of self-powered sensors [15,16,17,18,19,20,21]. An illustration of the TENG mechanism can be seen in Figure 1. Common triboelectric materials are metals, semiconductors, and insulators, such as synthetic polymers in various plastic materials [16,22,23].

A high triboelectric output requires a good electron-donor material (tribopositive) in combination with a good electron-acceptor material (tribonegative). The likeliness of either quality is ranked in a triboelectric series, where many materials are placed in comparison to each other [24]. Generally, the further apart two materials are in the series, the larger the possible electrical output.

To optimize the TENG performance, several parameters need to be considered. The specific material choices, surface charge density, effective contact surface area, and thickness of the tribolayers. For polymeric materials, much research has focused on chemical alterations, usually to introduce functional groups that boost the electron-donating or accepting ability of the material and increase the surface charge density [25]. Moreover, physical methods, such as the introduction of particles into polymer films, have been applied to alter the surface structure and to increase the effective contact surface area, while simultaneously tuning the dielectric properties of TENG materials. Reported examples include adding carbon structures, like graphene sheets and graphite particles, into the polymer matrix [26], or inorganic particles such as BaTiO3 particles to increase both surface roughness and the dielectric constant [27]. Techniques focusing purely on surface engineering are common [28]. A few examples include lithography templating techniques [17,29,30], plasma and laser-assisted methods [31,32,33], reactive ion-etching techniques [34,35], electrochemical anodization [36], paper imprinting to increase the effective friction area of a paper-based TENG [37] and printed cellulose nanofiber aerogels to increase surface area [38]. However, a drawback of many of the used modification techniques for optimizing TENG materials is related to complexity, scalability and cost.

Utilization of biobased materials, such as cellulose, can make the TENG more sustainable [27,38,39,40,41,42,43,44,45,46]. Cellulose has shown remarkable triboelectric properties and is a promising alternative for replacing synthetic polymers in TENGs [39]. Developing additional surface-treatment methods that are compatible with cost-efficient large-scale manufacturing, and that enhance triboelectric output by tailoring the chemical functionality of cellulose-based materials interfaces, remains critical for their implementation in practical applications. Therefore, in this paper, we investigate a cheap, simple and readily available alternative to physically modify cellulose-based surfaces for use in TENGs via directed toner printing. By using a conventional laser paper printer, the effect of the surface treatment on triboelectric open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current and power output density was studied on toner print-patterned regenerated cellulose (RC) and cellulose acetate (CA) films at different surface coverages. The surface treatment method revealed slightly antagonistic or distinctly synergistic triboelectric behavior depending on the nature of the cellulose-based substrate.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Materials and Chemicals

Sulfite cellulose dissolving pulp was provided by Domsjö Fabriker (Örnsköldsvik, Sweden), with a cellulose weight average molecular weight (Mw) of 3.2 × 105 g mol−1 and a polydispersity index of 10, as determined by size exclusion chromatography in a LiCl/DMAc solvent system [47]. The chemicals used for preparation of the regenerated cellulose films (RC) were LiOH (56.5%, Alfa Aesar, Karlsruhe, Germany), urea (≥99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich, Stockholm, Sweden), ethanol (≥99.8%, VWR Chemicals, Stockholm, Sweden) and milliQ water. Membrane filters (DURAPORE, 0.65 µm DVPP, Cork, Ireland) were used as protection while drying the films. For triboelectric measurements, Cu tape of 76.2 µm thickness from Elektrokit (Malmö, Sweden) and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) tape of 130 µm thickness from High-tech-flon® (Konstanz, Germany) were used. Cellulose acetate films (CA) with the degree of substitution, DS > 2.7, and 35 µm thickness were supplied from Electron Microscopy Sciences (Hatfield, PA, USA).

2.2. Sample Preparation

The cellulose pulp was dissolved with an aqueous solution of 4.6 wt% LiOH and 15 wt% urea that had previously been frozen at −40 °C and thawed to −12 °C [48,49]. The cellulose pulp was added at 4 wt% and mixed for two min at 1300 rpm with a Witeg HS-30D Stirrer (Witeg Labortechnik GmbH, Wertheim, Germany). The solution was frozen, thawed and mixed at −12 °C twice again before centrifugation for 10 min at 8000 rpm with a JLA-16.250 fixed angle rotor (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) to remove air bubbles and undissolved fibers. The cellulose solution was regenerated as thin films in an ethanol bath for two hours and subsequently rinsed in three Milli-Q water baths over three days before being dried between membrane filters under −0.94 bar pressure and 93 °C for 15 min in a Rapid Köthen (Frank-PTI GMBH, Birkenau, Germany).

In Figure 2, an illustration of the cellulose film regeneration process can be seen. The thickness of the prepared cellulose films was determined by measuring ten randomly selected spots with a micrometer (Uninor, Norrköping, Sweden), resulting in an average thickness of 24.8 ± 0.9 µm across all samples.

2.3. Pattern Design and Laser Printing

The printing pattern consisted of 500 µm square elements, the smallest reliably printable feature according to the printer’s resolution limit, arranged with variable spacing to achieve different overall print coverages. RC and CA films were taped to commercial copy paper and passed through a conventional laser printer (RICOH MP C5504ex, Stockholm, Sweden) to deposit black toner (RICOH Print Cartridge Black MP C6003, Stockholm, Sweden) onto the film surfaces. According to the supplier, the toner composition is polyester resin (60–90%), carbon black (1–20%), wax (1–20%), silica (<10%), and titanium dioxide (<1%). Target print coverages in this study were 0, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 90, and 100%.

2.4. Characterization

2.4.1. Light Optical Microscopy (LOM)

LOM photos of the cellulose-based films and laser printed patterns were taken using a LEICA M205 C (Mannheim, Germany). The toner print coverage of the samples was then determined via image analysis using ImageJ 1.54g open-source software.

2.4.2. Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy (FESEM-EDX)

Imaging of the printed samples were done with a TESCAN MAIA3 FESEM (Brno, Czech Republic) in secondary electron (SE) mode. The cross-sections on RC and CA were done using a Hitachi IM4000 Ion Milling System (Tokyo, Japan). The cellulose samples were coated with 5 nm Iridium using a Quorum Q150T ES (Laughton, UK) before image acquisition. Cross-sections of the printed film samples were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). EDX line scans were acquired across the full sheet thickness, starting from the bottom of the film and extending through the print layer. The silicon (Si) signal was used as a tracer for ink penetration. The cellulose substrate thickness was measured on the SEM SE-images using ImageJ software at 12 positions (18.4 ± 0.5 µm for RC and 36.0 ± 0.4 µm for CA) and this thickness was used as a reference for the print–film interface. The print height was 4.4 ± 0.6 µm for both CA and RC. The EDX data was processed in MATLAB R2023b. To reduce high-frequency noise while preserving the overall trend, the Si profiles were smoothed using a moving median filter followed by a Savitzky–Golay filter. Ink penetration depth was determined using a two-line intersection method applied to the smoothed Si signal. A linear regression was fitted to the baseline region of the film where no penetration was observed. A second linear fit was applied to the region immediately below the print interface, describing the increase in Si concentration due to ink infiltration. This penetration line was constrained to pass through the known film thickness to ensure physical consistency. The intersection between the baseline and penetration lines defined the onset of measurable Si enrichment. The ink penetration depth was calculated as the difference between the total cellulose film thickness and the intersection position. Ten EDX lines were analyzed per cross section.

2.4.3. Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

AFM was used to determine the surface roughness of the RC and CA films. The measurements were performed on a Park Systems NX20 (Mannheim, Germany), operated in non-contact mode A PPP-NCHR probe (Park Systems, Mannheim, Germany) with nominal resonance frequency of 330 kHz and force constant 42 N m−1 was used. AFM images of representative areas (10 × 10 μm2) on the samples were acquired using a scan rate of 0.9–1.0 Hz, ensuring more than 95% matching between forward and backward scans. The surface roughness parameter (Rq) was determined using the Park systems XEI 1.8.5 image analysis software.

2.4.4. Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR analysis was performed on a Nicolet Apex instrument (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), in attenuated total reflectance mode, and all samples were examined using a 2 cm−1 resolution and 50 scans in the range of 500–4000 cm−1 at ambient temperature.

2.4.5. X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)

XRD was carried out at room temperature using a Bruker D2 Phaser diffractometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) with Cu Kα radiation (wavelength 1.54 Å) at 30 kV and 10 mA, in θ–2θ geometry. The increment was fixed at 0.04°. The film samples were placed on a silicon single crystal specially cut to provide a low background free from any interfering diffraction peaks. The toner powder was placed on the powder sample holder. The XRD results on the replicate samples of each fabricated material were almost identical, with <10% variation. The intensity values were only subtracted from the blank run intensity of the sample holder and presented.

2.4.6. Triboelectric Measurements

The TENG setup was prepared by attaching the sample films to a copper tape (acting as electrode) and then fastened on a linear motor as one component, see Figure 1. The PTFE counter-tribolayer (130 μm), was polished with a smooth sandpaper (grain size 5 µm) to remove any oxide layer, fixed on a copper tape and mounted on the linear motor according to a method earlier reported [39]. The maximum separation distance between the tribolayers was 29 mm, the contact-separation speed of the TENGs was set to 0.3 m s−1, and the impact rate was 4.5 Hz. The relatively high speed gave the moving part a high kinetic energy (including the mechanical moving part), and the pressure sensor could not survive. However, the constant triboelectrification conditions make performance comparisons of different samples unaffected. The area of the TENG samples were A = 30 × 30 mm2. The samples were conditioned in an oven at a temperature of 40 °C for 24 h, taken out from the oven and measured directly. Electrical signals were recorded with a PXI4071 digital multimeter (National Instruments Corp., Austin, TX, USA). The sampling rate was 200,000 s−1. All samples were measured after 270 impact cycles at a room temperature between 20 and 21 °C, and a relative humidity of around 55%. The power output density was calculated from the stable open-circuit voltage (VOC) and short-circuit current (ISC) values divided by the sample area (A); P = (VOC · ISC)/A.

3. Results and Discussion

3.1. Surface Morphology and Structure of Printed Films

Microscopy Analyses

A conventional laser paper printer was used to modify the surfaces of regenerated cellulose (RC) and cellulose acetate (CA) films by print-patterning with black toner at varying surface coverages. RC and CA films were first taped to commercial copy paper and then passed through the printer. The printed pattern consisted of 500 µm square elements, the smallest reliably printable feature according to the printer’s resolution limit, arranged with variable spacing to achieve different overall print coverages. Before the pattern printing, the surface topography of the RC and CA films was characterized by AFM. Figure 3 shows the AFM non-contact mode height images of the 25 µm thick RC and 35 µm thick CA films, respectively. Both RC and CA show very smooth surfaces on scale of observation, with a roughness of Rq = 10.2 and 4.8 nm, respectively. Regarding RC, previous studies have shown that contributions to surface roughness from larger length scales are minimal, and commercial CA films are expected to exhibit comparable surface appearance [46,47]. Paper substrates suitable for uniform prints with minimal toner scattering in laser printing, such as uncoated copy paper, typically have a surface roughness of 3–5 µm, as measured by confocal laser profilometry [50]. This is substantially rougher than the films used in the present study.

After the pattern printing, LOM and FESEM images of the laser-printed films were acquired to evaluate the printed patterns. Figure 4 illustrates the appearance of the different toner print coverages on the RC and CA films. The print patterns were designed by assembling 500 µm-wide square units in a stacked layout to produce the specified coverage. Table 1 provides a summary of both the nominal pattern coverage defined during the design stage and the corresponding coverage values obtained through quantitative image analysis. The close agreement between the intended and measured coverages indicates that the printer is capable of reproducing exactly the same fine features with high resolution and minimal deviation from the target values on both RC and CA films. This consistency also demonstrates that the interaction between the deposited toner and the underlying substrates is reliable, supporting uniform adhesion and film formation on both the RC and CA materials without any print bleeding in the XY-direction. Overall, these results validate the suitability of the printing process for generating controlled coverage patterns across the two film types.

The FESEM images shown in Figure 5, visualizes the individual 500 µm-wide printed square units in a stacked layout for the RC and the CA films. The images show how the toner particles have been melted together in the fusing cycle, forming a porous print structure. Although this texture is not apparent to the naked eye, higher-magnification imaging reveals that the printed areas exhibit a relatively coarse surface morphology. This structure originates from the individual toner particles, which do not form a perfectly smooth, continuous layer but instead create a subtly granular topography.

As a result, the printed regions display a three-dimensional microstructure with a measurable height profile relative to the underlying film substrate. This elevated texture reflects both the particulate nature of the toner and how the particles fuse during printing, providing additional insight into the micro-scale characteristics of the printed films.

3.2. Chemical and Physical Characterization of Toner Print and Cellulose Films

3.2.1. FESEM-EDX Analysis

The film thickness and print height were analyzed using FESEM on cross-sections at 12 positions on films with 100% print coverage. The substrate film thicknesses for the RC and the CA films were 18.4 ± 0.5 µm and 36.0 ± 0.4 µm, respectively. The print height was 4.4 ± 0.6 µm for both the different film substrates. By using the silicon signal in EDX as a tracer for print migration, it was revealed that the Si penetration depth differed between the two substrate film types. The RC films exhibited a penetration depth of 2.3 ± 0.5 µm, indicating a relatively shallow and consistent Si migration into the cellulose structure. In contrast, the CA samples showed a higher mean interpenetration depth of 3.8 ± 1.9 µm. However, the substantially larger standard deviation indicates greater variability between measurements for the CA films. Overall, the results indicate that CA films allow more extensive print interpenetration compared to RC, but with less uniform behavior. Figure 6 shows examples of how the cross-section analysis of the RC and CA films were performed.

The determined differences could be attributed to the significantly lower glass-transition temperature (Tg) of CA, typically in the range of 185 °C to 200 °C, at CA DS-levels above 2.7 [51]. During the toner-printing fusing cycle, temperatures typically reach between 175 °C and 215 °C. At these elevated temperatures, the outermost layer of the CA film begins to soften, which facilitates partial interpenetration of the toner into the polymer surface. When the softened film is simultaneously subjected to pressure between the heated fuser roller and the compliant rubber backing roller, the toner becomes embedded within the near-surface region of the CA substrate, promoting strong adhesion and a more integrated print layer. In contrast, this mechanism is not expected to occur in the RC films, as the Tg of dried, non-derivatized cellulose is reported to be around 220 to 250 °C, significantly higher than the temperatures reached during the fusing process [52]. Consequently, the RC films remain in a rigid, glassy state throughout printing, preventing any appreciable softening and larger interdiffusion between the toner and the substrate.

3.2.2. FTIR Analysis

FTIR analysis allows for a deeper chemical characterization of the materials. Figure 7a shows the characteristic absorption bands for cellulose II (RC) at: 3332 cm−1 (O–H stretching), 2891 cm−1 (CH2 asymmetric stretching), 1645 cm−1 (H–O–H bending of adsorbed water), 1366 cm−1 (C–H bending), 1314 cm−1 (CH2 wagging), 1156, 1016, 895 cm−1 (C–O stretching in C–O–C and C–O–H fragments), and in the wavenumber region 700 to 500 cm−1, O–H out-of-plane bending [53,54]. Figure 7a,b also shows the toner spectrum, and the spectra of the RC and CA films at 100% toner print coverage, which almost fully resembles the toner spectrum. The toner’s absorption bands are situated at 2916, 2848 cm−1 (aliphatic C–H stretching), 1721 cm−1 (C=O stretching in ester carbonyl), 1606 cm−1 (aromatic C=C stretching or conjugated C=C), 1507 cm−1 (aromatic C=C stretching/ring vibrations), 1455 cm−1 (CH2/CH3 bending or aromatic ring vibrations), 1287, 1222, 1180 cm−1 (C–O stretching, O–C–C/C–O–C vibrations), 1103, 1069 cm−1 (Si–O–Si stretching), 828, 729, 699 cm−1 (aromatic C–H out-of-plane bending), 554 cm−1 (Si–O–Si bending). The absorption bands situated at 1721, 1287, 1222, and 1180 cm−1 clearly indicate the presence of polyester or styrene-acrylate resins [55]. The bands at 1606, 1507, 1455, 828, 729,699, and 573 cm−1, are significant for aromatic constituents in carbon black, like fused benzene rings. Typical absorption bands from amorphous silica are found at 1103, 1069, and 554 cm−1.

In Figure 7b, the spectrum of cellulose acetate (CA) shows the characteristic absorption bands at: 3485 cm−1 (O–H stretching), 2943 cm−1 (C–H stretching), 1736 cm−1 (C=O stretching), 1431 and 1367 cm−1 (C–H bending), 1213 cm−1 (acetate C–O–C stretching), 1160 cm−1 (C–O–C asymmetric stretching), 1031 cm−1 (C–O–C of the cellulose backbone), 899 cm−1 (C–O–C skeletal vibration), 602 cm−1 (C–O rocking or skeletal deformation), 556 cm−1 (C–O–C bending) [56].

3.2.3. XRD Analysis

The X-ray diffraction patterns of the regenerated cellulose (RC), cellulose acetate (CA), toner, and the printed samples are shown in Figure 8. As expected, the presence of carbon black was observed in the toner XRD profile, considering the intense diffraction peak located at ca. 22°, corresponding to (002) reflection plane [57]. Additionally, the appearance of a minor peak located around 24° can be attributed to the silica (SiO2) content in the toner, which is highly expected based on the FTIR results (see Figure 7) and FESEM-EDX (see Figure 6), and information provided by the supplier [58,59,60]. The broad peak between 2θ of 10 and 30° could be assigned to the amorphous contributions and the polymeric contents in the toner.

The regenerated cellulose (RC) films are consistent with cellulose II allomorph (Figure 8a). Cellulose II allomorph crystallizes as monoclinic crystals with the unit cell containing chains arranged in an antiparallel fashion [61]. The diffraction peaks are located at the diffraction angles (2θ) 12.6°, 20.6°, and 22.1°, corresponding to the crystallographic plane reflections (1–10), (110), and (020), respectively [62]. The XRD pattern of the printed regenerated cellulose sample (RC100) displays the presence of both carbon black and silica on cellulose II major crystalline peaks. However, a slight decrease in the intensity of the (1–10) reflection peak of cellulose II is observed in the RC100 sample. One possible reason could be that the laser-printing process causes minor structural changes. However, changes could mainly be attributed to the lower hydroxyl group content resulting from potential interactions between the hydroxyl groups and the toner materials. The dependency of the hydroxyl group content and the reflection plane intensity (1–10) in regenerated cellulose have been discussed in detail in our previously published work [53]. Comparing the FTIR analysis of the RC and the RC100 also confirms the decrease in the hydroxyl contents (characteristic absorption band at 3332 cm−1; O–H stretching) after printing (see Figure 7a). The XRD profiles of cellulose acetate (CA) and the printed one (CA100) are shown in Figure 8b. As observed, the diffractogram peaks are typical of semicrystalline cellulose acetate. The peaks are located at diffraction angles (2θ) of ca. 9, and 20° [63,64,65]. The presence of both carbon black and silica on cellulose acetate diffraction peaks is observed in the XRD pattern of the CA100. Although XRD analysis suggests that the structural characteristics of the CA film remain intact when printed with black toner, a reduction in peak intensity at 2θ of approximately 9° is observed. This can be due to the laser-printing process and potential interactions between CA and the toner materials. Considering the potential interactions, the FTIR analysis of CA and CA100 reveals that the minor hydroxyl group peak (characteristic absorption band at 3485 cm−1; O–H stretching) and the band at 1367 cm−1 representing the C–H bending of the acetyl group almost disappeared on the printed sample (see Figure 7b).

3.3. Triboelectric Measurements of Print-Patterned Cellulose Films

The triboelectric performance was evaluated for RC and CA films at different toner print coverage, using PTFE as counter-tribolayer. PTFE is one of the most commonly used tribolayer materials due to its high negative surface-charge density and stability, and therefore is a good reference material for comparisons.

Figure 9a–c (RC) and 9d–e (CA) present the open-circuit voltage (VOC), short-circuit current (ISC), and power output for all print coverages listed in Table 1. Figure 9g–h shows representative 2 s output profiles of open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current from the TENG setup for some selected print coverages on CA. In Figure 9i,j, images of toner-printed RC respective CA films after a long-term recording with 20,000 hitting cycles, equivalent to approximately 4500 s testing in the TENG setup, are shown. No obvious frictional wear and tear or compression due to the repeated contact–separation events was observed. From the TENG signal data, only a minor decrease in the power output of around 3% for both the types of substrates could be detected, which demonstrates excellent wear robustness and signal stability of the systems. The outcome displays significant differences between the RC and CA systems, both before and after the toner printing. At zero print coverage, the RC film shows both higher open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current than the CA film. Consequently, the power output is also higher for RC; 53 W m−2 compared with 46 W m−2 for the unprinted CA film. As print patterns with increasing toner surface coverage are applied to the films, the observed trends in open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current and power output for RC and CA deviate drastically from each other. At the lowest print coverage (RC10, CA10), a slight increase in VOC for RC from 283 V (unprinted) to 296 V, whereas a more pronounced effect from the print patterning is obvious for the CA film, increasing from 270 V (unprinted) to 309 V. In comparison with the unprinted samples, the short-circuit current decreases slightly for both RC10 (167 to 162 µA) and CA10 (152 to 143 µA). On the other hand, for these samples, the power output remains constant for RC10, while a minor increase from 46 to 49 W m−2 for CA10 can be noticed. As the print coverage on the films increases from 10% to 100%, the RC system shows a minimum in VOC of 272 V at 40% with an ISC of 163 µA, giving the lowest power output (49 W m−2) among all the measured RC films. As can be seen in Figure 9b, the ISC of the RC series shows small variations over the whole print coverage range and the highest value is obtained with the unprinted film. The results from the print patterning shown in Figure 9a–c indicate that varying the print coverage of the RC film samples has insignificant effect, with the power output remaining at 52 ± 2 W m−2. However, for the CA system shown in Figure 9d–f, both VOC and ISC increase continuously with increasing print coverage, indicating a corresponding rise in power output. Interestingly, the highest power output received is 65 W m−2 at 100% print coverage (CA100), an increase of 41% compared with the unprinted CA, and 20% more than the RC film at 100% print coverage (RC100). The results clearly show that a synergistic effect on triboelectric performance appears for cellulose acetate after surface patterning by toner printing. This is not seen for regenerated cellulose. From a contact-mechanics perspective at room temperature, RC and CA exhibit comparable elastic moduli of approximately 4 GPa, while PTFE is significantly softer, with an E-modulus of about 0.5 GPa [53]. Consequently, during TENG operation the contact deformation is dominated by PTFE, whereas RC and CA undergo only minimal elastic compliance. As a result, differences in surface roughness adaptation at the contact interface are expected to be limited and similar for RC and CA, making elastic modulus effects unlikely to account for the observed differences in triboelectric performance. Considering the physical properties of the RC and CA substrate films, the most significant difference is the presence and absence of a glass-transition temperature (Tg) for cellulose acetate and regenerated cellulose, respectively. As discussed earlier in Section 3.2.1., the temperature applied under toner printing exceeds the Tg of CA, making it possible for the toner to diffuse and intercalate between the polymer chains in the softened CA films [66]. This is determined from the cross-section analysis with FESEM, reported in Section 3.2.1. Moreover, the XRD analysis in Section 3.2.3. shows a reduction in peak intensity at 2θ of approximately 9°, supporting interactions between CA and the toner. Conversely, potential interactions between the toner and regenerated cellulose in the RC films are restricted to happen only at the outermost surface layer without any material mixing and should thereby be negligible in comparison to the CA system. In this context, the notable 20% difference in power output between RC and CA films at 100% print coverage should be highlighted, as it clearly demonstrates that the triboelectric response is not solely attributable to the toner layer but instead arises from material mixing, resulting in a synergistic effect. From the FESEM-EDX, FTIR, and XRD analyses of the toner together with the supplier’s additional information, the main chemistries introduced by the toner printing relate to polyester resin functionalities (carbonyl), carbon black (aromatic), wax (aliphatic), and silica. The investigated cellulose-based films are considered more tribopositive than the main toner component, the polyesters, but the silica in the toner has, on the contrary, an even more tribopositive character [67]. It has been shown that carbon black provides charge-trapping sites, thereby increasing the material’s dielectric constant and the overall density of negative charges [68]. However, when non-homogeneously dispersed, carbon black may also lead to increased conductivity and consequently faster charge dissipation. To summarize, the enhanced TENG power density of toner-printed CA films measured against PTFE likely results from synergistic physical effects induced by material mixing, which is not appearing in RC films due to the lack of Tg and melting point for regenerated cellulose (cellulose II). Since the toner applied by laser printing is equivalent for all the films, material mixing that changes the dielectric homogeneity properties and possibly induces charge-trapping is considered the underlying phenomena creating the different responses in power output evolution.

4. Conclusions

This study shows that laser toner surface patterning affects the triboelectric performance of regenerated cellulose (RC) and cellulose acetate (CA) films in fundamentally different ways. With PTFE as the counter layer, unprinted RC initially outperforms CA; however, increasing toner coverage has little effect on RC, maintaining an almost constant power density of 52 ± 2 W m−2. In contrast, CA exhibits a continuous enhancement with increasing print coverage, reaching a maximum power density of 65 W m−2 at full coverage, representing a 41% increase over unprinted CA and a 20% improvement over fully printed RC.

These contrasting responses arise from intrinsic material properties. Printing temperatures exceed CA’s glass-transition temperature, enabling polymer softening and partial toner intercalation, as evidenced by FESEM-EDX analysis of the cross-sections and subtle XRD changes. RC, lacking a glass transition in this temperature range, limits toner interaction to the surface, preventing comparable synergistic effects in triboelectric enhancement.

Overall, the results highlight that mechanical and structural compatibility between substrate and toner is crucial for enhancing triboelectric performance. For CA, toner printing induces synergistic modifications beyond the intrinsic contribution of the toner layer, demonstrating laser toner printing as a simple and effective strategy for tuning triboelectric materials through controlled surface engineering. However, further studies are still needed to fully outline the underlying mechanisms.
