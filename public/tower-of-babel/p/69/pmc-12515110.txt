Understanding hand grip measures in geriatric inpatients: associations with frailty, daily functioning and fatigue
Authors: Geeske Peeters, Myrthe M Swart, Liza De Dobbeleer, Ivan Bautmans, Marcel GM Olde Rikkert, René JF Melis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand grip measures are promising candidate markers for recovery in geriatric patients.AimsTo advance interpretation of these measures, we examined: (1) the associations between grip measures at admission and measures of frailty, daily functioning and fatigue to understand the constructs they measure; (2) alternate operational definitions of grip measures.Methods181 geriatric inpatients completed twice daily grip measurements using the Eforto® vigorimeter, including maximum grip strength (GSmax), fatigue resistance (FR), grip work (GW) and capacity to perceived vitality ratio (CPV). Associations with each of these measures and the outcomes frailty index (range 0-100), functional limitations (10–40), and total (4–20) and physical (20–100) fatigue were examined using linear regression. Analyses were repeated for the baseline value, average of first two measurements, within-person standard deviation of all values, and the coefficient of variation of all values for each of the grip measures.ResultsAssociations approached statistical significance for baseline GSmax and the outcomes frailty (B=-0.09, 99%CI=--0.21, 0.02), functional limitations (B=-0.10, 99%CI=-0.20, 0) and physical fatigue (B=-0.07, 99%CI=-0.14, 0). Baseline, average and standard deviation values for FR, GW and CPV showed trends towards associations with functional limitations, but not with frailty or fatigue.ConclusionsGSmax and CPV constructs overlapped more strongly with the construct of frailty than FR and GW. FR, GW and CPV overlapped with functional limitations, supporting their potential as early markers of recovery. Average values are more reliable than single values and variance measures may add additional information.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03195-1.Keywords: Grip strength, Functional limitations, Older adults, Intrinsic capacity, Physical resilience

Introduction

A key challenge for geriatricians is to estimate the recovery capacity of older adults admitted to the geriatrics ward. The recovery capacity importantly informs the decision for the best course of medical care, i.e. curative, recovery-oriented, symptom management or palliative care [1]. The geriatrician’s estimation of the recovery capacity is typically based on clinical judgement informed by the comprehensive geriatric assessment and clinical knowledge [2]. A single established measure of recovery capacity does not exist. Concepts such as frailty, intrinsic capacity and physical resilience approach the concept of recovery capacity from different complimentary angles (Box 1).

Box 1. Definitions of key concepts discussed in this paper.

Frailty = a syndrome of multi-system reduction in physiological capacity as a result of which an older person’s function may be severely compromised by minor environmental challenges, giving rise to the condition of ‘unstable disability’ [26]. Frailty is a state of increased vulnerability to poor resolution of homeostasis following a stress, which increases the risk of adverse outcomes [27].

Intrinsic capacity = the composite of all the physical and mental capacities that an individual can draw on at any point in time. It represents an individual’s overall health potential, covering the functions cognition, mobility, sensory abilities, emotional well-being, and vitality [28].

Physical resilience = ability to (functionally) resist and recover after a health stressor [11].

Grip measures tap into domains of frailty and intrinsic capacity and, therefore, are candidate markers of recovery capacity. Grip measures include maximum grip strength (GSmax), fatigue resistance (FR) and grip work (GW). GSmax is commonly measured to detect muscle weakness, which is one of the criteria for phenotypical definitions of frailty [3]. A FR test measures the time (in seconds) the person can maintain GSmax until it drops to 50% of its maximum. GW is the muscle work produced during the FR test (and calculated as the area under the force-time curve) [4]. FR and GW measure muscle fatiguability, reflecting a different characteristic of muscle function than strength. Previous studies in community-dwelling older adults, hospitalised older adults and abdominal surgery patients found correlations of FR and GW with self-perceived fatigue, physical functioning, physical activity and inflammation markers [5–8]. These findings suggest that FR and GW may be useful markers of recovery. Another metric that can be derived from grip measurements is the capacity to perceived vitality (CPV) ratio [9]. This ratio combines two domains of phenotypical frailty, i.e. (muscle) weakness measured as GW and exhaustion measured as self-perceived fatigue. CPV has been proposed as a marker of vitality [9], one of the domains of intrinsic capacity [10].

Physical resilience is defined as the ability to functionally recover after a health stressor [11]. Several methods exist to quantify physical resilience [12], one of which is dynamic indicators of resilience [12, 13]. This method builds on complex systems theory which assumes that “any complex system, like a human being, is permanently subject to natural perturbations from the environment” [13]. To assess the functioning of a complex system, it is important to evaluate both the structural, static characteristics of the system and the dynamics of the processes operated by the system. For example, how does the system respond to perturbation? Or how quickly does the system exhaust the available resources needed to maintain adequate functioning? From this theory, it follows that to measure the system’s functioning we need either an ‘exhaust’ measure or time-series data to capture the system’s response to health stressors [14]. First evidence supporting this theory comes from a study showing higher variance in self-rated health in frail than in non-frail older adults [13]. In line with this theory, the FR test can be seen as an exhaust measure and repeated grip strength measures may provide useful predictive value about the recovery capacity of a patient.

Building on previous research, and to advance use of grip measurements in geriatric research and clinical practice, this study addresses three aims. The first aim is to examine cross-sectional associations of grip measures with frailty, daily functioning, and self-perceived fatigue in geriatric inpatients at admission. While GSmax is a commonly used measure in geriatric research and clinical practice, FR, GW and CPV are still new concepts in this field. To better understand their potential as markers for recovery capacity in geriatric inpatients, insight is needed in how these measures map with related constructs.

The second aim is to examine which operational definition of the grip measurements shows the strongest associations with the outcomes frailty, daily functioning, and self-perceived fatigue (measured at admission): the baseline measurement, the average of the first two measurements, the standard deviation of all measurements, or the coefficient of variation of all measurements during admission. Previous research by our group showed that given the high within-person variance in grip measures, the average of two measurements is more reliable than a single measurement [15]. Moreover, in line with the theory of dynamic indicators of resilience explained above, ‘real’ variance of repeated measurements (i.e. not due to measurement error or noise) may hold useful information about the stability of the system, in this case the patient’s physical condition.

The third aim is to examine if imputing missing values as zero in case of inability to perform the FR tests leads to over- or underestimation of the true associations. The participants in this study are all acutely ill older adults admitted at the geriatrics ward. For some of our frailest patients, the three maximum grip tests require so much of their strength that they do not have enough strength left to subsequently complete the FR test [16]. Currently, the protocol for the FR test does not specify how to deal with the missing values for these patients. One option is to leave it as a missing value as the test could not be completed. However, this could lead to selection bias, with the weakest patients being excluded from the analyses due to missing data. Another option is to impute the missing value with a low score, e.g. zero seconds, indicating a very poor performance. However, this may overly amplify the variance in the grip measures.

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational study used data from the Geriatric Resilience Registry (GRR), which is a cohort study of patients admitted to the geriatrics ward of the Radboud university medical center, Nijmegen, The Netherlands. The overall aim of the registry is to develop and validate measures of (physical) resilience. Data collection for GRR involved a baseline questionnaire at admission, and thereafter twice daily measurements of grip measures. Additional data were extracted from medical records. Embedded within the GRR were the FORTO 1.0 and FORTO 2.0 sub-studies, which used the same protocol, but in FORTO 2.0 we added a self-perceived fatigue questionnaire at admission, and 3- and 6-month follow-up surveys. The protocols for GRR and FORTO 1.0 fell outside the remits of the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO) and did not require further ethical clearance. The protocol for FORTO 2.0 was reviewed based on the Medical Device Regulations and approved by the East Netherlands Medical Research Ethics Committee (protocol: NL77879.091.21).

All patients admitted to the ward between June 2021 and September 2023 were invited to participate in GRR if they met the following inclusion criteria: (i) aged 65 years or older, (ii) speaking and understanding Dutch, (iii) expected hospital stay of 3 or more days, and (iv) able to complete baseline measurements within 48 h of admission (72 h during weekends). Exclusion criteria were: (i) contact isolation; (ii) severe cognitive impairment at admission; (iii) low-stimulus care (e.g. for patients with severe delirium); (iv) life expectancy of < 2 weeks; and (v) physical inability to squeeze the rubber bulb of the Eforto® vigorimeter. Information on recruitment criteria was derived from medical records and by contacting the treating physician. Baseline measurements were completed as part of standard care, after which patients were asked if they were willing to participate in the registry. This involved completing twice daily grip measurements and a brief questionnaire during admission, and follow-up telephone surveys at 3 and 6 months. All participants provided written informed consent. For the current analyses, data were used from all participants in GRR (including the sub-studies) who completed at least two grip measurements during admission.

Grip measurements

Grip measurements were completed twice daily on weekdays during admission. The measurements were conducted using the Eforto® vigorimeter, which consists of a handheld device with a rubber bulb that connects via Bluetooth with the Eforto® smartphone application. The app guides the participant and assessor through the standardized measurement protocol including three GSmax measurements and one FR test with the dominant hand [16]. For the GSmax test, the participant is asked to maximally squeeze the bulb three times for five seconds with 30 s rest in between. For the FR test, the participant is asked to maximally squeeze the bulb and to maintain that strength for as long as possible until the strength drops below 50% of GSmax. The FR test requires the participant to reach to at least 80% of the GSmax value within the first five seconds of the test. If this minimum threshold was not reached, then the test was invalid. After 30 s of rest, a new attempt was offered, with a maximum of three attempts. If the third attempt failed, the test was aborted and set to missing.

GSmax (in kPa; 1 kPa = 0,0102 kg/cm2) is the highest value of the three GSmax tests and the highest value reached during the FR test. FR is the time in seconds that the strength is maintained during the FR test. GW is calculated as the integral of the strength-time curve during the FR test (in kPa*s). The GSmax, FR and GW measurements with the Eforto® vigorimeter are valid and reliable for use in robust, frail and hospitalised older adults [16].

CPV was calculated as GW divided by fatigue (measured with the MFI-20 as described below) [9]. The total fatigue score was used. Higher GW and lower fatigue scores reflect better health. Therefore, higher CPV scores reflect a better GW to fatigue ratio.

Frailty, functional limitations and self-perceived fatigue

At admission (baseline), participants completed the ‘The Older Persons and Informal Caregivers Survey Minimum DataSet’ (TOPICS-SF) questionnaire (link to English version: https://topics-mds.eu/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/TOPICS-short-form-2017-English.pdf) [17, 18], including items about daily functioning, health status and wellbeing. Based on the TOPICS-SF a 35-item Frailty Index was derived using standardised and validated methods (range 0–1) (link to syntax: https://topics-mds.eu/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/ShortForm2017_baseline_FrailtyIndex_35items.pdf) [19, 20]. The frailty index does not include grip strength measures. Functional limitations were measured using the TOPICS-SF Daily functioning subscale. On this subscale, participants indicate the difficulty they have with 10 daily activities on a 1–4 scale, with higher scores reflecting more difficulty (range 10–40). In the FORTO 2.0 sub-study, self-perceived fatigue was measured with the 20-item multidimensional fatigue index (MFI-20) [21]. The items cover the domains general fatigue, physical fatigue, reduced activity, reduced motivation and mental fatigue. In this study only the total score (range 20–100) and the physical fatigue domain (range 4–20) were used, with higher scores indicating more fatigue.

Descriptive variables

Descriptive variables include demographic and health factors. These variables were derived from the TOPICS-SF and medical records and classified as presented in Table 1. The treatment focus was derived from medical records and is the result of shared decision making between the patient and care team, informed by the comprehensive geriatric assessment. The treatment focus directs care-decisions made during hospital stay. Based on an estimation of the patient’s capacity to recover from the health problem(s) the patient is admitted for, the treatment focus is either (1) curative, (2) recovery oriented, (3) symptom management, or (4) end-of life care. A treatment focus of ‘end-of-life care’ is chosen when a patient has a life expectancy of less than 2 weeks, which is an exclusion criterion for the current study.

Statistical analyses

Aim 1: associations between grip measures and frailty, functional limitations and fatigue

To better understand the constructs measured by the four grip measures, we examined their cross-sectional associations with the outcomes frailty, functional limitations and fatigue using linear regression models. As fatigue is already embedded in the CPV measure, associations between CPV and fatigue were not examined. The frailty index was rescaled from 0 to 1 to 0-100 and results for GW were presented per 100 kPa*s for ease of interpretation.

Aim 2: operational definition of grip measures: baseline, average, SD and CoV

To examine whether a single (baseline) measurement, average of two measurements or the variance of all measurements during admission shows the strongest associations with the outcomes, all models described for aim 1 were run for each of the four operational definitions of the four grip measures. These definitions included: (1) the baseline value at admission, (2) the average of the first two measurements, (3) the within-person standard deviation (SD) of all measurements, and (4) the within-person coefficient of variation (CoV) of all measurements. This CoV was added to account for the observation that the variation was higher in participants with higher mean values.

Aim 3: imputing missing values for FR and GW due to insufficient strength as zero

To examine if imputing missing values as zero in case of inability to perform the fatigue resistance tests leads to changes in the associations with related constructs, we reran the models described for aims 1 and 2 for FR and GW after imputation of the missing values as zero. Missing values for FR and GW were imputed as zero only if: (1) a valid GSmax measurement was obtained, to ensure that an attempt was made to measure the FR, and (2) no technical problems were mentioned in the notes (which likely implies missing completely at random).

All models for aims 1, 2 and 3 were adjusted for age and sex. Interactions with sex were checked by adding product terms of the grip measures with sex. Statistically significant interactions (p < 0.05) were found for only one of the 60 models. Therefore, results are presented for men and women combined. Non-linearity was checked by adding a quadratic term and found to be statistically significant in only six of the 60 models. To facilitate comparisons across models, all associations were assumed to be linear in the main analyses. Sensitivity analyses including either the interaction with sex or the quadratic term (as relevant) were done for these seven models to examine the influence on the findings. To account for multiple testing and reduce the risk of type 1 error, the level of significance was set at 0.01 and 99% confidence intervals were reported.

Results

A total of 181 participants met the inclusion criteria, with a mean age of 81.5 ± 7.8 years and an even distribution of men and women (Table 1). Approximately 90% of participants lived independently and 8.8% in a nursing home. Participants had on average 4.6 ± 1.7 chronic conditions and a high level of frailty (FI 0.4 ± 0.1) and functional limitations (median 24, interquartile range 18–30). Of note, 77.7% of patients was admitted with a recovery-oriented treatment focus and 38.2% had cognitive impairment.

Mean GSmax values were markedly higher in men (49.4 ± 17.1 kPa) than in women (35.7 ± 14.2 kPa), while the FR was in the same range (27.6 ± 16.0 vs. 26.8 ± 21.8 s) (Table 2). Consequently, the GW and CPV values were also markedly higher in men than women (GW: 937.0 ± 679.5 vs. 670.2 ± 601.5 kPa*s; CPV: 15.9 ± 14.4 vs. 10.7 ± 9.3). The high within-person standard deviations for FR, GW and CPV reflect substantial variability in scores within participants over time. The values for FR and GW were somewhat lower after imputation of missing values by zero, as expected.

Aim 2: operational definition of grip measurements: baseline, average, SD and CoV

As described above, for GSmax, the associations approached statistical significance for the baseline measure with the frailty index, functional limitations and physical fatigue (Table 3). Similar trends were also found for the average of two measurements in the associations with functional imitations and physical fatigue. No statistically significant associations were found for the two variance measures of GSmax, SD and CoV.

For FR and GW, trends towards statistically significant associations with functional limitations were found for the baseline, average and the SD values, but not for the CoV (Table 3). No statistically significant associations were found for any of the definitions of FR or GW with frailty or fatigue (Table 3). For CPV, statistically significant associations were found for the baseline and average values in the association with frailty, and for the baseline, average and SD values in the association with functional limitations (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis in which the model accounted for non-linearity showed that a higher variance (expressed as SD or CoV) in GSmax was associated with a higher frailty index, but this association levels off for higher values of within-person SD or CoV of GSmax (Supplementary Table S1). Higher average GSmax, average GW, baseline CPV and average CPV were associated with lower functional limitations and these associations became stronger for higher levels of GSmax, GW and CPV respectively. Sensitivity analyses in which we accounted for the interaction with sex showed that the association between average FR and the frailty index approached statistical significance in women (B=−0.10, 99%CI=−0.20, 0) but not in men (B = 0.07, 99%CI=−0.07, 0.19) (Supplementary Table S1).

Aim 3: imputing missing values for FR and GW due to insufficient strength as zero

When comparing the models in which missing values for FR and GW were imputed as ‘zero’, with the models in which the missing values were set to missing (and thus excluded from the analyses), the point estimates were in the same range and confidence intervals were overlapping but marginally narrower. This was observed for the baseline, average and SD values of FR and GW, but not for the CoV (Table 3).

Discussion

This study explored how four grip measures map with constructs related to recovery in hospitalised older adults. We found that CPV was associated with frailty and functional limitations, while FR and GW showed trends towards associations with functional limitations only. Associations between GSmax and outcomes approached statistical significance for frailty, functional limitations and physical fatigue. Furthermore, we compared four operational definitions of these grip measures and found that the baseline measure for GSmax is sufficient to detect associations with the outcomes, while for FR and GW stronger associations were found for the average values of two measurements. For GW and CPV, associations with functional limitations were found with the SD values but not with the CoV, as measures of within-person variance over time. Finally, we found that imputing missing values for FR and GW as zero meant that more participants could be included in the analyses while the strength of the associations remained in the same range.

Muscle weakness and perceived fatigue (or exhaustion) are two of the criteria that form the phenotypical definition of frailty [3]. In line with this theory, we found that GSmax and CPV were indeed negatively associated with frailty (Table 3). FR and GW were not associated with frailty. It may be that our sample is too homogeneous to detect an association with frailty, with nearly all participants exceeding the cut-point of 0.25 for severe frailty on the frailty index [20]. This may also explain the relatively small magnitude of the associations of GSmax and CPV with frailty. Alternatively, it may be that FR and GW measure different constructs than GSmax, CPV and frailty. If this is true, then CPV may be another marker for frailty, while FR and GW may add new information in addition to GSmax and frailty.

The current associations of FR, GW and CPV with functional limitations suggest that these measures may be useful markers of recovery. Previous cross-sectional studies have reported similar associations of FR and GW with physical functioning in 40 relatively healthy older adults [5], with balance and mobility in 77 nursing home residents [22], and with functional limitations in a mix of 291 older patients, older healthy controls and younger controls [7]. Further support comes from two prospective studies. One study in 494 adults aged 80 years and over found an indirect association between muscle endurance (a factor derived through exploratory factor analysis which included GW), and 1-year decline in daily functioning and gait that was mediated by fatigue [23]. Previous analyses in the current sample showed that FR and GW measured at admission were associated with recovery in functional limitations in the first three months after discharge (Swart, et al. under review). Collectively, these cross-sectional and prospective associations strengthen the idea that FR, GW and CPV are promising candidate markers for recovery. Note, that we asked our participants about their functional limitations two weeks prior to admission. This measure of functional limitation could be viewed as the pre-admission reference level that patients should recover to after admission. It is however, not a direct measure of recovery. More prospective studies are needed to examine the robustness and added value of these markers in real-life clinical scenario’s.

Comparison of the four operational definitions of the grip measures showed equally strong associations for the baseline measure of GSmax as for the average of the first two measurements (Table 3). Averaging over multiple measurements is often used as a method to increase the reliability of strongly fluctuating measures. However, as a single test protocol already includes three maximum efforts, averaging over two test protocols adds little to the reliability of the GSmax measure. The effect of averaging was more pronounced for the FR, GW and CPV measures, as stronger associations were found for averaged than baseline FR and GW values in the associations with functional limitations (Table 3). For future studies, we recommend defining FR, GW and CPV as the average over two measurements.

Interestingly, the SD of FR, GW and CPV, but not the CoV, were associated with functional limitations (Table 3). Two explanations of these findings are possible. First, the CoV values were very small with little variation across participants (Table 2). It may be that this lack of variation in CoV in the sample explains the lack of significant associations. Second, the CoV corrects for the observation that the SD was greater for participants with higher average values. If that is the case, then the inconsistent results for SD and CoV may also suggest that variance measures do not add information beyond the baseline or average measures. Furthermore, in accordance with the theory of dynamic indicators of resilience [13], we expected that greater variance would be associated with poorer functioning. However, the results show the opposite: participants with greater variation in FR and GW (operationalized as the SD) had better functioning (or fewer limitations). Three potential explanations are possible:

It may be that the high fluctuations in FR and GW reflect measurement error rather than true fluctuations in capacity and that the significant associations are chance findings rather than true associations.

While in general the theory of the dynamic indicators of resilience suggests that greater variability reflects inability of the system to maintain an equilibrium in health state (and greater fragility of the system to external stressors), it may in some circumstances also reflect the opposite, namely better capacity to cope with external stressors. To know whether high variability reflects difficulty to maintain the system within the safe boundaries of the optimal equilibrium or instead reflects a healthy response to a stressor, we need to understand (i) the role of the signal we are observing within the larger system, (ii) the timescale and (iii) the external conditions [24]. For example, if we take a thermostat that is programmed to maintain a steady temperature in a room, high fluctuations in temperature in otherwise more or less stable circumstances may reflect a hyperresponsive thermostat that does not function well. Now suppose that the external influences cause the room-temperature to fluctuate, then high fluctuations in activity of the heater to mitigate these external influences reflect adequate functioning of the thermostat.

Which of these explanations is most likely, is yet unclear. Further research is warranted to understand whether greater variability in the FR, GW and CPV signals over time reflect better or poorer physical resilience.

In our sample of acutely hospitalised geriatric patients, 13% of cases had a missing value due to having insufficient strength to complete the fatigue resistance test. In these cases, the missing values reflected poor performance rather than a technical error or other reason for a failed measurement and can be labelled as ‘missing at random’ [25]. As the reason for missingness is known, we felt that a ‘worst case scenario’ imputation was more appropriate than other methods based on observed values (e.g. average or predicted value) as the observed values are based on successful measurements that reflect relatively better performance while the failed measurements reflect poor performance. As participants with missing values were frailer and less healthy than those without missing values [16], this way of imputation reduces selection bias. Moreover, it avoids overestimation of FR and GW (Table 2). This is relevant both when comparing between-group differences and when evaluating within-person trajectories of measurements over time. In the current analyses, imputation of missing values by zero minimally affected the estimated regression coefficients while confidence intervals were slightly narrower. While in the current study, imputation did not change the conclusion of the findings, for future research, we recommend the imputing missing values due to poor test performance to minimize bias.

Strengths of this study include the real-life clinical setting in which the data were collected, and the repeated grip measurements yielding a unique dataset. Grip measurements were done with the Eforto® vigorimeter, which facilitates standardisation and minimizes interobserver fluctuations [15]. Limitations include the somewhat selective sample of patients who were fit enough to participate in the repeated measurements. Patients admitted to the geriatrics ward are generally acutely ill, with high levels of frailty and morbidity, and with a high percentage of people with some degree of cognitive decline. While our sample on average also had high levels of frailty and multimorbidity, patients with a life expectancy of less than two weeks or severe cognitive impairments were excluded. Hence, our sample included the relatively healthier subsample of the population seen at the geriatrics ward, limiting the generalizability of the findings. However, this is the group in which testing for recovery capacity is most clinically relevant. Another limitation Is that the outcomes were measured at admission and not at discharge. Hence, these outcomes do not reflect actual recovery during admission. Although no measurements could be conducted at discharge (due to logistic/practical constraints) we did measure functional limitations at 3 and 6 months post-discharge. Prospective associations of grip measures with recovery in functional limitations are reported elsewhere (Swart et al., in progress). Furthermore, there is not one measure that captures the multidimensional construct of recovery. Further prospective studies are required that include a mix of measures that together capture recovery. Finally, many comparisons were made, raising the issue of multiple testing. To reduce the risk of type I error when interpreting single associations, we used a more conservative level of significance (alpha set at 0.01) and reported 99% confidence intervals. While the associations found were not statistically significant, the point estimates were in the expected direction and align with findings reported in the literature, as discussed above.

In conclusion, the current findings confirm that the constructs of grip strength and capacity to perceived vitality are partly overlapping with the construct of frailty, while fatigue resistance and grip work seem to reflect other constructs than frailty. The associations with functional limitations support the notion that fatigue resistance, grip work and capacity to perceived vitality are interesting candidate markers for recovery capacity in hospitalized older adults. When measuring these measures in research or clinical practice, we recommend using the average of two measurements to increase the reliability and sensitivity to detect associations with outcomes. The variance in measures over time may provide additional information but further research is needed. Furthermore, we recommend imputing missing values on the fatigue resistance test with low values (e.g. zero), when the missing values are caused by poor test performance to minimise bias. The high feasibility and low costs of these measurements warrant further prospective research.
