Hypoxic burden and respiratory support escalation within 72 hours after video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery pulmonary resection in lung cancer patients with combined fibrotic interstitial lung disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Lina Hao, Honglei Wang, Chunquan Liu, Haina Wu, Yefeng Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease (fILD) undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for lung cancer are at high risk of postoperative hypoxemia and respiratory deterioration. However, conventional assessment relies mainly on single-point peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) measurements, which fail to capture the cumulative burden of hypoxic exposure. This study aimed to evaluate whether a time-based postoperative hypoxic burden metric is associated with respiratory support escalation and postoperative outcomes.MethodsA total of 120 patients with fILD and lung cancer who underwent VATS pulmonary resection were retrospectively enrolled. Hypoxic burden proportion was defined as cumulative hours with SpO2 <90% divided by total monitoring hours within 0–72 h postoperatively. Patients were classified as no hypoxic burden (G0, n=36), mild hypoxic burden (G1, n=44), or severe hypoxic burden (G2, n=40). The primary outcome was respiratory support escalation within 72 h. Progressively adjusted logistic regression, sensitivity analysis excluding preoperative SpO2, and Firth penalized logistic regression were performed.ResultsRespiratory support escalation occurred in 24/120 patients (20.0%), including 2/36 (5.6%), 6/44 (13.6%), and 16/40 (40.0%) in the G0, G1, and G2 groups, respectively (P<0.001). Although severe hypoxic burden and continuous hypoxic burden were associated with escalation in unadjusted analysis, these associations were attenuated after adjustment. In the full model, neither severe hypoxic burden [G2 vs. G0: adjusted odds ratio (aOR) =1.21, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.11–13.01, P=0.87] nor continuous hypoxic burden (per 5-percentage-point increase: aOR =1.29, 95% CI: 0.89–1.89, P=0.18) remained significant. Sensitivity analysis excluding preoperative SpO2 showed a persistent association for continuous hypoxic burden, whereas Firth regression confirmed lobectomy and lower preoperative SpO2 as robust predictors. Severe hypoxic burden was associated with more postoperative pulmonary complications, prolonged oxygen therapy, and longer hospital/intensive care unit (ICU) stay.ConclusionsHypoxic burden was associated with postoperative complications and resource consumption, but its independent association with escalation was attenuated after adjustment. It may be better interpreted as a postoperative process marker rather than a standalone predictor.Keywords: Fibrotic interstitial lung disease (fILD), lung cancer, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), hypoxic burden, postoperative complications

Highlight box

Key findings

• Postoperative hypoxic burden was associated with respiratory support escalation in unadjusted analysis and was also related to postoperative pulmonary complications, prolonged oxygen therapy, and increased hospital/intensive care unit resource use. However, its independent association with escalation was attenuated after adjustment for baseline oxygenation reserve and surgical factors.

What is known and what is new?

• Postoperative hypoxemia is common after pulmonary resection, especially in patients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease (fILD). Traditional assessment mainly relies on single-point peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) values.

• This study introduces hypoxic burden as a time-based cumulative oxygenation metric and suggests that it may function as a postoperative process marker rather than a standalone independent predictor.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Hypoxic burden may help identify patients requiring closer postoperative surveillance and may support quality monitoring of perioperative respiratory care. Prospective multicenter studies using continuous oximetry and standardized fILD assessment are needed before routine clinical implementation.

Introduction

Fibrotic interstitial lung disease (fILD) is a group of disorders characterized by progressive pulmonary fibrosis, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern, connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease (CTD-ILD), and chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) with fibrotic phenotype (1). When fILD patients are complicated with lung cancer, the incidence of acute exacerbation (AE) after pulmonary resection is 9.3%, with mortality rates as high as 30–100%, representing one of the most formidable perioperative complications (2). A multicenter study in 2024 enrolling 4,073 lung cancer surgical patients demonstrated that persistent air leak, respiratory failure, and pneumonia were the most common postoperative complications in interstitial lung disease (ILD) patients, with significantly shortened overall survival (45 vs. 84 months) (3).

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) pulmonary resection (lobectomy or segmentectomy) has become the standard approach for fILD patients with lung cancer, reducing surgical trauma compared with thoracotomy (4). However, even with minimally invasive techniques, fILD patients still face substantial respiratory risks after surgery. A secondary analysis in 2023 identified one-lung ventilation (OLV) as an independent risk factor for postoperative AE in ILD patients [odds ratio (OR) =4.99, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.90–13.06] (1), while surgical extent directly impacted prognosis—lobectomy or segmentectomy increased AE risk by 3.83–5.7-fold compared with wedge resection (5). Recent multicenter registry evidence further highlighted the importance of surgical strategy in lung cancer patients with pre-existing ILD (6). A systematic review in 2025 further confirmed that perioperative antifibrotic therapy reduced AE risk by 69% [risk ratio (RR) =0.31] in IPF patients after pulmonary resection (7). Similarly, Bongiolatti et al. reported that perioperative antifibrotic treatment was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative AE in patients with IPF undergoing lung cancer surgery (8). These findings suggest that pharmacological intervention may improve outcomes, yet dynamic monitoring strategies during and after surgery remain to be optimized.

Postoperative hypoxemia is a common complication after pulmonary resection in fILD patients. Traditional assessment relies on single-point minimum SpO2 or binary hypoxic status (yes/no), which facilitates rapid clinical judgment but neglects the time-cumulative effect and dynamic fluctuation characteristics of oxygenation impairment (9,10). In recent years, the concept of “hypoxic burden” proposed in sleep medicine—defined as the proportion of time with nocturnal SpO2 below threshold (usually 90%)—has been demonstrated to predict cardiovascular mortality and atrial fibrillation recurrence more effectively than apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) (11). However, translation of the hypoxic burden concept from sleep settings to the perioperative domain, particularly for fILD patients with this “double hit” (underlying pulmonary fibrosis plus surgical trauma), has not been reported. The 72-hour period after VATS pulmonary resection represents a critical window for respiratory complications, during which dynamic oxygenation trajectories may contain rich prognostic information, yet traditional monitoring simplifies this into discrete data points, resulting in information waste. We hypothesized that hypoxic burden, as a quantifiable process phenotype, could characterize the continuous spectrum of early postoperative oxygenation exposure and identify respiratory deterioration risk more sensitively than single-point metrics.

This study introduces the hypoxic burden concept into postoperative management of fILD patients with lung cancer after VATS, aiming to: (I) establish a dynamic hypoxic burden calculation method based on routine nursing records; (II) evaluate the association between hypoxic burden and respiratory support escalation using progressively adjusted and sensitivity models; and (III) explore its clinical utility as a dynamic process marker for postoperative risk stratification and resource allocation. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0611/rc).

Methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective cohort study included adult patients diagnosed with lung cancer and preexisting fILD who underwent VATS segmentectomy or lobectomy at Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University between January 2019 and December 2024.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval No. 2025-P2-135-01; approval date: March 12, 2025), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patient selection and inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) lung cancer (preoperatively diagnosed or pathologically confirmed postoperatively); (III) surgical procedure: VATS segmentectomy or lobectomy; (IV) preexisting fILD; (V) postoperative 0–72 h SpO2 records meeting minimum quality threshold (valid time ≥70%).

Exclusion criteria: (I) preoperative endotracheal intubation/dependence on mechanical ventilation or ECMO; (II) surgical approach not meeting criteria: thoracotomy/conversion to thoracotomy, wedge resection as primary procedure, or combined with other major surgery; (III) immediate use of high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy (HFNC), noninvasive ventilation (NIV), or invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) within 0–1 h postoperatively; (IV) early postoperative death/transfer to another hospital resulting in inability to cover the observation window.

Definition of key variables

Definition of fILD: patients meeting either of the following criteria: (i) discharge diagnosis or respiratory specialist consultation confirming: IPF, UIP, pulmonary fibrosis, CTD-ILD, chronic HP with fibrotic phenotype, etc. (12); (ii) chest computed tomography (CT)/high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) imaging reports within 12 months preoperatively indicating fibrotic features in text description, such as “fibrosis”, “honeycombing”, “traction bronchiectasis”, “UIP-pattern”, “reticular opacities”, “predominantly subpleural and basal distribution”, etc.

Time zero and follow-up window: time zero (t=0) was defined as the time of first postoperative SpO2 recording. The primary observation window of this study was postoperative 0–72 h.

Exposure definition: pre-escalation hypoxic burden proportion: (i) postoperative SpO2 data (with timestamps) were extracted from nursing vital sign records and resampled hourly (fixed as “lowest SpO2 per hour”); (ii) hypoxic burden proportion was calculated as cumulative hours with SpO2 <90% divided by total available monitoring hours (10,13); (iii) for patients who experienced escalation, the hypoxic burden calculation interval was [t0, t_event); for patients without escalation, the calculation interval was [t0, t0 + 72 h) (truncated at discharge time if discharged earlier).

Grouping: patients were stratified into three groups according to the pre-escalation hypoxic burden proportion: no hypoxic burden group (G0, n=36), defined as a hypoxic burden proportion of 0; mild hypoxic burden group (G1, n=44), defined as a hypoxic burden proportion >0 and ≤ the median value among patients with non-zero hypoxic burden; severe hypoxic burden group (G2, n=40), defined as a hypoxic burden proportion above this median cutoff.

Observation indicators and outcome measures

Observation indicators

Demographics and comorbidities: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), coronary heart disease/heart failure, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), ILD subtype (IPF/UIP vs. non-IPF/unclassified). Baseline pulmonary function: preoperative resting SpO2. Surgical and anesthetic factors: surgical procedure (segmentectomy/lobectomy), operation time, blood transfusion (yes/no), postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) admission (yes/no). Nursing care factors: time to first ambulation (hours), pain score [Numerical Rating Scale (NRS)/Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), peak value within 72 h postoperatively]. Pathological diagnosis and pathological stage were also collected to characterize tumor-related baseline features.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome: first respiratory support escalation, that is, the first respiratory support escalation event within 72 h postoperatively, defined as escalation from conventional oxygen therapy (room air/nasal cannula/face mask) to HFNC, NIV, or endotracheal intubation/IMV, whichever occurred first as an unplanned escalation. The event time was determined by the timestamp of physician order execution/treatment initiation. If multiple escalation types occurred in the same patient, the earliest was recorded as the first event. Secondary outcomes: unplanned ICU transfer (transfer from general ward/recovery room to ICU postoperatively, excluding planned direct ICU admission postoperatively); prolonged oxygen therapy (oxygen therapy still required after >72 h); pulmonary complications (pneumonia/atelectasis/pleural effusion, etc.); length of hospital stay; length of ICU stay. Atelectasis requiring intervention referred to clinically documented atelectasis requiring active treatment, such as intensified airway clearance, respiratory physiotherapy, bronchoscopic suction/treatment, or oxygenation support adjustment when clinically indicated.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Continuous variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test, and categorical variables were compared using Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between hypoxic burden and respiratory support escalation within 72 h postoperatively. Hypoxic burden was analyzed both as a categorical variable, with the no hypoxic burden group as reference, and as a continuous variable per 5-percentage-point increase. To examine the influence of covariate adjustment, progressively adjusted models were constructed: Model 0 was unadjusted; Model 1 was adjusted for age and COPD; Model 2 was additionally adjusted for lobectomy; Model 3 was further adjusted for preoperative resting SpO2; and the full model was adjusted for age, COPD, lobectomy, preoperative resting SpO2, IPF/UIP, and operation time. A trend test was performed by entering hypoxic burden groups as an ordinal variable.

Because preoperative resting SpO2 may reflect baseline oxygenation reserve and may partly explain the relationship between postoperative hypoxic burden and escalation, sensitivity analyses excluding preoperative SpO2 were performed. Given the limited number of escalation events, Firth penalized logistic regression was further used to reduce small-sample bias and assess the robustness of the main findings. For secondary outcomes, categorical variables were analyzed using appropriate comparative tests or logistic regression when applicable, and continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. All tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 software and R software, as appropriate.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Between January 2019 and December 2024, 924 patients underwent VATS pulmonary resection for lung cancer. Among them, 176 patients had combined fILD and were further assessed for eligibility. After excluding 56 patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria, including thoracotomy or conversion to thoracotomy, wedge resection, combined major surgery, immediate postoperative use of HFNC/NIV/IMV within 0–1 h, insufficient valid SpO2 monitoring time, and early death or transfer preventing completion of the observation window, 120 patients were included in the final cohort (Figure 1). According to the postoperative 0–72 h hypoxic burden proportion, patients were divided into the no hypoxic burden group (G0, n=36), mild hypoxic burden group (G1, n=44), and severe hypoxic burden group (G2, n=40).

A total of 120 eligible patients undergoing VATS segmentectomy/lobectomy with combined fILD and lung cancer were enrolled, including 65 males (54.2%), with a mean age of 61.0±10.8 years; 34 patients (28.3%) had comorbid COPD, and preoperative resting SpO2 was 96.5%±1.8%. Patients were stratified into three groups according to pre-escalation hypoxic burden proportion: no hypoxic burden group (n=36), mild hypoxic burden group (n=44), and severe hypoxic burden group (n=40). Intergroup comparison of baseline characteristics showed that the severe hypoxic burden group was older (overall P<0.001), had lower preoperative resting SpO2 (overall P=0.007), and had a higher proportion of COPD (overall P=0.001). No significant differences were observed in sex, BMI, smoking history, coronary heart disease, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, CKD, ILD classification, pathologic diagnosis, or pathologic stage among the three groups (all P>0.05), indicating that tumor pathological features were generally comparable across hypoxic burden groups (Table 1).

Perioperative and early postoperative course

Comparison of perioperative surgical characteristics demonstrated that the severe hypoxia burden group had a higher proportion of lobectomy (37.5% vs. 11.1% vs. 22.7%, overall P=0.02), longer operation duration (179.6±28.6 vs. 144.6±30.6 vs. 147.7±33.7 min, overall P<0.001), more intraoperative blood loss (392.5±102.4 vs. 274.5±86.3 vs. 300.7±109.1 mL, overall P<0.001), and a higher rate of blood transfusion (30.0% vs. 16.7% vs. 4.5%, overall P=0.008).

Regarding early postoperative recovery, the severe hypoxia burden group had a higher peak pain score within 0–72 h after surgery (6.2±1.2 vs. 5.0±1.2 vs. 4.9±1.2, overall P<0.001), delayed time to first ambulation (35.0±8.3 h vs. 29.7±7.8 h vs. 29.9±6.4 h, overall P=0.002), and a higher rate of direct admission to the ICU postoperatively (37.5% vs. 13.9% vs. 20.5%, overall P=0.043) (Table 2).

Respiratory support escalation within 72 h

In the final included cohort, the overall incidence of first respiratory support escalation within 72 h after surgery was 20.0% (24/120). The upgrade was dominated by HFNC (14.2%), followed by NIV (3.3%) and reintubation/invasive ventilation (2.5%). After grouping by the proportion of pre-escalation hypoxia burden, the incidence of upgrade was 5.6%, 13.6%, and 40.0% in the no hypoxia burden, mild hypoxia burden, and severe hypoxia burden groups, respectively, with significant differences among groups (overall P<0.001) and an increasing trend (P for trend <0.001) (Table 3, Figure 3).

Association between hypoxic burden and respiratory support escalation

To clarify the attenuation of the association after covariate adjustment, progressively adjusted logistic regression models were constructed with hypoxic burden analyzed both as a categorical variable and as a continuous variable. In the unadjusted model, hypoxic burden was significantly associated with the risk of first respiratory support escalation within 72 h after surgery. Compared with the no hypoxic burden group, the severe hypoxic burden group had a markedly increased escalation risk (Model 0: OR =11.33, 95% CI: 2.38–53.94, P=0.002). When hypoxic burden was modeled as a continuous variable, each 5-percentage-point increase was also significantly associated with escalation risk (OR =1.53, 95% CI: 1.24–1.89, P<0.001). After sequential adjustment for age and COPD, the association between severe hypoxic burden and escalation remained significant (Model 1: aOR =9.96, 95% CI: 1.77–56.09, P=0.009). After further adjustment for lobectomy, the effect size decreased and became borderline significant (Model 2: aOR =5.82, 95% CI: 0.85–39.66, P=0.07). When preoperative resting SpO2 was additionally included, the association was substantially attenuated (Model 3: aOR =1.28, 95% CI: 0.14–11.84, P=0.83). In the full model adjusted for age, COPD, lobectomy, preoperative resting SpO2, IPF/UIP, and operation time, neither mild nor severe hypoxic burden remained significantly associated with respiratory support escalation (G1 vs. G0: aOR =0.69, 95% CI: 0.08–6.05, P=0.73; G2 vs. G0: aOR =1.21, 95% CI: 0.11–13.01, P=0.87). Similarly, the association for hypoxic burden as a continuous variable was attenuated after full adjustment (per 5-percentage-point increase: aOR =1.29, 95% CI: 0.89–1.89, P=0.18). These findings suggest that the unadjusted association between hypoxic burden and escalation was largely explained by baseline oxygenation reserve and surgical factors, particularly preoperative resting SpO2 and lobectomy (Table 4).

In the full multivariable model, decreased preoperative resting SpO2 and lobectomy remained the most stable predictors of respiratory support escalation, whereas age, COPD, IPF/UIP pattern, and operation time were not significantly associated with escalation risk (Table S1).

Because preoperative resting SpO2 may reflect baseline pulmonary reserve and may partly explain the relationship between postoperative hypoxic burden and respiratory support escalation, sensitivity analyses excluding preoperative resting SpO2 were performed. In these reduced models, hypoxic burden as a continuous variable remained significantly associated with escalation risk (reduced model 1: aOR =1.53, 95% CI: 1.15–2.04, P=0.003; reduced model 2: aOR =1.62, 95% CI: 1.15–2.27, P=0.006). In contrast, the grouped exposure showed a consistent but statistically non-significant direction (G2 vs. G0: reduced model 1, aOR =5.82, 95% CI: 0.85–39.66, P=0.07; reduced model 2, aOR =5.04, 95% CI: 0.61–41.31, P=0.13) (Table S2).

Given the limited number of escalation events, Firth penalized logistic regression was further conducted to reduce small-sample bias and model instability. The results were directionally consistent with the main logistic regression analysis. Hypoxic burden grouping remained non-significant after adjustment (extended Firth model: G1 vs. G0, aOR =0.69, 95% CI: 0.10–5.47, P=0.70; G2 vs. G0, aOR =1.14, 95% CI: 0.14–10.71, P=0.89). In contrast, lobectomy and lower preoperative resting SpO2 were consistently identified as robust predictors of respiratory support escalation (extended Firth model: lobectomy, aOR =10.51, 95% CI: 3.09–42.90, P<0.001; preoperative SpO2 per 1% decrease, aOR =2.21, 95% CI: 1.49–3.74, P<0.001) (Table S3).

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) and hospital outcomes

In the final cohort, the no hypoxic burden, mild hypoxic burden, and severe hypoxic burden groups exhibited a gradient of worsening across multiple secondary outcomes as the proportion of hypoxic burden increased. The incidence of unplanned ICU transfer was 22.2%, 27.3%, and 27.5% in the three groups, respectively, with no statistically significant difference among groups (overall P=0.80). In contrast, hospital length of stay (overall P<0.001), ICU length of stay (overall P<0.001), and prolonged oxygen therapy requirement (overall P<0.001) all showed significant differences, indicating that a higher proportion of severe hypoxic burden was associated with greater resource consumption. The overall incidence of PPCs was 19.2%, with rates of 13.9%, 11.4%, and 32.5% in the three groups, respectively (overall P=0.03). The severe hypoxic burden group had a higher incidence of PPCs compared with the other two groups. No formally documented AE-ILD or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) events were identified during the primary 72-h observation period (Table 5).

Discussion

Patients with fILD tend to deteriorate after invasive procedures and pulmonary surgery. Several studies have reported a significantly higher risk of postoperative AE and severe respiratory complications after pulmonary resection in patients with IPF (2,7). However, few studies have investigated the clinical value of continuous hypoxic burden in the early postoperative period. Our study demonstrated that 72-hour postoperative hypoxic burden was strongly associated with respiratory support escalation in unadjusted analysis, with the severe hypoxic burden group showing an escalation rate of 40.0%—7 times higher than that of the no burden group (5.6%). Nevertheless, this association was substantially attenuated after sequential adjustment, particularly after inclusion of lobectomy and preoperative resting SpO2, and hypoxic burden did not remain significant in the full model. Sensitivity analysis excluding preoperative SpO2 suggested that continuous hypoxic burden still retained an association with escalation risk, whereas Firth penalized regression confirmed lobectomy and lower preoperative SpO2 as the most robust predictors. Notably, hypoxic burden maintained strong associations with PPCs, prolonged oxygen therapy, and resource consumption (length of hospital and ICU stay), suggesting its potential value as a postoperative process marker rather than a standalone independent predictor.

The methodological innovation of this study resonates with advances in sleep medicine. Azarbarzin et al. found that hypoxic burden better predicted cardiovascular mortality than traditional AHI in sleep apnea studies [highest quintile in The Outcomes of Sleep Disorders in Older Men (MrOS) cohort: hazard ratio (HR) =2.73, 95% CI: 1.71–4.36; the Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS) cohort: HR =1.96, 95% CI: 1.11–3.43], whereas traditional AHI failed to significantly predict cardiovascular mortality in the same model, as hypoxic burden captured the cumulative biological effects of intermittent hypoxia (11). Similarly, we attempted to translate this “time-threshold” framework into the perioperative setting, but found its predictive power limited by the complexity of postoperative management in fILD patients. Compared with previous thoracic surgery risk prediction models, our study emphasizes the complementarity between dynamic process indicators and static baseline indicators. The pulmonary complication prediction model developed by Fernandez-Bustamante et al. relied on preoperative factors (age, BMI, pulmonary function) (14), while the Assess Respiratory Risk in Surgical Patients in Catalonia (ARISCAT) score by Canet et al. incorporated surgery-related variables (surgical site, duration, emergency surgery), yet neither integrated early postoperative continuous physiological data (15). A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that continuous pulse oximetry monitoring (CPOX) identified hypoxemia earlier than conventional monitoring (OR =11.94), but failed to significantly reduce ICU transfer or reintubation rates (16), suggesting that monitoring technology advancement alone does not equate to improved outcomes and requires integration with actionable intervention thresholds. Notably, a 2024 multicenter study reported that the combination of ILD disease course, non-cancerous interstitial pneumonia (IP) region maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), and D-dimer achieved an AUC of 0.963 for predicting postoperative AE (2), while a 2025 systematic review confirmed that perioperative antifibrotic therapy reduced AE risk by 69% in IPF patients (RR =0.31) (7). These studies collectively indicate that postoperative respiratory risk in fILD patients represents a function of baseline vulnerability, surgical trauma intensity, and perioperative management quality. Accordingly, postoperative hypoxic burden may be useful not because it independently replaces baseline predictors, but because it summarizes cumulative oxygenation exposure during early postoperative recovery.

The attenuated association between hypoxic burden and escalation risk after adjustment may involve the following mechanisms. First, preoperative SpO2 and postoperative hypoxic burden are likely located within a shared pathophysiological pathway rather than functioning as fully independent predictors. Patients with poor baseline oxygenation reserve are more prone to sustained hypoxia postoperatively, while baseline reserve represents a critical determinant of escalation requirements. Thus, preoperative SpO2 may function as an upstream determinant rather than a simple confounder, which explains why excluding it from sensitivity models partially restored the association between continuous hypoxic burden and escalation. In our study, each 1% decrease in preoperative SpO2 increased risk by approximately 2.4–2.6-fold (aOR =2.38–2.60), confirming the dominant role of baseline reserve. Second, respiratory support escalation represents a multifactorial decision process influenced not only by objective oxygenation data but also by subjective symptoms, clinician experience, and ICU bed availability (17,18). As a purely objective indicator, hypoxic burden may fail to capture these contextual factors. Third, our 72-hour assessment window may capture both early deterioration and subsequent treatment response. Escalation decisions are typically made within 24–48 hours postoperatively (19), whereas cumulative hypoxic burden may more reflect the overall postoperative oxygenation trajectory rather than an isolated imminent warning signal.

Despite failing to emerge as an independent predictor, the clinical value of hypoxic burden manifests at three levels: risk stratification refinement: hypoxic burden enables subdivision of postoperative patients into distinct recovery trajectory subgroups. Patients with severe burden (G2) demonstrated significantly higher PPC incidence (32.5%), prolonged oxygen therapy requirement (37.5%), and ICU length of stay (median 4 days) compared with other groups, indicating the need for enhanced care pathways (20). Hypoxic burden may also serve as a process quality indicator reflecting the integrated effects of surgical extent, baseline pulmonary reserve, analgesia, airway clearance, and early mobilization (21). In addition, because hypoxic burden is a continuous exposure metric, it may be useful as an exploratory endpoint in future perioperative intervention studies, especially when binary events such as respiratory support escalation are relatively infrequent (22). This is particularly important for intervention studies evaluating perioperative antifibrotic therapy or minimally invasive techniques. This study possesses several methodological strengths: First, we employed a time-threshold integration method to transform discrete SpO2 recordings into continuous exposure metrics, better aligning with cumulative injury models of pathophysiology than single-point measurements. Second, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria (excluding patients escalated within 1 hour postoperatively) ensured hypoxic burden calculation remained unaffected by treatment interference (23). Third, the use of progressively adjusted models, sensitivity analysis excluding preoperative SpO2, and Firth penalized logistic regression improved the transparency and robustness of the statistical interpretation, particularly given the limited number of escalation events.

There are several limitations in this study. First, this was a retrospective single-center study with only 24 escalation events, which limits causal inference, generalizability, and statistical power despite the use of Firth penalized regression. Second, SpO2 data were extracted from routine nursing records rather than continuous high-resolution monitoring; therefore, intermittent monitoring, nighttime gaps, and the fixed SpO2 threshold of <90% may have led to exposure misclassification or underestimation of hypoxic events. Third, the broad fILD definition based on clinical diagnosis and CT/HRCT reports may have introduced misclassification, and detailed ILD-related data, including spirometry, diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO), Gender, Age and Physiology (GAP) score, antifibrotic therapy, steroid use, and rescue pharmacological treatments after respiratory deterioration, were not consistently available. Finally, AE-ILD and ARDS were not predefined or systematically adjudicated outcomes, and the 72-h observation window may have underestimated their incidence. Future prospective multicenter studies should validate these findings using standardized fILD phenotyping, continuous oximetry, individualized thresholds, and standardized postoperative respiratory management.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort of fILD patients undergoing VATS pulmonary resection for lung cancer, early postoperative hypoxic burden was associated with respiratory support escalation in unadjusted analysis and was also related to PPCs, prolonged oxygen therapy, and increased hospital/ICU resource use. However, its association with escalation was attenuated after adjustment for baseline oxygenation reserve and surgical factors, particularly preoperative resting SpO2 and lobectomy. These findings suggest that hypoxic burden may serve as a dynamic postoperative process marker rather than a standalone independent predictor. Prospective multicenter studies using standardized fILD phenotyping and continuous oximetry are needed to validate its clinical utility.
