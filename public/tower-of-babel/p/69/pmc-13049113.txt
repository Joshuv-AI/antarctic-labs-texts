A lightweight convolutional neural network for real-time monitoring of smart mango orchard systems
Authors: Md Taimur Ahad, Noman H Chowdhury, Ainuddin Ahmed, Angkita Averi Noor, Bo Song, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advancements in Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), combined with the growing adoption of farm-applicable Internet of Things (IoT) devices, have expanded the application of precision agriculture in mango orchards. The Smart Mango Orchard can play a crucial role in ensuring mango trees thrive and produce high-quality fruit. However, state-of-the-art (SOTA) CNNs are built on numerous layers and many parameters; therefore, they are challenging to deploy in IoT devices. However, the lightweight CNN is a possible solution. This study developed a lightweight CNN, mangoNet, to deploy in an innovative mango orchard environment. The mangoNet is expected to monitor the mango leaf images and report them to farmers via a mobile app using the IoT system. The study was conducted using the primary dataset collected from the mango gardens in Rajshahi, Bangladesh. The mangoNet benchmark was evaluated using six SOTA CNNs. The mangoNet, with only 3,987,400 Parameters, outperforms SOTA CNNs’ accuracy (99.61%). In addition, this study employed SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM visualizations to identify and depict the image regions that contribute to mangoNet’s decision-making process. The mangoNet is integrated into a Streamlit web application and an Android mobile app, as researchers suggest for the practical use of CNNs. The novelty of mangoNet lies in its balanced sequential architecture, with a careful selection of kernels and progressive filter expansion, enabling early layers to capture low-level features and deeper layers to extract high-level features. As a result, the proposed mangoNet achieved high accuracy while requiring fewer computational resources and reduced training time. In addition, the mangoNet-powered website and mobile application empower both farmers and farming stakeholders by making real-time disease detection. In the future, the prototype is expected to be commercialized as Bangladesh is the 8Th mango-producing country in the world.Keywords: Mango leaf detection, Precision agriculture, Convolutional neural network, CNN, Lightweight CNN, SHAP, LIME, Grad-CAMSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Plant sciences

Introduction

Bangladesh is the world’s 7th-largest mango-growing country. Mango orchards are the primary source of income in the mango-growing zone, as other crops are not suitable for that land and weather. Mangoes are susceptible to leaf diseases, which significantly impact their quality and production. Mango leaf disease slowly spreads into the tree and the orchard. Diagnosing mango leaf diseases with the human eye can be difficult, time-consuming, and often inaccurate. The mango disease is caused by variations in environmental parameters1 and by climate change2. However, the frequency of mango disease is increasing, and its intensity is very severe. Early prediction of disease on mango leaves can help farmers prevent potential losses.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have revolutionized precision agriculture by enabling accurate and efficient disease identification3,4. Among deep CNNs, two forms of CNN, the ensemble model, which combines multiple models, and the transfer learning method, are highlighted as effective methods in mango leaf disease detection. However5, criticized deep CNN architectures for having a large memory footprint and high computational demand, a criticism also supported by6. Lightweight CNNs (L-CNNs), therefore, aim to reduce computational complexity and memory usage7. L-Nets are mainly developed using (1) network architecture5,8,9, (2) nonlinear activation functions7,10, (3) supervision components11, (4) regularization mechanisms12, and (5) optimization techniques13. However, L-Net has recently attracted significant attention from researchers in mango leaf detection and classification, e.g14–17., who have presented L-Net for mango leaf classification.

With the proliferation of the Internet of Things (IoT) and the adaptability of CNNs on edge devices in IoT settings, researchers are also proposing a smart mango orchard for real-time mango disease monitoring and management. The combined technologies of the Internet of Things (IoT) and CNNs can offer scalable, sustainable strategies for disease detection and management. Such technology is expected to bridge the gap between traditional and smart mango orchard management. High-speed internet, advancements in sensors, applications such as radio frequency identification (RFID) and wireless sensor networks (WSNs), and improved mobile technologies, combined, present an opportunity to transform traditional mango orchard management into a smart mango orchard. The advantages of a smart mango orchard can be achieved via deep learning. In such a system, computations are performed on an edge device, which reduces power consumption and latency. A smart mango orchard integrated with deep learning has the potential to create an enabling environment for early detection and real-time monitoring of mango leaf disease.

Another extension of CNN is the integration of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) to visualize the decision-making process of CNN models, thereby developing transparency and interpretability18. XAI visualization illustrates how CNN aids in understanding the model’s focus areas during decision-making; these techniques aim to identify the regions and features in input images that contribute most to the model’s predictions19. Local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME), Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), and Grad-CAM are some XAI.

The core problem of this study is that many studies have attempted to develop lightweight CNNs; a standard benchmark for lightweight CNNs is accuracy. For example14, developed a lightweight CNN with 101,000 parameters that achieves a 99.14% success rate in disease leaf identification. However, edge devices require efficient processing, reduced storage, and limited computational requirements from the mobile application, which can be achieved through edge computing without using the cloud20. However, these analyses are often not presented in a lightweight CNN. Furthermore, reducing ML models’ size without significant loss in accuracy is another important benchmark for ML Models on edge devices. In addition, the performance evaluation used images captured with high-resolution cameras or in a controlled environment, which are far from the real conditions that edge devices might encounter. Inference time after the model is loaded from disk into memory is also a critical aspect21. Another limitation of existing lightweight CNNs is that although many studies produce high-accuracy models, they do not report performance on unseen real-world mango leaf diseases. The gap exists in lightweight CNNs that balance model accuracy with resource efficiency.

Several motivating factors are involved in this study. The gradient losses in the CNN’s later layers motivated us to investigate a balanced CNN architecture with kernel and filter expansion, enabling early layers to capture low-level features and deeper layers to extract high-level features. We aimed to integrate the MaxPooling and dropout layers to provide robust feature learning and regularization, stabilize gradient flow, and reduce the risk of vanishing gradients during CNN training. The complex, intricate network architectures of CNNs are a limitation for applying them on edge and mobile devices. This leads the researchers to use a lightweight CNN.

A review of around thirty-five selected papers is presented in this paper. All of the articles cover the classification and detection of plant leaves using transformer-based deep learning techniques. Articles were selected for final review using a three-stage screening process based on a series of inclusion and exclusion criteria. After removing duplicate records generated by using two databases, articles were first screened solely by title. Hardware and software specifications

The experiments were conducted on a Dell Precision 7680 mobile workstation equipped with a 13th-generation Intel Core i9-13950HX vPro processor running Windows 11 Pro, an NVIDIA RTX 3500 Ada Generation graphics card, 32 GB of DDR5 memory, and a 1 TB SSD. Python (version 3.9) was used as the programming language, as this version supported TensorFlow-GPU, SHAP, and LIME.

Dataset description

In this study, two sets of mango leaf disease datasets were used. The primary dataset (Dataset A) was collected from mango orchards in Kushtia and Dhaka, Bangladesh, two major mango-growing regions in the country. Data collection occurred between August 19 and August 22, 2023. The corresponding and principal author is the owner of this dataset, which will be made available upon request. Additionally, the study utilized publicly available data from the following https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/aryashah2k/mango-leaf-disease-dataset.

Tree selection was based on accessibility, leaf availability, and maturity, ensuring a sufficient representation of the targeted diseases. While trees were randomly chosen, priority was given to those with an adequate number of affected leaves. Only mature and visibly diseased leaves were included in the dataset. After collection, leaves were sorted into disease-specific classes under expert guidance from the Bangladesh Agriculture Association to ensure accurate classification. Eight labelled buckets were used for sorting, and care was taken to select undamaged, non-rotten leaves. A dataset of healthy and diseased mango leaves was compiled, covering diseases such as Anthracnose, Bacterial Canker, Cutting Weevil, Die Back, Gall Midge, Powdery Mildew, and Sooty Mould.

The leaves were placed on a white background for high-resolution photography. Special attention was given to image clarity, as it is crucial to reliable identification and classification. To mitigate overfitting and enhance generalization, a unified set of augmentation techniques was applied across all datasets, including geometric transformations (± 15° rotations, center cropping) and photometric adjustments (brightness/contrast modulation, random scaling). Horizontal and vertical flipping were incorporated to increase dataset diversity, as commonly practiced in mammography deep learning to simulate orientation variations39,40. Image augmentation

Image augmentation has been a practical approach to enhancing the robustness of CNN models. This study uses image transformations, such as scaling, rotation, and contrast adjustment, to enhance training datasets and improve the performance of CNNs across a range of complex tasks. Specifically, in the context of mango leaf disease detection, the technique allows models to handle better variability in leaf disease presentation, including differences in size, shape, and location22,23,25–27,35.

Design choice and developing mangoNet for an IoT environment

Since the aim of the study was to develop a lightweight CNN for an IoT environment, ensuring efficient operation on resource-constrained IoT devices was the primary goal. Firstly, the model was created using a sequential CNN. A sequential design simplifies the network and reduces computational complexity. mangoNet CNN is designed to extract low-level to high-level features of the mango leaves. It first focuses on the edges, then on textures, and finally on objects or spots on the leaves. Each layer feeds into the next, providing a linear computational flow. Moreover, the sequential structure ensures faster training and inference times. mangoNet includes five convolutional layers with progressively expanding filter sizes (48, 128, 198, 128) (see Fig. 7 for a layered view and Fig. 8 for visualization). This layered approach helps the model effectively capture low-level features (edges, veins) in the early layers and high-level features (disease spots) in deeper layers. Number of kernels used: 48, 128, 198, and 128, and size 3 × 3. The 3 × 3 kernel size captures slightly smaller spatial features than 4 × 4 (with 9 parameters per filter compared to 16). This balance improves the model’s performance while keeping the number of parameters low. MaxPooling layers were used to achieve spatial invariance, ensuring that the model remains robust to changes in leaf orientation or size. The use of Dropout (50%) and early stopping ensures the model does not overfit. The Adam optimizer was chosen for its ability to converge quickly and effectively on datasets of limited size, ensuring optimal performance even in resource-limited settings. These choices are supported by empirical evidence in the literature, such as28, who demonstrated that lightweight CNNs optimized with Adam perform well in agricultural disease detection.

Discussion

This research aimed to present a lightweight CNN suitable for IoT devices. Unlike other studies45–72, which provided only machine learning models, this study adopted a practical approach by integrating mangoNet into the edge device. The main characteristics, therefore, of the lightweight CNN are limited parameters, non-intricate, and straightforward network architectures. Moreover, the model must provide high accuracy. The authors noted that the accuracy of machine learning models is insufficient, as they have shown poor performance in real-world settings42. With only 3,987,400 parameters and 99% accuracy on unseen test images, mangoNet is a suitable model for a smart mango orchard. The findings suggest that the proposed mangoNet effectively classifies mango diseases using leaf images and achieves the highest accuracy (99.61%) compared to SOTA CNN models.

The mangoNet was trained and evaluated using three Python data generation techniques: TensorFlow/Keras, flow_from_directory, flow_from_dataframe, and flow (used with NumPy arrays). It was evident that flow_from_directory provides better results than the other two data pipelines. The flow_from_directory function efficiently loads images batch by batch, reducing memory usage compared to loading the entire dataset into RAM using flow_from_dataframe and flow (which is typically used with NumPy arrays). Adam remains a stable performer, while RMSprop fluctuates across models, underscoring the importance of carefully selecting an optimizer based on architecture-specific convergence behavior.

Among the SOTA CNNs, DenseNet121 and VGG16 achieved the best accuracies of 99% and 98%, respectively (see Table 4; Fig. 24). InceptionV3, ResNet50, and Xception achieved 97% accuracy across most classes. However, MobileNetV2 performed the least, with 78% accuracy. Table 4 shows the classification report of SOTA CNNs. In this study, we have used MobileNet’s baseline architecture. Although MobileNet is often described as a lightweight CNN, its depth and width reduction techniques, along with image compression, result in lower classification accuracy38. The study39 discusses the limitations of MobileNetV2, particularly its suboptimal feature extraction compared to other lightweight architectures. Therefore, studies such as26,43,44 improved MobileNet for leaf disease detection. Among SOTA CNNs, the DenseNet architecture is more efficient in detecting and classifying mango leaf disease. Our findings are also supported by28,36. The study by28 Applied DenseNet121 and achieved the best result (99%) for mango leaf disease identification. The study by36 argued that the DenseNet architecture uses densely connected skip connections. The DenseNet connection pattern connects all layers directly, ensuring maximum information flow throughout the network. The feed-forward nature for each layer is maintained by receiving input from the previous layers. However, the authors have already noted the challenges posed by gradient-based loss functions in leaf image analysis37,45. In agricultural datasets, the imbalance class is also a common challenge for loss functions. In summary, DenseNet is a well-performing CNN model in which all layers are connected to the network.

Lastly, this study applied three (3) XAI techniques, LIME, SHAP, and Grad-CAM to expand our understanding of mangoNet’s decision-making process. The pixel-intensity analysis using a statistical method contributes to the growing literature on using XAI to enhance image analysis and diagnosis. It provides insights into the interpretability and transparency of CNN for mango leaf disease classification.

Contribution

The mangoNet was developed for an IoT-based mango orchard. The model was developed to detect mango leaf disease, and its accuracy in classifying two mango leaf datasets positioned it as a suitable lightweight model. The accuracy comparison with SOTA CNNs, transfer learning and ensemble models supports its acceptability over other variants. The acceptability of mangoNet is also achieved by including extensive computational cost analysis and statistical analysis. Lastly, the integration of XAI and in-depth analysis of pixel intensity of correctly classified and misclassified mango leaves has expanded the use of XAI techniques. The study articulates its objectives, encompassing model development, constraints, and performance evaluation relative to benchmark works. The novelty of mangoNet lies in its balanced sequential architecture with 4 × 4 kernels and progressive filter expansion, enabling early layers to capture low-level features and deeper layers to extract high-level features. With only 3,987,400 Total parameters, the mangoNet integrated website and mobile application is expected to empower farmers, agricultural departments, and farming stakeholders, enabling deployment in farming environments to support the diagnosis of mango leaves.

Conclusion and future work

This study presents a lightweight CNN, mangoNet, for smart mango orchards, enabling real-time disease monitoring. The results show that the lightweight mangoNet model outperforms 6 SOTA CNNs on a dataset of 7023 mango leaf images and is therefore more applicable in an IoT environment. By utilizing XAI techniques such as LIME, SHAP, and Grad-CAM, we gain insight into the model’s decision-making process. Additionally, pixel-intensity analysis played a vital role in understanding how the model distinguished between healthy and diseased leaves, thereby contributing to accurate classification.

Although this study makes several contributions, it also has some limitations. The primary limitation is that the model was trained on a custom dataset, which may still contain inherent biases in data collection and annotation. Secondly, the model was tested only on mango leaf images, limiting its generalizability to other plant species and disease types. In the future, the model should be applied to a broader range of plant diseases across different crops to assess its robustness. Additionally, the experimental results were not validated by plant pathology experts; instead, the results were based solely on computational evaluations. The results were tested on the unseen dataset, and the test results were manually confirmed to match the class. Furthermore, the outcome of the implemented Android APP was validated with the orchard owner, who had significant experience in mango farming. However, the outcome was not validated in a real-world setting, as no organization funded the study to purchase the necessary IoT infrastructure. Lastly, an agriculturist from the university confirmed the validity of the test result and the detection using the mangoNet APP. Future studies should incorporate expert validation to enhance the reliability of the findings. Furthermore, this study did not explore transfer learning; instead, it focused on training a lightweight CNN from scratch. Future research should investigate whether pre-trained models can further improve mango leaf disease classification. Finally, this study used Python (TensorFlow/Keras) for implementation; future work could explore different programming languages and frameworks to optimize performance and scalability.

In conclusion, our investigation demonstrates the effectiveness of CNN in detecting and classifying mango leaf diseases. Our model achieved high accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 scores on the test set, validating its performance. Additionally, LIME and SHAP visualizations provided both local and global explanations for the model’s predictions, highlighting critical regions for disease classification. Moving forward, we identify two key directions for future research: expanding the dataset to include a wider range of environmental conditions and exploring advanced deep learning architectures to improve generalization. Overall, our study presents a promising approach to a smart mango orchard for real-time leaf disease detection, and we hope our findings encourage further research in this important domain.
