Implementation and outcomes of mental health literacy training in pre-licensure health professions education: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Rouwida El Khalil, Imad Maalouf, Yasir Ahmed Mohammed Elhadi, Wafaa El Zaatari, Noon Alrabee, Anuradha P Gaydhanker, Moustafa Sherif, Aya Darwish, Messaouda Belfakir, Eny Qurniyawati, Abdulsalam El Mikkawi, Mohamad AlMekkawi, Mohammed AlMaqbali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMental health literacy (MHL) is increasingly recognized as a core competency for future health professionals. In pre-licensure health professions education, MHL encompasses competencies relevant to both patient care and students’ mental health and well-being. Although a wide range of educational interventions have been implemented to improve MHL among health professions students, evidence on their outcomes, underlying mechanisms, and contextual influences remains fragmented and heterogeneous. This review aimed to evaluate the outcomes of MHL interventions in pre-licensure health professions education and to identify the mechanisms and contextual conditions that influence their impact.MethodsA mixed-methods systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and PRISMA 2020 guidelines (PROSPERO CRD420251050891). Quantitative studies assessing changes in knowledge, attitudes, skills, confidence/self-efficacy, or behavioral and well-being outcomes were synthesized using a random-effects meta-analysis. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies exploring learner experiences and implementation factors were synthesized thematically. Findings were integrated using a convergent-segregated approach to provide explanatory insight into the observed quantitative effects.ResultsFifty-five studies were included. The meta-analysis demonstrated a statistically significant, moderate improvement in knowledge following MHL interventions (standardized mean difference = 0.56). In contrast, pooled effects for attitudes, skills, confidence/self-efficacy, and mental health or well-being outcomes were not statistically significant and showed substantial heterogeneity. Qualitative synthesis identified three interrelated mechanisms associated with deeper learning outcomes: experiential contact, structured reflective practice with guided debriefing, and person-centered, recovery-oriented framing. These mechanisms were implemented inconsistently across interventions, helping to explain variability and the attenuation of pooled effects. Short-term or single-session interventions were sufficient to improve knowledge but provided less consistent evidence of benefit for attitudinal, skill-based, and behavioral outcomes, which appeared to be better addressed by longitudinal, experiential, and contextually embedded approaches.ConclusionsMHL interventions in pre-licensure health professions education demonstrate consistent benefits for knowledge acquisition, while more complex outcomes depend on how interventions are implemented and embedded within curricula. Integrating cognitive learning with experiential and reflective pedagogies and delivering training longitudinally may be important for achieving meaningful and sustained outcomes. Mixed-methods integration provides insight into how MHL training may influence outcomes and the conditions under which it is most likely to be effective, informing the design of more robust and sustainable educational strategies for the future healthcare workforce.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09951-9.Keywords: Mental health literacy, Health professions education, Educational interventions, Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Curriculum design

Introduction

Mental disorders represent a major global public health challenge, contributing substantially to morbidity and overall disease burden worldwide [1]. The World Health Organization defines mental health as a state of well-being in which individuals can cope with the stresses of life, realize their abilities, learn and work effectively, and contribute to their communities [2]. Despite this recognition, approximately one in eight people globally live with a mental disorder, with anxiety and depressive disorders being the most prevalent [3]. Yet only a minority of affected individuals receive effective care. For example, an estimated 29% of people diagnosed with psychosis and approximately one-third of those with depression access formal mental health services [3]. These treatment gaps are particularly pronounced among young people, with around one in seven adolescents aged 10–19 years experiencing a mental disorder globally, with depression, anxiety, and behavioral disorders among the leading contributors to illness and disability in this age group [4]. Persistent under-resourcing of mental health services and workforce shortages further exacerbate these disparities across health systems [5].

In response to these challenges, health professions education has placed increasing emphasis on mental health training to equip future clinicians across medicine, nursing, and allied health disciplines with the competencies required to recognize, respond to, and help prevent mental health problems in diverse care settings [6]. Central to many educational approaches is mental health literacy (MHL), originally defined by Jorm and colleagues as “knowledge and beliefs about mental disorders which aid their recognition, management, or prevention” [7]. Contemporary conceptualizations of MHL have expanded this definition to encompass the promotion of positive mental health, reduction of stigma, enhancement of help-seeking efficacy, and the ability to support others experiencing mental distress [8]. Within health professions education, MHL has both professional and personal dimensions. Professionally, it includes the knowledge, attitudes, and skills required to recognize, assess, support, and appropriately refer individuals experiencing mental health problems. Personally, it encompasses awareness of one’s own mental health, help-seeking efficacy, self-management, and wellbeing. Educational interventions may target either or both dimensions, and outcomes should therefore be interpreted within the context of their intended focus. Among health professions students, higher levels of MHL have been associated with improved identification and management of mental disorders, greater professional confidence, and the delivery of more competent and non-stigmatizing care [8–10].

A wide range of educational interventions have been implemented to enhance MHL within pre-licensure health professions education, including didactic teaching, simulation-based learning, clinical placements, contact-based education, and online or blended learning formats [11, 12]. These interventions commonly target multiple outcome domains, such as knowledge acquisition, attitudes and stigma reduction, skills, confidence or self-efficacy, and, less frequently, behavioral and wellbeing-related outcomes. However, the existing evidence base is highly heterogeneous with respect to study design, intervention content and intensity, outcome measurement, and educational context. Moreover, previous systematic reviews have predominantly focused on community- or school-based populations or on the psychometric properties of MHL measurement instruments, rather than on the effectiveness and implementation of MHL interventions specifically within health professions education [9, 10, 13]. As a result, important questions remain regarding the outcomes of MHL training for health professions students, the pedagogical and contextual factors that influence its impact, and the extent to which learning translates into professional competence and practice readiness.

This study aimed to evaluate the outcomes of mental health literacy interventions implemented in pre-licensure health professions education and to examine the mechanisms and contextual conditions that influence their impact. The specific objectives were: (1) to quantify the effects of MHL interventions on students’ knowledge, attitudes and stigma reduction, skills, confidence or self-efficacy, and mental health or wellbeing outcomes; (2) to synthesize qualitative evidence on learner experiences, including perceived barriers, facilitators, and implementation considerations; and (3) to integrate quantitative and qualitative findings to develop an explanatory understanding of how, for whom, and under what circumstances MHL interventions are most likely to be effective.

Methods

Study design and registration

This mixed-methods systematic review was conducted to synthesize evidence on the outcomes of MHL interventions in pre-licensure health professions education. The review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for mixed-methods systematic reviews [14] and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [15]. A convergent, segregated mixed-methods design was employed, whereby quantitative and qualitative evidence were synthesized separately and integrated at the interpretation stage. All methods were pre-specified in a registered protocol (PROSPERO CRD420251050891). The review comprised a quantitative synthesis of intervention outcomes complemented by a qualitative synthesis of learner experiences and implementation factors, enabling explanatory interpretation of observed quantitative effects.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility was determined using the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, Study design). The search was restricted to peer-reviewed studies published in English from February 1997, corresponding to the formal introduction of the MHL construct, up to May 2025 [7].

Population

The review included pre-licensure undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled in health professions education programs, including medicine, nursing, pharmacy, dentistry, psychology, midwifery, and allied health disciplines. Studies involving qualified clinicians or students from non–health-related fields were excluded.

Intervention

Eligible studies evaluated structured educational interventions explicitly designed to improve one or more domains of MHL. Interventions were required to target at least one core MHL domain derived from established frameworks: (1) knowledge of mental health disorders, their etiology, or treatment; (2) attitudes or stigma-related outcomes, including confidence; (3) skills, such as communication, screening, or peer support; or (4) behaviors, including help-seeking or referral practices. General curriculum components without a specific mental health focus were excluded.

Comparator

Studies with any comparator condition (e.g., no intervention, wait-list control, or alternative educational intervention) were eligible. Single-group pre–post studies were also included to capture the breadth of available evidence on intervention outcomes.

Outcomes

For the quantitative synthesis, outcomes of interest included measurable changes in knowledge, attitudes or stigma reduction, skills, confidence or self-efficacy, and mental health or wellbeing, where reported. The qualitative synthesis focused on learner experiences, perceived barriers and facilitators, and contextual and implementation-related factors.

Study designs

To address the review’s dual aims, multiple study designs were included. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental studies with between-group or pre–post designs were eligible for quantitative synthesis. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies were included to explore learner experiences and contextual influences. Observational reports without evaluative outcome data were excluded from quantitative analyses but were included in the qualitative synthesis if they contained relevant experiential findings. Secondary research, non–peer-reviewed publications, expert opinion pieces, and studies involving non-human subjects were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, EBSCOhost databases (CINAHL, PsycINFO, and ERIC), and PsycArticles via ProQuest. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary 1A. Controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords were combined with Boolean operators to identify studies on health professions students, mental health literacy, and educational interventions. Reference lists of included studies were also screened to identify additional eligible publications.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers using Covidence. Full-text screening was subsequently conducted independently by two additional reviewers according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved through discussion and consensus or, when necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form developed for this review. Extracted variables included study identifiers (author, year, country, study design), participant characteristics (profession, level of training, sample size, age, and gender), and intervention characteristics (intervention name, theoretical basis, duration, delivery mode, facilitator role, and experiential components). Comparator details were recorded where applicable.

Quantitative outcome data were extracted for all reported MHL domains, including outcome labels, measurement instruments, scoring direction, assessment timing, and statistical results (means, standard deviations, sample sizes, effect estimates, confidence intervals, and p-values). Qualitative data, including reported themes and illustrative quotations, were extracted to capture learner experiences and implementation issues. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Corresponding authors were contacted to obtain missing or unclear data where necessary, and multiple reports arising from the same study were collated to avoid duplication.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

To support effect estimation and ensure comparability across study designs, a two-tiered approach to risk-of-bias and quality appraisal was implemented. Appraisal tools were applied hierarchically according to their intended analytic purpose. For quantitative studies, design-specific tools were applied: the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool was used for randomized controlled trials [16], while nonrandomized comparative studies were assessed using the ROBINS-I tool [17]. Assessments derived from RoB 2 and ROBINS-I were considered primary and directly informed the quantitative synthesis, sensitivity analyses, and interpretation of pooled effect estimates.

To maintain consistency across designs and to evaluate the methodological quality of qualitative and mixed-methods studies alongside quantitative ones, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was also employed [18]. MMAT assessments were used descriptively to characterize and compare methodological quality across study designs and did not influence study inclusion, effect size estimation, or weighting in meta-analyses.

Qualitative synthesis

Qualitative findings were synthesized using a thematic synthesis approach as described by Thomas and Harden (2008) [19]. This process involved three iterative stages: (1) line-by-line coding of study findings and participant quotations; (2) development of descriptive themes capturing recurring experiences and perceptions; and (3) generation of analytical themes providing higher-order interpretations across studies. Coding was conducted inductively and supported by an audit trail maintained in Excel to enhance transparency. The approach aligns with established principles of qualitative meta-synthesis emphasizing interpretive depth and theoretical contribution [20, 21].

Two reviewers independently coded all qualitative data, resolving discrepancies through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Rigor was enhanced through constant comparison, reflexive team discussions, and documentation of analytic decisions.

Effect size computation and quantitative synthesis

Continuous outcome data were extracted as means and standard deviations at baseline and at the first post-intervention assessment, along with corresponding sample sizes. For randomized and non-randomized controlled studies, standardized mean differences (SMDs) between intervention and comparator groups were calculated using Hedges’ g with 95% confidence intervals to correct for small-sample bias [22]. When only change-from-baseline data were available, SMDs were derived using standard Cochrane Handbook methods where sufficient information was provided [23].

Single-group pre–post studies with sufficient data were included in the meta-analyses; studies lacking essential data were summarized narratively. For knowledge, skills, confidence or self-efficacy, and mental health or wellbeing outcomes, positive SMD values indicated improvement. For attitudes and stigma measures in which higher scores reflected more negative attitudes, negative SMD values indicated a favorable effect. When multiple instruments were reported within a single outcome domain, the primary measure specified by the study authors or the most widely validated instrument was prioritized. Where multiple post-intervention time points were reported, the earliest post-intervention assessment was used for quantitative synthesis, while longer-term outcomes were summarized narratively.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.2) and the meta package, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I2 statistic, with values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [24]. Random-effects models were applied for all outcomes to account for anticipated clinical and methodological diversity [25]. Subgroup analyses by study design were conducted for outcomes with substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 75%) where data permitted. Meta-regression was not performed due to the limited number of studies. Sensitivity analyses employed a leave-one-out approach. Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using Egger’s regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test, with cautious interpretation for outcomes including fewer than ten studies [26, 27].

Mixed-methods integration

Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated using a convergent, segregated mixed-methods approach. Joint displays were used to juxtapose pooled quantitative effects by outcome domain with qualitative themes related to mechanisms, learner experiences, and contextual moderators. Integration focused on explanatory expansion, whereby qualitative findings were used to contextualize and interpret quantitative results rather than to adjudicate effect estimates. This approach facilitated the identification of convergent and complementary evidence and informed the development of an explanatory narrative describing how, for whom, and under what circumstances MHL interventions are most likely to produce meaningful outcomes [28].

Results

Study characteristics

A total of 3,461 records were identified through database searching, with an additional four records identified through reference-list searching. After removal of 300 duplicates, 3,165 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 3,040 were excluded. One hundred and twenty-five full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 70 were excluded primarily due to ineligible interventions, populations, or study designs. Fifty-five studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review (Fig. 1, Supplementary 1B).

The PRISMA flow diagram

The characteristics of the 55 included studies are summarized in Supplementary 1C. Studies were published between 1999 and 2025 and conducted across Europe, North America, Australia, Asia, Africa, and the Middle East, predominantly in university-based classrooms or online settings, with some incorporating clinical training. Study designs included RCTs (n = 6), quasi-experimental studies (n = 26), mixed-methods studies (n = 5), qualitative studies (n = 10), and observational designs (n = 8). Most studies involved undergraduate health professions students, primarily from nursing, medicine, pharmacy, and allied health, with some including postgraduate trainees; sample sizes ranged from small qualitative samples (n < 30) to large cohort and quasi-experimental studies involving several hundred participants. Nursing students were the most frequently represented participant group (n = 19, 34.5%), followed by medical students (n = 14, 25.5%) and mixed health profession cohorts (n = 14, 25.5%). In contrast, pharmacy (n = 2, 3.6%), physiotherapy (n = 1, 1.8%), public health (n = 1, 1.8%), and midwifery students (n = 1, 1.8%) were infrequently represented, while postgraduate resident populations were included in only four studies (7.3%), highlighting potential gaps in the evidence base across several health professions. Interventions commonly assessed multiple mental health literacy domains, including knowledge (n = 40), attitudes/stigma reduction (n = 38), skills (n = 34), confidence/self-efficacy (n = 30), self-awareness (n = 25), help-seeking (n = 18), and behavioral outcomes (n = 20). Fifteen studies evaluated single-session or brief interventions, while 20 examined multi-session or longitudinal curricula. Follow-up was most often immediate post-intervention (n = 40), with fewer studies including medium-term (n = 15) or longer-term follow-up beyond six months (n = 8), limiting the available evidence regarding the sustainability of intervention effects over time. More detailed mapping of publication trends, geographical distribution, study settings, intervention characteristics, and contextual factors is reported separately in a companion Evidence and Gap Mapping study, which is under consideration for publication elsewhere.

Risk of bias and methodological quality

Risk of bias assessments for RCTs and quasi-experimental studies are summarized in Supplementary 1D1 and 1D2. All six RCTs were judged to be at low risk of bias across all RoB 2 domains, indicating a low likelihood of systematic bias affecting effect estimates. Among quasi-experimental studies assessed using the ROBINS-I tool, most were rated as having low overall risk of bias, while a smaller number were judged to have moderate or high risk, most commonly due to potential confounding and participant selection. These judgments informed the interpretation of the findings and the assessment of the certainty of the evidence.

Methodological quality across all included studies, irrespective of design, is presented in Supplementary 1D3 using the MMAT. The results indicated generally moderate-to-high methodological quality, particularly among qualitative and mixed-methods studies. Consistent with the predefined appraisal hierarchy, MMAT findings were used descriptively and did not influence effect size estimation or study weighting.

Quantitative synthesis

Fourteen studies were included in the meta-analysis. Of the 53 studies in the overall review, 14 provided sufficiently comparable quantitative data for at least one predefined outcome, qualifying them for inclusion in the meta-analyses. The remaining quantitative studies were synthesized narratively because outcome measures were not comparable, data were missing, or the designs were purely descriptive. The random-effects meta-analysis results are summarized in Table 1, with corresponding forest plots in Fig. 2. Mental health literacy training showed statistically significant improvements in knowledge across four studies (n = 847), with a moderate pooled effect size (SMD = 0.56, 95% CI 0.27–0.85, p = 0.0002; Fig. 2B) and substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 74.3%). There was no evidence of publication bias based on Egger’s or Begg’s tests, and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings.

For attitudes and stigma reduction, eight studies (n = 1,042) showed a non-significant pooled effect (SMD = − 0.53, 95% CI − 1.30 to 0.25, p = 0.181; Fig. 2A), with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 97%). Publication bias was not detected, and sensitivity analyses indicated variability in effect estimates.

No statistically significant pooled effects were observed for mental health/wellbeing (k = 6, n = 1,061; SMD = 0.04, 95% CI − 0.31 to 0.40, p = 0.805; Fig. 2C), self-efficacy/confidence (k = 3, n = 442; SMD = 0.51, 95% CI − 0.72 to 1.73, p = 0.418; Fig. 2D), or skills (k = 3, n = 268; SMD = 0.62, 95% CI − 0.26 to 1.51, p = 0.166; Fig. 2E). All outcomes showed substantial to considerable heterogeneity (I2 range: 85.8%–95.3%). For skills, Egger’s test suggested possible small-study effects (p = 0.033), whereas Begg’s test was non-significant; these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of included studies.

Subgroup analyses by study design

Subgroup analyses of attitudes and stigma reduction found no statistically significant effects in either randomized controlled trials (RCTs; k = 3, SMD = − 0.09, 95% CI − 0.44 to 0.26) or quasi-experimental studies (k = 5, SMD = − 0.77, 95% CI − 1.98 to 0.44). Heterogeneity was moderate among RCTs (I2 = 53%) and very high among quasi-experimental studies (I2 = 98%). No significant differences were observed between subgroups. Similarly, subgroup analyses of mental health/wellbeing showed non-significant effects for both RCTs (k = 2, SMD = 0.25, 95% CI − 0.16 to 0.65) and quasi-experimental studies (k = 4, SMD = − 0.06, 95% CI − 0.56 to 0.45), with substantial heterogeneity in both subgroups. No significant subgroup differences were observed.

Summary of direction of effect

Across the included studies, the direction of effect was predominantly positive for the assessed outcomes (Supplementary 1E). Where reported, knowledge, attitudes/stigma reduction, skills, confidence, and behavioural outcomes most frequently demonstrated improvements following the intervention, with relatively few studies reporting no change and very few indicating negative effects. Positive directions of effect were most frequently observed for knowledge, skills, and confidence-related outcomes, while self-awareness and help-seeking outcomes were reported less frequently and showed greater variability. When a general direction of effect was reported, the overall effect was most often positive; however, these findings should be interpreted alongside the meta-analytic results, which demonstrated statistically significant pooled effects only for knowledge outcomes.

Qualitative data results

The thematic synthesis yielded five higher-order analytical themes and nineteen descriptive themes, representing interconnected processes through which educational interventions promote empathy, mental health literacy, and stigma reduction among healthcare and university students (Table 2). These themes collectively describe a pedagogical model of transformative, person-centred learning that enhances both emotional insight and professional capability.

Analytical Theme 1: Transformative learning via experiential and reflective pedagogies

Experiential and reflective methods were central to transformative learning across most studies. Students described emotional and cognitive shifts when exposed to immersive activities, including role-play, standardized patient (SP) encounters, cinematic simulations, Stoic reflection, and structured debriefs [29–34]. Across studies, experiential learning was consistently described as transformative, triggering attitudinal change through empathy, reflection, and embodied understanding of mental health care.

Descriptive Theme 1.1: Reflective practices

Approaches such as Stoic or mindfulness-based practices help learners regulate their emotions and recognize their biases. Participants articulated increased emotional self-awareness and empathic imagination, connecting more deeply with patients’ perspectives [29].

Descriptive Theme 1.2: Film and cinematic simulation

Watching mental health–related films, followed by guided reflection, created emotionally safe environments for understanding complex psychiatric experiences. Students reported reduced anxiety and greater empathy after cinematic exposure [34].

Descriptive Theme 1.3: Simulation and structured debriefing

SP encounters provided realism and authenticity, allowing students to practice communication, assessment, and teamwork in psychologically safe settings. Debriefs facilitated meaning-making, linking emotional reactions to professional growth [32].

Descriptive Theme 1.4: Interactive role-play and case work

Students valued case-based discussions and role-playing as a means of bridging theory and practice. These activities encouraged active participation, problem-solving, and self-reflection, deepening conceptual understanding of patient behavior and social context [30, 33].

Analytical Theme 2: Stigma reduction and empathy building through literacy, contact, and person-centred framing

Education that combines knowledge acquisition with human connection significantly reduces stigma and fosters empathy [31, 33, 36–39, 41, 42]. Together, these findings underscore that stigma reduction occurs when education integrates emotional engagement with cognitive understanding, transforming fear or avoidance into empathy and solidarity.

Descriptive Theme 2.1: Person-centred and recovery-oriented framing

Framing mental disorder as part of a person’s holistic experience shifted perceptions from pathology to personhood. Exposure to lived-experience narratives allowed students to “see the person beyond the diagnosis,” encouraging compassion and reducing fear [31, 39].

Descriptive Theme 2.2: Mental health literacy gains

Knowledge-focused interventions, such as the Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) and therapeutic courses, increased understanding of symptoms, recovery processes, and treatment options. Participants reported that improved literacy decreased stereotypes and enhanced willingness to engage with patients [36, 39, 41].

Descriptive Theme 2.3: Peer connection and belonging

Online psychoeducation groups [37] and peer support activities fostered inclusion and reduced feelings of isolation. Students perceived sharing personal experiences to normalize mental distress and promote mutual empathy.

Descriptive Theme 2.4: Contact-based learning

Direct or simulated contact with individuals experiencing mental disorders was critical in breaking down stigma. In-person interactions, patient-led discussions, or expert-by-experience teaching demystified psychiatric conditions, humanized patients, and inspired advocacy [33, 38, 42].

Analytical Theme 3: Professional confidence and practice readiness

A recurring outcome across both qualitative and mixed-method studies was enhanced confidence in managing mental health situations and applying learned skills in practice [31–34, 38, 40, 43]. Overall, educational interventions cultivated professional maturity, a blend of skill, empathy, and confidence essential for compassionate, evidence-based practice.

Descriptive Theme 3.1: Communication and counselling competence

Students reported increased comfort discussing mental health topics and supporting distressed individuals after exposure to MHFA and simulation-based learning. Role-play and SP interactions developed listening skills, empathy, and sensitivity to patient needs [32, 33, 43].

Descriptive Theme 3.2: Crisis and first-aid capabilities

MHFA training and similar courses improved readiness to identify, assess, and respond to mental health crises, fostering a sense of preparedness to intervene safely and effectively [38, 41].

Descriptive Theme 3.3: Assessment-to-plan integration

Structured activities that link clinical reasoning with care planning helped students operationalize theoretical knowledge, thereby improving their ability to assess patient needs and coordinate interdisciplinary care [34].

Descriptive Theme 3.4: Self-efficacy and role clarity

Through experiential practice and reflective supervision, learners gained confidence in their professional roles, recognized limits, and developed a clearer sense of identity within multidisciplinary teams [31, 40].

Analytical Theme 4: Interprofessional collaboration and integrated care models

Interprofessional education (IPE) featured prominently as a mechanism for bridging disciplinary silos and improving integrated care delivery [31, 32, 40]. This theme illustrates that collaborative, team-based training not only improves care coordination but also fosters more profound empathy through shared accountability and a greater understanding of systemic roles.

Descriptive Theme 4.1: Shared language and role modelling

Collaborative sessions allowed nursing, medical, and psychology trainees to learn “each other’s language,” observing how professionals from other disciplines assess, communicate, and plan care. Such role modeling reduced misunderstandings and fostered mutual respect [32].

Descriptive Theme 4.2: Team-based coordination

Joint simulations and interdisciplinary case discussions provided experiential understanding of coordinated care. Students reported developing an appreciation for warm handoffs and continuity between physical and mental health providers [32, 40].

Analytical Theme 5: Curriculum design, delivery context, and sustainability

Contextual and logistical factors significantly influenced the effectiveness and sustainability of interventions [30, 33, 34, 37, 39, 42]. Across all 15 studies, the thematic synthesis reveals that transformative, experiential, and reflective pedagogies, when culturally adapted and embedded over time, enhance empathy, reduce stigma, and foster professional competence. The findings converge on a learning pathway where experiential contact (feeling and doing) and critical reflection (thinking and meaning making) catalyze attitudinal and behavioral transformation. This transformation manifests in greater emotional resilience, empathy, interprofessional collaboration, and sustainable engagement with mental health care.

Descriptive Theme 5.1: Online and cost-effective modalities

Virtual psychoeducation groups and film-based simulations increased access to mental health learning, especially during resource constraints or pandemics. However, sustained engagement required active facilitation and interaction strategies [34, 37].

Descriptive Theme 5.2: Localized and culturally adapted content

In resource-limited contexts like Somaliland [33], contextualizing teaching materials to local culture and available resources improved relevance and learner engagement. Adaptation was seen as vital for inclusivity and sustainability.

Descriptive Theme 5.3: Longitudinal embedding

Participants across studies emphasized the need to integrate mental health education continuously throughout curricula, rather than as isolated workshops. Repetition reinforced skill retention and normalized mental health as a core professional domain [39, 41].

Descriptive Theme 5.4: System and time constraints

Structural barriers, such as limited clinic time, understaffing, and competing academic priorities, often hindered full implementation of experiential training. Educators emphasized the importance of striking a balance between fidelity and feasibility [32, 33].

Descriptive Theme 5.5: Sustainability and faculty capacity

The long-term impact of the educational intervention depended on institutional support, faculty preparedness, and the availability of trained facilitators. Programs co-developed with local educators and supported by leadership showed greater longevity and integration into professional identity formation.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative results

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings showed that qualitative evidence mainly contextualized quantitative results without challenging effect size estimates. Quantitative analyses indicated moderate improvements in knowledge, but non-significant and heterogeneous effect for attitudes, skills, confidence, and well-being, along with significant heterogeneity across outcomes. The qualitative synthesis offered insights into the underlying mechanisms and contextual factors.

For knowledge outcomes, the significant pooled effects were supported by qualitative findings that emphasized structured content delivery, repetition, and clarity of mental health concepts as crucial for learning. Experiential and reflective pedagogies, such as simulation, role-play, and guided discussion, reinforced conceptual understanding, which explains the consistent gains in knowledge. In contrast, the lack of significant pooled effects for attitudes, skills, and confidence, despite positive individual study effects, was attributed to qualitative evidence pointing to variability in pedagogical intensity, duration, and context. Qualitative findings indicated that changes in attitudes and skills require sustained exposure, emotional engagement, and practice opportunities, which were inconsistently present across interventions, contributing to the high heterogeneity in the meta-analyses.

Qualitative themes highlighted that experiential contact, reflective practice, and person-centred approaches were key to supporting empathy, reducing stigma, and enhancing professional confidence. While these elements were associated with positive changes, they were often underutilized in interventions seen in quantitative studies, which may clarify discrepancies between qualitative improvements and quantitative results. The integration of findings indicated that while short-term, single-session interventions effectively increased knowledge, evidence for their effects on behavior change, help-seeking, and sustained confidence was less consistent. Qualitative data underscored the need for longitudinal integration, institutional support, and cultural adaptation to explain variability in quantitative outcomes across different professions and settings.

Taken together, the integrated findings indicate that knowledge outcomes are most consistently supported when cognitive learning is coupled with experiential and reflective components and embedded over time. The qualitative synthesis thus strengthened the interpretation of the quantitative results by identifying mechanisms, contextual moderators, and implementation features that shape observed effectiveness, contributing to a coherent explanatory narrative of what works, for whom, and under what circumstances.

Discussion

This mixed-methods systematic review combined quantitative effect estimates with qualitative insights into learners' experiences and implementation contexts to evaluate MHL interventions for health professions students. The meta-analyses revealed a statistically significant, moderate improvement in knowledge; however, the pooled effects on attitudes, skills, confidence/self-efficacy, and mental health or well-being were not statistically significant and displayed considerable heterogeneity. Nevertheless, most individual studies indicated a positive direction of effect in these areas, suggesting variability in magnitude rather than consistently measurable benefits. The qualitative synthesis aligned with these quantitative findings, further elucidating them by identifying pedagogical mechanisms and contextual factors that facilitate or hinder impact. Overall, MHL interventions appear consistently effective for knowledge acquisition and show mixed evidence of effectiveness for deeper attitudinal, skill-based, and behavioral outcomes, especially when implemented as brief or standalone sessions.

Knowledge outcomes emerged as the most robust and consistent, aligning well with prior reviews of MHL education in both healthcare and community populations [9, 10, 13]. Learners attributed their knowledge gains to structured content delivery, clear objectives, and repetition, which facilitate the formation and consolidation of cognitive schemas [44, 45]. Experiential and reflective activities, such as simulation, role-play, film-based learning, and guided discussions, further reinforced understanding by connecting abstract concepts to emotionally relevant and clinically realistic scenarios.

In contrast, pooled effects for attitudes, skills, and confidence were non-significant, despite generally positive directional trends. This pattern likely reflects variability in intervention design, dosage, and implementation rather than indicating a universal lack of impact. Changes in attitude and skill are deeply intertwined with shifts in beliefs, emotions, and professional identity, necessitating sustained practice, feedback, and contextual application [46–49]. Many of the interventions included in the meta-analyses were brief, primarily didactic, or inadequately integrated into curricula, which limited their potential to support these deeper developmental processes. This disparity between intervention intensity and outcome complexity helps elucidate the attenuated pooled effects and high levels of heterogeneity observed. Most included studies assessed outcomes immediately following the intervention, with relatively few evaluating medium- or long-term effects. Consequently, conclusions regarding the sustainability of observed improvements, particularly beyond knowledge outcomes, should be interpreted cautiously.

The qualitative synthesis revealed three interrelated mechanisms that elucidate how MHL interventions affect change beyond mere knowledge acquisition: experiential contact, reflective practice with guided debriefing, and person-centred, recovery-oriented framing. Firstly, experiential contact, achieved through simulation, interactions with standardized patients, role-playing, or teachings from individuals with lived experiences, challenges stereotypes and promotes perspective-taking. This approach aligns with evidence suggesting that structured contact is one of the most effective strategies for reducing stigma and fostering empathy [50–52] and resonates with broader research on simulation in psychiatric education [12, 53]. Secondly, reflective practice and guided debriefing facilitate the transformation of experiences into learning. Facilitated reflection allows students to process their emotional responses, interrogate their assumptions, and integrate new insights into their evolving professional identities, consistent with theories of reflective and transformative learning[48, 49, 54, 55]. Thirdly, person-centred and recovery-oriented framing shifts the focus from pathology to personhood, emphasizing agency, hope, and collaboration. This aligns with contemporary recovery-oriented practices [56, 57]. It is important to note that these mechanisms were not consistently present in all interventions contributing data to the meta-analyses. As a result, outcomes dependent on affective engagement and identity formation, such as attitudes, skills, and confidence, were more variable than knowledge, which can be enhanced through well-designed informational instruction alone (Fig. 3).

Conceptual model of mechanisms underpinning the effectiveness of MHL interventions for health professions students

The findings presented suggest several important implications for curriculum development in the context of MHL. First, knowledge-focused objectives can be effectively achieved through structured and thoughtfully designed teaching methods, which may include online or cost-effective formats. However, it is vital that these objectives are reinforced to ensure sustained gains over time. Second, to positively influence attitudes, skills, and professional confidence, it may be more effective to embed MHL content longitudinally throughout programs rather than delivering it in isolated workshops. Providing repeated opportunities for practice, feedback, and application at various stages of training appears essential [46, 58]. Third, interventions should purposefully integrate interactions with individuals who have lived experience, structured reflection, and a recovery-oriented framework as fundamental design principles rather than treating them as optional enhancements. These elements may be important for diminishing stigma, enhancing empathy, and preparing trainees to effectively manage mental health presentations in practice. Finally, utilizing interprofessional formats may help to normalize mental health as a collective competency, clarify roles, and foster integrated care pathways. This approach underscores the relevance of mental health across diverse disciplines and settings.

Future research should aim to align intervention designs more closely with their intended outcomes and follow-up periods. Studies focused on changing attitudes, behaviors, or professional identity necessitate longer-term follow-up and more nuanced measures of stigma, empathy, and help-seeking, rather than relying solely on immediate post-intervention assessments. The use of mixed-methods designs is particularly beneficial for exploring mechanisms, contextual influences, and implementation processes, and should be adopted more frequently.

Additionally, there is a pressing need for research conducted in low- and middle-income countries and diverse cultural settings, utilizing culturally adapted materials and outcomes that are meaningful within local contexts [59]. Although studies were identified across multiple geographical regions, representation across settings was uneven, highlighting the need for further evidence from underrepresented cultural and healthcare contexts. Enhanced reporting of intervention content, pedagogical components, and dosage would support replication efforts and enable meaningful comparisons across programs.

Strengths and limitations

This review was conducted following a registered protocol, adhered to JBI guidance for mixed-methods reviews, and complied with PRISMA 2020 reporting standards. The application of design-specific risk-of-bias tools, a transparent evaluation of methodological quality, and the integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence enhance confidence in the findings and their relevance.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. Significant clinical and methodological heterogeneity, particularly concerning attitudinal and skill-based outcomes, requires caution when interpreting pooled estimates. Moreover, only a subset of studies contributed data for the meta-analyses. The reliance on quasi-experimental and pre–post designs restrict the ability to draw causal inferences. Additionally, limiting the search to English-language publications may have overlooked pertinent studies, particularly from low- and middle-income regions, thereby affecting cultural representation and the generalizability of the results. Consequently, the findings may not fully reflect the diversity of cultural and linguistic contexts in which MHL interventions are implemented and evaluated.

Conclusion

MHL interventions for health professions students consistently enhance knowledge, whereas evidence for their effects on attitudes, skills, confidence, and overall wellbeing remains mixed. Qualitative findings suggest that integrating structured content with experiential learning, guided reflection, and a person-centred, recovery-oriented approach, together with longitudinal curricular embedding, may support more profound and sustainable outcomes. By synthesizing quantitative and qualitative evidence, this review provides insight into the mechanisms and contextual conditions that may influence the effectiveness of MHL education, offering practical guidance for educators aiming to reduce stigma, promote empathy, and improve practice-readiness within the future health workforce.
