Effects of the bile duct bifurcation angle and position of the living donor on biliary complications in recipients after living donor liver transplantation
Authors: Taku Kimura, Akira Umemura, Hiroyuki Nitta, Hirokatsu Katagiri, Yoshiyuki Ihara, Shoji Kanno, Daiki Takeda, Taro Ando, Satoshi Amano, Toma Kawashima, Koji Kikuchi, Akira Sasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study was intended to identify new risk factors for biliary complications (BCs) in recipients after living donor liver transplantation (LDLT), focusing on the biliary anatomy of the donor.MethodsA total of 123 living donors who underwent donor hepatectomy between 2007 and 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. Using three-dimensional reconstructed drip infusion cholangiographic computed tomography images, we evaluated biliary branching patterns, the maximum bifurcation angle and rotation angle of the hepatic ducts, and the bifurcation position relative to the portal vein.ResultsBCs occurred in 25 recipients (20.3%). Donors who developed BCs had a wider bifurcation angle (105.9° versus 83.6°, p = 0.001), a greater rotation angle (25.6° versus 13.8°, p < 0.001), and a more cranial bifurcation position (52.0% versus 13.3%, p < 0.001). Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified cutoff values of 91.8° for bifurcation angle (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.731; 95% CI: 0.63–0.83) and 30.0° for rotation angle (AUC: 0.691; 95% CI: 0.56–0.82). Multivariate logistic regression revealed that bifurcation angle (odds ratio [OR]: 4.46), rotation angle (OR: 4.96), and bifurcation position (OR: 4.54) were independent risk factors for BCs.ConclusionsThis study suggests that donor biliary anatomy may be associated with the risk of BCs, underscoring the importance of detailed preoperative evaluation in living donor selection.

Introduction

In recent years, advancements in transplantation techniques and immunosuppressive therapy have significantly improved the survival rate of recipients after living donor liver transplantation (LDLT), with the cumulative five-year survival rate in Japan reaching 80% [1–3]. However, the incidence of biliary complications (BCs) in recipients after LDLT remains high, reportedly being two to three times higher than in deceased donor liver transplantation [4–6]. Jeon et al. reported that the right posterior bile duct anatomy of donors is an important factor in the occurrence of BCs in recipients after LDLT. They demonstrated an association between biliary branching pattern and BCs in the right lobe graft [7].

The bifurcation of the left and right hepatic ducts occurs in a two-dimensional plane, with the ducts branching to the left and right, making them easily identifiable through imaging modalities such as direct cholangiography, drip infusion cholangiographic computed tomography (DIC-CT), and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP). However, the actual bifurcation of the bile ducts forms a three-dimensional (3D) structure. As observed in the anatomy of the portal umbilical portion, the left hepatic duct runs ventrally, and the right one runs slightly dorsally. When considering the common bile duct as the central axis, this configuration results in a rightward rotational orientation.

We hypothesized that donors whose recipients develop BCs have the following characteristics in terms of biliary anatomy: First, the wider bifurcation angle between the left and right hepatic ducts of living donors may result in a more acute anastomotic angle between the graft bile duct and the recipient bile duct, increasing the risk of biliary outflow obstruction. Second, if the abovementioned axial rotation is large, the bile duct orifice may face ventrally in the right liver graft and dorsally in the left liver graft, potentially making anastomosis more difficult. Moreover, if the right and left hepatic ducts bifurcate cranially to the portal vein, the bile ducts to be anastomosed may be positioned farther from the hepatic hilum, leading to excessive tension in the bile ducts. The increased distance between the bile duct or intestine of the recipient and the bile duct of the liver graft may complicate anastomosis. Based on these hypotheses, comprehensive analyses of the biliary branching pattern by anatomical features may be informative for predicting BCs.

The objective of this study was to identify novel risk factors for BCs that could be evaluated preoperatively by focusing on the biliary anatomy of the donor, independent of recipient-related factors.

Materials and methods

Study subjects and database compilation

The potential participants in this retrospective study were given the opportunity to opt out by requiring preoperative informed consent, including comprehensive consent, and this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Iwate Medical University (ID number MH2019-119). From April 2007 to July 2024, 127 LDLTs were performed at Iwate Medical University Hospital, in Japan. Among these, 123 living donors whose biliary anatomy was evaluated preoperatively using 3D reconstructed images based on DIC-CT were included in this study. The first four cases were excluded because in the early phase of our LDLT program, biliary anatomy was evaluated using direct cholangiography instead of DIC-CT and, thus, 3D image data were not available (Fig. 1). The database was retrospectively compiled.

Hepatic graft harvesting and biliary reconstruction

Donor hepatectomy was performed as follows: The right or left hepatic lobe was mobilized, and the hepatic veins were identified. After hepatic parenchymal transection under the Pringle maneuver, the hepatic veins were taped. The hepatoduodenal ligament was carefully dissected, and after taping the hepatic artery and portal vein, the bile duct was identified. The bile duct transection site was determined using direct cholangiography via the cystic duct until August 2017, and from September 2017 onward, it was assessed using an indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging system [8]. When we could not identify the transection line using ICG imaging, we used direct cholangiography instead. Biliary reconstruction was performed using a standardized technique in our institution. Reconstruction consisted of bile-duct–to–bile-duct anastomosis, bile-duct–to–jejunal anastomosis, or a combination thereof. The posterior wall of the anastomosis was sutured with a continuous absorbable monofilament suture (5–0 or 6–0 depending on the duct diameter), whereas the anterior wall was closed with interrupted sutures. All knots were tied externally to avoid intraluminal knot protrusion. During the study period, biliary reconstruction was performed by multiple surgeons who had training and experience in hepatobiliary surgery. The same standardized institutional technique was consistently applied throughout the study period, with no major variation among surgeons.

Evaluation of biliary anatomy

Modalities for the assessment of biliary anatomy

Preoperative dynamic computed tomography (CT) and DIC-CT images of living donors were analyzed using a 3D image analysis system (SYNAPSE VINCENT, Fujifilm, Japan).

Anatomical variations in biliary branching patterns

The standard type, in which the right hepatic duct was normally formed (Type I), was classified as having no anatomical variation. Other variations were classified as anomalies, including cases in which the right anterior and right posterior hepatic ducts bifurcated simultaneously (Type II) and the right posterior or anterior bile ducts drained into the left hepatic duct (Type IIIA or Type IIIB, respectively; Fig. 2).

Maximum bifurcation angle of the left and right hepatic ducts and rotation angle

Using 3D reconstructed images of the bile ducts, the bifurcation angle of the left and right hepatic ducts was measured on a frontal projection view. Although the biliary tree was reconstructed in 3D, the measured angle represented a two-dimensional planar angle observed in the true frontal orientation and did not account for depth differences among the ducts. Type II was considered to have a very short right hepatic duct. The midline of the right anterior and posterior bile ducts and the angle of the left hepatic duct were measured. In cases without a right hepatic duct, the angle between the right anterior bile duct and the left hepatic duct was measured (Fig. 2). With the true frontal view set as 0°, the 3D reconstructed image was rotated 5° counterclockwise around the craniocaudal axis until it reached the right side of the patient (90°; Fig. 3). The bifurcation angle of the left and right hepatic ducts observed at each rotation angle was repeatedly measured. The maximum angle obtained was defined as the maximum bifurcation angle of the left and right hepatic ducts, and the rotation angle was also recorded (Fig. 4).

Since no standardized definition of these geometric parameters currently exists, we established a reproducible protocol using fixed rotation increments and clearly defined reference planes. All measurements were performed by the same investigator under identical image display conditions to ensure internal consistency.

Bifurcation position of the left and right hepatic ducts

The dynamic CT and DIC-CT images were combined to create a 3D reconstruction. The intersection of the two defined straight lines measuring the bifurcation angle was assessed to determine whether it was located cranial to the primary bifurcation of the portal vein (i.e., in a supra-portal position; Fig. 5).

Running of the right posterior bile duct

For Types I, II, and IIIA, the running of the right posterior bile duct was evaluated to determine whether it was of the supra-portal type, which refers to a pattern in which the right posterior bile duct runs along the craniodorsal side of the right portal vein and joins the right anterior bile duct to form the right hepatic duct.

Bile duct diameter

In right liver graft cases, the maximum short-axis diameter of the right hepatic duct or the anterior sectoral duct, in cases in which the right hepatic duct was not formed, was measured. In left liver graft cases, the maximum short-axis diameter of the left hepatic duct was measured directly after the bile duct bifurcation.

Definition of BCs

Bile leakage was defined as a bilirubin concentration in the drain fluid at least three times the serum bilirubin concentration on or after postoperative day three or as the need for treatment intervention resulting from biliary collections or bile peritonitis [9]. Biliary stenosis was defined as a condition in which drug-induced liver damage, rejection, or the worsening of the current disease was ruled out and biliary interventions were required or worsening liver damage or jaundice occurred.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations and compared using a two-sided Student’s t-test for normally distributed parameters. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. To identify variables related to biliary anatomical parameters that were independent predictors of BCs, variables with p < 0.2 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model, and explanatory variables were selected using a stepwise method based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC). For the logistic regression analysis, continuous variables were converted into binary variables by determining the cutoff values using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The variables were presented as the odds ratio (OR) with a confidence interval (CI) and p-value. Statistical significance was set to p < 0.05, and the statistical analyses were performed using R Version 4.3.1 and EZR Version 1.68. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Diagnostic accuracy indices, including positive and negative predictive values and likelihood ratios, were calculated based on the ROC-derived cutoff values.

Results

Characteristics of 123 living donors

The living donor group consisted of 58 men (47.2%) and 65 women (52.8%), with a mean age of 37.8 years. Of these, 26 donors (21.1%) underwent open surgery, and 97 donors (78.9%) underwent minimally invasive surgery (pure laparoscopic or laparoscopic-assisted surgery). There were 76 right liver grafts (61.8%) and 47 left liver grafts (28.2%). The bile duct transection site was evaluated using ICG fluorescence imaging system in 46 donors (37.4%). During biliary reconstruction, ductoplasty was performed in 24 cases (19.5%), and multiple ducts for anastomosis were present in 29 cases (23.6%). BCs occurred in 25 recipients (20.3%). Among these, 12 recipients (48.0%) had bile leakage alone, 11 recipients (44.0%) had biliary stenosis alone, and two recipients (8.0%) had both bile leakage and biliary stenosis (Table 1). Acute cellular rejection occurred in 21 recipients (17.1%). In the univariate analysis, no statistically significant differences were observed in the characteristics of the living donors between the groups with and without BCs, including differences in study period (2007–2015 versus 2016–2024; p = 0.503; Table 2).

Risk factors for BCs in the univariate analysis

Among living donors overall, the maximum bifurcation angle of the left and right hepatic ducts (83.6° versus 105.9°, p = 0.001) and rotation angle (13.8° versus 25.6°, p < 0.001) were significantly larger in the group with BCs than in the group without BCs. In addition, in the group with BCs, a significantly higher number of cases exhibited a bifurcation position of the left and right hepatic ducts that was cranial to the primary branch of the portal vein (p < 0.001). Right liver graft donors (right lobe graft, extended right lobe graft, and right posterior segment graft) exhibited similar results, with significant differences in the maximum bifurcation angle of the left and right hepatic ducts (78.2° versus 105.9°, p = 0.001), rotation angle (13.8° versus 25.6°, p < 0.001), and the bifurcation position of the left and right hepatic ducts (p < 0.001). In left liver graft donors (left lobe graft, left lobe and Segment 1 graft, left lateral segment graft, and other grafts), significant differences were observed in rotation angle (12.2° versus 25.0°, p = 0.012) and the bifurcation position of the hepatic ducts (p = 0.018; Table 3). In the right posterior bile duct, 91.3% of the total was of the supra-portal type. There was no significant difference in the running of the bile duct between those with and without BCs (Table 4).

Risk factors for BCs according to multivariate analysis

Among living donors overall, the ROC curve identified a cutoff value of 91.8° (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.731; 95% CI: 0.63–0.83; sensitivity: 76.0%; specificity: 69.4%) for the maximum bifurcation angle of the left and right hepatic ducts and 30.0° (AUC: 0.691; 95% CI: 0.56–0.82; sensitivity: 56.0%; specificity: 81.6%) for the rotation angle of the hepatic ducts (Fig. 6). The ROC-derived cutoff values were determined objectively using Youden’s index, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity. Based on these cutoff values, the negative predictive value, the positive predictive value, the positive likelihood ratio, and the negative likelihood ratio were 91.8%, 38.7%, 2.5, and 0.4 for bifurcation angle and 87.9%, 43.7%, 3.04, and 0.5 for rotation angle, respectively.

The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated good model calibration (p = 0.732).

Discussion

BCs in recipients after LDLT are complex to manage and often refractory to treatment [10]. BCs significantly affect not only recipients’ survival prognoses but also their quality of life. Therefore, reducing BCs remains a constant challenge in liver transplantation. In this study, we focused on the biliary anatomy of donors to identify novel risk factors for BCs based solely on objective donor factors that were independent of recipient characteristics and could be assessed preoperatively. Our analysis suggested a potential association between donor biliary anatomy and BCs. Specifically, a wider bifurcation angle of the right and left hepatic ducts, greater rightward rotation along the craniocaudal axis of the hepatic ducts, and a more cranial bifurcation position of the right and left hepatic ducts were associated with an increased incidence of BCs (Fig. 7). These geometric parameters may complement conventional clinical risk factors by providing additional preoperative anatomical information, which could contribute to improving future risk prediction models for BCs. From a practical standpoint, 3D biliary reconstruction using CT-based image analysis software has already been widely adopted in the preoperative evaluation of living liver donors. Therefore, applying the proposed geometric analysis would require only modest additional effort and would not impose a substantial financial or logistical burden. Because this approach uses routine preoperative 3D cholangiographic imaging (such as DIC-CT or MRCP) without the need for additional examinations, contrast administration, or radiation exposure, it can be seamlessly integrated into existing donor evaluation workflows. In particular, incorporating these geometric parameters into preoperative planning may offer supplementary insight into the potential risk of biliary complications and may be helpful when considering intraoperative strategies. Considering this, the method could reasonably be implemented in routine clinical practice.

Among right liver graft donors and liver graft donors overall, the bifurcation angle of the right and left hepatic ducts, the rotation angle of the bile ducts, and the bifurcation position of the right and left hepatic ducts were independent risk factors for BCs. In contrast, among left liver graft donors, only the bifurcation position of the hepatic ducts was identified as an independent risk factor. Although similar trends were observed across right liver graft donors, left liver graft donors, and donors overall, there were slight variations between these groups. The results among donors overall resembled those among the right liver graft donors, suggesting that the overall findings were strongly influenced by right liver graft donors. In contrast, the left liver graft donors had fewer risk factors. Rotation angle, which showed a significant difference in univariate analysis, also had a low AUC. As mentioned above, this may suggest that left lobe grafts are associated with a decreased risk of BCs [11]. An additional analysis excluding cases with posterior segment and left lateral segment grafts yielded results consistent with those of our main analysis. This consistency suggests that the bifurcation angle itself may play a more fundamental role in the development of BCs, regardless of graft type. When the bifurcation and rotation angles were analyzed as continuous variables, similar trends were observed. However, ROC-derived dichotomization was adopted in the final model to facilitate clinical interpretation and presentation of cutoff values.

Multiple ducts for anastomosis, postoperative bile leak, right lobe graft, multiple ducts for anastomosis, ductoplasty, ABO incompatibility, acute cellular rejection and donor bile duct size have been reported as predictors of BCs [12]. Among these risk factors, donor- and surgery-related factors are closely associated with the complexity and technical difficulty of biliary anastomosis, and it is evident that cases with more challenging anastomoses are more likely to develop BCs. While it is advisable to avoid known risk factors whenever possible, actual graft selection in LDLT prioritizes donor safety, graft-to-recipient weight ratio, and standard liver volume percentage for eligibility assessment. Consequently, these risks are sometimes unavoidable. Furthermore, in actual graft procurement procedures, various factors, such as the bile duct transection site and liver transection line, introduce variability, making precise preoperative prediction challenging.

Jeon et al. reported that the anatomical characteristics of donor bile ducts that are prone to biliary complications are a supra-portal right posterior bile duct (RPBD), a short right hepatic common duct, and a long caudal segment on the part of the RPBD [7]. They suggested that these features increase BCs incidence because the bifurcation of the right anterior bile duct and RPBD is close to the liver transection plane, making them more susceptible to direct injury. Generally, when the angle of the left and right hepatic ducts is wider, the transection of the donor bile duct is easier and safer. However, the results obtained in our study suggested the opposite. Cases with a smaller bifurcation angle of the right and left hepatic ducts had a lower incidence of BCs. We suspect that BCs may be caused by more fundamental and physical barriers to biliary reconstruction. Based on the results of our study, we hypothesize that early postoperative bile leakage may result from increased tension in the bile duct due to the lengthened distance between the donor and recipient bile ducts as well as the compression of the bile duct from the dorsal side through the portal vein. Also, we frequently encountered cases in which biliary dilation persisted and jaundice was difficult to resolve, even in the absence of a clear biliary stricture based on cholangiography. This suggests that postoperative cholestasis, including delayed strictures, may be associated with changes in bile duct alignment caused by liver regeneration and the expansion of the transection plane. To confirm this hypothesis, comparative evaluations performed before and after the onset of BCs are necessary, requiring further investigation. It has been reported that the greater the angle formed by the anastomosed donor and recipient bile ducts, the lower the success rate of endoscopic drainage [13]. This suggests that cases with a wider bifurcation angle not only have a higher risk of BCs but may also face challenges in term of successful endoscopic drainage, leading to a dual burden.

This study was conceived because of the impression that BCs are often not fully explained by technical errors alone. LDLT is a highly complex procedure requiring the full range of skills needed for hepato-biliary surgery. Thus, surgeons performing LDLT have at least average skill levels, and they all attempt to perform to the best of their abilities. Nevertheless, the incidence of BCs has not yet been reduced, and in this respect, we felt that something was off. Further investigation of other anatomical risk factors is also required. Portal vein branching patterns (such as type A/B/C variations), as well as variations in the hepatic artery and hepatic vein, may influence the complexity of biliary reconstruction and could potentially contribute to BCs. Although these vascular factors were not evaluated in the present study, their impact warrants future investigation. Given the persistent shortage of deceased donor liver grafts, the demand for LDLT is expected to continue to increase [14–16]. We hypothesize that the incidence of BCs may be reduced through preoperative simulation and intraoperative management aimed at correcting anatomical risk factors. In future work, we plan to explore modifiable factors, such as graft orientation and vascular or biliary configuration, and to investigate intraoperative techniques that may contribute to minimizing these risks.

This study has several limitations. First, the reproducibility and validation of the novel 3D measurement method were not evaluated. Because the analysis was conducted retrospectively using archived donor imaging data, inter-observer and intra-observer reliability could not be assessed. Therefore, the accuracy and consistency of the measurement technique remain to be verified. Future prospective studies involving multiple observers are required to validate the reproducibility and clinical applicability of this method. Secondary, the small number of cases with BCs may have limited the identification of additional risk factors. The findings may represent only a fraction of the risk factors, necessitating the further accumulation of cases and data to allow for a more comprehensive analysis from various perspectives. In addition, this study included a mixture of left and right cases, and the incidence of BCs was low. Thus, the detection frequency was insufficient. Third, bile duct diameter was not evaluated after dilating the bile duct with a contrast medium, as was previously done, because bile duct diameter was measured using DIC-CT, rather than direct cholangiography. The resolution of CT images is limited, which made it difficult to accurately evaluate bile ducts that were only a few millimeters in size from the images alone. A strong alternative method of evaluating biliary diameter preoperatively is direct cholangiography through ERCP, but this is an overly invasive test. Non-invasive tests, such as DIC-CT and MRCP, are adequate alternatives for the evaluation of bile duct anatomy, and we believe that a new evaluation method for bile duct diameter is needed in the future [17]. Moreover, because this study focused on donors’ anatomical factors, the immune system and surgical factors were excluded. Finally, this was a single-center study spanning 17 years, during which surgical and imaging technologies evolved considerably. Although this design ensured methodological consistency, center-specific factors and temporal changes may have influenced the results. Therefore, external validation in multi-center studies is warranted to confirm the generalizability and clinical applicability of the obtained cutoff values.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated a potential association between the biliary anatomy of donors and BCs in recipients after LDLT. A larger bifurcation angle of the left and right hepatic ducts, the rotation angle of the hepatic ducts at the maximum value of the bifurcation angle of the left and right hepatic ducts, and the bifurcation position of the left and right hepatic ducts cranial to the primary branch of the portal vein were identified as significant risk factors for BCs. In the future, establishing a preoperative simulation system that takes these factors into consideration will be important for reducing BCs.
