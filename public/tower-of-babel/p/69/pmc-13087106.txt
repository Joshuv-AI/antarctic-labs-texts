Longitudinal Monitoring of GRIm and HCC‐GRIm Scores Reveals Immunologically Relevant Prognostic Indicators in Hepatocellular Carcinoma Undergoing TACE Plus ICIs and Anti‐VEGF Antibodies/TKIs
Authors: Lihao Qin, Linzhou Zhu, Yu Wang, Wanci Li, Shuai Zhang, Jian Shen, Xiaoli Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the clinical significance and peripheral immunological basis underlying the dynamic changes of Gustave Roussy Immune (GRIm) score and the hepatocellular carcinoma‐specific GRIm (HCC‐GRIm) score in unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC) patients treated with transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and antivascular endothelial growth factor (anti‐VEGF) antibodies or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs).MethodsThis single‐center retrospective study included uHCC patients treated with TACE combined with ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies or TKIs between July 2019 and August 2024. Primary outcomes were the objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR), assessed at the first treatment evaluation at the end of the fourth cycle based on mRECIST criteria; secondary outcomes included overall survival (OS) and progression‐free survival (PFS). GRIm and HCC‐GRIm scores were calculated at four key timepoints: baseline (Cycle 0), second cycle (Cycle 2), fourth cycle (Cycle 4), and disease progression (PD). Their distributions and trajectory patterns were analyzed in relation to OS. Peripheral immune markers were measured at each timepoint to characterize immune response dynamics. Comparisons of immune marker levels and trends were performed between GRIm high and low score groups, as well as between objective responders (OR) and nonresponders (nOR) across treatment stages. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to derive an integrated immune‐metabolic index (PC1) from GRIm components, and its correlations with immune markers were evaluated.ResultsA total of 68 uHCC patients treated with TACE plus ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs were included. At the first evaluation, the ORR was 52.9% and the DCR 86.8%. After a median follow‐up of 18.4 months, the cohort showed a median overall survival (mOS) of 30.1 months and a median progression‐free survival (mPFS) of 10.6 months. GRIm and HCC‐GRIm scores remained stable across treatment phases, with their prognostic value for OS confined to baseline (Cycle 0) assessment (GRIm: p = 0.011; HCC‐GRIm: p = 0.001). Patients with persistently low scores had significantly longer OS than those with increasing trajectories (GRIm: p = 0.025; HCC‐GRIm: p = 0.048). Following treatment initiation, Igλ (p = 0.001), Igκ (p = 0.001), IgG (p = 0.002), and IgA (p = 0.001) levels showed an overall upward trend. At the first evaluation, these markers were significantly elevated in OR compared to baseline (Igλ [p = 0.022], Igκ [p = 0.032], IgG [p = 0.036], IgA [p = 0.019]), whereas no significant changes were observed in nOR (all p > 0.05). Among all timepoints, significant differences between high and low GRIm score groups were noted only at baseline, with higher Igλ (p = 0.001), Igκ (p = 0.014), and IgG (p = 0.009) levels in the high‐score group. In patients with low GRIm scores, linear regression revealed increasing trends in Igλ (p = 0.042), Igκ (p = 0.017), and IgG (p = 0.019) during treatment. PCA showed PC1 correlated positively with Igλ (r = 0.259, p = 0.033), Igκ (r = 0.293, p = 0.015), IgG (r = 0.355, p = 0.003), and complement factor B (CFB; r = 0.314, p = 0.009).ConclusionThe GRIm and HCC‐GRIm scores were shown to be effective prognostic indicators only at baseline in uHCC patients treated with TACE combined with ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies or TKIs. Patients with low GRIm scores demonstrated treatment‐associated increases in Igλ, Igκ, and IgG levels. Igλ, Igκ, and IgG represent measurable links between GRIm‐defined risk categories, immune activation following treatment, and clinical outcomes. Integration of GRIm dynamics and immunoglobulin monitoring into clinical workflows may enhance risk stratification and support personalized treatment strategies in advanced HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver malignancy worldwide, with high morbidity and mortality posing a significant global health challenge [1]. As most patients are diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages, opportunities for curative resection are often limited, rendering systemic therapy a cornerstone of management [1]. In recent years, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and antivascular endothelial growth factor (anti‐VEGF) monoclonal antibodies or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for unresectable HCC (uHCC), owing to its synergistic antitumor effects [2]. Accumulating evidence from phase III clinical trials and real‐world studies supports the superior efficacy of this combination approach in uHCC. The LEAP‐012 trial demonstrated a significant improvement in progression‐free survival (PFS) with lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab combined with TACE (14.6 vs. 10.0 months) [2]. Similarly, the CARES‐005 study reported a median PFS of 11.0 months and an objective response rate (ORR) of 65.0% for camrelizumab and apatinib in combination with TACE, outperforming TACE monotherapy [3]. Real‐world data from the CHANCE001 cohort further corroborated these findings, revealing a median PFS of 9.5 months and an overall survival (OS) of 19.2 months [4]. Subsequent CHANCE series analyses consistently reported ORRs approaching 60% with combination regimens [5, 6]. Despite these advances, clinical responses to TACE‐based combination therapies remain highly heterogeneous, underscoring the pressing need for robust predictive tools to stratify patients, monitor treatment dynamics, and optimize therapeutic outcomes [7].

The Gustave Roussy Immune (GRIm) score, originally developed based on the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and serum albumin (ALB) levels, is a composite index reflecting systemic inflammation and nutritional status. Its prognostic value in patients undergoing ICIs‐based therapy has been validated across several solid tumors [8]. The HCC‐GRIm score was proposed as an HCC‐specific adaptation of the GRIm score, incorporating additional liver function–related parameters including the aspartate aminotransferase (AST)/alanine aminotransferase (ALT) ratio and total bilirubin. Nevertheless, most current studies have focused solely on the prognostic role of baseline (static) GRIm scores, with limited attention paid to their longitudinal evolution and underlying immunological implications throughout the course of treatment. In particular, whether dynamic changes in the GRIm score carry prognostic significance in patients with HCC undergoing TACE combined with immunotherapy and targeted therapy has yet to be systematically investigated.

Furthermore, although the GRIm score is widely used as a clinical prognostic tool, the specific immune landscape it reflects remains poorly defined. Emerging evidence suggests that systemic inflammation can modulate the effectiveness of immunotherapy by altering T‐cell infiltration and antigen presentation within the tumor microenvironment [9, 10]. Therefore, elucidating the temporal trajectories of GRIm‐related peripheral immune markers, especially components of the humoral immune response such as immunoglobulins and complement factors, may provide an immunological foundation for the interpretation of the GRIm score and enhance its clinical applicability.

In this study, we conducted a longitudinal evaluation of the GRIm and HCC‐GRIm scores in a real‐world cohort of patients with uHCC receiving TACE in combination with ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs. We analyzed the dynamic distribution of these scores before treatment, during follow‐up, and at disease progression (PD). The prognostic utility of the GRIm score for OS was assessed, and the association between distinct score trajectory patterns and treatment efficacy was explored. Additionally, peripheral blood based multiparametric immune profiling and principal component analysis (PCA) were conducted to characterize the immunobiological features associated with the GRIm score. Longitudinal immunoassays were further performed to delineate the relationship among GRIm score dynamics, immune marker trajectories, and clinical outcomes. This study aims to provide both clinical and immune basis insights into the utility of the GRIm score as a stratification and monitoring tool in the context of combination immunotherapy for uHCC.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the Ethics Committee. Since the study was retrospective, informed consent was not required. From July 1, 2019, to August 1, 2024, patients with uHCC who underwent TACE plus ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs were included. The eligibility criteria for patient inclusion were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of HCC confirmed either histologically or clinically, in accordance with the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines; (2) Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage B or C; (3) liver function classified as Child–Pugh class A or B, with no evidence of uncontrollable ascites or hepatic encephalopathy; (4) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 0 or 1; (5) treatment with TACE plus ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs. All patients enrolled at our center have been managed according to a standardized protocol of TACE combined with immunotherapy and antiangiogenic therapy, ensuring a consistent treatment framework throughout the study period.

Patients were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), combined hepatocellular‐cholangiocarcinoma, sarcomatoid variant, or fibrolamellar subtype of HCC; (2) presence of concurrent malignancies other than HCC; (3) previous exposure to systemic therapies including ICIs, anti‐VEGF agents, TKIs, or conventional chemotherapy; (4) the absence of key hematologic or immunologic data rendered outcome analysis unfeasible and precluded the calculation of dynamic GRIm and HCC‐GRIm scores, as well as the assessment of longitudinal changes in immune indicators; (5) a follow‐up duration of less than 3 months following initiation of the combination treatment.

TACE Procedure

All TACE procedures were performed by two experienced interventional radiologists, each with over a decade of clinical practice. To reduce interoperator variability and improve embolization precision, a standardized precision TACE protocol was implemented [11]. Subsequent “on‐demand” TACE sessions (typically evaluated after 3–4 treatment cycles) were administered based on dynamic changes in tumor markers and radiological findings. TACE was discontinued under any of the following conditions: (1) progression to Child–Pugh class C, evidenced by complications such as refractory ascites, severe jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, or hepatorenal syndrome; (2) ECOG performance status exceeding 2; or (3) sustained progression of target lesions after three consecutive TACE sessions, as determined by the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) [12]. In patients with extrahepatic disease, TACE was performed with liver‐directed intent, primarily for liver‐dominant intrahepatic tumor burden, to achieve intrahepatic control/debulking and support ongoing systemic therapy. Eligibility for TACE was determined by MDT assessment, considering ECOG performance status (0–2), liver function (Child–Pugh A/B), and procedure suitability.

ICIs and Anti‐VEGF Antibodies/TKIs Administration

All patients included in this study received combination therapy consisting of ICIs (camrelizumab or sintilimab) alongside either anti‐VEGF antibodies (bevacizumab) or TKIs (lenvatinib or sorafenib), all of which were approved by the National Medical Products Administration and available for clinical use in China. In routine clinical practice, systemic therapy was administered using one of the following patterns: (i) ICI plus an anti‐VEGF antibody (bevacizumab), or (ii) ICI plus an oral TKI (lenvatinib or sorafenib). Regimen selection and sequencing were individualized according to national guidelines and routine clinical protocols [4]. For those treated with oral TKIs, therapy was withheld for 2–3 days before and after each TACE session to minimize procedural complications. All drugs were given at standard dosages and schedules in accordance with national guidelines and routine clinical protocols. Treatment with ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies or TKIs was continued until radiographic evidence of PD or the occurrence of unacceptable toxicity.

Molecular targeted therapies, including TKIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies, were administered at their standard recommended doses and schedules. For oral TKIs, treatment was temporarily withheld for 2 days before and after each TACE session in the absence of significant postprocedural symptoms. Dose modifications for TKIs and anti‐VEGF agents were allowed in accordance with product labeling and clinical judgment to manage treatment‐related adverse events (AEs). ICIs were given at standard dosing regimens without dose reductions; however, temporary discontinuation was permitted for immune‐related AEs of grade ≥ 2 and resumed once toxicity improved to grade ≤ 1, with a maximum interruption period of 12 weeks. TKIs were paused in the event of grade ≥ 2 toxicities, such as hypertension, proteinuria, or hand–foot skin reaction, and subsequently restarted at the same or a reduced dose following recovery. All treatment adjustments followed established clinical protocols and were tailored to individual patient tolerance and organ function. AEs were classified and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [13].

Follow‐Up and Data Collection

The primary outcomes of this study were the ORR and disease control rate (DCR) assessed at the first treatment evaluation (at the fourth treatment cycle). Secondary outcomes included OS, PFS, and AEs. AEs were evaluated according to CTCAE v5.0. Following the initiation of combination therapy, patients underwent follow‐up assessments every 6–9 weeks. Each visit involved imaging evaluations, such as contrast‐enhanced abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and noncontrast chest‐abdomen computed tomography (CT), alongside a comprehensive set of laboratory tests. These included complete blood counts, biochemical panels, tumor marker assays, HBV‐DNA levels, cytokine profiles, immune status evaluations, chest pain screening, and thyroid function assessments.

ICIs were administered at 3‐week intervals (one treatment cycle), with laboratory evaluations conducted prior to each treatment cycle, including hematologic parameters, biochemical tests, immune indices, chest pain panels, and thyroid function assessments. The baseline, second, and fourth treatment cycles were designated as Cycle0, Cycle2, and Cycle4, respectively. Biochemical and immune indices were collected and recorded at Cycle0, Cycle2, Cycle4, the time of first radiologic assessment, and at the point of PD, if applicable, for subsequent analyses.

Peripheral Immune Markers

Peripheral blood samples were collected before TACE and every 3 weeks prior to each ICI cycle. Immune profiling included lymphocyte subsets, namely the proportions (%) of CD3+ T cells, CD3+CD4+ helper T cells, CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, CD3−CD16+56+ natural killer (NK) cells, and CD3−CD19+ B cells, together with the CD4+/CD8+ ratio. Additional serum immunological markers included immunoglobulin λ (Igλ, mg/dL) and κ (Igκ, mg/dL), complement C3 (g/L) and C4 (g/L), immunoglobulin classes M (IgM, g/L), A (IgA, g/L), and G (IgG, g/L), as well as complement factor B (CFB, mg/dL).

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density‐gradient centrifugation (Ficoll‐Paque) and resuspended to a final concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Flow cytometry was performed on a FACSCalibur system (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Serum immunoglobulins and complement proteins were quantified using immunoturbidimetric assays under routine clinical laboratory quality‐control procedures. The NLR was derived from standard complete blood count data.

Relevant Definitions

The GRIm score was determined based on three parameters: serum ALB (< 3.5 g/dL = 1 point), serum LDH (>220 U/L = 1 point), and NLR (> 6 = 1 point). Patients scoring 0–1 were categorized as low‐risk, while those with 2–3 points were considered high‐risk. The HCC‐GRIm score incorporated two additional liver function indicators, AST/ALT ratio (≥ 1.44 = 1 point) and total bilirubin (≥ 1.3 mg/dL = 1 point), in addition to the original GRIm components. Based on the total score, patients were classified into low‐risk (0–2 points) or high‐risk (3–5 points) groups.

The criteria for the timing of combination therapy were defined as simultaneous use of the initial TACE and the first ICI treatment or within a 1‐month interval, with TKIs used simultaneously with TACE or ICIs. At least four cycles (every 3 weeks constitutes a cycle) of ICI treatment should be used after TACE. Survival data were updated as of January 2025. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow‐up, while PFS was defined as the interval between diagnosis and either PD or death, whichever occurred first. Initial treatment response was assessed at the fourth treatment cycle or after 3 months of therapy, in accordance with mRECIST criteria. The first evaluation is often conducted in the fourth cycle or the third month. Based on the first evaluation, patients were categorized into PD, stable disease (SD), and objective response (OR, including complete response [CR] and partial response [PR]) groups. The ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR or PR, while the DCR was defined as the proportion of patients with CR, PR, or SD. All included HCC patients had chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection as the underlying etiology. Clinically significant HBV activation was defined as a serum HBV DNA level > 60 IU/mL in the context of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positivity.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were expressed as means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. For normally distributed variables, differences were analyzed using the chi‐squared test or two‐tailed paired Student's t‐test. For non‐normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test or Mann–Whitney U test was applied. OS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between survival curves were compared using the log‐rank test. Simple linear regression was used to evaluate changes in immune indicators across treatment timepoints (e.g., Cycle 0, Cycle 2, Cycle 4). PCA was conducted on GRIm score components (ALB, NLR, LDH) after standardization. PC1, which explained the highest proportion of variance, was selected for further analysis. Pearson's correlation was used to assess associations between PC1 scores and peripheral immune markers. Statistics were judged to be significant when p values were less than 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

From July 1, 2019, to August 1, 2024, a total of 163 patients undergoing TACE plus ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs were screened for eligibility. Patients were excluded for the following reasons: histologically confirmed intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, mixed HCC, sarcomatoid HCC, or fibrolamellar HCC (n = 5); coexistence of other malignancies (n = 5); prior systemic therapy (n = 11); incomplete hematological or immunological data, resulting in insufficient follow‐up information (n = 70); and follow‐up duration of less than 3 months after initiation of combination therapy (n = 4). After applying these criteria, 68 patients with complete clinical and follow‐up data were included in the final analysis. Efficacy and Safety

At the first treatment assessment, the ORR was 52.9%, and the DCR reached 86.8%. As of the last follow‐up on January 1, 2025, the median follow‐up duration was 18.4 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 11.2–29.5). The median overall survival (mOS) for the cohort was 30.1 months (95% CI: 18.3–32.6), and the median progression‐free survival (mPFS) was 10.6 months (95% CI: 8.3–12.1).

AEs observed during the study were generally mild and manageable, with no treatment‐related deaths recorded. The overall incidence of AEs was 58.8% (40 out of 68 patients), with the most common events being fever (55.9%), abdominal pain (45.6%), liver function abnormalities (19.1%), hand‐foot skin reaction (22.1%), hypertension (14.7%), thrombocytopenia (8.8%), proteinuria (16.2%), and immune‐related myocarditis (1.5%).

GRIm‐Based Score Dynamics During Treatment and Progression

The distribution of GRIm scores remained relatively stable across treatment phases. At Cycle 0, 73.5% of patients were classified as low GRIm score, which was similar at Cycle 2 (72.1%) and Cycle 4 (73.5%). At PD, the proportion decreased slightly to 63.8%, with no significant overall change (p = 0.652). A similar trend was observed for HCC‐GRIm scores. The proportion of patients in the low‐score group increased from 69.1% at Cycle 0 to 75.0% at Cycle 4, then slightly declined to 66.0% at PD. No significant differences in HCC‐GRIm score distribution were detected across phases (p = 0.739, Table 2).

Survival Assessment

Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to assess the prognostic value of GRIm and HCC‐GRIm scores for OS at different treatment stages (Figure 1). At baseline (Cycle0), both scores significantly stratified patient prognosis, with worse OS observed in the high‐score groups (GRIm: p = 0.011; HCC‐GRIm: p = 0.001). At Cycle2, Cycle4, and during PD, the differences in OS between high and low score groups were not statistically significant for either GRIm or HCC‐GRIm scores (Cycle2—GRIm: p = 0.23, HCC‐GRIm: p = 0.32; Cycle4—GRIm: p = 0.073, HCC‐GRIm: p = 0.098; PD—GRIm: p = 0.069, HCC‐GRIm: p = 0.068).

Prediction of OS by scores at different stages.

Patients were classified into three groups based on GRIm and HCC‐GRIm score trajectories: consistently low, gradually increasing, and other patterns (Figure 2, Table 3). For the GRIm score, the consistently low group had the longest median OS (34.0 months, 95% CI: 26.9–Not Reached [NR]), compared to 23.8 months (95% CI: 7.8–NR) in the gradually increasing group and 20.4 months (95% CI: 13.6–56.8) in the other group. The difference between the consistently low and gradually increasing groups was significant (p = 0.025). Similarly, the HCC‐GRIm score showed longer OS in the consistently low group (34.0 months, 95% CI: 18.3–NR) versus 23.8 months (95% CI: 7.8–NR) and 23.1 months (95% CI: 11.5–56.8) in the gradually increasing and other groups, respectively (p = 0.048).

Longitudinal Changes in Peripheral Immune Markers Following Treatment Initiation

The dynamic profiles of 14 immune markers were analyzed at Cycle0, Cycle2, and Cycle4 to evaluate immunological alterations following initiation of immunotherapy (Figure 3). In the overall study population, Igλ, Igκ, and IgG levels showed an overall upward trend during treatment (p < 0.01 for all comparisons). In contrast, CD3−CD19+ B cell percentages and CFB levels exhibited an overall downward trend over time.

Longitudinal changes in peripheral immune markers before (Cycle 0) and after (Cycle 2 and Cycle 4) initiation of immunotherapy. Thirteen immune indicators were evaluated at Cycle 0, Cycle 2, and Cycle 4. Significant increases in Igλ, Igκ, and IgG were observed following treatment initiation. These changes reflect enhanced humoral immune activation during therapy and correspond to markers differentially expressed across GRIm score groups at baseline (Igλ, Igκ, and IgG).

Associations Between Immune Marker Changes and Treatment Response

To investigate the relationship between immune indicators and objective treatment response, immune profiles were compared between OR and nOR patients at baseline (Cycle0) and first treatment evaluation (Table 4). In patients who achieved an OR, significant increases from baseline (Cycle0) to the first evaluation were observed in Igλ (576.00 [472.25–764.00] vs. 674.00 [584.00–811.75], p = 0.022), Igκ (1070.00 [844.00–1435.00] vs. 1285.00 [1060.00–1622.50], p = 0.032), IgG (12.50 [10.60–16.90] vs. 14.85 [12.48–18.10], p = 0.036), and IgA (2.82 [2.53–3.81] vs. 3.69 [2.93–4.73], p = 0.019). Meanwhile, the proportion of CD3−CD19+ significantly decreased (9.78 [6.74–16.18] vs. 6.54 [4.57–10.58], p = 0.018). By contrast, patients who did not achieve an objective response (nOR) showed no significant changes in any immune markers over the same period (all p > 0.05).

GRIm Score–Related Immune Profiles and Temporal Trends

To evaluate the immunological relevance of GRIm score, 14 peripheral immune markers were compared between high and low GRIm score groups across four treatment stages (Cycle0, Cycle2, Cycle4, and during PD) (Figure 4). Significant differences were observed only at baseline (Cycle0), where Igλ (p = 0.001), Igκ (p = 0.014), and IgG (p = 0.009) levels were all elevated in the high‐score group. No significant differences were found in lymphocyte subsets or other humoral markers at this stage. During Cycle2, Cycle4, and PD, no consistent differences in immune parameters were detected between the groups, apart from a transient elevation in CD4+/CD8+ ratio at Cycle2 (p = 0.02), which was not observed at subsequent timepoints.

PCA of GRIm Score Components and Correlation With Immune Markers

To explore the underlying structure of GRIm score components and their relationship with immune markers, PCA was performed using three variables: ALB, NLR, and LDH. All data were standardized prior to analysis. The scree plot revealed that the first principal component (PC1) accounted for the largest proportion of total variance (67.0%) and was selected for subsequent analyses (Figure 5A). The loading plot (Figure 5D) and component matrix indicated that ALB contributed negatively to PC1, while both NLR and LDH contributed positively. PC1 scores were then calculated and shown to follow a near‐normal distribution (Figure 5C). Scatterplots further confirmed clear associations between PC1 scores and each of the original variables (Figure 5B), with higher PC1 values corresponding to lower ALB and higher NLR/LDH, suggesting that PC1 effectively summarizes the proinflammatory and hypoalbuminemic profile captured by the GRIm score. To assess the immunological relevance of PC1, Pearson correlation analysis was performed between PC1 scores and 13 immune‐related parameters (Figure 5E; Table 5). PC1 scores were positively correlated with Igλ (r = 0.259, p = 0.033), Igκ (r = 0.293, p = 0.015), IgG (r = 0.355, p = 0.003), and CFB (r = 0.314, p = 0.009). A subset of these markers had also been previously identified as significantly associated with treatment response and GRIm score stratification.

Discussion

The GRIm and HCC‐GRIm scores, initially developed as prognostic indices integrating systemic inflammation and nutritional status, have demonstrated promising predictive value in various malignancies treated with ICIs [15, 16]. However, the dynamic changes and immunological basis of these scores in the context of HCC undergoing combination therapy remain poorly defined. In this study, we systematically evaluated the temporal patterns of GRIm‐related scores, their association with peripheral immune markers, and their potential mechanistic links to therapeutic response in a cohort of patients with uHCC treated with TACE in combination with ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies or TKIs.

We first found that both GRIm and HCC‐GRIm scores remained relatively stable throughout the treatment course, with no significant shifts across timepoints. Notably, their prognostic value was primarily confined to the baseline (Cycle 0), where patients with high GRIm scores exhibited significantly shorter OS. The predictive value diminished progressively during follow‐up, suggesting that these scores reflect the host–tumor immune equilibrium prior to therapy initiation and have a critical role in determining downstream treatment response [17]. Moreover, patients who maintained consistently low GRIm or HCC‐GRIm scores during treatment had significantly longer OS than those with gradually increasing or fluctuating scores. This implies that sustained low inflammatory burden and favorable nutritional status may preserve immune responsiveness and contribute to better therapeutic benefit [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. These findings reinforce the notion that inflammation, nutritional status, and immunity are interdependent elements shaping treatment tolerance and efficacy in immuno‐oncology [21, 22, 23].

Through longitudinal immune profiling, this study revealed a robust three‐way relationship among GRIm score dynamics, immune marker trajectories, and clinical outcomes, further supporting the biological plausibility of the GRIm score as a mechanistic biomarker of immunotherapy response. At baseline, patients with high GRIm scores had significantly elevated levels of Igλ, Igκ, and IgG compared to those with low scores. However, these differences did not persist or expand during the early (Cycle 2) and intermediate (Cycle 4) treatment phases, suggesting early nonspecific humoral activation in GRIm‐high patients without subsequent amplification. Further stratified analysis showed that Igλ, Igκ, and IgG levels significantly increased between baseline and first response evaluation in patients achieving an OR, whereas no such changes were observed in nOR. This response‐specific trajectory suggests that enhancement of humoral immunity during treatment may be a key mechanism driving therapeutic efficacy [24, 25, 26].

When immune trajectory was analyzed by GRIm score subgroups, only patients with low GRIm scores demonstrated increases in Igλ, Igκ, and IgG over time. In contrast, GRIm‐high patients had elevated baseline immunoglobulin levels but showed minimal further changes throughout treatment. These findings suggest that while GRIm‐high patients exhibit a phenotype of preactivated immunity, they may lack the capacity for further adaptive immune engagement due to factors such as immune exhaustion, B‐cell dysfunction, or impaired antigen presentation [27, 28, 29]. The gradual increase in immunoglobulin levels observed in the GRIm‐low group aligned with higher OR rates and improved OS, forming a logical axis of low score—dynamic immune activation—favorable outcome. Thus, beyond reflecting a static immune‐metabolic state, the GRIm score also serves as a dynamic classifier of patients' immunological responsiveness and therapeutic sensitivity. This triadic biomarker framework links prognostic scoring, immune reactivity, and treatment outcomes, offering mechanistic insight into the clinical utility of the GRIm score for patient stratification.

To further validate the immunological relevance of the GRIm score, PCA was used to synthesize its three core components (NLR, LDH, ALB) into a single index (PC1) representing systemic immune‐metabolic status. Higher PC1 values corresponded to stronger inflammatory burden and poorer nutritional reserve. Correlation analysis demonstrated positive associations between PC1 and Igλ, Igκ, IgG, and CFB, indicating that systemic inflammation is associated with humoral immune activation. Nevertheless, this association should be interpreted with due caution. Elevated immunoglobulin levels may reflect nonspecific polyclonal B‐cell activation rather than effective antitumor immunity, particularly in chronic inflammatory states such as cirrhosis, viral hepatitis, or advanced solid tumors [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Such elevations may arise from persistent antigenic stimulation or immune dysregulation and are not necessarily accompanied by effective T‐cell or dendritic cell coordination [36, 37, 38]. Therefore, the increased immunoglobulin levels observed in GRIm‐high or PC1‐high patients likely reflect nonfunctional immune activation rather than productive therapeutic responses.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the retrospective, single‐center design and modest sample size may have introduced selection bias, particularly in relation to the availability and completeness of immune profiling data; some cases were excluded due to missing hematologic or immunological parameters. Second, immune markers were assessed exclusively in peripheral blood, without parallel evaluation of tumor‐infiltrating immune cells or the local tumor microenvironment, which may have constrained the biological insight into immune dynamics. In addition, circulating Igλ, Igκ, and IgG reflect overall humoral activity and may be influenced by nonspecific polyclonal B‐cell activation and chronic inflammation in HBV‐related liver disease; therefore, these changes should not be interpreted as direct evidence of tumor‐specific antitumor immunity. Third, although the use of PCA allowed for an integrated assessment of inflammatory and nutritional status, the results warrant validation in larger, prospective, and ideally multicenter cohorts. Incorporation of additional multiomics approaches could also enhance the mechanistic depth of future investigations.

Finally, all patients had HBV‐related HCC, which limits extension of these findings to other etiologies (e.g., HCV or MASH/ALD). Because etiology influences the immune‐inflammatory background, validation in multietiology cohorts with etiology‐stratified analyses is warranted.

In conclusion, this study is the first to systematically link GRIm score dynamics, peripheral immune marker trajectories, and clinical outcomes in uHCC patients undergoing TACE combined with immunotherapy and anti‐VEGF agents. The GRIm score was shown to be not only a static prognostic tool but also a functional marker of immunological plasticity. Patients with low GRIm scores exhibited dynamic humoral immune activation and superior outcomes, while those with high scores may have entered a state of immune exhaustion, limiting further responsiveness to treatment. These findings provide a robust immunological rationale for using GRIm‐based models in the stratification and monitoring of patients undergoing combination therapy for HCC.
