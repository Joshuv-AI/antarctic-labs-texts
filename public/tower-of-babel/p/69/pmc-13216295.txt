Unveiling the antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory and anti-hemolytic activities of Raphanus sativus leaves through in-vitro and in-silico studies
Authors: Rukiye Hasret Şahin, Emine Yalçın, Kültiğin Çavuşoğlu, Burak Özkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Raphanus sativus L. (Brassicaceae) is a widely consumed plant known for its nutritional and medicinal properties. This study comprehensively investigated the phytochemical composition and multifaceted biological activities of R. sativus leaves collected from the Bulancak (Giresun-Türkiye) region. Unlike previous studies in the literature, which generally focus on the root portion, this study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of the leaves with a multidisciplinary approach. In this study, the extraction, phytochemical analysis, and the activities of locally sourced R. sativus leaves were investigated. High total phenolic (45.6 mg GAE/g) and flavonoid (35.6 mg QE/g) contents were detected in the R. sativus extract. At the same time, rosmarinic acid, p-coumaric acid, and caffeic acid were identified as the major components by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry. The extracts exhibited antidiabetic activity via enzyme inhibition, with IC50 values ​​ranging from 31.56 to 51.15 µg/mL for α-amylase and 28.4 to 49.65 µg/mL for α-glucosidase. Molecular docking analysis was performed to support the in-vitro antidiabetic activity. Rosmarinic, caffeic, and p-coumaric acids were found to bind to α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes via hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. For anti-inflammatory activity, inhibition of albumin protein denaturation was investigated, and the highest activity was found in the chloroform fraction (72.6%). To confirm protein denaturation inhibitory activity, the spectroscopic interactions of the main compounds, rosmarinic acid and caffeic acid, were investigated. It was observed that as the proportion of rosmarinic acid increased (from 1:1 to 1:4), the absorbance decreased across all wavelengths. In the anti-hemolytic activity test, the chloroform fraction showed the highest effectiveness against 2,2’-Azodiisobutyramidine dihydrochloride-induced erythrocyte hemolysis with 69.35% protection at 100 µg/mL. Overall, the results indicate that R. sativus leaves possess potent antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, and antihemolytic properties, making them an important natural resource with potential for the development of functional foods or pharmaceutical products.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47636-x.

Introduction

The extensive diversity of plant species worldwide represents an important source for the discovery of novel compounds with potential biological activity. Plant secondary metabolites – including alkaloids, terpenoids, phenolics, and flavonoids – exhibit a wide variety of pharmacological activities1–3. Radish (Raphanus sativus L.) belongs to the Brassicaceae (Cruciferae) family and is a nutrient-rich vegetable with a wide variation, a broad growing area, and high production. R. sativus is widely distributed worldwide and is predominantly found in hilly regions between March and August. It exhibits a variety of external characteristics, including leaf type, leaf arrangement, flower petal colour, flower symmetry, and fruit type4,5. The coloration of R. sativus flower petals can be categorized as pink, white, or pale red. Two distinct types of leaves can be observed: compound leaves, composed of two or more separate leaflets, and simple leaves, which may be lobed or unlobed but are not divided into leaflets. The root of R. sativus shows considerable variability in shape, color, and size, depending on the specific variety or cultivar. The variety with red roots is referred to as hazelnut radish, the white variety as chestnut radish, and the black variety as horseradish3. The use of these divergent nomenclatures serves to highlight and distinguish the characteristics of various radish varieties6,7.

Approximately 7 million tons of R. sativus are produced annually worldwide, representing about 2% of total vegetable production8. A significant portion of production in Türkiye occurs in the Mediterranean, Aegean, and Central Anatolia regions9. R. sativus is classified as a root vegetable due to its unique hypocotyl structure, which allows the roots to store starch and other compounds10,11. In addition to being rich in vitamins A, B, and C, calcium, phosphorus, potassium, and iron, R. sativus contains substantial amounts of anthocyanin pigments and isothiocyanates12,13. The anthocyanins contained in R. sativus are compounds primarily identified in the roots, shoots, and bark. Of these compounds, which exist in the form of anthocyanidin glycosides and acetylated anthocyanins, cyanidin, delphinidin, pelargonidin, peonidin, petunidin, and malvidin are predominant in R. sativus14. Rich in flavonoids, including quercetin and kaempferol, the plant also contains pigments, polysaccharides, proteoglycans, sulfur compounds, and β-carotene15. Since the tubers of R. sativus are primarily used for culinary purposes, comprehensive studies on phytochemicals found in the root structure are available, while research on other tissues, such as leaves, is limited. However, although rare, similar studies can be found on the leaves, which are consumed both raw and cooked. While R. sativus leaves contain high concentrations of calcium, potassium, sodium, fiber, fatty acids, and non-flavonoid polyphenols, the sprouts are richer in flavonoids, especially anthocyanins, β-carotene, and vitamin C16. Beevi et al.17 reported that dry methanolic and acetone extracts of leaves and stems had total polyphenol contents of 86.16 mg/g and 78.77 mg/g, respectively, and these values ​​were similar to those of green and black tea, traditional sources of polyphenols. Goyeneche et al.18 found that the protein concentration in the leaves (3810 mg/100 g) was higher than in the roots (570 mg/100 g). Leaves have higher levels of amino acids, protein, calcium, and ascorbic acid compared to roots. Furthermore, the total phenol content in leaves is twice as high as in roots. This is consistent with the leaves’ high free radical scavenging ability. Radish leaves can be considered an excellent source of bioactive compounds, particularly those known to possess antioxidant potential. Leaves and sprouts can be considered part of a healthy diet and have high potential as raw materials for the development of nutraceuticals17,18. R. sativus has been shown to possess a multitude of biological activities, attributable to the presence of active compounds within its composition. It has been demonstrated to possess antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiproliferative, anticancer, and antidiabetic properties, thereby protecting against hypertension and cardiometabolic disorders19,20. As demonstrated by Wang et al.21, a plethora of in-vitro experiments have indisputably evidenced the antioxidant and prooxidant properties of various red radish species. It has been demonstrated that extracts of R. sativus exhibit a substantial capacity to impede the peroxyl radical-induced oxidation of linoleic acid. This phenomenon is attributed to the presence of polyphenols in R. sativus. These polyphenols have been demonstrated to exhibit a protective effect against the peroxidation of linoleic acid and also to protect against oxidation-mediated diseases17. Daily dietary consumption of R. sativus has been shown to provide significant protection against cardiovascular disease, and this effect has been associated with the presence of saponins, flavonoids, tannins, phenols, and alkaloids22. It has also been found that R. sativus leaves and tubers contain various polar and non-polar compounds exhibiting antimicrobial activity. The literature reports that R. sativus extracts have the capacity to inhibit the growth of E. coli, P. pyocyaneus, S. typhi, and B. subtilis in-vitro7,23. R.sativus has been reported to contain sulforaphane, which has the capacity to inhibit the proliferation of colon cancer cells24. Clarke et al.25 demonstrated that R. sativus application inhibits carcinogenesis through various mechanisms. The hypoglycemic effect of R. sativus roots, which have been found to have antidiabetic activity, is related to the flavonoids and anthocyanins they contain26. The presence of anthocyanins such as pelargonidin has also demonstrated the potential effectiveness of R. sativus in lowering blood sugar levels27. The literature contains studies investigating antidiabetic and anti-inflammatory activities, which were also evaluated in the present study. Dhanalakshmi et al.28 reported that the methanol extract of R. sativus leaves exhibited amylase inhibition in the range of 79.34%−74.74% and supported this result in silico. Park et al.29 showed that the chloroform fraction of R. sativus leaves suppressed NO release in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages, reduced iNOS and COX-2 expression, and showed a significant anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway.

Unlike previous studies, this study investigated the biological effects of R. sativus leaves within a single experimental framework to provide a broader and more comprehensive evaluation. The analyses included phytochemical profiling (LC-MS/MS), antidiabetic activity (α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition), anti-inflammatory activity (protein denaturation assay), anti-hemolytic activity, and in silico molecular docking analyses. Although diabetes, inflammation, and hemolysis appear as clinically distinct conditions, they share common pathophysiological mechanisms that underlie their close relationship. At the basis of these three conditions lies chronic oxidative stress, free radical production, protein and membrane damage, which together create a self-reinforcing cycle. Diabetes, characterized by chronic hyperglycemia, leads to prolonged high glucose levels, mitochondrial dysfunction, and excessive production of reactive oxygen species. This, in turn, increases cellular oxidative stress and triggers inflammatory signaling pathways30. Increased cytokine production and free radical generation during inflammatory processes exert significant effects, particularly on erythrocytes and their membrane proteins. Increased oxidative stress during inflammation leads to decreased erythrocyte membrane fluidity, structural degradation of membrane proteins such as band 3 and glycophorin A, and weakening of membrane integrity, thereby increasing susceptibility to hemolysis31,32. In summary, diabetes, inflammation, and hemolysis can be considered different biological outcomes of a single pathological axis centered on oxidative stress. Therefore, evaluating these three parameters together is crucial for a holistic understanding of the effects of herbal extracts, especially those with antihemolytic, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic potential. Thus, this study not only reveals a single biological effect but also allows for the evaluation of the systemic and multifaceted protective potential of plant material. Another innovative aspect of the study is the mechanistic elucidation of the obtained in-vitro biological activity findings through supporting analyses such as in-silico molecular docking. Demonstrating the interactions of major phenolic compounds such as rosmarinic acid, caffeic acid, and p-coumaric acid with target enzymes and proteins validated the results not only at the phenotypic but also at the molecular level. In addition, sourcing of samples from the Bulancak (Giresun-Türkiye) region highlights the influence of geographical conditions on phytochemical composition, thereby adding region rich plant diversityal specificity to the study. In these respects, the study makes a significant and original contribution to the literature by advancing both the discovery of plant-derived natural agents and the assessment of biological effects based on geographic variation.

Materials and methods

Sample processing and targeted phytochemical extraction

R. sativus samples were collected from the Giresun-Bulancak region (40.938398oN, 38.280795oE) on 01 February 2023, and the tubers and diseased leaves were discarded, while healthy green leaves were used for the study. Plant identification was performed by Zafer Türkmen at the Department of Botany, Giresun University, and the specimens were subsequently deposited in the herbarium under the voucher number BIO-Rsat-2023. All experimental procedures involving plant material, including the collection of plant specimens, were conducted in accordance with institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation. The leaves of R. sativus were dried in an oven at 35 °C and subsequently ground into a fine powder. Powdered leaf samples (2 g) were extracted by maceration using a variety of solvents, including water, chloroform, butanol, and hexane (100 mL). The extraction process was conducted in a shaking incubator at ambient temperature for a duration of 24 h. The solvent phase was then filtered using Whatman No. 4 filter paper to remove solid particulate matter. The filtrate was subjected to a centrifugation process at a speed of 5000 rpm for a duration of 10 min, after which the resulting upper layer was collected and concentrated using a rotary evaporator. The residue obtained after solvent evaporation was collected and used in subsequent analyses. The extraction efficiency for each solvent was calculated as the percentage of extract yield relative to the initial plant material33.

Phytochemical characterization and profiling

The total flavonoid content (TFC) of R. sativus extract was determined using a spectrophotometric method32. Quercetin (QE) was used as the standard compound, and flavonoid amounts were expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalents (QE) per gram of dry weight. The total phenolic content (TPC) of the R. sativus extract was determined using the Folin- Ciocalteu method34. TPC is expressed in terms of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) on a per gram basis of dry weight. The quantitative determination of phenolic compounds in R. sativus leaves was performed using liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. For this purpose, 1 g of plant sample was extracted in a methanol–dichloromethane (4:1, v/v) solvent system. The resulting extract was filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter and subjected to analysis. LC-MS/MS analyses were carried out on a Thermo Scientific instrument equipped with an ODS Hypersil column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The column oven temperature was set at 30 °C, the mobile phase flow rate was 0.7 mL/min, and the total analysis time was 34 min. The gradient profile used is presented in Table S1 in the supplementary material. LC-MS/MS analyses were conducted at Hitit University HUBTUAM laboratory35,36.

in-vitro and in-silico antidiabetic potential of R. sativus

The in-vitro antidiabetic potential of R. sativus extracts was evaluated using α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzyme inhibition tests. For α-amylase inhibition, 250 µL of extract (5–100 µg/mL) was mixed with 125 µL of 0.02 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.9) and α-amylase solution at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. The mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 10 min, after which 250 µL of 1% starch solution was added. The reaction mixture was further incubated at 25 °C for 10 min and then terminated by adding 0.5 mL of dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) reagent. The mixtures were heated in a boiling water bath for 5 min and subsequently diluted with 5 mL of distilled water. Absorbance values ​​were recorded at 540 nm. For α-glucosidase inhibition, 50 µL of extract (5–100 µg/mL) was mixed with 100 µL of α-glucosidase (1 U/mL) dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.9) in 96-well plates and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. After incubation, 50 µL of 5 mM p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG) substrate solution prepared in the same buffer was added to each well. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 25 °C for 5 min, before absorbance values ​​were measured at 405 nm. The percentage of enzyme inhibition was calculated using Eq. 1. Inhibition rates were plotted against inhibitor concentration, and IC50 values ​​were determined as the concentration required to inhibit enzyme activity by 50%. Anti-inflammatory potential

The serum albumin protein denaturation inhibition assay was used to evaluate the potential anti-inflammatory activity. A volume of 1 mL of 1% bovine serum albumin in phosphate-buffered saline (pH 6.4), was added to 1 mL of extract at varying doses, ranging from 5 to 100 µg/mL. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20 min and then heated at 70 °C for 5 min. After cooling to room temperature, the turbidity of the resulting solution was measured at 660 nm. The experiment was repeated with aspirin as the positive control. Abscontrol denotes the absorbance of the control (without sample), whereas Abssample represents the absorbance in the presence of the sample. The IC50 values were determined as the concentration corresponding to 50% inhibition41.

To support the protein denaturation inhibition activity and to elucidate the underlying mechanism, the interaction of two major compounds detected in the R. sativus extract with albumin was investigated spectroscopically. Rosmarinic acid and caffeic acid were used as ligands. Absorption spectra were recorded using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer in the wavelength range of 240–340 nm. UV spectra of BSA (3 mg/mL) solutions were recorded in the absence and presence of ligands. Albumin/ligand mixtures at ratios of 1:0, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4 were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and the absorbance of each solution was measured. All experiments were conducted in triplicate for each mixture.

Molecular docking-supported in-vitro anti-hemolytic activity

The anti-hemolytic activity of R. sativus extract was evaluated using a 10% erythrocyte suspension (hematocrit) obtained from a healthy donor, which was pretreated with different extract concentrations (5–100 µg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Blood samples used in this study were obtained from routine laboratory examinations conducted for members of the research team during regular health check-ups, independent of the experimental procedures. As part of these examinations, several hematological and biochemical parameters were evaluated, including RBC, Hb, Hct, MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW, WBC, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, serum bilirubin, ALT, AST, BUN, creatinine, LDH, PLT, MPV, PDW, and PCT. All evaluated parameters were within normal clinical reference ranges, and no abnormalities were detected in the volunteer from whom the blood sample was obtained. The use of human-derived biological material in this study was limited to an in vitro anti-hemolytic assay using peripheral blood and did not involve any clinical or interventional procedures. The study was conducted within the framework of a laboratory practice involving minimal risk, anonymized data, and using only erythrocyte suspension. Written informed consent was obtained from the donor, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study received an ethics committee exemption issued by Giresun University, Faculty of Science and Art, Department of Biology. For anti-hemolytic activity test, 500 µL of 2,2’-Azodiisobutyramidine dihydrochloride (AAPH) solution (200 mM) was added, and the reaction mixtures were incubated under the same conditions for 4 h. Subsequently, the final volume of all tubes was adjusted to 4.5 mL with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and the tubes were then subjected to a centrifugation process at 2000 rpm for a period of 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 540 nm. The same procedure was repeated using ascorbic acid as a positive control under identical experimental conditions. To support the antihemolytic effect determined in-vitro, major plant compounds were interacted with erythrocyte membrane proteins using the in-silico molecular docking method. For this purpose, band 3 protein, which facilitates anion exchange and found in the erythrocyte membrane42, and glycophorin A, which causes negative surface charge on mature erythrocytes and prevents erythrocyte aggregation43, were used as target molecules. Protein structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Band 3 (PDB ID: 4YZF)44 and glycophorin A (PDB ID: 1AFO)45 data were loaded into Maestro BioLuminate 5.0 version 5.0) ((https://www.schrodinger.com/platform/products/bioluminate/) (Schrödinger Release 2023), and their structural deficiencies and missing hydrogen atoms were identified and corrected. Energy minimization of the processed protein structures was subsequently performed using Swiss-PdbViewer (v. 4.10) (version 4.10) (https://spdbv.unil.ch/)46. Data for the plant compounds selected as ligands and the ascorbic acid (positive control agent) were obtained from the PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database. Energy minimization was performed using the Avogadro program (version 1.2.0) (https://avogadro.cc/)47 for the structures of ascorbic acid (PubChem ID: 155903693), caffeic acid (PubChem ID: 689043), p-coumaric acid (PubChem ID: 637542), and rosmarinic acid (PubChem ID: 5281792), which are major compounds found in the plant (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The binding sites of the target proteins were predicted and scored using the PrankWeb server (https://prankweb.cz/)48–51. Then, the proteins and chemical compounds were docked with AutoDock Vina version (version 4.2.6) (https://vina.scripps.edu/)37 and visualized using the Biovia Discovery Studio 2020 Client (version 24.1.0) (https://www.3ds.com/products/biovia/discovery-studio) (BIOVIA 2023) program.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using the “IBM SPSS Statistics 22” software package, and the obtained data are presented as mean ± SD (standard deviation). The statistical significance between means was determined using the Duncan test and One-way ANOVA, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

The leaves of R. sativus were extracted using the maceration method using different solvents. Among the solvents used, the highest extraction yield was obtained in butanol extraction with a rate of 4.8%. The highest level of total phenolic and flavonoid content were obtained in butanol extraction with 45.6 ± 0.9 mg GAE/g TPC and 35.6 ± 0.8 mg QE/g TFC (Table 1). In terms of phenolic content, the solvents followed the order butanol > water > hexane > chloroform, whereas for flavonoid content the order was butanol > water > chloroform > hexane. Several studies have investigated the phytochemistry of R. sativus, particularly with respect to its phenolic and flavonoid constituents. Riaz et al.52 reported total phenolic contents of 640.69 ± 6.411 mg GAE/100 g in the methanol extract and 154.98 ± 1.574 mgGAE/100 g in the hexane extract of R. sativus leaves collected from Faisalabad (Pakistan). In the same study, the highest flavonoid content was determined in the methanol extract (103.56 ± 1.041 mg CE/100 g).

The quantitative phenolic content of R. sativus was determined by LC-MS/MS analysis, and the results are presented in Fig. 1. In the LC-MS/MS analysis of R. sativus samples collected from the Bulancak district of Giresun, 29 standard compounds were analyzed, of which 12 were detected. Blank chromatogram was given in Figure S1. Among the tested standards, several phenolic compounds were detected, including protocatechuic acid, protocatechuic aldehyde, caffeic acid, vanillin, syringic acid, syringic aldehyde, p-coumaric acid, salicylic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, rosmarinic acid, rutin, and quercetin. Rosmarinic acid (136.382 mg phenolic/g) was detected at the highest concentration in the R. sativus extract, followed by p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid, quercetin, protocatechuic aldehyde, salicylic acid, sesamol, and rutin. The phenolic compounds identified in the plant have been reported to contribute significantly to its biological activity. Rosmarinic acid, identified as the major compound in R. sativus, is an ester of caffeic acid and 3,4-dihydroxyphenyllactic acid and is therefore classified as a hydroxycinnamic acid derivative. Rosmarinic acid contains two phenolic groups, each bearing two hydroxyl groups in the ortho position. It has been reported to exhibit protective activity against HIV-1 and anticoagulant effects. It also possesses various biological activities, including antihepatitis, antiviral, and antibacterial properties. Additionally, rosmarinic acid exhibits strong free radical scavenging activity due to the presence of two electroactive catechol groups in its structure53,54. These properties also support its use in potential therapeutic applications. The anti-inflammatory activity of this compound is mainly attributed to its inhibitory effects on lipoxygenase and cyclooxygenase enzymes. It is also known to protect against cancer and is used in the cosmetics industry for its antioxidant properties55. Al-Dhabi et al.56 emphasized that rosmarinic acid attenuates diabetic ischemia/reperfusion injury and protects the brain against ischemia/reperfusion injury by reducing blood-brain barrier disruption. Tavafi et al.57 determined that 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg rosmarinic acid doses ameliorated glomerulosclerosis and leukocyte infiltration in diabetic nephropathy models. Another important phenolic substance identified in R. sativus is p-coumaric acid. p-Coumaric acid has been reported to be a potent antioxidant and an effective scavenger of reactive oxygen species and free radicals58. Furthermore, it has been reported to have antidiabetic, antihyperlipidemic, anticancer, antimicrobial, antiinflammatory, antiulcer, anxiolytic, antipyretic, analgesic, antiarthritic, and neuroprotective effects, as well as effects on preventing platelet aggregation. p-Coumaric acid has also been reported to exhibit protective effects in various experimental ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) models59.

Caffeic acid, detected at a concentration of 23.384 µg/g in R. sativus leaves, is also an important phenolic compound. Also known as 3,4-hydroxycinnamic acid, caffeic acid is widely distributed in the human diet and is present in many food sources. In addition to its positive effects on the immune system, caffeic acid is widely used in the pharmaceutical industry due to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties60. Pharmacological studies have reported that caffeic acid exerts protective effects against damage associated with cerebral ischemia and oxidative stress. This protective effect, together with associated biochemical changes, may also prevent behavioral impairments related to brain damage61. Quercetin (3,3’,4’,5,7-pentahydroxyflavone) is a type of flavonoid widely found in plants and known as a secondary metabolite62. A diet high in quercetin has been reported to be an important factor in reducing the risk of diabetes63. Long-term intake of quercetin has also been shown to protect against oxidative damage to the heart, kidney, and liver associated with diabetes. In addition to the major compounds detected in R. sativus leaves, phenolic substances detected at moderate and low levels also contribute to the formation of biological activity. Protocatechuic aldehyde, detected at 5.263 µg/g, has been identified as a potent aldose reductase inhibitor and has been shown to have potential use in the prevention or treatment of diabetic complications64,65. Protocatechuic aldehyde has been observed to significantly reduce lens opacity induced by cataract in streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice66. Salicylic acid, a phenolic substance detected by LC-MS/MS analysis, plays a fundamental role in the regulation of growth, development, and interaction with other organisms. Accordingly, salicylic acid functions as a plant growth regulator and can trigger a series of metabolic and physiological responses67. Rutin, a flavonoid with the molecular formula C27H30O16, was identified in the phenolic profile of R. sativus. In addition to its antioxidant properties, it has been reported to increase blood vessel elasticity and exert therapeutic effects on atherosclerosis and circulatory disorders. Rutin has also been reported to lower blood pressure and stimulate vitamin C utilization68. Rutin has been used in the treatment of diabetes and hypertension. It also inhibits lipid peroxidation and exhibits neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, anti-carcinogenic, anti-proliferative, and anti-oxidative effects69. These phenolic compounds have been demonstrated to confer a wide range of biological activities on R. sativus, most notably antidiabetic and anti-inflammatory properties. Many studies have reported that numerous natural and plant-based materials contain various bioactive compounds and exhibit significant protective effects due to these constituents70–75.

in-vitro antidiabetic potential of R. sativus

The inhibitory effects of R. sativus extracts on α-amylase and α-glucosidase are shown in Fig. 2. Enzyme inhibition was determined based on IC50 values. The IC50 values for the α-amylase enzyme were found to range from 31.56 to 51.15 µg/mL. The lowest IC50 value for α-amylase was obtained with the chloroform extract, followed by the water, hexane, and butanol, respectively. The IC50 value of acarbose, which was used as a standard inhibitor, against α-amylase was found to be 10.96 µg/mL. The chloroform extract of R. sativus exhibited higher inhibitory activity than the other solvents tested. This difference may be due to the variable solubility of phytochemicals in the plant across different solvents. Although butanol produced the highest extraction yields in this study, chloroform extract exhibited the greatest α-amylase inhibition. Butanol is a more polar solvent than chloroform. Consequently, more polar secondary metabolites, such as phenolics, flavonoids, and saponins, are extracted in greater amounts with butanol, resulting in higher overall extraction yields. Chloroform is a semi-polar solvent and more effective at dissolving various compounds, including terpenes, aglycone flavonoids, some alkaloids and lipophilic phenolics. These compounds may exhibit stronger inhibitory effects on enzymes such as α-amylase and α-glucosidase. Overall, although the butanol extract contains a greater diversity and quantity of compounds, metabolites responsible for enzyme inhibition may be more concentrated in the chloroform fraction. Therefore, a high extraction yield does not necessarily correspond to high biological activity. An important factor is the solvent in which the specific compounds responsible for the inhibitory effect are concentrated76,77. The results of the extraction efficiency and enzyme inhibition tests suggest that, although butanol provides a higher extraction yield, chloroform is a more effective solvent for extracting enzyme-inhibiting metabolites. Previous studies have reported the α-amylase inhibitory activity of R. sativus extracts obtained from different regions and prepared using various solvents. Riaz et al.52 found that the ethyl acetate extract of R. sativus collected in Faisalabad (Pakistan), inhibited α-amylase by 36.29%, whereas acarbose inhibited it by 62.5%. The same study reported that the inhibition degrees of the butanol and methanol extracts were similar, with both extracts inhibiting α-amylase by 35.36%. The IC50 value of R. sativus extracts for α-glucosidase was calculated to be in the range from 28.4 to 49.65 µg/mL. The hexane extract exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity against α-glucosidase. The hexane extract of R. sativus exhibited 1.15-fold higher inhibitory activity compared to the water extract, which exhibited the closest activity. The IC50 value of acarbose, used as the standard inhibitor, against α-glucosidase was found to be 13.76 µg/mL. Several studies have reported the inhibitory activity of R. sativus collected from different regions against the α-glucosidase enzyme. Ramachandran et al.78 reported that the chloroform extract of R. sativus collected from Doddabetta (Nilgiris) had an IC50 value of 39.65 µg/mL against the α-glucosidase enzyme.

Enzyme-ligand docking studies for antidiabetic activity

in-vitro enzyme inhibition studies were also supported by in-silico models and validated using molecular docking analysis. The biological activities exhibited by plant extracts originate from the phytochemicals present in the plant matrix. Accordingly, major phenolic compounds identified by LC-MS/MS analysis were selected as ligands, and their interactions with the α-amylase enzyme were investigated (Fig. 3; Table 2). Caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, and rosmarinic acid, selected as ligands, interact with α-amylase through different amino acids. These interactions occur via hydrogen bonds, pi-anion, pi-pi stacked, pi-alkyl and unfavorable donor-donor. The binding energies calculated for the interactions of acarbose, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, and rosmarinic acid with α-amylase were − 12.98, −4.90, −4.62, −6.02 kcal/mol respectively. Several studies have reported the in-silico interaction of phenolic compounds with α-amylase. Huang et al.79 found that α-amylase and p-coumaric acid interact and that a binding energy in the range of −6.30 to −4.50 kcal/mol is formed. Tshiyoyo et al.80 reported that rosmarinic acid interacts with human pancreatic α-amylase enzyme obtained from Saccharomyces cerevisiae with a binding energy of −6.0 kcal/mol. Lei et al.81 reported binding energies of − 7.8 kcal/mol for acarbose, a well-known standard inhibitor, in molecular docking studies with α-amylase. The α-glucosidase inhibitory activity of R. sativus determined in-vitro was also supported by molecular docking, and the interaction of the ligands with α-glucosidase and the inhibition data are presented in Fig. 4; Table 2. Binding energies calculated for the interactions of acarbose, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, and rosmarinic acid with α-glucosidase were − 11.87, −3.19, −3.25, −4.78 kcal/mol respectively. Although the binding energy values calculated for both enzymes indicate moderate inhibition potential compared to the standard inhibitor acarbose, it is important to consider that partial inhibition of carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes may be therapeutically advantageous. Unlike complete inhibition, which can cause gastrointestinal side effects such as bloating or diarrhea, partial inhibition can help modulate postprandial glucose levels in a balanced manner while minimizing side effects. Therefore, the observed binding interactions of rosmarinic acid, caffeic acid, and p-coumaric acid highlight their potential as complementary modulators of α-amylase and α-glucosidase activity rather than complete inhibitors. The binding of these phenolic compounds to α-amylase and α-glucosidase with different interaction kinetics also confirms the enzyme inhibition detected in-vitro. These interactions affect enzyme activity by causing changes in the enzyme structure, active site, and modification regions. Such structural changes are reflected in enzyme function, leading to disruptions and delays in carbohydrate digestion and, consequently, contributing to the emergence of anti-hyperglycemic activity.

Anti-inflammatory potential

Protein denaturation inhibition assays are becoming a useful in-vitro technique for finding possible anti-inflammatory drugs without involving live subjects. Protein denaturation is a critical stage in the inflammatory process associated with the development of arthritic and inflammatory disorders and is closely linked to autoantigen production. As a potential substitute for synthetic anti-inflammatory drugs, the anti-inflammatory properties of plant extracts are currently being closely examined. Herbal extracts provide a biologically diverse range of easily accessible and safe sources, making them an attractive alternative to traditional medications, which have drawbacks in terms of toxicity and unfavorable side effects41. Albumin denaturation inhibition results of four different R. sativus extracts are given in Fig. 5. The most pronounced inhibition was observed for the chloroform extract, which exhibited 72.6% inhibition at a concentration of 100 µg/mL. At the same concentration, the denaturation inhibition rates of the butanol, hexane, and water extracts were calculated as 62.3%, 61.7%, and 60.23%, respectively. The order of effectiveness in terms of IC50 values was as follows: chloroform > butanol > hexane > water. The IC50 values were calculated as 45.16, 53.33, 56.3, and 58.95 µg/mL, respectively. The chloroform extract exhibited the lowest IC₅₀ value, indicating a more pronounced inhibitory effect compared to the other extracts. Aspirin, used as a positive control, showed 100% inhibition at 580 µg/mL, with an IC₅₀ value of 220 µg/mL. A lower IC50 value indicates higher activity, whereas a higher IC50 value indicates a higher concentration required to achieve the desired inhibition, meaning decreased activity. Basini et al.82 reported that hydrogels containing R. sativus extract significantly reduced albumin protein denaturation and that this activity was related to the polyphenols and terpenoids contained in the plant. The protein denaturation inhibition activity of plant extracts, defined as their ability to prevent structural protein degradation, is generally associated with their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. This activity refers to their ability to prevent proteins from denaturing under conditions of elevated temperature, pH changes, or chemical stress. Plant extracts frequently contain polyphenols, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, tannins, and lignans. These compounds have been shown to assist in maintaining the three-dimensional structure of proteins by forming hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, or π–π interactions with proteins. This prevents the exposure of hydrophobic regions during denaturation. A considerable number of phenolic compounds are recognized for their ability to stabilize proteins under thermal or chemical stress by binding to the protein surface83,84. Precupas et al.85 reported that when caffeic acid binds to the albumin surface, it acts like a “sheath” and contributes to the arrangement of water molecules, the preservation of hydration shells, and the reduction of protein folding energy, thus increasing the stability of the protein structure. The same study showed that the interaction of caffeic acid with albumin improves the thermal stability of the protein. Specifically, the binding of caffeic acid can prevent protein denaturation at elevated temperatures. The protein denaturation inhibition assay employed in this study does not represent a direct measure of anti-inflammatory activity. It provides an indirect and supportive assessment. Protein denaturation is a next-stage event associated with inflammatory states, particularly through the formation of autoantigens and structural protein damage under stressful conditions. Therefore, the ability of a compound or extract to prevent protein denaturation reflects its potential to stabilize protein structures and mitigate inflammation-related biochemical changes. This assay is a commonly used preliminary screening tool to support anti-inflammatory potential; however, it does not directly assess classical inflammatory pathways such as cytokine release, cyclooxygenase inhibition, or nitric oxide production.

To confirm protein denaturation inhibitory activity, the spectroscopic interactions of the main compounds, rosmarinic acid and caffeic acid, were investigated, and the results are shown in Fig. 5. It was determined that as the amount of rosmarinic acid increased (1:1→1:4), albumin absorbance decreased at across wavelengths. This finding indicates that the conformations of the chromophore regions of albumin (especially aromatic amino acids: tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine) were altered as a result of protein-ligand interactions. While the absorbance of albumin at 280 nm was 1.50, the absorbance values decreased to 1.11–1.54-fold upon the addition of rosmarinic acid. This band is typical of the absorption region of tyrosine and tryptophan residues in proteins. The decrease in absorbance, referred to as the hypochromic effect, suggests that rosmarinic acid forms interactions with these aromatic regions, resulting in stabilization of the protein conformation. As with rosmarinic acid, absorbance values ​​decreased at all wavelengths as the amount of caffeic acid increased (1:1 → 1:4). However, this decrease was more moderate and gradual than with rosmarinic acid. The decreases observed at 280 nm indicate that caffeic acid interacts with the tyrosine and tryptophan sites of albumin, but binds more weakly or forms a more superficial interaction compared to rosmarinic acid. Both rosmarinic acid and caffeic acid produced a dose-dependent hypochromic effect with albumin. This suggests that ligands interact strongly with the aromatic chromophore regions of albumin, stabilize the protein conformation, and reduces the optical density in solution. It was observed that increasing ligand concentration led to a gradual tightening of the conformation of albumin occurs, resulting in the protein becoming more resistant to denaturation. Spectral shift analyses performed with UV-Vis spectroscopy show the interaction of major phenolic compounds in the plant extract with albumin, and the stabilization of protein conformation as a result of this interaction. These interactions may constitute the possible molecular mechanism underlying the inhibitory effect of the extract on protein denaturation. The resulting hypochromic effect suggests that ligand-protein binding may limit protein denaturation and that this may be related to the anti-inflammatory effect. However, these analyses do not directly measure an anti-inflammatory response; they are merely supportive at the mechanistic level.

Similarly, Radibratović et al.86 demonstrated that the binding of the ligand phycocyanobilin to albumin stabilizes the protein structure by reducing conformational flexibility and increasing resistance to thermal and proteolytic denaturation. Jahanban-Esfahlan et al.87 reported that caffeic acid interacts with albumin and that non-covalent bonds involved in the interaction stabilize the resulting complex structure.

Anti-hemolytic activity

The protective activity of the extract against induced hemolysis in red blood cells is presented in Table 3. The AAPH-induced hemolysis model is a widely utilized method for the assessment of the antioxidant and anti-hemolytic potential of plant extracts in erythrocytes. AAPH generates a low but constant flux of peroxyl radicals, which in turn cause oxidation of lipids and proteins in the erythrocyte membrane. The process of free radicals disrupting erythrocyte membrane integrity is known as hemolysis. In terms of antihemolytic activity, the order of effectiveness of the extracts was chloroform > butanol > hexane > water. A dose-dependent increase was observed in all fractions, suggesting that the antioxidant compounds in the extracts exert a concentration-dependent effect. The most pronounced activity was obtained with the chloroform extract, which showed the highest effect at all doses.

The chloroform extract at 100 µg/mL was the most protective fraction, with a protection rate of 69.35 ± 5.67%. This result may be associated with the strong protection provided by the semi-polar lipophilic compounds in the chloroform fraction. The lowest protection rates were observed in the water fraction (39.87% at 100 µg/mL). This result suggests that phenolic compounds with high antioxidant capacity are less concentrated in the water fraction. At 100 µg/mL, the chloroform and butanol fractions provided higher protection than ascorbic acid. This finding supports the potential of R. sativus extracts as effective natural sources of antioxidants. The protection exhibited by R. sativus extracts against AAPH-induced hemolysis may be attributed to its antioxidant activity. Phenolics are compounds that act as hydrogen atom donors. The antioxidant effects of plant compounds and the protection they provide against various toxicities are widely documented in the literature88–98. Through their antioxidant activity, herbal extracts stabilize free radicals, thereby increasing the resistance of erythrocytes to oxidative stress. Furthermore, phenolic compounds present in the extracts can donate one or more electrons to neutralize AAPH radicals, thereby preventing hemolysis. Moreover, polyphenols can interact with the hydrophilic portion of the lipid membrane, causing changes in the configuration of lipid polar head groups. To date, no studies have reported the in vitro anti-hemolytic activity of R. sativus extracts. However, several studies have clearly demonstrated their antioxidant and radical-scavenging activities. Beevi et al.99 reported that extracts of R. sativus exhibit concentration-dependent radical scavenging, with DPPH scavenging activity, in particular, being higher than that of standard antioxidants. Similarly, Reddy et al.100 reported that ethanol, methanol, and water extracts of R. sativus exhibited dose-dependent DPPH radical scavenging, with the ethanol extract exhibiting stronger activity. They attributed this result to the higher yields obtained in ethanol extraction and the higher phenolic content.

The interactions of major compounds identified in R. sativus with erythrocyte membrane proteins band 3 and glycophorin A were investigated by molecular docking, and the results are presented in Table 4; Figs. 6 and 7. These interactions were used to support the anti-hemolytic activity of R. sativus and to evaluate possible molecular mechanisms. In addition to caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, and rosmarinic acid, which are major components of R. sativus, the interactions with ascorbic acid, a powerful antioxidant, were also examined as a control. Rosmarinic acid exhibited the strongest binding affinity toward Band 3, with a binding energy approximately 2.5 times greater than that of ascorbic acid. Furthermore, the other studied molecules exhibited stronger binding profiles than the control. The cluster RMSD values of all studied molecules were consistent and within acceptable ranges. Ascorbic acid formed only hydrogen bonds with the Band 3 protein, whereas rosmarinic acid formed not only hydrogen bonds but also π-alkyl and π–π T-shaped interactions through its aromatic rings. The binding profiles of caffeic acid and p-coumaric acid were almost identical, forming hydrogen bonds with the amino acid residues Pro419, Phe532, and Lys539. The interaction of the studied molecules with the Band 3 protein may enhance its resistance to lipid peroxidation and mechanical stress, thereby preventing hemolysis. All three selected ligands bound to Glycophorin A with more negative binding energies than ascorbic acid. The most negative binding energy was observed for rosmarinic acid. Rosmaniric acid interacted with both the α- and β-chains of Glycophorin A. This interaction may contribute to the preservation of the dimeric structure of Glycophorin A. Caffeic acid and p-coumaric acid formed hydrogen bonds with amino acid residues of the polypeptide chain. These interactions, particularly those involving hydrophobic amino acid residues, suggest that the compounds may support membrane structure by anchoring to the lipid bilayer. Hydrogen bonds enhance structural integrity and provide conformational stability in the protein structure. As a result of these interactions, the compounds may bind to membrane proteins, strengthen membrane-protein interactions, increase the flexibility and durability of the erythrocyte membrane, and contribute to the preservation of membrane integrity, particularly under oxidative stress conditions. These interactions may provide a mechanistic explanation for the anti-hemolytic activity of R. sativus extracts. In the literature, molecular docking is used to confirm and support many biological activities tested in vitro and in vivo. This approach allows the elucidation of the interaction mechanisms of bioactive compounds with target proteins and the interpretation of experimental findings at the molecular level101–107.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the phytochemical composition and multifunctional biological activities of Raphanus sativus L. (radish) leaves collected from the Giresun-Bulancak region of Türkiye. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed rosmarinic acid, p-coumaric acid, and caffeic acid as the major phenolic compounds, supported by substantial total phenolic and flavonoid contents. The extracts demonstrated significant in-vitro antidiabetic potential through inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes, anti-inflammatory effects via protein denaturation inhibition, and protective activity against AAPH-induced hemolysis. These findings confirm that R. sativus leaves, often undervalued compared to the roots, are a rich source of bioactive compounds with potent pharmacological potential. The biological effects observed in this study were evaluated using in vitro assays supported by in silico molecular docking analyses. The multidisciplinary design of the study-integrating phytochemical profiling, in vitro experiments, and molecular docking-fills a significant gap in the literature, as previous research has largely focused on the roots of R. sativus. However, further studies are needed to validate R. sativus leaf extracts in experimental models, assess toxicity and bioavailability, and elucidate the mechanisms of action at the cellular and molecular levels in more detail.
