Ultrasound-Assisted Alkaline Hydrogen Peroxide Pretreatment for Biopolymer Recovery from Exhausted Purple Corn Cobs
Authors: Evangelina Pasca Supanta, Lizbet León Félix, David G. Pacheco-Salazar, Eduart Gutiérrez-Pineda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the fractionation of exhausted purple corn cobs (EPCC) (a lignocellulosic byproduct remaining after bioactive compound extraction) through alkaline hydrogen peroxide (AHP) pretreatment, with and without ultrasonic assistance. Initial biomass characterization revealed a composition of 33.08% cellulose, 21.19% hemicellulose, and only 2.69% lignin, confirming its suitability as a biopolymer source. AHP pretreatment was performed under mild conditions (10% H2O2, pH 11.5), with ultrasound applied for 0, 1, 5, and 10 min. Mass balance analysis showed that ultrasonic assistance significantly enhanced the removal of hemicellulose and lignin. After 10 min of treatment, delignification reached 97.4%, hemicellulose removal 58.7%, and cellulose recovery 41.3%. Biopolymers were subsequently recovered from both solid and liquid fractions. Infrared spectroscopy and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) indicated progressive fractioning of EPCC. Morphology analysis revealed increased fiber compaction with longer ultrasound exposure. X-ray diffraction confirmed that cellulose crystallinity increased from 64.6% to 76.6% with ultrasound exposure, and thermal analysis showed enhanced thermal stability, with degradation occurring at 345 °C. These findings highlight the effectiveness of ultrasound-assisted AHP pretreatment for the selective extraction of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin from EPCC, supporting its valorization as a feedstock for biobased materials in circular bioeconomy frameworks. Moreover, the use of AHP under mild conditions aligns with green chemistry principles, offering a sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional biomass pretreatments.

Introduction

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for 2030 highlighted lignocellulosic biomass as a key feedstock for the production of renewable and environmentally friendly commodities and biomaterials. , These materials aim to reduce dependency on fossil-based resources and minimize the ecological footprint of synthetic production processes. Among the most promising derivatives, cellulose-derived materials (CDMs) stand out for their biocompatibility, low cytotoxicity, biodegradability, water retention capacity, hydrophilicity, and customizable functional properties. This versatility has driven increasing interest in diverse applications, including drug delivery, carbon capture, colored paper production, and 3D printing. ,

Peruvian purple corn (Zea mays L.), a hallmark Andean crop, is highly valued for its rich anthocyanin content, which confers antioxidant and health-promoting properties. In response to increasing demand for nutraceuticals and natural dyes, its cultivation has grown substantially, with national production surpassing two million tons in 2023. − Regions such as Arequipa have played a crucial role in driving this expansion. However, the industrial processing of purple corn, particularly for anthocyanin extraction for use in functional beverages and food products, generates large quantities of lignocellulosic residue, primarily in the form of cobs. Exhausted purple corn cobs (EPCCs) (the residue left after bioactive-compound extraction) make up roughly 15–20% of the corn ear’s weight, yet they are commonly discarded despite their rich lignocellulosic content. The high carbohydrate content and low lignin content make them a promising source for the extraction of sustainable biopolymers, further enhanced by residual phenolics and minor starch fractions.

In this context, incorporating EPCCs into biorefinery represents a strategic opportunity to advance Peru’s circular bioeconomy. Realizing this potential depends on sustainable scalable pretreatments that disassemble biomass while preserving its biopolymers. A range of physical, chemical, biological, and hybrid methods have been explored to improve biomass accessibility by removing lignin, reducing cellulose crystallinity, and breaking down lignin–carbohydrate complexes. Among these methods are dilute acid hydrolysis, alkaline treatment, metal-assisted and enzymatic delignification, laccase oxidation, surfactant-enhanced hydrolysis, and microwave and ultrasound-assisted techniques −

Alkaline hydrogen peroxide (AHP) pretreatment is a versatile and environmentally friendly method that utilizes hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in an alkaline medium, typically sodium hydroxide, under mild conditions. It selectively lignin and hemicellulose while preserving cellulose, which is crucial for processes such as enzymatic hydrolysis or nanocellulose production. − Under alkaline conditions, H2O2 generates reactive species such as superoxide (O2 –) and hydroxyl radicals (HO–), which cleave ether and ester linkages in lignin, promoting its solubilization while preserving carbohydrate integrity. Additionally, sodium hydroxide enhances fiber accessibility without causing significant degradation of polysaccharides. The mild and selective nature of AHP makes it a green, promising pretreatment. Unlike harsher chemical methods, it operates at moderate temperatures and low pressure, minimizing the formation of fermentation inhibitors such as furfural, HMF, and phenolics. Its benign byproducts (water, oxygen, and salts) further support its use in sustainable biorefineries. , AHP has shown high lignin removal and cellulose retention across various biomasses, including sugar cane bagasse, corn stover, bamboo, ginger stalks, and walnut shells.

Despite its advantages, AHP can be further enhanced through physical intensification. Among these, ultrasonic-assisted pretreatment has shown strong potential due to acoustic cavitation, which disrupts biomass by forming and collapsing microbubbles under ultrasonic frequencies (16 kHz to 1 MHz). This process creates localized zones of high temperatures (up to 15,000 K) and pressures (>5000 atm), producing shear forces, shockwaves, and intense microscale turbulence that break down lignocellulosic structures and improve mass transfer. When combined with AHP, ultrasound enhances lignin solubilization, hemicellulose extraction, and cellulose accessibility. Cavitation also accelerates H2O2 decomposition into hydroxyl radicals, promoting deeper lignin oxidation. , Reported benefits include delignification of up to 96%, hemicellulose removal of over 75% in organosolv systems, and a 15% increase in hemicellulose yield from buckwheat husks. Ultrasound has also improved lignin solubilization in systems using dilute acids, deep eutectic solvents, and ionic liquids, reaching up to 48.15% removal and enhancing cellulose purity.

Under mild alkaline hydrogen peroxide (AHP) conditions (≤60 °C, pH ≈ 11–12, 1 atm), our process achieves ∼97.4% delignification and ∼58.7% hemicellulose removal from anthocyanin-spent exhausted purple corn cobs (EPCCs). These values lie within the upper range reported for alkaline-peroxide pretreatments on lignocellulosics. − Although ultrasound has been explored with other chemistries (e.g., organosolv, acids, ionic liquids), the dose-dependent effect of short ultrasound exposures inside an AHP system (and its direct linkage to product specifications) has not been systematically quantified, particularly for low-lignin EPCCs. To avoid overgeneralization, we center our mechanistic discussion on AHP (alkaline activation of H2O2 and radical-mediated cleavage) rather than extrapolating from non-AHP systems.

Accordingly, this work makes four specific contributions: (i) it delivers the first ultrasound dose–response study (0–10 min) within AHP applied to EPCCs, a rarely studied, low-lignin feedstock; (ii) it quantitatively connects fractionation selectivity (97.4% lignin removal; 58.7% hemicellulose removal) to product quality (cellulose Iβ retention, CrI increase from 64.6% to 76.6%, DTG_max ≈ 335–345 °C, and morphology) via an integrated workflow (mass balance, FTIR, PCA, XRD, TGA, SEM); (iii) it shows that these outcomes arise under environmentally benign conditions intrinsic to AHP, while clarifying the ultrasound intensification window within AHP rather than claiming cross-chemistry generality; and (iv) it identifies a practical yield–selectivity trade-off in which short ultrasound doses (≈1–5 min) preserve cellulose recovery while accelerating lignin/hemicellulose removal, whereas 10 min maximizes purity and crystallinity at the expense of yield. This study aims to develop a green, ultrasound-assisted AHP process to selectively separate exhausted purple corn cobs (EPCCs) into cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin streams, and to measure how short ultrasound treatments impact both mass yields and material properties (CrI, thermal stability, morphology). In doing so, we demonstrate a proof-of-concept for cascade valorisation of EPCCs that supports green chemistry and circular bioeconomy goals.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The raw material was purple corn cobs (PCCs) that were collected from a local market (Arequipa, Perú). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) pellets, 98% sulfuric acid (H2SO4) solution (97% v/v), and hydrochloric acid (HCl) (37%) were purchased from Merck. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution, 30%, was purchased from Delta Química SRL. Ethanol absolute (CH3CH2OH) 99.7% was purchased from J. T. Baker. Citric acid anhydrous (HOC­(COOH)­(CH2COOH)) was purchased from Scharlau. In all cases, aqueous solutions were prepared using deionized water with a resistance < 18.2 MΩ cm2 (25 °C).

Raw Material and Initial Characterization

Exhausted purple corn cobs (EPCC) with 12.6% moisture were dry-cleaned, milled using a Grondoy GR-PV40B grinder, and sieved to obtain particle sizes between 0.425 mm and 0.250 mm (#40–#60 mesh). The compositional analysis was performed in triplicate following the TAPPI standard protocols: T 264 (sample preparation), T 257 (moisture), T 211 (ash), T 204 (solvent extractives), T 429 cm-23 (α-cellulose on paper), and T 222 (Klason lignin).

Ultrasound-Assisted Alkaline Hydrogen Peroxide Pretreatment

Alkaline hydrogen peroxide (AHP) pretreatment was employed to selectively solubilize hemicellulose and lignin from the amorphous regions of the EPCC lignocellulosic matrix (the byproduct that remains after bioactive-compound extraction), thereby facilitating the recovery of a highly purified cellulose-enriched solid fraction. In a typical experiment, 10 g of oven-dried EPCC were mixed with 200 mL of 10% (w/v) hydrogen peroxide solution, and the pH was adjusted to 11.5 using 4 N NaOH under continuous magnetic stirring. The reaction was carried out in a 2 L beaker for 1 h, during which the exothermic nature of the process led to a spontaneous temperature increase from 19 °C to approximately 58 °C. To enhance the efficiency of the AHP pretreatment, high-intensity ultrasound was applied using a probe (Hielscher UP400 St, 400 W, 24 kHz) operating at a power of 300 W, a frequency of 20.8 kHz, and an amplitude of 70%. Ultrasonication was applied at specific time intervals (0, 1, 5, and 10 min) after 2 h of reaction. The integration of focused ultrasound aimed to intensify cavitation-induced effects, thereby improving reagent diffusion into the lignocellulosic matrix and promoting the cleavage of structural bonds, which enhanced delignification and hemicellulose solubilization compared to conventional methods. After treatment, the mixture was vacuum-filtered to separate the cellulose-rich solid from the liquid hydrolysate. The solid was washed with distilled water to a neutral pH and oven-dried at 45 °C for 24 h. The liquid fraction was reserved for further recovery of hemicelluloses and lignin. Recovery of Hemicellulose and Lignin

The liquid fraction obtained from AHP pretreatment was neutralized to a pH of 6.5–7.0 by dropwise addition of 2 M HCl under continuous stirring. To precipitate hemicelluloses, two volumes of 95% ethanol were added, and the mixture was left to stand overnight. The resulting precipitate was recovered by vacuum filtration, washed with 70% aqueous ethanol, and dried at 50 °C for 36 h.

The remaining filtrate underwent ethanol removal via simple distillation. The resulting residue was then acidified to pH < 2 with 2 M HCl to precipitate acid-insoluble lignin. After 24 h of standing, the mixture was vacuum-filtered, and the lignin precipitate was washed with distilled water (pH 2.0) and dried at 45 °C. A schematic of the recovery process is shown in Figure .

Mass Balance and Characterization of the Solid Fractions

To evaluate the efficiency of AHP pretreatment assisted by focused ultrasound in the selective extraction of cellulose (C) and the recovery of hemicellulose (H) and lignin (L) from the solid and liquid fractions (as illustrated in Figure ), a mass balance was performed based on the dry weight of the obtained fractions. This analysis was carried out at sonication times of 0, 1, 5, and 10 min following 2 h of AHP reaction. All solid fractions were washed and dried as described above. Characterization of Solid Fractions

X-ray Diffraction

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to assess the effect of AHP pretreatment, with and without ultrasound, on the crystallinity of the extracted biopolymers. Measurements were carried out using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 diffractometer, operated at 40 kV and 15 mA with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). Diffractograms were recorded in the 2θ range of 5–60°, with a scan rate of 2°/min and a step size of 0.02°. The crystallinity index (CrI) was calculated using eq , based on the Segal method, , while the Scherrer eq (eq ) was used to estimate the crystallite size. , 3 CrI=I002−IamI002×100 where I am is the minimum intensity corresponding to amorphous portion at 2θ ≈ 18.0 and I 002 is the maximum diffraction intensity of crystalline region at 2θ ≈ 22.3 4 D=kλβcos⁡θ where, D is the crystallite size, k is the Scherrer constant (usually around 0.9), λ is the X-ray wavelength, β is the full width at half-maximum (fwhm) of the diffraction peak, and θ is the Bragg angle.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to identify the chemical modifications and the appearance of new functional groups in the biopolymer surfaces after pretreatment. Spectra were collected using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS10 spectrometer in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode, over the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm–1, with a resolution of 2 cm–1 and 32 scans per sample. PCA was applied to the spectral data sets to elucidate differences and similarities between the fractions obtained under different treatment conditions. Triplicate spectra were collected for each condition. Spectra were baseline-corrected, vector-normalized, mean-centered, and PCA was conducted on the combined 3800–2800 and 1800–850 cm–1 regions (∼1900 variables per spectrum). The data set included 39 spectra (EPCC and cellulose/hemicellulose/lignin at 0, 1, 5, 10 min; n = 3 each).

Scanning Electron Microscopy

SEM analysis was conducted to observe the surface morphology of the EPCC biomass and the solid fractions after AHP pretreatment. Samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold and imaged using a Thermo Scientific Scios 2 DualBeam microscope operated at an accelerating voltage below 15 kV. Images were acquired at magnifications of 1000×, 1500×, and 10,000×.

Thermogravimetric Analysis

Thermal behavior and stability of the EPCCs and extracted biopolymers (C, H, L) were evaluated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). A Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC1 STARe System analyzer was calibrated according to ASTM E1582-00. Samples were heated from 25 to 500 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min heating rate under a nitrogen atmosphere. Mass loss percentages corresponding to dehydration, decomposition, solvent loss, and plasticizer evaporation were recorded.

The EPCC composition on a dry-weight basis (Table ) comprises 33.08% α-cellulose, 21.19% hemicellulose, 2.69% acid-insoluble lignin, 12.16% ethanol-soluble extractives, and 2.59% ash. The accounted fraction (∼71.7%) reflects the measured components; the balance arises from constituents not quantified here (e.g., acid-soluble lignin, proteins, residual starch/pectins/uronic acids). Reported values for corn cobs in the literature span wide ranges owing to cultivar, anatomical fraction, and analytical protocols; our EPCC values fall within the carbohydrate ranges while showing a lower acid-insoluble lignin content, consistent with prior solvent extraction and the anatomical origin of the feedstock. ,

In addition, ash contents were within the expected range for lignocellulosic biomass, further supporting the suitability of PCCs as a viable feedstock for biorefinery applications. Notably, the low lignin content may facilitate downstream processing, such as enzymatic hydrolysis or chemical delignification, for the recovery of other polymers, positioning PCCs as a promising and underutilized raw material for the sustainable production of high-value bioproducts.

Recovery (left) and removal (right) percentages of cellulose (C), hemicellulose (H), and lignin (L) after AHP pretreatment of EPCC biomass, with increasing ultrasound exposure times (0, 1, 5, and 10 min).

Cellulose (C) recovery showed a declining trend with increasing ultrasonic exposure time, decreasing from 65.51% at 0 min to 41.27% at 10 min. This inverse relationship is likely due to progressive delignification and hemicellulose removal of the biomass matrix, which expose cellulose fibers to more intense mechanical and chemical interactions. Despite the reduced mass recovery, the cellulose obtained after prolonged sonication showed greater structural purity and crystallinity, as indicated by the diminished presence of coextracted amorphous components (H and L). This finding suggests that, although the yield is lower, the structural integrity and compositional quality of the recovered cellulose are improved, attributed that may favor downstream applications such as nanocellulose production or enzymatic saccharification. ,

In parallel, hemicellulose (H) showed an increase in recovery (from 23.7% to 41.1%) and a decrease in removal (from 89.1% to 58.73%) in the solid and liquid fractions, respectively, with increasing ultrasound exposure time. This contrasting behavior indicates partial solubilization of hemicellulose and its redistribution between phases. At shorter sonication times (0–1 min), whitish precipitates were observed, suggesting the presence of relatively unmodified hemicellulosic fragments. In contrast, prolonged sonication produced browner residues, indicative of more extensive degradation and possible partial condensation. These results highlight the sensitivity of hemicellulose to ultrasonic cavitation, which promotes its breakdown and solubilization in the alkaline medium.

Complementing these findings, lignin (L) removal remained consistently high in all treatments, exceeding 97%, which confirms the effectiveness of AHP for delignification. However, lignin recovery in the liquid fraction was low (≤2.9%), suggesting either extensive solubilization or degradation into low-molecular-weight compounds not recovered in the precipitate. A noticeable change in the color of recovered lignin, from light brown to dark brown, indicates structural modifications caused by ultrasonic irradiation, likely involving partial oxidation and depolymerization. ,, This loss of structural integrity could be advantageous for valorization routes that benefit from depolymerized or functionalized lignin derivatives. Ultrasound treatment facilitated biomass deconstruction by disrupting hydrogen bonds and increasing the accessibility of the AHP solution within the biomass matrix.

This led to improved fractionation efficiency, evidenced by changes in cellulose crystallinity and high purity of the recovered material. The synergistic effect of ultrasound and AHP significantly promoted the selective and efficient recovery of high-value biopolymers. The observed decrease in recovery with increasing hemicellulose and lignin removal highlights the need to optimize ultrasound intensity and duration. Striking the right balance can maximize biopolymer recovery while preserving their structural integrity for subsequent valorization. The residual gap is attributed to acid-soluble lignin (not quantified here), nonprecipitated hemicellulose oligomers, and routine losses during washing/filtration; optimizing liquor workup and measuring acid-soluble lignin are the subject of ongoing work.

Finally, since EPCCs are low in anthocyanins and have an unusually low Klason lignin content (2.69% dwb), delignification is reported relative to the initial lignin content to better reflect process selectivity. For feedstocks with higher lignin content, the AHP–ultrasound severity (including H2O2 loading, pH, power density, and exposure time) would require retuning; however, the overall behavior observed here (improved lignin and hemicellulose removal under mild conditions with only a moderate effect on cellulose yield) is likely to remain consistent within AHP systems.

Structural Characterization of Biopolymer Fractions Obtained via AHP Pretreatment ATR–FTIR Analysis

The solid fractions obtained from the fractionation of EPCC through ultrasonic-assisted AHP pretreatment were analyzed using ATR–FTIR to characterize the changes in chemical structure at each stage of the process. The FTIR spectra of the cellulose (green line), hemicellulose (blue line), and lignin (brown line) fractions are presented in Figure , highlighting two main regions of interest: the high-wavenumber region (3800–2800 cm–1) and the fingerprint region (1800–850 cm–1). A summary of the typical functional groups and their associated signals is provided in Table S1.

FTIR–ATR spectra of EPCC and the cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin fractions obtained after AHP pretreatment. Spectra recorded at 0, 1, 5, and 10 min of reaction time are presented.

All three extracted fractions exhibited a broad absorption band in the 3000–3750 cm–1 region (dashed line 1), attributed to O–H stretching vibrations. The peak observed around 2899 cm–1 (dashed line 3) corresponds to C–H stretching vibrations, and its slight shift indicates the presence of amorphous cellulose domains. Additionally, a peak at 2836 cm–1 (dashed line 4) is associated with symmetric CH2 stretching in hemicellulose, while for lignin, characteristic bands at 2940 cm–1 (dashed line 2, aliphatic and aromatic C–H stretching) and 2840 cm–1 (dashed line 5, CH stretching of methoxy groups) were observed.

In the fingerprint region, the absence of the 1735 cm–1 band (dashed line 6) (typically associated with acetyl groups in hemicellulose) suggests their cleavage during alkaline pretreatment. A broad absorption around 1650 cm–1 (dashed line 8) was attributed to O–H bending vibrations of water absorbed in cellulose, while a band between 1632 and 1641 cm–1 (dashed line 9) was linked to water interactions with carboxyl groups in hemicellulose and lignin. A strong, broad feature near 1700 cm–1 (dashed line 7) was assigned to the carbonyl stretching of aldehyde groups in lignin. − In addition, aromatic skeletal vibrations were consistently detected at approximately 1600 and 1500 cm–1 (yellow band) across all lignin samples. The medium-intensity peak at 1501 cm–1 was related to C–H deformations in methyl and methylene groups, whereas the band at 1447 cm–1 combined aromatic skeletal vibrations with in-plane C–H deformations. In the cellulose and hemicellulose fractions, a weak shoulder at 1437 cm–1 and a peak at 1376 cm–1 were detected, indicative of residual aromatic skeletal vibrations and methoxyl groups, likely due to minor lignin contamination. Additionally, the absorption band at 1430 cm–1, often referred to as the “crystallinity band”, corresponds to symmetric CH2 bending; a decrease in its intensity suggested a reduction in crystallinity during pretreatment. The peaks at approximately 1427 cm–1 and 1371 cm–1 were also associated with CH2 and CH vibrations in cellulose. −

A small peak at 1317 cm–1 (dashed line 10) was attributed to C–O stretching in syringyl ring units of lignin. The sharp peak at 1158–1160 cm–1 (dashed line 12) corresponded to asymmetrical C–O–C stretching vibrations, characteristic of glycosidic linkages in cellulose and hemicellulose. The spectral region between 1000 and 1150 cm–1 was dominated by strong C–O and C–O–C stretching vibrations, reflecting the glycosidic bonds between sugar units. The intense absorption at 1017 cm–1, attributed to C–O stretching in cellulose, became more pronounced after removing hemicellulose and lignin, indicating cellulose enrichment in the samples. ,,,

Additional lignin-associated bands were observed at 1294 cm–1 and 1241 cm–1 (dashed line 11), corresponding to CO stretching vibrations of syringyl and guaiacyl rings, respectively. The bands at 1097 and 1035 cm–1 were linked to C–O stretching vibrations in secondary and primary alcohols, overlapping with etheric and aromatic C–H vibrations that remained stable despite the pretreatment. Finally, a band at 891 cm–1 was assigned to C–H rocking vibrations in cellulose, while the presence of a peak around 898 cm–1 was attributed to β-glycosidic linkages between sugar units (green band). A small peak at 822 cm–1 (dashed line 13), was associated with C–H stretching vibrations in lignin. ,,, Beyond phase identification, ATR–FTIR was used as a process-selectivity readout to monitor the dose–response of ultrasound-assisted AHP. As sonication time increased, spectra showed (i) a gradual reduction in lignin signatures (aromatic CC/C–H at ∼1595–1600 and ∼1500 cm–1; G/S C–O at 1260–1230 cm–1), (ii) the disappearance of the 1735 cm–1 carbonyl band (acetyl/uronic esters) indicating alkaline deacetylation of hemicellulose, and (iii) the increase of polysaccharide bands (C–O/C–O–C at ∼1156 and 1050–1030 cm–1; β-1,4 at ∼896 cm–1; CH2 bending around 1430–1437 cm–1). These patterns illustrate how lignin and acetylated hemicellulose are selectively removed under mild conditions, while cellulose becomes enriched.

XRD Analysis of Fractions

Figure shows the XRD patterns used to evaluate the crystalline structure of the cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin fractions obtained through AHP pretreatment under varying ultrasound times (0, 1, 5, and 10 min). XRD analysis of the cellulose fraction indicates that ultrasound-assisted AHP does not enlarge crystallites but enhances crystalline order. The crystallinity index (Segal) increases from 64.6 ± 0.7% (0 min) to 76.6 ± 0.7% (10 min), while the Scherrer crystallite size remains essentially unchanged (D002 ≈ 2.6–2.7 nm; k = 0.94, Cu Kα) (Table ). − This increase in CrI is therefore attributed to the selective removal of amorphous hemicellulose/lignin and mild hornification or reorientation of the glucan phase, rather than to crystallite growth. The cellulose Iβ reflections at 2θ ≈ 16.2°, 22.4°, and 34.9° sharpen with sonication, consistent with FTIR–PCA evidence of delignification/deacetylation and polysaccharide enrichment. , This enhancement in crystallinity is likely driven by ultrasonic-induced fibrillation and partial depolymerization of cellulose fibers. Additionally, the localized temperature rise (∼60 °C) generated by acoustic cavitation may promote the targeted degradation of amorphous glucose polymers, thereby enhancing cellulose structural integrity without compromising its molecular backbone.

In the hemicellulose fraction, weak diffraction signals that appear at longer sonication times (e.g., 2θ ≈ 11–13°, 19–23°, 25–32°) are interpreted as drying-induced ordering (pseudocrystallinity) or hornification of hydrated arabinoxylans, rather than true crystallization of highly substituted xylans. We explicitly avoid overinterpreting these signals and describe them as ordering during isolation or drying, in line with reports in existing literature on branched hemicelluloses. , This interpretation aligns with FTIR deacetylation markers (loss of ∼ 1735 cm–1) and with the known tendency of alkali-treated polysaccharides to hornify upon drying, without implying formation of stable, native xylan crystals.

Finally, the lignin fractions exhibited XRD patterns characteristic of amorphous materials, with a broad peak centered at 2θ ≈ 22.1° across all treatments, consistent with the inherently disordered structure of lignin. However, a gradual increase in both the intensity and width of this peak was observed with prolonged exposure to ultrasound. This trend may reflect partial structural reorganization and enhanced homogeneity of the lignin, likely resulting from fragmentation and redistribution processes induced by acoustic cavitation. Furthermore, the progressive sharpening of the signal could suggest an increase in the purification of the lignin fraction, as residual hemicellulose and cellulose are effectively removed during AHP-ultrasound pretreatment.

Thermal Behavior

TGA Curve Analysis

The thermal behavior of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin fractions obtained from 10 min of ultrasonic-assisted AHP pretreatment of EPCC was analyzed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA and DTG), as shown in Figure . Each fraction displayed a distinct thermal degradation profile, reflecting differences in chemical composition, structural organization, and purity. In the TGA curves (Figure a), all samples exhibited an initial mass loss below 120 °C, attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed moisture and low-molecular-weight compounds volatiles. , This loss was minimal, amounting to approximately 0.7% for cellulose, 1.6% for hemicellulose, and 0.45% for lignin, indicating low residual moisture content in the extracted fractions. Thermal analysis shows purification and increases molecular order (Figure b). Cellulose exhibits a narrower central depolymerization peak with T max around 335–345 °C after ultrasound treatment, indicating a purer and more ordered glucan matrix consistent with the rise in CrI. , Hemicellulose demonstrates multistage mass loss between approximately 218–329 °C, typical of branched arabinoxylans, , while lignin degrades across a broad temperature range of 200–450 °C due to its heterogeneous aromatic structure. The small initial mass losses (<2%) across fractions are caused by physisorbed water and volatile compounds. Overall, the sharper DTG peaks for cellulose and the expected profiles of hemicellulose and lignin align with the selective removal of amorphous phases by mild ultrasound-AHP.

Despite the lower cellulose mass at 10 min ultrasound, XRD (Cellulose Iβ; CrI 64.6 → 76.6%, crystallite size ∼ 2.6–2.7 nm), FTIR, and TGA/DTG (DTGmax ∼ 335–345 °C) all indicate a preserved and more ordered glucan backbone. The yield decrease is attributed to selective removal of amorphous cellulose, cavitation-induced fines loss, and alkaline “peeling” in AHP, rather than wholesale degradation of the crystalline cellulose. Together, these results develop an AHP-specific ultrasound intensification map that quantitatively connects removal/recovery metrics to structural improvements (CrI, DTGmax, SEM), moving beyond phase identification to aid in process selection. Conclusions

We provide an AHP-specific, ultrasound-enhanced pretreatment plan for EPCCs that measures the dose–response over 0–10 min and directly connects fractionation selectivity to product quality metrics. Compared with conventional AHP (0 min sonication), 10 min of ultrasonic exposure increased delignification to 97.4% and hemicellulose solubilization to 58.7%, while cellulose recovery declined from 45% to 41.3% highlighting the trade-off between yield and purity. FTIR-PCA confirmed progressive removal of lignin and hemicellulose, whereas XRD showed the cellulose crystallinity index rising from 64.6% to 76.6% and hemicellulose developing partial xylan-type order. SEM micrographs revealed fiber compaction and lignin fragmentation after sonication, corroborating enhanced cell wall disruption. The thermogravimetric analysis demonstrated improved thermal stability, with cellulose exhibiting a main DTG peak at 345 °C and narrower degradation profiles compared to raw biomass. Hemicellulose and lignin decomposed over broader ranges, consistent with their amorphous nature. Overall, the combined mechanical (acoustic cavitation) and chemical (radical-mediated oxidation) actions of ultrasonic-AHP pretreatment selectively fractionated EPCC into high-purity, thermally resilient biopolymers suitable for advanced biocomposite and material applications. Operationally, short ultrasound doses (≈1–5 min) provide a favorable yield–selectivity balance, whereas more prolonged exposure (10 min) enhances purity and crystallinity but reduces cellulose recovery. This clear description of ultrasound’s operating window within AHPmild conditions, quantified trade-offs, and specification-grade cellulose represents the main novelty and practical value of the study. Future work should refine ultrasound power and residence time to maximize cellulose yield without compromising crystallinity, integrate solvent and peroxide recycling to lower the process footprint, and evaluate techno-economic and life-cycle metrics to guide industrial adoption.
