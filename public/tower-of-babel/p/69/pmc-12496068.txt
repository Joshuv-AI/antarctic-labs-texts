The power we have to constitute our social reality is exceptional. This power is not simply imposition of one’s will over another, but, as Ian Hacking highlights, drawing on the work of Michel Foucault, it is ‘we’ “who participate in anonymous, unowned arrangements we called power … ‘power through which we constitute ourselves as subjects acting on others’”,1 towards others, and with others.2 Power is often hard to locate and ranges from the subtle to the overt. It is enmeshed in the institutions of society, the practices of individuals, the histories we are told, and the communities we create. Power is everywhere. The positionality statement is one small, but deliberate way to name, explore and redress hierarchies and forms of power in research and other processes. The positionality statement can serve as a platform to make visible who we are as individuals and to critically reflect on how we engage with each other as human beings in complex societies. It is to offer one’s position and describe one’s process in service of more equitable and just relationships and arrangements. The aim of this paper is to draw attention to how the positionality statement can serve as a platform to present a reflexive description of research processes that are informed by principles aimed at fostering equity and justice.

Positionality statements are conceptualised in different ways but are generally tied to ideas about (in)justice, privilege, social change and social experience. Such statements are meant to make explicit ‘the position a research has chosen to adopt within a given research study’3 and the ‘accounting of one’s privilege’.4 Positionality statements often take the form of presenting identity categories along lines of sexuality, gender, race, class, ethnicity and geographic location. This identification is meant to provide the reader with insight into the relationship that the researcher has with the people and topic being researched. For example, when an author notes that they share the same gender category as the participants involved in their study, it can indicate to the reader that there was shared understanding, ‘insider’ status, or interpretative alignment between researcher and participants. One way to view such identifications is as a way to attend to research(er) subjectivity. Another way is to see such a statement as a way to ‘increase transparency, raise awareness of inclusion and diversity in academia’.5 The positionality statement is at least partly justified by the idea that prejudice, inequity and injustice are often perpetuated by silent assumptions. Assumptions like, “I know more than the participants in my study”, or “this community needs my help” or “this country is struggling because they don’t have resources”. There are innumerable assumptions that shape how a researcher engages with team members, participants, institutions, students or communities. Positionality statements certainly represent a step towards bringing the author and their assumptions, perspectives or goals into conversation with their social world, including those who read their work.

In addition to statements of researcher identity, positionality statements can contribute to reflexive academic practice by articulating equity- and justice-informed process. What I suggest is that reflexive statements of principle-informed process can serve as extensions of category-based identity statements. This suggestion aligns with scholarship that highlights the important potential that positionality, often called ‘reflexivity’, statements have for articulating the research process along lines of equity, inclusion and justice. In the context of decolonial projects, Zembylas makes the point that ‘positionality statements can transcend the static, checklist-like format they often take, and instead become dynamic reflections of a scholar’s commitment to addressing coloniality and its enduring legacies’.6 This aim also addresses much of the criticism that positionality statements are too limited in what they convey.7 For example, points that suggest that “reducing bias … does not hinge on the biographical details of individual scholars but on the integrity of the collective process of truth-seeking”.8 To extend the value of positionality statements in advancing equity-based processes, it is important to attend to actions taken. As Olmos-Vega et al note, “reflexivity is an ongoing process that extends across the entire duration of a research endeavor”.9 To expand the utility of the positionality statement, it is important to describe the process of the research, including but not limited to the challenges faced, the decisions and adjustments made within the research team, the steps taken towards inclusion and participation in the research process (see box 1). A positionality/reflexivity statement that integrates positionality with a description of reflexive processes can provide rich insights into how researchers navigate equity, inclusion, justice and the application of other principles in the research process. A reflexive statement of principle-informed process encourages reflexivity in a team during the research process and to present a description of this process in the final outputs. This type of description of the research process can provide a catalogue of learnings for scholars working to pursue equity and justice in their own research processes. The justification for reflexive processes often focuses on ‘strengthen(ing) research quality’10 or attending to ‘how a scientist’s subjectivity can affect upcoming findings’.11 While the scholarly output is important, equally or perhaps more important at this moment in our history is making explicit the commitment to principles associated with equity and justice. To develop scholarly capacity within an equity-oriented and justice-oriented framework means that more of humanity is developing their capacity for inquiry.12 Inclusion, genuine reciprocal partnership and epistemic diversity are all preconditions for sound global scholarship. It is not simply an instrumental step towards ‘good research’ but a practice of ‘world-making’, constituting a more equitable and just social reality.

The importance of describing action, adaptation, reaction, response and ultimately process lies in the ability to capture the dynamic nature of enacting principles. A reflexive statement of principle-informed process can begin by identifying the principles that do or should inform the research process and then describing how these principles were applied in the process. For example, the discourse on global health equity and decolonisation has helped the field identify principles that inform global health justice like equitable participation and knowledge sharing, equitable distribution of resources for global health research, distribution of power within a team of partners that hold different levels of historical advantage in the space, recognition and value attributed to different ways of knowing and doing and the avoidance of hegemonic practices.13 14 Being conscious and then explicit about the principles that inform thoughts and actions and the explicit description of these principle-informed processes can facilitate the practice of documenting and being deliberate about engaging with principles at each stage of the research process. These descriptions can be viewed as experiments in linking aspiration (eg, I want to develop a project with community partners) with action (eg, I have a grant deadline and I don’t have time to meet and consult with the community partners). Enacting principles of inclusion does not always go the way we intend and is not fully represented by noting identity categories of team members. For example, when categories are presented alone, there is ample room for assumptions and prejudices from the reader. The reader is compelled to assume a shared understanding of where privilege is located in the social order and how this privilege or disadvantage shapes interactions and relationships during the research process. But categories like race or gender do not operate uniformly, as the extensive research on intersectionality illustrates. While the association between identity category and privilege is often viewed as a given, their givenness requires reflexivity to identify intersectional and nuanced aspects of one’s life that links experience, being, thinking and doing. There are countless examples that can be provided to illustrate this point. For example, one may be disadvantaged by racial prejudice and experience a loving family situated in a place with strong community unity and belonging. One can receive advantages due to the ‘accident of birth’ in a particularly monetarily affluent family and neighbourhood but experience alienation or abuse within a family or isolation within a community.

The nuance of intersectionality coupled with the relationality of the research process should encourage us to think about how positionality intersects with context, principled aspiration and action. To describe in detail where a team struggled to include different perspectives of team members in the interpretation of qualitative data, to note strategies used to ensure that participants were sufficiently engaged in the review of study findings, approaches to expand representation on the core team, all have important contributions to make to how we do equity-pursuant and justice-pursuant research. For example, the National Collaborating Centre for Determinants of Health (NCCDH) in Canada provides a rich, detailed description of the principles that inform their own pursuit of equity and justice. They explicitly note that the principles that inform their work are ‘humility, compassion, solidarity, accountability and justice’ and articulate commitment to embracing ‘antioppressive, intersectional and collaborative relationships and ways of knowing to support collective change, transformation and well-being’.15 This explicit description provides an anchor to hold NCCDH to account, but more importantly, in my view, to learn from their efforts. To follow their work and seek to gain insights into the things that worked, the things that were hard, the things that were misaligned, and how they adapted to particular situations in particular contexts.

It is not easy to apply principles, and different contexts may bring different challenges in application. The positionality statement offers a systematic way to ensure that these lessons are being shared in as many settings as possible. The requirement, or at least encouragement to provide a reflexive statement of principle-informed process, would make journals an exceptionally rich venue to share these lessons. If the authors of every research article in every issue provide a reflexive statement of their principle-informed process, then the ecosystem of journals across disciplines becomes an extensive catalogue for learning. This learning can be gained across different geographic settings, with different composition of partners, through the application of different principles, and across different disciplines using different methodologies. The research community would gain rich and diverse perspectives on how to enact principles of justice and equity in research.
