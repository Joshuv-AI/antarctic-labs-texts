Identifying good organizational practices through the McKinsey 7S framework: A case study of an ophthalmology clinic1
Authors: Bounjerte Imane, Fatih Ibtissam, Zidi Mohamed, El Turk Joumana, Haidar Ibtisam, Kettani Anass, Saile Rachid, Bennani Houda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePatient safety represents a cornerstone of high-quality healthcare and holds particular significance in ophthalmology, where precision, governance, and adherence to best practices are essential. To sustain and improve such standards, strategic management tools like the McKinsey 7S Framework offer a comprehensive approach to evaluate internal organizational alignment and its impact on performance, communication, and continuous improvement.MethodsThe study was conducted at the Nour Ophthalmology Clinic in Casablanca, Morocco. Data were collected through a validated questionnaire based on the McKinsey 7S framework, administered to 30 staff members. Data collection was conducted using Sphinx V5 and Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V25.ResultsThe findings revealed a strong alignment of the clinic's practices with the components of the 7S model. Leadership was found to be particularly effective, characterized by engagement, optimal communication, and a focus on continuous improvement. Management emphasis on single-use equipment was also seen as a factor enhancing both patient safety and staff engagement.ConclusionsDespite positive overall performance, the study identified areas for improvement, notably the need to formalize and strengthen training program evaluations, clarify roles and responsibilities, and improve awareness of process mapping. While the results reflect one clinic's context, they provide valuable insights and highlight the potential of the McKinsey 7S model for assessing organizational performance in healthcare.

Introduction

“The process by which an organization makes patient care safer” is the definition of patient safety. Along with providing high-quality care, it is especially pertinent in ophthalmology [1].

Closely tied to patient safety is the concept of clinical governance. Adequate staffing levels, suitable facilities, and proper managerial support are necessary to maintain organizational standards of practice for ophthalmic care. Maintaining and improving ophthalmic patient care and service delivery requires meticulous attention to detail and careful consideration of the patient pathway [2].

To accomplish this, efficient clinical governance in ophthalmology must focus on several core principles:•Continuous improvement of patient care and services.•Adopting a patient-centered approach.•A commitment to quality that ensures ophthalmic teams follow the latest procedures and receive appropriate supervision when needed.•Avoidance of errors whenever possible, along with a commitment to learning from mistakes and sharing that knowledge in a non-blaming, supportive culture.•Optimal use of resources [2].

This aligns with broader systemic goals outlined by the Institute of Medicine (IOM) [3], which identifies six critical areas for improvement in healthcare systems. According to the Institute, high-quality healthcare should be:•Safe: Preventing patients from suffering harm while receiving care intended to benefit them.•Effective: Offering services based on scientific knowledge to everyone who might benefit, while avoiding underuse and overuse by not providing services to those unlikely to benefit.•Patient-centered: Ensuring all clinical decisions are guided by the patient's values, providing care that is sensitive and respectful of each patient's unique preferences, needs, and values.•Timely: Reducing wait times and sometimes harmful delays for both caregivers and recipients.•Efficient: Minimizing waste, including waste of equipment, materials, ideas, and energy.•Equitable: Providing healthcare that is consistent in quality regardless of individual factors such as gender, socioeconomic status, location, or ethnicity.

Patient needs would be much better met by a healthcare system that made significant progress in these six key areas. Patients would benefit from care that is safer, more dependable, responsive, integrated, and accessible. The full range of acute, chronic, and preventive services that patients are likely to need could be guaranteed. Clinicians and other healthcare providers would also benefit from such a system, as they would be able to deliver care that is more reliable, patient-centered, and coordinated than it is currently [3]. To implement and sustain these improvements, strategic management tools are essential, with one of the most comprehensive being the McKinsey 7S Framework. Developed in the 1980s by McKinsey & Company, this model examines and assesses an organization's internal elements that can affect its effectiveness. The name “7S” refers to seven interrelated components, all starting with the letter “S” [4], [5], [6]. These components are categorized into two groups: soft elements (Style, Shared values, Skills, and Staff) and hard elements (Strategy, Structure, and System). Research has shown that management culture influences the hard elements, while organizational culture typically impacts the soft elements. Although soft elements are more difficult to define and manage, they hold strategic importance for innovation, organizational structure, and contribute significantly to competitive advantage [7].

The following is a breakdown of the seven components:(1)Strategy: The organizational road map for achieving its aims and objectives is covered by this component. It includes the organization's overall business plan, as well as its approach to markets, competition, and growth strategies.(2)Structure: The idea of structure centers on the organization's structural arrangement. This includes its reporting structures, hierarchy, and the way different departments or functions are divided and organized.(3)Systems: The variety of protocols, guidelines, and practices that control how work is carried out inside the organization are referred to as systems. This includes the existing formal and informal systems.(4)Skills: The workforce's proficiencies and competencies are indicated by their skills. It includes the expertise, abilities, and knowledge that staff members possess.(5)Staff: This refers to the people who make up the organization, including their numbers, roles, and qualifications. It also takes into account things like hiring, training, and development.(6) Shared Values: These include the core values, tenets, and corporate culture that are present in the organization. The organization's identity is defined by these values, which also act as a set of guidelines for its decisions and actions.(6)Style: It explores the management and leadership philosophies that are prevalent in the organization. It includes the traits and conduct of upper management as well as their dealings with staff members [5], [7], [8].

In healthcare settings, each component of the 7S framework can be associated with established determinants of quality and patient safety. For instance, Shared Values and Style are closely related to safety culture and leadership engagement, which are known to influence error reporting and continuous improvement. Staff and Skills reflect workforce capacity, teamwork, and clinical competence, all critical for safe and effective care delivery. Systems align with process standardization and reliability, central to reducing variability and errors. Structure and Strategy relate to governance, resource allocation, and organizational priorities, which shape the implementation of quality and safety initiatives. This mapping supports the relevance of the 7S model as a comprehensive framework for evaluating organizational performance in healthcare contexts [3], [9], [10], [11].

The framework's ability to incorporate various aspects of an organization's operations enhances its usefulness in identifying problems, planning, managing change, and promoting alignment, factors that have a significant positive impact on organizational effectiveness and success [12].

This is the primary motivation for employing the McKinsey 7S Framework in this study, a valuable tool for assessing and analyzing the performance of healthcare organizations, such as ophthalmology clinics. However, its application in ophthalmology settings remains limited, and little is known about how these dimensions interact to influence perceived quality and patient safety at the operational level.

In this study, organizational performance is assessed through staff perceptions, as healthcare professionals are directly involved in care delivery processes and organizational functioning. Previous research has shown that staff-reported measures, including perceptions of teamwork, leadership, and work environment, are strongly associated with patient safety outcomes, quality of care, and organizational effectiveness. Therefore, staff perspectives provide a relevant and practical factor for evaluating internal organizational performance, particularly in contexts where objective performance indicators may be limited or not readily accessible [13], [14].

While quality management systems (QMS), including ISO-based audits, primarily focus on compliance with standardized procedures and documentation, they often provide limited insight into the internal alignment between organizational elements such as leadership style, staff competencies, and shared values. In contrast, the McKinsey 7S framework offers a more integrative perspective by examining both structural (“hard”) and cultural (“soft”) dimensions of organizational performance [4], [15].

The objective of this study is to evaluate organizational performance in an ophthalmology clinic in Casablanca using the McKinsey 7S framework, with a focus on identifying alignment across organizational dimensions and exploring how staff perceptions of these elements relate to quality management practices and operational effectiveness.

Research methodology

This study was conducted at Nour Ophthalmology Clinic in Casablanca, Morocco, a clinic specializing in the diagnosis and surgery of vitreous and retinal diseases, cataracts, glaucoma, and eyelid disorders. The clinic was created in 2010. It obtained the ISO 9001:2008 certification for its quality management system in 2014 and then passed the ISO 9001:2015 certification in 2017.

Our goal was to evaluate the organizational performance of the clinic by using a questionnaire based on the McKinsey 7S framework, to assess staff satisfaction with each component of the model (Shared values, Skills, Staff, Style, System, Strategy & Structure).

The survey was carried out with staff from different departments of the clinic. The sampling frame involved all eligible staff members of the clinic (N = 67), including both medical (doctors, nurses …) and administrative personnel. Eligibility was restricted to employees with a tenure of at least one year to ensure sufficient familiarity with organizational processes and members of staff who had joined the clinic less than one year prior to the study were excluded. Also, the sampling procedure was based on voluntary participation, where participants were recruited from different departments to reflect organizational diversity, and those who were available and agreed to take part in the survey were included. No differences in sampling procedures were applied between medical and administrative staff.

The initial sample consisted of 32 participants out of a total of 67 eligible ones. However, the responses of two participants were excluded from the final results as they had recently joined the clinic (3 months and 5 months). Therefore, the final sample consisted of 30 participants which corresponds to a response rate of 44.8%. Nonresponse was exclusively due to staff unavailability during the data collection period. No refusals to participate were recorded. Additionally, some staff were excluded prior to recruitment based on the predefined criterion (<1 year).

The data collection instrument was a questionnaire previously developed and validated in an earlier study by members of the research team. For the present study, the same instrument was employed without modification to evaluate the same dimensions in a different organizational context. Prior to its administration, the questionnaire underwent a validation process to ensure its content validity and reliability. Content validity was established through expert review, involving a specialist with substantial professional experience and relevant certifications in the field. Their feedback contributed to refining the questionnaire, enhancing its alignment with the study objectives and its suitability for the target population. A dedicated review meeting was conducted to assess key criteria, including clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness. Furthermore, the revised version was pilot-tested to confirm its practicality and ease of understanding. Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha, which yielded a coefficient of 0.906, indicating a high level of internal consistency among the questionnaire items [16] (The questionnaire is provided as Supplemental file 1). A 4-point Likert scale was used to collect responses to the survey, which had 31 closed-ended questions that corresponded to each of the seven McKinsey 7S model dimensions. Shared Values were explored through three items, while Strategy was similarly captured using three questions. The Structure dimension was examined through four items, and Systems were addressed using five questions. Style was assessed through six items, including one question specifically directed at managers to capture their perceptions of employees willingness to assume additional responsibilities. The Staff dimension comprised five items, and Skills were measured using four questions. A final item was included to capture an overall perception of organizational performance. It is important to note that these measures reflect participants subjective perceptions of organizational functioning across the seven domains. The scale applied to the first 30 questions was as follows: Totally disagree (1), Rather disagree (2), Rather agree (3), and Totally agree (4). Additionally, the following scale was applied to the 31st question regarding overall performance: the numbers 1 through 4 represent very low, low, good, and very good performance, respectively.

Data collection was organized in planned sessions with participants according to their convenience and availability. Each session consisted of a 15- to 20-min interview aimed at facilitating the completion of the questionnaire and ensuring data accuracy. These interviews were semi-structured, with participants answering a validated questionnaire and then engaging in open-ended discussions. The study was carried out at the clinic over a three-month period, from November 2024 to February 2025.

Data analysis

The collected data were initially entered and managed using Sphinx V.5 software, which is commonly employed for survey design and response processing through statistical summaries and data visualization tools. After data validation, the dataset was exported to IBM SPSS Statistics V.25 for further statistical analysis.

The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As several variables did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric statistical methods were employed. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize responses to individual Likert-scale items, expressed as percentages for each response category (ranging from 1 to 4). No inferential statistical tests were applied to these distributions, as the objective was descriptive rather than comparative. To compare aggregated scores between the hard elements (Strategy, Structure, Systems) and soft elements (Shared Values, Style, Staff, Skills) of the McKinsey 7S framework, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. This test was selected because the two sets of scores were derived from the same respondents, making the data paired.

Detailed descriptive statistics and domain-level analyses are provided in the supplementary files 2, 3 and 4 (tables A to E), illustrating item-level responses, central tendencies, and composite scores across the 7S framework.

Ethical considerations

All participants gave their informed consent prior to data collection, and the study complied with ethical standards. The confidentiality of their answers was guaranteed, and participation in the study was entirely voluntary. The top management of the clinic also examined and approved the survey, guaranteeing that it complied with ethical standards and organizational policies.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, recruitment, or dissemination of this study.

Informed consent and voluntariness

Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. To minimize any potential influence related to the employment relationship, the recruitment was conducted by non-supervisory personnel and no direct managers were involved in inviting participants. In addition, participants were explicitly informed that their decision to participate or not wouldn't have any impact on their employment status, evaluation, or professional relationships. To further ensure confidentiality, data were collected using fully anonymized questionnaires. No direct identifiers (such as names, identification numbers, or contact details) were collected, and no indirect identifiers were included that could enable re-identification of participants. Individual responses were not accessible to managers or the administration, and only aggregated results were reported. No incentives or penalties were associated with participation. These measures were implemented to ensure that consent was freely given and without any form of pressure.

Data protection and governance

The present study was performed according to the applicable laws on data protection in Morocco, especially the Law No. 09–08 concerning the protection of individuals with respect to the processing of their personal data. This law is supervised by the National Commission for the Control of Personal Data Protection (CNDP). No identifiable data were collected during this study, as the questionnaire was completely anonymous. The study also complied with the internal policies of the institution that hosted this study, with respect to research conduct and the confidentiality of collected data. As for storage and role-based access, data were collected first as anonymous printed questionnaires. The research team securely stored complete questionnaires and then entered the data into analysis software using a computer that was secured with a password and could only be accessed by the investigators. Data entry and analysis involved only authorized members of the research team. According to good research practice, paper questionnaires and electronic data will be stored until a period of 3 years has elapsed and then securely destroyed.

Language of the instrument

French is the working language of the study setting, so the questionnaire was originally developed and administered in French. No translation was required for data collection, and all participants responded to the French version of the instrument. For the purposes of publication, the questionnaire was translated into English, without affecting the content of the original instrument or the responses obtained.

Social desirability bias

Social desirability bias is a real concern with employer-based surveys. People sometimes feel pressured to answer more favorably, especially when they're worried about what their supervisors might think or how their responses could affect their jobs. This is especially true in healthcare, where organizational hierarchies are strict. To address this, a few measures were taken. Participation was entirely voluntary and respondents were clearly informed that their responses would be used solely for research purposes. Also, the questionnaire was administered anonymously with no identifying information collected which reduces the risk of response traceability. Additionnally, participants completed the questionnaire in absence of supervisory influence. Despite these precautions, we recognize that some degree of social desirability bias may persist, and this should be considered when interpreting the findings.

We also found that clinic staff did not express total disagreement with any item on the questionnaire (i.e., no respondent selected a rating of 1 for any question). Only one item, related to the evaluation of training program effectiveness, showed a slight level of disagreement: 3.45% of respondents selected a rating of 2 (Rather disagree), 10.35% selected 3 (Rather agree), and 86.20% selected 4 (Totally agree). This suggests potential concerns regarding the assessment of training outcomes. Indeed, during the interviews, several staff members mentioned that although training programs are implemented regularly, their evaluation (“immediate evaluation” or “delayed evaluation”) is not carried out systematically. In some cases, practical assessments are conducted following the sessions; however, these remain informal and lack standardized procedures or documentation, which limits the ability to measure the actual impact of training on professional practices.

As for the remaining results, although the overall feedback was positive, certain items revealed areas for improvement, as indicated by varying levels of partial agreement.

For example, in response to the question “Do employees understand their job description?”, 33.30% of respondents selected a rating of 3 (Rather agree), while 66.70% selected a rating of 4 (Totally agree). Similarly, for the question “Is there a set of common and clearly understood processes that underpin core activities?”, 66.70% of respondents gave a rating of 3 (Rather agree), and 33.30% gave a rating of 4 (Totally agree).

Based on these results, some analytical conclusions can be drawn. The overall level of satisfaction is relatively high, which aligns well with the McKinsey 7S framework. Different views on training evaluation and procedure clarity raise the possibility that operational difficulties may prevent performance. Management appreciates employees initiative, which is highly beneficial for fostering a strong organizational culture and developing staff competencies. The quality management system (QMS) is perceived positively, indicating that quality management practices are functioning effectively. Nevertheless, some minor areas would benefit from more formal evaluation and standardization.

Conversely, the areas for improvement reveal structural and procedural shortcomings that could obstruct the consolidation of quality practices. The limited awareness of process mapping suggests that while processes exist, they are not yet fully understood by all staff members. Similarly, the definition of roles and responsibilities, although present, remains informal, potentially leading to ambiguity in accountability and task execution. Additionally, the expressed need for more structured and evaluated training programs highlights the importance of formalizing capacity-building initiatives to ensure that professional development efforts are both consistent and aligned with organizational objectives.

To enhance the practical relevance of these findings, the identified improvement areas were translated into targeted implementation actions within the context of the studied clinic. First, to address the limited awareness of process mapping, a structured process mapping initiative could be deployed, including staff training sessions and the development of standardized process documentation. To improve clarity in roles and responsibilities, the formalization of a responsibility assignment framework such as a RACI matrix is recommended, involving the documentation of roles for key processes and ensuring dissemination among staff. In response to the need for structured training, a formal training evaluation protocol should be established, incorporating pre- and post-training assessments and participant feedback.

Discussion

According to the Moroccan law 06–22, relating to the national health system, the organization and management of healthcare facilities must comply with the quality norms and standards [17]. This legal framework emphasizes the importance of engaging healthcare professionals in continuous quality improvement (QI) efforts.

In this context, all healthcare professionals have a professional obligation to improve patient outcomes and care quality. Ophthalmologists and their teams must acquire the skills necessary for quality improvement considering the growing demand for ophthalmological services as a consequence of our aging population. This will ensure that patients receive, timely, safe, patient-centered, effective, efficient, and equitable care [18].

Quality improvement initiatives typically address at least one of the six IOM domains of healthcare quality. While a wide range of quality issues may be identified, prioritizing those that are clinically significant, prevalent, and feasible to address is essential [19]. Also, involving key stakeholders is essential after identifying a significant, prevalent, and manageable quality issue [18].

Therefore, this study focuses on evaluating the organizational performance of the Nour Ophthalmology Clinic of Casablanca from the perspective of a key stakeholder group: its staff. To achieve this, staff perceptions were assessed using the McKinsey 7S Model, which provides a strong framework for analyzing the internal alignment and effectiveness of organizational elements. Moreover, the clinic's quality improvement and patient safety practices are aligned with World Health Organization (WHO) frameworks on patient safety and clinical governance, emphasizing the equity dimension of care as highlighted in the Institute of Medicine's six aims. By situating these efforts within Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.8 on quality of care under Universal Health Coverage, our findings highlight lessons that extend beyond a single facility and contribute to broader public health objectives.

The results obtained reflect a high level of satisfaction, indicating that the organization is well aligned with McKinsey's 7S model. No respondent expressed complete disagreement with any statement. This alignment was further confirmed statistically. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed no significant difference between Hard S scores (Strategy, Structure, Systems; Mdn = 3.93) and Soft S scores (Skills, Staff, Style, Shared Values; Mdn = 3.95), Z = −0.856, p = .392, r = 0.16 (small effect). The small effect size confirms that this null result reflects genuine parity between dimensions rather than insufficient statistical power, and suggests that the clinic achieves a rare balance between its structural and human elements.

In fact, the clinic staff conveyed their satisfaction with the efforts of management to facilitate communication and strongly approved of its commitment to continuously improving operational effectiveness. This high level of satisfaction is not only indicative of internal alignment but may also have broader implications for the quality of care. Organizational performance is believed to be influenced by staff satisfaction, and research shows that higher nurse satisfaction is associated with better patient satisfaction, shorter hospital stays, and safer prescriptions, while more satisfied doctors are more likely to prescribe safely and achieve better patient adherence [20].

Furthermore, during the interviews, staff highlighted the strict adherence to the use of single-use equipment as a significant positive aspect. This practice ensures patient safety and prevents cross-contamination, contributing to the positive perception of staff. It also aligns with broader concerns in ophthalmic surgery regarding the risks associated with reusing single-use equipment. In many developing countries, particularly in Africa and Asia, the reuse of medical equipment originally intended for single use remains a common practice [21]. However, numerous studies have addressed the risks associated with this practice. Regulatory bodies such as the FDA (Food and Drug Administration), MHRA (Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency), WHO (World Health Organization), and JCI (Joint Commission International) emphasize strict protocols (or discourage reuse altogether) due to potential safety and performance issues. The reuse of single-use equipment raises serious concerns about bacterial and viral cross-contamination, as well as an increased risk of endophthalmitis due to challenges in effective cleaning, sterilization, and material degradation. In particular, device fatigue, endotoxin contamination, and compromised functionality (especially in instruments with narrow lumens) pose significant risks [22], [23], [24]. Nour Ophthalmology Clinic in Casablanca, one of the few clinics in Africa that strictly adheres to this practice, conducted its own study to assess the effects of reusing single-use medical equipment, particularly phacoemulsification cassettes used in cataract surgeries. The study confirmed the presence of microbiological contamination. It also discussed the economic motivations for reuse in many contexts, explaining that while reuse may initially reduce costs, it is ultimately not cost-effective since the additional expenses related to managing complications outweigh the short-term financial savings [21], [24].

Conversely, while employee perceptions were generally favorable, certain areas emerged as requiring improvement. Significantly, despite the implementation of training programs, the item related to the effectiveness of training program evaluations raised some concerns, with 3.45% of participants selecting “Rather disagree” and 10.35% selecting “Disagree.” This suggests that although training is being provided, its outcomes may not be consistently evaluated or effectively communicated. This highlights the clinic's recognition of the vital role that continuous training plays in driving organizational performance. According to [25], the quality of work, production, and services within the organization is greatly enhanced through effective training. Ultimately, the development of the healthcare industry depends largely on the performance of physicians, technicians, and support medical teams; it cannot be measured solely by the expansion of treatment facilities or the availability of modern equipment. However, according to [26], training evaluation helps in assessing to what extent the training and development efforts contribute to improved performance and results, which stress how evaluation is considered a key element of training programs effectiveness. Furthermore, the ISO 9001 standard also requires to evaluate the effectiveness of the actions taken to acquire competence [27]. From a 7S perspective, these training gaps primarily reflect a misalignment in the Skills and Systems dimensions: while the clinic invests in training (Skills), the absence of systematic evaluation mechanisms (Systems) prevents feedback loops that would drive continuous improvement. Similarly, the partial awareness of process mapping points to a gap in the Systems domain, where formalized procedures are not yet fully internalized across staff levels. The high engagement scores, by contrast, reflect strong alignment in the Shared Values and Style dimensions, as staff consistently perceive management as communicative and committed, which is consistent with a transformational leadership culture.

Additionally, some questions revealed partial agreement, particularly concerning the clarity of job descriptions and awareness of process mapping, indicating potential gaps. Process mapping plays a crucial role in enhancing process transparency and visibility. By providing a valuable tool for visualizing and analyzing organizational activities, it helps stakeholders better understand the current state, identify issues, clarify roles and interdependencies within processes, and ultimately make well-informed decisions [28]. A lack of awareness in this area may limit the ability of employees to engage effectively in process improvement initiatives. Similarly, according to [29], improved work performance is consistently linked to job descriptions that clearly define objectives, responsibilities, authority, and lines of accountability. Conversely, employees often struggle to maintain consistent productivity when they are unclear about their work priorities or when those priorities frequently shift. Additionally, in line with the ISO 9001 standard, top management should ensure that the responsibilities and authorities are assigned, communicated and understood within the organization [27].

Furthermore, managers were asked about their perception of staff willingness to take on greater responsibilities. Their responses indicated a generally positive view. This aligns with research indicating that employee engagement tends to be higher when managers demonstrate trustworthy behaviour [30], which is also considered a key component of effective leadership [27]. Moreover, studies on perceived supervisor support suggest that when employees feel supported by both their managers and the broader organization, their engagement significantly increases. This high engagement is strongly associated with greater organizational identification and a stronger sense of connection to the workplace [31].

Conclusion

This study illustrates the feasibility of using a structured framework, such as the McKinsey 7S model, to evaluate organizational performance in a single healthcare setting. By capturing the perspectives of clinic staff, the study provides insights into operational strengths and potential areas for improvement within this specific context. While the findings are not generalizable beyond similar settings, they highlight practical lessons for organizational assessment and clarify the distinction between normative standards (e.g., ISO 9001 requirements) and the actual performance observed in the clinic.

The findings also indicate that the Nour Ophthalmology Clinic of Casablanca is generally well-aligned with the 7S model, as evidenced by the overall positive staff assessments across all components of the framework. The leadership commitment in the clinic is shown by a clear engagement to contribute to the effectiveness of the activities, optimal communication and continuous focus on improvement. Moreover, the study highlights how management's commitment and adherence to using single-use equipment not only enhances patient safety and security but also reinforces clinic staff engagement.

However, some areas for improvement were identified. In particular, the evaluation of training programs requires both enhancement and formalization. These measures would ensure that the effectiveness of training is consistently measured, feedback is systematically collected, and opportunities for improvement are clearly identified. More awareness is also needed concerning the process mapping to guarantee a clear understanding of the activities of the clinic among all staff. Also, a better definition of roles and responsibilities can help further improve work performance.

Limitations and future research

This study was conducted in a single clinic with a relatively small sample of staff, using a cross-sectional design. Results may be affected by potential nonresponse and social desirability biases, and patient-level outcomes were not assessed. In addition, the findings reflect the organizational context of a specialized healthcare facility and may differ in other settings depending on factors such as institutional size, resource availability, staffing levels, leadership practices, and the maturity of quality management systems. Therefore, the findings should not be generalized beyond similar healthcare settings.

Furthermore, this study relied exclusively on self-reported perceptions and did not incorporate objective quality management system indicators such as audit results, incident reporting data, rework rates, or waiting times. The absence of such data limits the ability to triangulate findings and may lead to an overestimation of organizational performance due to perception biases. Future research could expand to multiple clinics, incorporate patient safety and quality indicators, and consider pre-post evaluations to assess the impact of quality improvement initiatives. Integrating subjective assessments with objective performance metrics would allow for a more comprehensive and robust evaluation of organizational effectiveness.
