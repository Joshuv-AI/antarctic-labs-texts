Drug Costs in Federally Sponsored Cancer Clinical Trials
Authors: Hong Xiao, Michael L. LeBlanc, Monica M. Bertagnolli, Sara Ashley Threlkel, Matthew Chansky, Dawn L. Hershman, Joseph M. Unger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat are the total drug acquisition costs in federally sponsored cancer clinical trials if all experimental agents were purchased directly, and how do these costs vary by trial phase, therapeutic class, and sponsor type?FindingsIn this systematic review of 1378 US federally sponsored cancer clinical trials from 2017 to 2023, the estimated median trial-level drug costs were $7.6 million, with higher costs for phase 3 trials ($56.3 million) and trials involving immunotherapy ($9.3 million), targeted therapy ($6.6 million), or their combination ($15.1 million) compared with chemotherapy. Experimental agents accounted for approximately 80% of total drug costs, and per-patient costs were higher in trials solely funded by federal agencies compared with cosponsored trials.MeaningThese findings underscore the significant financial burden of trial conduct and the need for policy innovations to reduce costs and better support clinically relevant federally funded research.

Introduction

Federally sponsored cancer clinical trials (FS-CCTs) address a broad spectrum of critical clinical cancer research questions often ignored by industry (eg, pharmaceutical or biotechnology companies), which primarily aims to receive new drug approvals and indications for existing therapeutic agents.1 FS-CCTs commonly evaluate novel combinations of treatment modalities, repurpose approved drugs, investigate rare cancers, and examine de-escalation regimens to reduce treatment burden without compromising efficacy.2 FS-CCTs also support translational research and dose optimization and enable comparative effectiveness studies such as within-class and head-to-head comparisons of agents.3 In addition, FS-CCTs play a key role in expanding access to innovative treatments by improving care delivery models and generate evidence that informs clinical guidelines.4,5 By focusing on questions often neglected by industry, these trials support advances that can improve patient outcomes and equity in cancer care.2,6,7,8

Despite their importance, FS-CCTs face growing financial pressures, particularly in covering the costs of anticancer drugs.9,10 Federally sponsored trials represent a shrinking proportion of cancer clinical research, while industry now sponsors about 90% of randomized clinical trials for cancer treatment and accounts for a substantially higher proportion of patient enrollment, with an estimated industry-to-federal (lead sponsor–defined) enrollment ratio of 17:1 for cancer drug and biological therapy trials among adults in recent years.10,11

While federal funding supports trial infrastructure and operational expenses, it typically does not extend to the provision of costly therapeutic agents. In practice, drug acquisition for FS-CCTs is commonly supported through a combination of mechanisms with industry, including in-kind provision of study drugs, cosponsorship agreements, or pricing arrangements negotiated with manufacturers.12,13 Although data regarding the extent to which differing mechanisms are used are not consistently reported in publicly available datasets, previous analyses have described industry participation as common and necessary to enable access to high-cost or investigational agents in federally sponsored trials.1,12,14,15 These arrangements often involve complex contractual processes that can delay trial initiation and may influence the selection of interventions, comparators, or study design.9,16 As a result, reliance on industry contributions has raised concerns regarding the feasibility, scope, and independence of federally sponsored trials.17

Given the central role that FS-CCTs play in shaping evidence-based cancer care, understanding the economic burden posed by drug procurement is vital for policymakers, clinical investigators, and funding agencies. In this study, we systematically estimated direct-purchase-equivalent drug acquisition costs associated with FS-CCTs. Our objectives were to quantify the magnitude of these costs and to identify trends across sponsor types and drug classes.

Methods

This systematic review used publicly available, trial-level aggregate data and did not involve human participants; accordingly, the need for study approval and informed consent were both waived by the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center Institutional Review Board. The study used a systematic identification of trial-level data and was reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) reporting guideline.

Inclusion Criteria

We included all US-based (ie, those with any US sites) clinical trials of pharmaceutical or biological interventions to treat cancer that received any National Institutes of Health or other federal sponsorship. Trials were identified from ClinicalTrials.gov using a structured query of registry records (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Eligibility was restricted to interventional oncology trials with start dates between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2023. Included studies consisted of phase 1 to 3 trials across all cancer types. Eligible trials were further filtered based on sponsorship classification to identify those with any level of federal sponsorship and at least 1 US site. Treatment trials evaluating drug and/or biological products as investigational interventions were included in the final analysis. Data were retrieved on July 10, 2024.

Data Sources and Variables

Trials and Agent Attributes

We extracted trial and agent characteristics from ClinicalTrials.gov records and trial protocols, including drug names, dose strength and unit, number of treatment cycles, dosing frequency, route of administration (eg, oral, intravenous), and enrollment per agent. Additional trial-level characteristics, including trial phase, sponsors and collaborators, and therapeutic modalities (eg, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery, immunotherapy, targeted therapy), were extracted and systematically categorized. Trials listed with combined phases (eg, phases 1-2) were classified according to the higher phase. Federal sponsorship was categorized as sole, lead (not sole), or collaborative.

For trials missing treatment schedules or dosage information in the registry, we consulted supplementary sources, including protocols from the Cancer Trials Support Unit, the Clinical Trials Reporting Program, and linked publications. When dosage frequency, dose strength, or cycle information remained unavailable, values were supplemented based on standard regimens from clinical guidelines, peer-reviewed literature, and validated drug information resources (eg, Drugs.com), accounting for treatment regimen, patient population (pediatric vs adult), and cancer type and stage.

For treatments administered until disease progression or unacceptable toxic effects, the number of treatment cycles per patient was estimated by dividing the total treatment duration by the length of a typical cycle (eg, 28 days). Treatment duration was defined as the shorter of 2 times: the expected progression-free survival (PFS) or the mean time from enrollment to the study’s primary completion date. When published or otherwise directly reported PFS estimates (eg, median PFS) were available, these observed values were preferentially used. For studies without reported PFS, expected PFS was modeled as a function of 3 binary variables: trial phase (phase 1-2 vs 3), cancer stage (advanced, metastatic, or recurrent vs other), and therapy type (immunotherapy or targeted vs other), yielding a 2 × 2 × 2 stratification structure. The 8 resulting strata were used to estimate study-specific PFS when PFS was not directly reported. Estimates were derived from a reference dataset of studies, with at least 5 studies included in each stratum (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). The mean time from enrollment to primary completion date was calculated from study start, enrollment close, and primary completion date (ie, date of final participant assessment or intervention for primary outcome).

Drug Price Components and Estimation Algorithm

Drug prices (hereinafter referred to as the wholesale acquisition cost [WAC] for a given drug) was obtained from a drug reference dataset (Micromedex Red Book; IBM Corporation18) by matching the product or active ingredient names and the administration route. Both current and historical WAC were included if the effective date of the list price fell within the trial’s study period (±2 years); otherwise, the most recent listed price was used. For each agent, the price per standard unit (eg, USD per mg, µg, or IU) was calculated using the drug’s metric size, strength, unit of measure, price per unit, and packaging information. Prices were reported in nominal dollars and adjusted to constant 2025 USD using the Consumer Price Index.

For each agent in a trial, the price was estimated by multiplying the dose strength in standard units by the median WAC per unit across all matched WAC entries. When WAC was unavailable but average manufacturer price (AMP) or suggested retail price (SRP) was available, WAC values were imputed using the corresponding year-specific average WAC-to-AMP or WAC-to-SRP ratios derived from the drug reference dataset. For products not recorded in the drug reference dataset, SRP from alternative sources (eg, MedChemExpress, Drugs.com, MedKoo Biosciences, and Google searches) were converted to WAC using the WAC-to-SRP factor. Newly approved or investigational agents lacking pricing information were assigned WAC estimates based on product type (drug vs biological) and therapeutic class (eg, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy). All data sources and the algorithm flowchart used in the analysis are presented in eFigure 2 in Supplement 1.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted between June 1 and September 20, 2025, using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). We derived estimates of both the planned and actual drug acquisition costs. Estimated planned total drug acquisition costs (hereinafter referred to as estimated costs) represent the anticipated total drug costs if all patients complete their protocol-specified full course of treatment. Estimated actual drug acquisition costs account for differences between actual and planned enrollment. These estimates serve complementary purposes in guiding trial planning and policy decisions.

To estimate planned costs, we calculated the acquisition cost for each drug in a trial as the product of treatment cycles, doses per cycle, WAC per dose, and the number of participants to be treated. The total trial-level drug cost was obtained by summing the acquisition costs of all drugs used in the trial. Per-patient drug acquisition cost was calculated by dividing the total trial-level drug cost by the total expected participants. Estimated actual drug acquisition costs were computed using the same algorithm, substituting actual enrollment for planned enrollment. For ongoing trials (eg, with status such as recruiting or not yet recruiting), actual enrollment was estimated by multiplying the registered expected enrollment by the observed actual-to-expected enrollment ratio derived from a larger external sample of cancer trials with closed enrollment.10

Analyses were stratified by sponsorship type, trial phase, and therapy type to assess variations across study characteristics. Drug acquisition costs for experimental agents were calculated separately. For phase 2 or phase 3 randomized clinical trials that included both intervention and control arms, we compared drug acquisition costs between arms. Comparisons were performed using Mann-Whitney tests (2 groups) or Kruskal-Wallis tests (>2 groups). Two-sided P ≤ .05 was considered statistically significant. Because WACs do not incorporate confidential rebates or discounts, we conducted a sensitivity analysis applying a 10% class-wide rebate adjustment, based on published estimates for oncology drugs.19

Results

We reviewed 1530 US-based cancer treatment clinical trials involving drugs and/or biological products as part of the intervention and included 1378 trials with 140 066 participants in the final analysis (eFigure 1 and eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Trials were excluded if their primary purpose was not cancer treatment or if drugs or biological products were not the main intervention of interest. Among the included trials, 450 (32.7%) were phase 1, 834 (60.5%) were phase 2, and 94 (6.8%) were phase 3 (Table 1). Regarding sponsorship, 401 (29.1%) trials were solely sponsored by federal agencies, 43 (3.1%) were led by federal agencies in collaboration with other entities, and 934 (67.8%) listed federal agencies as secondary, tertiary, or other sponsors. Of the 1378 trials analyzed, 1162 (84.3%) investigated immunotherapy and/or targeted therapy, while 102 (7.4%) focused primarily on chemotherapy.

The total median planned drug costs required to conduct the trials were $7.58 million (IQR, $2.11-$19.88 million) overall. Total drug costs differed by sponsor category with median values of $11.08 million (IQR, $4.20-31.04 million) for trials with federal sponsorship only, $13.68 million (IQR, $2.82-$30.51 million) for trials led by federal sponsorship, and $6.05 million (IQR, $1.37-$15.89 million) for trials with secondary, tertiary, or any participation by federal sponsors (P < .001 for federal collaboration). The median trial-level drug costs increased as the trial phase increased, from $4.84 million (IQR, $0.99-13.30 million) for phase 1 to $8.12 million (IQR, $2.54-$20.21 million) for phase 2 and $58.92 million (IQR, $12.25-$127.17 million) for phase 3 trials (P < .001 for phases 2 and 3). Discussion

In this comprehensive analysis of 1378 US-based cancer clinical trials initiated between 2017 and 2023, drug acquisition costs in FS-CCTs were estimated to be substantial and to vary markedly by trial phase, therapeutic modality, and sponsor category. The mean drug cost per trial exceeded $23 million, with costs escalating dramatically in phase 3 studies and trials involving immune and/or targeted therapies. On average, experimental arms alone accounted for more than 80% of the total drug expenditures, underscoring the financial intensity of testing novel agents. Given these financial burdens, federal sponsors have traditionally relied primarily on in-kind drug donations or cofunding from industry partners to proceed with trials. This reliance may limit the independence with which federally sponsored groups conduct trials and likely constrains the scope of research questions that are examined. These findings reflect the economic challenges of conducting trials and the complexities inherent in providing trial support.

The high costs associated with FS-CCTs are particularly concerning given the critical role these trials play in filling evidence gaps left by industry-sponsored research.20 FS-CCTs often investigate treatment strategies for rare cancers, explore de-escalation protocols, and address questions related to multimodal therapy or repurposed drugs, areas in which pharmaceutical firms tend to underinvest due to long development timelines, short-term corporate decision-making, and limited market exclusivity.8,14 While public funding typically supports infrastructure and trial operations, it rarely covers the direct costs of investigational agents, further highlighting the need for alternative financing models to ensure the viability and independence of this essential component of the cancer research ecosystem.13,15 Instead, public funders must secure industry cooperation to obtain drug supply, effectively giving pharmaceutical companies authority over which trials proceed. This dependence can constrain trial feasibility, particularly when manufacturers are unwilling to provide agents that may be tested against competing or potentially superior therapies. As a result, trials requiring active comparator arms, especially head-to-head comparisons between agents in the same therapeutic class, are difficult to conduct and remain uncommon, despite their importance for informing clinical decision-making.21 Our analysis showed that trials solely funded by federal agencies incurred the highest total and per-patient drug costs compared with those with joint or secondary sponsorship. Several factors may explain this pattern. First, these trials more commonly investigate complex or combinatorial regimens, including the use of multiple investigational agents or treatment modalities, which substantially increase drug acquisition costs.2 Second, trials solely sponsored by federal funding often prioritize research questions that align with public health objectives, such as rare cancers, underrepresented populations, or de-escalation strategies. These trials may require larger or more heterogeneous patient cohorts, extended follow-up periods, or broader inclusion criteria to ensure generalizability.1,22 These scientifically and clinically valuable design features tend to elevate the total cost per enrolled participant. In contrast, trials with industry collaboration may benefit from in-kind drug donations, preferential pricing, or streamlined trial designs aimed at regulatory approval, all of which can reduce the financial burden on trial sponsors.

Importantly, trials investigating immune checkpoint inhibitors, chimeric T-cell receptor therapies, or molecularly targeted agents were associated with much higher drug costs than those focusing on conventional chemotherapy. This is consistent with the rapidly increasing costs of new oncology drugs in the US health care system, where launch prices and subsequent spending have both risen dramatically during the past decade.23,24,25 Our findings suggest that clinical trial expenditures, often seen as distinct from clinical practice, may serve as a leading indicator of future treatment costs. As more FS-CCTs adopt novel agents as the standard comparator arm or as part of combination regimens, the downstream implications for health care budgets, patient access, and drug pricing policy could be substantial.

The economic pressures identified in this study underscore the urgent need for strategic reforms. First, greater transparency and oversight are needed to ensure that cost-sharing arrangements between federal and industry partners do not compromise scientific independence or restrict research priorities. Second, policy approaches should explore mechanisms that support independent evaluation of therapies, such as expanding public manufacturing or purchasing authority and conditional pricing models for trials deemed to have high societal value. Third, because industry-sponsored trials benefit from federally supported research infrastructure, mechanisms to return value to publicly funded trial systems should be considered. These may include structured cost-sharing agreements, proportional contributions to trial infrastructure, or reinvestment models that support federally sponsored research programs.14,26,27 Last, our findings highlight the need for the field of cancer clinical research to address the structural tensions between independence and feasibility. Potential approaches include strengthening public funding for trials addressing noncommercial research questions,14 establishing publicly governed mechanisms for prioritizing trial questions independent of sponsor influence,28 and expanding master protocols and improving trial matching systems at institutional and network levels.29,30 Without industry collaboration, many trials cannot proceed; yet with such collaboration, some research questions, particularly those not aligned with commercial interests, may never be pursued.

Strengths and Limitations

This study offers several notable strengths. First, it is, to our knowledge, the most comprehensive analysis of drug costs in FS-CCTs to date, covering 1378 US-based trials and providing a broad, multiple-year overview across cancer stages, phases, and sponsors. In addition, we used a standardized and authoritative drug pricing source, compiling nearly 400 000 historical price records.18 This enabled consistent cost estimates across diverse therapies, including immunotherapies and targeted agents, while incorporating trial-specific dosing and enrollment to better reflect complexity in a clinical context.

This study also has important limitations. First, nonexperimental agents, including supportive medications, standard-of-care comparators, and background regimens were not consistently reported in ClinicalTrials.gov, likely leading to underestimation of total drug costs, particularly in combination or multiple-arm trials where only experimental agents were explicitly specified. In addition, we did not capture the evolving practice in some industry-cosponsored trials of shifting costs of control arm therapies to patients’ insurance coverage, which may reduce apparent sponsor drug expenditures but raises concerns regarding cost attribution and equity.31 Second, although we aimed to capture the most current and relevant pricing data, actual drug transaction prices in the US are notoriously variable, lack transparency, and are subject to confidential rebates, discounts, and tiered pricing agreements. We based cost estimates on WAC, representing manufacturers’ list prices, which are typically higher than actual transaction prices.19 WAC nevertheless serves as a proxy for estimating the economic value of trial drugs, and relative comparisons across trial phases, therapeutic classes, and sponsor types are unlikely to be materially affected by these pricing differences. Even under conservative downward adjustments, estimated drug costs remain substantial relative to existing federal mechanisms for trial drug procurement. Third, our analysis was limited to federally sponsored cancer clinical trials that were successfully initiated and registered and therefore did not capture trials that failed to launch or were discontinued prior to activation due to drug supply, commercial priorities, or funding constriants.16,32,33,34 These trials are not systematically documented, so our findings reflect only the drug costs for trials that reached activation and do not capture upstream constraints influencing which trial concepts are pursued. Future studies should track noninitiated or early-discontinued trials to better understand how drug availability and industry dependence shape federally sponsored research.

Conclusions

In this systematic review of FS-CCTs, drug acquisition costs were substantial and highly variable, with higher costs in later-phase trials, trials involving immunotherapies or targeted agents, and federally sole-sponsored studies. These findings reflect the increasing complexity of oncology trials and the financial challenges of publicly funded research. Dependence on high-cost agents and industry support highlights potential tensions between feasibility and scientific independence. Sustained public investment and innovative financing strategies are needed to ensure the viability and independence of FS-CCTs and to support research aligned with public health priorities.
