The association between gut microbiota and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD): a case-control study
Authors: Pejman Rohani, Shima Shojaie, Ali Nikparast, Mohsen Shaygantabar, Fateme Ettehad Marvasti, Golaleh Asghari, Hanieh-Sadat Ejtahed, Mohammad Hassan Sohouli, Shirin Hasani-Ranjbar, Seyed Davar Siadat, Bagher Larijani, Afshin Ostovar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a growing concern among children and adolescents, with emerging evidence highlighting the role of gut microbiota in its pathogenesis. This case-control study aimed to explore the association between gut microbiota composition and NAFLD in overweight and obese Iranian children and adolescents. Given the recent updates in nomenclature, we refer to NASLD as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), in accordance with new international consensus.MethodsA total of 66 participants, including 33 MASLD cases and 33 controls, with a case control study design were enrolled. MASLD diagnosis was done using an ultrasonography scan of the liver and gastroenterologist confirmation. Furthermore, gut microbiota composition was analyzed using real-time PCR, and metabolic, anthropometric, and dietary data were collected.ResultsThe mean age of the study population (39.4% girl) was 136.82 month and mean BMI Z-score was 3.05. Regarding the gut microbiota composition, the only considerable variation was the lower abundance of Coprococcus genera in the MASLD group. Based on the analysis in the crude model and with adjustments for age, sex, and BMI, an inverse correlation was detected between the number of Coprococcus bacteria and the odds of MASLD(OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.77–0.98; P trend = 0.032 and OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.67–0.99; P trend = 0.046, respectively). However, this correlation was no longer present in the fully adjusted model. Furthermore, a negative correlation was noted between Coprococcus and ALT levels. On the other hand, the Prevotella bacteria represented significant positive correlations with both ALT and AST levels.ConclusionSignificant differences in gut microbiota composition were observed between MASLD and control groups, including reduced SCFA-producing and increased pro-inflammatory bacteria. These findings highlight the microbiota’s potential role in MASLD pathogenesis, though confirmation in larger cohorts is needed.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), refers to hepatic fat accumulation not attributed to excessive alcohol intake or other known liver pathologies [1]. MASLD encompasses a spectrum of conditions ranging from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma [2, 3]. Recent consensus guidelines emphasize the role of metabolic dysfunction as the central driver of the disease, prompting the adoption of the new terminology [4].

Although MASLD has been traditionally viewed as an adult disease, its prevalence in pediatric populations is rising in parallel with the global obesity epidemic. Current estimates suggest that MASLD affects approximately 7.6% of children in the general population and up to 34.2% of children with obesity [5, 6]. Risk factors include obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and genetic predisposition [7, 8]. However, the precise mechanisms underpinning MASLD pathogenesis remain incompletely understood.

Emerging evidence highlights the crucial role of the gut–liver axis, particularly the intestinal microbiota, in the onset and progression of MASLD [9]. The gut microbiome—a complex and dynamic ecosystem of trillions of microorganisms—participates in nutrient absorption, immune regulation, and host metabolism [10]. Disruption of gut microbial balance, known as dysbiosis, may lead to increased gut permeability, systemic inflammation, and translocation of microbial byproducts such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) into the portal circulation, thereby contributing to hepatic injury [3, 11]. Recent pediatric studies have indicated that MASLD is associated with distinct alterations in gut microbial composition, including reductions in beneficial short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria and increases in pro-inflammatory genera [12, 13]. However, the results remain inconsistent across studies, likely due to heterogeneity in study designs, age groups, diagnostic criteria, and analytical techniques. Moreover, the majority of existing research originates from Western populations, with limited data from Middle Eastern children, whose unique genetic, dietary, and environmental profiles may influence the microbiota–liver interaction [14].

Despite increasing interest in this area, there is a notable gap in research examining the association between gut microbiota and MASLD in pediatric populations from the Middle East, particularly Iran. Furthermore, few studies have concurrently evaluated microbial composition, dietary intake, and metabolic biomarkers in these children. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the composition of gut microbiota and its association with MASLD among overweight and obese Iranian children and adolescents. By integrating anthropometric, dietary, biochemical, and microbial data, this study seeks to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the gut–liver axis in pediatric MASLD and to identify potential microbial targets for early diagnosis or therapeutic intervention.

Methods and materials

Study design and study population

The current case-control study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences of Iran, under protocol number IR.TUMS.EMRI.REC.1401.050. The study included 66 overweight/obese children and adolescents with and without MASLD as a case and control groups, respectively, who were referred to the Children’s Medical Center of Tehran University of Medical Sciences. Children and adolescents were selected to participate in the study according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study’s goals and methods were explained to the children and adolescents and their parents, and if they wanted to participate, they were asked to read and sign the written consent form.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria in the case group were: (1) Children and adolescents 8–18 years; (2) Willingness to cooperate and sign an informed consent form; (3) Z score of BMI higher than 1 and lower than 3 for age and gender based on world health organization (WHO) criteria; (4) Diagnosis of MASLD is based on ultrasonography findings and physician assessment, with or without the presence of NASH (indicated by ultrasonography showing grade 2 or higher steatosis and a CAP score greater than 263), along with normal or abnormal serum levels of ALT and AST enzymes; (5) Absence of chronic and acute liver disorders (hepatitis B, C, etc.), Liver cirrhosis or fibroscan above 14 kilopascals, biliary disease, metabolic or autoimmune diseases, and hereditary disorders affecting the liver (storage disease of iron, copper, etc.); (6) Avoiding the use of hepatotoxic medications such as phenytoin, tamoxifen, and lithium; (7) Not affected by celiac disease, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases (CVD), lung disease, kidney disease, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and insulin resistance; (8) Not using milk thistle, omega 3, coenzyme Q10, and carnitine; (9) Not taking any antibiotics 2 months before the study; (10) No history of gastrointestinal surgery; 11) Not taking drugs or supplements that affect appetite, weight, or metabolism at least 6 months before the study; 12) Not having any weight loss diet or any heavy exercise program in the last 6 months. Also, participants who reported regular consumption of artificial sweeteners (e.g., aspartame, sucralose, or saccharin) within the past three months were excluded due to their potential impact on gut microbiota composition.

The inclusion criteria for the control group were similar to those for the case group, except that eligible individuals in this group included children without evidence of fatty liver disease. The participants of the two groups were matched based on gender, age, and maturity status. The control group was selected from overweight/obese children and adolescents without MASLD relatives of the case group or from among the clients of other departments of the medical center who did not have any disease affecting the intestinal microbiota composition. Also, the maturity status of the participants was determined based on the Tanner stage.

Exclusion criteria for control and case groups were: (1) Any acute disease or incident that affects a person’s health during the study; (2) Unwillingness to continue participating in the study or taking antibiotics during the study.

Laboratory measurements

Fasting blood samples were collected and immediately frozen at −80 °C. The blood samples were centrifuged within 30 min of collection. Serum total cholesterol (TC), high density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C), and triglyceride (TG) levels were measured using Pars Azmoon commercial kits (Tehran, Iran) by a biochemistry autoanalyzer. Serum low density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were calculated using the Friedewald Equation [15]: LDL-C = TC - (HDL-C) - TG/5. Fasting blood sugar (FBS) was determined using commercial kits from Pars Azmoon (Tehran, Iran) and serum insulin levels were determined using the immuno-turbidimetry technique. Serum was assessed for ALT, AST, Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) levels using Biotecnica (BT) autoanalyzer 3000 (Rome, Italy) with Pars Azmoon reagents kit (Tehran, Iran).

Dietary assessments

To assess dietary habits over the preceding year, a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), previously validated for accuracy, was employed [16]. This questionnaire included 168 commonly consumed food items based on typical Iranian eating patterns and standardized portion sizes. Participants were asked to report both the typical portion size and how frequently each food item was consumed. Frequency categories ranged from “never” to “daily,” including intermediate options such as 2–3 times per month, once per week, 2–4 times per week, and 5–6 times per week. Portion sizes were estimated in grams using standardized household measurements applicable in Iran [17]. The nutrient intake on a daily basis was then calculated using data from the USDA national nutrient database. To determine total daily energy and nutrient intakes, the Nutritionist IV software (First Databank, San Bruno, CA, USA), adapted for Iranian foods, was utilized [18].

Anthropometric assessments and physical activity level

Each patient’s weight was measured in kilograms over Lighting clothing and standing upright. The measurements were taken to the nearest 100 g using a calibrated scale (Seca, Germany). The height of the participants was measured standing up using a tape measure, without shoes, twice and with an accuracy of 0.5 cm, and the average of the two times was recorded. Subsequently, the body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the weight (in kilograms) divided by the square of the height (in meters). The waist circumference (WC) was assessed using a non-elastic tape measure with a maximum error of 0.5 cm. The ratio of waist circumference to height was also calculated by dividing the values ​​of waist circumference by the height, values ​​lower than 0.5 were considered normal and values ​​higher than 0.5 were considered abnormal. Z-score of BMI is a measure of relative weight and height, which is adjusted according to the reference standard (WHO) for age and sex. These scores are more suitable for assessing longitudinal changes in body weight and obesity and are a suitable measure for comparing group mean values. Therefore, z-score of BMI is used to evaluate body weight changes in participants. The level of physical activity of people was checked by the Iranian version of International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [19]. The level of physical activity was calculated by considering the coefficients related to the activity and recorded as Met-min/week. The Met coefficients for walking, moderate activity, and heavy activity are 3.3, 4, and 8 respectively. To calculate the amount of physical activity per week, the duration of the activity is multiplied by the number of minutes and the number of days per week. The sum of these calculations represents the total amount of physical activity per week.

Genomic DNA extraction, and analysis of the Microbiome composition

10 g of stool samples were taken from people and stored in a dark-colored stool collection container. The fecal samples were immediately frozen at −80 °C after collection and stored until analyzed. Bacterial DNA was extracted from stools using the FavorPrepTM DNA Stool Isolation Mini Kit (Favorgen® Biotech Corp, Pingtung, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The real-time- polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to identify and measure the desired bacteria load in the stool samples of patients. Specific primers were used for each bacterial genus. The 16 S ribosomal RNA gene of Escherichia coli bacteria is used as a standard in this study. The amount of each bacterium was calculated by comparing the obtained curve and comparing it with the standard curve and based on the dilution series of each of candidate bacteria by the same device.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and qualitative variables were reported as counts and percentages. Independent t-test or its non-parametric equivalent, i.e. Mann-Whitney test, was used to compare the mean of quantitative outcomes between the two studied groups. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare qualitative factors between two groups. Regression model was used to examine the correlation between variables. The association of gut microbiome composition and odds of MASLD among the participants was assessed by applying logistic regression. The analyses were adjusted for probable confounders, e.g., age, sex, BMI, ALT, GGT, physical activity and intake of cholesterol, fiber, vitamin D. We adjusted the results in three models using a priori selected potential confounders. In the modified models, the confounders used a statistical and conceptual approach, respectively. In this way, variables with Pvalue < 0.2 were considered as possible confounders and entered into logistic regression and the odds of disease were examined. Also, in the conceptual approach of modifying confounders in the second model, potential confounders were selected based on clinical concepts as well as based on previous articles and added to other confounders. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 16 software and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The mean (± SD) age of the study population (39.4% girl) was 136.82 ± 33.44 months. The mean (± SD) BMI Z-score was 3.05 ± 1.30. The socio-demographic characteristics, anthropometry, and biochemical parameters of the case (MASLD patients) and control groups are shown in Table 1. Compared with controls, MASLD subjects had significantly higher Age, BMI, ALT, GGT, but had lower physical activity. However, no significant differences were found for other characteristics among cases and controls. Also, in this study, mean frequency of gut bacteria, including Bacteroides, Ruminococcus, Ecoli, Coprococcus, Prevotella, Faecalibacterium, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium was compared between the case and control groups (Table 1). The results showed that pediatrics with MASLD had a significantly lower frequency of Coprococcus compared to the control group, but no significant difference was reported for other bacteria.

The results obtained from Table 3 in the crude model and also in Model 1 adjusted for age, sex and BMI, indicate a reduction in the odds of MASLD with increased frequency of Coprococcus bacteria (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.77–0.98; P trend = 0.032 and OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.67–0.99; P trend = 0.046, respectively). However, after additional adjusting for ALT, GGT, physical activity and intake of cholesterol, fiber, vitamin D, no significant relationship was observed between this microorganism and odds of MASLD. Regarding other microorganisms, no significant association was reported between these microorganisms and the odds of developing MASLD in all three adjusted models. Table 4 examines the relationship between gut microbiome composition with metabolic parameters. The reported results of the linear regression showed a significant correlation between liver enzymes (ALT and AST) (correlation coefficient = 0.88, Pvalue = 0.016 and correlation coefficient = 0.64, Pvalue = 0.004, respectively) and Prevotella bacteria. In addition, for every unit increase in Coprococcus bacteria, ALT decreased by 0.97 IU (correlation coefficient= −0.097, Pvalue = 0.013). However, no significant correlation was observed between other metabolic parameters and gut microbiome composition.

Discussion

Accumulating data has been emphasizing that MASLD pathogenesis, in both children and adults, is linked to the disruption of gut microbiota. Still, evidence concerning the microbiome variations between obese pediatrics with and without MASLD, especially in the Middle Eastern regions remains scarce.

This case-control study was designed to explore the gut microbiome composition and its potential metabolic associations in obese Iranian children and adolescents with and without MASLD. Results have demonstrated notable differences between the study groups, including significantly lower physical activity, and higher BMI, ALT, and GGT levels in the MASLD cases. The dietary intake did not indicate any statistically significant difference in the cases and controls. Regarding the gut microbiota composition, the only considerable variation was the lower abundance of Coprococcus genera in the MASLD group. Based on the analysis in the crude model and with adjustments for age, sex, and BMI, an inverse correlation was detected between the number of Coprococcus bacteria and the odds of MASLD. However, this correlation was no longer present in the fully adjusted model. Furthermore, a negative correlation was noted between Coprococcus and ALT levels, suggesting a potential protective role for this genus. On the other hand, the Prevotella bacteria represented significant positive correlations with both ALT and AST levels, supporting the notion of its involvement in liver-related inflammation. Our findings are mostly aligned with those of previous studies investigating the role of gut microbiota in NAFLD/MASLD, which have similarly reported alterations in microbial diversity and reductions in SCFA-producing bacteria [20–22].

In terms of microbial composition, Coprococcus, a member of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, had a significantly lower abundance in the MASLD group in our study. Also, this genus was found to have a negative correlation with ALT, to the extent of 0.97 IU decrease in ALT levels with every unit increase in Coprococcus. This could be a confirmation of the potential protective role suggested for this genus against liver dysfunction. It is important to note that Coprococcus also demonstrated a significant statistical association with reduced odds of MASLD when adjusted for age, sex, and BMI (Model 1), however, the statistical significance was lost when further adjustments were made (Model 2), including ALT, GGT, physical activity, intake of cholesterol, fiber, and vitamin D. This implies that physical activity may mediate the relationship between MASLD and this genus, and since dietary factors did not differ significantly between the groups, they seem to have made no contribution to this mediation. In other words, while Coprococcus generally is assumed to benefit liver health, its protective effects seem dependent upon metabolic health and physical activity. Our findings are consistent with prior studies in adults and pediatrics, which present complex bidirectional interactions between Coprococcus and various metabolic dysfunctions [23–27]. On one hand, evidence manifest that elements such as sedentary behavior or metabolic and liver disease progression alter the abundance and functionality of SCFA-producing bacteria, including Coprococcus [28, 29]. On the other hand, the reduction of these bacteria and their metabolites has shown to further aggravate inflammatory and metabolic disorders. As an illustration, the work of Zhu et al. [30] detected a notable decrease in Coprococcus genera among children with MASLD compared to healthy controls. A more recent study conducted by Pan et al. [31], the microbiota composition differences among three groups of children, including children with MASLD, children with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and obese children without MASLD were evaluated. Butyrate-producing bacteria (including Coprococcus) were significantly decreased in the NASH group. According to this study, the reduced abundance of these bacteria was associated with dysregulations in glucose and lipid metabolism and MASLD progression.

As mentioned previously, Coprococcus is known for producing SCFAs, especially butyrate. SCFAs are generally beneficial in hemostasis by improving gut-barrier integrity and fat metabolism, as well as reducing inflammation [21, 32]. Butyrate in particular, can have suppressing effects on inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, and regulate NF-κB pathways, which are related to MASLD progression [21]. Therefore, reduction in these genera could lead to pro-inflammatory state and impaired gut-barrier. Consequently, harmful substances, such as endotoxins, lipopolysaccharides (LPS), and secreted cytokines, could leak into the portal circulation, potentially inducing hepatic inflammation, steatosis, and fibrosis [33, 34].

In contrast to Coprococcus, the association between Prevotella and MASLD is much more conflicting across the literature. While several studies, parallel to ours, implicated this genus in hepatic injury and MASLD [30, 35–37], others reported countering results [38, 39]. Our analysis demonstrated a significant correlation between Prevotella and liver enzymes (AST and ALT), which can add to the evidence pointing to the potential role of this genus in hepatic inflammation. Research conducted by Amrousy et al. [35], evaluated the microbiota changes in obese adolescents with MASLD and elevated ALT, compared to healthy controls. Amongst their findings was a relationship between the reduced abundance of the Prevotella genus and MASLD. In another study, Michail et al. [36], explored the gut microbial profiles of obese children with and without MASLD and healthy lean controls. Prevotella was found more abundant solely in the MASLD group, with no significant changes in obese children without MASLD and lean controls. The observation of more abundant Prevotella in obese MASLD patients compared to obese children without MASLD, suggests a specific association between this genus and MASLD beyond the general condition of obesity. On the contrary, a study of adults with MASLD noted that patients with NASH and more severe liver fibrosis (grades 2 and above) had fewer numbers of Prevotella, suggesting a prognostic role for this genus [38]. Monga et al. explored the relationship between gut microbiota and MASLD in obese youth. One of their observations included a decreased abundance of Prevotella in obese children with MASLD compared to obese children without MASLD [39].

Prevotella seems to take part in several mechanisms that could explain the dual potential of this genera in both mitigation and exacerbation of liver injury and inflammation. For instance, by fermenting carbohydrates and producing SCFAs like acetate and butyrate, Prevotella could present anti-inflammatory properties. However, in cases of SCFA imbalance, undesirable effects might occur. In these situations, SCFAs (especially acetate) can negatively affect metabolism and promote lipogenesis, systemic and hepatic inflammatory states [21, 36, 40]. Also, Gram-negative bacteria including Prevotella, can produce byproducts such as LPS, which is a potent toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) activator. Following this activation, pro-inflammatory cytokines are released, and this inflammatory response can contribute to hepatic steatosis and progression to NASH [41]. Additionally, this genus can influence metabolic processes and energy harvest, which are critical in the context of MASLD, by modifying bile acid metabolism [42] and interaction with other members of the microbial community, such as hydrogen-oxidizing methanogens [36]. Hence, the balance of advantageous and disadvantageous effects of Prevotella is hinged on the overall dietary and metabolic background.

Interestingly, our investigations found no significant variations between groups in the abundance of other bacterial genera commonly linked to MASLD, such as Bacteroides, Ruminococcus, E. Coli, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Faecalibacterium. These results contrast some former findings that have shown changes in these genera in MASLD [21, 27, 35]. This discordance could be due to the factors affecting the gut microbial composition, such as different age groups, diets, ethnicities, genetics, and disease stages. Moreover, differences in diagnostic criteria, study designs, or methods used for microbiota analysis might cause different observations across studies. This adds to the complexity of the comprehension of the relationship between microbiota and MASLD pathogenesis, making it rather impossible to draw firm conclusions and define a single signature composition in pediatrics.

According to our observations, regardless of both groups being obese, the MASLD group presented with more severe dysregulations in metabolic indices, such as BMI, physical activity, and liver enzymes. On the other hand, the comparison of nutritional variables did not reveal any significant differences between cases and controls, which could be a result of the potential dietary assessment limitations (e.g. underreporting of dietary habits) or similarly poor diets in both groups. Though, this combination of outcomes could suggest that elements other than diet, such as genetic predisposition, gut microbiota variations, and physical activity dissimilarities, might play more prominent roles in the development of MASLD. Several studies in adults are in agreement with this concept [43, 44], such as the systematic review conducted by Whitsett et.al [45], which highlights the considerable impact of physical activity on MASLD. This review demonstrates that exercise, independently from diet and weight loss, can ameliorate MASLD and underlying metabolic abnormalities and enhance liver enzymes, hepatic steatosis, and insulin resistance. Moreover, some studies in pediatrics similarly state that sedentary behavior is an independent risk factor for MASLD and physical activity seems to improve inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance, all of which are involved in MASLD development [46, 47].

Recent evidence suggests that certain dietary components, particularly antioxidants and prebiotics, can significantly influence gut microbiota composition and function. Dietary antioxidants such as polyphenols (e.g., from berries, green tea, and dark chocolate) have been shown to promote the growth of beneficial microbial species while reducing oxidative stress and inflammation associated with MASLD [48, 49]. Likewise, prebiotic fibers—such as inulin, fructooligosaccharides (FOS), and galactooligosaccharides (GOS)—can stimulate the proliferation of SCFA-producing bacteria, improve gut barrier integrity, and reduce endotoxemia [50, 51]. These dietary factors may represent promising adjunctive strategies for MASLD prevention or treatment through gut microbiota modulation.

While our study provides valuable insights into the gut microbiota composition in children with MASLD, it is important to acknowledge that fecal samples may not fully capture the entire microbial landscape of the gastrointestinal tract. Mucosa-associated microbial communities, particularly those in the small intestine and proximal colon, might differ substantially from fecal microbiota. Future studies utilizing colonoscopy-derived or mucosal biopsy samples may offer a more comprehensive view of microbial alterations in MASLD.

Strengths, limitations and future directions

This study, pioneering in investigating the gut microbiota in obese Iranian children and adolescents with and without MASLD, has notable strengths, including a focus on an under-studied demographic and geographical area, and controlling for numerous confounding factors. Nonetheless, it also has several limitations, including the insufficiency of the Real time PCR method in providing microbial diversity and detecting species-level variations in the gut microbiome. Also, as MASLD pathogenesis and microbiota composition are multifactorial entities, other potential confounders, such as unmeasured nutritional variables and genetic factors, may have influenced the observed results. Lastly, the cross-sectional design of the current study prevents it from establishing a causal association between microbiome changes and MASLD. One limitation of our study is that gut microbiota analysis was based solely on fecal samples, which may not accurately reflect the microbiota composition along the entire gastrointestinal tract. Mucosa-associated microbiota, which can be sampled via colonoscopy or mucosal biopsies, might provide a more representative profile, particularly in relation to host–microbiota interactions and immune modulation. Another important limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, which may reduce the statistical power and limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are needed to confirm these associations and explore potential causal relationships.

To overcome the shortcomings of the evidence at hand, future endeavors are recommended to incorporate longitudinal assessments, SCFA quantification, and diversity metrics to enhance comparability across studies. Furthermore, combining microbiome, metabolomics, and immune profiling can prove useful as they provide more profound mechanistic insights into MASLD and gut microbiota interactions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study underlines the complex association between gut microbiota, obesity and MASLD pathogenesis in children and adolescents. Our findings demonstrated significant variations in microbial communities of obese pediatrics with and without MASLD. Moreover, the possible protective role of Coprococcus, and the potential inflammatory role of Prevotella in liver health were noted in this study. These results suggest that microbial composition could serve as a biomarker for MASLD progression or a target for therapeutic interventions. However, the conflicting outcomes across existing studies necessitates the investigation of these relationships in diverse populations and through longitudinal studies, so that the comprehensive understanding of gut-liver axis could lead to more personalized approaches in prevention and management of MASLD in the pediatric population.
