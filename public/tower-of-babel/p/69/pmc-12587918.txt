Phenylacetylglutamine, anaemia and outcomes in ischaemic heart failure: a cohort study
Authors: Qingfen Zhou, Jun-Tao Zhao, Xiao-Shuang He, Wen-Kai Chen, Feng-Mei Hu, Feng-Ru Zhang, Lin Lu, Qiu-Ya Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to measure the level of phenylacetylglutamine (PAGln) and its correlation with anaemia in ischaemic heart failure (HF) patients.DesignA prospective cohort study.SettingThe study was conducted at Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China.ParticipantsA total of 89 ischaemic heart failure (IHD) patients confirmed by symptoms, echocardiography and coronary angiography and 75 healthy controls were enrolled (all aged≥18 years).Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the association between serum PAGln levels and low haemoglobin (HGB). The secondary outcome was to explore the composite endpoint of cardiovascular death and HF hospitalisations during 1 year of follow-up.ResultsThe PAGln levels were significantly higher in IHD patients compared with the control population (716 (440–1097) vs 411 (202–697) ng/mL, p<0.001). There were 27 IHD patients who had low HGB (27/89). In HF patients with IHD, after adjustment for age and log N-terminal brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), the tertiles of PAGln were associated with low HGB (OR, 2.610; 95% CI: 1.251 to 5.447; p=0.011). Moreover, during the 1 year follow-up, PAGln levels continued to demonstrate the relationship with HF endpoints (19/73, 26.0%) (HR=3.547, 95% CI: 1.254 to 10.034, p=0.017), even after adjusting for HGB and log NT-proBNP in Cox regression analysis.ConclusionsIn HF patients with IHD, the elevated PAGln levels were related to reduced HGB. Additionally, the increased PAGln concentrations with low HGB were linked to poor HF endpoint events.

Background

Heart failure (HF), the ultimate common outcome of various cardiac injuries, is primarily driven by ischaemic heart disease (IHD), which contributes significantly to its global morbidity and mortality.1 2 Emerging evidence highlights the microbial metabolites, especially those from dietary nutrients, can play paracrine and endocrine roles, participate in metabolism and ultimately affect the pathophysiological processes of cardiovascular (CV) diseases and increase susceptibility to HF.35 The ‘gut hypothesis’ posits that intestinal wall oedema and compromised intestinal barrier function could also facilitate bacterial translocation and augment inflammation in HF.6

Among these metabolites, phenylacetylglutamine (PAGln)—a gut microbiota-dependent metabolite derived from dietary phenylalanine to phenylacetic acid (PAA)—has been shown to enhance the risk of atherosclerotic CV disease.7 The possible mechanism of PAGln is to promote platelet prothrombotic activity through adrenergic receptor signalling.8 Additionally, PAGln may induce other pathological pathways, including inflammatory activation and oxidative stress.9 Some clinical studies based on multicentre cohorts of HF with reduced ejection fraction (EF) showed that elevated PAGln level was correlated with HF occurrence and high mortality of CV disease.10 11 Nevertheless, there are still relatively few studies on PAGln and HF. Moreover, as chronic HF progresses in severity, the prevalence of anaemia rises correspondingly, contributing to higher postmyocardial infarction mortality and poorer outcomes following revascularisation.12 However, whether PAGln contributes to anemia-related HF deterioration in IHD patients remains unknown. This study aims to investigate whether PAGln interacts with anaemia to exacerbate HF disease in patients with IHD.

Methods

The institutional review board of Ruijin Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine in Shanghai, China, approved this study (Ethics Committee reference number: 2016-019), which was carried out following the principles outlined in the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before their enrolment. The cross-sectional analysis was conducted on all 164 participants to examine the association of serum PAGln levels and the presence of HF with IHD. The prospective study was performed on 81 HF patients with IHD to assess the HF endpoint.

Study population

The study encompassed 102 persistently HF inpatients with IHD who were on diuretics for at least 3 months and had been hospitalised for HF at least once in the previous year. Participants consisted of both male and female individuals aged 18 years or older. However, those with recent acute coronary syndrome (n=2), acute HF (n=4), infectious disease (n=2), malignant tumour (n=1), stroke (n=1), mechanical ventilation needs (n=1) or kidney replacement therapies (n=2) were excluded. Ultimately, this study enrolled 89 HF patients with IHD. All patients had stable coronary artery disease, presented with HF-related symptoms and were confirmed by echocardiography to have left ventricular systolic dysfunction (EF≤50%).13 Then, the 89 patients were categorised into the low PAGln group (PAGln<516.35 ng/mL, n=30), the medium PAGln group (516.35 ng/mL≤PAGln<945.37 ng/mL, n=30) and the high PAGln group (PAGln≥945.37 ng/mL, n=29). Additionally, 75 healthy subjects were collected as controls (figure 1). In the Cox regression analysis, the patients were redivided into a low group (n=37, PAGln<717.20 ng/mL) and a high group (n=36, PAGln≥717.20 ng/mL) on a binary classification of PAGln values. This was an exploratory study aimed at generating preliminary data for future large-scale research.

Follow-up and outcomes

The HF endpoints consisted of CV death and hospitalisation due to HF. The follow-up survey was conducted by hospital visits or through telephone. All endpoints were confirmed by independent cardiologists intensively. Recurrent HF was diagnosed on account of signs and symptoms, abnormal laboratory parameters, as well as imageological examination.

Clinical evaluation, biochemical examination and intestinal flora measurement

Demographic information and clinical characteristics were comprehensively gathered from all participants. The Body Mass Index (BMI) was determined using the formula weight divided by height squared, expressed in kilograms per square metre. Blood pressure (BP) was taken from the arm while the patient was seated after resting for 10 min. Hypertension was identified according to the criteria outlined in the seventh report of the Joint National Committee on prevention, detection, evaluation and treatment of high BP (JNC7).14

Blood samples were collected after an overnight fasting period for subsequent laboratory analysis. Plasma concentrations of creatinine, triglycerides and total cholesterol (TC) were measured using the HITACHI 912 Analyzer from Roche Diagnostics in Germany. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated based on the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation.15 Glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels were assessed with ion-exchange high-performance liquid chromatography equipment provided by Bio-Rad Laboratories in the USA. Alanine transaminase (ALT) levels were evaluated using the Beckmann AU5821 automatic biochemical analyser along with appropriate reagents. N-terminal brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) concentrations were determined utilising a commercially available ELISA kit from Roche Diagnostics. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels were measured with an ELISA kit sourced from Biocheck Laboratories located in Toledo, OH, USA. Routine blood tests—including red blood cell (RBC) count, haemoglobin (HGB) and other parameters—were conducted using the XS-800i automatic blood analyser manufactured by SYSMEX, Japan. The current study defined anaemia according to the Chinese criteria: HGB concentrations lower than 120/110 g/L (for men/women).16 Iron and total iron binding capacity were analysed/detected by the Beckmann AU5821 automatic biochemical analyser and matching reagents. Serum intestinal flora (betaine, L-carnitine, trimethylamine, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), PAGln, acetylcarnitine and choline)) levels were analysed using AB Sciex 4500MD mass spectrometry.

Echocardiographic examination

Two-dimensional (2D) echocardiography and Doppler flow imaging were executed on the second day after admission. The imaging was acquired utilising the GE Vivid-I system equipped with a 1.9–3.8 MHz phased-array transducer, capturing images from standard parasternal and apical four-chamber perspectives at a frame rate ranging from 60 to 100 frames per second. Digital data were analysed offline through EchoPac V.7 software by two cardiologists who were oblivious to the laboratory outcomes. Measurements like left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, left atrial diameter, aortic dimensions, interventricular septal thickness and left ventricular posterior wall thickness were attained by M-mode echocardiography. Left ventricular EF was computed using Simpson’s biplane approach on 2D apical four-chamber views. The inferior vena cava (IVC) was assessed using established echocardiographic criteria. A dilated IVC (>2.1 cm in diameter) demonstrating suboptimal inspiratory collapse (<50%) was interpreted as indicative of elevated right atrial pressure (10–20 mm Hg). These above parameters were used to assess high central venous pressure (>10 mm Hg), consistent with contemporary guidelines for non-invasive haemodynamic assessment.17

Patient and public involvement

No patient has been involved in the protocol design or implementation of the study.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data with non-normal distributions were presented as median (IQR), while normally distributed data were expressed as mean±SD. Categorical data were summarised as frequencies or percentages. To analyse trends across groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed data, followed by post-hoc analyses with the least significant difference method. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test (a non-parametric alternative to ANOVA) was applied to compare medians between groups. If the Kruskal-Wallis test yielded significant results (p<0.05), Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction was performed for pairwise comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests.

Logistic regression models in univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to calculate ORs along with 95% CIs. The receiver-operating characteristic curves (ROC curves) analysis was performed by using the continuous variables or prediction probabilities carried out by logistic regression, and the best cut-off value and the area under the curve (AUC) were determined. The association between serum PAGln levels and other markers was examined through simple correlation analysis. Kaplan-Meier curves facilitated the assessment of event-free survival rates, while log-rank tests evaluated differences in survival across PAGln tertiles. Cox proportional hazards analysis was used to estimate HRs with 95% CIs. Statistical evaluations were conducted by employing SPSS V.22.0 software with a significance threshold set at p<0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics in all subjects

The baseline characteristics of all participants (n=164) were presented in table 1. Among the 164 individuals, 75 (45.7%) served as healthy controls, and 114 (69.5%) were male. The 89 HF inpatients with IHD were categorised into three approximately equal groups based on their tertiles of serum PAGln levels. The tertiles of PAGln groups and controls offer a comprehensive summary of the demographic data and clinical traits in the study. Compared with the control group, the PAGln tertiles (missing 0%, n=164) of HF with IHD exhibited a higher male ratio, increased levels of FBG (missing 3.66%, n=164), HbA1c (missing 24.39%, n=164; 2.25%, n=89), hsCRP (missing 38.41%, n=164; 0%, n=89) and choline (missing 0.61%, n=164), as well as a higher incidence of low HGB (controls vs lowest to highest PAGln tertile, 1.3% vs 20.0%, 26.7% and 44.8%, all p<0.001) (missing 0%, n=164) (p<0.05). Additionally, HDL-c (missing 0.61%, n=164) and eGFR (missing 0%, n=164) were significantly lower in the PAGln tertiles of HF with IHD (p<0.05), with the lowest eGFR observed in the highest tertile of PAGln (p<0.05). In comparison with the control group and the low to medium tertiles of PAGln, the highest PAGln tertile within the HF with IHD cohort demonstrated lower TC and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) levels (missing 0.61%, n=164) but higher L-carnitine and TMAO concentrations (missing 0%, n=164) (p<0.05). The high and medium tertiles of HF with IHD demonstrated significantly higher PAGln levels than both the low tertile and control groups (p<0.001). No significant trends were observed for the remaining indices (p>0.05).

Receiver-operating characteristic curve analysis of PAGln for HF with IHD

The PAGln levels were significantly higher in IHD patients compared with the control population (716.21 (440.34–1097.40) vs 410.93 (202.27–696.98), p<0.001). In all subjects, the optimal cut-off value of PAGln for predicting HF with IHD was determined to be 709.15 ng/mL, identified by maximising Youden’s index (J=0.315). This yielded an AUC of 0.709 (95% CI: 0.631 to 0.787), with a sensitivity of 52.81% and a specificity of 78.67% (p<0.001) (figure 2).

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis for low HGB

In HF patients suffering from IHD, the univariate and multivariate regression analyses showed that age and log NT-proBNP were associated with low HGB (all p<0.05). The tertiles of PAGln were correlated with low HGB by the univariate analysis (p<0.05). After adjustment for age and log NT-proBNP, the tertiles of PAGln remained associated with low HGB (OR, 2.610; 95% CI: 1.251 to 5.447; p=0.011). Moreover, when comparing the highest tertile to the lowest tertile of serum PAGln, a significant 5.763-fold increased odds of low HGB (OR, 6.763; 95% CI: 1.553 to 29.453; p=0.011) (table 2 and online supplemental table 1).

Receiver-operating characteristic curve analysis of PAGln for low HGB

In HF subjects with IHD, the optimal cut-off point, identified by maximising Youden’s index based on the predicted probabilities from a logistic regression model using age and log NT-proBNP to predict low HGB, was 0.568 and the AUC was 0.799 (CI: 0.698 to 0.901; sensitivity 77.78%, specificity 79.03%; p<0.001). Meanwhile, the optimal cut-off point, determined by maximising Youden’s index based on the predicted probabilities from a logistic regression model that included age, log NT-proBNP and PAGln tertiles to predict low HGB, was 0.610 and the AUC was 0.852 (CI: 0.765 to 0.939; sensitivity 85.19%, specificity 75.81%; p<0.001). The inclusion of PAGln tertiles significantly improved the predictive value of the model, as demonstrated by a more clinically meaningful ROC curve (AUC: 0.852 vs 0.799, p=0.022) (figure 3).

Prognostic value of PAGln

To evaluate the prognostic significance of PAGln levels in chronic HF, a cohort of 81 patients was followed up for an average period of 9.09±4.45 months with 8 patients lost to follow-up. The analysis concentrated on composite endpoint events associated with HF within 1 year after discharge, encompassing CV mortality and the first readmission for HF. Among the initial cohort, eight patients who died during the initial hospitalisation were eliminated. Out of the remaining 73 HF patients, a total of 19 individuals (26.0%) experienced these composite endpoints, consisting of 19 (26.0%) readmissions for HF and 3 (4.1%) CV deaths. Then, the patients were classified based on the previous tertiles of PAGln values: group 1 (n=25, PAGln<516.35 ng/mL), group 2 (n=25, with PAGln ranging from ≥516.35 ng/mL to <945.37 ng/mL) and group 3 (n=23, PAGln≥945.37 ng/mL). The incidence of composite endpoints progressively rose from group 1 to group 3, at rates of 4.0%, 32.0% and 43.5%, respectively (p=0.006). Kaplan-Meier survival curves also depicted a gradual ascent in the correlation between PAGln tertiles and HF endpoints (log-rank p=0.003) (figure 4).

The patients were redivided into two groups based on a binary classification of PAGln values: a low group (n=37, PAGln<717.20 ng/mL) and a high group (n=36, PAGln≥717.20 ng/mL). Discussion

This study found increased PAGln levels in HF patients with IHD compared with controls. Moreover, among HF patients with IHD, elevated PAGln levels often co-occurred with anaemia and were associated with adverse HF outcomes.

We first observed that the elevated levels of PAGln were associated with the presence of HF with IHD, though not every HF with IHD patient had increased PAGln levels. PAGln is a by-product of the intestinal microbial catabolism of the essential amino acid phenylalanine. Phenylalanine is metabolised to phenylpyruvic acid and phenylacetic acid by intestinal flora. Subsequently, liver enzymes catalyse phenylacetic acid and glutamine to generate PAGln.18 19 When alterations in dietary habits, environmental factors, intestinal infections and other factors give rise to changes in the type and quantity of intestinal microbes in the adult, intestinal ecological disorders can be induced, causing inflammation and metabolic disorders, thereby promoting the development of chronic vascular diseases.20 Simultaneously, when the cardiac output of HF patients is decreased, leading to hypoperfusion and gastrointestinal congestion, intestinal ischaemia or oedema can also be triggered, resulting in variations in the composition, function, morphology and permeability of intestinal flora. Secondary bacterial translocation in the gut could also accelerate the systemic inflammatory response along with elevated circulating endotoxin levels. Then, the activated inflammatory cytokines further deteriorate HF.6 21 22

Subsequently, we observed that the serum PAGln levels were related to HGB reduction in HF patients with IHD. Previous research indicated that IHD constitutes the clinical manifestation of the progressive pathological process of chronic inflammation in coronary atherosclerosis.23 There is an interaction with increased gut permeability and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) release in the circulation with ensuing inflammation.2427 However, the in vitro study by Fu et al supports a PAGln-mediated pathway of inflammation and cardiac dysfunction, which differs from the LPS-induced pathway and has not been shown in vivo to be a direct effect of PAGln.9 It is found that elevated PAGln levels are frequently associated with a concurrent rise in TMAO levels which could also promote ischaemic HF via inflammation, histone methylation-mediated vascular fibrosis, platelet hyperactivity and thrombosis, as well as cholesterol accumulation and the activation of MAPK signalling.28 In our cohort, the TMAO levels were also increased following PAGln tertiles. Although their linkage is expected to be further validated by additional experiments, it is established that elevated PAGln levels in IHD patients may signal inflammatory alterations within the body.

The Anaemia of Inflammation theory, also known as Anaemia of Chronic Disease, is the second most prevalent type of anaemia globally (after iron deficiency anaemia) and represents the dominant form among hospitalised patients or those with chronic conditions.29 30 Anaemia inflammation is mediated by immune system activation, leading to dysregulated iron metabolism, suppressed erythropoiesis and shortened survival of RBCs.31 It should be noted that gut barrier disruption causes inflammation and anaemia, and PAGln by itself is a sort of diagnostic sign for a worse situation.

Finally, our prospective study showed that among HF patients afflicted with IHD, elevated serum PAGln levels were associated with increased risk of encountering HF-related endpoint events. This observation concords with previous findings that high levels of PAGln were associated with poor prognosis independent of traditional cardiac risk factors.11 Furthermore, our Cox regression analysis indicated that adjusted PAGln and low HGB were independently correlated with HF endpoint events. Several studies have shown that HF patients with anaemia exhibited greater deterioration of cardiac function and poor prognosis.32 33 It is possible that anaemia in HF is overwhelmingly characterised by impaired erythropoietin production, which is exacerbated with left ventricular EF, inflammation and disease severity.34

Clinical implications

It is the first study to investigate the association between serum PAGln levels and anaemia among HF patients with IHD. Understanding how PAGln relates to anaemia within the framework of HF with IHD may yield important implications for improving prevention and management approaches for these conditions.

Study limitations

We acknowledge that this study has several limitations. First, we did not gather any data on dietary patterns to analyse the impact of different dietary nutrients, particularly those rich in phenylalanine, on PAGln levels. Second, the study focused on HF patients with HFrEF or HFmrEF, and the applicability of PAGln as an indicator in HFpEF remains unexplored. Third, this exploration study represents small-scale exploratory research, lacking in-depth investigation of related mechanisms. Future research endeavours should aim to address these limitations by examining a more diverse range of HF-associated factors and incorporating them into a comprehensive assessment tool for managing HF patients with IHD.

Conclusions

An increase in circulating PAGln levels was associated with HF caused by IHD and was significantly associated with the presence of anaemia in this condition. Higher PAGln concentrations with low HGB could further predict an increased risk of adverse HF-related events. These results position PAGln as a promising biomarker for risk stratification in IHD-associated HF, warranting prospective validation to assess its utility in guiding patient management.
