Prediction of Moderate to Severe Delayed Chemotherapy‐Induced Nausea Using a Novel Algorithm Incorporating Individual Patient Factors
Authors: Alisha P Maity, Stephanie D Kjelstrom, Kathryn J Fleck, Erik L Zeger, Zonera A Ali, Arezoo Ghaneie, Aarti L Shevade, Paul B Gilman, Deric C Savior, U Margaretha Wallon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeChemotherapy‐induced vomiting and nausea (CINV) is difficult to accurately predict, especially in the delayed phase and can negatively affect a patient's' quality of life as well as reduce their motivation to continue lifesaving therapy. We have previously reported on a blood‐based assay that can be used to tailor an individualized antiemetic prophylaxis. The purpose of this current study was to validate our previous finding in a larger cohort of patients treated with a platinum‐containing regimen.MethodsBlood samples and patient reported outcomes from 202 cancer patients aged 18 years and older who started treatment with a platinum agent during February 15, 2016, through May 18, 2023, were analyzed. The glutathione recycling capacity in red blood cells was compared to reported nausea during the delayed phase and verified by comparison to notes in medical records. An algorithm for risk score prediction was generated and predicted risk scores were compared to observed delayed nausea severity.ResultsPatients with a low glutathione recycling capacity, indicating prolonged exposure to oxidative stress, were experiencing moderate/severe delayed chemotherapy‐induced delayed nausea more than those who had an efficient recycling capacity to offset the excess radical oxygen formed by chemotherapy. Calculated risk scores from participating patients demonstrated a good correlation between predicted and observed outcomes for the risk score groups.ConclusionRisk score, based on glutathione recycling and other intrinsic factors, can predict the risk of delayed nausea for patients undergoing chemotherapy, allowing for a more aggressive preventive treatment upfront to avoid or reduce delayed nausea symptoms.Keywords: chemotherapy‐induced nausea and vomiting, delayed nausea, oxidative stress, platinum agents, risk prediction

Introduction

Chemotherapy‐induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) is a common and distressing side effect for oncology patients [1, 2, 3]. Nausea and vomiting in these patients can be acute (≤ 24 h after treatment) or delayed (> 24 h after treatment) [4]. Unaddressed CINV affects patients' abilities to tolerate chemotherapy and can lead to worsening quality of life and performance status, anticipatory nausea, and result in dose reductions or early termination of chemotherapy [5]. The management of CINV is complex, as it is impacted by both the emetogenicity of the chosen chemotherapeutic agent and the occurrence of two distinct pathophysiological phases (acute and delayed) which can result in different outcomes and require distinctive treatment approaches [4, 6]. Therefore, organizations such as the Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer (MASCC) [7, 8], the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) [9], and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) [10] have generated guidelines to aid clinicians in optimal prophylaxis of CINV. These guidelines recommend that when oncologic treatments are chosen, the emetogenic potential of chemotherapeutic agents should be assessed so that providers can prescribe appropriate prophylactic antiemetic agents [10, 11].

Even with these guidelines, CINV remains an issue for many patients, prompting numerous investigations into potential risk factors for CINV, with the goal of determining a prophylactic strategy for medications. These factors include history of CINV with prior chemotherapy and/or perceived susceptibility to nausea, female sex, low chronic alcohol intake, and younger age [10, 11] and have been used to assess patients' risk for the development of CINV [12]. However, the patients' individual biology is not included in previous predictive algorithms.

The pathophysiology of CINV, and nausea especially, is not well understood, though one proposed mechanism suggests that CINV is triggered by free radicals in the small intestine induced by chemotherapy. These free radicals, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), cause release of serotonin and substance P from enterochromaffin cells [13, 14], subsequently activating their respective receptors and inducing nausea and vomiting [15].

Based on this mechanism, we have previously developed a hypothesis that a patient's risk of CINV is directly related to their efficiency in scavenging free radicals via glutathione (GSH), a major antioxidant, after exposure to chemotherapy. Specifically, we have focused on measuring recycling of GSH (GSHrc) as a mechanism by which cells detoxify the reactive oxygen species released after chemotherapy [16, 17]. In brief, the method is based on enzymatic conversion of the bioactive probe hydroxyethyl‐disulfide (HEDS). Due to reported single‐nucleotide polymorphisms among the enzymes in the recycling process [18], individual variation in the rate of GSHrc, and thereby detoxification, can affect ROS levels and in extension release of neurotransmitters which activate the emetic response [15, 19]. We have previously described preliminary results for our development of the glutathione recycling (GSHrc) assay and outlined its relationship to CINV [20, 21].

We developed a predictive model for chemotherapy‐induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) using data from 202 patients who received platinum‐based chemotherapy for colorectal, lung, esophageal, head and neck, and other non–hormone‐driven malignancies. These cancer types were selected to ensure balanced representation by sex and inclusion of all three commonly used platinum agents—carboplatin, cisplatin, and oxaliplatin. Our prospective analysis led to the creation of a risk score to estimate individual CINV risk. This model may support clinicians in making personalized decisions for the prevention and management of CINV.

Study Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to develop a predictive model for chemotherapy‐induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) that integrates both clinical risk factors and a patient‐specific biological measure of glutathione recycling (GSHrc). We aimed to generate a practical risk score that can stratify patients by their likelihood of developing CINV after platinum‐based chemotherapy. With the larger cohort, we could utilize machine learning techniques, allowing for the inclusion of more variables to improve personalized prophylaxis and optimize supportive care for patients undergoing chemotherapy.

Methods

Patient Cohort

Our prospective observational study was performed as a single‐center investigation conducted at Lankenau Medical Center through a collaboration between the Alliance Cancer Specialists oncology clinic and the Lankenau Institute for Medical Research (LIMR). A total of 428 patients with a cancer diagnosis who initiated treatment with chemotherapy between February 15, 2016, and May 18, 2023, were enrolled in the study as approved by the local Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Reference ID: F/N‐R12‐3146 L). Applying exclusion criteria (Figure 1) and removing patients lost to follow‐up resulted in a cohort of 202 patients with analyzable data. Patients across all socioeconomic groups, races, and ethnicities were encouraged to participate. However, the population in our health system area is predominantly Black and White, with approximately 10% identifying as Hispanic, Asian, or other. Written and signed informed consent was obtained from all patients. Adults (> 18 years old) of both sexes were permitted following the protocol, and all patients had to be chemotherapy naive. Patients with a recurrence or a second primary were not enrolled in this study. All patients with a solid tumor and receiving a platinum‐based therapy were treated according to current medical standards; the medical staff had exclusive authority in recommending therapy based on stage of disease and/or co‐morbidities. Patients treated with a highly emetogenic chemotherapy (HEC) [5, 10] including cisplatin, were given 0.25 mg palonosetron IV, aprepitant 150 mg IV, and 10 or 20 mg dexamethasone IV prior to chemotherapy (all antiemetic regimens given are described in Table 1). Most patients treated with a carboplatin‐containing regimen (85%) received the agent at Area Under the Curve (AUC) 2, which is considered a moderately emetogenic chemotherapy (MEC) [10] and given with a 2‐drug antiemetic preventive treatment (0.25 mg palonosetron IV and 10 or 20 mg dexamethasone IV) [10, 22]. Oxaliplatin was given at either 85 mg/m2 or 130 mg/m2 depending on the regimen. This patient group received the same antiemetic regimen as patients treated with carboplatin AUC 2. All patients were given prescriptions to fill for ondansetron and prochlorperazine if treated with oxaliplatin or carboplatin (AUC < 4). Patients treated with cisplatin or carboplatin (AUC ≥ 4) were prescribed the same agents with the possible addition of olanzapine. All prescriptions were for pro re nata (PRN) use during the 24–96 h post‐treatment, and instructions were given by the medical oncology staff for dosing frequency.

Blood Samples and Patient‐Reported Outcomes

Each patient donated a tube of blood (12 mg K2EDTA) the day of their first treatment. Blood samples were collected prior to antiemetics and chemotherapy, at the time medically necessary samples were obtained to determine eligibility for chemotherapy and were subsequently transferred for research analysis.

Blood samples were analyzed within 1–2 h of collection using the glutathione recycling kit (Mynari Biomedical LLC) and mixed with the bioactive probe HEDS which is converted into mercaptoethanol (ME) through the enzymatic activities of the glutathione recycling pathway [20, 21, 23]. The amount of ME produced was determined by adding Ellman's reagent to clarified reaction mixtures and measured at 412 nm in a spectrophotometer. Glutathione recycling (GSHrc) capacity was calculated by converting read‐outs to ME per millions of red cells [20, 21]. GSHrc results obtained from chemotherapy‐naïve patients were used to predict the occurrence of delayed nausea during any of the subsequent planned treatment cycles.

Patients self‐reported nausea symptom and severity using the Rotterdam Symptom Checklist (RSCL) [24] directly prior to each cycle. Questionnaires were distributed and collected by a study coordinator, who was available to address study‐related questions.

Delayed nausea was defined as symptoms occurring 24–120 h following chemotherapy. All patients included in the analysis were prospectively followed for the full duration of their planned treatment course. No changes to antiemetic regimens were made prior to the first report of delayed nausea.

Nausea severity was dichotomized into two groups, none or mild and moderate or severe. Mild nausea corresponds to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade 1, for which no intervention was required. Moderate and severe nausea corresponded to CTCAE grades 2 and 3, respectively, which necessitate changes in management or hospitalization. Reports of nausea were compared to those documented in the electronic medical records.

Statistical Methods

Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using two‐sample t‐tests, chi‐square tests of independence, and univariable logistic regression to display the effect size of each variable. To incorporate more predictors than the original binary cut‐point identified using CART, elastic net regression was used to build predictive models and a risk score. Elastic net regression with a logit link function was applied to estimate optimal coefficients while minimizing the risk of overfitting. By combining the penalty terms from both LASSO (L1) and Ridge (L2) regression, elastic net enhances model generalizability and improves predictive performance [25]. The model incorporating the GSHrc score, age, sex, ethnicity, and treatment type was developed using Elastic Net regression. To assess calibration, the dataset was divided into ten groups based on predicted probabilities. The observed outcome incidence was then plotted against the mean predicted probability for each group. Calibration was further quantified using the expected‐to‐observed ratio (E:O) to detect under‐ or over‐estimation of risk, the calibration slope to assess overfitting or underfitting, and the calibration‐in‐the‐large (CITL) to evaluate systematic over‐ or under‐prediction. Model discrimination was measured using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). In addition, 95% confidence intervals for slope and CITL were calculated using logistic regression with predicted probabilities as the only predictor, and for AUC using Stata's roctab command.

For internal validation, both 10‐fold cross‐validation and bootstrap cross‐validation were employed. The 10‐fold cross‐validation assessed out‐of‐sample performance by partitioning the data into ten subsets, iteratively training the model on nine parts and validating on the remaining part, and bootstrap cross‐validation was used to estimate model optimism without partitioning the dataset, improving the robustness of internal validation. Validation metrics included the root mean square error (RMSE) and the AUC to reflect both prediction error and discriminative ability.

Once the model was validated, a risk score was constructed using the penalized coefficients from the Elastic Net regression. All continuous variables were standardized by Stata before fitting the Elastic Net model. Penalized coefficients were then transformed back to the original scale for the predictors for model presentation and risk‐score calculation. Each score was converted to a predicted probability using the formula: Predicted Probabilities = 1 (1 + exp. (−Risk Score)). The predicted probabilities from the risk score were then validated to ensure they matched the predictive model using a univariable logistic regression model, with nausea severity as the outcome. The risk score probabilities' discrimination and calibration were assessed by calculating the area under the AUC, slope, CITL, and O:E. These metrics matched the original elastic net model metrics. These probabilities were used to estimate the risk of moderate to severe nausea and to identify the corresponding predicted probability risk thresholds. Based on these thresholds, patients were categorized into low, medium, or high‐risk groups. All analyses were conducted using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) and R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

After applying exclusion criteria (see Figure 1), the analyzed cohort consisted of 202 patients who had a median age of 66 years (range 25–91 years) and follow‐up information for the planned regimen. Younger patients were significantly more likely to report moderate/severe delayed nausea (p < 0.0001, Table 2) than older patients. Enrolled patients predominantly identified as White (81.7%) with Black patients constituting 16.8% of the cohort and the remaining 1.5% representing all other races. Race did not significantly contribute to delayed nausea (p = 0.745, Table 2). Forty‐six percent of participants were female and no significant difference in delayed nausea severity was observed between the sexes (p = 0.485, Table 2).

Approximately half of all patients were treated with an oxaliplatin‐containing regimen and 32% of patients in this treatment group reported moderate/severe nausea. Moderate‐to‐severe nausea was reported by 29.6% and 6.7% of patients receiving cisplatin or carboplatin, respectively. Patients reporting moderate/severe nausea were more likely to have been treated with oxaliplatin than the other two agents (Table 2).

Results from blood testing revealed that those with moderate/severe nausea had a lower mean GSHrc score (1.58 ± 1.41 (SD) vs. 1.76 ± 1.67 (SD)), though the blood assay as a single variable did not reach a statistically significant p‐value (p = 0.06).

The Elastic net regression identified GSHrc score, age, female, race (non‐white), carboplatin, and cisplatin as coefficients in the model. Discussion

In this study, we present an improvement of our previously published findings on a blood‐based test designed to predict individual risk of delayed chemotherapy‐induced nausea. Elastic net modeling allowed for the inclusion of more variables and use of predicted probabilities on a continuous scale, resulting in higher accuracy in prediction of delayed chemotherapy‐induced nausea. The performance of the prediction algorithm increased the AUC to 0.809 compared to the AUC of 0.716 for the binary cut‐off analysis.

Despite advances in the prevention of chemotherapy‐related side effects, CINV—particularly delayed nausea—remains a distressing and costly complication of cancer therapy [4, 6, 19, 26]. Accurately predicting which patients will develop delayed CINV remains a major clinical challenge [26, 27]. Identifying those at highest risk enables clinicians to apply aggressive prophylactic measures selectively, avoiding overtreatment of low‐risk patients. In addition to the considerable reduction in patients' quality of life [19], the average national cost for an emergency department visit due to severe delayed nausea has been estimated at up to $15,120 [28, 29, 30]. Consequently, multiple intrinsic (e.g., sex, age, race) and extrinsic (e.g., alcohol use, diet, smoking) risk factors have been investigated for their predictive value in CINV [31, 32, 33, 34]. In our work, we excluded variables were the patients might be reluctant to provide accurate habits, such as alcohol consumption and dietary preferences. A strong prediction factor for CINV is previous CINV symptoms during previous cycles. Therefore, all patients were chemotherapy naïve at the time enrollment.

Oxidative stress generated by chemotherapeutic agents in the upper small intestine has been proposed as a key mechanism underlying nausea and vomiting [6, 15]. Based on this rationale, we developed the GSHrc assay, which previously demonstrated promising sensitivity and specificity in identifying at‐risk patients using a CART algorithm [20, 21]. In the present study, we enrolled patients with solid tumors expected to benefit clinically from platinum‐based therapy. To minimize sex‐related bias—since sex is a known determinant of nausea risk [31, 32]—we focused on non–sex‐specific cancers, yielding a balanced cohort of 202 patients (46% female; mean age, 66 years).

Cut‐point analysis performed in Stata 17.0 using the Lui option [35] replicated our earlier observation: patients who developed moderate or severe delayed nausea had lower pre‐treatment GSHrc scores compared to those with no or mild symptoms (1.58 vs. 1.76, p = 0.06). Although not statistically significant, this trend supports the GSHrc score as a potential predictor of delayed CINV.

Next, we evaluated established risk factors to determine whether combining intrinsic factors could enhance predictive accuracy. Consistent with prior reports identifying younger age as a risk factor [12, 31, 36, 37], patients who experienced moderate/severe nausea were significantly younger than those with milder symptoms (54.2 vs. 67.3 years, p < 0.0001). Chemotherapy type was also associated with nausea severity: patients treated with the moderately emetogenic compound oxaliplatin [5, 10] accounted for 71.1% of moderate/severe cases (p < 0.0001).

Using a validated model, we generated a composite risk score by applying coefficients derived from Elastic Net regression [25] to their respective variables, as described in the Results section. This risk score was then validated through univariable logistic regression using nausea severity as the outcome.

Among cisplatin‐treated patients (N = 27), 29.6% reported grade 2/3 nausea, representing 17.8% of all moderate/severe cases. As expected for a highly emetogenic chemotherapy (HEC) [5, 10], all cisplatin‐treated patients received standard triple antiemetic prophylaxis with a 5‐HT3 antagonist (palonosetron), an NK1 antagonist (aprepitant), and dexamethasone. In contrast, carboplatin (N = 75)—administered at AUC 2 (MEC) in 80% and AUC 5–6 (HEC) in 20% of patients [5, 10]—was associated with significantly lower rates of delayed nausea (6.7% overall, constituting 11.1% of grade 2/3 cases). Notably, oxaliplatin‐treated patients (N = 100) exhibited the highest incidence of delayed nausea (32%), accounting for the majority (71%) of grade 2/3 events (p < 0.0001). This finding aligns with observations by Hesketh et al. [38], who reported delayed nausea in 42.9% of oxaliplatin‐treated patients despite excellent acute‐phase CINV control using a two‐drug prophylaxis (5‐HT3 antagonist plus dexamethasone).

Predicted probabilities derived from our model were categorized into low‐, medium‐, and high‐risk groups (Table 3) and compared with observed outcomes (Figure 3). Patients with risk scores ≥ 60 were correctly classified as low risk, indicating adequate protection from current standard antiemetic regimens. Scores of 45–59 corresponded to a 25%–50% risk, consistent with previously reported rates of nausea among patients receiving MEC or HEC therapy despite guideline‐concordant prophylaxis [3, 39, 40, 41]. Clinical trials suggest that these patients benefit from adding an additional antiemetic agent during subsequent treatment cycles [5, 40]. In contrast, patients with risk scores ≤ 40 exhibited a predicted 70% and observed > 80% risk of developing delayed nausea, indicating the need for more intensive antiemetic prophylaxis. Our findings, consistent with prior reports [38], underscore that oxaliplatin‐treated patients contribute disproportionately to delayed nausea incidence when treated with the currently recommended two‐agent regimen.

Current prophylaxis guidelines for CINV are largely determined by the emetogenicity of the chemotherapy regimen [10]. Although major oncology organizations [9, 10, 42, 43] agree on best practices for both acute and delayed CINV prevention, nausea—more frequent and impactful than vomiting—remains insufficiently controlled. This challenge is compounded by the subjective nature of nausea assessment, in contrast to the objective measurement of vomiting frequency and duration. Incorporating objective biomarkers such as GSHrc, alongside intrinsic factors, may enable a more personalized and precise risk prediction for delayed nausea, reducing bias from patient‐reported measures.

Given the differing biological half‐lives of platinum agents, glutathione recycling may play a significant role in patient susceptibility. Carboplatin and cisplatin exhibit half‐lives of 1–2 days, whereas oxaliplatin persists for approximately 5 days. Thus, patients with slower GSHrc rates receiving oxaliplatin may experience prolonged oxidative stress, leading to sustained enterochromaffin cell injury and neurotransmitter release. Such patients may benefit from extended or intensified antiemetic coverage after infusion. Our future goal for the project is to determine the effect of additional antiemetics and the effects on severity and duration of symptoms. Furthermore, the GSHrc test could promote adherence to prescribed oral antiemetics by providing an objective rationale for their continued use.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary teaching hospital located in a suburb of Philadelphia, PA, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, the outcome measures were based on patient self‐reports. Although we compared patient‐reported outcomes with documentation of nausea symptoms in medical records and adjustments to antiemetic regimens, recall and reporting bias may still have influenced the results due to memory limitations and the time constraints of clinical documentation. Additionally, while all patients received antiemetic therapy consistent with the standard of care—based on the agent with the highest emetogenic potential—and no modifications were made to regimens prior to reported nausea symptoms (as described in the Methods section), we cannot be certain about the extent to which patients used their prescribed PRN antiemetics. Patients who were predicted as medium/high risk of developing delayed CINV but never reported symptoms could have been compliant with their antiemetics and therefore able to prevent any experience of CINV. Third, no external validation was performed to assess the generalizability of the findings to other populations or clinical settings. Although statistical evaluation of the risk score suggests that the findings may be reproducible in similar patient cohorts, a dedicated validation study is needed to confirm these results and reduce potential sources of bias. Future prospective studies are recommended to include a more diverse racial distribution and collect more detailed treatment data, particularly regarding the use of rescue medications.

Implications of Findings

Delayed chemotherapy‐induced nausea remains a major unmet need in supportive oncology care, frequently persisting despite guideline‐recommended antiemetic prophylaxis. Existing risk models, based primarily on demographic and treatment‐related factors, offer limited predictive accuracy. This study introduces a blood‐based glutathione redox cycling assay as an objective biomarker to identify patients at high risk for delayed nausea following platinum‐based chemotherapy. When integrated with clinical variables, GSHrc produced a validated risk score that stratified patients into low‐, intermediate‐, and high‐risk groups, with clear implications for individualized prophylaxis. High‐risk patients—regardless of the emetogenicity of the chemotherapeutic regimen—showed poor symptom control under standard antiemetic protocols and may benefit from intensified preventive therapy. The addition of an NK1 antagonist as a third agent in the antiemetic mixture prior to chemotherapy infusion or a transdermal patch for long‐term delivery of a 5‐HT3 antagonist could be effective approaches to eliminate delayed nausea among the high‐risk patients. Future prospective studies using these agents among high‐risk patients will be needed to ensure efficacy. Incorporating redox‐based biomarkers into CINV risk assessment may enable precision‐guided prevention, enhance patient quality of life, and reduce healthcare utilization associated with uncontrolled nausea.

Conclusions

Integrating individual patient biology into a multifactorial predictive algorithm enhances the accuracy of identifying those at risk for delayed chemotherapy‐induced nausea. This personalized approach enables clinicians to proactively deliver intensified antiemetic therapy to high‐risk patients, thereby reducing delayed nausea, improving quality of life, and supporting adherence to potentially life‐saving chemotherapy regimens.
