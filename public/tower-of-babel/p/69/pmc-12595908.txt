Medicinal plant use in a context of coloniality: Congolese perspectives on lost and preserved healing traditions in Belgium
Authors: Emiel De Meyer, Sarah Derveeuw, Césarine Sinatu Bolya, Eduardo de la Peña, Melissa Ceuterick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Coloniality refers to patterns of power that emerged as a result of colonialism beyond the strict limits of colonial administration. Members of the Congolese community in Belgium -being the formerly colonized in the country of the former colonizer- live in an obvious context of coloniality. Medicinal plant use is common among the Congolese community in Belgium, and cultural forces play a significant role in the health and healthcare beliefs and perceptions of minoritized groups. Therefore, our main objective was to understand the lived experiences of the Congolese community in Belgium regarding their use of medicinal plants, with a focus on the influence of coloniality dynamics and cultural transmission in a postcolonial setting. We conducted 22 semi-structured interviews with people of Congolese heritage in Belgium and analysed the data using reflexive thematic analysis. Medicinal plant use dynamics were found to be deeply influenced by experiences of living in the context of coloniality. Our results suggest that in contexts where migrant and diaspora communities are minoritized, subject to discrimination and/or racism, and particularly where host countries are shaped by or continue to be influenced by a colonial past, the preservation of traditions, cultural concepts, habits, and belief systems regarding health and healthcare, including perceptions of medicinal plant knowledge and use, cannot be understood without acknowledging the context of coloniality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-025-00830-z.

Introduction

Theoretical framework: Coloniality and epistemic injustice

Coloniality refers to patterns of power that emerged as a result of colonialism and continue to shape intersubjective relations, culture, politics, labour, and knowledge production beyond the strict limits of colonial administration [1]. Coloniality can thus be seen as a remnant, or a continuation of the legacy of colonialism [1, 2]. One aspect of coloniality is the coloniality of knowledge, which refers to the epistemic hegemony of European perspectives (i.e. Eurocentrism) that originated during the colonial period and persists today. It highlights how Western ways of knowing and understanding, or epistemologies, have been privileged over other forms of knowledge, leading to the marginalization, suppression, and silencing of non-western epistemologies [3–5]. The coloniality of knowledge, by definition results in epistemic injustice toward all non-Western knowledge systems, disadvantaging those who hold such knowledge in their capacity as knowers [6, 7].

According to Fricker [6], epistemic injustice refers to the systematic undermining of individuals or groups in their capacity as knowers, whereby their credibility, access to, or participation in the production and circulation of knowledge is unjustly constrained [6]. It comprises two forms: testimonial and hermeneutical injustice. Testimonial injustice arises when a speaker’s credibility is unjustly undermined due to prejudice, leading others to discount their statements. Hermeneutical injustice occurs when people do not have access to the shared concepts or frameworks needed to make sense of, or express, their social experiences, which puts them at a structural disadvantage. For example, this occurs when groups with a migration background experience culturally bound syndromes that are not recognized or well understood in the cultural context to which they have migrated. Coloniality and consequent epistemic injustice extend towards communities of non-Western heritage in Western settings, which can both be considered part of the racialization of these communities, resulting in racism, discrimination, and prejudice [6, 8, 9].

The Democratic Republic of Congo and the Congolese community in Belgium

The case of the Democratic Republic of Congo demonstrates how colonial structures institutionalized forms of epistemic injustice that continue to affect Congolese populations both in the DRC and in Belgium. The present-day Democratic Republic of Congo (henceforth DRC) was colonized from 1885 to 1908 by Leopold II, King of the Belgians, and was administered as his private property. Under international pressure because of the atrocities committed against and general mistreatment of the Congolese people, the so-called Congo Free State was annexed by the Belgian parliament in 1908, which took over its administration as the colony of Belgian Congo. In the late 1950s, pro-independence movements negotiated independence with the Belgian government, and in 1960 the country became independent under the leadership of President Joseph Kasavubu and Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba [10–12]. Atrocities against the Congolese Indigenous populations during the colonial period are well documented (see e.g. [10]). After independence, with Lumumba’s assassination marking a key point in the growing instability, DRC’s governance has been and remains characterized by political exclusion, instability, corruption, conflict, and war (see e.g. [13, 14]). We refer to Gondola (2002) [15] and Van Reybrouck [11] for a comprehensive elaboration of DRC’s history.

Only after independence did migration from Congo to Belgium become possible. Starting in 1960, these post-independence migratory flows have grown into a community of about 108,000 people of Congolese descent residing in Belgium as of 2024 (Figures from the National Register, compiled by Statbel, edited and calculated by Myria) (personal communication, 2025). It is now the largest group with a migratory background of sub-Saharan African origin in Belgium [16]. The Congolese community was originally, and regarding social and business activities still partly is, concentrated in the Matongé neighbourhood of Brussels, where the first immigrants resided. Although people of Congolese descent in Belgium have, on average, a higher level of education than other groups with a migration background, they have a disproportionally high unemployment rate, which is at least partly related to the fact that they still face direct and indirect discrimination and racism in (and regardless of) the labour market [1, 17]. The Congolese community in Belgium, being the formerly colonized in the land of their former colonizer, is thus living in an obvious context of coloniality.

Today, the Congolese community in Belgium includes both Belgian-born children whose parents originally immigrated from Congo (i.e., the second generation) and Congolese migrants, some of whom have lived longer in Belgium than they have in their country of birth. However, first-generation Congolese who were born in Congo and later immigrated to Belgium in adulthood still make up the largest proportion of this community. This is important, as first-generation groups tend to at least partially maintain their cultural concepts and customs regarding health, disease, and medicine, including strategies for seeking healthcare, after migration [18, 19]. In many low-income settings, healthcare relies predominantly on plants and plant-based products. As a result, medicinal plants constitute an essential component of healthcare systems for populations in these regions. Following migration, the use of medicinal plants often persists among individuals with a migratory background [20, 21]. Several possible reasons have been put forward to explain the continued use of medicinal plants by migrants and their descendants. Among others, it has been explained as a strategy to seek identity, a way to treat culturally bound syndromes, a coping strategy for people in precarious socioeconomic positions, and a health-conscious consumer choice [19, 22].

Although Belgium has a highly accessible biomedical healthcare system [23, 24], a part of the population of Congolese descent in Belgium sees, uses and even prefers medicinal plants as a primary healthcare source. The trade, use and knowledge of medicinal plants is omnipresent in Belgium, and centred in the Matongé neighbourhood of Brussels. However, most of the trade in and use of herbal medicine among the Congolese community in Belgium occur underground, remain hidden to the public eye, and discussing it with outsiders remains taboo [19, 25–27].

Furthermore, communities with a migration background seem to reshape and adapt their use of medicinal plants to the new environment, in which, besides accessibility, cultural forces play a significant role in the adaptation mechanism [25, 28]. Several studies reported that traditional knowledge and practices, including medicinal plant knowledge and use, in a migration context is at least partially determined by social dynamics and migrants’ perceptions within the migration context [12, 22, 25]. In the context of COVID-19, De Meyer et al. [25] report that people of Congolese descent in Belgium used medicinal plants out of mistrust in biomedical solutions and governmental communications. Van Damme et al. [27] further report that gender roles and stigmatization play a role in the use of plants in the context of sexual health among people of Congolese descent in Belgium.

However, the impact of coloniality dynamics on traditional knowledge and practices in Western urban settings remains largely unknown [25]. Gaining insights into these dynamics is not only essential for unravelling the mechanisms of biocultural knowledge preservation and transmission but also crucial for understanding healthcare choices and the broader health and healthcare dynamics of people with a migration background in Western settings.

The main objective of this study is to understand the lived experiences of the Congolese community in Belgium regarding their use of medicinal plants, with a focus on the influence of coloniality dynamics and cultural transmission in a postcolonial setting. In doing so, we specifically seek to address the following research question:-How does the context of coloniality shape medicinal plant practices, transmission of knowledge, and healthcare choices among the Congolese community in Belgium?

Materials & Methods

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted between October 2019 and February 2023. The interviews analysed in this paper were originally conducted as part of a broader research project on medicinal plant use among Congolese communities in urban contexts of the Democratic Republic of Congo and Belgium. The semi-structured interview guide was designed to elicit open-ended accounts of participants’ experiences in relation to that topic, but participants’ responses often ranged more widely. This richness enabled us to explore the current research question in a secondary analysis. We used purposive sampling to obtain maximum variation in age, gender, professional background, and place of residence [29]. Inclusion criteria for potential participants were: having a minimum age of 18 years, having at least one parent of Congolese (DRC) descent, primarily residing in Belgium, and self-declaring an affinity for the use of plants in a medicinal context. Initial participants were sought by approaching individuals and non-profit organizations both online and in real-life in the Matongé quarter. Further participants were sought through snowball sampling [30]. All interviews took place in person at a location of the participant’s preference. Verbal informed consent was obtained prior to each interview, and the purpose of the study was explained to the participants. Verbal consent was used initially to build trust. Written informed consent was obtained after the interviews, once participants had agreed to the use of their data. To minimize bias related to the COVID-pandemic, we excluded interviews conducted online during lockdowns. All interviews were audio recorded, fully transcribed, and anonymized. Open-ended questions were asked on which medicinal plants participants knew, and used in Belgium, why they used these plants, and how herbal and traditional medicine were perceived compared to pharmaceuticals. During the development of the methodologies and after the first interviews, we organized informal one-on-one feedback sessions with participants to co-produce interview guides and recruit initial participants. All interviews were conducted by the first author in either Dutch or French, depending on the participant’s preference. As key participant, the third author played a coordinating role in recruiting participants and served as an interpreter of the data during the analysis and writing of the manuscript.

Interviews

Twenty-two qualitative, semi-structured interviews using open-ended questions were conducted with people of Congolese descent in Belgium. Nine interviews were conducted online during the COVID-19 lockdowns and were excluded from the data analysis for this manuscript to avoid bias related to the acute prominence of the pandemic. Interviews lasted between 30 min and 4.5 h and were conducted in Dutch or French, depending on the participant’s preference. Thirteen men and eight women participated in the study. The age of participants ranged between 24 and 70 years old, and their average age was 46.2 years. Participants’ profiles are presented in Table 1. Sixteen participants were first-generation Congolese immigrants in Belgium, and their residence time in Belgium ranged from 5 to 62 years. Five participants were born in Belgium to one or two Congolese parents. Six participants were retired, one was unemployed, two owned Africa-oriented grocery stores, and the others had various jobs in the secondary and tertiary sectors. Data analysis

Interview transcripts were analysed in the language in which they were conducted (i.e. Dutch or French). We conducted a reflexive thematic analysis [31]. Transcripts were coded in several stages following Braun and Clarke [31, 32]. Design themes were discussed with all authors in an iterative process and led to four final overarching themes and four subthemes. Transcripts were translated into English for use as quotations in this paper.

Results

Theme 1: the context of coloniality: to be Congolese in Belgium

When talking about the context in which they were living, participants referred to “Europe” more broadly instead of Belgium specifically and referred to the DRC or Africa when referring to the context of their heritage. Nearly all participants shared feeling a sense of displacement in Belgium, describing encounters in which they were made to feel like outsiders. This sentiment was not only expressed by first-generation immigrants, some of whom held Belgian nationality, but also by second-generation individuals born in Belgium. They described instances of being treated as “not truly Belgian”, as well as experiences of social isolation, discrimination in employment, and subtle forms of prejudice in everyday life because of their racial background. Some participants felt strongly enough about these experiences that they aspired to return to or move to the DRC, even if they were born in Belgium, as illustrated in the following quotes:I would like to go back to Congo later, to live there, to make a difference there. So yeah… I can’t keep fighting that, proving… that I am Belgian, you know? I want to live somewhere where I am fully accepted, and I think that place is Congo. But I love Belgium very much. Taken together, contemporary experiences of racism, marginalisation and the feeling of being “not truly Belgian” can therefore be considered lived expressions of coloniality: they reflect broader social patterns that echo the hierarchies established during the colonial era, when African identities were constructed as marginal to European modernity. The legacies of colonisation thus persist in these subtle exclusions that participants encounter today and their feelings of displacement can be understood as lived expressions of coloniality.

Theme 2: The (perceived) lack of value attributed to medicinal plants

Subtheme 2.1: Contesting the Western appropriation and exploitation of medicine

Many participants described a deep distrust of Western pharmaceutical industries and their intentions. In expressing this mistrust, they often referred to the Belgian government and pharmaceutical industries under a single umbrella. Several participants explicitly spoke of colonial era exploitation continuing in contemporary times, referencing, for example, how drug companies derive “chemical” medications from African plant molecules without reciprocating benefits to African communities. They believed that while these industries profit from traditional plant knowledge, they fail to acknowledge or properly credit the societies from which this knowledge originates. All participants stated that pharmaceutical companies prioritize profit over healing and emphasized that pharmaceuticals cause side effects that medicinal plants do not. Consequently, they questioned the continued use of pharmaceutical medicines when plants, already used in biomedical formulations, could be used directly to treat the same illnesses without side effects.They know. Very well, yes. Yes, what they know. They colonized Congo, so in a way, the studies on plants have already been done, there is a great knowledge in Belgium, similar to what we have. Understand that the pharmaceutical industry also uses them to create drugs because they have active ingredients to make the medications. So, in a way, they search in plants or in, you know, and then they transform them, they form alliances with other products…. It’s not my field, but it’s not that they lack knowledge, okay? Subtheme 2.2.: The story of soursop as example of governmental and institutional mistrust

One specific example that surfaced repeatedly was the case of soursop (Annona muricata L.). Many participants believed that, because the consumption of soursop fruits is an effective treatment or preventive for cancer, the fruits are deliberately confiscated at Belgian airports, and this is the reason why they cannot access it in Belgium. This was frequently framed as evidence of a system that restricts the free use of natural remedies in order to maintain the profitability of pharmaceuticals.Soursop, it’s a medicine for cancer. But when you bring it here to Europe, it gets blocked at the airport. Yes, they confiscate it from you. It’s ridiculous. All the medications that they know come from Africa. They forbid them from passing through the airport. Yes, fruits that they know have no other purpose, those can pass. But what is truly medicinal, it doesn’t pass. It’s not normal. However, no participant reported knowledge of European phytosanitary regulations governing the import of plant materials, suggesting a possible lack of awareness of the institutional or legal reasons behind confiscations. This perception that Western industries therefore profit from African plant knowledge while excluding Africans from its benefits may to some participants resonate with colonial histories of extraction, where resources and knowledge were appropriated without reciprocity.

Theme 3: Tension between faith and traditional healing

A clear tension surfaced between faith-based beliefs (often influenced by Christianity) and the practice of using traditional medicinal plants.

One group of participants felt torn because Christianity -a religion introduced during colonial times but that has since taken a life of its own through African-led and Pentecostal Christianity- historically often labelled traditional plant healing as “witchcraft” or “evil powers.” Participants state that even present-day churches and pastors often discourage congregants from consulting traditional healers, pitting religious devotion against longstanding medicinal practices.Yeah, there are many who heal with plants… You see, maybe I only have a superficial knowledge, but those people—they really know. You take them into a forest, and they immediately recognize which plant can heal. These are people who, when you bring them an illness, they know exactly what mixture to prepare for you. But we shouldn’t forget that nowadays, healers are viewed negatively. (…) You see? They heal, but it seems like spirits exist, you know? Congolese people have also become very involved in spirituality—churches, pastors… And these pastors are slowly putting it into people’s minds, including mine, that you shouldn’t go to a healer, that they use evil powers to heal with plants. Discussion

Living in the context of coloniality shapes how people of Congolese descent access healthcare and how they pass on health knowledge, including the use and transmittance of biocultural heritage in the form of medicinal plant use and knowledge. Coloniality takes shape in many different forms and had and still has contrasting influences on the dynamics of traditional medicinal plant knowledge and use. In general, participants either retreated into their own perceptions and practices of health and healthcare—prioritizing medicinal plant use as a primary form of care out of mistrust—or gradually abandoned this in favour of dominant Western healthcare options, with a biomedicine-centred approach being the most prominent. In the following sections, we first elaborate on the forms coloniality takes in perceptions and narratives of plant knowledge and use among people of Congolese descent, and then on how coloniality has influenced specific mechanisms underlying health perceptions and healthcare practices.

We found that coloniality emerged mostly in indirect ways in our participants’ narratives. Many participants, formally Belgian, described experiences of everyday racism and discrimination, and expressed feelings of “nonbelonging”. Such experiences affect minoritized groups in many, if not all, contexts globally and are particularly pronounced in postcolonial settings [33]. Participants shared lived experiences of still being perceived as “outsiders” which they linked directly or indirectly to Belgium’s colonial legacy. These everyday experiences of alienation in the former colonizing country illustrate how colonial structures continue to permeate social interactions. Second generation individuals aspiring to “go back” to the context of their parents before migration can be seen as a result of intergenerationally inherited migration trauma [34], further fuelled by lived experiences of colonial relations that persist across contemporary settings, including the labour market, social services, and daily interactions [1, 2]. This resonates with the concept of coloniality as the continuation of power structures that extend beyond formal colonial rule [1]. However, the indirect ways in which coloniality manifests make it difficult to grasp, interpret, and register unless the people who experience it are genuinely heard.

Many participants perceive that Western pharmaceutical industries have appropriated or profited from African plant knowledge while undervaluing and failing to credit Congolese expertise on these plants. There is a perception that pharmaceutical companies extract compounds from plants for modern day medicines. In this context, participants viewed pharmaceutical industries as exploitative entities that prioritize profit over healthcare and associated Western medical practices with this profit driven strategy. Additionally, they narrated that they were being prevented from using plant medicine outside this commercial circuit. The soursop (Annona muricata L.) narrative included confiscations at customs or bans, which participants perceived as deliberate efforts to block “natural cures”. Although phytosanitary and import regulations likely explain much of this phenomenon, participants interpret it within a broader context of institutional distrust and perceived conspiracies. Through this example, governmental involvement was explicitly linked to the exploitative behaviour of pharmaceutical industries. Although, A. muricata is native to Central and South America and was introduced elsewhere [35], it has been reported in pharmacopoeias in the DRC, albeit not for its alleged anticancerogenic properties [42]. Its recent popularity as an anticancer preventive and remedy among people of Congolese background is more likely attributable to the influence of online and social media, where it is widely promoted, coinciding with increasing scientific attention (see e.g., [46, 47]), than to a continuation of traditional healing practices [48].

This recurring anecdote exemplifies how coloniality informs interpretations of regulation: participants readily connect current import restrictions with long standing (neo-) colonial patterns of extraction. Furthermore, these narratives, while grounded in specific experiences, may also carry a symbolic resonance. In speaking of plants being extracted, restricted, or commodified, participants may also be articulating a deeper sense of themselves as carriers of knowledge and culture being similarly devalued or excluded within a Western context. In this way, plant narratives become a means of expressing broader feelings of erasure and resistance shaped by the enduring legacy of coloniality.

Participants’ acknowledgment of this “extraction” today points to the persistence of perceptions developed during colonial times, as well as to the continued biological documentation and exploitation of resources—another layer of the effects of coloniality. In a neocolonial context, it is not surprising that such perceptions exist, as the Democratic Republic of the Congo has existed—and continues to exist—as an extractive country. Today, it is considered by many foreign governments a region of interest due to its mineral wealth, and it remains the origin of interethnic and international conflicts [13]. This reinforces the perception that resources, including plants and plant knowledge, are continuously extracted for profit. The perceived neglect of associated Congolese knowledge is a clear example of testimonial epistemic injustice [6], whereby Congolese medicinal knowledge remains marginalized and unacknowledged in formal healthcare and research environments. In this context, the concept of the coloniality of knowledge [3, 4] is particularly relevant, as participants’ distrust of formal healthcare systems is not merely anecdotal—it stems from historical evidence of extractive practices and unequal power dynamics in knowledge production. The repeated invocation of plant extraction may serve as a narrative strategy and more accepted way of expressing broader disillusionment with institutions that have historically undermined or ignored Congolese voices. Speaking about the exploitation of plants thus becomes a way to speak about the exploitation of self. Annesley (2020) [35] alludes to this in noting that epistemic injustice in healthcare settings can lead to rational mistrust of the healthcare system and belief in health-related conspiracy theories. As a response, some participants turned to Congolese medicinal plant use and viewed it as the only “healthy” healthcare option. In doing so, they cultivated self-trust and epistemic agency as a form of resistance to epistemic injustice directed at this knowledge system [8, 37]. This aligns with earlier research showing how health-related distrust can flourish in systemically marginalized communities, a dynamic that was globally present but particularly pronounced among migrant communities during the COVID-19 pandemic [22, 25].

Although Christianity was introduced to the DRC in the fifteenth century, the enduring presence of Christian faiths, rooted in extensive Christianization practices during colonial rule in African societies and among communities of African descent, can be seen as a clear example of coloniality [38–40]. During colonial times in the DRC, traditional belief systems, including medical practices, were systematically marginalized through Christianisation [41]. The marginalization of ancestral practices during colonization did not end with the formal close of missionary presence, however. Instead, these dynamics were reconfigured in the rise of African Pentecostalism, which at once created more room for African spiritual expression as a response to colonial domination, while also intensifying the rejection of indigenous healing as witchcraft or demonic [43]. The interviews therefore reveal a fault line, where some modern-day churches, continue to brand traditional healing practices and plant use as deviant “witchcraft”. This resonates with findings elsewhere: Eves & Kelly-Hanku [44] describe how Pentecostal movements in Papua New Guinea intensify demands for rupture with vernacular therapies, framing them as “satanic” and insisting on exclusive reliance on Christian healing. Ademiluka [45] furthermore makes references to modern-day churches in Nigeria where the “teachings of the missionaries are upheld” and the use of traditional medicine is likened to heathenism. We therefore observe, far from breaking away from the past, contemporary and Pentecostal Christianity in Africa continues the colonial Christian project of delegitimizing ancestral healing, emerging in our findings as a major driver of knowledge loss and stigma.

At the same time, our findings add nuance to this literature. Several participants created a counternarrative that embraces the belief that God created medicinal plants precisely to help humanity. This blending of belief systems likely stems from the fact that in many regions of the DRC, herbal medicine remains the most accessible and sometimes the only available form of healthcare, and can be seen as an effort to reconcile spiritual legitimacy with cultural continuity, symbolically reaffirming the place of ancestral knowledge within a moral and religious framework (see e.g. [42]). This complexity underscores how religion can function both as a channel of Western colonial influence and as a source of spiritual legitimacy for traditional knowledge.

In contrast to those who show a stronger preference for medicinal plants as a primary source of healthcare due to mistrust of biomedical and governmental systems, some participants gradually abandoned their use of medicinal plants. Several participants openly stated that they do not transmit plant knowledge to their children, sometimes as a way to protect them from mockery. In addition, practical barriers such as the scarcity of suitable plants and uncertainty about correct dosages contributed to the partial abandonment of medicinal plant use in Belgium. This is particularly remarkable, given that earlier research has shown that knowledge about plant uses is quickly and easily transmitted online between Belgium and the DRC, and that newly emerging plant uses are rapidly shared across transnational networks [25, 48]. In this context, some participants stated that they do or would return to the DRC to receive what they consider to be proper treatment with plants. This suggests that, as a result of the dominant narrative surrounding Western healthcare, participants did not feel confident using medicinal plants in Belgium. This may be understood as a consequence of hermeneutical injustice and aligns with Fricker [6], who argues that one of the harms of epistemic injustice is that people lose confidence in their own capacity as knowers. Indeed, in contexts of migration, it has been shown that changes in environment, exposure to unfamiliar medical systems, and generational shifts significantly affect traditional medical knowledge [12, 18]. Figueiredo et al. [12] argue that patterns of cultural adoption among Congolese immigrants in Belgium are shaped by collective memories of colonialism. They found that individuals with more positive recollections of Belgian colonialism tend to adopt Belgian culture more readily while still preserving elements of Congolese culture, whereas those with more negative memories are less inclined to adopt Belgian culture and more focused on maintaining Congolese traditions. However, in our study, the abandonment of plant use was more often linked to a loss of confidence, practical barriers, and a form of self-protection as a result of epistemic injustice than to the deliberate adoption of Western healthcare influenced by positive collective memories of the colonial period.

The clear impact of testimonial and hermeneutical injustice toward members of the Congolese community and their medicinal plant knowledge systems on the dynamics of their plant use shows how the legacy of colonialism continues to shape health-related choices and trust in institutions within diasporic communities. These dynamics may have far reaching implications for the preservation of biocultural heritage and for healthcare among communities in migration contexts. The coloniality of knowledge and epistemic injustice may reinforce patterns of acculturation and the transformation of cultural practices. In this context, the influence of digital and social media, along with the emergence and rapid dissemination of both dominant and alternative narratives and trends, may further accelerate these dynamics and contribute to the marginalization of parallel knowledge systems [48, 49].

Medicinal plant use in Western urban settings has multiple times been reported to exist within a sphere of taboo [19, 21, 28]. Van Andel and Fundiko [19] argue that the medicinal plant trade in Matongé predominantly takes place informally to reduce costs and avoid taxes and oversight by authorities. However, we suggest that stigmatization and marginalization resulting from epistemic injustice may also play a role in this protective approach to medicinal plant trade and use, shielding it from outsiders.

Additionally, as participants turn away from or partially conceal their use of medicinal plants, they may lose access to valuable preventive or curative remedies. This could mean missing out on accessible, culturally rooted healthcare options and leaving plant use or preferences unaddressed during medical consultations. Conversely, exclusive reliance on plant remedies without proper information or guidance on their use and dosage may result in missed opportunities for receiving adequate healthcare or in harmful outcomes due to misinformed use (see e.g., [50]).

Conclusion

Diverse aspects of life in the context of coloniality have shaped the medicinal plant use repertoires of people of Congolese descent in Belgium. Some participants prioritized medicinal plants as their primary source of healthcare due to mistrust in pharmaceuticals and governmental systems. Others gradually abandoned their medicinal plant practices and actively ceased transmitting their knowledge in a broader cultural shift towards “modern” (Western) practices at the expense of ancestral knowledge.

Both dynamics have far-reaching consequences for culturally sensitive healthcare in Western settings and for the preservation of biocultural health heritage. In many contexts where migrant communities are minoritized groups subject to discrimination and/or implicit expressions of racism, and particularly where host countries are shaped by or continue to be influenced by a colonial past, the preservation of traditions, cultural concepts, habits, and belief systems—including perceptions of medicinal plant knowledge and use—cannot be understood without acknowledging the context of coloniality.
