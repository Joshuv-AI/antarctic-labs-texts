Using machine learning to develop Iraqi-specific spirometric reference equations
Authors: Walid Al-Qerem, Alaa Alsajri, Anan Jarab, Judith Eberhardt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpirometry is essential for diagnosing and managing respiratory diseases. Accurate interpretation relies on reference equations that reflect population-specific lung function. Global equations, such as those from the Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI), may not suit all populations, including Iraqis.ObjectiveTo develop sex-specific spirometric reference equations for healthy Iraqi adults using machine learning (ML) and compare their performance with the GLI 2012 and 2022 equations.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 3,959 healthy, nonsmoking Iraqi adults aged ≥18 years. Spirometry was performed per ATS/ERS guidelines. Five ML models (linear regression, random forest, support vector machine, gradient boosting machine (GBM), and k-nearest neighbors) were trained using age and height. Data were split into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets. Performance was assessed using RMSE, R2, and z-score calibration. GBM was selected as the best model.ResultsGBM outperformed all other models and GLI equations. In females, R2 was 0.4473 for FEV1 and 0.4519 for FVC; in males, 0.3509 and 0.3674, respectively. GLI equations underestimated lung volumes, while GBM predictions were well calibrated with mean z-scores near zero.ConclusionGBM-derived equations show improved accuracy and calibration over GLI standards for Iraqi adults, offering a more suitable tool for spirometry interpretation.

Introduction

Spirometry remains the mainstay of respiratory evaluation, providing objective measurement of lung function, particularly with the utilization of FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC ratio [1]. These measurements are critical for the diagnosis, staging, and follow-up of a range of respiratory conditions such as asthma, COPD, and restrictive lung disease [2]. The precise interpretation of spirometric findings relies on the application of appropriate reference equations that accurately reflect the population’s demographic and physiological conditions [3,4].

Respiratory illnesses are a significant public health burden, as these conditions are among the leading causes of mortality and morbidity worldwide, contributing to millions of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and deaths annually [5]. COPD is expected on its own to be the third leading cause of mortality worldwide. Furthermore, these conditions have a substantial impact on the patients’ quality of life [6].

In Iraq, respiratory diseases are also common and augmented by region-specific risk exposures, with a high prevalence of tobacco consumption, air pollution, occupational exposure, and long-term impacts of conflict-driven environmental degradation [7]. Recent population-based surveys in Iraqi adults have demonstrated trends in respiratory symptoms, asthma, and undiagnosed airflow limitations [8]. Spirometry is still underutilized, and there are no nationally referenced equations to provide accurate diagnosis and disease tracking. Using international references, for instance, from the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) [9,10], can lead to misclassification due to demographic, genetic, and environmental variations in populations [4]. Several studies have questioned the universal applicability of GLI reference equations in non-western populations [11–13]. Specifically, a study involving 738 healthy, nonsmoking Iraqi adults concluded that the GLI-2012 equations did not provide an adequate fit for this population, with z-score distributions and lower limit of normal (LLN) frequencies deviating significantly from the expected norms. This highlights the inappropriateness of using GLI-2012 equations for interpreting spirometric values in Iraqi adults [14], as the absence of locally derived reference norms represents a critical gap in the literature, not only for Iraq but for many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [15]. This gap perpetuates diagnostic inequity, as reliance on ill-fitting international standards can lead to the misclassification of disease and suboptimal patient care [15]. The development of such appropriate equations is highly relevant to current global health policy, aligning with calls from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the European Respiratory Society (ERS) for context-adapted lung-function standards to ensure more equitable and accurate respiratory diagnostics worldwide [16]. Such equations would not only improve clinical diagnostic accuracy for individuals but also support more reliable public-health surveillance of the national respiratory disease burden [6].

Traditionally, equations have been developed with generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS), which are flexible in their ability to model age-related variation and distribution properties of spirometric variables [17]. However, GAMLSS modeling requires specialist statistical knowledge, relies on high-level spline transformations, and typically invokes parametric distributions that do not completely describe variability in real-world populations [17]. This can make it more difficult to implement in routine clinical environments or resource-constrained contexts.

Machine learning (ML) may be a reasonable substitute for predictive modeling in medicine. ML methods are naturally data-driven, with the ability to learn complex and nonlinear relationships without the need for specified statistical distributions [18]. Evolving progress has validated the applicability of ML in healthcare, including diagnostic assistance and clinical risk stratification. In spirometry, there is potential for the simplification of reference equation formulation, preserving, or even improving predictive precision and responsiveness [19,20].

The main objective of this study was to derive sex-specific machine learning models for predicting FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC ratio in a representative Iraqi adult population. The performance of the developed equations was compared with the widely accepted reference standards, namely the GLI 2012 and 2022 models. Model fit, agreement between predicted and LLN values, and diagnostic agreement in terms of concordance were assessed between the different models. This study addresses the long-standing need for context-specific spirometry references for the Iraqi population and measures the promise of machine learning-based approaches in facilitating improved clinical interpretation of pulmonary function measures. This study hypothesized that the machine learning models trained on Iraqi data would demonstrate lower prediction error and better calibration than the GLI reference equations.

Methodology

This was a cross-sectional, community-based study conducted in two phases: the first phase aimed to develop spirometric reference equations using machine learning algorithms, while the second phase compared the performance and suitability of the created equation with other globally used equations, such as the Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) 2012 and GLI 2022 equations.

Participants

Lung function measurements were obtained from healthy Iraqi adults who had never smoked and had no history of chronic illness. Recruitment took place through personal outreach and various social media platforms. Participants’ smoking history and general health status were assessed using a structured survey administered in Arabic, the national language. The survey included an overview of the project and its objectives and was adapted from validated tools used in previous spirometry research. Data collection occurred between October 2022 and January 2025.

The screening questions on the survey were “Do you currently smoke?” “Do you live with someone who smokes?” “Are you an ex-smoker?” and “Do you have chronic illnesses?” Those who responded “yes” to any of these questions were excluded. Those with colds, influenza, or other acute respiratory infections within one month of data collection were also excluded, due to the potential impact on lung function and the risk of infection.

Participants were recruited from various governorates of Iraq across the northern, central, and southern regions. Participants were selected using a convenience-sampling approach, which was stratified by sex, age group, and geographic region. This strategy was designed to ensure proportional representation from Iraq’s northern, central, and southern governorates and included participants from both urban and rural communities. Recruitment sites included public areas, universities, hospitals, and community pharmacies, providing a mix of socioeconomic and occupational backgrounds. All necessary permissions were obtained from relevant institutions. The purpose of the study and any potential risks, such as dizziness or fatigue during testing, were clearly explained to each participant before data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion criteria were Iraqi nationality, age 18 years or older, good general health, nonsmoking status, and the ability to perform spirometry. Exclusion criteria included being under 18 years old; smoking or living with someone who smokes; a history of chronic respiratory or general illness (e.g., asthma, hypertension); recent surgery; pregnancy; or an inability to complete a valid spirometry maneuver. No exclusion was applied based on physical-activity level or sports participation, as the objective was to capture the full range of normal lung function among healthy, nonsmoking adults. This sampling approach provides a reasonably representative snapshot of the adult Iraqi population and is in alignment with methodologies applied in GLI equations [9,10].

Measurement of lung function

Lung function was evaluated using standard methods prescribed in the European Respiratory Society (ERS) and American Thoracic Society (ATS) [21]. Before testing, participants were asked to remove their shoes and socks to ensure accurate height measurements. The researcher maintained hygiene by washing their hands between each session.

Height was recorded in centimeters using a wall-mounted scale marked from 100 to 220 cm, and weight was recorded in kilograms on an electronic scale. Participants were seated upright on a firm chair and the spirometry procedure was described. They were instructed to begin by breathing normally, then take a deep breath in over one second, followed by a forceful exhalation until all air was expelled. To maximize effort, the researcher repeated the phrase, “Continue, continue, continue.” In line with standard infection control practices, each participant used a sterile, disposable mouthpiece to prevent cross-contamination.

Materials

The same electronic weighing scale, accurate to 100 g and with a maximum capacity of 200 kg, was used throughout the study to ensure consistent weight measurements. A fixed, wall-mounted measuring tape was used to obtain height measurements for enhanced accuracy. Spirometry was performed using an MIR Minispir spirometer (Medical International Research, Rome, Italy), which complies with ATS/ERS standards and was operated via WinspiroPRO software (version 8.5) [22]. The device provides a volume accuracy of ±3% or 50 mL, and a flow accuracy of ±5% or 200 mL/s. It has been used in numerous studies to assess lung function, and its reliability, portability, and ease of use in field settings make it a preferred option for population-based research [23]. Calibration verification was performed with a certified 3-L syringe at the start of each testing day and repeated after device relocation or notable room-temperature change, in line with ATS/ERS technical standards [21]. Biological control was conducted by testing a healthy reference subject at set intervals and comparing FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC to that individual’s baseline to detect drift or operator bias.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R software version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables were expressed as counts and proportions. A random 70% subset of the full sample was used to train the models, while the remaining 30% was reserved for external validation. Random sampling was stratified by sex and age to ensure comparable distributions across datasets. To assess the comparability of the training and validation sets, spirometric values were compared across age and height strata using independent t-tests. Bonferroni adjustments were applied to correct for multiple comparisons.

To create sex-specific predictive models for spirometric measures, five models were trained using distinct machine learning algorithms with age and height as predictors: linear regression (LM), random forest (RF), support vector machine with a radial kernel (SVM), gradient boosting machine (GBM), and k-nearest neighbors (KNN). All models were implemented using the caret package and optimized through repeated 5-fold cross-validation on the training set to identify the best-performing parameters. Model performance was evaluated using root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R2). The model with the lowest mean RMSE for each outcome and sex was selected. Model accuracy was then tested on the validation set using the same evaluation metrics.

Z-scores for all predicted spirometric outcomes were calculated using the following formula: z−score = Observed−Predicted value/Residual standard deviation

The lower limits of normal (LLNs) were calculated by subtracting 1.645 times the residual standard deviation from the predicted value. These z-scores were then stratified and plotted by age group to assess model calibration and detect any systematic drift across demographic subgroups.

The study also compared predicted values and LLNs from the machine learning models with those generated using the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) 2012 equations (Caucasian and Other/Mixed categories) and the GLI 2022 global reference equations.

To evaluate the external performance and calibration of the machine learning (ML) reference equations, predictions from the final ML model were compared with those from the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) 2012 equations (Caucasian and Other/Mixed categories) and the GLI 2022 global equations. This comparison was conducted separately for males and females using the validation dataset. For each spirometric outcome, mean squared error (MSE) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using bootstrap resampling (1,000 replicates), along with the mean predicted and observed values, and the mean and standard deviation of z-scores. These metrics were used to assess both prediction accuracy and systematic bias. A lower MSE and a mean z-score closer to zero were interpreted as indicators of better model fit and calibration.

To enhance clinical interpretability, age–height grids were generated separately for males and females. These grids covered ages 18–70 in one-year intervals and included height increments of 2 cm. Predicted values and LLNs for FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC were reported for each grid cell.

Results

Table 1 presents the anthropometric and spirometric characteristics of the study sample. After the exclusion of 117 females and 121 males due to an unacceptable spirometry test, the dataset was divided into training and validation sets for both male and female participants. Among males, 1,401 participants (69.3%) were assigned to the training set and 622 (30.7%) to the validation set. Among females, 1,352 (69.2%) were included in the training set and 600 (30.8%) in the validation set. In males, the median age was 35.24 years (25.49–47.84) in the training set and 35.10 (25.63–49.82) in the validation set. In females, the corresponding values were 38.93 (29.94–50.20) and 39.36 (30.43–51.77), respectively. Pulmonary function measures showed sex-specific differences. In males, median FEV1 was 4.11 L (3.60–4.73) in the training set and 4.06 L (3.53–4.69) in the validation set. In females, values were 3.01 L (2.55–3.43) and 2.95 L (2.56–3.36), respectively. Median FVC in males was 4.77 L (4.20–5.42) in the training set and 4.68 L (4.11–5.39) in the validation set. In females, the values were 3.41 L (2.92–3.90) and 3.37 L (2.92–3.88), respectively. The FEV1/FVC ratio was similar across groups, with median values ranging from 0.86 to 0.89.

Based on the stratified analysis comparing spirometric values between the training and validation datasets for both sexes, results showed overall consistency across age and height categories. In most strata, no statistically significant differences were observed between training and validation subsets for FEV1, FVC, or FEV1/FVC values, as indicated by non-significant p-values from the t-tests. This suggests that the random sampling process successfully preserved the underlying distribution of spirometric measurements within each sex-specific subgroup and confirm that the training and validation subsets provide the baseline context for subsequent model-performance analyses.

As shown in Table 2, the comparative performance analysis of machine learning models in predicting spirometric indices revealed clear sex-specific differences across all evaluated outcomes: FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC. Among males, the linear regression model (LM) and the gradient boosting machine (GBM) consistently outperformed the other algorithms across all three outcomes. For FEV1, the GBM model achieved the lowest root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.7612 and the highest coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.3509), slightly outperforming LM (R2 = 0.3359). Similar results were observed for FVC, where LM and GBM models produced the highest R2 values (0.3675 and 0.3674, respectively), although the differences among models were relatively small. For the FEV1/FVC ratio, the GBM model again had the highest R2 value (0.0423), but overall predictive power was limited for this outcome in males, with all models showing R2 values below 0.05.

Among female participants, prediction accuracy was generally higher than in males across all outcomes and models. For FEV1, the GBM and LM models achieved the highest R2 values (0.4473 and 0.4469, respectively), with RMSE values of 0.5390 and 0.5397, indicating strong and nearly equivalent performance. In predicting FVC, GBM again outperformed the other models, with an RMSE of 0.5989 and R2 of 0.4519, followed by LM. For the FEV1/FVC ratio, although all models demonstrated modest predictive ability, GBM provided the highest R2 (0.0481), suggesting a slightly better capacity to capture variability in this outcome compared to other approaches. Overall, GBM and LM were the most consistent performers across both sexes, with notably higher predictive accuracy observed in the female subgroup.

In external validation analyses, the Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) model demonstrated consistent and reliable predictive accuracy across spirometric outcomes for both females and males, although notable differences by sex were observed. Among females, the GBM model showed strong generalizability for predicting both FEV1 and FVC. For FEV1, it achieved an RMSE of 0.5329 and an R2 of 0.4223, indicating that the model explained over 42% of the variance in an independent dataset. A similarly strong performance was observed for FVC, with an RMSE of 0.5973 and R2 of 0.4336. These findings support the model’s suitability for predicting key spirometric parameters in female populations. However, for the FEV1/FVC ratio, the GBM model’s explanatory power declined considerably, with an external R2 of only 0.0088. This suggests limited ability to capture meaningful variation in this ratio in unseen data, despite acceptable error metrics (RMSE = 0.0516; MAE = 0.0420).

For the male sample, the external validation results confirmed the robustness of the GBM model across all spirometric indices. The model achieved RMSE values of 0.7738 for FEV1 and 0.8413 for FVC, with corresponding R2 values of 0.3131 and 0.3520, respectively. These results highlight the model’s ability to explain a moderate proportion of variability in lung function outcomes among males. For the FEV1/FVC ratio, the model produced an RMSE of 0.0537 and an R2 of 0.0393, which slightly exceeded its performance in the female validation sample. To enhance the clinical utility of the newly developed ML models, age–height prediction grids were generated separately for each sex (Supplementary Material).

The age-stratified z-score distributions for machine learning–predicted spirometric indices in both females and males demonstrate well-calibrated and stable model performance across the adult lifespan. Among females, z-scores for FEV1 and FVC were symmetrically distributed around a median of zero, with interquartile ranges generally confined within ±1, and most values falling between −2 and +2 across all age groups. This pattern suggests consistent predictive accuracy and minimal heteroscedasticity. The FEV1/FVC ratio also showed median values near zero, with slightly increased variability observed at the younger and older age extremes. Similarly, in the male dataset, the machine learning models demonstrated strong calibration, as reflected by median z-scores close to zero across most 5-year age bands. For FEV1 and FVC, interquartile ranges remained stable and mostly within ±1, although a slight downward shift in older males suggests minor underestimation in that subgroup. A higher incidence of positive outliers in younger males may reflect individuals with relatively superior lung function. Z-scores for the FEV1/FVC ratio in males showed minimal spread and a strong central tendency, with most values falling within the ±2 reference range (Figures 1 and 2).

Comparative analysis of smoothed age trajectories for predicted spirometric values and corresponding LLNs revealed consistent discrepancies between ML-based reference equations and GLI standards in both females and males (Figure 3). Among females with median height, ML predictions generated using GBM produced higher expected values for FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC than those from the GLI 2012 (Caucasian and Other/Mixed) and GLI 2022 global equations. This divergence was parallel across age and particularly marked for FEV1 and FVC, where ML curves showed a more convex, age-related decline, becoming increasingly distinct from GLI estimates in older age groups. The FEV1/FVC ratio exhibited less age-related decline in the ML model, maintaining consistently higher values beyond age 40 compared to the GLI references. ML-derived LLNs were also uniformly higher across all three parameters, suggesting a narrower threshold for defining abnormal lung function in females using the ML equations.

A similar pattern was observed in males. ML-derived predictions for FEV1 and FVC consistently exceeded those of all GLI reference groups (Caucasian, Other/Mixed, and Global), particularly among younger and middle-aged adults. This suggests that GLI equations may underestimate normative lung volumes in Iraqi males, potentially leading to misclassification of functional impairment. The LLNs from the ML models also remained consistently higher than those of the GLI across all age groups, further reinforcing this concern. For the FEV1/FVC ratio, while differences between models were less pronounced, ML-predicted values were slightly higher across the adult age range. The corresponding LLN followed a flatter trajectory compared to the more noticeable age-related decline seen in the GLI equations.

The comparative evaluation of ML and GLI reference equations using the validation dataset for both males and females revealed that the ML model consistently demonstrated superior calibration and predictive accuracy across all outcomes (Table 3). Among males, the ML model produced the lowest mean squared error for FEV1 (0.599 [95% CI: 0.495–0.703]), with the predicted mean (4.16) closely matching the observed mean (4.15) and a near-zero mean z-score (−0.02), indicating minimal bias. In contrast, the GLI 2022 and GLI 2012 (Other and Caucasian) equations showed higher errors and consistently underestimated predicted values, resulting in inflated z-scores ranging from 0.61 to 1.25. A similar pattern was observed for FVC, where the ML model yielded the lowest error (0.708), strong agreement between predicted and observed values, and minimal bias (z = −0.03). For FEV1/FVC, the ML model again outperformed all GLI equations, with an error of 0.003 and a mean z-score near zero (−0.01), while GLI models underestimated the ratio, producing z-scores between 0.65 and 0.75.

In the female cohort, the ML models demonstrated even greater predictive precision. For FEV1, the ML model yielded the lowest MSE (0.284 [0.244–0.324]) and showed strong agreement between observed and predicted means (both 3.01), with negligible bias (z = −0.01). In contrast, the GLI equations consistently underestimated lung function, resulting in higher z-scores, particularly for the GLI 2012 reference (z = 1.56). FVC results followed a similar pattern: the ML model had the lowest MSE (0.357) and near-perfect z-score alignment (z = 0.01), while the GLI references again showed higher error and underestimation, with z-scores ranging from 0.38 to 1.12. For the FEV1/FVC ratio, the ML model achieved the lowest MSE (0.003) and the z-score closest to zero (−0.05), whereas the GLI models underestimated the ratio and produced z-scores as high as 0.78.

Discussion

This study presents the first machine learning–based spirometry reference equations developed from a nationally representative sample of Iraqi adults, addressing the long-standing absence of context-specific norms for this population. By training multiple ML algorithms on height and age, we found that gradient boosting machines (GBM) consistently outperformed both conventional statistical approaches and internationally accepted reference standards (GLI 2012 and GLI 2022) across all key spirometric indices. These results demonstrate clear improvements in prediction accuracy, calibration, and model fit, with particularly strong performance observed among female participants.

The GBM model demonstrated superior performance in both internal cross-validation and external testing datasets. For FEV1 and FVC, it consistently produced lower mean squared errors and higher coefficients of determination (R2) than all other models evaluated, including linear regression and support vector machines. These improvements were particularly notable among women, where the GBM model explained more than 44% of the variance in FEV1 and 45% in FVC, outperforming the GLI reference equations by a substantial margin. Even among men, where greater between-individual variability was observed, GBM maintained strong predictive performance, with R2 values exceeding 35% for both outcomes. The FEV1/FVC ratio remained more challenging to predict, consistent with previous studies [12,24]. However, in both sexes, the GBM model again provided the best calibration, even though the R2 values for this outcome were modest.

One of the most striking findings was the consistent under-prediction of lung volumes by the GLI equations in the Iraqi population. Across all GLI subtypes (Caucasian, Other/Mixed, and Global), the predicted means for FEV1 and FVC were systematically lower than the observed values. This resulted in spuriously high positive z-scores and an increased risk of misclassification [14]. In contrast, the ML-based predictions closely aligned with the actual data, producing mean z-scores near zero and indicating minimal bias. These findings reinforce ongoing concerns about the suitability of GLI equations in regional contexts. Similar issues have been reported in studies from North Africa [12] and South Asia [25], where GLI equations failed to reflect local lung physiology. These discrepancies likely reflect the combined effects of genetic background, environmental exposures, and broader patterns in body size and development.

The stability of z-score distributions across age further supports the validity of the ML models. Visual inspection of z-score boxplots showed that FEV1 and FVC values were symmetrically distributed around the median throughout adulthood, with little evidence of heteroscedasticity. Minor deviations in older males may reflect slight underestimation of lung function in late life, while positive outliers among younger males likely represent individuals with exceptionally strong lung function rather than a flaw in the model. For the FEV1/FVC ratio, both sexes displayed narrow interquartile ranges and minimal age-related drift, suggesting that the model maintained consistent calibration in capturing both airflow preservation and decline over time.

This study also compared the predicted values and LLNs from the GBM model with those from the GLI equations, using a fixed median height for each sex. The resulting age-scaled trajectories showed consistent divergence, with ML predictions and LLNs remaining higher than those of the GLI across all age groups. Among women, this gap widened with increasing age, suggesting that the GLI equations may progressively underdiagnose abnormalities in older individuals. This has important implications for disease classification and treatment eligibility, particularly in borderline cases [26]. Similar patterns were seen in men, where the GLI equations significantly underestimated FEV1 and FVC in younger and middle-aged adults. These systematic discrepancies raise concerns about the potential under-recognition of early airflow obstruction or restrictive disease when relying on imported reference standards.

These findings support the use of locally trained machine learning models over traditional parametric methods such as GAMLSS. Although GAMLSS is flexible, it requires complex specifications and relies on strong distributional assumptions. In contrast, ML models can be updated or retrained as population characteristics change, making them suitable for generating adaptive reference standards that reflect shifts in demographics, environmental exposures, or anthropometric trends [27–29]. The use of only two simple predictors, height and age, also enhances clinical applicability and may reduce barriers to implementation in primary care and low-resource settings.

The adoption of Iraqi-specific reference equations can directly improve the accuracy of COPD and asthma diagnosis, preventing both over- and under-treatment. Embedding these ML-based equations in spirometry software and national screening programs could standardize interpretation across hospitals and occupational clinics. At a policy level, this initiative aligns with WHO’s Global Alliance against Chronic Respiratory Diseases (GARD) objectives and provides a framework for other LMICs to develop locally calibrated reference tools, thereby promoting more equitable respiratory care in the region [16].

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study has several important strengths. It is the first to develop spirometric reference equations using machine learning in a large, nationally representative sample of healthy Iraqi adults. The use of data-driven modeling methods, along with rigorous internal and external validation, strengthens the reliability and relevance of the findings. The models were built using only two routinely collected variables, age and height, which enhances their practicality in clinical settings, particularly in primary care and low-resource environments. The comparative analysis with GLI standards also underscores the potential value of adopting region-specific reference equations.

However, some limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design does not allow for causal conclusions or tracking of changes over time within individuals. Although the models performed well using age and height alone, future studies could consider adding other variables, such as environmental exposures, occupational history, or socioeconomic status, to further refine prediction accuracy. Additionally, while the sample was diverse within Iraq, validation in other Middle Eastern populations is necessary to determine regional generalizability. Future research should explore the integration of these models into clinical decision-making tools and evaluate their impact on diagnostic accuracy, treatment decisions, and patient outcomes in routine healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that machine learning–based reference equations, particularly those developed using gradient boosting machines, provide superior accuracy and calibration compared to the GLI 2012 and 2022 equations for Iraqi adults. The ML models closely aligned with observed spirometry values, minimized prediction bias, and reduced the risk of diagnostic misclassification. This work provides the first nationally validated, data-driven spirometric reference equations for Iraq, filling a critical gap in regional respiratory diagnostics and offering a replicable framework for other LMICs. As the use of spirometry continues to expand in low- and middle-income settings, machine learning offers a practical and adaptable alternative to traditional parametric modeling. Future research should focus on integrating these models into clinical spirometry software, validating their performance in patient populations with respiratory diseases, and establishing a regional Middle Eastern lung-function consortium to harmonize reference standards and policy implementation. These steps will promote more context-specific, equitable, and evidence-based respiratory care.
