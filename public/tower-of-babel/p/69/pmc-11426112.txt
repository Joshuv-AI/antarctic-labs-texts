Video Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery: A Tool to Consider in Vascular Thoracic Outlet Syndrome Treatment
Authors: Roberto Sánchez
Published: 2024

For some time, the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment of thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) has been controversial. Understanding this enigmatic syndrome requires thorough knowledge of the normal and pathological anatomy of the cervico-axillary tract. It is also necessary to know the role of the dynamic morphological factors that provoke compression at different levels (namely the scalene triangle, and costoclavicular and subcoracoid spaces), with the consequent appearance of neurovascular symptoms.1 In venous TOS, compression occurs in the costoclavicular canal between the subclavius muscle and the first rib. In this context, resection of the first rib by a transaxillary approach may be the solution for cases of uncomplicated venous compression.2 However, for complicated cases (Paget–Schroetter syndrome) or recurrent cases, they can be treated through a supraclavicular or infraclavicular route, or even a combination of these, with a lower risk of complications.3

Unlike other publications looking at non-vascular pathologies,4 this study by Buero et al. describes the technique of first rib resection by video assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS),5 and with clear video images and appropriate graphic highlights they give the reader the opportunity to capture its feasibility in detail. Like any minimally invasive technique, VATS has the advantage of being less traumatic and providing a better vision to carry out rib resection. The disadvantages of VATS – such as the impossibility of resolving issues such as compression of high roots of the brachial plexus, presence of cervical rib, enlarged transverse process of C7, and cases that merit vascular reconstruction – are rather inconvenient for arterial or neurological TOS, emphasising the need for appropriate case selection. Certainly, the authors will seek to improve and advance the performance and scope of VATS in venous TOS treatment by incorporating new tools such as robotics, on which there are already some data.6, 7, 8

In summary, this article revisits and graphically details a technique that is an interesting contribution to the range of approaches for first rib resection which continues to evolve but may not apply to all types of TOS. VATS is undoubtedly a great contribution in the efforts to improve both the short term outcomes and long term results of first rib resection in venous TOS treatment.9 As long as controversy persists in the management strategy of this complicated syndrome, it is justified to be cautious in the surgical approach for these patients, and it is thus advisable to offer an à la carte solution for each individual patient.
