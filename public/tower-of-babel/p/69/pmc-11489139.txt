Communicating across distances – Biological functions of extracellular vesicles
Authors: Aila Akosua Kattner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis issue of the Biomedical Journal features a special section on extracellular vesicles (EVs), covering their role in neurological diseases, viral infections, trogocytosis, allogeneic organ rejection and tolerance, as well as EV biodistribution. Two articles explore the mechanisms of Parkinson's disease, focusing on white matter and exosomes. This journal issue also examines polyomavirus-induced damage in renal transplant grafts, proposes a miRNA signature as a diagnostic biomarker for Kawasaki disease, discusses neural gating and associated brain wave alterations, and further clarifies the relationship between gut microbiota and immune checkpoint inhibitors. Additionally, the importance of therapeutic drug monitoring is reaffirmed.Keywords: Extracellular vesicle, Parkinson's disease, Polyomavirus, Allograft, Kawasaki disease

Spotlight on reviews

The evolution of language is an elaborate and ongoing process. While early theories posited that phonetic systems emerged primarily from quasi-mechanical processes, shaped by social factors like community and cultural transmission, the influence of the physical environment on language development cannot be overlooked. The physical properties of the medium through which voiced sounds are transmitted influences how speech is produced and heard.

In different animal species, communication systems are known to be ecologically adaptive. For example, urban birds pitch their songs higher to rise above city noise than their rural counterparts, while mongoose species modify their vocalizations depending on vegetation density and habitat type to propagate sound more efficiently. Historically, the evolution of human speech was not thought to follow the acoustic adaptation hypothesis. However, more recent findings suggest a deep connection between linguistic diversity and climate.

Dense vegetation and high humidity can impede the transmission of consonants, while hot air can distort them or limit unvoiced sounds by absorbing their high-frequency. Conversely, dry air increases the phonatory effort of the larynx, creating challenges for producing voiced sounds. Sonority, or the loudness of speech sounds, is affected by the openness of the vocal tract, and languages of high sonority are found to be concentrated near the equator and in the Southern Hemisphere.

In regions with high temperatures and humidity, languages tend to rely more on vowels. In reverse, high-altitude environments favor the use of ejective or voiceless consonants like “k" and “q.” At these altitudes, speakers often adjust their vocalizations to conserve moisture by keeping their vocal folds more closed, thus limiting exposure to the dry outside air. Languages in colder climates with sparse vegetation often depend more on consonants, one might think of Eastern Europe, while low humidity appears to correlate with a lower prevalence vowels.

Complex tonal systems like Mandarin's tonal distinctions (e.g., “ma” meaning “horse,” “mother,” “scold,” or “hemp” depending on tone), are more common in humid climates. Tonal languages require a precise tone production, and vocal chords are less precise in dry and cold environments. This leads to languages with complex tonality to emerge less in desiccated regions. Research has furthermore shown that in elderly individuals vocal fatigue is reduced in humid environments.,1,2,3 [[1], [2], [3]]

The uniformitarian principle assumes that processes, forces and mechanisms we observe today in speech and language are at work today in a similar way as they were in the past [4]. However, accelerated changes in climate on a global level raise the question of how temperature, humidity levels, altitude and other climate-related factors will shape the evolution of phonetic systems in the future.

White matter matters

Uniformitarianism has not only been applied as a foundational principle in the field of historical linguistics. It was coined in contrast to catastrophism, a principle proposed by British naturalists in the late 18th century to explain Earth's development. It was commonly accepted at that time that the planet was shaped by supernatural forces and a series of catastrophic events, such as the biblical flood or asteroid impacts.,4,5

The London surgeon-apothecary James Parkinson was a multifaceted figure deeply involved in both science and politics. His political activism led him to join two secret societies, and in an era lacking freedom of speech or press, he wrote anonymous pamphlets, advocating for social justice, the rights of the poor, children, people with intellectual disabilities, and opposing child abuse. Parkinson was also an accomplished geologist and paleontologist and avid collector of fossils and minerals, whose illustrations of former worlds became standard references in paleontology at his time. Parkinson was a staunch supporter of creationism and catastrophism. In 1817, Parkinson wrote an essay describing “shaky palsy,” the first detailed account of what is nowadays known as Parkinson's disease (PD). At the time, this essay garnered little attention [5,6].

PD is now recognized as one of the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorders. Its diagnosis primarily relies on observing key motor symptoms, though non-motor functions like attention and language are also implicated in the pathophysiology of PD. Ongoing research aims to improve diagnostic accuracy, focusing on biomarkers such as the pathological marker protein alpha-synuclein in extracellular fluids and advanced techniques like diffusion tensor imaging [7,8].

Alterations in white matter (WM) serve as early indicators of cognitive impairment in PD patients. In a study published in this issue of the Biomedical Journal, Liao et al. examined WM changes in patients with PD mild cognitive impairment (PD-MCI) compared to those with PD and normal cognition (PD-NC). Their findings showed significant WM degeneration in the corpus callosum and superior corona radiata in the PD-MCI group [9].

Crossroads of inflammation and degeneration

Neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration frequently co-occur in central nervous system (CNS) disorders. In conditions like multiple sclerosis (MS), initial inflammation can trigger secondary neurodegeneration, while in diseases such as Alzheimer's (AD), PD, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and Huntington's disease, neurodegeneration predominates, followed by secondary, reactive inflammation [10].

Exosomes, a class of extracellular vesicles (EVs), are membrane enclosed particles devoid of nuclei and incapable of replication. They serve as essential mediators of intercellular communication with critical roles in both physiological and pathological conditions. EVs transport a variety of molecules, including nucleic acids, proteins, and metabolites, to nearby or distant targets. EVs are categorized based on their cellular origin, e.g., apoptotic bodies, microvesicles, and exosomes [Fig. 1], each with distinct, though sometimes overlapping roles in cell communication. Due to their ability to trace back to the originating cell, EVs are suggested as potential markers for disease. Exosomes in particular are lipid bilayer-enclosed vesicles secreted by all brain cell types, and act as key players in the neuroinflammatory microenvironment, regulating pathological processes via the delivery of bioactive molecules within the CNS [[11], [12], [13]].

The three major classes of extracellular vesicles (EVs) depicted on the basis of their origin. Microvesicles and apoptotic bodies are respectively generated via budding and blebbing of the plasma membrane. Exosomes are formed through the endocytic pathway by exocytosis of the intraluminal vesicles of multivesicular bodies that fuse with the plasma membrane. Graph by Tang et al. [10].

As Tang's review highlights, CNS EVs in disease states may carry oxidative, inflammatory, or amyloidogenic substances, contributing to both neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. Exosomes, however, also hold promise as therapeutic tools, such as in liquid biopsies for MS and AD [10].

Editorial

Kanellopoulos, Rieux-Laucat, and Ojcius introduce this special issue of the Biomedical Journal, which features articles on the role of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in various biological processes. Topics include EVs in neurological diseases, viral infections, their involvement in trogocytosis, the role of EVs in allogeneic organ rejection or tolerance, and the biodistribution of EVs [14].

Cellular snack or strategic defense?

Trogocytosis (from the Greek trogos: to gnaw) was first documented in T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells. The process involves the transfer of plasma membrane fragments from antigen-presenting cells to lymphocytes. Initially, this was proposed to serve as a mechanism for eliminating cytotoxic T lymphocytes through “fratricide.” Alternatively, from an evolutionary standpoint, trogocytosis may have evolved as a symbiotic interaction, where leukocytes feed on other cell types in exchange for protecting the organism against pathogens [15].

Martinez-Martin and Alarcon have further explored the nuances of trogocytosis, focusing among other aspects on the formation of multivesicular bodies and the role of plasma membrane-associated exosomes [16].

Hijacking pathways

Long-distance trade notably shaped cultural, religious, and artistic exchanges between the major civilizations of Europe, North Africa, Arabia and Asia in antiquity. The well-known Silk, Spice, and Incense Routes served as the communication highways of the ancient world, transmitting not only raw materials and goods but also new inventions, religious beliefs, artistic styles, languages, and social customs. Active from the 2nd century BCE until the mid-15th century, these trade routes were fraught with dangers. Among other things, travelers had to navigate wars, earthquakes, sandstorms, and thieves. Some researchers propose that merchants traveling these routes may have at one point inadvertently transported a hitchhiker, the plague bacterium Yersinia pestis, which later caused the Black Death.,6,7,8

Martin et al. looked into how viruses exploit EV biogenesis pathways. Since these pathways and viral replication cycles are closely intertwined, viruses may interfere both with the secretion and content of EVs. It is noteworthy that depending on the context, EVs may take on a double-sided role in those situations, either enhancing or reducing viral infection [17].

Unlocking advantages through cross-dressing

Around 3000 years ago, nomadic steppe tribes are believed to have invented the first two-legged garments to facilitate bareback horse riding, as they were among the earliest to domesticate horses. Over time, however, the wearing of pants became restricted to men in many cultures, often enforced by social customs or laws. In some regions, women could be arrested for wearing clothing not prescribed for their assigned gender identity. Runaway enslaved individuals used pants as a disguise, and cross-dressing in pants was in general frequently employed to gain the privileges granted to those permitted to wear pants in order to escape discrimination based on sexism, racism, or homophobia. Even in the 20th century, male-passing individuals were required to wear jackets and ties in U.S. medical laboratories, while female-passing technicians were expected to wear skirts or dresses. It wasn't until the 1970s that some of those women felt comfortable enough to regularly wear trousers in the workplace.,9,10 [18]

In a scientific context, “cross-dressing” refers to an alternative mechanism of antigen presentation, wherein fully functional major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules are transferred between immune cells. This process involves trogocytosis, the intercellular transfer of plasma membranes, as well as exosomes and nanotubes [19]. Benichou and Lancia provide a detailed overview of the pathways involved in T lymphocyte allorecognition, examining how trogocytosis and extracellular vesicles contribute to both allograft rejection and tolerance [20].

Survival of the friendliest

Human tolerance and friendliness may not have been a natural progression but rather a response to ecological pressures. While most animals tend to be defensive toward outsiders, humans possess the remarkable ability to care for individuals beyond their own kin or local groups. A computer simulation study suggests that between 30,000 and 300,000 years ago, archaeological evidence points to increased mobility and more frequent interactions among different groups of our human ancestors. As they migrated from Africa during a period of increasingly harsh and unpredictable environments, survival favored those willing to share resources both within and between groups.11

Maternal acceptance of the fetus can be seen as a true form of tolerance. The mother's immune system accepts the fetus, which expresses paternally inherited alloantigens, by modulating what would typically be a destructive immune response into one of tolerance. This delicate balance requires precise control of immune interactions between mother and child [21,22].

Burlingham's article explores how EVs play their part in the mother's peripheral immune system, allowing her to maintain resistance to pathogens while tolerating the semi-allogeneic fetus. Mechanisms of local transplantation tolerance appear to be applicable as well to maternal-fetal tolerance, mediated by regulatory T cells delivering exosomal cytokines [23].

EV-olution: the powers of UCMSC-EVs

Umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs) exhibit strong regenerative and immunomodulatory properties, and their EVs are emerging as a promising natural source of nanoparticles to enhance organ transplantation outcomes and manage inflammatory diseases [24]. The application of these versatile stem cells in tissue engineering and gene therapy has led to notable advancements, with recent studies confirming their ability to even inhibit tumor cell proliferation and migration to cancer sites [25].

To further the understanding of the biodistribution and pharmacokinetics of UCMSC-EVs, Chung et al. conducted an in vivo SPECT imaging study. Their research identified the organs with the highest uptake of radiolabeled 99mTc-UCMSC-EVs, offering insights into the stability of these EVs and contributing to the groundwork for future biomedical applications [26].

Original articles

To suppress or not to suppress

BK Polyomavirus (BKPyV) may cause renal dysfunction in transplanted grafts, a condition historically misdiagnosed due to clinical and histopathological similarities with acute rejection. While BKPyV is widespread in the human population and often remains asymptomatic, treatment in graft patients typically involves reducing the immunosuppression. However, this reduction may elevate the risk of graft rejection [27].

Wu et al. conducted an in vitro study revealing that interferon alpha (IFNα) and its induced protein MxA effectively diminish BKPyV replication. Type I interferons have been previously documented to exhibit antiviral properties via IFN-induced proteins that engage with various viral domains [28].

Decoding Kawasaki disease with precision

Kawasaki disease (KD) is an acute, self-limiting vasculitis of medium-sized vessels, primarily targeting the coronary arteries. In high-income countries, it is the leading cause of acquired heart disease, and is gradually surpassing rheumatic heart disease in majority world regions. Though most common in children, KD can occur at any age, with a slight male predominance.12

In a retrospective study, Chen et al. confirmed the association of 10 specific miRNAs with KD. These miRNAs demonstrated potential as diagnostic biomarkers for KD, exhibiting exceptional specificity and sensitivity. The validated miRNA signature effectively distinguished both complete and incomplete KD cases from patients presenting with similar symptoms or fever [29].

Take a breath

While modern yoga practice often centers on asana (physical postures), traditional yoga integrates several practices, with the control of breath or life force, pranayama, as one crucial element. There are more than 50 traditional pranayama techniques that manipulate inhalation, exhalation, and breath retention in various durations or ratios. These methods range from rapid breathing, such as Kapalabhati (akin to hyperventilation although with emphasis on the exhalation), to slow, controlled diaphragmatic breathing like Ujjayi, which involves constricting the throat muscles of the glottis. Pranayama is traditionally believed to burn toxins and purify the blood vessels. Studies show that pranayama positively affects cardiac-vagal baroreflex sensitivity, enhances oxygen saturation, pulse rate, and respiratory function, reduces blood pressure, and alleviates anxiety. It has also been proposed as a potential intervention for conditions related to hyperinsulinemia like metabolic syndrome and polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS).13 [[30], [31], [32]]

Outside of yogic practices it is known that respiratory activity alters the membrane potential of vagal and sympathetic motoneurons, constantly modulating their response to stimuli. A prominent example of respiratory gating is respiratory sinus arrhythmia, the cyclic heart rate variation in sync with breathing, which is more pronounced during slow breathing and diminished with rapid breathing [33,34].

Liang et al. examined the neural gating of respiratory sensation using time- and frequency-domain analyses. In response to respiratory occlusions, the study found a correlation between perceived discomfort and cortical theta oscillations, potentially linked to emotional processing of respiratory mechanosensation [35].

Gut guardians and disruptors in cancer

Most gut microbes are either harmless or beneficial, protecting against pathogens, extracting nutrients from the diet, and supporting normal immune function. However, dysbiosis on the other hand has been linked to obesity, malnutrition, inflammatory bowel disease, neurological disorders, and cancer. Alpha diversity indicates microbial richness in a given community. Beta diversity examines the factors that correlate with differences in microbiota composition. For instance, do individuals of similar age, cultural background, or disease state share more microbial lineages? Dietary diversity has been positively associated with alpha diversity in the gut microbiota, with a diverse diet contributing to a healthier microbial environment. Additionally, diet quality is closely linked to both alpha and beta diversity in the gut.14 [[36], [37], [38]]

Wen-Cheng Chang et al. explored the connection between gut microbiota and the response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in advanced cancer patients. Their findings propose that alpha diversity may act more as a prognostic factor than a predictive one, with higher alpha diversity being associated with prolonged survival [39].

Revisiting the role of therapeutic drug monitoring

The history of antiseizure medications (ASMs) has for the most part been marked by serendipitous discoveries and limited therapeutic efficacy. Before the 19th century, the common neurological disorder epilepsy was often attributed to spiritual and supernatural causes. Although Hippocrates recognized epilepsy as a brain disorder, this understanding was largely ignored for centuries. It wasn't until the 19th century that potassium bromide became the first drug used to treat epilepsy, despite its narrow therapeutic index and significant side effects, including severe skin reactions and central nervous system effects like lethargy, delirium, psychosis, paralysis, and even exacerbation of seizures [40].

Lim et al. draw attention to the wide use of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) with older ASMs due to their complex pharmacokinetics, high potential for drug-drug interactions, and narrow therapeutic windows. While the need for TDM with newer ASMs has generally been considered less essential, Lim et al.’s retrospective study demonstrates the continued value of TDM. Their findings show that TDM, when applied with clear indications, led to improved seizure control and a reduction in side effects for the majority of patients [41].
