Eco-evolutionary responses of species distributions to climate change
Authors: Pengdong Chen, Wei Huang, Evan Siemann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Species elevational shifts are well-documented responses to climate change, with many moving upslope to track suitable conditions. However, these shifts can vary considerably in both direction and rate, and the underlying causes of this variability are not well understood. This study examines how elevational shifts depend on geographical zones along with species’ climatic niches, global prevalence, and evolutionary history by analyzing paired lower and upper edge shifts across 845 plant and animal species records worldwide. We find distinct effects of these drivers on upper versus lower distribution limits. Tropical species experienced more rapid upward shifts of their lower edges than did temperate species. Species with warmer and wetter optimal climatic niches displayed faster upper-edge shifts, while those only with wetter ones showed more rapid lower-edge shifts. Globally prevalent species expanded their distributions with climate change by combining faster upper-edge advances with slower lower-edge contractions, likely reflecting their drier climatic adaptation. Importantly, these ecological effects overlapped substantially with phylogenetic effects, and phylogenetic conservatism independently explained a notable portion of the variation in elevational responses. These findings highlight the complexity of evolutionary history and ecological processes in shaping species’ climate responses and underscore the climate vulnerability of some species due to their evolutionary inertia.

Introduction

Global biodiversity is under significant threat from climate change, making it crucial to understand climate-induced range shifts for effective biodiversity conservation1–3. Mountain ecosystems include areas with high biodiversity that are critical for understanding species range shifts in response to climate change, with many species moving to higher elevations to remain in suitable habitats4–6. Global meta-analysis shows that the average rate of upward shift is about 1.22 meters per year7 or 1.78 meters per year8,9. Traditionally, it has been assumed that upward range shifts improve species’ chances of survival in a warming climate, with species that fail to track these climate changes at increasing risk of local extinction as conditions become less suitable for them, but some downward shifts have also been observed10–13. Despite substantial progress in understanding elevational range shift patterns, significant gaps in knowledge remain, particularly regarding the causes of the high variability in both the direction and rate of these shifts. Investigating the complex ecological and evolutionary mechanisms driving these shifts is essential, especially given the diverse responses to similar climate changes. Many life history patterns that influence species extinctions reflect a combination of species-specific responses to contemporary environmental pressures and evolutionary traits inherited over time14–16. However, the extent to which the variability in elevational range shifts is shaped by the phylogenetic conservatism of intrinsic traits and extrinsic ecological characteristics, and how these factors interact, remains unclear.

The primary focus on upper distribution limits (“upper edges” hereafter) while largely ignoring lower distribution limits (“lower edges” hereafter) has hindered understanding of the causes of variability in shifts in species’ distributions. Although some studies have highlighted the importance of considering both lower and upper range edges17,18, twice as many studies have studied upper versus lower edge shifts8,9. This limits our understanding of species’ overall responses to climate change because the dynamics of the lower and upper edges are not independent of each other. Asynchronous shifts, for example, when the upper edge moves upward quickly while the lower edge moves upward slowly (or moves downward), lead to range expansions, while more rapid upward shifts of lower versus upper edges (or downward shifts of upper edges) lead to range contractions. Considering lower and upper edge shifts together offers a more complete picture of species’ redistribution strategies and likelihood of persistence in a changing climate.

To address these gaps, we analyze a comprehensive dataset comprising 845 records of plant and animal species documenting elevational range shifts at both lower and upper edges across nine taxonomic classes from a global database, BioShifts8,9 (Fig. 1a, b). We first examine key ecological drivers by integrating species’ biogeographical distribution patterns (latitude, global prevalence reflected by occurrence records and distribution breadths) with their climatic niche characteristics (optimal conditions and niche breadth), thereby assessing how broad geographic distributions and climate experiences shape local elevational responses. These predictors represent broad, mechanistic axes expected to govern climate-driven range dynamics: (i) latitude captures large-scale biogeographic and climatic gradients; (ii) climatic niche optima and breadths reflect the climatic space a species can occupy and its physiological tolerances; (iii) global prevalence serves as a proxy for demographic dominance and potential dispersal opportunities; and (iv) phylogenetic relationships encapsulate shared evolutionary constraints and conserved traits. We focus on these variables to detect generalizable, macroecological patterns across diverse taxa. This also allows us to indirectly capture the influence of more specific life-history traits, including dispersal ability, underlying these broad predictors. Our findings help reveal the adaptive strategies of species under climate stress and improve our ability to predict future distributions.

Results and discussion

Elevational shifts and ecological drivers

Overall, these species records exhibited consistent upward shifts in their lower edges, upper edges, and means of their elevational ranges even after controlling for geometric and temporal constraints, while maintaining relatively stable range extents (Fig. 1d). However, substantial variation was observed among records (Fig. 1c).

To assess the overall role of ecological factors in these shifts, we extracted the first two axes from a principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 2a). PC1 primarily represented biogeographic distributions (absolute latitude along with global prevalence, distribution and occurrence) and summer precipitation optima (Bio18: precipitation of warmest quarter), whereas PC2 primarily reflected thermal optima (Bio10: mean temperature of warmest quarter; Bio11: mean temperature of coldest quarter). Then we evaluated the combined effects of these two axes on elevational shifts using linear mixed-effects models. These analyses revealed contrasting ecological influences on lower versus upper edge shifts (Fig. 2b). Specifically, upslope lower edge shifts were more rapid for tropical, rare and/or narrowly distributed species and those poorly adapted to summer drought (Fig. 2b, PC1 effect). In contrast, upslope upper edge shifts were more rapid for species with warmer temperature optima (Fig. 2b, PC2 effect). Together, these distinct ecological drivers of lower and upper boundaries collectively shaped mean range shifts (Fig. 2c) and range extent changes (Fig. 2d). Latitudinal patterns of elevational shifts

Further analyses indicated how species’ elevational responses varied with latitude: both lower edges (Fig. 3a) and mean ranges (Fig. 3b) shifted upslope more rapidly in tropical regions than at higher latitudes. This finding is consistent with studies showing that tropical mountain species respond more strongly to global warming than temperate ones19,20. That is to say, tropical species are likely more strongly impacted by changing climates and/or are able to respond more quickly, resulting in larger shifts in their elevational ranges than temperate species. For example, a study on global patterns in germination temperature showed that tropical plants are closer to their upper thermal limits21, which would make them more vulnerable to greater shifts. Changes in overall range extents did not depend on latitude (Fig. 3c), so while ranges shift quickly, the total occupied elevational range extent of tropical species remains relatively stable. This seems to imply that local extinctions of tropical species at thermal boundaries are unlikely to lead to erosion of their overall abundance because they maintain their elevational range extents. However, as species move upslope, the surface area of mountains is expected to decrease due to the narrowing of mountains at higher elevations. Conversely, some species may encounter increases in available area as they shift upslope in the short-term, as mountain topography exhibits complex area-elevation patterns22. Therefore, determining changes in the available area of occupancy during redistribution elevation will help to understand more realistic responses of species to climate change in different regions, but it remains an open question. Although the climate sensitivity of tropical species has been reported repeatedly, these patterns require more rigorous testing that controls for additional interacting variables and stronger empirical validation. Caution is therefore warranted when interpreting these latitudinal trends.

Effects of climatic niches on elevational shifts

Our investigation into climatic niches demonstrated that optimal climate conditions of species had stronger effects on elevational shifts than did the breadth of their climatic niches. Linear mixed-effects models and hierarchical variance partitioning were applied to evaluate the effect size and relative importance of four climatic niche dimensions (temperature optimum, precipitation optimum, temperature breadth, and precipitation breadth) on elevational range shifts. These four dimensions were derived by grouping 38 original bioclimatic variables (19 climate medians and 19 climate breadths) into temperature- or precipitation-related categories, conducting principal component analysis within each group, and extracting the first principal component of each as the four representative variables (Supplementary Fig. 1). Temperature and precipitation median variables (i.e., niche optima) always explained more variation in elevation shifts and range extent changes than did the corresponding breadth variables (Fig. 4d–f). In addition, the rates of elevational shifts and range extent changes were more influenced by the optimal conditions of bioclimatic variables rather than by their breadths in most cases (Fig. 4a–c). In addition, we used redundancy analysis (RDA) to evaluate the combined effects of 19 bioclimatic niche optima and breadths on elevational shifts, with separate models for optima and breadths. For climatic niche optima, RDA1 and RDA2 explained 9.19% and 5.09% of the variance in elevational shifts, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In contrast, for climatic niche breadths, RDA1 explained 5.86% of the variance, while RDA2 had no significant effect (2.07% of variance; Supplementary Fig. 2b). Although climatic niche breadth is known influence geographic range size and extinction risk of species14,23, its relationship with range shift rates remains inconclusive, with mixed evidence across taxa and studies24,25. Our findings reveal that climatic niche breadth accounts for only a small fraction of the variation in elevational shifts. This could be due to the fact that macroclimatic estimates of niches may not fully capture species’ responses to climate, whereas realized niches, shaped by interactions with factors like microclimate26, competitors27 and mutualists28, may better reflect true climate responses. Nonetheless, our findings suggest that optimal climatic conditions are more predictive of species’ climatic responses, potentially due to demographic advantages in these optimal conditions.

We further demonstrated distinct effects of optimal climatic niches on upper and lower edge shifts. Specifically, temperature niches only affected upper edges, but precipitation niches affected both lower and upper edges (Fig. 4a). Temperature niches impacting upper edge shifts likely reflect the physiological constraints imposed by temperature on thermophilic species at higher elevations. Consequently, climate warming is expected to promote upper edge shifts of thermophilic species more strongly by creating new suitable habitats at higher elevations, contributing to overall range expansion (Fig. 4c). Precipitation niches had significant positive effects both on lower and upper edge shifts, indicating that species that globally occur in areas with higher precipitation had more rapid upward shifts in their overall range. That is, those species that experience drier climates had their overall range shift upward more slowly (Fig. 4b). While temperature is widely considered a primary driver of species redistributions, there is growing evidence that precipitation may contribute to more complex distribution dynamics1,29. For example, precipitation can buffer temperature-driven local extinctions of moth species at warm range margins30. However, whether and how species’ elevational distribution dynamics track water balance are highly controversial31. From the perspective of climate niches (i.e., the climatic experiences throughout species’ distributions), our findings highlight the distinct roles of temperature and precipitation on different aspects of elevational shifts and underscore the importance of considering multiple climatic dimensions when predicting species’ responses to climate change.

Global prevalence and elevational shifts

Our analysis reveals that species with high global prevalence (i.e., common and/or widely distributed) tend to expand their elevational ranges more rapidly. Specifically, globally prevalent species tend to exhibit slower upward shifts at their lower edges and faster upward shifts at their upper edges, culminating in more rapid overall range expansions (Fig. 5). These rapid range expansions could be facilitated by greater genetic diversity or plasticity of globally prevalent species, allowing them to quickly adapt to a wider variety of environmental conditions (i.e., ecological generalization)32,33. Conversely, less globally prevalent species (i.e., rare and/or narrowly distributed) do not experience such expansions, and may even experience contractions in their overall range (Fig. 5). The capacity of these species to adapt to climate changes may be hindered by factors such as habitat specificity and limited adaptability23,34. Our findings are consistent with a recent study focusing on plant species in the European Alps, which showed that IUCN red-listed species are contracting their elevational ranges faster than common species35. These results further support the finding that widespread species increase their regional occupancy over time36. One particularly interesting point is that species with high global prevalence shift their lower range edges more slowly (Fig. 5a). This implies that their ability to persist in novel climatic conditions may allow them to thrive in a changing climate, which runs counter to the traditional expectations that rapid range shifts maintain historical climatic conditions and safeguard against population declines. Recent research on marine fishes has also challenged the assumption that rapid range shifts tracking climate change are necessarily associated with positive abundance trends37,38.

Lenoir et al.13 call this unexpected change contrary to the prevailing climate trend “going against the flow”, which may be caused by factors such as competition release and habitat change. Considering our finding that lower edge shifts are primarily influenced by precipitation niches rather than temperature niches, with species that experienced drier climates showing slower upward retreats (Fig. 4), we infer that the slower upward movement of globally prevalent species’ lower edges may stem from their adaptation to arid conditions. Our finding that species’ global prevalences are more strongly negatively correlated with optimal summer precipitation than with temperature variables (Fig. 6) supports this interpretation. These findings thus call into question the traditional understanding that species lagging behind in tracking climate warming are inevitably “losers” in climate change. This advantage of globally prevalent species may benefit from the unique evolutionary variation of populations at thermal boundaries driven by local selection. A study has shown little genomic differentiation between leading edge and core populations, but higher differentiation between trailing edge and core populations39, thus providing a basis for this advantage. Although species can persist through dispersal or by adapting to the new climates, adaptive evolution appears to be more important for avoiding extinction than dispersal40,41, although this remains a controversial topic. For example, a study found that high genetic variance in warmer regions and low dispersal best minimized extinction risks42. This question becomes particularly important when considering the fact that range shifts of many species seriously lag behind climate change velocities.

Phylogenetic conservatism of elevational shifts

We reveal that phylogenetic structure significantly shaped elevational shifts of species. Across nine taxonomic classes, elevational shift rates differ substantially, with animals, particularly insects, shifting more rapidly than plants (Fig. 7). This highlights the importance of phylogenetic context in shaping species’ responses to climatic change. Indeed, phylogenetic vectors (PVs), identified through phylogenetic eigenvector regression (PVR), significantly influenced elevational shifts for four variables (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Fig. 3). Our findings echo the studies on specific taxa, such as butterflies25 and fish43, which detected phylogenetic signals for some aspects of climate-induced range shifts. These results suggest that shared evolutionary pathways have shaped species’ capacities to adjust their distributions, as closely related species tend to exhibit similar shifts. These phylogenetic clusters likely reflect that life history traits and physiological adaptations, such as dispersal ability, are typically conserved within clades, resulting in comparable responses to climatic changes44,45. For instance, species in the class Insecta are overall shifting their elevations upward especially rapidly (Fig. 7). Notably, the variation explained by ecological descriptors overlapped substantially with that explained by phylogeny (Fig. 8). Ecological predictors accounted for 3.7%, 3.4%, 0.6%, 6.2%, and 0.8% of the variation in paired edge shifts (lower + upper), lower edge shifts, upper edge shifts, mean range shifts, and range extent changes, respectively, while sharing an additional 2.8%, 5.8%, 3.3%, 2.5%, and 2.8% of variance with phylogenetic components. This shared variance likely reflects ecological traits that carry phylogenetic signal, indicating that ecological responses to climate change are partly structured by evolutionary history. For instance, climatic niches and geographic distributions are often phylogenetically conserved, so closely related species may share similar ecological sensitivities and exhibit comparable elevational responses to contemporary climate change. Therefore, ecological and phylogenetic effects should not be viewed as independent drivers, but rather as interacting influences that jointly structure species’ elevational shifts.

In addition to this shared component, phylogenetic structure alone explained a substantial proportion of the remaining variation: 25.5% of the variation in paired edge shifts, 41.0% in lower edge shifts, 54.0% in upper edge shifts, 35.9% in mean range shifts, and 52.9% in range extent changes (Fig. 8). Such strong phylogenetic effects may reflect unmeasured ecological or physiological traits that are evolutionarily conserved but were not explicitly captured by the ecological descriptors included in this study. Similarly, the strong phylogenetic influence observed in studies of species extinction risk15,16 suggests that phylogeny is expected to bridge our understanding of species’ range shifts and their extinction vulnerabilities in changing climates.

Implications and limitations

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the eco-evolutionary factors driving variability in species’ elevational range shifts on a global scale, focusing on four key aspects: latitudinal gradients, climatic niches, global prevalence, and phylogenetic constraints. These dimensions shed light on the complex responses of species to climate change, revealing general patterns that vary across taxonomic groups, climatic conditions, and geographical regions. Furthermore, our efforts focused on range extent change by pairing lower and upper edge shifts, which provided a greater understanding of species climate responses than focusing on just one edge shift. By integrating these broad perspectives, our findings bridge several lines of evidence and identify new avenues for further exploration. Importantly, our findings underscore the large influence of evolutionary history in shaping species’ distribution responses to climate change. Thus, integrating evolutionary history into ecological models will greatly improve predictions of species’ resilience or vulnerability to global climate change.

While our analyses capture broad-scale eco-evolutionary drivers, we recognize that localized or species-specific factors, such as acid deposition47, wildfires48, and animal behavioral plasticity49, also significantly influence distributional shifts. In particular, as an important part of global change, invasive alien species are spreading from lowlands to high mountains at an extraordinary rate in introduced areas4. In complex ecosystems, these interacting factors may play pivotal roles that were beyond the scope of our study. Additionally, the inability to account for stochastic processes further limits our capacity to fully explain range shift variability.

The need to consider random expectations to improve reporting of species redistribution has been mentioned several times13,50,51. The “null effect” covariate (transect extent/survey duration) in our main analyses captures background rates of elevational change determined by site-level geometric constraints and observation time. However, this term is constant within a transect. To further assess potential random expectations of species-specific boundary dynamics, we conducted supplementary null model analyses on the subset of species for which complete historical elevational limits were available (Supplementary Fig. 4; Supplementary Note). These components help to separate geometric constraints from differential dynamics across range edges. The null model tests, which standardized observed shifts against the maximum possible movement within each transect, broadly supported our main findings, such as the strong effects of phylogenetic history and distinct effects of ecological drivers on lower versus upper edge shifts, while highlighting that certain signals (for example, latitudinal trend) can weaken when geometric expectations are explicitly controlled. Because the necessary data were available for only about half of the species records, these null model results are presented as a complementary line of evidence rather than as a replacement for our main analyses. We therefore caution that some residual influence of elevational geometry may remain. Future studies with more complete historical distribution data will be essential to fully validate and refine these patterns.

Another limitation in interpreting the results stems from geographic and taxonomic biases in the database, which is heavily weighted toward temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere and dominated by vertebrates and vascular plants, with little coverage in Africa, South America, and invertebrates. These imbalances may constrain the global generality of our conclusions and limit our ability to capture responses from underrepresented tropical regions or less-studied clades. For example, the stronger upward shifts observed in tropical species should be interpreted cautiously, as limited tropical sampling reduces confidence in the magnitude of these effects.

Ultimately, this study contributes to the growing recognition of the multidimensional nature of species’ responses to climate change. By emphasizing the interplay between evolutionary heritage and ecological factors, it advocates for approaches that go beyond immediate ecological pressures to include the deeper evolutionary processes that shape species’ adaptability. This perspective is crucial for a more comprehensive understanding of biodiversity loss driven by rapid climate change, as some species inevitably become vulnerable due to their evolutionary inertia. Our findings reiterate the urgency of embedding evolutionary insights into conservation biology to ensure that species protection efforts account for the complex interplay among their ecological needs, geographical contexts, and both demographic and evolutionary inertias.

Ecological factors construction

Latitude

The latitude of each survey was determined by reviewing the 36 original publications and identifying the central coordinates for each of the 42 survey sites. For sites without reported geographic coordinates, we used Google Maps to relocate them based on descriptions provided in the literature. Although this method introduces some local-scale errors, these are unlikely to significantly affect the overall latitudinal gradient patterns, as the survey sites spanned both the Southern and Northern Hemispheres (from −38.87° to 53.71°). To account for the random range shifts expected from temporal and geometric constraints along elevational gradients, we also extracted the elevational boundaries of survey transects and the temporal spans between historical and resurvey periods from the original publications. We then calculated an index, referred to hereafter as the “null effect” covariate, by dividing the elevational extent of each transect by the duration of the survey interval, representing the expected random variation in species distributions along the elevational gradient.

Climatic niches

The climatic niches of species can be estimated by matching occurrence records with spatial climate data54. To estimate the climatic niches of the studied species, we first obtained (in March 2024) global occurrence records for each of the 762 species using the “rgbif” R package55, which accesses the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF, 10.15468/dd.ffrzfc)56. For about 10% of the species with larger datasets, we manually downloaded the data. In total, we acquired global occurrence records for 740 of the 762 species, as some species lacked available occurrence data or had non-unique species keys. For each species, we extracted latitude, longitude, and temporal information (year and month) from the occurrence records.

We used bioclimatic data from WorldClim (https://www.worldclim.org/)57 to determine values for 19 bioclimatic variables for each occurrence of the species, applying the extract() function from the “raster” R package58. For each species, we calculated the 0.5th, 50th (median), and 99.5th percentiles for each bioclimatic variable. The 50th percentile represented the climatic niche optimum. For niche breadth, we used the 0.5-99.5th percentiles rather than the raw minimum-maximum range. This method balances capturing the full climatic distribution of species while minimizing the influence of extreme or erroneous records59. Here are full names of 19 bioclimatic variables: Bio01 [Annual Mean Temperature], Bio02 [Mean Diurnal Range (Mean of monthly (max temp–min temp))], Bio03 [Isothermality (B02/B07) (×100)], Bio04 [Temperature Seasonality (standard deviation ×100)], Bio05 [Max Temperature of Warmest Month], Bio06 [Min Temperature of Coldest Month], Bio07 [Temperature Annual Range (B05-B06)], Bio08 [Mean Temperature of Wettest Quarter], Bio09 [Mean Temperature of Driest Quarter], Bio10 [Mean Temperature of Warmest Quarter], Bio11 [Mean Temperature of Coldest Quarter], Bio12 [Annual Precipitation], Bio13 [Precipitation of Wettest Month], Bio14 [Precipitation of Driest Month], Bio15 [Precipitation Seasonality (Coefficient of Variation)], Bio16 [Precipitation of Wettest Quarter], Bio17 [Precipitation of Driest Quarter], Bio18 [Precipitation of Warmest Quarter], Bio19 [Precipitation of Coldest Quarter].

For bird species, due to their seasonal migrations, we separately extracted occurrence records during the summer (June, July, and August for the Northern Hemisphere; December, January, and February for the Southern Hemisphere) and winter (December, January, and February for the Northern Hemisphere; June, July, and August for the Southern Hemisphere). We then matched these seasonal records with the appropriate climatic variables: Bio10 (temperature of the warmest quarter), Bio18 (precipitation of the warmest quarter), Bio11 (temperature of the coldest quarter), and Bio19 (precipitation of the coldest quarter). Using this data, we calculated climatic optima and breadths for each of the four variables for each species. For 15 bird species with over five million occurrence records, we set a random seed and randomly selected five million records using the sample() function for further analysis.

Global prevalence

We assessed the global prevalence of each species along two dimensions: worldwide abundance and distribution breadth. Worldwide abundance was estimated by counting the total number of global occurrence records with coordinate information from GBIF. Distribution breadth was determined by counting the number of continents on which these records were distributed. To calculate the number of continents, we first obtained geographical boundary data using the “rnaturalearth” R package60. Then, we used the st_filter() function from the “sf” R package61 to assign each occurrence record to its corresponding continent. Finally, the total number of continents occupied by each species was counted. For example, when a species only occurs in Asia and North America, the number of continents is two. Since Antarctica is not included, the maximum value of the continent number is six. We defined global prevalence as the product of the natural logarithm of the number of occurrences and the number of continents distributed: global prevalence = ln (occurrence number) × continent number. A higher value indicates greater global prevalence or distribution success, while a lower value suggests rarity.

Ecological analysis

Data analysis strategy

Our analytical framework followed a hierarchical approach designed to both reduce multicollinearity among ecological predictors and to distinguish broad macroecological patterns from more specific mechanistic relationships. First, we constructed a global model in which all ecological indicators (latitude, climatic niche characteristics, and global prevalence) were combined and summarized using PCA. This dimensionality reduction step minimized collinearity among predictors and allowed us to test the overall ecological signal by relating the resulting principal components to the range-shift response variables. Second, we examined the relationships between range-shift responses and each ecological dimension separately (latitude, climatic niche, and global prevalence) to better interpret the specific ecological drivers. For the climatic niche variables in particular, we applied an additional PCA to the 19 bioclimatic variables to extract the major climatic gradients representing species’ climatic niches before including them in the models.

Overall ecological effects

To investigate the overall ecological effects on elevational shifts, we first conducted a PCA on eight ecological variables: Bio10 median, Bio11 median, Bio18 median, Bio19 median, absolute latitude, occurrence, distribution, and prevalence of species worldwide. The PCA was performed using the PCA() function from the “FactoMineR” R package62, with visualization supported by the “factoextra” package63. We extracted the first two principal components, which together captured the major ecological gradients among species (PC1: 40.7% and PC2: 27.1%).

We then fitted two sets of linear mixed-effects models using the lmer() function in the “lme4” R package64. For lower and upper edge shifts, the two variables were combined into a single response, with PC1, PC2, range position (lower vs. upper), and their two-way interactions (PC1 × position, PC2 × position) included as fixed effects. Species identity nested within site identity was specified as a random intercept to account for methodological differences among studies and the paired structure of lower and upper edges. For mean range shifts and range extent changes, we fitted two separate linear mixed-effects models with PC1 and PC2 as fixed effects and site identity as the random intercept. In all the above models, the “null effect” covariate (transect extent divided by survey duration) was included to help control for expected random shifts. The unit of replication was the species record. To assess the significance of fixed effects, we used t-tests with Satterthwaite’s approximation, implemented through the “lmerTest” R package65. Species with available global occurrence records were included in these analyses (n = 822 records).

Latitude

To investigate latitudinal patterns of elevational shifts, we fitted two sets of linear mixed-effects models using the lmer() function in the “lme4” R package. For lower and upper edge shifts, the two variables were combined into a single response, with absolute latitude, range position (lower vs. upper), and their interaction included as fixed effects. Species identity nested within site identity was specified as a random intercept to account for methodological differences among studies and the paired structure of lower and upper edges. For mean range shifts and range extent changes, we fitted two separate linear mixed-effects models with absolute latitude as the fixed effect and site identity as the random intercept. In all the above models, the “null effect” covariate was included to help control for expected random shifts. The unit of replication was the species record. To test the significance of fixed effects, we used t-tests with Satterthwaite’s approximation, implemented through the “lmerTest” R package. All of the 845 species records were included in these analyses.

Climatic niches

To investigate the distinct influences of four climatic niche dimensions on elevational shifts, we derived four principal component (PC) variables from 38 original bioclimatic descriptors. Variables were placed into one of four groups according to climate dimensions: temperature medians (Bio01-Bio11 medians), precipitation medians (Bio12-Bio19 medians), temperature breadths (Bio01-Bio11 breadths), and precipitation breadths (Bio12-Bio19 breadths). For each group, we performed PCA using the PCA() function in the “FactoMineR” package, supported by the “factoextra” package for visualization, and extracted the first principal component (PC1) as a representative variable (Supplementary Fig. 1). This yielded four independent PCs representing temperature optimum, precipitation optimum, temperature breadth, and precipitation breadth, respectively.

We then fitted two sets of linear mixed-effects models, following the same procedure as in the latitudinal and overall ecological analyses, using the lmer() function in the “lme4” package. For lower and upper edge shifts, the two variables were combined into a single response, with the four climatic PCs, range position (lower vs. upper), and their interactions included as fixed effects. Species identity nested within site identity was specified as a random intercept. For mean range shifts and range extent changes, we fitted separate models with the four climatic PCs as fixed effects and site identity as the random intercept. In all models, the “null effect” covariate was included, and the species record was used as the unit of replication. The significance of fixed effects was assessed using t-tests with Satterthwaite’s approximation via the “lmerTest” package. These analyses included 601 species records after excluding those with fewer than 20 occurrence records to avoid potential underestimation of climatic niche and excluding birds because most of their climate variables are not available due to potential seasonal migration.

To further evaluate the relative importance of each climatic niche dimension, we applied hierarchical variance partitioning using the glmm.hp() function from the “glmm.hp” package66. This analysis decomposed the model outputs above into variance contributions attributable to each climatic dimension. Specifically, this approach decomposes the explained variance into unique and shared components, and allocates the shared variance equally among correlated predictors67, thereby quantifying the independent contribution of each climatic dimension. Uniquely, for the first set of models (lower and upper edges combined), we obtained both the main effects of the four climatic niche dimensions and their interactions with range position, providing a detailed assessment of their relative importance in driving elevational shifts.

Global prevalence

To investigate how global prevalence influences species’ elevational shifts, we fitted two sets of linear mixed-effects models using the lmer() function in the “lme4” R package. For lower and upper edge shifts, the two variables were combined into a single response, with species’ global prevalence, range position (lower vs. upper), and their interaction included as fixed effects. Species identity nested within site identity was specified as a random intercept to account for methodological differences among studies and the paired structure of lower and upper edges. For mean range shifts and range extent changes, we fitted two separate linear mixed-effects models with species’ global prevalence as the fixed effect and site identity as the random intercept. In all the above models, the “null effect” variable was included as a covariate to help control for expected random shifts. To test the significance of fixed effects, we used t-tests with Satterthwaite’s approximation, implemented through the “lmerTest” R package. The unit of replication was the species record. Species with available global occurrence records were included in these analyses (n = 822 records).

Phylogenetic conservatism

To investigate the phylogenetic structure in species’ elevational shifts, we generated a phylogenetic tree for 730 species in the dataset using the “rotl” R package68, which accesses the Open Tree of Life69 (https://tree.opentreeoflife.org). Thirty-one species that could not be matched to any entries in the Open Tree of Life were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, one species (Asarum europaeum [ott882085]) was removed due to inconsistencies between its branch information in the tree and its taxonomic information. Branch lengths were computed using Grafen’s method70, a standard approach for scaling branch lengths in phylogenetic trees to reflect their hierarchical structure. We used the computer.brlen() function from the “ape” R package71 to implement this method. Grafen’s method is particularly useful when examining phylogenetic structures, as it accounts for the evolutionary relationships among species in the tree. However, these branch lengths are relative rather than absolute and therefore do not represent precise diverging times or genetic distances. Our goal here was to test the dependence of range shift responses on overall tree hierarchical structure, but for studies targeting other specific questions, using dated phylogenies would be highly beneficial for obtaining more precise evolutionary inferences. The phylogenetic tree, along with its associated annotation data, was visualized using the “ggtree” R package72. For species that were recorded in multiple studies, we calculated the mean values for each of the four range shift variables (lower edge shift, upper edge shift, mean range shift, and range extent change) across all studies in which the species appeared (i.e., one set of values per species).

We employed the methods of phylogenetic eigenvector regression (PVR)73 and redundancy analysis (RDA) to extract and select phylogenetic vectors (PVs) that influence species’ elevational shifts. First, we calculated a phylogenetic distance matrix using the cophenetic() function from the R core package “stats” and extracted eigenvectors from the phylogenetic distance matrix through principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) using the pcoa() function from the “ape” R package71. This process produced n−1 PVs (where n = 716 species). Although our original phylogenetic tree included 730 species, 14 species were pruned due to the absence of occurrence data required to estimate climatic niches and global prevalence, aligning the tree with ecological descriptors. The resulting PVs were used as explanatory descriptors in variation partitioning to represent phylogenetic distances among species16,74.

Next, we selected the most important PVs influencing elevational shifts using forward selection with a double-stopping criterion75. This procedure involved two key steps: (1) running a global RDA that included all phylogenetic eigenvectors, and (2) executing a forward selection process that retained only explanatory variables with significant p-values. The adjusted R2 threshold of the global test served as a secondary criterion, alongside a significance level of 0.05, to identify relevant explanatory variables. Forward selection was conducted using the ordiR2step() function from the “vegan” R package76. The combination of significant PVs allowed us to quantify the phylogenetic structure of elevational shifts. For all four range shift variables, significant PVs were identified: 51 for paired edge shifts (lower-upper paired vector matrix), 80 for lower edge shift, 111 for upper edge shift, 62 for mean range shift, and 98 for range extent change. These results suggest that phylogeny significantly influences elevational shifts. For further details on this methodology, refer to the work of Gianuca et al.15 and their code repository77, which we followed in our analysis.

As a synthesis in our study, we used variation partitioning based on partial regression (single response variable) or RDA (lower-upper paired vector matrix) to evaluate the variances explained by ecological and evolutionary descriptors. This allowed us to quantify the contributions of ecological descriptors (encompassing absolute latitude, climatic niches, species’ global prevalence and its two components) and evolutionary descriptors (represented by all identified PVs above) to the shifts of 716 species. Specifically, for the climate niche variables, we included the median values of the four variables Bio10, Bio11, Bio18 and Bio19 because the other variables were not available for birds. The proportion of variance explained by each component and its shared effects was quantified using adjusted R2. The “null effect” calculated as transect extent divided by survey duration was included as an additional component to deduct random expectation effects, both those they explained uniquely and those they shared with other components. Variation partitioning was performed using the varpart() function in the “vegan” R package, and the significance of purely ecological and purely evolutionary components was tested using the rda() function with 999 permutation tests. All data analyses involved in this study were performed in R (version R-4.5.1)78.
