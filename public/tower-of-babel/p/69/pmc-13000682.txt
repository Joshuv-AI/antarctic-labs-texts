Soil beneficial bacteria: Harnessing tools for optimizing citrus growth and soil health in pH‐stressed soils
Authors: Shankar Shrestha, Laura Waldo, Arnold Schumann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRhizosphere bacteria can support crop production by promoting plant health, soil fertility, and resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses. However, the potential role of soil‐beneficial bacteria, particularly plant growth‐promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), remains inadequately explored in citrus production grown on pH‐stressed soil. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of PGPR on citrus growth and soil health indicators under pH‐stressed Florida sandy soil. A greenhouse study was conducted at the University of Florida, Lake Alfred, which followed a 3 × 3 factorial randomized complete block design with three soil pH levels (5.0, 6.5, and 8.0) and three PGPR (P)/fertilizer (F) combinations (P + 75% F, P + 100% F, and 100% F only). Soil pH conditions were adjusted using a soil neutralization curve generated by soil incubation methods. The results showed that the soil pH factor significantly influenced most crop growth parameters, with optimal results at a pH of 6.5. PGPR significantly (p < 0.05) improved the plant height (27.84 cm), root mass density (7.65 mg/cm3), stem diameter (4.89 mm), and aboveground biomass (2.64 g/ plant) compared to without PGPR application. No significant difference between 75% and 100% fertilizer rates were found in crop and soil attributes when PGPR was applied. Soil organic matter and microbial respiration were also significantly improved (p < 0.05) with PGPR inoculation by 1.15 and 1.65 folds, respectively, compared to 100% F‐only treatment. The availability of soil nutrients was influenced by PGPR treatments, which likely enhanced nutrient uptake and improved crop growth even with a reduced fertilizer rate. The results suggested that PGPR inoculation combined with partial fertilization can improve soil health indicators and plant growth, even in pH‐stressed sandy soils.

INTRODUCTION

Huanglongbing (HLB), caused by Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus, is the most severe bacterial threat to citrus production due to its complexity, destructiveness, and resistance to management (Alvarez et al., 2016; Kadyampakeni et al., 2015; Morgan et al., 2009). HLB‐affected trees suffer reduced soil nutrient uptake, primarily due to fibrous root decline, leading to deficiencies and lower yields (Gottwald, 2010; Johnson et al., 2014; Kadyampakeni, Morgan, Schumann, & Nkedi‐Kizza, 2014; Kadyampakeni, Morgan, Schumann, Nkedi‐Kizza, & Mahmoud, 2014; Hamido et al., 2019). Florida's sandy soils exacerbate these challenges with their poor nutrient retention and high permeability, causing nutrient leaching and reducing fertilizer efficiency (Alva & Tucker, 1999; Obreza & Collins, 2008; Obreza & Schumann, 2010; Atta et al., 2021). The combined impact of HLB and soil limitations threatens citrus sustainability. Efforts to manage citrus tree nutrition have been ineffective in sustaining tree health (Obreza & Morgan, 2008). Strategies like enhanced nutritional programs, fertigation, and foliar sprays show inconsistent results (Morgan et al., 2016). Research suggests increasing fertilizer supply may alleviate nutrient deficiencies caused by HLB (Atta et al., 2020, 2021; Kadyampakeni et al., 2023; Kwakye et al., 2022; Morgan et al., 2016; Zambon et al., 2019). However, excessive fertilization can worsen environmental issues like groundwater contamination and soil acidification without boosting productivity (Atta et al., 2020; Obreza & Schumann, 2010; Zekri et al., 2021). Additionally, alkaline irrigation combined with over‐fertilization disrupts soil pH, further limiting nutrient availability and crop growth (Atta et al., 2021; Vashisth & Kadyampakeni, 2019). Given these constraints, alternative strategies are needed to enhance nutrient efficiency, improve soil health, and reduce environmental impact.

In recent years, considerable interest has been witnessed in unraveling the potential role of soil microbial inoculants, particularly plant growth‐promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), in improving crop growth and soil health (Compant et al., 2019; A. Khan et al., 2022; Malik et al., 2022; Mylona et al., 1995). The reports showed that plants have complex interactions with microbes in the rhizosphere due to the soil's abundance of microbiome richness, activity, and diversity (Lakshmanan et al., 2014; Muller et al., 2016). This is primarily because many root exudates and cells contain nutrient‐rich compounds for microbe‐associated roots (Daguerre et al., 2017; Meharg & Killham, 1990). PGPR offers promising solutions for improving crop performance and resilience by modulating phytohormone production, improving nutrient availability, and bolstering resistance to pathogens. These microorganisms increase nutrient availability through nitrogen bio‐fixation, solubilization of essential minerals such as phosphorus and potassium, and enhancing iron uptake (Bhat et al., 2019; N. Khan et al., 2020). PGPR have been shown to enhance germination, seedling emergence, and overall growth and yield in crops such as tomato (Mayak et al., 2004), lettuce (Han & Lee, 2005; Barassi et al., 2006), radish (Yildirim et al., 2008), and strawberry (Karlidag et al., 2013). These growth‐promoting effects may result from the production of secondary metabolites like phytohormones, riboflavin, and vitamins (Dakora, 2003), or through improved nutrient availability (Glick, 1995; Chabot et al., 1996; Yanni et al., 1997). Their potential for sustainable crop production has garnered significant attention, particularly for addressing various biotic and abiotic stresses, making PGPR a promising tool in modern agriculture. The commonly used microorganisms in agriculture are Azospirillum, Bacillus, Burkholderia, Enterobacter, Flavobacterium, Clostridium, Frankia, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Rhizobium, Trichoderma, Beauveria, and Streptomyces (Van Oosten et al., 2017; Gouda et al., 2018).

As climate change exacerbates harsh growing conditions and soil health declines, sustainable solutions are critical for agricultural productivity and conservation. Beneficial microbes, particularly PGPR like Bacillus spp., have been widely recognized for improving soil fertility (A. Khan et al., 2022) and helping plants adapt to nutrient imbalance and drought stresses (Lim & Kim, 2013; Mylona et al., 1995; Setiawati et al., 2022). These microbes contribute directly and indirectly to plant growth by improving nutrient availability, phytohormone production, antimicrobial compound synthesis, and induced systemic resistance. Bacillus spp. alleviates plant stresses by producing 1‐aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylate deaminase, which reduces stress‐induced ethylene levels, maintaining normal plant growth (Glick et al., 2007). They also secrete exopolysaccharides and siderophores, aiding in ionic balance, water transport, and pathogen suppression (Radhakrishna et al., 2017). Advanced technologies, such as biofertilizer nanoparticles, offer targeted nutrient delivery, minimizing fertilizer waste and preventing environmental runoff (Mishra & Kumar, 2009). Specific strains like Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, Bacillus subtilis, and Bacillus megaterium have shown efficacy in enhancing nutrient availability and uptake, providing resilience to salt and drought stress tolerance, and also increasing the organic carbon (Cano‐Castro et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2020; Setiawati et al., 2022). Labiadh et al. (2023) revealed that the consortium of Bacillus sp., including B. subtilis, significantly stimulated the root growth of sour oranges under in vitro experiments. The result demonstrated that the PGPR strain B. subtilis inoculation in the tomato plant improved root dry weight, root length, and yield per plant by 10%–26%, 13%–15%, and 25% respectively, compared to the control treatment (Mena‐Violante & Olalde‐Portugal, 2007). Pérez‐Piqueres et al. (2025) reported that B. subtilis inoculation significantly increases the mandarin total biomass by 25% over the noninoculated treatment. Authors also reported that Bacillus application increases macro‐ and micronutrients, which increases the nutrient uptake and use efficiency in citrus (Pérez‐Piqueres et al., 2025). Fan et al. (2017) reported increased shoot nitrogen uptake in tomato following inoculation with B. amyloliquefaciens and B. pumilus. Similarly, Kuan et al. (2016) observed elevated nitrogen content in maize treated with B. subtilis. These findings supported that Bacillus spp. can improve nutrient use efficiency (NUE), crop growth, and reduce chemical fertilizer needs. These multifaceted benefits highlight the critical role of PGPR in fostering resilience and sustainability in modern agriculture.

While PGPRs have been extensively studied in various crops and stress conditions, their targeted use in Florida's pH‐stressed sandy soils for citrus production remains poorly understood and significantly underreported. Soil pH is a major factor shaping microbial community structure and function, directly influencing microbial viability, nutrient transformations, and plant‐microbe interactions (Biswas et al., 2007). The novelty of this study lies in evaluating the effectiveness of specific Bacillus‐based PGPR consortia in enhancing citrus growth, nutrient dynamics, and soil health under the naturally low‐buffering capacity and pH fluctuations of Florida's sandy soils. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to assess PGPR‐mediated citrus performance under these specific soil stress conditions, offering new insights into microbial adaptation and biofertilizer potential in subtropical fruit systems. By addressing this knowledge gap, our work provides a novel, field‐based framework for integrating PGPR into regenerative citrus nutrient management strategies in Florida. Accordingly, this study tested the hypothesis that PGPR inoculation would enhance citrus growth, improve nutrient availability, and promote soil health in Florida's pH‐stressed sandy soils.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Research site and soil description

The greenhouse experiments were conducted at the University of Florida, Citrus Research and Education Center (CREC), Lake Alfred, FL. One‐month‐old healthy seedlings of Murcott mandarin (Citrus reticulata × C. sinensis) were used in 2023 and planted in plastic pots (3.78 L) filled with sandy soil, while in 2024, the experiment was repeated using Duncan grapefruit (Citrus Paradisi) seedlings. Both years followed a similar experimental design, including treatments and environmental conditions. The soil for the experiment was collected from the CREC Teaching Block, which is mapped as Candler fine sand (USDA‐NRCS, 2019). The Candler series comprises about 96% sand and is classified as hyperthermic, uncoated, typic Quartzipsamments, a soil typically found in central Florida orange orchards. It is an excessively drained, rapidly permeable soil in upland areas (Morgan et al., 2006; Obreza & Collins, 2008). Initial physicochemical properties of the collected soil had 0.94% organic matter; soil pH of 5.7; cation exchange capacity of 3 meq/100 g; low in soil available nutrients (N, K, Mg, S, B); and optimal range P, Ca, Mn, Fe, Cu.

Experimental design and treatment management

The experiment was conducted using a 3 × 3 factorial arrangement in a randomized complete block design comprising nine treatments with four replications. The first factor included three soil pH levels (5.0, 6.5, and 8.0), adjusted to simulate field‐specific pH stress conditions. The second factor consisted of three combinations of PGPR (P) and fertilizer rates (F): PGPR with 75% fertilizer rate (P + 75%F), PGPR with 100% fertilizer rate (P + 100%F), and 100% fertilizer as a control (100%F only). In this experiment, a 25% fertilizer reduction (i.e., applying 75% of the full rate) was used to avoid potential nutrient deficiency symptoms while assessing comparable benefits. The complete layout of the greenhouse experiment is presented in Figure S1.

Soil pH adjustments were made using Ca(OH)2 or Al2(SO4)3 guided by a soil neutralization curve developed through soil incubation methods. To generate a neutralization curve, 100 g of air‐dried soil samples were placed in individual 150‐mL plastic containers and treated with increasing rates of Ca(OH)2 (0–10 g/kg) and Al2(SO4)3 (0.1–5 g/kg) with three replications. The samples were wetted with ultrapure deionized water to achieve field capacity and incubated at room temperature for 5 days. After 5 days, the soil pH was measured using a pH meter at a 1:1 soil‐to‐water ratio using a Corning 150 pH meter (Corning, Incorporated). The incubation pH values measured individually for increasing levels of Ca(OH)2 and Al2(SO4)3 were used to generate unique regression curves and equations. The regression equation was used to calculate the quantities of Ca(OH)2 and Al2(SO4)3 required to achieve target pH levels of 5.0, 6.5, and 8.0 in the experimental soil. Throughout the experiment, soil pH was monitored monthly and adjusted to maintain the desired pH levels as part of the treatment protocol. While pH‐adjusting amendments may have introduced secondary ionic (Ca2 +, Al3 +, and SO4 2 −) effects beyond pH, they were not independently evaluated.

In this study, the Bacillus spp. strains were used as PGPR applied to the soil using the drench method. Two commercial PGPR products—TerraGrow (BioSafe Systems) and LalRise Vita (Lallemand Plant Care), were applied to potted seedlings at their respective recommended rates every 15 days interval. TerraGrow contains five Bacillus strains: B. licheniformis (20 × 109 cfu/g), B. subtilis (6.0 × 108 cfu/g), B. pumilis (6.0 × 108 cfu/g), B. amyloliquefaciens (3.0 × 108 cfu/g), and B. megaterium (3.0 × 108 cfu/g), while LalRise Vita contains B. velezensis (2 × 109 cfu/g). The TerraGrow product was prepared at a concentration of 0.3 g/L water, suitable for seedlings in pots deeper than 6 in. Similarly, according to the recommended guidelines, LalRise Vita was mixed at a concentration of 1.75 g/L in water. These products, each containing multiple Bacillus strains, were intentionally applied together as a consortium‐based treatment to simulate practical field conditions and to enhance microbial functional diversity under the targeted stress conditions of sandy soil. A limitation of this study was that the simultaneous application of both PGPR products prevented identification of individual strain—or product‐specific effects. The prepared PGPR solutions were applied as drenches, with 100 mL applied to each experimental pot for the respective treatments. All essential plant nutrients were provided based on a nitrogen (N) concentration of 200 mg/L to ensure adequate nutrient supply for citrus seedlings. This was achieved by applying 0.83 g/L of Miracle‐Gro (Scotts Miracle‐Gro), which contains N (24%), P2O5 (8%), K2O (16%), and micronutrients (B, Cu, Fe, Mn, Mo, and Zn). In addition, supplemental Fe and Mg were supplied using 0.17 g/L of Sequestrene 138 (BASF) and 0.4 g/L of Epsom salt, respectively. A 100 mL fertilizer solution was drenched to the 100% fertilizer treatment, while 75 mL was applied to the 75% fertilizer treatment. Although certain nutrients, such as P, Ca, Mn, Fe, and Cu, were already within optimal levels, they were still applied because either their inclusion was inherent to the use of Miracle‐Gro or the lower end of the optimal range. The pH of the fertilizer solutions was not adjusted prior to application; any influence on soil pH was accounted for through the regular monitoring and adjustments.

Each experimental plot consisted of five tall plastic pots (20 cm depth × 12 cm diameter), each holding approximately 3.3 kg of soil. Three pots were used for data collection within each replication, with three pots per treatment. Muslin cloth and fine mesh were placed at the bottom of each pot to prevent soil loss with drained water. The month‐old vigorous and uniform‐sized seedlings were transplanted into each plastic pot filled with the respective soil pH‐adjusted treatments. The experimental plants were fertilized at 15‐day intervals for 6 months, with PGPR inoculation applied to the designated treatments 1 day after each fertilization event. Irrigation was applied every 2 days to ensure adequate soil moisture (approximately 10% v/v) was maintained at field capacity.

Plant growth, biomass, and root parameters

All plant growth and biomass measurements (plant height, stem diameter, and dry biomass) were recorded at the end of the experiment (6 months after the treatment began). Seedling stem diameter was measured from just above the potted soil surface using a digital caliper with an accuracy of ± 0.02 mm (Husky Tools). Three trees from each experimental plot were destructively sampled, with stems, leaves, and roots separated to quantify aboveground and belowground biomass. Root mass fraction (RMF) was calculated by dividing root dry weight by total plant dry weight. For root growth measurements, freshly sampled roots were washed with tap water, scanned in an EPSON (V550 Photo) flatbed scanner, and analyzed to measure root length density (RLD), root surface area (RSA), and root volume (RV) using Rhizovision root analysis software (Seethepalli et al., al., 2021). Sampled roots, stems, and leaves were dried in a convection oven (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 65°C for 72 h to measure their respective dry matter biomass. Specific root length (SRL) was measured by dividing the total fine root length by the fine root dry mass.

Leaf and soil analysis for nutrient accumulation

At the end of the experiments, leaves were sampled to analyze leaf nutrient concentration and uptake. The oven‐dried samples were ground in a Mini Thomas Wiley Grinding Mill (Thomas Scientific) equipped with a 40‐mesh sieve. The processed samples were sent to Waters Agricultural Laboratories, Inc. to determine the elemental concentration of nutrients N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, Zn, Mn, Fe, Cu, and B. Finally, respective leaf nutrient uptakes were calculated by multiplying the leaf nutrient concentration and total leaf dry biomass.

Soil samples collected from the experimental pots were air‐dried, ground, and sieved through a 2‐mm mesh to prepare them for analyzing chemical and microbial properties such as soil pH, electrical conductivity (EC), soil organic matter (SOM), soil nitrate (NO3–N), soil extractable nutrients, and soil microbial respiration. Soil pH was determined by the soil–water (1:1, w/v) method using a Corning 150 pH meter (Corning, Incorporated). EC was determined using a 1:5 soil:water ratio method with an Accument 50 EC meter (Fisher Scientific). SOM was measured using the loss on ignition method (Davies, 1974). To determine the soil NO3–N concentration, the soil sample was extracted by a 2 M KCl solution (Kadyampakeni, Morgan, Schumann, Nkedi‐Kizza, & Mahmoud, 2014) and then measured by a colorimetric method using an automatic intermittent chemical analyzer (Smartchem 170, KPM Analytics). For other soil chemical properties analysis, soil samples were sent to Waters Agricultural Laboratories, Inc., where Mehlich‐3 extractant nutrients (P, K, Ca, Mg, Na, Zn, Mn, Cu, and B) were analyzed using the inductively coupled plasma spectrometer method (Mehlich, 1984). Soil microbial respiration was estimated using incubation and titration methods as described by Franzluebbers (2016); this measurement reflects short‐term microbial activity rather than microbial biomass, diversity, or community structure. The quantity of microbial CO2 evolved from the sample soil was calculated using the following formula: CO2−Cmg/kgofsoil=mLblank−mLsample×N×M/S where N is the normality of acid (mol/L), M is the mass conversion from cmolc to g C (6000 is a conversion factor derived from the atomic weight of carbon and the stoichiometry of the CO2 trapping reaction), and S is the soil weight in g.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, measured variables were averaged across three pots per treatment within each replication to obtain a single mean value; individual pots were not treated as separate subsamples. Treatment effects on crop growth, dry biomass, leaf nutrients, and soil parameters were assessed using a linear mixed‐effects model (“lmer”). It considered soil pH and PGPR/fertilizer as fixed factors, and cultivar/year and replication as random factors. Cultivar/year and replications were included as a random factor in the statistical model to account for inherent variability between years, cultivars, and blocks, thereby improving the robustness of treatment effect estimation. The interaction term (soil pH × PGPR/fertilizer) was included in the full model to determine whether one factor's effect depended on the other's level. ANOVA was conducted using the “lmerTest” package, and post hoc comparisons were performed with the “emmeans” function, applying Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference test (α = 0.05) for mean separation. Assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test, Levene's test, and Q–Q plots, with Box‐Cox and log transformations applied as needed. If assumptions were violated, nonparametric methods or a “glmer” model were used. Analyses were conducted in RStudio using “lme4” and “emmeans” (R Core Team, 2024).

RESULTS

Crop growth characteristics and biomass partition

Soil pH and PGPR applications significantly influenced most crop growth parameters. Specifically, soil pH 6.5 enhanced plant height and stem diameter by 80% and 34%, respectively, compared to pH‐stressed soil conditions (Figure 1). Similarly, the combined application of PGPR and a 75% fertilizer rate resulted in statistically significant increases in plant height and stem diameter, showing 23% and 9% increments, respectively, compared to without PGPR application (100% F). The graphical image (Figure 3) demonstrates that treatments PGPR with 100% fertilizer (P + 100%F) resulted in the highest plant height compared to the treatment 100%F across all soil pH conditions (pH 5.0 and pH 6.5).

Plant height and stem diameter of citrus as affected by soil pH stress and plant growth‐promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) inoculation in Florida sandy soils. Bars with different lowercase letters are significantly different at p < 0.05.

Total dry biomass accumulation, including aboveground (stem and leaf) and belowground (root), was greatly influenced by soil pH and PGPR treatment factors. A soil pH of 6.5 significantly enhanced biomass yield to 8.41 g/plant, increasing whole biomass by 1.4‐fold, aboveground biomass by 1.5‐fold, and root dry mass by 1.3‐fold compared to pH‐stress conditions. Applying PGPR combined with either 75% or 100% fertilizer significantly (p < 0.01) increased biomass yield (5.66 g/ plant) by nearly 35% compared to the 100% fertilizer treatment alone, with increases of 44% in aboveground biomass and 23% in root biomass (Figure 2). The graphical image (Figure 3) illustrates that the application of PGPR with 75% fertilizer (P + 75%F), led to a denser root mass compared to the treatment with 100% fertilizer alone (100%F). This trend was consistent across all soil pH conditions. RMF indicates the plant's biomass allocation to roots, emphasizing its effort to enhance nutrient and water uptake from the soil. Higher RMF was observed under low pH conditions (0.43) and in treatments without PGPR (0.42), significantly differing within respective factors. The interaction effect of soil pH and PGPR/fertilizer was insignificant (p > 0.05) in all crop growth parameters (results not presented), indicating that the effects of each factor were independent.

Root growth dynamics

The result revealed that soil pH significantly affected root growth dynamics, including RLD, RSA, RV, and root mass density (RMD). An optimal mean of 12.78 mm/cm3, 33.47 mm2/cm3, 12.56 mm3/cm3, and 1.59 mg/ cm3 of RLD, RSA, RV, and RMD parameters were observed at a soil pH of 6.5 with a significant (p < 0.001) increment by 2.8, 3.08, 3.72, and 2.28 folds than in Ph‐stressed soil conditions, respectively. This improvement in root growth indicates that nutrient and water uptake are maximized at this pH level. This is likely due to the efficient availability of nutrients in the soil and enhanced root system functionality, allowing better exploitation of available resources. Although PGPR applications had minimal impact on most root growth parameters, they notably increased RMD by 1.24 folds when combined with 75% fertilizer compared to the 100%F only (p < 0.05). Numerical trends (Table S1) also suggested a positive influence compared to fertilizer‐only treatments, highlighting the potential of PGPR to complement traditional fertilization in boosting root performance and nutrient uptake efficiency.

Interestingly, SRL was significantly higher under pH‐stress conditions (soil pH 8.0). While the SRL was not statistically significant in the absence of PGPR or with 100% fertilizer (F) treatment, it exhibited a numerically higher trend (Figure 4). This indicates that plants adapt to nutrient‐limited environments by producing finer roots to enhance nutrient acquisition. However, this adaptation is likely to come at the cost of a shorter root lifespan, as finer roots are typically less robust and more transient. Additionally, there were no significant interaction effects between soil pH and PGPR application on root growth parameters. These suggest that the combined influence of soil pH and microbial amendments may not play a significantly complex role in root development, particularly under pH‐stressed sandy soil conditions.

Leaf nutrient concentration and uptake

Two years of combined analysis of leaf nutrient composition in citrus under varying soil pH and PGPR treatments highlighted significant trends in nutrient dynamics. Interestingly, soil pH of 5.0 or 8.0 resulted in significantly higher leaf nutrient concentration than the normal 6.5 soil pH, except leaf Fe at the end of the experiment, possibly due to limited crop growth and less dilution effect. Better crop growth and high biomass production cause comparatively more dilution with efficient nutrient uptake and internal distribution under normal soil pH 6.5. Most macro‐ and micronutrients were within the optimum to excess range of the critical concentrations for Florida nutrition concentration, regardless of soil pH stress conditions and soil bacteria inoculation treatments (Table S2). Nevertheless, at soil pH 5, macronutrients such as P (0.10%) and Ca (2.05%) were deficient, while other nutrients were abundant due to perhaps increased solubility in acidic conditions. Transitioning to pH 6.5, leaf Ca and Mn concentrations were below the optimum range, while micronutrients Mn and Zn were in the deficiency range in high soil pH (8.0).

Combined analysis showed that PGPR treatments significantly affected leaf K, Mg, and S concentration. Leaf K concentration was measured significantly higher (2.50%) in treatment P + 100%F, followed by P +7 5%F. These results revealed that applying PGPR increased K nutrient uptake and leaf concentrations. In contrast, nutrients Ca, Mg, and S were significantly less in the PGPR‐applied treatment than only 100% fertilizer applied, possibly due to nutrient redistribution and dilution effect with higher crop biomass. The PGPR with 75% fertilizer treatment resulted in excessive levels of N, K, and Mn but retained optimal levels for P, Ca, and S and key micronutrients, demonstrating the potential of PGPR to enhance nutrient availability while reducing fertilizer dependency. The P + 100%F treatment also showed excessive N and K accumulation, with optimal levels of most other nutrients, reflecting the synergistic effect of PGPR but deficient leaf Ca concentration. In contrast, the 100% fertilizer‐only treatment resulted in excessive N, K, and S, with optimal levels of other nutrients.

Soil pH conditions significantly influenced leaf nutrient uptakes, where the maximum accumulations of most nutrients were recorded in a soil pH of 6.5 (Table 1). Interestingly, total leaf Mn uptake was statistically low under alkaline soil conditions (6.9 µg/plant), almost 32 folds lower than at soil pH 6.5. It might be due to low leaf Mn concentration and biomass accumulation under high pH conditions. Likewise, the inoculation of Bacillus species as PGPR and fertilizer (75% or 100%) significantly increased leaf nutrient accumulation for most nutrients compared to only fertilizer‐applied treatments, except S and Mn. Results revealed that the total uptake of leaf nutrients N, P, K, Ca, Zn, Cu, Fe, and B increased significantly (p < 0.05) with PGPR inoculation by 1.48, 1.32, 1.85, 1.36, 1.59, 1.35, 1.35, and 1.52 folds, respectively (Table 1) compared to without microbial inoculation treatment (100%F). The interaction effect of soil pH and PGPR/fertilizer was insignificant (p > 0.05) in total leaf nutrient uptakes and concentrations (results not presented), indicating that the effects of each factor were independent.

Soil health indices and nutrient availability

Soil health indicators, including SOM, microbial respiration, and soil EC, were significantly influenced by varying pH levels and microbial inoculation treatments (p < 0.05). SOM was significantly higher under acidic soil conditions compared to neutral (pH 6.5) and alkaline soils (pH 8.0). This trend suggested that acidic conditions favored organic matter stabilization, whereas neutral and alkaline conditions likely accelerated decomposition, leading to slightly reduced SOM (Figure 5). Microbial activity, measured as soil respiration (CO2 flux), exhibited a significant (p < 0.001) increase with soil pH (Figure 5). The highest flux was recorded at pH 8.0 (45.81 mg CO2/kg), followed by moderate activity at pH 6.5 (34.14 mg CO2/kg), while pH 5.0 showed the lowest activity (32.52 mg CO2/kg). These results imply that alkaline soils potentially provided nutrients and reduced acidic stress to enhance microbial functioning. However, the moderate activity at neutral pH suggested a more balanced environment for microbial processes. EC significantly recorded a high in soil pH 5.0 (38.10 mS/m) and pH 8.0 (21.67 mS/m) compared to pH of 6.5 (Table S2). The high EC reflected elevated soluble salt concentrations in pH stress soils (both high and low H), which may stress plants and nutrient limitations. Neutral pH conditions were associated with the lowest EC, indicating improved salinity conditions and potential benefits for plant growth.

Soil organic matter and soil microbial respiration as affected by soil pH condition and plant growth‐promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) inoculation in Florida sandy soil. Bars with different lowercase letters are significantly different at p < 0.05.

In microbial inoculation treatments, PGPR combined with fertilizer significantly improved microbial activity, SOM, and EC compared to 100%F treatment. Notably, SOM was highest with P + 75%F (1.07%), slightly lower with P + 100%F (1.05%), and significantly reduced with 100%F alone (0.93%) (p < 0.05). Soil microbial respiration, a proxy for microbial activity and overall soil vitality, was significantly (p < 0.001) higher (mean 43.09 mg CO2/kg soil) under P + 100%F (1.66 folds), followed by P + 75%F (1.63 folds) compared to the100%F alone (26.3 mg CO2/kg) suggesting that PGPR stimulated microbial metabolic functions even under challenging pH conditions (Figure 5). EC was also highest with P + 75%F (28.84 mS/m), followed by P + 100%F (26.18 mS/m), and lowest under 100%F alone (18.94 mS/m) (p < 0.01). These results demonstrated that PGPR enhanced microbial activity, stabilized SOM, and maintained EC, whereas the absence of PGPR in the 100% F treatment reduced SOM and suppressed microbial activity. These findings collectively underscore the potential of PGPR in ameliorating adverse effects of pH stress on soil health, thereby contributing to improved soil functionality and resilience in sandy soils.

Soil nutrient availability was significantly affected by soil pH factors and soil‐beneficial bacteria drenching in the Florida sandy soils. This study found that soil NO3–N and K were significantly low at 18.7 and 80.8 mg/kg, respectively, at soil pH 6.5 compared to pH‐stressed conditions (either of pH 5.0 or 8.0), perhaps due to efficient nutrient uptake for higher biomass accumulation by the citrus plant at adequate soil pH 6.5. Conversely, soil available Mg and Ca were recorded significantly low at 5.0 pH conditions, while soil P, S, Fe, Mn, and Cu were significantly lower at high pH conditions. These observations demonstrated that soil pH significantly influences nutrient availability. Furthermore, PGPR inoculation significantly increases soil available nutrients compared to no PGPR (Table S2). Soil without PGPR‐applied treatments recorded a significant decrease in soil NO3‐N, P, K, B, and Mn retention of 22.2, 131.1, 73.3, 0.83, and 22.6 mg/kg, respectively, despite less nutrient uptake and biomass accumulation, which might be lost probably through leaching or fixed as insoluble complex. However, most available soil nutrients were observed to be relatively optimum to higher ranges (Melhich 3 soil extraction parameters). The interaction effect of soil pH and PGPR/fertilizer was insignificant (p > 0.05) in soil nutrient accumulation parameters (results not presented), indicating that the effects of each factor were independent.

DISCUSSION

This study explored the effects of soil pH and PGPR on citrus growth under soil stress conditions, focusing on parameters such as plant height, stem diameter, biomass, RMD, and leaf nutrient uptake. Results emphasized the critical role of an optimal soil pH of 6.5, aligning with prior research on nutrient availability and plant growth, as deviations increase stress (Alva et al., 1999). Long et al. (2017); Obreza & Morgan (2008) found that near‐neutral soil conditions significantly improved citrus stem, root, leaf biomass, and nutrient concentration. Anderson (1972) reported yield increases of 50% over 7 years and 200% over 10 years with pH adjustments around 6.5–7.0, supported by Koo (1984) and Quaggio et al. (1994). Conversely, pH deviations cause nutrient imbalances, reduced root and shoot growth, and shorter root lifespans (Marschner, 1990; Bian et al., 2013; Lin & Myhre, 1990; von Uexküll & Mutert, 1995). Citrus trees are highly sensitive to salt, limiting toxic ion uptake and transport from roots to shoots (Srivastava & Ram, 2000). Acidic or alkaline imbalances correlate negatively with citrus yield, canopy volume, and root mass (Obreza, 1995; Kadyampakeni, 2012; Kadyampakeni, Morgan, Schumann, & Nkedi‐Kizza, 2014). Salt‐induced hormonal imbalances exacerbate stress, reduce yield, increase leaf abscission and root mortality, and lower photosynthetic efficiency (Ghimire et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2016; Roosta & Rezaei, 2014). Our results confirmed poor citrus growth under pH stress due to nutrient deficiencies and element toxicity. High soil pH restricts micronutrient uptake, including Fe, Mn, and Zn (Graham et al., 2014; Masaoka et al., 2011). High pH reduces crop growth, as seen in decreased grape biomass (Ksouri et al., 2007) and lower plant height, leaf biomass, and root mass in blueberry and rose (Jiang et al., 2016; Roosta & Rezaei, 2014). Low pH limits N, P, K, Ca, Mg, and Mo availability, while H+ and Al3+ toxicity inhibits root growth and nutrient uptake (Kamaluddin & Zwiazek, 2004; Bian et al., 2013; Kaur et al., 2019). Additionally, low pH impairs water uptake by reducing root hydraulic conductivity, hindering nutrient absorption and growth (Kamaluddin & Zwiazek, 2004; Yang et al., 2011). Our findings support these observations, as extreme pH significantly reduced plant height, stem diameter, biomass, and nutrient uptake. Poor citrus growth under pH stress likely results from H+ and Al3+ toxicity and macro‐ and micronutrient deficiencies, highlighting the need to maintain optimal pH for better crop performance and nutrient accumulation.

Notably, the application of PGPR significantly improved all measured plant growth parameters, biomass, and nutrient accumulation regardless of soil pH variability. These results corroborated previous research, which emphasized the potential of PGPR in promoting plant growth through multiple mechanisms such as nutrient solubilization, phytohormone production, and stress mitigation (Compant et al., 2019; A. Khan et al., 2022; Malik et al., 2022; Mylona et al., 1995). A greenhouse trial conducted in a peat moss potting mixture with Bacillus inoculation resulted in a significant increment of plant height, stem diameter, root, and shoot biomass of the grapefruit seedings (Cano‐Castro et al., 2024). Such growth‐promoting effects are often attributed to enhanced root development, which improves water and nutrient uptake, thereby supporting overall plant growth (Shahwar et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2020). Similar results supported the synergistic benefits of various Bacillus species on corn, soybeans, wheat, kiwifruit, and pigeon peas as well (Akinrinlola et al., 2018; Aloo et al., 2019; Tilak & Reddy, 2006). Our study demonstrated that integrating PGPR with 75% or 100% fertilizer rates significantly improved plant growth parameters and nutrient accumulation compared to the sole application of 100% fertilizer. These findings underscored the potential of microbial inoculation to reduce fertilizer inputs while maintaining or enhancing productivity. This aligns with Adesemoye et al. (2009), who reported that PGPR synergistically improved NUE and decreased reliance on chemical fertilizers. Similar results have been observed in other studies, where PGPR enhanced nutrient acquisition, increased stress resilience, and promoted plant growth and productivity in rice (Biswas et al., 2000) and chickpeas (Elkoca et al., 2007). Overall, the results suggest that inoculants could be used to allow reductions in the current high rates of fertilizer and minimize environmental issues without compromising plant productivity.

The absence of significant interaction effects between soil pH and PGPR application underscores the consistency of PGPR's beneficial effects across varying pH levels. This finding demonstrates the robustness of these microbial inoculants under diverse soil conditions, likely due to distinct mechanisms such as nutrient solubilization and acquisition and phytohormone production (Goswami et al., 2016). Recent studies corroborated the role of beneficial microbes in helping plants cope with various stresses, such as nutrient imbalance, salinity, and drought (Mylona et al., 1995; Lim & Kim, 2013). Previous studies reported that B. amyloliquefaciens promotes rice and corn growth under salt‐stress conditions (Chen et al., 2009; Nautiyal et al., 2013). These findings highlight the crucial role of PGPR application in maintaining optimal soil pH, thereby promoting sustainable citrus growth and nutrient acquisition. Furthermore, the results of this study show that combining PGPR and reduced fertilizer rates presents a promising strategy for enhancing plant growth under pH‐stressed conditions in Florida soils, outperforming scenarios without PGPR application.

This study demonstrated significant variations in soil health parameters, including SOM, microbial respiration, EC, NO3–N, and Mehlich‐extracted nutrients across different soil pH and PGPR treatments. Deviation from optimal pH impairs microbial activity and biosynthesis crucial for organic matter maintenance (Rietz & Haynes, 2003; Tripathi et al., 2006; Yuan et al., 2007). Surprisingly, microbial respiration was highest at pH 8.0, possibly reflecting microbial adaptation to alkaline stress. Elevated respiration under such conditions may indicate increased carbon mineralization or stress‐induced metabolic activity rather than improved soil conditions. (Jeanbille et al., 2016; Radhakrishnan et al., 2017; Torbaghan et al., 2017). Additionally, pH‐stressed soils had higher EC than pH 6.5, which hinders plant growth and microbial biomass activity while affecting biochemical processes essential for organic matter (Johnson et al., 2014; Keren, 2000; Rietz & Haynes, 2003). The pH adjustment introduced Ca2 +, Al3 +, and SO4 2 − ions, which may have influenced soil electrochemical properties and EC, although these were not measured in the study. Soil pH is critical for nutrient availability, with near‐neutral pH (∼6.5) being optimal (Obreza & Morgan, 2008). Extreme pH disrupts nutrient dynamics by altering solubility and mobility, limiting uptake and increasing leaching (Wu et al., 2020; Alva, 1992). Consistent with previous studies, reduced Ca and Mg at low pH and increased P, S, Mn, Fe, and Cu at high pH decreased crop biomass and root growth. Conversely, pH 6.5 showed high P, Mg, Ca, S, and Mn availability but lower N, K, Mn, Fe, and Cu, indicating better nutrient uptake and higher biomass production.

Soils treated with PGPR, combined with either 75% or 100% fertilizer, exhibited significantly higher SOM, EC, microbial respiration, and available nutrient levels compared to soils receiving 100% fertilizer alone. This improvement highlights the multifaceted role of PGPR in the rhizosphere through organic matter accumulation, solubilization and mobilization of nutrients, and stimulation of microbial activity (Choudhary et al., 2011; Hayat et al., 2010). These results are consistent with previous reports that introducing microbial inoculum induces changes in the soil's overall microbial community, organic carbon, and organic volatile compounds (Ren et al., 2022; Stamenov et al., 2017). Bacillus spp. can solubilize phosphate and potassium, resulting in increased availability of nutrient ions in the soil, which the plants can easily take up (Masood & Bano, 2016; Saeid et al., 2018; Wani et al., 2007). The benefits of reduced fertilizer applications suggest that PGPR can enhance NUE, potentially lowering the need for chemical fertilizers and contributing to sustainable soil management practices. Interestingly, treatments without PGPR (100% F only) exhibited significantly lower values for all soil health parameters and available nutrients, emphasizing the critical role of PGPR in maintaining soil quality. Without microbial assistance, the soil ecosystem may lack biological activity to support nutrient cycling and organic matter maintenance, especially under pH‐stressed conditions.

CONCLUSION

This research supports the development of environmentally sustainable and cost‐effective agricultural practices by showcasing the ability of soil‐beneficial bacteria in pH‐stressed Florida sandy soil to improve citrus growth and reduce reliance on chemical fertilizers. The results also highlighted the importance of combining soil pH management with microbial amendments to enhance soil health and fertility and increase crop growth. Our results under controlled conditions indicate that incorporating PGPR reduces fertilizer inputs by 25%, suggesting potential cost savings for growers without compromising citrus growth, and providing a promising approach to boosting SOM, microbial activity, nutrient availability, and overall soil functionality.

A limitation of this study is that microbial responses were inferred from changes in plant physiological performance and soil properties, rather than being directly linked to microbial community composition or specific functional assays. In addition, detailed characterization of microbial community dynamics was beyond the scope of this project due to resource constraints. Future research should therefore focus on the long‐term effects of PGPR, strain‐specific contributions, and direct assessments of microbial community structure and functional mechanisms, including molecular and ecological processes that govern plant‐microbe interactions under varying soil conditions, particularly in HLB‐affected citrus systems.
