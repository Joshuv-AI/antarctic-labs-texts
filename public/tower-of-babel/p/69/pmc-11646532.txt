Heart Failure Risk Prediction in a Population With a High Burden of Diabetes: Evidence From the Strong Heart Study
Authors: Irene Martinez‐Morata, Arce Domingo‐Relloso, Ying Zhang, Amanda M Fretts, Gernot Pichler, Jose Manuel Garcia Pinilla, Jason G Umans, Shelley A Cole, Yifei Sun, Daichi Shimbo, Ana Navas‐Acien, Richard B Devereux
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the high burden of diabetes and cardiovascular risk factors in American Indian communities in the United States, prospective studies of heart failure (HF) in this population group are scarce, and the generalizability of previous HF risk scales may be limited. We developed a parsimonious HF risk prediction equation that accounts for relevant risk factors affecting American Indian communities, focusing on diabetes and kidney damage.Methods and ResultsA total of 3059 participants from the SHS (Strong Heart Study) (56±8 years of age, 58% women) were included. Five hundred seven developed HF. Progressively adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify risk factors for HF and HF subtypes. Predictors of risk at 5 and 10 years included older age (hazard ratio [HR], 1.79 [95% CI, 1.43–2.25]; HR, 1.68 [95% CI, 1.44–1.95]), smoking (HR, 2.26 [95% CI, 1.23–4.13]; HR, 2.08 [95% CI, 1.41–3.06]), macroalbuminuria (HR, 8.38 [95% CI, 4.44–15.83]; HR, 5.20 [95% CI, 3.42–7.9]), microalbuminuria (HR, 2.72 [95% CI, 1.51–4.90]; HR, 1.92 [95% CI, 1.33, 2.78]), and previous myocardial infarction (HR, 6.58 [95% CI, 2.54–17.03]; HR, 3.87 [95% CI, 2.29–6.54]), respectively. These predictors, together with diabetes diagnosis and glycated hemoglobin were significant at 10 and 28 years. High discrimination performance was achieved (C index, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.76–0.84]; C index, 0.78 [95% CI, 0.75–0.81]; and C index, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.74–0.78] at 5, 10, and up to 28 years of follow up, respectively). Some associations varied across HF subtypes, although diabetes, albuminuria, and previous myocardial infarction were associated with all subtypes.ConclusionsThis prospective study of HF risk factors in American Indian communities identifies that smoking, body mass index, and indicators of diabetes control and kidney damage (glycated hemoglobin and albuminuria) are major determinants of HF. Our findings can improve HF risk assessment in populations with a high burden of diabetes.Keywords: albuminuria, American Indian, cardiovascular, diabetes, heart failure, risk predictionSubject Categories: Heart Failure, Epidemiology, Primary Prevention

Type 2 diabetes is the main risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) in American Indian populations. 1 , 2 , 3 The prevalence of diabetes among the 5.2 million people who self‐identify as American Indian and Alaskan Native in the United States is 3 times higher (24%) than the prevalence documented in White populations (8%), 1 , 2 , 4 with prevalence rates as high as 70% of adults documented among some communities in the Southwest. 5 Diabetes can impact cardiac structure and function, and it has been identified as an independent risk factor of heart failure (HF) incidence and mortality in epidemiological studies. 6 , 7 , 8 Heart failure is a complex progressive clinical syndrome with low survival rates after clinical diagnosis (<50% at 5 years). 9 , 10 , 11 Identifying early predictors of HF is key to optimizing prevention strategies and reducing the burden of HF. American Indian communities and additional Indigenous populations in the United States suffer from high burdens of diabetes, hypertension, central obesity, and other risk factors with a potential impact on cardiac structure and function. 1 , 2 , 3 , 12 , 13 However, the burden of HF in these population groups remains understudied, and studies of HF risk in these communities are scarce.

In this study, we leveraged data from the SHS (Strong Heart Study), the largest cohort of American Indian adults in the United States (US), to develop a parsimonious HF risk prediction model that accounts for the most pressing CVD risk factors affecting American Indian communities. Importantly, HF risk prediction scales developed in other US cohorts did not include American Indian participants or focus on populations with a high burden of diabetes, and their generalizability to these population groups is unclear. Because diabetes management can influence the development of HF, we assessed measures of diabetes control and kidney damage in addition to clinical indicators and sociodemographic factors readily available in primary care settings. We also evaluated HF subtypes 13 to identify unique risk factors 14 , 15 in a subset of the population with data available. We hypothesized that clinical and preclinical predictors of diabetes control and kidney damage will play a key role in early HF risk detection in the SHS population, including clinical indicators that are not included in HF risk scales previously developed in other US cohorts.

METHODS

All data were collected, analyzed, and reported under agreements made with the sovereign tribal nations that have partnered in this research. Requests to access the data set from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to the Strong Heart Study Coordinating Center at https://strongheartstudy.org/. Requests will be reviewed by tribal research partners before data may be released. This policy is consistent with the National Institutes of Health Policy for Data Management and Sharing: Responsible Management and Sharing of American Indian/Alaska Native Participant Data. 16

Study Population

The SHS cohort recruited 4549 men and women 45 to 74 years of age from 13 different American Indian tribes in North Dakota and South Dakota (Northern Plains), Oklahoma (Southern Plains), and Arizona (Southwest). The baseline visit was conducted between July 1989 and January 1992. All participants provided signed informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by institutional review boards, participating tribal communities, and the respective area Indian Health Service institutional review boards. 17 One community subsequently withdrew participation, leaving 3517 participants at baseline eligible for the present analysis. After excluding participants without available information on HF endpoints (n=18) or on factors associated with CVD risk (including 278 participants missing glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), 63 missing data on diabetes, and 99 missing data on other risk factors), a total of 3059 participants were included (Figure S1). The baseline characteristics of excluded participants are described in Table S1. Incident CVD events including HF were confirmed by physician members of the SHS Mortality and Morbidity Committees following a standardized protocol. Loss to follow‐up was <0.2%. 2 For this analysis, we restricted the follow‐up until the end of 2019.

Heart Failure Events

HF events were assessed over the study follow‐up by annual surveillance of medical records, including outpatient and emergency room visits, hospitalizations, and medical tests. Mortality was assessed through review of death certificates, hospital and outpatient records, autopsy and coroner's reports, and informant interviews. Potential HF deaths were reviewed by 2 physicians, and any conflicts were resolved by a third independent physician. Fatal and nonfatal HF was diagnosed when 2 major or 1 major and 2 minor criteria were present concurrently in the absence of a condition such as end‐stage renal failure leading to massive fluid overload. Major criteria include paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea or orthopnea, neck vein distention, rales, cardiomegaly, acute edema, S3 gallop, venous pressure >16 cm water, and hepatojugular reflux; minor criteria include ankle edema, night cough, dyspnea on exertion, hepatomegaly, pleural effusion, vital capacity less than two‐thirds of predicted, or heart rate ≥120 bpm. Weight loss ≥4.5 kg in 5 days in response to treatment can serve as a major or minor criterion. A subset of SHS participants (n=245) with incident HF diagnosis had concurrent measurement of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), documented as percentage, available from echocardiogram, magnetic resonance imaging, or nuclear imaging. Participants with LVEF information were classified using the 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America guidelines 18 , 19 as HF with reduced LVEF (HFrEF) when LVEF is ≤40%, HF with mildly reduced LVEF (HFmrEF) when LVEF is 41% to 49%, and HF with preserved LVEF (HFpEF) when LVEF is ≥50%.

Covariates

Detailed information about baseline examination, physical exam procedures, and reporting of clinical covariates have been previously published. 2 Briefly, self‐report was used to ascertain sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral factors, and medical history, including previous clinical events, comorbidities, and medication use. Fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, and lipid profile (total cholesterol and high‐density lipoprotein [HDL] cholesterol) were measured by standard methods. Height and weight measurements were obtained to calculate body mass index (BMI). Type 2 diabetes (defined as fasting glucose ≥7 mmol/L or antidiabetic treatment) and impaired fasting glucose (6–6.9 mmol/L) were diagnosed by the 1997 American Diabetes Association recommendations. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the chronic kidney disease epidemiology collaboration (CKD‐EPI) equation and categorized into 2 categories: above and below 60 mL/min per1.73 m2. 20 Urinary albuminuria was estimated by the urinary albumin/creatinine ratio, and classified as normal (albumin/creatinine ratio<30 mg/g), microalbuminuria (albumin/creatinine ratio=30–299 mg/g), and macroalbuminuria (albumin/creatinine ratio ≥300 mg/g). Incident myocardial infarction (MI) events during the study follow‐up were identified from clinical records through the mortality and morbidity questionnaire surveillance.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic and relevant clinical variables according to incident HF status, as well as by HF subtypes. Rates per 1000 person‐years (P‐Y) were calculated for incident HF events and reported overall, and by sex, study center, age categories, diabetes status, HbA1c levels, and albuminuria.

Cox proportional hazards (PH) models were used to evaluate associations of covariates with time to HF events and time to HF subtypes events. Individuals without incident HF events were censored at death, loss to follow‐up, or at the end of follow‐up (December 31, 2019). Progressively adjusted Cox PH multivariable models were fitted, and hazard ratios (HRs) for the risk of HF and their corresponding 95% CIs were calculated for all covariates included in the models. All models were hypothesis‐driven and included variables relevant to CVD. The simplest model (Model 1) included sociodemographic (age, sex), and lifestyle (smoking, BMI) predictors; it was stratified by field center (Arizona, Oklahoma, and North Dakota and South Dakota) to allow for a different baseline hazard for each center. Model 2 additionally included established CVD predictors (systolic blood pressure, hypertension treatment, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, eGFR, and diabetes status). Model 3 additionally included indicators of diabetes control (HbA1c) and early kidney damage (albuminuria). Model 4 included having a previous MI preceding the HF event (yes/no) as an additional predictor. Analogous progressively adjusted Cox PH models were fitted for the 3 HF subtypes (HFpEF, HFmrEF, and HFrEF). 18 For each subtype, only incident HF events for that given subtype were considered, and other incident HF events were treated as censoring events. Because the follow‐up available in this cohort is relatively long (up to 28 years), and shorter‐term risk estimation may be more meaningful for clinical decision making, we ran 3 set of models restricting the follow‐up period to events up to 5 years, 10 years, and 28 years after enrollment. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed for every model by testing scaled Schoenfeld residuals. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor before including covariates in the models. Generally, a variance inflation factor >10 is interpreted as an indicator of multicollinearity. The variance inflation factor ranged between 1.02 and 1.49 in the fully adjusted models, which indicates there is no multicollinearity in our model. We conducted sensitivity analyses including education, hypertension diagnosis, and use of diabetes medication.

We used Harrell‐Pencina‐D'Agostino survival C statistics to evaluate the discrimination performance of risk prediction models. 21 In addition, we estimated Brier scores of the fully adjusted models up to 5, 10, and 28 years follow‐up to assess model calibration. The Brier score values were <0.1 across time points, which indicates a strong calibration performance of the proposed models. Internal validation was conducted using 1000 bootstrap samples. Internal validation using bootstrapping methods has been shown to provide more stable estimates for optimism‐corrected performance compared with other internal validation methods. 22

To assess the effects of competing events on the subdistribution HR, we implemented Fine‐Gray subdistribution HRs, treating death and previous myocardial infraction as competing events. 23 Fine‐Gray models allow the estimation of the event rates in those subjects who are either currently event free or who have previously experienced a competing event. The consistency between the main Cox PH models analyses and Fine‐Gray subdistribtion HRs was assessed. 24

As an additional analysis, we leveraged the clinical information available from subsequent study exams in the SHS cohort (visit 2 [1993–1995, N=2425], and visit 3 [1998–1999, N=1925]), to build Cox PH models including the predictors from our main models (smoking, BMI, systolic blood pressure, hypertension treatment, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, eGFR, diabetes, HbA1c, albuminuria, and previous MI) as time‐varying predictors. To run these models, we implemented a data augmentation approach as previously described. 25 Briefly, we included the baseline values of each predictor for all participants and subsequently added new rows to the data set for each participant and year of follow‐up, updating the covariate values at subsequent evaluation points (e.g., visit 2 and visit 3), and including the most recent measurements available for all participants until the occurrence of a HF event, death, or end of the follow‐up period (December 31, 2019). This approach allowed the investigation of the impact of temporal variations of selected predictors on HF risk. For the time‐varying predictor analyses, we built progressively adjusted models following the same structure as the main Cox PH models described above (Model 1, Model 2, and Model 3). All statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.0.

RESULTS

Incidence of Heart Failure

A total of 577 (16.5%) of the 3499 participants developed incident HF from 1989 to 1991 (baseline) through 2019, at an incidence rate (IR) of 9.5 per 1000 P‐Y (Figure). The highest IR was observed among participants with diabetes at baseline (16.9 cases/1000 P‐Y), HbA1c levels >7% (19.2 cases /1000 P‐Y), and albuminuria (17.9 cases/1000 P‐Y for microalbuminuria and 28.7 cases/1000 P‐Y for macroalbuminuria). Heart failure incidence was higher among participants with impaired fasting glucose at baseline compared with those with no diabetes (6.7 versus 5.8 cases per 1000 P‐Y); higher among participants with hypertension diagnosis compared with those with normal blood pressure (13.0 versus 7.7 cases per 1000 P‐Y); higher among women than men (9.9 versus 9.0 cases per 1000‐PY); and for older age groups (6.7 versus 12.2 and 15.1 cases per 1000 P‐Y for participants 45 to 54 years of age, 55 to 64 years of age, and 65 to 74 years of age at baseline, respectively). Participant Characteristics By Incident HF

The mean±SD age of the study participants was 56±8 years, 57.9% were women, 70% were either current or former smokers, 36.7% had a diagnosis of hypertension, 15.2% had a diabetes diagnosis, and 38.7% had impaired fasting glucose. At baseline, 25.4% and 22.1% of participants were taking medication for diabetes and hypertension, respectively (Table 1). Among participants with incident HF and available information on LVEF (n=245), those who later in the study follow‐up developed HFpEF were mostly women (73.1%) and had the lowest rate of previous MI (Table 2). The prevalence of diabetes and impaired fasting glucose was 66.4%, 81%, and 61.4% for participants who developed HFpEF, HFmrEF, and HFrEF, respectively over the study follow‐up. Participants with HFrEF had the highest rates of previous MI (8.4%).

Risk Factors and Prediction of Incident HF

During the study follow‐up (median, 18.7 [interquartile range, 9.48–26.41] years), among 3059 participants with complete covariate information, 507 (16.5%) developed incident HF. A total of 106 participants developed HF over the first 5 years (21% of the total number of cases) and 233 over the first 10 years (46% of the total number of cases).

In the fully adjusted 5‐year risk model (Table 3), age, former and current smoking, microalbuminuria and macroalbuminuria, and previous MI were significant predictors of incident HF. The HRs (95% CIs) for microalbuminuria and macroalbuminuria were 2.72 (1.51–4.90) and 8.38 (4.44–15.83), respectively, and for previous MI it was 6.58 (2.54–17.03). In the fully adjusted 10‐year risk model, the significant predictors identified in the 5‐year risk model remained statistically significant with the addition of hypertension treatment, diabetes diagnosis, and HbA1c. Albuminuria and previous MI remained strongly associated with HF risk at 10 years, although the magnitude of the HRs was attenuated compared with the 5‐year model. The C indexes (95% CIs) were 0.81 (0.76–0.84) and 0.78 (0.75–0.81) in the model up to 5 and 10 years, respectively.

In progressive models with the longest follow‐up period (up to 28 years), significant predictors of incident HF in the basic clinical model (Table 4 [Model 1]) were age, former and current smoking, BMI, systolic blood pressure, hypertension treatment, and diabetes diagnosis. The statistical associations identified in Model 1 persisted after the addition of HbA1c, an established indicator of long‐term diabetes control, and albuminuria, a biomarker of early kidney damage previously associated with HF risk, although the HR (95% CI) for diabetes was markedly attenuated from 2.62 (2.12–3.23) in Model 1 to 1.77 (1.36–2.31) in Model 2. 26 Both HbA1c and micro‐ and macroalbuminuria were significant predictors of incident HF, even after accounting for diabetes in the model (Table 4 [Model 2]), and after accounting for MI (Table 4 [Model 3]). The C statistic for the longest follow‐up period increased from 0.73 in Model 1 to 0.75 and to 0.77 in Models 2 and 3, respectively, with overlapping 95% CIs.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used variables readily available in clinical care to develop a parsimonious risk prediction model for overall incident HF and HF subtypes in the SHS, the largest longitudinal cohort of American Indian adults in the US. Our models were internally validated and remained robust in sensitivity analyses and after accounting for competing risks. Having a previous MI and albuminuria, a marker of kidney damage, at baseline were strong predictors of 5‐year risk, which remained significant but attenuated for long‐term risk (up to 10 years and 28 years), suggesting they are proximal risk factors for near development of HF. For diabetes and HbA1c, an indicator of diabetes control, risk prediction was significant long term (up to 10 and 28 years) but not short term (up to 5 years). The association of diabetes with incident HF was attenuated with the addition of HbA1c and albuminuria, suggesting that diabetes control explains part of the HF risk attributable to diabetes. Age, smoking, albuminuria, and previous MI were significant predictors of incident HF risk at all follow‐up time points. These results were consistent across the models including clinical indicators as time‐varying risk factors, supporting the hypothesis that temporal variations in smoking, hypertension, history of myocardial infarction, diabetes, and indicators of kidney function are relevant for the development of incident HF.

The overall IR of HF in the SHS (9.5 cases per 1000 P‐Y) is 2‐ to 3‐fold higher than IRs observed in other population‐based cohorts such as the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis), an urban multiethnic epidemiological cohort including Black (IR: 4.6 cases per 1000 P‐Y), Hispanic (IR: 3.5 cases per 1000 P‐Y), and White (IR: 2.4 cases per 1000 P‐Y) participants 27 ; and the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study (IR: 5.7 HF cases per 1000 P‐Y), a biracial epidemiological cohort of mid‐ and low‐income Black and White participants. 28

The role of diabetes in HF incidence and prognosis is well documented across population‐based studies, 29 , 30 , 31 and HF remains a leading cause of death among patients with diabetes. 32 Consistent with our findings, the HRs (95% CIs) of HF for diabetes were 1.40 (1.04–1.95) and 1.85 (1.05–3.28) in previous studies in the SHS cohort and in MESA, respectively. 8 , 33 In both studies, diabetes remained a significant risk predictor of incident HF after accounting for myocardial infarction and clinical indicators of hypertension, obesity, and renal function. 8 , 33 These findings support the role of diabetes to independently induce myocardial dysfunction, known as diabetic cardiomyopathy, beyond the effects mediated through other cardiovascular risk factors. 29 , 34 , 35 A growing body of evidence indicates that sustained hyperglycemia and insulin resistance can induce functional and structural myocardial changes, 7 , 29 , 32 , 36 , 37 and previous studies have consistently documented cardiac hypertrophy among patients with diabetes and HF. 34 , 35

Sustained hyperglycemia can impact the kidney filtration barrier by inducing vascular dysregulation and hyperfiltration of proteins in the kidney leading to the onset of albuminuria. 38 , 39 Albuminuria reflects changes in kidney filtration dynamics and serves as an indicator of kidney function and structural kidney damage. 26 It is also a marker of general endothelial dysfunction. Previous studies have identified that changes in albuminuria can appear at normal eGFR values, suggesting that albuminuria may be a sensitive marker for early kidney damage. 26 In our study, a baseline eGFR of <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 was not associated with higher incident HF risk, whereas albuminuria consistently predicted incident HF risk at all follow‐up time points in our study. Similar findings have been documented in the FHS (Framingham Heart Study) (HR, 1.71 [95% CI, 1.25–2.34] for albuminuria versus no albuminuria), 40 ARIC (HR, 2.49 [95% CI, 1.77–3.50] for microalbuminuria and HR, 3.47 [95% CI, 2.10–5.72] for macroalbuminuria versus no albuminuria), 41 and MESA (HR, 4.31 [95% CI, 1.58–11.76] for albuminuria versus no albuminuria), after accounting for other clinical predictors of CVD. 42 The magnitude of the association between albuminuria and incident HF in the SHS was the largest of these cohorts, likely related to the higher burden of diabetes and poor diabetes control in the SHS population at baseline (1989–1991). Albuminuria can occur as a result of several pathophysiological mechanisms including damage to the kidney filtration barrier, vascular endothelial dysfunction, activation of neurohormonal mechanisms, and chronic inflammation induced by diabetes. 26 , 41 , 43 Albuminuria is not exclusively a biomarker of diabetes‐induced damage, and comorbidities such as hypertension, obesity, higher lipoprotein production, and systemic inflammation have been associated with higher levels of albuminuria. 44 Previous population‐based studies in participants without diabetes identified an association between albuminuria and higher risk of HF. 44 , 45 In our study, the association between baseline albuminuria and incident HF risk persists after the models are adjusted by diabetes diagnosis, HbA1c, systolic blood pressure, and hypertension control indicators, supporting the existence of an independent association between albuminuria and incident HF risk. Albuminuria can thus be used in HF risk prediction, because it can serve as a sensitive risk marker readily available in health care settings.

HF subtypes are underlined by unique pathophysiology, cardiac remodeling, and response to therapy, and previous studies have identified different clusters of risk factors that predict different HF subtypes. 46 , 47 , 48 Consistent with previous epidemiological evidence, we identified differences in risk predictors across HF subtypes in the SHS population. For example: risk of HFpEF was higher among women, and risk of HFmrEF and HFrEF was higher among men, HDL cholesterol showed a protective association for HFmrEF, albuminuria was associated with HFpEF and HFrEF but not HFmrEF, and MI was a risk factor across all subtypes, but the magnitude of the association was larger for HFmrEF and HFrEF compared with HFpEF. Higher BMI was significantly associated with higher HFpEF risk but not with the other HF subtypes. These findings for BMI support that obesity control strategies can likely reduce the risk of HFpEF in this population and are consistent with a growing body of evidence pointing at obesity and increased adiposity as important risk factors for the development of HFpEF. 49 Despite these observed differences across several risk factors, diabetes and HbA1c were consistent indicators of risk across all subtypes, suggesting the important role of diabetes and diabetes control in the pathogenesis of all HF subtypes in this population.

Our study has some limitations. First, information on ejection fraction and HF subtypes was only available in a subset of the participants who developed incident HF during the study follow‐up, which limits our statistical power for the HF subtype analyses. Second, we were not able to assess the temporality between previous MI and the development of incident HF, because several participants with a previous MI at baseline did not have information on the date of the previous MI. Residual confounding and indication bias cannot be eliminated in this observational study, as shown by the association of hypertension treatment with a higher risk of HF. Last, although this study relied on the evaluation of traditional clinical and nonclinical CVD risk factors, a growing body of evidence is pointing out the importance of other factors, including involuntary environmental exposures such as air pollution and exposure to toxic metals, as contributors to CVD risk. 50 , 51 Historically, American Indian communities in the United States have suffered a disproportionate burden of exposure to environmental hazards, 1 , 52 and evidence in young American Indian adults supports that metal exposure is associated with markers of cardiac function and imaging. 53 Studies evaluating the contribution of these factors to HF burden in this population are warranted.

Our study has several strengths. The baseline examination of the SHS is a well‐characterized cohort with standardized assessment of relevant cardiovascular risk factors including diabetes and diabetes‐related outcomes, hypertension, lipid profile, kidney biomarkers, and lifestyle indicators, and protocol for HF adjudication. The loss to follow‐up was minimal. This, added to the long duration of the study (up to 28 years), allows for the assessment of short‐term and mid‐ to long‐term risk factors of HF.

Heart failure is a complex, progressive clinical syndrome with a long preclinical and usually asymptomatic phase. 54 Despite advances in therapeutic approaches in the past decades, the burden of HF is increasing as the population ages and the burden of diabetes increases. Improving early detection of high‐risk individuals without structural heart disease (HF stage A (American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association) guidelines) 18 is needed to implement targeted prevention strategies and therapeutic interventions for HF early in the disease process.

Available HF risk prediction scales previously developed in other US adult populations, including FHS, 55 ARIC, 56 and MESA, 33 may be insufficient to be generalized to American Indian communities and other populations affected by a high burden of diabetes and other cardiovascular risk factors with potential impact on cardiac structure and function including hypertension and metabolic syndrome. 1 , 2 , 3 , 12 , 13 Clinical indicators of diabetes diagnosis and diabetes management and complications including HbA1c and albuminuria, together with history of myocardial infarction and other CVD risk factors are risk predictors for HF both in the short and long term. These measures are routinely available in the clinic and can be used to identify high‐risk individuals and to set interventions and therapeutic goals for HF prevention. Our proposed model may serve as a relevant tool for early risk detection and prevention of HF in American Indian communities and other populations with a high burden of diabetes.
