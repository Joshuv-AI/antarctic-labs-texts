DHA and EPA alleviate atherosclerosis by synergistically regulating the aorta–liver axis via miRNA networks
Authors: Junli Feng, Yiran Jin, Yu Zhang, Yibin Mei, Yibin Pan, Qing Shen, Chunlai Zeng, Yong Cui, Ning Huangfu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe anti-atherosclerotic effects of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) are well-documented, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms, particularly via cross-organ crosstalk, remain unclear. Here, a high-fat diet-induced atherosclerosis (AS) model in ApoE–/– mice was constructed. Compared with controls, AS mice exhibited elevated serum total cholesterol, triglyceride and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, along with reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, impaired hepatic and cardiac function, enlarged aortic plaque area, and pronounced hepatic steatosis and cardiac lipid deposition. DHA/EPA intervention reversed these biochemical and histopathological abnormalities, and conferred superior hepatoprotection than Atorvastatin. Transcriptomics identified 1453 and 1663 differentially expressed genes in liver and aorta of AS mice, respectively. DHA/EPA up-regulated genes related to hepatic cholesterol metabolism and bile acid synthesis, and aortic genes encoding ABC transporters and PPAR signaling components. Conversely, they suppressed hepatic IL-17 signaling and endothelial adhesion molecules. MicroRNA quantification showed significant modulation of aortic miR-33a, miR-146a-3p, miR-155 and miR-223-3p levels, implicating regulation of lipid metabolism and inflammation. Collectively, these data indicate that DHA and EPA exert synergistic anti-atherosclerotic effects via the aorta-liver axis, regulating lipid metabolism, inflammation and vascular remodeling via a miR-30a/146a-3p/223-3p mediated network. These findings provide experimental and mechanistic supports for nutritional intervention of AS.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research, Molecular biology, Physiology

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disease involving large and medium-sized arteries throughout the body. Its core pathological features include lipid deposition in the arterial intima, foam cell formation, fibrous tissue hyperplasia and plaque formation, which ultimately lead to vascular lumen stenosis or even occlusion, triggering severe cardio-cerebrovascular events such as coronary heart disease and cerebral infarction1. According to statistics from the World Health Organization, more than 17.9 million people worldwide die from AS-related cardio-cerebrovascular diseases each year, accounting for 32% of the total deaths, making it a major threat to global public health2. The pathogenesis of AS is complex, involving multiple links such as lipid metabolism disorder, oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and endothelial dysfunction3. Among them, the oxidative modification and deposition of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) are the initiating factors of AS. Oxidized LDL-C can induce vascular endothelial cell injury, promote monocyte infiltration and transformation into foam cells, and simultaneously activate inflammatory signaling pathways to release a large number of inflammatory factors, exacerbating plaque formation and instability4. Currently, statins are the main clinical means for preventing and treating AS, which reduce serum LDL-C levels by inhibiting cholesterol synthesis. However, long-term use is prone to side effects such as liver injury, muscle soreness, and increased blood glucose, and even drug resistance5,6. Surgical treatments such as interventional surgery and bypass grafting can quickly improve vascular stenosis, but carry risks of postoperative restenosis and infection7. Therefore, exploring safe and effective natural active substances for the prevention and intervention of AS has important clinical significance and application values.

Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) are essential ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids in the human body, mainly derived from natural foods such as deep-sea fish and seaweed. Due to the lack of Δ12 and Δ15 desaturases in the human body, ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids must be obtained through dietary supplementation8,9. In recent years, numerous studies have confirmed that DHA and EPA can modulate lipid metabolism, suppress inflammatory responses, enhance endothelial function, and stabilize arterial plaques, offering good application prospects in the prevention and treatment of AS10–12. The latest population study has shown that DHA and EPA supplementation not only significantly reduces serum TG and LDL-C levels while increasing HDL-C levels, but also drives overall remodeling of plasma lipidome and gut microbiome characteristics, thereby ameliorating systemic metabolic disorders13. Meanwhile, results from randomized controlled clinical trials and animal experiments indicate that DHA and EPA significantly reduce the levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-18, and up-regulate the expression of anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-1014,15. However, most current studies focus on a single organ or a single signaling pathway, lacking systematic analysis of the cross-organ synergistic regulatory mechanisms of DHA and EPA. In fact, the occurrence and development of AS are not the result of the independent action of a single organ or system, but the synergistic response of multiple physiological systems such as metabolism, immunity, and nerves to external stressors under genetic background. Among them, the bidirectional regulatory relationship between the liver and aorta exerts an important impact on the pathological process of AS.

As the core organ of lipid metabolism in the body, the liver maintains circulating lipid homeostasis through cholesterol synthesis, transformation, and excretion, and its dysfunction is considered an important metabolic basis for the occurrence of AS. When hepatic lipid synthesis is enhanced or cholesterol clearance capacity is impaired, the levels of cholesterol and lipoproteins in the circulation increase, providing a material basis for lipid deposition in the aortic wall, foam cell formation, and plaque progression16. Recent studies have further revealed that the liver directly participates in the regulation of cholesterol metabolism and inflammation in the aorta by secreting signaling molecules such as small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) and non-coding RNAs. Under hepatic steatosis, liver-derived sEVs can inhibit the macrophage cholesterol efflux pathway and accelerate aortic plaque formation17. In addition, the molecular sensing of cholesterol load by the liver regulates systemic cholesterol homeostasis through liver-derived small RNAs and significantly affects the occurrence and development of AS18. Beyond the effect of the liver on the aorta, increasing evidence supports that AS-related vascular lesions further affect hepatic lipid metabolism, forming a bidirectional vicious cycle. Upon aortic endothelial injury, locally infiltrated immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils are continuously activated and release various pro-inflammatory factors, which can lead to increased systemic inflammatory load and metabolic pressure, aggravate fat deposition and hepatic inflammation19,20. Therefore, this cross-organ inflammatory-metabolic crosstalk may also play an important regulatory role in the occurrence and progression of AS.

The bidirectional regulation between the liver and aorta relies on complex gene expression networks and molecular pathways. Transcriptomics and non-coding RNA analysis have become key tools for deciphering this network. Transcriptomic technology enables precise identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the liver and aorta during pathological progression, through systematic comparison of gene expression profiles between healthy controls and AS states. It comprehensively characterizes dynamic alterations in cross-organ regulatory gene networks and provides multi-omics evidence supporting the bidirectional regulatory mechanism underlying the liver–aorta axis. MicroRNA (miRNA), as a class of small non-coding RNAs with a length of approximately 22 nucleotides, can regulate gene expression by targeting mRNA, participate in multiple physiological and pathological processes such as lipid metabolism and inflammatory response, and serve as an important molecular bridge connecting regulatory pathways in different organs. Combining transcriptomics with miRNA analysis is expected to systematically clarify the molecular mechanism by which DHA and EPA improve AS through aorta-liver axis, providing a new theoretical basis for the nutritional intervention of AS21–23.

Based on the pathological characteristics of AS and the natural advantages of DHA/EPA, this study utilized the ApoE–/– AS mouse as the research object, and combines technical methods such as serum biochemical detection, histopathological observation, transcriptomic analysis, and miRNA quantitative detection to systematically explore the ameliorative effects of DHA and EPA on AS. Furthermore, the underlying molecular mechanisms through which these omega-3 fatty acids exert their protective effects were explored, with particular emphasis on modulation of the aorta-liver axis.

Results

Ameliorative effects of DHA and EPA on biochemical and pathological characteristics of AS mice

After 12 weeks of intervention, the results of body weight changes in each group showed that the body weight of mice in the M group was significantly higher than that in the CK group (P < 0.0001), while there was no significant difference between the DM group, EM group and M group (P > 0.05, Fig. 1). Observation of organ morphology revealed that the livers of mice in the M group were enlarged, softened, and pale in color, showing typical characteristics of fatty liver, and the weight of epididymal fat was 2.5–2.8 times higher than that in the CK group. After DHA/EPA intervention, the liver morphology of mice in DM and EM groups was significantly improved, with reduced volume and restored normal color, and the weight of epididymal fat was decreased to some extent. These results suggested that DHA and EPA mainly act to reduce lipid deposition rather than suppress overall metabolism in mice (Fig. 1).

In terms of serum biochemical regulation, compared with the CK group, the serum levels of TG (1.50 ± 0.4 mmol/L), CHOL (19.15 ± 0.91 mmol/L), and LDL-C (5.76 ± 0.34 mmol/L) in the M group were significantly increased, while the HDL-C level (0.48 ± 0.12 mmol/L) was significantly decreased, indicating that the high-fat diet successfully induced lipid metabolism disorder in ApoE–/– mice. After DHA intervention, the serum HDL-C and CHOL levels in the DM group were significantly elevated, showing statistically significant differences compared with the M group. In contrast, EPA intervention led to significant recovery of serum TG, HDL-C, and CHOL levels, among which LDL-C was decreased by 20.1% in the EM group when compared with the M group (Fig. 2A and B). Hepatic and cardiac function indices showed that the serum levels of ALT, AST, CK, and CK-MB in the M group were significantly higher than those in the CK group, suggesting obvious hepatic and myocardial injury in AS model mice (Fig. 2C and D). The serum ALT level in the EM group was close to that in the CK group, with a significant difference compared with the M group. Meanwhile, the serum CK and CK-MB levels in both the DM and EM groups were significantly lower than those in the M group. In contrast, the serum ALT and AST levels in ATV treated mice (group A) were significantly higher than those in the M group, indicating that DHA and EPA have superior hepatoprotective effects than ATV.

Regarding the improvement of histopathology, Oil Red O staining results showed that a large amount of lipid deposition occurred in the whole aorta and aortic root of mice in the M group, forming obvious atherosclerotic plaques, with the plaque areas being 6.34 times and 6.05 times those of the CK group, respectively. After DHA/EPA intervention, the plaque areas in the whole aorta and aortic root of the DM and EM groups were significantly reduced compared with the M group, confirming that DHA and EPA can effectively inhibit aortic lipid accumulation and alleviate AS plaque formation (Fig. 2E, F, I and J). Besides these, oil Red O staining confirmed that hepatic lipid deposition in the M group was significantly increased (Fig. 2G and K). At the hepatic pathological level, HE staining results showed that hepatocytes in the CK group were neatly arranged with normal morphology, while those in the M group were loosely and disorderly arranged, with a large number of vacuoles (lipid droplets) in the cytoplasm, necrosis of some hepatocytes, and obvious inflammatory cell infiltration. After DHA/EPA intervention, the disordered arrangement of hepatocytes, vacuole formation, and necrosis in the DM and EM groups were significantly alleviated, inflammatory infiltration was reduced, and the area of hepatic lipid deposition was significantly decreased (Fig. 2M). Oil Red O staining results also showed that lipid deposition in cardiac tissues of the M group was significantly higher than that of the CK group, indicating abnormal cardiac lipid metabolism and lipid accumulation accompanied by the occurrence of AS. After DHA/EPA intervention, the area of cardiac lipid deposition in the DM and EM groups was significantly decreased, suggesting that DHA and EPA could effectively improve cardiac lipid deposition in AS mice (Fig. 2H and L).

Transcriptomic data analysis

Transcriptomic results demonstrated that a total of 1,453 DEGs were identified in the liver tissues of the M group compared with the CK group, among which 1,288 genes were up-regulated and 167 genes were down-regulated (Fig. 3A–D). GO functional enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were predominantly enriched in biological processes such as lipid metabolic process, cholesterol metabolic process, and bile acid biosynthetic process (Fig. 3E–H). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis also confirmed significant enrichment of DEGs in cholesterol metabolism, bile acid biosynthesis and other relevant pathways (Fig. 3I–L, Table 1). Further analysis revealed that the DM and EM treatments significantly up-regulated the expression of genes related to hepatic cholesterol/fatty acid/lipid metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, calcium signaling, MAPK signaling pathway, and the IL-17 signaling pathway. In the DM group, expression of Cyp11a1, Abca12, Acer1, Acsbg1, and Calm4 were significantly increased, whereas Hspa1a and Hspa1b exhibited the most pronounced down-regulation. In the EM group, Abca12, Acer1, and Elovl4 were significantly up-regulated, while S100a8 and S100a9 were most significantly down-regulated. Among them, Cyp11a1 encodes the rate-limiting enzyme in steroid hormone biosynthesis, which catalyzes the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone (the initial step in the synthesis of all steroid hormones). Abca12 promotes cholesterol efflux to apolipoprotein A-I (apoA-I), thereby facilitating the de novo assembly of HDL and mediating reverse cholesterol transport (a critical molecular mechanism conferring protection against AS). Elovl4 encodes an enzyme that specifically catalyzes the de novo synthesis of C26-C38 very long-chain fatty acids, playing an essential role in preserving cellular membrane structural integrity and maintaining lipid homeostasis. The S100A8/S100A9 heterodimer functions as a canonical damage-associated molecular pattern, can exacerbate endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and monocyte/macrophage infiltration by activating pro-inflammatory signaling pathways such as TLR4/NF-κB, thereby acting as a key driver of inflammation during the initiation and progression of AS. Expression changes of several DEGs in liver tissues across experimental groups were further validated by qRT-PCR and WB (Fig. 4). These transcriptional alterations exhibited strong concordance with serum biochemical results and histopathological features of aortic tissues, suggesting that DHA and EPA may systemically restore liver-derived lipid homeostasis, suppress cross-organ propagation of inflammatory signals, and ultimately attenuate chronic vascular inflammation, stabilize atherosclerotic plaques and delay AS progression (Table 1).

Transcriptomic analysis of aortic tissues showed that 1,663 DEGs were identified in the M group compared with the CK group, with 1,201 genes up-regulated and 462 genes down-regulated (Fig. 5A–D). GO functional enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were mainly enriched in biological processes such as lipid transport, cholesterol transport, and lipid homeostasis maintenance (Fig. 5E–H). Upon DM and EM treatments, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the statically significant DEGs mainly belonging to the cholesterol metabolism (Apoa2, Apoe, Cyp7a1), ABC transporter family (Abcc6, Abcg5, Abcg8), and the PPAR signaling pathway (Slc27a5, Apoa5) (Fig. 5I–L, Table 2). Among them, Apoa2 is the major component of HDL, its up-regulation can activate hepatic lipase and promote the hydrolysis of triglycerides and phospholipids in chylomicrons and very low density lipoproteins. Cyp7a1 encodes the rate-limiting enzyme in the bile acid synthesis pathway, its up-regulation accelerates the conversion of cholesterol into bile acids and facilitates their fecal excretion, thereby effectively reducing systemic cholesterol accumulation. Notably, the expression of Cyp7a1 in the EM group is 61.2-fold higher than in the M group, suggesting that EPA exerts a potent cholesterol-lowering effect in the aorta. ABC transporters, including ABCG5/ABCG8, mediate cholesterol and lipid efflux from vascular wall cells into the extracellular space, enabling subsequent entry into circulation and ultimate hepatic uptake and catabolism, thus facilitating local cholesterol clearance. The PPAR signaling pathway serves as a central regulator of lipid metabolism reprogramming and homeostasis. With in this pathway, the upregulation of Slc27a5 (encoding the fatty acid transport protein 5) and Apoa5 (a key regulator of lipoprotein lipolysis and triglyceride metabolism) further underscore the pathway’s coordinated role in lipid processing and metabolic adaptation. Expression changes of several DEGs identified in aortic tissues were also confirmed by qRT-PCR assay (Fig. 6A). Based on these findings, it is speculated that DHA and EPA may accelerate aortic lipid transport by up-regulating key genes involved in lipid clearance, thereby suppressing lipid accumulation and attenuating atherosclerotic plaque formation (Table 2).

Synergistic regulatory mechanism of the aorta-liver axis from the perspective of inflammatory signaling transduction

Transcriptomic analysis of mouse liver tissues revealed activation of the IL-17 signaling pathway in the M group, evidenced by the markedly elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as Cxcl1, S100a8 and S100a9 (Table 1). The IL-17 signaling pathway is a central mediator of inflammatory responses in AS, its activation promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Upon entering the systemic circulation, these cytokines act on aortic endothelial cells, facilitating monocyte adhesion and transendothelial infiltration—thereby accelerating foam cell formation and exacerbating aortic inflammation. As shown in Table 2, expression of inflammation-related genes (Angpt1, Mmp10, and Mmp13) was up-regulated in the aortic tissues of the M group. Angpt1 stimulates pathological angiogenesis and contributes to plaque vulnerability, whereas Mmp10 and Mmp13 degrade extracellular matrix components, compromise fibrous cap integrity, and promote lesion instability. Critically, these aorta-derived inflammatory signals may circulate systemically and exert retrograde effects on the liver, further aggravating hepatic metabolic dysfunction. Collectively, these findings support the existence of a “hepatic inflammation activation/aortic endothelial injury/hepatic metabolic disorder” inflammatory vicious cycle that accelerates AS progression.

Following intervention with DHA/EPA, this pathogenic cycle seemed to be significantly blocked. Transcriptomic results showed that in the EM group, the hepatic expression of S100a8, S100a9, and Cxcl1 in liver tissues were down-regulated by 93%, 90%, and 68% relative to the M group, respectively, which would markedly suppress activation of the hepatic IL-17 signaling pathway (Table 1). Concurrently, the expression of VCAM-1 in the EM group was reduced by 37% compared with the M group, suggesting diminished monocyte adhesion to endothelial cells (Table 1). In the DM group, Mmp10 exhibited the most pronounced down-regulation, implicating a marked reduction in pathological angiogenesis and improved stability of atherosclerotic plaques. Collectively, both the DM and EM groups significantly down-regulated the expression of several key genes closely related to the pathological processes of AS. These data indicate that DHA and EPA may ameliorate atherosclerotic inflammation and enhance the structural integrity of atherosclerotic plaques through coordinated inhibition of key inflammatory signaling pathways in both the liver and aorta.

Synergistic regulatory mechanism of the aorta-liver axis: signaling pathways and miRNA-mediated molecular bridges

In liver tissues, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs in the M group were predominantly enriched in inflammation-related pathways and lipid metabolism-related pathways (Fig. 3I–L), when compared with the CK group. Following DHA intervention, DEGs were manly enriched in the calcium signaling pathway and PI3K-AKT pathway, including genes such as Calm4, Calml3, NOS2, Ryr1/Ryr2, and Pik3r1. The up-regulated expression of NOS2 can promote NO release. The NO exerts vasodilatory effects, inhibits platelet aggregation, and attenuates endothelial injury. The PI3K-AKT pathway also regulates cell proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses. In the EPA treated mice, KEGG analysis showed the MAPK pathway and IL-17 signaling pathway were significantly enriched, with the core genes including Cxcl1,Cxcl10, Hspa1a/b, Lcn2, and S100a8/9. Both the MAPK and IL-17 signaling pathways are involved in regulating inflammatory responses and cellular stress responses, and their suppression can reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines secretion and thereby alleviate hepatic inflammatory injury (Table 1).

In aortic tissues, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that DEGs in the M group were mainly enriched in cholesterol metabolism pathway, PPAR signaling pathway and ABC transporter pathway (Fig. 5I–L). Both DHA and EPA interventions significantly modulated these pathways, with core genes including Apoc2, Apoa2, Apoe, Cyp7a1, Abcg5/8, Abcc6, Slc27a5 and Cyp8b1. By synergistically modulating the expression of these genes, aortic lipid metabolism may be restored, thereby mitigating lipid deposition and inhibiting atherosclerotic plaque formation (Table 2).

Regarding lipid metabolism regulation, qRT-PCR results showed that DHA intervention significantly reduced the expression of hepatic miR-33a by 68% compared with the M group (Fig. 6B). Bioinformatic target prediction indicated that miR-33a could directly bind to the 3’ untranslated regions (3’-UTRs) of ABCA1 and ABCG1. Consequently, DHA-mediated down-regulation of miR-33a may alleviate its inhibitory effect on ABCA1 and ABCG1, thereby enhancing cholesterol efflux via reverse cholesterol transport and reducing intracellular cholesterol accumulation. In contrast, EPA treatment increased aortic miR-223-3p expression by 2.1-fold compared with the M group. MiR-223-3p directly targets and suppresses HMGCS1, which encodes the rate-limiting enzyme in de novo cholesterol biosynthesis. Thus, miR-223-3p may inhibition of HMGCS1 impedes cholesterol synthesis and contribute to reduced aortic lipid deposition (Fig. 6B, Table 3).

In both DHA and EPA treated groups, the expression of miR-146a-3p and miR-155 in aortic tissues was significantly up-regulated. These miRNAs target the 3’-UTRs of IRAK1 and TRAF6, respectively, suppressing translation of these key adaptor proteins and thereby inhibiting activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. This suppression leads to decreased production and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, both DHA and EPA can elevate the expression of miR-30a, which targets CaMKIIδ and blocks the downstream phosphorylation events in the MAPKs signaling cascade, ultimately inhibiting vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and attenuating atherosclerotic plaque development. In addition, DHA and EPA up-regulated the expression of miR-155-5p, which targets and suppresses the expression of ACE. This suppression consequently reduces angiotensin II production, enhances vascular vasodilatory function, and mitigates vascular injury (Fig. 6B, Table 3). Correlation analysis showed the significant negative correlations between the expression levels of selected miRNAs and their respective target genes (P < 0.05), supporting the functional regulatory role of these miRNAs on their targets. WB results further also showed that the protein levels of ERK1 and p38-MAPK in the aortic tissue were significantly lower in EM group than in M group (Fig. 4C and D), indicating inhibition of MAPK pathway activation. These findings collectively suggest that miRNA-mediated regulation of signaling pathways contributes to the pathogenesis of AS.

Discussion

Under high-fat diet induction, ApoE−/− mice, due to the absence of apolipoprotein E, can develop stable hypercholesterolemia and AS, which is a well-established international AS model23. In the present study, the AS model mice exhibited typical dyslipidemia, increased in liver function indicators and myocardial injury markers, accompanied by histopathological changes including lipid deposition in the aortic root, hepatic steatosis and myocardial interstitial fibrosis. These data confirmed successful construction of AS model. Supplementation with DHA and EPA significantly improved systemic lipid profiles, normalized biomarkers of hepatic and cardiac function, and markedly attenuated pathological lesions in target organs, indicating their potential anti-AS effects. Notably, unlike ATV treatment, DHA and EPA did not elevate serum ALT and AST levels, suggesting superior hepatosafety and greater promise for long-term nutritional intervention in AS patients.

The liver serves as the source organ of lipid metabolism, and the dysregulation of hepatic cholesterol synthesis and excretion pathways represents an initiating event in AS pathogenesis. Meanwhile, the aorta functions as the target organ of AS injury, its impaired lipid clearance capacity can exacerbate plaque formation. Through transcriptomic analysis, this study proposed that DHA and EPA could synergistically regulate the expression of lipid metabolism-related genes in both the liver and aorta, thereby ameliorating lipid metabolism disorders in AS. This cross-organ regulatory mechanism not only deepens understanding of the anti-atherosclerotic effects of DHA and EPA, but also provides new experimental and theoretical insights into the multi-organ pathophysiological networks underlying AS development.

Inflammatory responses are widely recognized as central drivers in the initiation and progression of AS, and the crosstalk of inflammatory signals between the liver and aorta represents a critical mechanism for exacerbating AS-related injury24,25. This study supports the existence of a liver-aorta inflammatory vicious cycle in AS model mice and provides speculates that DHA and EPA may effectively disrupt this cycle, by simultaneously suppressing hepatic inflammation activation and aortic endothelial injury through targeted regulation of inflammation-related genes in both organs. These findings further improve the current understanding of the coordinated regulatory mechanisms governing the liver-aorta axis, indicating that DHA and EPA exert anti-atherosclerotic effects not only by ameliorating lipid metabolic disorders but also by modulating key inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby highlighting their potential as multi-target therapeutic agents.

Signaling pathways are the core mediators of gene expression regulation, and miRNAs function as pivotal molecular regulators capable of influencing multiple signaling cascades via post-transcriptional control of target genes expression. This study revealed distinct regulatory patterns of DHA and EPA in the liver and aorta. That is, DHA exerted a more pronounced effect on the calcium signaling pathway and PI3K-AKT pathway in the liver, while EPA predominantly regulated the MAPK pathway and IL-17 signaling pathway in the same organ. Nevertheless, both DHA and EPA exhibited highly concordant regulatory effects on lipid metabolism-related pathways in the aorta. Meanwhile, miRNAs function as “molecular bridges” in cross-organ communication. By targeting genes involved in lipid metabolism and inflammation, miRNAs tightly integrate the regulatory networks of the liver and aorta, thereby achieving synergistic regulation along the liver-aorta axis. Elucidating this molecular mechanism provides a robust experimental foundation for advancing our mechanistic understanding of the intrinsic anti-AS effects of DHA and EPA.

In conclusion, here a high-fat diet was successfully used to induce an AS model in ApoE–/– mice, and the model mice exhibited characteristic pathological manifestations including serum lipid metabolism disorder, hepatic and cardiac function injury, aortic plaque formation, hepatic steatosis and cardiac lipid deposition. In contrast, DHA (purity 85% EE) and EPA (purity 90% EE) exerted beneficial effects on the progression of AS. These protective effects appear to be closely associated with coordinated modulation of the “aorta-liver axis”. Specifically, at the hepatic level, DHA and EPA may systemically enhance the efficiency of cholesterol transport and excretion by up-regulating the expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism (Acer1 and Elovl4) and cholesterol efflux (ABCA12)—thereby significantly reducing systemic cholesterol burden26,27. At the aortic level, they can up-regulate the expression of genes encoding ABC transporters (Abcg5/8, Abcc6) as well as downstream effectors in the PPAR signaling pathway (Apoa5, Slc27a5), to facilitate cholesterol clearance28. Meanwhile, DHA and EPA may synchronously suppress the hepatic IL-17 signaling pathway and the expression of aortic MMP10/13, thereby breaking the inflammatory vicious cycle and markedly reducing plaque vulnerability and rupture risks. At the molecular mechanism level, DHA preferentially modulated calcium signaling pathway, PI3K-AKT pathway and MAPK signaling pathway, whereas EPA exhibited greater influence on the hepatic IL-17 signaling pathway, cholesterol/lipid/fatty acid metabolism and PPAR signaling pathway. Both DHA and EPA likely ameliorate AS pathology by mediating transcriptional regulation of target genes, via microRNAs such as miR-30a, miR-146a-3p, and miR-223-3p, thereby integrating lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses and vascular remodeling. However, validation of these regulatory relationships requires next-step experiments, such as the dual-luciferase reporter assays to confirm direct binding to target mRNA 3′-UTRs, or time-course measurements of miRNAs abundance, target mRNAs decay kinetics and translation repression. Despite these limitations, this study systematically elucidates the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of DHA and EPA on AS, with a focus on the aorta-liver axis crosstalk. These findings provide a robust experimental foundation and theoretical framework for nutritional interventions targeting AS, as well as for the development of functional foods aimed at promoting cardiovascular health.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials and instruments

DHA (purity 85% EE) and EPA (purity 90% EE) were purchased from Chengdu Yuanda Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; Atorvastatin (ATV) calcium tablets were obtained from Pfizer Inc., USA; Oil Red O staining solution and hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining solution were purchased from Hangzhou Haoke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; RNAiso Easy Extraction Kit, RNAiso for Small RNA Kit, cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit, Mir-X™ miRNA First Strand Synthesis Kit and TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II FAST quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) Kit were purchased from Dalian Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; ERK1 antibody, p38-MAPK antibody, GAPDH antibody, and corresponding secondary antibodies were purchased from Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; Organic reagents such as methanol, ethanol, and xylene were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.; Kits for detection of blood lipid indices (TG, CHOL, HDL-C, LDL-C), liver function indices (ALT, AST), and cardiac function indices (CK, CK-MB) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Experimental animals included 13 wild-type C57BL/6 J mice (male, 6–8 weeks old, weighing 20–22 g) and 47 male ApoE–/– mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing 20–22 g), all purchased from Nanjing Model Animal Research Institute. All mice were acclimatized for 1 week before grouping and housed in a specific pathogen-free environment at the Laboratory Animal Center of Quzhou People’s Hospital, with a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50–60%, a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, and free access to water and food. The experimental protocol was approved by the Laboratory Animal Management and Ethics Committee of Quzhou People’s Hospital (Approval No. 20240209-001).

Animal experimental design

After acclimatization, mice were randomly divided into five groups as follows: Negative control group (CK group): 13 wild-type C57BL/6 J mice, fed a standard chow diet and gavaged daily with an equal volume of soybean oil; Model group (M group): 12 ApoE–/– mice, fed a high-fat diet (containing 21% fat and 0.15% cholesterol) and gavaged daily with an equal volume of soybean oil; DHA intervention group (DM group): 13 ApoE–/– mice, fed a high-fat diet and gavaged daily with DHA (1 g/kg·d, dissolved in soybean oil); EPA intervention group (EM group): 13 ApoE–/– mice, fed a high-fat diet and gavaged daily with EPA (1 g/kg·d, dissolved in soybean oil); Positive control group (A group): 9 ApoE–/– mice, fed a high-fat diet and gavaged daily with ATV (10 mg/kg·d, dissolved in normal saline). The dosage of DHA, EPA, and ATV administered to mice was determined based on human-equivalent dose conversion, using the recommended daily intake of 4 g of either DHA or EPA, or 40 mg ATV for adults to reduce serum lipids levels29,30.

The intervention lasted for 12 weeks, and mice were weighed at a fixed time every week to record body weight changes. After the intervention, mice were fasted for 12 h, anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 5% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), and blood was collected from the orbital sinus of the anesthetized mice. Then, serum was separated by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Following blood collection, mice were humanely euthanized through cervical dislocation while under anesthesia, and tissues including the liver, aorta, heart and epididymal fat were rapidly dissected. Part of the tissues was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for pathological section preparation, while the remaining tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C for RNA and protein extraction.

Histopathological examination

Firstly, stored serum samples were used to measure the serum indices related to lipid metabolism (TG, CHOL, HDL-C, LDL-C), liver function (ALT, AST), and cardiac function (CK, CK-MB), using the reagent kit instructions and the operating protocols of the automatic biochemical analyzer. Each sample was assayed in triplicate, and the mean value was taken as the final detection result.

The operation steps of Oil Red O staining were as follows: The whole aorta tissues, which fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, were subjected to gradient dehydration, incubated in Oil Red O staining solution at 37°C for 1 h, destained with 75% ethanol until the background was colorless, rinsed thoroughly with distilled water, and photographed; The plaque area was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Tissue sections were dewaxed, infiltrated with 60% isopropanol for 5 min, incubated in Oil Red O staining solution at 37 °C for 10 min, then destained with 60% isopropanol for 30 s, counterstained with hematoxylin staining solution for 5 min, rinsed with distilled water, air-dried, and mounted with neutral balsam. Lipid deposition was observed under a microscope, and the lipid deposition area was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

The operation steps of hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining of liver were as follows: The fixed liver tissues were subjected to gradient dehydration, paraffin infiltration, embedding, and sectioning (5 μm thickness). After dewaxed, the sections were stained with hematoxylin staining solution for 5 min, rinsed with tap water to remove excess stain, differentiated with 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 30 s, rinsed again with tap water, stained with eosin staining solution for 5–8 s, subjected to gradient dehydration, clearing, and mounting with neutral balsam. Hepatocyte morphology, arrangement, and inflammatory infiltration were observed under a microscope and photographed for documentation.

Transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 50 mg of liver and aorta tissues stored at -80°C using the Trizol method, following the instructions of the RNAiso Easy Extraction Kit. The integrity of the extracted RNA was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, purity was assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 (A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0), and concentration was quantified with a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer. The mRNA samples of each group were separated from qualified RNA samples using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, fragmented with Fragmentation Buffer, and then used as a template to synthesize the first-strand cDNA, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. After purification, end repair, poly A-tailing, and ligation of sequencing adapters, RNA-seq libraries were generated via PCR amplification. Qualified libraries were subjected to 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing on the Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 sequencing platform.

Raw sequencing reads were filtered using Cutadapt software to remove low-quality reads (Q20 < 20), adapter sequences, and reads with a high proportion of N bases, yielding the clean reads. The quality of clean reads was validated using FastQC software, which were then aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39). DEGs were screened using the DESeq2 software, with the following criteria: |log2FC | ≥ 1 (namely fold changes ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5), and false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected P-value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed on the identified DEGs to identify the biological processes and signaling pathways involved31–33.

MiRNA expression and target gene analysis

The miRNA samples were extracted from 50 mg of aortic root-heart junction tissues stored at -80°C using the RNAiso for Small RNA kit (Takara). The integrity of the extracted miRNA were verified by 15% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, their purity was assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA), and concentrations were quantified with a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After that, miRNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Mir-X™ miRNA First Strand Synthesis Kit. U6 snRNA was used as the internal reference gene, and the expression levels of target miRNAs (miR-30a-3p, miR-146a-3p, miR-33a-3p, miR-223-3p, and miR-155-5p) were detected using a qPCR kit, with the miRNA-specific forward primers listed in Table 4. Besides, mRNA levels of partial DEGs identified by transcriptomic data were also validated by qRT-PCR, using the same RNA samples in RNA sequencing and gene-specific primers listed in Table 4. The β-actin gene was used as the internal reference for mRNA quantification, and PCR reaction conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 20 s. The relative expression levels of tested miRNAs and mRNAs were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with technical triplicates (n = 3) performed for each sample.

The above-mentioned miRNAs were selected based on previous literature reports. Namely, existing studies consistently support their distinct regulatory roles in the initiation and progression of AS. Specifically, miR-33a-3p and miR-223-3p are involved in the modulation of lipid metabolism homeostasis34,35. MiR-146a-3p and miR-155-5p are modulators of key inflammatory signaling pathways, including the NF-κB pathway and the NLRP3 inflammasome36,37. The miR-30a-3p, miR-155-5p and miR-223-5p contribute to vascular wall structural integrity and functional homeostasis, including the maintenance of endothelial barrier, the aberrant proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells, as well as dynamic remodeling of extracellular matrix17,37–39.

Subsequently, candidate target genes of tested miRNAs were predicted using online databases including TargetScan, miRanda and PicTar, and was restricted to DEGs identified in the transcriptomic analysis. The genes consistently predicted as targets across all three databases were designated high-confidence candidate targets of tested miRNAs. On this basis, a miRNA-target gene regulatory network was constructed by integrating target gene expression changes across experimental groups and their significant negative correlations with corresponding miRNA levels. Through GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, the key biological processes, molecular functions and signaling pathways regulated by the core miRNAs were systematically analyzed.

Western blot (WB)

Total protein was extracted from 50 mg of liver and aorta tissues stored at −80 °C by adding RIPA lysis buffer (containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors), grinding on ice to a homogenate, and centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the total protein extract. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein quantification kit. A 20 μg protein sample was separated by 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane after electrophoresis. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1.5 h, incubated with primary antibodies (ERK1, p38-MAPK, GAPDH; dilution ratio 1:1000) at 4 °C for 18 h, washed three times with TBST buffer (10 min each), and incubated with secondary antibodies (dilution ratio 1:5000) at room temperature for 1 h. After three washes with TBST buffer, protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit and photographed using a gel imaging system. GAPDH was used as the internal reference protein, and the gray value of bands was quantified using Image J software to calculate the relative expression levels of target proteins.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, and measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance, and pairwise comparisons between groups were conducted using Tukey’s multiple comparison test. A FDR-corrected P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
