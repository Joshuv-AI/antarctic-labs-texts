Extracellular domain 1 of human FcγRI (CD64) identified as the binding site for anti-FcγRI antibodies
Authors: Tosca Holtrop, Elsemieke M Passchier, Sophie O’Toole, W Joost Kraan, Kevin Budding, Jeanette HW Leusen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFcγRI (CD64) is the only Fcγ receptor capable of high-affinity binding to monomeric IgG and found on monocytes, macrophages, eosinophils, neutrophils, and dendritic cells. FcγRI contains three C2-type immunoglobulin (Ig) extracellular domains (EC1-3), while all other Fcγ receptors contain only two EC domains. For detection, several FcγRI-specific antibodies have been described. The most frequently used commercial antibody is clone 10.1, which is proposed to bind the membrane proximal domain EC3. Other anti-FcγRI antibodies include 197, m22/H22 and C09, but their exact binding domains are unknown. A clear overview of binding affinities and functional properties for all these antibodies is lacking. We identified the binding characteristics and functional properties of five anti-human FcγRI antibodies via flow cytometry, LigandTracer and luminol-based chemiluminescence assays. Subsequently we verified their domain specificity using chimeric FcγRI receptors in which EC1-EC3 were swapped with their murine counterparts. Surprisingly, all anti-FcγRI antibodies bind to EC1 of FcγRI, while swapping of EC3 had no effect on binding. Affinity measurements showed similar affinities amongst all antibodies, despite varying association and dissociation rates, except for clone 10.1, which has a > 100-fold lower affinity. These findings strengthen the notion that EC1 is critical for receptor folding, structural integrity, and high-affinity IgG recognition, reinforcing its importance in FcγRI and its potential implications for targeted therapies. By redefining the binding domain of anti-FcγRI antibodies, this study provides a more accurate framework for utilizing FcγRI as a biomarker and therapeutic target in immunotherapy and diagnostics.KEYWORDS: Antibodies, CD64, Fc receptor, FcγRI, IgG

Introduction

Fc gamma receptors for human IgG (FcγRs) are a family of integral membrane proteins that bind immunoglobulin Fc regions. Crosslinking FcγRs with immune complexes causes receptor clustering, which initiates downstream activation to trigger important host effector functions, such as phagocytosis, antigen presentation, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), and cytokine release.1,2 For example, targeting human FcγRI (hFcγRI, CD64) with bispecific antibodies results in strong activation with efficient tumor cell killing, increased antigen presentation and stimulation of long-term T cell immunity.3–5 FcγRI is the only FcγR that binds monomeric IgG with high affinity, but this binding does not trigger cellular activation. This IgG can effectively be displaced by immune complexes through a process known as ‘inside-out signaling,’ which involves cytokine-induced changes in the nanoscale organization of FcγRI in the cell membrane, enhancing the avidity for immune complexes without increasing receptor expression levels.6,7 The overactivation of hFcγRI has been linked to several autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematous, lupus nephritis, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, and neuropathies.5

hFcγRI is expressed on monocytes, macrophages, eosinophils, and dendritic cells and can be induced on neutrophils.8 Expression is upregulated by interferon-α (IFN-α), IFN-γ, and interleukin-12.9–11 FcγRI is structurally unique, with an extracellular region comprising three C2-type immunoglobulin (Ig) domains, unlike the low affinity receptors FcγRII (CD32) and FcγRIII (CD16), with only two domains. In hFcγRI, extracellular domain EC2, and to a small extent EC1, interact with the Fc portion of IgG, while EC3 maintains the receptor’s conformation and stability, and may also function as spacer to accommodate binding of the Fab regions.8–11 Previously published domain swap experiments and deletion mutants of hFcγRI showed a significant decrease in IgG binding when specific extracellular segments were altered.12–18

Over the past 30 years, several antibodies targeting hFcγRI have been developed and partially characterized, including clone 10.1 (mIgG1), 197 (mIgG2a), m22 (mIgG1) and its humanized version H22 (hIgG1), and C09 (hIgG1, Fc silent with LALAPG mutation).10,15,19–22 Some of these antibodies can block the interaction between FcγRI and IgG, thereby acting as antagonists that inhibit receptor activation. In some cases, formatting the variable regions of these antibodies as single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), which lack Fc-mediated effects, was reported to enhance their antagonistic activity thus dampening inappropriate immune activation in autoimmune diseases.21,23–26 In contrast, full-length mAbs or bispecific antibodies, have been shown to activate hFcγRI, promoting immune cell activation in contexts such as infection or cancer.3,10,27 Interestingly, some clones, including 197 and H22, have displayed either antagonistic or agonistic behavior depending on their format and experimental context.10,26,28,29

Although interest in FcγRI peaked 20–30 years ago, there is a growing interest in exploring this receptor as a therapeutic target, especially in cancer and inflammatory diseases. H22, for example, has recently been repurposed in antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) for the treatment of acute monocytic leukemia,30 while clone 10.1 remains the gold standard blocking antibody in FcγRI research. These renewed applications underscore the need to revisit and clarify the functional and epitope profiles of these well-used antibodies, many of which are still incompletely characterized.

To date, there is no direct comparison of anti-hFcγRI antibody binding kinetics and functional profiling, and only for clone 10.1 is the binding epitope reported to be located within or near EC2,21 or to EC3 of hFcγRI.15 For all other clones, binding is suggested outside the ligand binding domain.10,19,20 The specific domain to which an antibody binds can affect receptor activation, either enhancing or inhibiting receptor clustering and thus downstream signaling. Identifying which domains are targeted by specific antibodies and their functional characteristics is thus critical for both fundamental research and therapeutic applications involving hFcγRI.

The current binding notions were based on studies using FcγRI-FcγRII chimeras, monocytic cell lines such as THP-1 and U937, or primary monocytes/macrophages, which were limited by technological constraints that prevented the generation of stable receptors lacking human EC3.10,21,25,28,31 In this study, we generated cynomolgus monkey FcγRI and chimeric FcγRI receptors in which individual human extracellular domains (EC1-EC3) were replaced with their murine FcγRI (mFcγRI) counterpart. Due to the high sequence homology between human and mouse FcγRI, these chimeras maintained stable expression and structural integrity, and allowed the creation of constructs lacking human EC3. Since all anti- hFcγRI antibodies were raised in mice, they have no Fab-mediated cross-reactivity with the murine receptor.

Here, we present our findings and discuss the broader implications for the understanding and application of hFcγRI-targeted immunotherapy, research, and diagnostics. For example, in both research and diagnostic settings, it is important to block FcγRI with a suitable antibody, as nonspecific, Fc-mediated binding of staining antibodies targeting other membrane proteins can otherwise lead to false-positive signals in assays such as flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry. By clarifying the exact binding domain and the functional characteristics of these commonly used anti-hFcγRI antibodies, we aim to support a more accurate and informed use of hFcγRI as both a biomarker in biomedical research and diagnostics, and a therapeutic target in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

The anti-FcγRI monoclonal antibodies m22 (mIgG1) and 197 (mIgG2a) were a kind gift from Medarex/Bristol Myers Squibb, and purified from hybridoma supernatant by standard methodology employing ion exchange chromatography.32 C09 was produced as an Fc-silent human IgG1 (LALAPG mutation).22 Clone 10.1 (mIgG1; Biolegend, Cat# 305,002), H22 (MDX-33, hIgG1, AntibodySystem, Cat# FHC93420-100), and H22 Fc SilentTM (hIgG1, Absolute Antibody, Cat# Ab00731-10.3, denoted as H22FcX) were purchased. A mouse IgG2a isotype control was used as the internal standard for binding to all chimeras. APC-Cy7-conjugated mouse Ig light chain kappa (Biolegend, Cat# 409,503) and Alexa647-conjugated human IgG F(‘ab)2 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 109–606-606) were used as secondary staining antibodies. An overview of the Fab and Fc properties of all anti-FcγRI antibodies and isotype controls is given in Table 1. Most antibodies used in this study were commercially obtained and validated by suppliers to have minimal Fab-mediated cross-reactivity with murine FcγRI. Additionally, for clone C09, we experimentally confirmed specificity by testing binding to blood cells from human CD64 transgenic and wild-type C57BL/6 mice, observing no detectable binding to murine FcγRI-expressing cells (Supplemental Figure S1). This supports the suitability of our chimeric receptor approach using murine FcγRI domains. Moreover, all antibodies were tested in their native Fc-containing forms to reflect their physiological and clinical use. Fc-silent variants were included where available, and an mIgG2a isotype control was used to account for potential Fc-mediated binding of clone 197.

Cell lines

SKBR3 (HTB-30, human breast cancer cell line) and EL4 (TIB-39, mouse T lymphoma cell line) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. EL4, wildtype and transfected SKBR3 cells were maintained in RPMI with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), 1% penicillin and streptomycin (P/S), and 5 µg/mL puromycin dihydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# P8833). All cells were transduced using amphotropic viral particles made in HEK293T (human embryonic kidney cell line). The retroviral vector pMX human FcγRI was described previously.33 Puromycin selection (5 μg/ml) produced stable SKBR3-FcγRI, EL4-FcγRI and all EL4 chimera cell populations. All cells were kept in a humidified incubator at 37°C at 5% CO2. Cells were routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination (Mycoalert mycoplasma detection kit, Lonza, Cat# LT07-318) and discarded after passage 30. SKBR3 cells were used in initial binding analysis and LigandTracer experiments due to their strong adherence, required by the assay format. EL4 cells were used in subsequent binding and receptor expression studies due to their suspension nature, which is better suited for flow-based assays and stable transduction protocols, and reflects the physiological context of hFcγRI+ immune cells.

Flow cytometry assays

For flow cytometry assays, 1 × 105 cells/well were added to a 96-well plate (Greiner). All incubation steps were performed for 1 hour at 4°C, unless stated otherwise. In each experiment, 10 μg/mL (labeled) antibody was used or a titration range, unless stated otherwise. After each incubation step, cells were washed with 100 μL fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sigma, Cat# D8537) + 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma, Cat# 10,735,094,001) + 0.1% sodium azide (Merck, Cat# 02–0033-01)) and centrifuged at 300 x g for 5 minutes, unless stated otherwise. After final staining, cells were washed once with PBS, fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at 4°C and resuspended in FACS buffer. All measurements were performed in duplicate or triplicate using a FACSCanto II (BD Sciences) and accompanying software FACSDiva and FlowJo.

Real-time binding affinity measurements

The binding kinetics of the antibodies to human FcγRI were examined by using the LigandTracer Technology (Ridgeview Instruments AB). SKBR3-FcγRI and SKBR3-WT were plated out on opposite sides of a culture dish in an elliptical shape, at a concentration of 8 × 104 cells/ml and incubated overnight at 37°C to attach to the plate. Next, the plate was washed with culture medium and transferred to the LigandTracer apparatus. First, a baseline measurement of 15 minutes was performed. Second, association was measured at 5 nM FITC-labeled antibody for 1 hour and subsequently at 15 nM for 1 hour. Then, dissociation was measured for 2 hours by replacing the culture medium containing 15 nM antibody for antibody-free medium. The affinity of the antibodies was calculated by using a OneToOne with bulk interaction (BI) fitting model in TraceDrawer (v1.9.2) software (Ridgeview Instruments AB).

Design and production of cynomolgus and domain swapped chimeric receptors

The cynomolgus monkey receptor and all human FcγRI:mouse FcγRI domain swap chimeric receptors were constructed in the pMX_puro vector.33 Receptor sequences were obtained from UniProt; human FcγRI (Homo sapiens, P12314), cynomolgus monkey (Macaca fascicularis, Q8SPW5) and murine FcγRI (Mus musculus, P26151). For the domain swap, chimeric receptors, extracellular domains 1, 2 or 3 were replaced with the murine equivalent, and they all contained the human transmembrane region and cytoplasmic tail. Full alignment of chimeric constructs and predicted secondary structures is shown in Supplementary Figure S2. For mEC1-hEC2-hEC3, 91.3% of hEC1 was replaced by mEC1, as hEC1 and hEC2 share a 7 amino acid overlap and we wanted to keep the whole EC2 binding site intact. For hEC1-mEC2-hEC3, the whole hEC2 was replaced, including the overlap with hEC1, hence hEC1 was 7 amino acids shorter than the original variant. For hEC1-hEC2-mEC3, hEC3 was directly replaced by mEC3. The obtained plasmids were retroviral transduced into HEK293T cells, the virus supernatant was harvested and added to EL4 cells. Selection was performed after 7–14 days with puromycin.

Binding analysis of anti-FcγRI abs to FcγRI and chimeric receptors

To evaluate binding, 1 × 105 transduced cells were added per well, after which a titration range up to 10 µg/ml of the antibodies was added. After 1 hour incubation at 4°C, a secondary anti-mouse IgG (F(ab’)2 goat anti-mouse IgG Fcγ fragment-specific, AF647, AffiniPure, Cat# 115–606-008) or anti-human IgG (goat polyclonal anti-human F(ab’)2, AF647, Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 109–606-006) was added and incubated for 30 minutes at 4°C. Binding was evaluated via flow cytometry on a BD Canto II and analyzed with BD FACSDiva™.

Superoxide induction

Human polymorphonuclear leucocytes (PMNs, neutrophils) were isolated from peripheral blood from healthy donors by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. Samples from healthy donors were obtained through the in-house donor facility of the UMC Utrecht, under approval of the medical ethical committee of the UMC Utrecht (UMCU approval form F11P50). Erythrocytes were lysed using RBC Lysis Buffer (Biolegend, Cat# 420,302). Neutrophils from 6 healthy donors were cultured overnight with 100 U/mL IFN-γ (ImmunoTools, Cat# 11,343,534) and 100 U/ml G-CSF (ImmunoTools, Cat# 11,343,133) in RPMI with 10% FCS and 1% P/S to induce FcγRI. All subsequent pipetting steps were performed on ice to limit superoxide burst. 197, m22, H22, 10.1, and C09 were added at 10 µg/ml in 50 µl, after which 4 × 105 cells in 100 µl per well were added. Lastly, 150 µM luminol (Sigma, Cat# 123,072) and anti-mouse IgG or anti-human IgG (1:100 dilution) in 50 µl was added and placed a luminometer (SpectraMax M3, Molecular Devices) set at 37°C. Measurements of spontaneous production of superoxide were made every 40 to 60 seconds, starting immediately following luminol-crosslink antibody addition. The production of superoxide was measured in relative light units (RLU) for 20 minutes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Comparison of multiple groups was performed using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction, repeated measures one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction, or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.3.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.). p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

No significant differences in binding affinity between anti-hFcγRI antibodies to hFcγRI, except for clone 10.1

To facilitate interpretation of the results, we summarized the key molecular and functional properties of all antibodies used in this study, including isotype, and Fab- and Fc-mediated binding to human and murine FcγRI, as shown in Table 1. Assessment of the binding characteristics by flow cytometry showed that clones 197, m22 and C09 exhibited steeper binding curves than hIgG1, suggesting stronger binding (Figure 1A). In contrast, clone 10.1 displayed a similar binding profile to hIgG1, a gradual increase in binding and did not reach saturation. EC50 analysis confirmed that hIgG1 had the weakest binding to hFcγRI, requiring significantly higher concentrations to achieve half-maximal binding compared to clones 197, m22, and C09 (p < 0.0001, Figure 1B). Clone 10.1 also required significantly higher concentrations than 197, m22, and C09 (p = 0.0008, p = 0.0002 and p = 0.0001, respectively). No significant differences were observed between clones 197, m22, and C09.

To validate and expand on these binding differences, we next performed real-time affinity measurements using LigandTracer (Figure 1C-G, Table 2). In contrast to the equilibrium conditions during flow cytometry (done at 4°C for 1 hour), LigandTracer is conducted at room temperature with lower concentrations to discern distinct kinetic differences among the antibodies. During the first association phase (5 nM), hIgG1, m22, and C09 reached a plateau around the halfway mark, and during the second phase (15 nM) they reached saturation almost instantly (Figure 1C,E,F). Notably, m22 and C09 exhibited robust association rates of 14.3 × 104 and 12.5 × 104 M−1 s−1, respectively, significantly faster than hIgG1 at 2.3 × 104 M−1 s−1 (Figure 1H and Table 2). The discrepancy between similar binding curves and different ka values comes from using normalized data for visualization, which compresses the vertical scale and obscures early binding phases, while the ka were calculated from raw data. Likewise, clone 197 only reached a plateau after 45 minutes in the first association phase and failed to saturate at 15 nM, resulting in a much slower association (8.03 × 104 M−1 s−1). However, it was significantly slower than m22 (p = 0.011) but faster than hIgG1 (p = 0.014, Figure 1H). Clone 10.1 never reached saturation (Figure 1G) and had the slowest association kinetics with a ka of 0.17 × 104 M−1 s−1. Interestingly, no significant differences in dissociation rates were observed, likely due to the limited dissociation of most antibodies (Figure 1I).

The highest binding affinity for hFcγRI was observed with clone 197 (KD = 0.168 nM), followed by C09 (0.173 nM) and m22 (0.192 nM, Figure 1J). The affinity of hIgG1 was ±7-fold lower at 1.19 nM (p <0.0001). Notably, clone 10.1 showed the weakest binding, with an apparent KD of 21.85 nM, which is 18-fold lower than hIgG1 and ±123-fold lower than clones 197, m22, and C09 (p < 0.0001). Because the association curves for clone 10.1 did not reach full saturation in assays, these KD values should be interpreted as estimates derived from kinetic fitting. Taken together, the LigandTracer and flow cytometry results indicate that all tested anti-hFcγRI antibodies bind more strongly than hIgG1, with the exception of clone 10.1. Although clone 10.1 exhibits significantly lower affinity in kinetic measurements, its binding in flow cytometry appears less affected, probably due to the use of higher antibody concentrations and measuring steady-state binding, where slow association is a less important factor.

Given previous reports suggesting that anti-hFcγRI antibodies function as effective blocking agents, we sought to directly assess and compare the ligand blocking capacity. We also included H22, the humanized version of m22, with an active hIgG1 tail (also known as MDX-33) and as Fc silent (denoted as H22FcX) to discern Fab interactions. As expected, both 197 and H22 are effective blockers based on Fc interaction, with ~95% ligand inhibition (Figure 1K). However, excluding or silencing the Fc portion by using 197 F(ab)2 or H22FcX drops inhibition to 42.7% and 60.5%, respectively (p < 0.0001). Interestingly, H22FcX retains significantly higher blocking capacity via steric hinderance than all other clones (p < 0.0001), despite binding the same epitope as m22. In contrast, clone C09 appears most suitable in contexts where preservation of endogenous IgG binding is desired, as it only achieves 30.8% inhibition, significantly lower than m22 (39.7%, p = 0.034) or 10.1 (42.3%, p = 0.002).

Lastly, we evaluated the potential of different anti-FcγRI antibodies to induce receptor activation, to confirm if some of these clones exhibit dual functionality. A luminol-based chemiluminescence assay was used to measure superoxide release by IFN-γ stimulated neutrophils. The addition of clones 197 and H22 (MDX-33, functional Fc) resulted in a clear superoxide burst, whereas the other clones induced only a minor response, although all significantly more than with PBS (p < 0.0001, Figure 1L). Moreover, 197 induced a greater superoxide burst than H22, which is consistent with prior reports.28,31 Upon anti-mIgG or anti-hIgG crosslinking, both clones triggered a robust oxidative burst, with H22 eliciting a stronger response than 197 (p = 0.029, Figure 1M,N). In contrast, clones that only have Fab-mediated binding to hFcγRI (i.e. m22, C09 and 10.1), hardly induced activation, showing responses similar to the PBS control. As expected, 197 and H22 exhibit dual functionality, where they can block ligand binding through both their functional Fc region and partially via steric hinderance, and their capability of activating the receptor. In contrast, the remaining clones do not effectively block ligand binding and do not lead to clear receptor activation.

High conservation of cynomolgus FcγRI limits its use for mapping domain specificity of anti-hFcγRI antibodies

Previous studies have used FcγRI-FcγRII chimeras to define ligand-binding domains,15 but all constructs included human EC3, limiting the ability to resolve contributions from other domains. Due to the functional co-evolution of FcγR and IgG protein families, there is limited sequence variation and quite some interspecies cross-reactivity.34–36 Thus, using FcγRI orthologs to generate chimeric receptors preserves inherent high affinity binding properties, while likely introducing sufficient sequence variation to allow domain-specific binding analysis. Multiple sequence alignment between human and cynomolgus monkey FcγRI (cynoFcγRI) revealed high conservation of the FCGR1 gene, with an overall identity of 89.6% (Figure 2A and Table 3). EC3 is the most well conserved, with 97.8% identity, followed by EC1 and EC2 with ~93% identity.

To assess whether this minimal variation was sufficient for differential antibody recognition, we expressed cynoFcγRI in EL4 cells and tested binding of several anti-hFcγRI monoclonal antibodies. hIgG1 was included as a control for receptor expression, as it binds cynoFcγRI with higher affinity than hFcγRI.34 Clone 197 was excluded because its mIgG2a isotype confers both Fab- and Fc-mediated binding to human FcγRI,10 and likely retains high-affinity Fc binding to cynoFcγRI. Binding profiles showed that hIgG1 and clone 10.1 bound cynoFcγRI similarly to hFcγRI, while C09’s curve shifted from a distinct plateauing S-shape to resembling the curve of hIgG1 and 10.1, suggesting altered interactions (Figure 2B). Only m22 retained a binding profile comparable to hFcγRI. EC50 analysis confirmed that hIgG1 bound slightly more efficient to cynoFcγRI, needing lower concentrations for equal binding, while m22 showed comparable concentrations for both species (Figure 2C). Both C09 and, to a lesser extent 10.1, required higher concentrations to achieve equivalent binding (p < 0.0001 and p = 0.0025, respectively). These findings indicate that cynoFcγRI is not a suitable ortholog for mapping domain-specific interactions, as it failed to differentiate clone m22 binding.

Given the limitations of cynoFcγRI for resolving domain-specific interactions, we next evaluated murine FcγRI (mFcγRI), which displayed greater sequence divergence, with 59.6% identity to hFcγRI (Figure 2A, Table 3). Notably, EC1 and EC3 are less conserved (80.5%), offering more potential for differential domain analysis. Murine FcγRI was not tested in its wild-type form, as all anti-hFcγRI antibodies used in this study are of murine origin (generated via hybridoma or phage display technologies),10,21,22 hence no Fab-mediated cross-reactivity with mFcγRI is expected (Supplemental Figure S1).

EL4 cells stably expressed human FcγRI, with 99.9% of cells positive (Figure 3B). All chimeric constructs showed comparable expression: mEC1 on 98.8% of cells, mEC2 on 99.8% and mEC3 on 99.7%. Histograms revealed a more heterogenous expression profile for mEC1 and mEC3, with low- and high-expressing subpopulations (Figure 3B-C). In contrast, hFcγRI and mEC2 showed a narrower and more uniform distribution, suggesting more consistent surface expression levels. Human-to-murine domain swapping had a clear impact on antibody binding, as all chimeras displayed reduced mIgG2a binding compared to wildtype hFcγRI (Figure 3D). The mEC2 chimera showed the most pronounced reduction in binding, which may reflect the lower intrinsic affinity of mIgG2a for mFcγRI (~1 ×10−7 M)35 compared to hFcγRI (3.6 × 10−8 M)36. In the mEC1 and mEC3 chimeras, altered receptor folding may have partially hindered access to the human EC2 domain.

Attempts to generate additional constructs, mEC1-mEC2-hEC3 and hEC2-hEC3, were unsuccessful, as no surface expression was detected, likely due to improper folding or impaired receptor trafficking (data not shown).

All anti-FcγRI antibodies bind to extracellular domain 1

Lastly, the domain specificity was assessed, and while binding outside the ligand-binding domain is generally stated,10,19,20 specific information (i.e., binding to EC3) is provided for only clone 10.1.15 Surprisingly, we found that the binding of clones 197, m22, H22FcX, C09, and 10.1 remained detectable when hEC3 was replaced by the mouse variant, whereas replacing hEC1 with mEC1 completely abolished binding (Figure 4A-B). In contrast, H22 continued to bind to mEC1 to a similar degree as hIgG1, suggesting Fc-mediated interaction to hEC2 of this chimera. Similarly, clone 197 exhibited binding comparable to the isotype control (both mIgG2a), again indicating Fc-mediated interactions.

When hEC2 was replaced, neither hIgG1 nor mIgG2a showed efficient binding, whereas all Fab-mediated anti-FcγRI antibodies effectively bound to the mEC2 chimera. The fold-change in binding compared to isotype control ranged from 10.5-fold for clone 197 to 17.8-fold for clone C09 (p < 0.0001, Figure 4C). When EC3 is swapped to mouse, hIgG1 and all clones, except for m22, showed a significantly increased fold-change compared to the isotype control, indicating that swapping to mEC3 did not affect binding (Figure 4C). Of note, swapping hEC3 to the mEC3 had minimal impact on the binding curve, although the overall mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was much lower compared to hFcγRI (Figure 4A). Thus, the intrinsic binding affinity does not seem to change, but overall receptor numbers or the accessibility of antibody epitopes might be affected.

To highlight the contributions of Fab- and Fc-mediated interactions, we focused on a representative mAb, H22, and its Fc-silent variant H22FcX, alongside m22 (mIgG1, Fc-silent) and hIgG1 controls (Figure 5). Flow cytometry analysis across hFcγRI and domain swap constructs showed that H22 binds to all constructs, whereas m22 and H22FcX bind only to EC1-containing constructs (e.g., no binding to mEC1). These results demonstrate that Fab-mediated recognition is preserved in the absence of Fc engagement and confirm EC1 as the primary epitope for variable region binding, while Fc engagement enables H22 to also interact with EC2. Discussion

In this study we investigated the binding characteristics of widely used anti-hFcγRI antibodies. These antibodies are primarily used in research to identify FcγRI+ cells in antibody-based assays like flow cytometry, and in diagnostic assays to, for example, monitor neutrophil activation in sepsis patients.38 Additionally, most of these clones have been investigated for their therapeutic potential in autoimmune diseases or as anti-cancer therapy.20,24–26,39–41 Contrary to the long-held view that these antibodies, and particularly clone 10.1, target EC3 of FcγRI, our domain replacement experiments demonstrated that all clones investigated have Fab-mediated binding to EC1 of hFcγRI. This observation, combined with the different functional profiles, not only challenges the usage of anti-hFcγRI antibodies, but also strengthens our understanding of FcγRI structure – function relationships.

The exact epitope (e.g., EC1 vs. EC3) may not dramatically impact standard biomarker detection. However, it becomes crucial in functional studies, or when designing bispecific antibodies, physically masked antibodies, payload delivery systems, or blocking therapeutics. For example, antibodies binding EC2 allow for hFcγRI targeting even when the receptor is saturated with IgG without impairing natural functioning. Such clones are often used in bispecific formats to recruit cytotoxic effector cells to selectively eliminate malignant cells. MDX-210, a m22 F(ab) and anti-HER2/neu F(ab), enhanced ADCC and phagocytosis in vitro, and showed moderate biological activity in Phase I/II clinical trials for breast and ovarian cancer.27,40,42 The humanized bispecific MDX-H210, with a H22 F(ab’) fragment, showed promising results and minimal toxicity in an initial clinical trial, but almost half of the patients eventually developed anti-drug antibody responses.41

A major challenge in using anti-FcγRI bispecific antibodies for solid tumors is their high-affinity binding to circulating monocytes and tissue macrophages, which reduces tumor bioavailability. This sink effect can be mitigated by modulating Fab-arm affinities; enhancing binding to the tumor-associated antigen while lowering FcγRI affinity. However, this approach has limitations: study results using HER2/CD3-targeted bispecifics have shown that high CD3 affinity triggers off-target T cell activation and cytokine release, while overly strong HER2 binding risks damaging healthy HER2-expressing tissues.43 An alternative is the use of conditionally active bispecifics, such as protease-activated formats where the hFcγRI-binding arm is masked by a recombinant extracellular domain, which is cleaved by proteases present in the tumor microenvironment.44 For such designs, precise knowledge of the antibody’s binding domain is crucial.

Fab-mediated antibodies targeting EC2 of FcγRI are more suitable for therapeutic inhibiting pathogenic IgG binding in autoimmune conditions,5 where immune complexes formed by autoantibodies against self-antigens activate FcγRI and drive inflammation.9,45–47 Anti-hFcγRI antibodies binding near or within EC2 may prevent endogenous IgG and immune complex binding, thus preventing receptor activation and offering insights into the role of FcγRI in autoimmunity. Our data indicate that interference between antibody Fab engagement and Fc binding is unlikely to result from direct epitope overlap: for m22 and H22, Fab-mediated binding occurs outside the ligand-binding domain (EC2), and for clone 197, prior studies suggest binding outside EC2.10,19,27 However, most Fab-binding anti-hFcγRI antibodies tested here (10.1, m22, H22FcX) only achieve 40% inhibition. Dual-binding clones 197 and H22 do show strong ligand binding inhibition, but activate the receptor upon binding, making them less suitable in an overactive immune system. Fc-modified formations (F(ab), F(ab')2, scFvs variants or Fc-silenced mutants) reduce hFcγRI activation, but also impair blocking efficacy, stability, and manufacturability.

Nonetheless, clones 197, 10.1 and H22 (scFv) have shown promising results in treatment of immune thrombocytopenia purpura, rheumatoid arthritis and the neutralization of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor.24,26,48,49 It is possible that EC1-directed antibodies sterically or mechanically interfere with Fc interactions, thereby disrupting immune complex-induced receptor clustering, a key step in signal initiation.50 To date, no antibodies specific to EC3 have been described, and antibodies recognizing EC2 were only recently reported by us.51

It is also important to consider the impact of FcγRI in antibody-based assays such as flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry. Many anti-human monoclonal antibodies targeting other membrane proteins are of mouse IgG2a or rat IgG2b isotype, or a rabbit polyclonal, which cause nonspecific binding to FcγRI, independent of their intended antigen. This can lead to false-positives, especially when looking in mixed populations such as peripheral blood mononuclear cells, which contain naturally FcγRI-expressing cells such monocytes and macrophages. This consideration is also important when looking at neuronal cell populations, as FcγRI is also expressed on neurons.5,52,53 Using specific FcγRI-blocking antibodies could prevent these undesired artifacts.

The identification of EC1 as the primary binding domain calls for a reevaluation of FcγRI structural dynamics. Constructs lacking EC1, such as the hEC2-hEC3 chimera, failed to reach the cell surface, consistent with a critical role for EC1 in receptor folding and proper extracellular domain assembly. Structural and mutagenesis studies have suggested that EC1 contributes to stabilizing the extracellular domain assembly, indirectly facilitating high-affinity IgG recognition.12–14 Affinity toward hIgG1 is reduced when hEC1 is replaced with its murine or FcγRII counterpart while preserving specificity.15 FcγRI stability is maintained through interactions with EC2 and EC3. EC2 directly engages the lower hinge region of Fc, serving as a primary IgG contact site, while EC3 contributes to overall receptor conformation and extracellular positioning.12,13,16 In our study, the mEC2 chimera consistently showed higher MFI, suggesting improved receptor folding or EC1 epitope accessibility. In contrast, we observed an overall reduction in MFI with the mEC3 chimera, while the binding profile remained the same. Since the antibodies and hIgG1 isotype have no Fab-mediated binding to this domain, it is likely that the reduced overall binding reflects conformational changes that affect the accessibility of epitopes in EC1 and EC2, as there is large flexibility possible in the EC2-EC3 linker region.54 These findings support a model of inter-domain dependency, in which optimal affinity and activation of FcγRI relies on the combined and balanced contributions of all three extracellular domains, where EC1 ensures proper receptor conformation and affinity, EC2 facilitates direct ligand binding, and EC3 refines the receptor’s overall orientation and thus affinity.

Our findings refine the current understanding of FcγRI by showing that all commonly used anti-hFcγRI antibodies bind specifically to EC1, contrary to prior assumptions. This has direct implications for biomedical research and therapeutic development, as functional differences among antibodies are not due to the differences in domain-specificity or binding affinity, but rather stem from Fc-related properties or from structural changes in receptor orientation upon binding. Researchers and clinicians using anti-FcγRI antibodies should therefore consider these functional differences when designing experiments, interpreting results, or developing targeted therapies. Future studies, such as site-directed mutagenesis of EC1 residues or structural analysis of Fab:FcγRI complexes, could provide direct mechanistic evidence of EC1 engagement and further inform the design of improved FcγRI-targeted therapeutics. In conclusion, this study highlights the need for rigorous validation of antibody specificity and function, and by identifying EC1 as the true binding domain, we aim to support more accurate and effective use of hFcγRI as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target.
