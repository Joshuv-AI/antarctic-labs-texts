Calcium in major trauma: in search of the Holy Grail?
Authors: Dries Helsloot
Published: 2025

In their meta-analysis, Palitnonkiat et al confirm that hypocalcemia is associated with increased mortality in major trauma.1

This finding raises the question: could calcium supplementation be the Holy Grail, a low-risk and low-cost intervention to improve survival? Yet, before embarking on this quest, we must carefully reflect on what this finding truly means.

A common pitfall in research is to mistake correlation for causation. From a distance, they can appear similar; up close, they may be very different. While this systematic review found a correlation between hypocalcemia and mortality, it remains unclear whether it is a causal factor or only a marker to predict it.

In critical care, the normalization heuristic, a tendency to normalize every laboratory value, can be seductive.2 However, striving for euboxia may be misleading, particularly when based on single measurements without accounting for dynamic physiological processes. No strong evidence has been found yet to support the benefit of calcium supplementation in trauma.3 Moreover, in critical illness, mild hypocalcemia may actually protect cells from calcium overload and mitochondrial death.4

Our understanding of ionized calcium kinetics during trauma remains incomplete. Without clarity on the underlying mechanisms, the risk of overcorrection and consequent hypercalcemia, also associated with poor outcomes, cannot be ignored. The relationship between ionized calcium and outcome is indeed parabolic, not linear.5

Moreover, before considering intervention trials, we need a clearer path: agreement on precise definitions of hypocalcemia and normocalcemia, identification of appropriate patient subgroups, and determination of clinically meaningful endpoints. A one-size-fits-all strategy is unlikely to be successful in a condition as heterogeneous as trauma.

This meta-analysis is an important signpost in our search for the Holy Grail. The real challenge lies ahead: seeking the right direction by deepening our understanding of calcium physiology before attempting to correct it. Let’s not forget to treat the patient, not just the numbers.
