Proteomics to Metabolomics: A New Insight into the Pathogenesis of Hypertensive Nephropathy
Authors: Yasin Eshraghi, Maryam Abedi, Yousof Gheisari
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertensive nephropathy (HN) is a high-burden disorder and a leading cause of end-stage renal disease. Despite huge investigations, the underlying mechanisms are yet largely unknown. Systems biology is a promising approach to providing a comprehensive insight into this complex disorder.MethodsProteome profiles of kidney tubulointerstitium and outer and inner cortex from a rat model of HN were retrieved from the proteomics identification database, and the quality of the datasets was assessed. Proteins that exhibited differential expression were detected and their interactions were analyzed in the kidney sub-compartments. Furthermore, enzymes were linked to the attributed metabolites. Functional enrichment analyses were performed to identify key pathways and processes based on the differentially expressed proteins and predicted metabolites.ResultsProteasome-mediated protein degradation, actin cytoskeleton organization, and Rho GTPase signaling pathway are involved in the pathogenesis of HN. Furthermore, tissue hypoxia and dysregulated energy homeostasis are among the key underlying events. The metabolism of purine and amino acids is also affected in HN.ConclusionAlthough the proposed pathogenic mechanisms remain to be further validated in experimental studies, this study contributes to the understanding of the molecular mechanisms of HN through a systematic unsupervised approach. Considering the significant alterations of metabolic pathways, HN can be viewed as an “acquired error of metabolism.”

Introduction

Hypertension is the foremost contributor to both cardiovascular disease and premature mortality on a global scale [1]. Hypertensive nephropathy (HN) is a progressive disorder and the second leading cause of end-stage renal disease [2]. Characterized by chronic hypertension-induced damage to the kidneys, HN encompasses a range of pathological alterations including vascular remodeling, glomerular hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Multifaceted interactions between hemodynamic, immunological, and molecular factors are involved in HN [3].

Despite its high burden, the molecular aspects of HN are not yet comprehensively understood. The current therapeutic strategies for HN remain limited due to an incomplete understanding of the precise mechanisms driving the disease progression. Hence, it is critical to provide comprehensive insights into the pathogenesis of HN. The recent opportunity to generate high-throughput data in line with the advancements of big data analysis allows deep investigation of complex disorders [4]. This systems approach enables the identification of key biomolecules, signaling pathways, and networks involved in the development and progression of HN.

In this study, a systematic pipeline was pursued to re-analyze two proteome profiling datasets obtained from the kidney cortex and tubule of a rodent model of HN. While proteomics datasets provide useful insights into alterations within a critical biomolecular layer, they are most useful when integrated with other layers. Hence, the primary objective of this research was to build comprehensive integrative networks that shed light on key processes within distinct kidney sub-compartments. The findings of this study underline the pivotal role of metabolic alterations in HN.

Methods

Proteomics Data Acquisition

The PXD012889 dataset has been previously generated by Vethe et al. [5] to explore the proteome of the inner and outer cortex using a two-kidney, one-clip (2K1C) rat model of HN. The rats were sacrificed 23 weeks after the surgery and the non-clipped kidneys and corresponding kidneys from the control animals were harvested and frozen at −20°C. The juxtamedullary cortex and the outer cortex tissues were dissected and processed for protein extraction and proteome profiling. The PXD002106 dataset was generated by the same group to explore the alterations of tubulointerstitium in HN [6]. Twenty-three weeks after clipping, the rats were sacrificed and the non-clipped kidneys were harvested. Protein extraction was performed on microdissected tubulointerstitial samples that were isolated from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues. The above datasets were retrieved from the proteomics identification (PRIDE) database [7].

Identification and Quantification of Proteins

MaxQuant v1.6 [8] was used to convert raw datasets to data tables. The search was carried out for the Uniprot_Rattus norvegicus proteome database (January 26, 2019, 24,523 entities) by Andromeda, the integrated search algorithm [9]. Datasets were analyzed with the following parameters: (a) trypsin was specified as a cleavage enzyme and up to 2 missed cleavages were allowed, (b) carbamidomethylation was selected as a fix modification and protein N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were selected as variable modifications, (c) match between runs parameter was enabled, and (d) false discovery rate (FDR) was considered at 1% for protein and peptide identification. For the mass analyzer parameters, the precursor and fragment mass tolerance were set to 20 ppm. To quantify the identified proteins, the minimum ratio count was set at two. The “revert” option was used for decoy database construction.

Quality Control Assessment

The quality of datasets was tested using principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering with the Euclidean cluster technique, along with the robust estimation of parameters in the Mahalanobis distance. These analyses were performed using ggplot2 package [10] of R software [11].

Differential Expression Analysis

Perseus version 1.6.1.4 [12] was used to analyze the label-free quantification intensities obtained by the preliminary analysis of MaxQuant. First, the identified irrelevant protein groups including contaminants, those only identified by site, and hits to the reverse database were eliminated. Then, the data were transformed into the logarithmic scale with base 2. Proteins with at least three valid values were filtered out from each group. Missing values were imputed by assuming a normal distribution with a width of 0.5 and 1.8 downshifts. Student t test (permutation-based FDR ≤0.05) was carried out to detect DEPs.

Prediction of Metabolites

To predict metabolites, which may have a connection with the differentially expressed enzymes, all the related metabolites were first identified by Metscape plugin version 3.0 [13] of Cytoscape version 3.7 [14]. Then, the metabolites related to specific enzymes in metabolic pathways were selected using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) version 49932394 (QIAGEN Inc.) (https://www.qiagenbioinformatics.com/products/ingenuity-pathway-analysis).

Pathway Enrichment Analysis

IPA was applied for core analysis. The directional changes were identified by the calculated z-score using the Ingenuity Knowledge Base. Z-score >2 was considered as a common significant threshold for screening the altered pathways. Fisher’s exact test was utilized to select significant pathways with adjusted p value ≤0.05.

Protein-Protein Interaction Network Construction and Functional Enrichment Analysis

Interactions between the DEPs were identified using the STRING database (version 11.1) of the Cytoscape CluePedia plugin (version 1.5.5) [15]. ClueGO plugin (version 2.5.5) [16] was applied for the functional enrichment analysis with a threshold of FDR ≤0.05. The highly connected sites of the networks were determined by Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) plugin (version 1.5.1) [17].

Metabolite-Protein Interaction Network Construction

The identification of links between metabolites and enzymes was facilitated by the MetScape plugin of Cytoscape. The search in MetScape was based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, using the pathway-based option. The constructed network includes compounds, reactions, enzymes, and genes.

Metabolites Functional and Pathway Enrichment Analysis

MetaboAnalyst 4.0 was utilized to perform enrichment analysis for the predicted metabolites using data retrieved from KEGG and Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB) [18–20]. Out-degree centrality was employed for pathway topology analysis and the Fisher’s exact test was utilized for the over-representation analysis.

Results

Considering the increasing trend in big data generation and rapid advancements in bioinformatics tools, the re-analysis of pre-existing datasets for deciphering molecular pathogenic mechanisms is of utmost importance. In this study, a systematic approach was adopted to generate a map of key events during the progression of HN (Fig. 1). Two proteomics datasets previously generated from kidney sub-compartments of four HN rats and six sham controls are here re-analyzed for the identification of key pathogenic processes. The investigators who developed these datasets demonstrated the involvement of fatty acid β-oxidation, Rho signaling, and protein turnover pathways in the tubulointerstitial tissue [6]. Furthermore, they highlighted the role of the platelet-derived growth factor receptor-b signaling pathway in the kidney cortex of hypertensive rats [5].

Quality Assessment and Detection of DEPs

Using MaxQuant, we identified 2,074, 1,925, and 2,015 proteins (FDR ≤0.01) in the tubule, inner, and outer cortex sub-compartments, respectively (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000534354). Since we have previously highlighted the importance of assessing the quality of datasets as an initial step in the analysis of omics data [21], the suitability of the tubule and cortex datasets was assessed with principal component analysis which demonstrated the scattering of most samples according to the experimental groups. However, one sample in the tubule dataset and two samples in the inner and outer cortex that were far from the main clusters were omitted to improve the overall quality (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, the segregation of samples was statistically assessed using the Mahalanobis distance test that confirmed the separation of 2K1C versus sham samples in the tubule (p value: 0.012), inner cortex (p value: 0.026), and outer cortex (p value: 0.019). Consistent with these assessments, hierarchical clustering provided further support for the quality of the datasets (Fig. 2b).

Assessment of the quality of the datasets and identification of differentially expressed proteins: Unsupervised quality control using PCA demonstrates clear sample segregation according to experimental groups, indicating the overall acceptable quality of the datasets. For improved data quality, a few samples that deviated from this pattern were excluded and represented by unfilled symbols (a). Hierarchical clustering heat maps provide a visual representation of the relationships and similarities among the samples, highlighting distinct expression patterns (b). Volcano plots depict the differential expression analysis results, showcasing proteins that are significantly up-regulated (depicted in green) or downregulated (depicted in red) with an FDR below 0.05 (c).

Perseus was applied to determine DEPs among the identified proteins considering permutation-based FDR ≤0.05. In the tubule, inner and outer cortex, 166, 378, and 320 proteins were differentially expressed, respectively (Fig. 2c; online suppl. Table 2). Among these variably expressed proteins, are those with well-known contributions in the pathogenesis of HN, including the components of the complement system [22], angiotensinogen [23], and proteasome [24] (Table 1). In agreement with previous studies [5, 6], the magnitude of protein expression alterations in the cortex was higher than that in the tubule, implying the significant involvement of the cortex in this disease.

Of note, the numbers and identities of the proteins recognized as differentially expressed were only partially similar between the current analysis and the reports of the investigators who originally generated the datasets (online suppl. Fig. 1). This could potentially be due to the employment of different search engines, dissimilar filtration criteria, and statistical thresholds. This discrepancy underscores the importance of developing standard pipelines for the analysis of high-throughput data [25].

Functional Enrichment Analysis of DEPs

A considerable fraction of the DEPs were attributed to the class of enzymes, providing an initial clue on the key role of metabolic pathways in the disease pathogenesis (Fig. 3). To evaluate the collective functional roles of the DEPs, biological process, molecular function, and cellular component gene ontology enrichment analyses were carried out that indicate the enrichment of a variety of metabolic pathways, especially in the cortex (Fig. 3). Terms related to the cytoskeleton and proteasome complex were among the other significantly enriched terms.

To further validate the above findings on the role of metabolic processes, pathway enrichment analysis was conducted on the DEPs, demonstrating that a considerable fraction of the enriched pathways were related to metabolic processes, especially for the inner cortex (Fig. 4). Additionally, the Rho signaling pathway was enriched in all sub-compartments. Notably, several previous studies have reported the role of these pathways in vasoconstriction and hypertension [26–28]. Moreover, other significantly enriched pathways such as VEGF signaling, complement system, and pathway of inositol have been previously recognized to be involved in kidney failure and hypertension [29–33].

Construction and Analysis of Multilayer Networks

To identify the interaction map of the DEPs, PPI networks were constructed for all sub-compartments using Cytoscape CluePedia plugin. Since the central genes in networks are the key drivers of biological pathways and processes [34], the topology of the constructed networks was analyzed and central proteins were identified in terms of degree and betweenness centrality (Table 2) [35]. Noteworthy, a substantial number of the central DEPs, such as Kyat1, Decr1, Fbp1, and Sdha are enzymes involved in metabolic pathways. This could further signify the critical role of metabolic processes in the progression of HTN.

Functional Enrichment Analysis of Metabolites

To annotate the functional role of the predicted metabolites, enrichment analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst that underscore the significance of hypoxia (as represented by the term “Warbug effect”) as well as energy homeostasis in this disorder. Furthermore, a considerable fraction of terms are related to amino acid and purine metabolism (Fig. 7). Hence, representatives of such metabolic processes were further investigated; several enzymes in tryptophan degradation such as Kmo, Kyat1, Kynu, and Afmid are among downregulated DEPs. Moreover, key enzymes for valine degradation including Bckdha, Echs1, and Hibadh are downregulated. Also, several enzymes in purine metabolism showed upregulation in the examined datasets potentially leading to the accumulation of urate as the end product of this pathway (Fig. 8). To provide a holistic view of the functional relationships between key dysregulated enzymes, the map of affected processes is depicted (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Hypertension is a global health problem and a major risk factor for noncommunicable disorders such as cardiovascular and kidney diseases. Despite huge investigations, the complex underlying mechanisms of these disorders have not been comprehensively understood. However, the recent advancements in high-throughput technologies and holistic systems biology approaches have provided the opportunity to shed light on these complexities. In this study, a systematic approach was followed to provide clinically valuable knowledge through the re-analysis of two proteomics datasets on HN.

The proteomics datasets exploited in this study were originally produced using a rodent model of HN. The advantage of their approach is the examination of expression profiles in kidney sub-compartments separately. It was previously shown that the kidney cortex and medulla possess distinct gene expression patterns and stress response mechanisms [39]. While, high-throughput techniques are vulnerable to a variety of confounders and technical errors [21], the datasets examined in this study were appropriately generated as shown by quality control measures. Also, considering the importance of holistic insight and integration of biological layers as a trending approach to generate inclusive maps for complex disorders, the multilayer networks are here constructed for the interactions of different elements in HN. Despite these advantages, the current study has some limitations; the analyses are based on datasets with small sample sizes. In addition, manual retrieval of metabolite-to-protein associations potentially leads to a biased and incomplete representation of molecular interactions. Integrating metabolomics data from the same animal model could improve the robustness of the findings. Furthermore, considering the involvement of various types of biomolecules in the complex pathogenic processes, the construction of an inclusive map of HN is reliant on the availability of multi-omics data.

Various methods such as pathway and gene ontology enrichment, protein classification, and network analysis were employed to explore the functional role of DEPs. All these approaches coherently revealed the pivotal role of energy homeostasis and metabolic processes in the pathogenesis of HN. In agreement, a recent study on the glomerular proteome profile of archived kidney sections from HN patients has revealed that metabolic pathways were generally enriched in those patients who progressed to end-stage renal disease [40]. Furthermore, our results indicate that actin cytoskeleton organization is involved in the response of all kidney sub-compartments to hypertension. Accordingly, a large and growing body of evidence indicates the pivotal role of the actin cytoskeleton in the progression of kidney diseases [41–43]. Moreover, previous studies have highlighted the Rho GTPase pathway as a key regulator of the actin cytoskeleton in podocytes and vascular injury [44–46]. In this regard, Fasudil, a Rho-kinase inhibitor, was shown to be protective in a rat model of HN [47]. Notably, our results revealed a considerable dysregulation of this pathway in all sub-compartments. In addition, Cdc42, the main element of the Rho GTPase pathway and a critical protein in the regulation of the actin cytoskeleton, is among the central nodes in the inner cortex network [48]. Furthermore, proteasome degradation is another significant term. Interestingly, the potential role of proteasomes in regulating the actin cytoskeleton was first experimentally demonstrated by Haarer et al. [49]. Remarkably, analysis of the constructed PPI networks highlighted proteasome components. Taken together, in line with previous investigations, our findings underscore the role of Rho GTPase pathways and proteasomes in the progression of HN which is potentially mediated via actin cytoskeleton dysregulation.

Consistent with previous studies, our results underline the role of energy hemostasis in the pathogenesis of HN. Different elements in the TCA cycle are among the DEPs in the cortex. However, the terms related to energy metabolism are mainly enriched in the tubule dataset which can be attributed to the high energy demand of ion transporters. The proteins of NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase supernumerary subunits (NDUF) are detected to be differentially expressed in the tubule dataset. Dysregulation of this protein family is shown to be linked with reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [50]. Notably, the above-discussed Rho GTPase pathway is also involved in ROS accumulation [51]. In agreement, glutathione-mediated detoxification and glutathione redox reaction are enriched in the cortex section. It should be noted that a high-metabolic-rate organ such as the kidney is vulnerable to ROS overload and the consequent inflammatory response.

Remarkably, the Warburg effect emerged as one of the most highly enriched terms across all sub-compartments. This effect, well-established in cancer, highlights the reliance of cancer cells on glycolysis in hypoxic environments [52]. However, it is noteworthy to mention that extensive evidence has demonstrated the significant involvement of hypoxia in chronic kidney disease, particularly in the context of diabetic kidney disease [53]. Despite the scarcity of direct evidence linking hypoxia to HN, this intriguing finding could serve as an initial clue regarding the potential role of the hypoxia pathway in HN pathogenesis.

The regulation of metabolic processes has been long known as one of the key functions of the kidney. However, the role of these processes in the pathogenesis of HN is just recently investigated. Our results indicate that a majority of DEPs, especially in the cortex, are functionally attributable to the metabolism of amino acids, in particular, tryptophan, methionine, and valine. Remarkably, a bundle of recent evidence indicates the attenuation of kynurenine enzyme activity and accumulation of tryptophan pathway metabolites in HN [54, 55]. Additionally, dysregulation of methionine metabolism is shown in this disorder [56]. Cianciolo et al. [57] also demonstrated that folate deficiency perturbs the metabolism of methionine and results in hyperhomocysteinemia in chronic kidney disease. Accordingly, the downregulation of MTHFD1, a key enzyme in folate metabolism, is observed in this study. We observed that different enzymes in valine degradation are downregulated in the inner cortex. Moreover, this process was detected in the enrichment analyses as well. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first report on the involvement of valine degradation in HN. This finding is further supported by a recent study conducted by Rinchen et al. [58] demonstrating a reduction in acetylvaline, a derivative of valine, in the kidneys of an animal model of HN. Further validation through experimental studies, including the analysis of valine-related metabolites in clinical samples, and exploring the potential of valine degradation as a biomarker or therapeutic target for HN are interesting arenas for future research.

Our analysis is in favor of purine metabolism dysregulation and uric acid accumulation in HN. On the other hand, previous studies have shown that uric acid activates the renin-angiotensin system via ROS accumulation which will finally result in systemic hypertension and potential kidney damage [59]. Hence, it seems that purine metabolism dysregulation and uric acid overload form a vicious cycle worsening kidney damage in HN, making this process a reasonable candidate for therapeutic targeting. Fortunately, there are safe drugs in the market for xanthine dehydrogenase inhibition whose repositioning for HN can be investigated in future trials. In agreement with this suggestion, allopurinol is being assessed for diabetic nephropathy [60, 61].

Taken together, among a variety of affected pathways and processes, the alteration of oxygen and energy balance, as well as amino acid and purine metabolism, is prominent in the pathogenesis of HN. Hence, this disorder can be viewed as an “acquired error of metabolism.” This insight will hopefully pave the way for the development of novel therapeutics.
