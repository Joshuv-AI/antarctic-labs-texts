Effects of different potassium sulfate to potassium chloride ratios on lettuce growth, nutritional quality, and rhizosphere microbial community
Authors: Kejian Fu, Yucui Sun, Darun Yang, Yingying Li, Heng Li, Wei Jiang, Xiongtao Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPotassium fertilizers differ in their accompanying anions, which may alter crop performance and rhizosphere processes beyond potassium nutrition alone. This study examined how contrasting sulfate–chloride proportions in potassium supply affect Italian lettuce growth, nutritional quality, soil chemical status, and microbial community organization under equivalent total nutrient input.ResultsPotassium source composition produced distinct responses across plant, soil, and microbial traits. A mixed sulfate–chloride supply provided the most balanced improvement in biomass formation, root development, and quality-related metabolism, whereas sulfate-only fertilization favored selected nitrogen-assimilation and protein-related indicators. Shifts in anion supply modified soil sulfur, chloride, salinity, and nitrate-related conditions, thereby creating differentiated edaphic niches. Bacterial and fungal assemblages responded unevenly to these changes: bacterial communities showed stronger treatment-specific turnover and a greater prevalence of transient specialists, while fungal communities contained more persistent and broadly distributed taxa. Multivariate and network analyses further indicated that nutrient availability, salinity-related factors, and selected keystone genera jointly shaped community structure and potential functional stability.ConclusionsThe sulfate–chloride balance in potassium fertilization acts as an integrated regulator of lettuce productivity, produce quality, soil nutrient status, and rhizosphere microbial assembly. Introduction

Potassium is an essential macronutrient that supports plant growth by regulating photosynthesis, assimilate transport, enzyme activation, stomatal movement, and cellular osmotic balance [1–3]. Adequate potassium nutrition also improves crop tolerance to environmental stress and contributes to yield stability and product quality [4]. Among commercial potassium fertilizers, potassium chloride (KCl) and potassium sulfate (K₂SO₄) are the most widely used because of their availability, solubility, and economic feasibility [5, 6]. Although both fertilizers supply readily available K⁺, they differ in their accompanying anions, Cl⁻ and SO₄²⁻, which may impose distinct physiological and ecological effects beyond potassium nutrition itself [7, 8]. Therefore, potassium source selection should not be viewed solely as a question of K supply, but also as a regulation of anion input into the plant–soil system.

Lettuce is a major leafy vegetable cultivated worldwide under both open-field and protected production systems. Its short growth cycle, high market demand, and sensitivity to nutrient supply make it an appropriate crop for evaluating fertilizer source effects. According to FAO statistics, global lettuce production reached approximately 27.15 million tons in 2022, with China contributing more than half of total output. Intensive lettuce cultivation requires fertilization strategies that sustain yield and quality while limiting salt accumulation and maintaining rhizosphere function. Because leafy vegetables directly accumulate nutrients and soluble metabolites in edible tissues, changes in the sulfate-to-chloride balance of potassium fertilizers may have immediate consequences for both crop quality and soil nutrient dynamics.

The biological effects of Cl⁻ and SO₄²⁻ are not equivalent. Chloride can be beneficial at low concentrations because it participates in osmotic adjustment, charge balance, and vacuolar functions, but excessive Cl⁻ may inhibit nitrate uptake, disturb photosynthetic processes, and reduce plant performance [9–11]. Sulfate, in contrast, is the primary sulfur source for the synthesis of cysteine, methionine, glutathione, and other sulfur-containing compounds. Sulfur metabolism is closely linked with nitrogen assimilation through sulfate transporters, nitrate transport systems, and redox regulation, thereby influencing carbon–nitrogen allocation and antioxidant capacity [12]. These contrasting functions indicate that the relative inputs of SO₄²⁻ and Cl⁻ may determine whether potassium fertilization promotes balanced metabolism or induces ionic and nutritional stress. Evidence from different crops further suggests that potassium source effects are context dependent. In potato, the assumed superiority of K₂SO₄ over KCl is not always reflected in tuber yield or starch accumulation, and some responses may depend on crop stage, cultivar, or environmental conditions [13]. Interactions between potassium and other nutrients, such as zinc in maize, also demonstrate that potassium fertilization affects broader nutrient networks rather than a single element alone [14]. In tobacco, K₂SO₄ and KCl have been reported to differentially modify soil electrical conductivity, pH, exchangeable cations, and microbial groups involved in sulfur and chloride-related processes [15]. These findings collectively show that the effects of potassium fertilizers are shaped by accompanying anions, soil chemical reactions, and crop-specific physiological responses.

At the mechanistic level, Cl⁻ and SO₄²⁻ may interact during uptake, translocation, and metabolism. High Cl⁻ availability can interfere with sulfate uptake and transport, while cytosolic Cl⁻ accumulation may alter redox balance and suppress sulfur reduction, ultimately affecting glutathione biosynthesis and antioxidant defense [16]. Adequate sulfate supply can enhance nitrogen assimilation by supporting the glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase pathway and increasing nitrate reductase and nitrite reductase activities [17]. In oilseed rape, Cl⁻ and SO₄²⁻ stresses differ in their regulation of ion homeostasis, sulfate transporter expression, sulfur reduction, and carbon metabolism [18]. Similar source-dependent changes in soluble sugars and organic acids have been observed in tomato supplied with KCl or K₂SO₄ [19]. These studies support the view that potassium source effects arise from coupled regulation of ion balance, sulfur–nitrogen metabolism, and carbon allocation. Despite these advances, a focused knowledge gap remains. Most studies compare KCl and K₂SO₄ as separate fertilizer sources or evaluate potassium application rates, but few systematically vary their combined proportions under an equivalent total potassium supply. As a result, it remains difficult to distinguish the contribution of K⁺ itself from the regulatory effects of the accompanying SO₄²⁻/Cl⁻ ratio. Moreover, limited information is available on how graded sulfate-to-chloride inputs simultaneously affect lettuce growth, quality traits, soil nutrient status, and bacterial and fungal community assembly. This gap is particularly relevant for leafy vegetables, where rapid biomass accumulation, nitrate metabolism, and rhizosphere nutrient turnover are tightly connected.

To address this issue, the present study established seven K₂SO₄/KCl ratios under a fixed total potassium input and evaluated their effects on Italian lettuce growth, quality formation, soil physicochemical properties, and rhizosphere microbial communities. The objectives were to determine how the sulfate-to-chloride ratio regulates lettuce biomass accumulation and root development, to assess its influence on nitrogen-related enzymes and quality attributes, and to clarify how associated changes in soil properties are linked with bacterial and fungal community structure. We hypothesize that partially replacing KCl with K₂SO₄ can optimize lettuce growth and quality by balancing sulfur/nitrogen metabolism and fostering favorable rhizosphere conditions. This study provides a basis for refining potassium fertilizer formulations for leafy vegetable production and for integrating crop performance with soil and microbial sustainability.

Materials and methods

Experimental site, plant material, and greenhouse conditions

The pot experiment was conducted in March 2025 in a farmer-managed greenhouse at Honghai Phosphate Fertilizer Co., Ltd., Jinning District, Kunming City, Yunnan Province, China. The experiment lasted 40 d after transplanting. The greenhouse was operated according to local outdoor greenhouse cultivation practices; no active temperature or relative humidity control was applied, and plants were grown under natural sunlight. During the experimental period, both cloudy and sunny weather occurred, and greenhouse temperature fluctuated with ambient weather conditions. The monthly average greenhouse temperature was approximately 26 °C, and the monthly average relative humidity was approximately 81%. Italian lettuce was used as the test crop. Seeds were purchased from Xinglin Seed Industry Company, Jinning, Yunnan Province, China. The seed purity was ≥ 95%, and the germination rate was ≥ 85%, meeting the national quality standards for vegetable seeds. Seedlings were raised for 26 d before transplanting, and uniform seedlings were selected for the pot experiment.

Soil, fertilizer materials, and treatment design

Each pot was filled with 10 kg of soil, and the baseline soil nutrient status is presented in Supplementary Table S1. All treatments received the same fertilization rate, with a liquid fertilizer supplying N: P₂O₅:K₂O at 40:40:120. Urea containing 46% N was used as the nitrogen source, ammonium polyphosphate (18:58:0) was used as the phosphorus source, and potassium chloride containing 60% K₂O and potassium sulfate containing 52% K₂O were used as potassium sources at different ratios to establish the experimental treatments. Humic acid was applied uniformly across all treatments as the organic amendment. All fertilizers used in the experiment were provided by Yunnan Yuntianhua Co., Ltd. Under an equal total K₂O application rate, seven potassium-source treatments were established by adjusting the relative contribution of potassium sulfate and potassium chloride to the total potassium supply. The K₂O-equivalent ratios of potassium sulfate to potassium chloride were 0:120, 20:100, 40:80, 60:60, 80:40, 100:20, and 120:0, respectively. Each treatment included three independent biological replicate pots, and each pot served as one experimental unit for all subsequent measurements and statistical analyses. When multiple plants, soil subsamples, or DNA extracts were obtained from the same pot, they were treated as technical or within-pot subsamples and were not considered independent biological replicates. For microbiological analyses, subsamples collected from the same pot were composited or processed to represent one pot-level biological replicate. Therefore, the biological replication level throughout the study was the independent pot, with n = 3 per treatment. All conditions in the pot experiment were strictly standardized, including soil amount, seedling age, fertilizer application rate, and greenhouse management practices.

No basal fertilizer was applied before lettuce transplanting, and all fertilizers were applied by solution drenching. According to the nitrogen demand pattern of lettuce at different growth stages, the total fertilizer amount per pot was split into three topdressing applications: the first on day 7 after transplanting, the second on day 18, and the third on day 28. The solution volumes applied at the three fertilization events were 5.00, 8.75, and 11.25 mL pot⁻¹, respectively, resulting in a total application volume of 25.00 mL pot⁻¹. Irrigation was conducted according to local conventional management practices for large-pot cultivation, and no additional fertilizer was supplied other than the prescribed fertilizer solution. The detailed fertilization schedule and time points for agronomic trait measurements are shown in Fig. 1.

Analysis of lettuce quality indices and key enzymes in nitrogen metabolism

Following the conclusion of the test period, the third to fifth functional leaves were harvested and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, subsequently stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer at -80 ℃ for later analysis. Fresh leaves were utilized for the immediate assessment of quality indicators. The vitamin C content was quantified through titration with 2, 6-dichlorophenol [20]. The soluble protein content was measured using the Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 staining method [21], while the soluble sugar content was determined via the anthrone colorimetric method [22]. Nitrate Reductase (NR) and Glutamine Synthetase (GS), which are key enzymes in nitrogen metabolism, facilitate the initial conversion of inorganic nitrogen and the critical steps of organic nitrogen synthesis, thereby forming the core pathway of nitrogen metabolism. Both enzymes were assessed using the trace method, following the protocols outlined in the Nitrate Reductase activity detection kit and the Glutamine Synthetase activity detection kit, which were procured from Beijing Solaibao Technology Co., LTD. The main root length of the remaining root system was measured after the soil was removed. Concurrently, a root system scanner was employed to capture digital images and obtain specific root system index parameters.

Analysis of soil physicochemical properties

Upon completion of the lettuce sample collection, soil sampling was conducted concurrently. During the collection process, soil samples were individually labeled and catalogued according to the pre-established treatment schemes representing different potassium source ratios. Routine physicochemical analyses were performed on all soil samples using the following methodologies: available sulfur was determined via calcium chloride extraction followed by ion chromatography [23]; chloride content was quantified by silver nitrate titration (Mohr’s method) [24]; nitrate-nitrogen was measured using potassium chloride extraction coupled with ultraviolet spectrophotometry at 210 nm [25]; organic matter content was assessed by the potassium dichromate oxidation–external heating method (Walkley–Black method) [26]; exchangeable calcium and exchangeable magnesium were determined by ammonium acetate (NH₄OAc, pH 7.0) extraction followed by atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) [27]; available potassium was measured by ammonium acetate (NH₄OAc, 1 mol L⁻¹, pH 7.0) extraction followed by flame photometry (FP) [27]; and available phosphorus was determined using 0.5 mol L⁻¹ NaHCO₃ extraction (pH 8.5) with the molybdenum-antimony anti-colorimetric method [27].

High-throughput sequencing of the soil microbial community

For each pot-level soil sample, 100 g of soil was taken, ground with liquid nitrogen, and divided into three equal subsamples. Genomic DNA was extracted from each subsample individually, and the extracted DNA from the three subsamples was then pooled into a single DNA sample representing the corresponding original pot-level biological replicate to minimize extraction-related variability. These three subsamples and their corresponding DNA extracts were treated as technical extraction subsamples rather than independent biological replicates. Thus, each pooled DNA sample corresponded to one independent pot and was used as one sequencing sample for downstream microbiome analysis. DNA quality was assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. For bacterial community analysis, the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the 16 S rRNA gene were amplified using primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). For fungal community analysis, the ITS1 region was amplified using primers ITS1F (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’) and ITS2R (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’). PCR amplification was performed using TransGen AP221-02 TransStart Fastpfu DNA Polymerase on an ABI GeneAmp® 9700 PCR instrument. Each pooled DNA sample was amplified in triplicate, and the three PCR products were pooled to reduce PCR-related technical variability. These PCR triplicates were considered technical replicates and were not treated as independent biological replicates.Amplicons were visualized on 2% agarose gels, purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (AXYGEN), and eluted with Tris-HCl.

Library construction was performed using the TruSeq™ DNA Sample Prep Kit, involving adapter ligation, gel purification, and NaOH denaturation to generate single-stranded DNA fragments. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NextSeq platform (paired-end, 2 × 300 bp) by Shanghai Majorbio Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. No mock communities or negative controls were included in this sequencing run. Therefore, control-based identification and removal of potential background contaminants could not be performed, and this limitation was considered when interpreting low-abundance taxa, treatment-specific indicator taxa, and apparent microbial community shifts. Raw paired-end reads were demultiplexed and quality-filtered using the QIIME2 pipeline (version 2023.5). Specifically, reads were truncated at positions with a Phred quality score < Q20, and those shorter than 50 bp after trimming were discarded. Denoising and ASV (Amplicon Sequence Variant) inference were performed using the DADA2 plugin in QIIME2 with the following parameters: --p-trunc-len-f 280 --p-trunc-len-r 220 --p-max-ee 2.0 --p-trunc-q 20. For bacterial data, the forward and reverse reads were truncated to 280 bp and 220 bp, respectively; for fungal ITS data, the truncation lengths were set to 250 bp and 210 bp based on quality profiles. Taxonomy was assigned to bacterial ASVs using the SILVA 138.1 database and to fungal ASVs using the UNITE 9.0 database. Sequencing depth and species coverage were evaluated using rarefaction curves (observed ASVs and Shannon index), generated by randomly subsampling sequences without replacement. Curves approached saturation for all samples, indicating sufficient sequencing depth for downstream diversity analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SE, based on three independent pot-level biological replicates per treatment (n = 3), with each pot considered one experimental unit. No formal power analysis was performed for the present dataset; therefore, the replicate number should be considered when interpreting treatment-level effects, particularly microbiome community patterns and treatment-specific taxa. For measurements involving multiple plants, soil subsamples, or DNA extracts from the same pot, values were averaged, composited, or otherwise summarized at the pot level before statistical analysis. These within-pot subsamples and pooled DNA extracts were treated as technical or within-pot subsamples and were not considered independent biological replicates. Sample allocation was randomized, and measurements were performed blindly where feasible. Normality and variance homogeneity were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene/Bartlett tests, respectively; variables that deviated from normality were transformed before parametric analysis, specifically log10 transformation for soil available phosphorus, bacterial Sphingomonas abundance, and fungal Olpidiaster relative abundance, and square-root transformation for plant biomass and fungal Fusarium relative abundance. If normality remained unachieved after transformation, non-parametric alternatives (Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s post-hoc and Benjamini–Hochberg correction) were used. Normally distributed homoscedastic data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD, while non-normal or heteroscedastic data were analyzed by Kruskal–Wallis test. Linear regression explored relationships between plant traits and response variables; correlation coefficients (Pearson or Spearman) were computed with FDR correction for multiple testing. For microbiome analyses, each sequencing sample represented one independent pot-level biological replicate. Sequencing depth was standardized by rarefaction (minimum depth: 35,000 reads for bacteria, 50,000 for fungi) or CLR transformation. α-diversity indices (Sobs, Shannon, Simpson) were compared between groups using the same parametric/non-parametric framework. β-diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis distances, visualized by NMDS (stress values reported), and tested with PERMANOVA (adonis, 999 permutations). Constrained ordination (db-RDA/CAP) related community structure to environmental variables, with collinearity reduced by stepwise selection (VIF < 5). Differential taxa were identified by LEfSe (LDA ≥ 2.0). Co-occurrence networks were constructed from Spearman correlations (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05), and key topological parameters (nodes, edges, average degree, modularity, centrality) were reported. Mantel tests (999 permutations) assessed correlations between community distance matrices and environmental variables. Given the limited number of independent biological replicates, microbiome-related statistical results were interpreted mainly as treatment-associated patterns rather than definitive evidence of stable treatment-specific microbial recruitment. All tests were two-sided, with significance indicated in figures and text. Analyses were performed using Excel 2019, GraphPad Prism 8.0, MATLAB 2019, and R (phyloseq, vegan, picante); networks were visualized in Cytoscape.

Results

Differential effects of potassium source regimes on plant growth and biomass production

Under pot conditions, the ratio of potassium sulfate to potassium chloride affected lettuce growth and biomass accumulation, with treatment differences becoming more evident after fertilization (Fig. 2). Before fertilization, plant height, canopy width, and SPAD values were broadly comparable among treatments. During the post-fertilization period, especially at the later sampling stages, treatments with a higher proportion of potassium sulfate generally maintained greater shoot growth and leaf chlorophyll status than chloride-dominant treatments. At the final sampling stage, R-100:20 and R-120:0 showed relatively higher plant height and canopy width, while SPAD values were also maintained at higher levels under sulfate-enriched regimes. Biomass responses differed between shoots and roots. Shoot fresh weight was highest under R-100:20, and this treatment was significantly greater than R-0:120, R-40:80, R-60:60, R-80:40, and R-120:0, while R-20:100 showed an intermediate response. In contrast, fresh root weight was highest under R-40:80, which did not differ significantly from R-0:120 or R-80:40 but was significantly greater than R-60:60 and R-120:0. Overall, R-100:20 was the most favorable regime for aboveground biomass accumulation, whereas R-40:80 was more beneficial for root fresh weight. These results indicate that an appropriate balance between sulfate- and chloride-derived potassium improves lettuce growth, while exclusive or excessive reliance on either potassium source may limit biomass accumulation.

Potassium sources influence lettuce quality and the activities of key nitrogen metabolic enzymes

Different ratios of potassium sulfate to potassium chloride altered lettuce nutritional quality and the activities of key enzymes involved in nitrogen metabolism (Fig. 3). Vitamin C content was highest under R-100:20 and was comparable to R-120:0, whereas chloride-dominant treatments such as R-0:120 and R-20:100 maintained lower levels. Soluble protein responded differently, with R-120:0 showing the highest accumulation, while R-80:40 remained at a relatively low level. For soluble sugars, R-0:120 had the highest content, whereas R-100:20 had the lowest value, indicating that sugar accumulation did not follow the same response pattern as vitamin C or soluble protein. The activities of nitrate reductase and glutamine synthetase also showed distinct responses to potassium source composition. Nitrate reductase activity was enhanced under sulfate-enriched regimes, with R-120:0 and R-100:20 showing higher activities than chloride-dominant treatments. In contrast, glutamine synthetase activity reached its highest level under R-80:40 and declined when the potassium source shifted to full potassium sulfate application. Overall, these results indicate that potassium source ratio regulates lettuce quality and nitrogen metabolism in a trait-specific manner. R-100:20 was favorable for vitamin C accumulation and nitrate reductase activity, R-120:0 promoted soluble protein and nitrate reductase activity, whereas R-80:40 was more beneficial for glutamine synthetase activity.

Differential effects of potassium formulations on root phenotype and architecture

The potassium sulfate-to-potassium chloride ratio markedly affected lettuce root morphology and architecture (Fig. 4). Root images showed that single-source potassium treatments produced less developed root systems, particularly R-120:0, whereas mixed potassium-source treatments promoted greater root expansion. Among the measured root traits, R-100:20 showed the highest total root length and root surface area, indicating a stronger capacity for root extension and soil exploration. For main root length, R-40:80 and R-100:20 performed best, with no significant difference between them. Root volume was also relatively higher under mixed-source regimes, especially R-40:80, R-80:40, and R-100:20, while R-0:120 and R-120:0 showed comparatively limited root development. Overall, R-100:20 was the most favorable treatment for total root length and surface area, whereas R-40:80 also contributed positively to main root elongation and root volume. These results suggest that a balanced combination of potassium sulfate and potassium chloride is more beneficial for lettuce root architecture than either potassium source applied alone.

Potassium source ratio as a key driver in regulating soil multi-nutrient availability

The potassium sulfate-to-potassium chloride ratio substantially reshaped soil nutrient availability and ionic composition (Fig. 5). Soil available sulfur increased under sulfate-containing treatments and reached the highest level in R-100:20, followed by R-120:0, whereas R-0:120 showed the lowest level. In contrast, soil Cl⁻ concentration was highest under R-0:120 and decreased as the proportion of potassium sulfate increased, with R-120:0 showing the lowest Cl⁻ level. Soil nitrate-nitrogen and electrical conductivity showed a non-linear response to potassium source composition: both were relatively elevated in R-20:100, decreased to their lowest levels under R-80:40, and reached their highest values under R-120:0. Soil organic matter showed no significant difference among treatments. Available potassium was highest in R-120:0 and relatively high in R-100:20, whereas R-80:40 had the lowest value. Other nutrients showed treatment-specific responses, with available phosphorus peaking under R-60:60, exchangeable calcium being higher under chloride-dominant treatments, and exchangeable magnesium reaching its highest level under R-80:40. These results indicate that potassium source composition not only changed sulfur, chloride, and potassium availability directly, but also modified the availability of other nutrients in a ratio-dependent manner.

Alterations in soil physicochemical properties under different potassium source ratios

Effects of different potassium sources on soil microbial community structure

Potassium source composition was associated with clear shifts in soil bacterial and fungal community profiles (Fig. 6). The Venn analysis showed that 324 bacterial genera and 104 fungal genera were shared across all seven treatments, while the number of unique genera differed among treatments but did not follow a consistent gradient with the potassium sulfate-to-potassium chloride ratio (Fig. S1). At the bacterial phylum level, the community was mainly composed of Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, and Bacteroidota. Pseudomonadota was relatively more abundant under R-120:0, whereas Actinomycetota was more pronounced under R-40:80. At the genus level, unclassified_f_Micrococcaceae, Sphingomonas, and Flavobacterium were among the dominant bacterial groups across treatments, indicating that potassium source ratio mainly changed the relative distribution of dominant taxa rather than causing a complete replacement of the bacterial community. The fungal community showed a more distinct response at both phylum and genus levels. Olpidiomycota and Ascomycota were the dominant fungal phyla, with Olpidiomycota reaching its highest relative abundance under R-120:0, while Ascomycota was relatively enriched in treatments containing higher proportions of potassium chloride or balanced potassium sources. At the genus level, Olpidiaster was the dominant fungal genus and was most abundant under R-120:0. In contrast, several genera, including Penicillium and Wallemia, were more closely associated with R-0:120 or R-60:60 according to the ternary plot analysis (Fig. S2). The ternary plot also showed that bacterial genera were distributed more centrally among R-0:120, R-60:60, and R-120:0, whereas fungal genera exhibited stronger treatment-associated separation. Overall, these results suggest that potassium source ratio altered the composition of soil microbial communities, with fungal taxa showing a more differentiated distribution pattern than bacterial taxa.

Potassium-driven shifts in microbial taxonomy

NMDS ordination and LEfSe analysis further showed that potassium source ratio was associated with shifts in bacterial and fungal taxonomic profiles (Fig. 7). At the genus level, both bacterial and fungal communities showed treatment-related separation in the ordination space, with fungi displaying a more dispersed distribution than bacteria. The stress value was 0.229, indicating that the ordination provided an acceptable representation of community dissimilarity. The genus-level heatmap showed that several dominant bacterial genera, including Sphingomonas, maintained relatively high abundance across treatments, whereas genera such as Nitrosospira and Bacillus were more closely associated with specific potassium source ratios (Fig. S3). For fungi, Olpidiaster was the dominant genus across treatments, while the relative abundance of several less dominant genera varied among potassium source regimes.

LEfSe analysis identified treatment-associated indicator bacterial and fungal taxa, indicating that the potassium sulfate-to-potassium chloride ratio was associated with differences in the characteristic taxonomic composition of the soil microbial community. For bacteria, R-0:120 was characterized by taxa such as Cyclobacteriaceae, Pseudohongiella, Lysobacter, and Rheinheimera; R-20:100 was mainly associated with Bacillus; R-40:80 was enriched in Anaerolineaceae-related taxa; R-60:60 was characterized by Thermoactinomyces and Pontibacter; R-80:40 was associated with Pseudoxanthomonas and Holosporaceae-related groups; R-100:20 was characterized by Rubritaleaceae-, Lutolibacter-, and Verrucomicrobiales-related taxa; and R-120:0 was associated with Kapabacteriales-related groups and Candidatus Kaiserbacteria. For fungi, R-0:120 was characterized by Exophiala_cancerae and Pleosporaceae, R-20:100 by Alternaria and Papiliotrema_laurentii, R-40:80 by Rozellomycota-related taxa, R-60:60 by Sarocladium_glaucum and Monoblepharidomycetes_sp, R-80:40 by Monoblepharomycota-related taxa, and R-120:0 by Schizothecium_inaequale, Trichosporonales, Powellomycetaceae, and Didymellaceae. Overall, these results suggest that different potassium source ratios did not simply shift the abundance of a single dominant group, but were associated with distinct bacterial and fungal marker taxa across treatments. Because LEfSe is an association-based method, these taxa should be interpreted as treatment-associated indicators rather than as taxa directly recruited or regulated by potassium source composition.

Divergent ecological strategies of bacteria and fungi under different potassium source regimes

The trend regression, niche breadth, and persistence analyses revealed distinct taxonomic distribution patterns between bacterial and fungal communities under different potassium source regimes (Fig. 8). For bacteria, the number of shared genera was significantly and positively correlated with community similarity, whereas no significant relationship was detected for fungi, indicating that bacterial community similarity was more closely associated with shared taxonomic composition than fungal community similarity. Niche breadth analysis further showed that bacterial communities contained a relatively higher proportion of specialist taxa, while fungal communities were more dominated by generalists. This difference suggests that bacterial taxa were more heterogeneous in niche occupancy, whereas fungal taxa tended to occupy broader ecological niches. The persistence analysis also showed contrasting community strategies between bacteria and fungi. Across potassium source treatments, bacterial communities were dominated by transient taxa in terms of taxon number, while persistent taxa contributed a relatively large proportion of community abundance. In contrast, fungal communities consistently showed a stronger contribution of persistent taxa, particularly in relative abundance, and generalist taxa also accounted for a larger share of the fungal community. Overall, these results suggest that bacterial communities were characterized by higher turnover and a larger transient component, whereas fungal communities showed greater persistence and broader niche occupancy. No single potassium source ratio was identified as consistently superior based on these association-derived taxonomic indicators.

Associations between soil environmental variables and microbial diversity

Correlation and ordination analyses showed that soil properties, lettuce quality traits, nitrogen metabolism enzymes, and microbial taxa were associated in a trait-specific manner (Fig. 9). At the soil–plant level, available sulfur was significantly negatively correlated with Cl⁻, and soluble sugar showed opposite correlations with these two ions. NR and GS were significantly positively correlated with vitamin C, while NR was also positively correlated with soluble protein and negatively correlated with Cl⁻ and exchangeable Ca. At the microbial level, available phosphorus was correlated with several bacterial and fungal genera, showing positive correlations with Lysobacter, Streptomyces, Neobacillus, Cercospora, Penicillium, and Neopyrenochaeta, but negative correlations with Bacillus and Nitrospira. Available K showed significant negative correlations mainly with fungal genera, including Rhizophlyctis, Mortierella, and Cladosporium, whereas no significant correlation was detected with the bacterial genera examined. For quality-related indicators, Pseudomonas and Gemmatimonas were positively correlated with soluble protein, while Cladosporium and Plectosphaerella were negatively correlated; Dokmaia and Ascobolus showed opposite correlations with vitamin C.

The db-RDA results further indicated that microbial community composition was associated with different environmental gradients. The first two axes explained 16.45% of the variation in bacterial communities and 18.22% in fungal communities. Bacterial community variation was mainly associated with SOM, available P, available K, and EC, whereas fungal community variation was more closely related to available P, exchangeable Ca, EC, and NO₃⁻-N. These results suggest that bacterial and fungal community compositions were differently associated with soil nutrient conditions under different potassium source regimes. Linear regression analysis showed no significant relationship between bacterial or fungal Sobs index and either available sulfur or Cl⁻, suggesting that α-diversity was not significantly explained by either single anion alone in this dataset (Fig. S4). Because db-RDA and correlation analyses provide association-based evidence, these patterns should not be interpreted as direct causal effects of individual soil variables on microbial community composition.

Integrated mantel test and co-occurrence network analysis

Mantel-test and co-occurrence network analyses showed that soil environmental variables were associated with bacterial and fungal community structures in different ways under the potassium source regimes (Fig. 10). For bacteria, available sulfur and Cl⁻ exhibited contrasting correlation patterns with several soil properties, and the Mantel-test links indicated that bacterial community composition was associated with multiple soil variables across the potassium sulfate-to-potassium chloride gradient. Similar associations were observed for the fungal community, although the strength and direction of the relationships differed from those of bacteria. These results suggest that bacterial and fungal communities were differentially related to changes in soil ionic and nutrient conditions, rather than responding uniformly to a single factor. The co-occurrence networks further indicated that microbial taxa showed distinct Spearman correlation-based association patterns across treatments. In the bacterial network, genera such as Sphingomonas, Flavobacterium, Nitrospira, and Massilia appeared across multiple treatment groups and showed relatively high connectivity. In the fungal network, Fusarium, Penicillium, Cladosporium, Mortierella, and Olpidiaster were among the taxa with broader network associations. The dominant links in both networks were positive, while negative associations were less frequent and mainly involved specific fungal taxa. At the genus level, Flavobacterium and Sphingomonas showed strong positive Spearman correlation links with other bacterial genera, whereas Olpidiaster was connected with several fungal taxa through negative correlations. Overall, the network results suggest that different potassium source ratios were associated with shifts in microbial co-occurrence structure, but these associations should be interpreted as correlation-based patterns rather than direct microbial functional changes. Importantly, the edges in these networks represent statistical correlations only and do not demonstrate direct biological interactions, competition, cooperation, trophic relationships, or functional roles among microbial taxa. Therefore, highly connected taxa in the present networks should be interpreted as taxa with broad correlation patterns rather than as validated keystone taxa or functionally important organisms.

Discussion

The ratio of SO₄²⁻ to Cl⁻ regulates the trend changes of vitamin C and soluble sugar in lettuce by coupling sulfur-carbon metabolism

This study revealed that the SO₄²⁻/Cl⁻ ratio in potassium fertilizer distinctly regulated lettuce leaf AsA and soluble sugar accumulation, with AsA increasing under higher SO₄²⁻ supply, whereas soluble sugars showed a U-shaped response along the KCl-to-K₂SO₄ substitution gradient [28, 29]. This divergent response indicates that the effect of potassium source was not limited to K nutrition itself, but was closely associated with the coupled regulation of sulfur metabolism, carbon allocation, and ionic balance. The increase in AsA content under high-proportion K₂SO₄ treatment may be mainly attributed to SO₄²⁻-enhanced regeneration efficiency of the AsA-GSH cycle. Rather than directly accelerating de novo AsA biosynthesis, adequate sulfur nutrition can promote glutathione (GSH) biosynthesis [30, 31]. As the specific electron donor for dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR), GSH is a key factor driving the reduction of oxidized ascorbate, namely dehydroascorbate (DHA), back to AsA. Therefore, higher SO₄²⁻ supply may strengthen the DHAR-mediated AsA regeneration pathway by maintaining the GSH pool, thereby establishing an AsA homeostatic mode characterized by “reduced oxidative depletion and enhanced recycling.” Recent omics studies further indicate that SO₄²⁻ supply mainly upregulates genes associated with GSH metabolism and AsA regeneration, such as GR and DHAR, while exerting relatively limited effects on key genes involved in de novo AsA biosynthesis, such as VTC2 and GLDH [32–34]. In addition, sufficient SO₄²⁻ supply may enhance the antioxidant capacity of plants, reduce non-specific oxidative consumption of AsA, and thereby synergistically promote AsA accumulation. By contrast, the U-shaped response of soluble sugars may reflect the dose-dependent role of Cl⁻ in carbon–nitrogen balance and osmotic regulation [35]. At high Cl⁻ levels, soluble sugar accumulation is likely associated with mild osmotic stress and the need for osmotic adjustment. Meanwhile, excessive Cl⁻ may competitively inhibit NO₃⁻ uptake, disturbing carbon–nitrogen metabolism and redirecting photosynthetically derived carbon skeletons toward soluble sugar storage rather than nitrogen assimilation and growth [36, 37]. When Cl⁻ is nearly absent, plants may also activate compensatory responses, including changes in vacuolar ion homeostasis, enhanced sucrose transport, and starch degradation to replenish the cytosolic sugar pool. Such responses may be related not only to osmotic adjustment but also to carbon metabolic reprogramming triggered by energy-stress signaling through the SnRK1 pathway [38]. Therefore, high soluble sugar accumulation under both excessive and deficient Cl⁻ conditions should be interpreted as an adaptive response to suboptimal ionic environments rather than as direct evidence of improved photosynthetic performance.

The contrasting patterns of AsA and soluble sugars further suggest an antagonistic coupling between SO₄²⁻-mediated sulfur metabolism and Cl⁻-regulated carbon allocation. At intermediate SO₄²⁻/Cl⁻ ratios, the decline in soluble sugar content may result from a partial release of Cl⁻-induced osmotic pressure, together with changes in nitrate uptake, nitrate reductase activity, and photosynthetic carbon partitioning [39, 40]. In this context, potassium source composition should be understood as a regulatory factor affecting nutrient-use efficiency and metabolic distribution, rather than merely as a supplier of K⁺. Supporting this view, a recent study on shallot showed that combining organic residual potassium from banana peel compost tea with a moderate amount of KCl improved potassium uptake and yield more effectively than either organic or mineral potassium alone, whereas excessive reliance on KCl did not produce the best response [41]. Although the crop and potassium-source system differed from the present study, this finding supports the broader concept that optimized potassium-source combinations can improve nutrient-use efficiency by balancing immediate K availability, residual nutrient supply, and ionic stress. In summary, the increase in vitamin C content under high-proportion K₂SO₄ treatment was mainly associated with SO₄²⁻-mediated enhancement of the AsA-GSH recycling system, whereas the U-shaped response of soluble sugars reflected the dual role of Cl⁻ in regulating carbon–nitrogen partitioning and osmotic balance. Future studies should integrate metabolic flux analysis, key enzyme activity assays, and transporter-level evidence to further quantify how sulfur and carbon metabolic fluxes are redistributed across the SO₄²⁻/Cl⁻ ratio gradient.

Discussion on the causes of the impact of different potassium source ratios on nitrate reductase

The observed elevation of nitrate reductase (NR) activity with increasing proportions of potassium sulfate cannot be attributed to a direct provision of reducing equivalents (NADH/NADPH) or to post-translational activation, but rather to transcriptional regulation mediated by sulfur availability. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the promoter region of the NIA2 gene encoding NR contains functional sulfur-responsive elements (SURE) [42]. When sulfate is abundant, the transcription factor SLIM1 binds directly to this SURE sequence, thereby upregulating NIA2 transcription and enhancing NR activity [43]. Furthermore, the global transcription factor group E proteins GTE7 and GTE2 have been identified as key regulators of sulfur homeostasis, with GTE7 directly binding SURE elements in sulfur transporter promoters [44]. Importantly, while the thioredoxin system (Trx-h3) can post-translationally activate NR via reduction of a critical cysteine residue under light conditions, this mechanism is not sulfate responsive; the sustained elevation of NR activity under high sulfate treatment in this study is therefore attributed predominantly to SLIM1-mediated transcriptional upregulation of NIA2, rather than to direct allosteric activation or enhanced electron supply. This transcriptional cascade provides a mechanistic explanation for the higher NR activities observed in the high-sulfate treatments (R-100:20 and R-120:0) in this study.

Conversely, the reduction in NR activity associated with elevated chloride levels is unlikely to result from direct enzyme inhibition. Instead, evidence from model systems indicates an indirect effect stemming from disrupted nitrate homeostasis. Chloride ions competitively inhibit nitrate uptake via dual-affinity transporters such as NRT1.1 (CHL1), leading to a depletion of the intracellular nitrate pool [42, 45]. Given that NR is a substrate-inducible enzyme, a reduction in cytosolic nitrate concentration directly limits its in vivo activity. Hydroponic studies in Calendula officinalis L and Arabidopsis have demonstrated that a decline in tissue nitrate concentration precedes the reduction in NR activity under chloride stress, an effect that can be partially reversed by exogenous nitrate supplementation [46, 47].

Additionally, abiotic stresses such as heavy metal exposure, osmotic stress, and oxidative stress have been shown to reduce NR activity indirectly by altering nitrate availability, substrate utilization, and intracellular signaling. For example, Pb and Cr exposure in vegetables and crops caused reductions in cortical cell area, vascular bundle size, and leaf thickness, as well as changes in proline, soluble sugars, and protein accumulation, which collectively impact nitrate metabolism and NR activity [48]. These observations suggest that NR activity is not only sensitive to chloride-mediated substrate limitation, but also to broader stress-induced metabolic constraints. Therefore, the differential NR activities observed across the potassium source gradient are best explained by a combination of sulfate-driven transcriptional enhancement, chloride-mediated restriction of substrate availability, and stress-induced indirect modulation of enzyme activity.

Limitations and future research directions

From a practical standpoint, the present findings provide a formulation-oriented basis for refining potassium-source combinations in lettuce production systems in which both crop quality and soil nutrient balance are major concerns. Because all treatments were supplied with the same total N₂O₅₂O ratio, the observed differences among treatments can be mainly attributed to the replacement of KCl by K₂SO₄ rather than to differences in total nutrient input. The positive association between a higher sulfate proportion and nitrate reductase (NR) activity suggests that adjusting the K₂SO₄ ratio may be a feasible strategy to enhance nitrogen assimilation while avoiding exclusive dependence on chloride-based potassium fertilizer. This is particularly relevant for protected vegetable cultivation, short-cycle leafy vegetable production, and systems where repeated fertilization may increase the risk of Cl⁻ accumulation, nitrate residue, or nutrient imbalance [49].

These results also have practical value for compound fertilizer design. Instead of simply increasing total fertilizer input, the formulation can be improved by modifying the relative contribution of sulfate- and chloride-based potassium sources under a constant NPK supply. For fertilizer producers and growers, this means that partial substitution of KCl with K₂SO₄ could be considered as a formulation adjustment to coordinate potassium supply, sulfur nutrition, nitrogen metabolism, and soil chemical properties. In practice, such formulations should be applied together with routine monitoring of soil EC, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, nitrate-N, and exchangeable Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺, especially in greenhouse soils or continuously cropped vegetable fields [50, 51]. The operational advantage of this approach is that it does not necessarily require additional field operations; rather, it modifies the nutrient composition of the fertilizer itself. However, because K₂SO₄ is generally more expensive than KCl, the agronomic benefits observed in this study need to be further translated into cost–benefit relationships before a precise region-specific recommendation can be made. Moreover, the present agronomic implication should be limited to the scope of this 40-day greenhouse pot experiment using one lettuce cultivar and one soil background. Multi-season field validation across different soils, cultivars, irrigation regimes, and production systems is required before broad fertilizer recommendations can be made.

This study has several limitations that should be explicitly considered when interpreting the results. First, the experiment lasted only 40 days and covered a single lettuce growth cycle. Such a short experimental duration is suitable for detecting short-term physiological, quality, soil chemical, and microbial responses, but it cannot fully capture seasonal fluctuations, cumulative Cl⁻ effects, or the long-term consequences of repeated KCl or K₂SO₄ application. Future studies should extend the experimental duration across multiple growing seasons and monitor soil salinity, Cl⁻/SO₄²⁻ accumulation, nitrate-N dynamics, exchangeable Ca/Mg ratios in the 0–60 cm soil profile, and changes in soil buffering capacity over time [52].

Second, the experiment was conducted using one soil background and one lettuce cultivar. Soil texture, pH, organic matter content, initial sulfur availability, and chloride retention capacity may strongly influence crop responses to different K₂SO₄ ratios, while different lettuce genotypes may vary in sulfur uptake, chloride tolerance, and nitrate assimilation capacity. Therefore, the conclusions should be validated in soils with contrasting physicochemical properties and across multiple lettuce cultivars or other leafy vegetable genotypes before broader extrapolation is made.

Third, the study was conducted as a pot experiment under greenhouse conditions rather than as a field trial. Pot systems allow better control of nutrient inputs and sampling conditions, but restricted root volume, altered water movement, limited soil buffering capacity, and different nutrient leaching patterns may either overestimate or underestimate the response to fertilizer formulation compared with field conditions. Future work should include micro-plot and field-scale trials to test whether the observed effects on plant quality, nitrogen metabolism, soil properties, and microbial communities remain consistent under realistic irrigation, drainage, and root growth conditions [53].

Fourth, although 16 S rRNA and ITS amplicon sequencing provided useful information on bacterial and fungal community composition, this approach has inherent technical limitations. Amplicon sequencing mainly reflects relative abundance and taxonomic composition, and it cannot directly determine microbial functional activity, nutrient transformation rates, or gene expression. In addition, PCR amplification bias, primer specificity, and limited taxonomic resolution may affect the interpretation of microbial community shifts, particularly when functional inference is made from genus-level patterns [54]. Another important limitation is that no mock communities, extraction blanks, or sequencing negative controls were included in the present sequencing run. Therefore, potential background contaminants, reagent-derived sequences, or low-level cross-sample signals could not be evaluated using control-based filtering. This limitation is particularly relevant for the interpretation of low-abundance taxa, treatment-specific indicator taxa identified by LEfSe, and subtle microbial community shifts among potassium-source treatments. Accordingly, microbial taxa with low relative abundance or taxa detected only in specific treatments should be interpreted cautiously, and the main microbiome-related conclusions of this study should be considered as community-level associations rather than definitive evidence of treatment-specific microbial recruitment. Future studies should combine amplicon sequencing with mock communities, extraction blanks, PCR no-template controls, metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, quantitative PCR of functional genes, and enzyme activity assays related to nitrification, denitrification, sulfur oxidation, and sulfur reduction.

Fifth, the physiological mechanism linking sulfur supply with enhanced NR activity remains incompletely resolved. Although NR and GS activities were measured, the present study did not directly determine the status of Mo and Fe cofactors, electron donor availability, or redox-related metabolites such as NAD(P)H and GSH/GSSG, which may participate in nitrate reduction and nitrogen assimilation [55, 56]. Future studies should integrate transcriptomic, proteomic, RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and targeted biochemical analyses to clarify whether sulfur affects NR activity mainly through transcriptional regulation, cofactor availability, improved electron supply, or changes in plant redox balance.

Overall, this study provides preliminary evidence that adjusting the sulfate-to-chloride contribution in potassium fertilizer can influence lettuce nitrogen metabolism, soil nutrient status, and microbial community structure under controlled greenhouse conditions. Within the scope of this study, R-100:20 showed the best overall compromise among the measured growth, root, quality, and nitrogen-metabolism traits, but this ratio should not be regarded as universally optimal because different treatments performed better for specific traits. Nevertheless, multi-site, multi-soil, multi-cultivar, and multi-season field trials, together with isotopic tracing of nitrogen and sulfur fluxes, are needed to confirm the stability of these effects and to support the development of optimized NPK compound fertilizer formulations that balance productivity, quality improvement, soil sustainability, and economic feasibility.

Conclusions

Under equal total potassium supply, the effects of K₂SO₄:KCl ratios on Italian lettuce were trait-specific and associated with coordinated changes in plant physiological traits, soil chemical properties, and microbial community patterns. Moderate sulfate-dominant formulations, especially R-100:20, achieved a coordinated improvement in shoot biomass, root extension, root surface area, and vitamin C accumulation, whereas full potassium sulfate (R-120:0) enhanced nitrate reductase activity and soluble protein but did not maximize overall growth. Meanwhile, R-40:80 showed advantages in root fresh weight and main-root-related traits, and R-80:40 was more favorable for glutamine synthetase activity. These patterns indicate that potassium source effects are not simply additive, but result from anion-mediated regulation of sulfur–nitrogen metabolism, antioxidant capacity, and osmotic/ionic balance. At the soil level, increasing sulfate proportion elevated available sulfur and potassium, while chloride favored Cl⁻ accumulation and exchangeable calcium retention, reflecting an antagonistic distribution that was associated with differences in rhizosphere nutrient conditions. Microbial communities showed different association patterns: bacteria showed higher turnover and transient taxa, whereas fungi exhibited broader niche occupancy and greater persistence. Notably, Olpidiaster showed relatively broad correlation-based network associations under sulfate-enriched regimes, but this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence of a validated keystone role or direct biological interaction. These findings support a mechanistic framework in which K₂SO₄:KCl ratios influence lettuce performance via three interconnected pathways: (1) sulfate-driven enhancement of sulfur–nitrogen metabolism and antioxidant recycling, (2) chloride-mediated modulation of nitrate assimilation and ionic homeostasis, and (3) microbial community shifts associated with soil nutrient and salinity shifts. From a practical perspective, R-100:20 showed the best overall compromise among the measured growth, root, quality, and nitrogen-metabolism traits under the present experimental conditions, whereas R-120:0 may be preferred when nitrogen-assimilation-related traits are prioritized. Therefore, R-100:20 should not be regarded as a universally optimal potassium-source ratio, but rather as a promising balanced formulation within the scope of this experiment. It should be emphasized that this conclusion is based on a 40-day greenhouse pot experiment using one Italian lettuce cultivar and one soil background. Therefore, multi-season field validation across different soils, cultivars, irrigation regimes, and production systems is required before broad fertilizer recommendations can be made.This integrated view provides guidance for optimizing potassium fertilization in leafy vegetable production while highlighting the need to balance crop performance, soil nutrient status, and economic feasibility.
