The effect of low-dose ketamine on electroencephalographic spectrum during gynecology surgery under desflurane anesthesia
Authors: Yu-Pin Huang, Shih-Pin Lin, Huann-Cheng Horng, Wen-Kuei Chang, Cheng-Ming Tsao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The perioperative administration of low-dose ketamine has shown potential in postoperative pain management, opioid sparing, and enhancing pain control. This study aimed to investigate the impact of low-dose ketamine on processed electroencephalography (EEG) signals during anesthesia.Methods:Forty patients with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I-II undergoing elective gynecological surgery were enrolled. EEG monitoring was initiated upon induction of anesthesia. Anesthesia was maintained with desflurane and alfentanil immediately after induction. Fifteen minutes after induction, the ketamine group received a 0.3 mg/kg bolus followed by 0.05 mg/kg/h infusion until completion of surgery. The control group received equivalent saline. Postoperative assessments included pain score (visual analog scale), morphine usage, and quality of recovery.Results:The ketamine group had significantly higher Patient State Index (PSi) values at 10, 20, and 30 minutes after ketamine administration compared to the controls. Ketamine administration led to significant alterations in EEG patterns, including reduced relative power in delta and theta frequency bands, and increased relative power in beta and gamma frequency bands at 10 minutes post-administration. Relative power in the alpha frequency band significantly decreased at 10, 20, and 30 minutes post-administration. However, there were no differences in intraoperative alfentanil consumption, postoperative morphine usage, and pain scores between the two groups.Conclusion:Low-dose ketamine administration during desflurane anesthesia led to notable changes in EEG patterns and PSi values. These findings provide valuable insights into the impact of ketamine on brain activity, and offer essential information for clinical anesthesiologists.Keywords: Desflurane, Electroencephalography, Gynecology, Ketamine

1. INTRODUCTION

Electroencephalography (EEG) is widely used to measure cortical activity across the brain, defined as gamma (30-40 Hz), beta (13-30 Hz), alpha (8-12 Hz), theta (4-8 Hz), and delta (0.5-4 Hz) waves. Oscillations in these waves vary based on the type and dosage of anesthetic agents in general anesthesia. Intraoperative high alpha-band power not only reflects balance between hypnosis and adequate analgesia, but is also associated with reduced delirium in the postoperative recovery room.1

Ketamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, is widely used as a hypnotic agent in anesthesia, especially in patients with septic shock or difficult airway condition. Ketamine has also been shown to inhibit nociceptive pathways, modulate the release of neurotransmitters, and inhibit central sensitization.2 In addition, low-dose ketamine used as an adjuvant agent during surgery has been shown to enhance postoperative pain management, decrease opioid consumption, and improve pain control.3 The application of ketamine has recently been extended to depression and chronic pain management.4

Different doses of ketamine infusion cause a variety of responses perioperatively. In high doses, ketamine produces anesthesia and analgesia and is often used in diagnostic and surgical procedures that do not require skeletal muscle relaxation. However, lower doses of ketamine may reduce perioperative opioid use and decrease related side effects such as dizziness and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV).5 In general, lower doses (ie, 0.1-0.5 mg/kg) may be sufficient to achieve an adequate balance between analgesia and adverse effects.6 Previous studies have shown that administering a 0.3 mg/kg IV bolus of ketamine followed by a 0.05 mg/kg/h infusion until the end of cholecystectomy and hysterectomy surgeries could enhance early pain control and reduce opioid consumption.7,8 Therefore, we adopted this ketamine regimen for our study.

Importantly, ketamine induces a unique dissociative state in the brain, leading to a totally different state of anesthesia and EEG pattern compared with other anesthetic drugs. The induction dose of ketamine has been shown to reduce alpha power and increase gamma power, in contrast to both propofol and sevoflurane.9 The distinct brain activity pattern may lead to misinterpretation of the depth of anesthesia from EEG spectral array, potentially resulting in inaccurate decision-making. Therefore, in this study, we conducted a randomized controlled trial to assess the effects of low-dose ketamine on serial EEG changes during surgery under desflurane anesthesia. In addition, we investigated postanesthetic side effects including hallucinations and PONV.

2. METHODS

2.1. Study design

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan (IRB no. 2022-03-005B) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the study.

2.2. Study participants

We enrolled patients aged 20 to 70 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I-II who were undergoing elective gynecological surgery requiring general anesthesia scheduled for more than 60 minutes at Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan. The exclusion criteria were known allergies to any of the study medications or anesthetic drugs, renal insufficiency, epilepsy or a history of convulsions, psychiatric problems (such as bipolar disorder and schizophrenia), previous analgesic treatment (opiates and NMDA receptor blockers), or substance abuse.

2.3. Randomization and blinding

All patients in this study were randomly assigned to either the control group or ketamine group based on a computer-generated list with a 1:1 allocation. Information regarding group assignment was hidden in sealed opaque envelopes. When the patient arrived at the operating room, the anesthesiologist opened a sequentially numbered envelope, and the card inside determined into which group the patient was placed. An anesthesia nurse who was not involved in the study prepared the study medication. The participants and all study staff (including those involved in recruitment, data collection and entry, and laboratory analyses) were blinded to the treatment allocation throughout the entire study period. The attending anesthesiologist could not be blinded as they were required to record the different drug administration protocols.

2.4. Anesthetic protocol and interventions

None of the enrolled patients received any premedication before the administration of anesthesia. Standard anesthesia monitoring was performed, including electrocardiography, noninvasive arterial pressure, capnography, and pulse oximetry.

The depth of anesthesia was monitored using a SEDline EEG monitor (Masimo Corporation, Irvine, CA). The SEDline monitor calculates an index of hypnotic depth ranging from 100 (fully awake) to 0 (very deep hypnosis), called the Patient State Index (PSi), along with EEG power spectrum and density spectral array (Fig. 2A). Pain and analgesia were assessed using the Analgesia Nociception Index (ANI) with an ANI monitor (V1, MDoloris Medical Systems, France). The ANI ranges from 0 (maximum nociception or sympathetic dominance) to 100 (complete analgesia or parasympathetic dominance), and is based on the analysis of heart rate variability resulting from respiratory fluctuations to assess the balance between nociception and antinociception. Electrodes for the SEDline and ANI monitors were placed on the forehead and chest, respectively, following the manufacturers’ guidelines. Signal quality was regularly monitored to ensure optimal electrode skin contact, with raw data continuously recorded and processed for each patient throughout the surgical procedure. The exact time of the following stages of the surgery were analyzed: baseline, start of induction, end of intubation, 0, 10, 20, 30, and 60 minutes following ketamine or saline administration, end of surgery, and extubation.

General anesthesia was induced with propofol (1-2 mg/kg IV) and alfentanil (10 μg/kg IV), and neuromuscular blockade was achieved using rocuronium at a dose of 0.6 mg/kg. The patients were intubated with an orotracheal tube, and mechanical ventilation was adjusted to maintain an end-tidal carbon dioxide level of 34 to 36 mmHg, with a tidal volume of 6 to 8 mL/kg of ideal body weight. Axillary temperature was maintained above 36°C using a forced-air warming system (Bair-Hugger, Arizant Healthcare Inc., Eden Prairie, MN).

Anesthesia was maintained with 5% to 6% desflurane, delivered at a fresh gas flow rate of 2 L/min in a mixture of oxygen and air. Subsequently, the patients received alfentanil via target-controlled infusion (using the Scott model and Injectomat TIVA Agilia device from Fresenius Kabi, Sèvres, France), with an initial effect-site target of 10 ng/mL. The infusion rate of alfentanil was adjusted to maintain an ANI score between 50 and 70. Excessive analgesia was defined as an ANI score greater than 80. If the ANI score remained below 50 or above 80 for 5 minutes, the target concentration of alfentanil was adjusted by 10 ng/mL. Data were recorded using event markers at the time of surgical incision or in cases of bradycardia (heart rate <45 bpm) or asystole. The depth of neuromuscular block was maintained with additional injections of 10 to 20 mg of rocuronium as required.

The ketamine group received a 0.3 mg/kg IV bolus followed by a 0.05 mg/kg/h infusion of ketamine until the end of surgery. The control group received the equivalent bolus and infusion rate of normal saline as the ketamine group. The initial dose of the study medication was administered 15 minutes after intubation.

At the end of the surgery, any residual neuromuscular blockade was reversed with neostigmine and atropine, and the infusions of desflurane and alfentanil were discontinued to facilitate patient awakening. Subsequently, the patient was transferred to the postoperative recovery room. Postoperative pain was evaluated using an 11-point visual analog scale (VAS), with 0 denoting no pain and 10 the worst possible pain. Any postoperative side effects, including nausea and vomiting, dizziness, hallucinations, drowsiness, headache, and itching, were documented during the follow-up period. Pain levels, sedation, opioid usage, and side effects were assessed at 1, 4, and 24 hours after surgery by a research assistant who was blinded to group randomization.

2.5. EEG processing

EEG data were collected using the SEDline monitor and saved in the European data file format. The EEG was sampled at a rate of 178 Hz, with a paper speed of 30 mm per second and an amplitude resolution of 5 µV per mm. To analyze the power spectral density of the EEG, the data were segmented into 5-second intervals with a 1-second overlap. The Scipy signal package was utilized, incorporating the Hann window function and nperseg setting of 512.10 The data were first band-pass filtered between 0.5 and 40 Hz in order to remove slow drifts and high frequency noise. From each recording, 5-second epochs were analyzed for artifacts, and any contaminated epochs were removed from the dataset. Data with a Z-score of ±3 were considered to be contaminated. Relative power was computed as the ratio of the power within a specific frequency band to the total sum of power across all frequencies (Fig. 2B).

2.6. Statistical analysis

The necessary sample size for each group was calculated to detect a significant 25% difference in EEG relative power, with a statistical power of 0.8 and a significance level of 5%. Based on these parameters, a minimum of 12 patients per group was required to observe the expected EEG difference. To accommodate potential dropouts, we aimed to enroll a total of 20 patients in each group.

Continuous variables including age, weight, height, and EEG relative power were summarized using means and SDs. The normality of these variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test before analysis. In this randomized controlled study, we used repeated measures analysis of variance to examine the impact of the intervention at various time points. If significant differences were found, post-hoc tests, specifically the independent t test, were used to identify the specific time points at which these differences occurred between groups. This methodology helped to control for potential confounding variables, thereby strengthening the robustness of our findings. In cases where the data did not meet normality assumptions, a nonparametric test, specifically the Mann-Whitney U test, was used. Categorical data including surgical history were presented as frequencies, and analyzed using appropriate statistical tests such as the chi-square or Fisher exact test to identify significant associations. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Inc., Cary, NC).

All of these patients successfully completed the study, and their data were included in the final analysis. 4. DISCUSSION

The main findings of this randomized controlled trial are that low-dose ketamine-induced significant changes in the EEG power spectrum but did not impact early pain control or opioid consumption in patients undergoing gynecological surgeries.

Ketamine acts as an anesthetic adjunct and analgesic primarily by binding to NMDA receptors and hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated channel 1 (HCN1) channels in the brain and spinal cord.11 At low-to-moderate doses, it affects inhibitory interneurons, enhancing activity in downstream excitatory neurons, leading to increased cerebral metabolism and producing effects such as hallucinations, dissociative state, euphoria, and dysphoria.12

The PSi has been shown to be a viable alternative to the Bispectral Index (BIS) for evaluating consciousness during general anesthesia.13 PSi values range from 0 to 100, with lower values indicating increasing levels of hypnosis. In the present study, the patients who received ketamine consistently had higher PSi values compared to the control group while receiving similar concentrations of desflurane. A higher PSi value raises concerns over whether the patient is unconscious, however, no intraoperative awareness with recall was found in either group in this study. This finding is in line with previous studies which reported that ketamine and nitrous oxide can lead to inaccurate BIS measurements.14,15 Notably, increased anesthetic requirements to achieve an equivalent processed EEG index may result in undesirable clinical effects such as hypotension following anesthesia induction.16 In contrast, a previous study reported that BIS values decreased after a bolus of low-dose esketamine, an active enantiomer of ketamine, was administered for anesthesia induction in elderly patients undergoing knee arthroplasty.17 This difference in the depth of anesthesia after ketamine administration may be due to the effects of aging.

During induced anesthesia, desflurane has been shown to lower the alpha activity peak to around 8 Hz, compared to approximately 10 Hz for propofol.18 However, ketamine has been shown to significantly increase the median PSi values under desflurane anesthesia compared to the combination of sevoflurane and propofol.19 The effects of ketamine lasted for 30 to 45 minutes, and gradually decreased after 30 minutes.20,21 Significant alterations in spectral analysis were noted in the relative power of fast frequency bands, indicating a shift in neural activity following the loading dose. These beta-gamma oscillations typically commenced 2 minutes after the initial ketamine dose.22 Previous research has reported gamma oscillations in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus linked to the antidepressant effects of ketamine.23 A human pharmaco-neuroimaging study found that ketamine altered resting-state functional connectivity between the thalamus and noradrenergic nucleus, the locus coeruleus,24 suggesting that ketamine augments gamma power through noradrenergic signaling. Notably, we found a reduction in the relative power of delta, theta, and alpha frequency bands, accompanied by an increase in beta and low gamma frequency bands, consistent with findings from other studies.9,22,23 Ketamine has been demonstrated to dose-dependently suppress anterior-to-posterior connectivity in the alpha bandwidth, identified in the precuneus and temporal-parietal junction.12,25,26 Alpha rhythms are considered crucial for conscious orientation to space and time, as they involve coordinated oscillatory activity essential for neural information processing.27

Of note, the effect of ketamine on neural oscillations is dose-dependent. When used as a sole agent, 1 to 2 mg/kg of ketamine has been shown to introduce oscillations in the high beta range and low gamma range (24-32 Hz).9,22 The subanesthetic dose of ketamine used in our study (0.3 mg/kg + 50 µg/kg/h) appears to be at the lower end of ranges reported in the literature.7,8 This dosing regimen of ketamine significantly increased the PSi values and fast frequency bands during desflurane anesthesia. In a randomized controlled study, 0.5 mg/kg of ketamine notably increased BIS values (from 40 to 63) in patients undergoing general anesthesia with propofol and fentanyl, whereas 0.2 mg/kg did not increase BIS values.28 In our study, the EEG changes observed within 30 minutes after ketamine administration seemed to be mainly caused by the initial 0.3 mg/kg bolus. The effect of 0.05 mg/kg/h infusion on EEG appeared to be minimal. Further studies are required to evaluate the potential dose-response effect of ultra-low-dose ketamine on EEG.

A subanesthetic dose of ketamine is commonly used as an analgesia adjuvant for acute pain.29,30 Several studies have demonstrated that intraoperative ketamine administration reduced postoperative opioid consumption and pain scores in various surgical procedures.6,31,32 However, we found no significant differences in perioperative opioid equivalent consumption and intraoperative ANI values between the two groups. This may be due to the relatively low subanesthetic infusion dose of ketamine and the small sample size in our study. In addition, there were no significant differences in PONV, recall, and hallucinations between the two groups. Therefore, our choice of a relatively low dose of perioperative ketamine did not significantly enhance postoperative analgesia or exacerbate common postoperative complications.

There are several potential limitations to this study. The study population involved only female patients undergoing lower abdominal surgery, and a previous study on sex differences in resting EEG found that the reductions in parietal midline delta and theta, parietal alpha, and parietal midline beta were significantly larger in females compared to males.33 In addition, minimally invasive laparoscopic surgical procedures and a short surgical duration are associated with less postoperative acute surgical pain. Further comparative studies including a sufficient number of both male and female patients and encompassing various surgeries where chronic pain is a potential concern are needed to verify our findings.

In conclusion, our study illustrated the impact of low-dose perioperative ketamine on EEG patterns and PSi values in patients undergoing gynecologic surgery under desflurane anesthesia. The distinct and dose-dependent EEG changes induced by ketamine provide valuable insights into its effects on brain activity, offering essential information for anesthesiologists and researchers. Further research exploring optimal dosages and specific patient groups may enhance our understanding and contribute to the development of improved anesthesia protocols in the future.
