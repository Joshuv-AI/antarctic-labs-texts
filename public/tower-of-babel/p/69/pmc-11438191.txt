The value of surfactant protein a in evaluating the severity and prognosis in community-acquired pneumonia patients
Authors: You-Peng Deng, Jing Sun, Qi-Yuan He, Ying Liu, Lin Fu, Hui Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious research has discovered that surfactant protein A (SP-A) is involved in the pathophysiology processes of certain lung illnesses. However, no definitive clinical studies have delved into the function of SP-A in individuals afflicted with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). A prospective cohort study was used to investigate the relationships between blood SP-A levels and the severity and prognosis among CAP patients.Materials and methodsThis study included 260 patients with CAP. Clinical traits and demographic data were examined during hospitalization. The concentrations of serum SP-A and serum interleukin-6 (IL-6) were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). In addition, to evaluate the severity of CAP, a variety of scores, including the CURB-65, PSI, SMART-COP, and APACHE II, were employed.ResultsThe serum levels of SP-A at admission exhibited a gradual decline as the severity scores of CAP increased. Through Spearman correlation analysis, we observed an association between serum SP-A and some clinical indicators among CAP patients. Furthermore, results from a multiple linear regression model suggested changes in PSI scores (-17.868 scores, 95% CI: -32.743, -2.993) affect serum SP-A more than CURB-65 (-0.547 scores, 95% CI: -0.964, -0.131), SMART-COP (-1.097 scores, 95% CI: -1.889, -0.304) and APACHE II (-3.475 scores, 95% CI: -5.874, -1.075) with age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebral infarction, coronary heart disease, and bronchitis adjusted. In addition, the prognosis in CAP patients was monitored. Throughout their hospital stay, higher serum levels of SP-A decreased the risks of mechanical ventilation (RR: 0.315; 95% CI: 0.106, 0.937), vasoactive agents (RR: 0.165; 95% CI: 0.034, 0.790), intensive care unit (ICU) admissions (RR: 0.218; 95% CI: 0.066, 0.717) and longer hospital stays (RR: 0.397; 95% CI: 0.167, 0.945).ConclusionIn CAP patients, inverse dose-response correlations exist between serum SP-A levels with severity scores as well as prognosis at admission, suggesting that SP-A may take part in the CAP pathophysiological processes. Moreover, lower serum SP-A on admission is associated with an elevated prognostic risk of mechanical ventilation, the use of vasoactive agents, longer hospital stays, ICU admission, and mortality. Therefore, as a biomarker, SP-A may have the potential to predict the severity and poor prognosis of CAP patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-024-03297-y.

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a common acute respiratory infection that impacts the alveoli and distal airways and refers to the acquisition of the disease by an individual who has not been hospitalized in the month preceding the onset of symptoms [1]. The most typical signs of CAP include cough, dyspnea, chest pain, phlegm, and fatigue [2, 3]. It is widely accepted that the pathogenesis of CAP involves changes in the lung microbiota, ranging from physiological homeostasis to ecological dysregulation. This dysregulation is associated with low microbial diversity, high microbial burden, and a corresponding immune response [4, 5]. CAP has long been a widespread respiratory disease [6, 7]. As per the 2019 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) research [8], children under 5 and adults over 70 are the most populations affected by lower respiratory infections, contributing to 489 million cases of infection, 11 million cases of endemicity and 2.49 million cases of deaths. Symptoms of CAP may be less apparent in elderly patients [9] and individuals undergoing treatment with steroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and antibiotics [10], potentially leading to a higher incidence of misdiagnosis [11]. Despite ongoing advances in laboratory and imaging techniques, existing diagnostic techniques still have certain shortcomings that prevent effective early definitive diagnosis and assessment of prognosis. Early prediction and risk assessment for CAP still pose significant challenges due to the lack of a single symptom or finding with sufficient sensitivity or specificity to diagnose or exclude pneumonia. In recent years, serological markers have gained attention due to their objectivity, accuracy, simplicity, and cost-effectiveness. Studies on serological markers for CAP have demonstrated the effective prediction of poor outcomes linked with CAP by procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP) [12, 13]. Nevertheless, for some CAP with atypical symptoms, the levels of PCT and CRP in these patients are normal or slightly elevated.Recognizing the limitations of currently used ancillary tests in the diagnosis of clinical disease, it is imperative to develop better biomarkers that can accurately assess the severity of CAP and simplify diagnostic procedures for early diagnosis, prevention of progression, treatment, and prognosis evaluation.

Lung surfactant substances are a complex blend of lipids and proteins that are essential for the maintenance of normal breathing function [14] and are thought to contribute to lung immune defense. Lung surface-active proteins contain surfactant protein A (SP-A), surfactant protein B, surfactant protein C, and surfactant protein D [15]. SP-A, an oligomeric glycoprotein, belongs to the collection proteins family, which includes collagen-like and Ca(2+)-dependent carbohydrate recognition domains [16]. SP-A takes part in the metabolic process of surfactants to identify the molecular patterns associated with pathogens and thus performs their biological function, leading to microbial clearance, conditioning, or permeabilization [17]. SP-A is also important for maintaining alveolar epithelium homeostasis and essential to ensure proper parenchymal surface integrity [18]. In recent years, the link between SP-A and lung disease has been gradually emphasized. Previous studies found that human SP-A mRNA was seen to be highly expressed in alveolar epithelial type II cells [19], Clara cells [20], and others. Numerous studies showed that SP-A was involved in the physiological processes of various lung illnesses, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis(IPF), cystic fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), mycoplasma pneumonia, and respiratory distress syndrome [21–26]. Furthermore, several researches suggested that altered SP-A expression might be correlated with lung inflammation [27, 28].

These studies suggested that SP-A was involved in the pathophysiology of pulmonary illnesses, but its role in CAP patients seemed obscure. Additionally, the relationship between SP-A and the outcomes of CAP patients was unclear. Consequently, we believed that it would be meaningful to evaluate the level of serum SP-A in patients with CAP. To address this, our research designed a prospective cohort study with a hospital-based sample to explore the correlations between serum SP-A with the severity scores and prognosis of patients with CAP.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

The study included 311 patients, among whom 39 patients had incomplete information, 8 patients lost blood specimens, and 4 patients dropped out. Ultimately, 260 patients were selected for recruitment, comprising 149 males and 111 females (Supplemental Fig. 1). The patients were diagnosed with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) and met the criteria for admission. They were recruited from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University in Hefei, Anhui Province, China, between August 2021 and March 2023. After obtaining informed consent from the patients, we gathered relevant demographic characteristics and clinical information. All patients diagnosed with CAP must meet the following clinical diagnostic criteria [29]: ① Occurred in the community, not hospital; ②age > 18 y, ③occurred in a patient who was not hospitalized or residing in a long-term care facility for more than 2 weeks before the onset of symptoms; ④the presence of cough, sputum production, and dyspnoea; core body temperature higher than 38.0 °C; auscultatory findings of abnormal breath sounds and rales; or the counts of white blood cell more than 10 × 109 L or less than 4 × 109 L. Patients < 18 years old, those who were pregnant, those with concurrent lung disease (such as pulmonary malignancy, tuberculosis, immunodeficiency, COPD, IBL, respiratory distress syndrome), or those who had received antibiotic therapy or interventions before hospitalization were excluded [30, 31]. Subsequently, pneumonia severity was measured by several scores, which included the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) score, CURB-65 score, SMART-COP score, CURXO score, and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) [32].

Fasting peripheral blood specimens were obtained from CAP patients within one day before administration of antibiotics [30, 33, 34]. During hospitalization, we gathered their personal information and underwent routine blood and biochemical tests. Population demographics, comorbidities, laboratory test data, and vital signs after admission were acquired from the hospital database, with their agreement. The Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (YX2021-147) provided authorization for the research.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Fasting blood specimens, approximately 5 milliliters per person, were collected from the patients at 6 a.m. When the centrifugation was completed at 3,500 RPM at 4◦C [30, 31], the specimens were stored in a refrigerator that maintained a -80℃ temperature. The concentrations of SP-A and serum inflammatory factors were quantified by ELISA following the instructions previously [31, 35, 36]. SP-A ELISA kits were obtained from Cusabio, Wuhan, China (https://www.cusabio.com/). IL-6 (JYM0140Hu) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (JYM1194Hu) ELISA kits were obtained from Wuhan ColorfulGene Biological Technology Co (http://www.jymbio.com/product/316-cn.html).

Statistical analysis

We used two software programs, SPSS 20.0 and GraphPad Prism 6.0, to conduct the relevant statistical analysis. In Table 1, continuous variables were expressed as mean for normally distributed data and median for skewed data. We compared continuous variables using Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA. In addition, classification variables were represented using the chi-squared (χ2) test. Due to the characteristics of nonnormal distribution, the values of serum SP-A level were log-transformed. Using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for normality distribution data, the clinical indicators in Fig. 2 were tested. Subsequently, the correlation between SP-A and clinical indicators was analyzed using the Spearman correlation coefficient. Besides, multiple linear regression models were employed to estimate the effect between serum SP-A and severity scores of CAP in Table 2, and adjusted for age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebral infarction, coronary heart disease, and bronchitis. Additionally, we used chi-square tests and mixed logistic regression models to evaluate the interconnection between serum SP-A and prognosis in Table 3. To mitigate the influence of potential confounding variables on the relationships between serum SP-A and CAP severity scores, stratified analyses were performed using a mixed logistic regression model. This difference was statistically significant when the p-value did not exceed 0.05.

Results

Demographic features and clinical information

We acquired basic demographic information and clinical characteristics of 260 CAP patients, and then compared and analyzed the data (Table 1). According to the tertiles of log serum SP-A using ELISA, we divided these patients into three parts: 85 cases in group T1 (< 2.206), 89 cases in group T2 (2.206 ~ 2.515), and 86 cases in group T3 (> 2.515). The results in Table 1 revealed a median age of 59.1 years and a median BMI of 22.2 kg/m2. In addition, of the CAP patients recruited, 149 (57.3%) were male. The median heart rate was 89.1 beats per minute, while the median respiratory rate was 19.8 breaths per minute. The median fingertip oxygen saturation was 94.7%, with a median body temperature of 36.7 °C, a median systolic pressure of 125.2 mmHg, and a median diastolic blood pressure of 75.1 mmHg. During hospitalization, we found that 68 cases (26.2%) had hypertension, 26 cases (10%) had diabetes mellitus, 25 cases (9.6%) had cerebral infarction, 14 cases (5.4%) had coronary artery disease, and 3 cases (1.2%) had bronchitis. In addition, several median values for inflammation-related indicators were observed: procalcitonin 0.08 ng/L, D-Dimer 0.8 mg/L, tumor necrosis factor-alpha 7.6 pg/mg, and IL-6 14.5 pg/mg. No distinctions were measured in sex, heart rate, respiratory rate, fingertip oxygen saturation, body temperature, blood pressure, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebral infarction, coronary artery disease, bronchiectasis, gammaglobulin, and IL-6 among these three tertiles. Besides, we found that age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebral infarction, coronary heart disease, and bronchitis differed significantly in three tertiles of serum SP-A. As indicated in Table 1, we evaluated averages of CURB-65, PSI, SMART-COP, and APACHE II scores were 1.1, 0.9, 66.2, 1.7, and 7.7, respectively.

SP-A levels among CAP patients of various severity scores

Serum SP-A concentrations were markedly decreased among CAP patients in contrast to the control group (Fig. 1A). In CAP patients, the levels of serum SP-A were minorly lower in the 3–5 score group based on CURB-65 scores compared to the 0–1 score group and 2 score group (Fig. 1B). As indicated in Fig. 1C, among CAP patients with SMART-COP scores of 7–8, the serum SP-A contents were the lowest. In addition, Fig. 1D suggested that serum SP-A concentrations associated with V grade of PSI scores were lower than any other grade (Fig. 1D). When compared to CAP patients with APACHE II scores of < 4, the amounts of serum SP-A were less in the groups of 4–6 scores (Fig. 1E).

Associations between serum SP-A and clinical characteristics in patients with CAP

This research examined the correlations between serum SP-A levels with liver function, kidney function, myocardial function, routine blood parameters, and inflammatory cytokines among patients with CAP. These associations were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient and Pearson’s rank correlation. These findings indicated negative connections between serum SP-A levels with D-Dimer and CRP (Fig. 2), while no significant correlations were observed between serum SP-A and other indicators.

The impact of severity scores on serum SP-A in CAP patients

As shown in Table 2, multiple linear regression models were used to evaluate the interaction between log serum SP-A levels and severity scores among CAP patients, with age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebral infarction, coronary heart disease, and bronchitis adjusted. The findings of Table 2 suggested that a decrease in tertile of log serum SP-A was related to changes in -0.547 scores (95% CI: -0.964, -0.131), -1.097 scores (95% CI: -1.889, -0.304), -17.868 scores (95% CI: -32.743, -2.993), and − 3.475 scores(95% CI: -5.874, -1.075) [17] for CURB-65, SMARTCOP, PSI, and APACHE II, respectively. Furthermore, mixed logistic regression models showed that log serum SP-A tertiles were negatively correlated with these severity scores in CAP patients (Table 2). By estimating the change (95% CI) in each tertile of log serum SP-A, in CAP patients scored on SMART-COP, cases in the highest log serum SP-A group were 0.297 times more frequent than those in the lowest serum SP-A group, with P-values of 0.006, respectively. However, there was no statistically significant difference in cases between the serum SP-A groups among CAP patients with CURB-65, PSI, and APACHE II scores.

Correlation between serum SP-A and prognosis in patients with CAP

As shown in Table 3, several variables were selected to measure prognosis, including mechanical ventilation, vasoactive agents, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, death, and longer hospital stays, and then followed in these patients. Based on the levels of log SP-A, a total of 260 CAP patients were categorized into 3 groups: T1 (86 patients), T2 (87 patients), and T3 (87 patients). The counts of patients diagnosed with CAP who were treated with mechanical ventilation and ICU admission exhibited a greater occurrence in the T1 and T2 groups, in contrast to the T3 group. Similarly, the cases of vasoactive agent usage, death, and longer hospital stays were increased with reduced serum SP-A. Moreover, logistic regression analysis revealed that the relative risks in the T3 group of mechanical ventilation (0.315; 95% CI: 0.106, 0.937), vasoactive agents (0.165; 95% CI: 0.034, 0.790), ICU admission (0.218; 95% CI: 0.066, 0.717) and longer hospital stays (0.397; 95% CI: 0.167, 0.945) were lower than that in the T1 group.

Discussion

The present study centered around a prospective cohort analysis aiming to evaluate the relationship between serum SP-A levels, severity scores, and unfavorable prognosis in individuals diagnosed with CAP. It was observed that serum SP-A levels gradually declined with increasing CAP severity scores when they were hospitalized. Furthermore, a negative correlation was identified between serum SP-A levels and CAP severity scores at the time of admission. Lastly, a decrease in serum SP-A levels at admission may elevate the risks of patients with CAP requiring vasoactive medications and admission to the ICU.

SP-A had surfactant-modulating properties and immunomodulatory functions [37]. SP-A was highly expressed in alveolar epithelial type II cells [19] and Clara cells [20] in previous studies, and was associated with lung disease [37]. In a mouse model of acute lung injury, mice lacking SP-A exhibited increased inflammation and destroyed the integrity of epithelial cells [38]. Additionally, the expression of SP-A levels was decreased in the lung tissue of COPD patients [23]. Consistent with this result, the expression of SP-A mRNA was down-regulated in a mouse model infected with Staphylococcus aureus [39]. However, the relationship between CAP patients and serum SP-A levels was not exact. Therefore, we examined circulating SP-A levels in patients with varying severity of CAP. We found that in CAP patients, serum SP-A levels declined progressively in parallel with the severity score. Moreover, a negative correlation was confirmed between serum SP-A at admission and the severity score of CAP through mixed linear and logistic regression models. In addition, our team found that serum SP-A was weakly and inversely associated with D-dimer and CRP levels among patients diagnosed with CAP. It is noteworthy that the observed trends in serum SP-A and some of the CAP severity scores did not align perfectly. This result is consistent with that of other previous studies [40, 41] and may be related to the characteristics of the CAP severity score. In particular, the CURB-65 and PSI scores were employed to evaluate the risk of mortality, whereas the CURXO score was utilized to assess the suitability of advanced life-supportive therapy in critically ill patients with severe CAP [32]. Our findings also demonstrated a statistically significant correlation between SP-A and the SMART-COP score, as well as ICU admission and the administration of vasoactive drugs. These findings showed a potential connection between serum SP-A and the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of CAP and suggested that lower levels of serum SP-A indicate greater severity in CAP patients.

A previous study has hinted that SP-A can indirectly involve the prognosis of several diseases, either by viral neutralization or by maintaining lung homeostasis and suppressing inflammatory effects [42]. In the tumor and adjacent normal bronchial epithelial cells, an increased rate of SP-A gene deletion contributes to the decreased survival time of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients [43]. In renal cell carcinoma, elevated levels of SP-A expression are associated with a favorable prognosis [44]. In addition, serum SP-A levels can be used as a marker to predict disease progression in IBL [45]. Nevertheless, the relationship between serum SP-A and prognosis was unclear. Then we evaluated the association between serum SP-A levels at admission and several prognosis indicators in patients with CAP. Our study results demonstrated that the lower admission serum SP-A levels increased the risks of mechanical ventilation, vasoactive agents, ICU admission, and longer hospital stays. The findings above indicated that diminished levels of serum SP-A were positively associated with an unfavorable prognosis among CAP patients.

The above findings suggested that serum SP-A levels in patients with CAP exhibited negative correlations with both disease severity and prognosis, but the mechanism was not clear yet. It was well known that SP-A, as a member of the collection protein family [16], played an important role in the recognition of apoptotic cells and nucleic acids and their clearance [46, 47], and was widely expressed in lung epithelial cells [17]. When there is an invasion of pathogens, inflammatory cytokines, and toxic metabolites in the lung epithelial cells or other lung cells, all of these cause the release of inflammatory factors. Inflammation in patients with CAP is manifested by elevated cytokines, including IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-8, IL-1β, and TNF-α, as well as other factors belonging to the T-helper (Th) 17 subgroup [48]. Similarly, higher levels of inflammatory markers CRP, PCT, serum amyloid A (SAA), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and IL-1β are observed in the serum of pediatric patients with community-acquired pneumonia [49]. A study [50] has confirmed the association of SP-A with infection and inflammation. In lung tissues of COPD mice, there was a reduction in SP-A expression, accompanied by an increase in TGF-β, TNF-α, and NF-κB, as well as airway mucus hypersecretion. Indeed, SP-A had multiple functions in innate immune host defense. It regulates inflammatory chemotaxis through collagen-like regions and is an inducer of enhanced macrophage migration [17]. The regulation of SP-A was dependent on cytokines, particularly TNF-α [51]. Previous studies had suggested that TNF-α was a strong inhibitor of SP-A [51–54]. TNF-α downregulates SP-A mRNA expression in alveolar epithelial cells by phosphorylating the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway [55]. Therefore, we speculated that the “inflammatory storm” in patients with CAP inhibits the mRNA expression of SP-A, resulting in decreased expression in the lung and decreased penetration of SP-A into the serum.

Although some previous papers had examined the relationship between serum SP-A and diseases such as bacterial pneumonia, IBL, COPD, and lung cancer, the specific association with CAP had rarely been mentioned and had the shortcomings of small sample size and the lack of establishing links between clinical data with prognosis and severity. Likewise, there were some limitations to mention in this research. Firstly, the sample size was relatively insufficient in our cohort study because all patients were selected from the same hospital. Future multicenter studies and larger sample sizes are needed for better observation. Secondly, though serum SP-A levels were detected, the levels of SP-A expression in lung tissue, sputum, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from CAP patients remained unclear. Thirdly, the present study was a sole epidemiological study of the population, and the mechanism behind the decrease in SP-A has not been clarified. Further investigations involving animal experiments are necessary to explore the specific mechanism in animals with CAP.

Conclusion

The current investigation primarily examined the associations between serum SP-A levels and the severity and prognosis of patients with CAP through a prospective cohort study. Our results indicated a gradual decrease in serum SP-A levels upon entering the hospital, as the CAP severity scores increased. Furthermore, we observed a negative association (mechanical ventilation, vasoactive agents, ICU admission, and longer hospital stays) between serum SP-A levels and poor prognosis in CAP patients during their hospitalization. Consequently, these results indicate that serum SP-A could potentially serve as a valuable biomarker for diagnosing, prognosticating, and managing CAP patients in future clinical practice.
