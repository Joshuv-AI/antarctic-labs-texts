Receipt and Use of Tobacco Product Coupons Among Adults Who Use Cigarettes, Cigars, and Smokeless Tobacco: Reasons for Use and Association With Subsequent Tobacco Product Purchases
Authors: McKinley E Saunders, Naa A Inyang, Caryn F Nagler, Ellen M Coats, Sherry T Liu, Brett R Loomis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study examines the prevalence and correlates of receipt and use of discount coupons for cigarettes, cigars, smokeless tobacco, and e-cigarettes, reasons for coupon use, and likelihood of future tobacco product purchases among US adults who use tobacco.MethodsData were obtained from the National Panel of Tobacco Consumer Studies (TCS Panel), a nationally representative sample of US adults who use cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco. TCS Panel members (n = 3922) were invited to participate in a survey in 2020; 1989 participants (57% weighted) completed it. Weighted prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals and logistic regression models were calculated.ResultsAn estimated 41.7% of US adults who use cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco received a coupon for a tobacco product in the past 12 months; of those, 78.7% redeemed them. Most received cigarette coupons (34.3%), followed by smokeless tobacco (8.8%), e-cigarette (6.1%), and cigar (4.4%) coupons. Coupon use is high among recipients: cigarettes (76.8%), smokeless tobacco (63.9%), cigars (60.3%), and e-cigarettes (37.1%). Many provided contact information to sign up for coupons (31.6%)—the strongest predictor of coupon receipt (odds ratio [OR] = 5.45) and coupon use (OR = 1.94).ConclusionsCoupon receipt and use is common among adults who use tobacco; they often receive and use coupons for multiple tobacco products, illustrating how tobacco companies cross-market products. The findings suggest tobacco companies’ efforts to reach customers and keep them engaged through discount offers are largely successful.ImplicationsProviding contact information is the strongest single factor determining tobacco coupon receipt and use, suggesting tobacco companies are largely successful at building relationships with consumers through electronic apps or other means and at keeping consumers engaged by regularly offering discounts and coupons. Many of the people who use tobacco receive and use coupons for tobacco products that they may not currently use, perhaps prompting progression to new product initiation.Keywords: Risk for tobacco use, Marketing, Survey research

Introduction

With the signing of the Family Smoking Prevention and Tobacco Control Act in 2009, the US Food and Drug Administration obtained the authority to regulate the manufacture, marketing, and distribution of tobacco products. Distribution and redemption of coupons are permitted under federal law, although coupons may not be redeemed through mail order (per 21 CFR §1140.16(c)(2)(i)).1,2 Tobacco companies have increasingly and successfully employed direct-to-consumer price-minimizing strategies, such as including discount coupons in direct-to-consumer mailings and product packages3 to engage potential and existing consumers and influence purchasing and consumption behavior.3–5 A 2015 study found, in one US market, that 87% of direct-mail marketing for well-known brands of cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco contained at least one discount coupon.6 Adult consumers may also intentionally or inadvertently opt in to communication distribution lists to receive coupons from tobacco companies.4

The price-minimizing strategies have effective reach.5–11 A 2018 study found that in the United States, 12.4% of adults who did not smoke cigarettes and 36.2% of adults who currently smoked reported receiving coupons directly via mail/e-mail.5 Consumers in some US markets are motivated to minimize cigarette costs and do so by shopping different locations for lower prices and promotions, substituting with less expensive roll-your-own or pipe tobacco products, and switching to less costly brands.12,13 Research indicates coupon receipt is effective: coupon recipients often redeem coupons for their cigarette purchases.14–16 Using coupons and price promotions to reduce costs has been found to be positively associated with initiating daily e-cigarette use5,17 and progression to smoking cigarettes more frequently,18,19 and negatively associated with stopping e-cigarette19 use, smoking cessation,20 and smoking quit attempts,20 including among youth,9 which has implications for public health. Studies have examined receipt and redemption of e-cigarette and cigarette coupons, but few have assessed coupon use for other tobacco products.

This study aimed to explore patterns of coupon receipt and redemption among adults who use cigarettes, cigars, and/or smokeless tobacco while also including individuals who concurrently use e-cigarettes. Studies have examined the receipt and redemption of cigarette coupons and/or other tobacco product coupons broadly, but few have assessed coupon use for other individual tobacco product categories.5,7,9,11,15

This is the first national study to describe the prevalence and correlates of coupon receipt and redemption for these products among a large, representative sample of US adults currently using cigarettes, cigars, and/or smokeless tobacco and their association with subsequent tobacco purchases. Additionally, we describe the reasons for using a discount coupon and likelihood of future tobacco product purchases.

Methods

Participants and Procedures

Data were obtained from participants in the National Panel of Tobacco Consumer Studies (TCS Panel), which has been fully described by Liu et al.21 The TCS Panel was designed to be nationally representative of US adults who use cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco. It employed a probability-based design to ensure adequate sociodemographic representation and sample size for detecting changes in key outcomes. The target population was noninstitutionalized, non-active-duty military, English- or Spanish-speaking US adults of ages 18 or older who used (daily or some days) cigarettes, cigars, and/or smokeless tobacco. Adults aged 18–25 with annual household incomes of less than $30 000—a population group with lower cigarette smoking prevalence—were oversampled. To enroll, adults had to report current cigarette, cigar, and/or smokeless tobacco use.

The TCS Panel used a national address-based sampling frame with a stratified four-stage design.21 A screening survey was mailed to sampled households, followed by in-person screening for those reporting at least one adult who uses tobacco. An eligible adult was randomly selected to join the panel based on predetermined selection probabilities. The selected adults confirmed eligibility, provided consent, and chose to participate online or by mail. The baseline survey collected data on cigarette, cigar, and smokeless tobacco use, including use frequency, intentions to quit within the next 30 days, dependence, and use of other tobacco products (eg, e-cigarettes, hookah, pipe tobacco). Panel members could be invited to participate in up to eight surveys over a 3-year period, each with its own focus.

This study features results from a survey among TCS Panel members on the use of tobacco price reducing/minimizing behaviors. TCS Panel members (n = 3922) were invited to participate in the survey from April 15 to July 12, 2020; 1989 participants (n = 1700 web; n = 289 mail) completed the survey (weighted response rate: 57%). The sample included adults who reported currently using cigarettes (n = 1406; 71.6%), cigars (n = 488; 25.1%), smokeless tobacco (n = 290; 14.9%), e-cigarettes (n = 283; 14.7%), and hookah (n = 71; 3.7%); 597 adults (30.0%) used more than one product. Participants received $15 for a completed survey. Surveys were available in English and Spanish. Participant characteristics are available in Table S1. Additional information about the TCS Panel and comparisons of panel member characteristics to national distributions are available in a previously published paper.21

Measures

For this study, we defined a discount coupon (hereafter referred to as “coupon”) as a physical coupon or electronic promotion code that made tobacco products cheaper but not free. Discount coupons offer discounts such as dollar-off rebates; buy one, get one free; two for the price of one; and 50% off. Discount coupons do not include vouchers or coupons that could be redeemed for a sample at no cost.

The survey asked participants about their use of cigarettes, cigars, smokeless tobacco, e-cigarettes, and hookah tobacco. For each product type, participants were asked how many discount coupons they had received in the prior 12 months; for what brands (additional brand-related information and results can be found in Table S2); and mode of receipt (eg, e-mail, text, social media, various in-person locations). Participants were also asked how many coupons they had used in the prior 12 months; location redeemed; reason for redemption; and the likelihood of purchasing the discounted product again.

Dependent variables in the analysis included receipt of any tobacco product coupon, use of any tobacco product coupon, likelihood of purchasing the product again after coupon use, and provision of contact information to a tobacco company to receive discounts. Participants indicated coupon receipt by selecting at least one option in response to the question “In the past 12 months, have you received discount coupons for [tobacco product] in the following ways? Please select all that apply.” Response options included “In person (such as at a store, fair, or bar)”; “In or on a tobacco product package”; “In the mail”; “In an email”; “On a cell phone or tablet app (such as Spot You More, Cartwheel, or Marlboro MHQ)”; “In a text message”; “Through social media (such as Instagram, Facebook, or Twitter)”; “On a website”; or “Some other way.” Coupon use was indicated by answering “Yes” to the question “You said that you received a discount coupon for [tobacco product] in the past 12 months. Did you use any of these coupons to purchase [tobacco product]?” The likelihood of purchasing a product again after coupon use was indicated by answering “Somewhat likely” or “Very likely” to the question “Think about the most recent [tobacco product] purchase that you made using a discount coupon. How likely are you to purchase this product again?” Participants were considered to have provided their contact information to a tobacco company if they answered “Yes” to the question “Have you ever shared your address, email, or phone number to sign up for coupons, discounts, or sweepstakes from a tobacco or e-cigarette company?” Respondents had the option to refuse to answer any question. For most questions, a “Don’t know” option was not available; however, some questions about respondent characteristics explicitly included “Don’t know” as a response option. For analysis purposes, both refusals and “Don’t know” responses were coded as missing.

The regression analysis covariates included measures of current and dual/poly use of tobacco products as well as having provided contact information to a tobacco company. Current cigarette use was defined as answering “Yes” to the question “Have you smoked 100 or more cigarettes in your life?” and answering “Every day” or “Some days” to the question “Do you now smoke cigarettes every day, some days, or not at all?” Current cigar use was defined as answering “Every day” or “Some days” to the question “Do you now smoke regular cigars, cigarillos, or little filtered cigars every day, some days, or not at all?” Current smokeless tobacco use and e-cigarette use were similarly defined. We defined dual/poly use as use of more than one tobacco product. Table S3 contains information on current tobacco use status by exclusivity of use. Study participants also answered questions related to tobacco product flavoring. We included binary indicators for mint/menthol, flavored, and unflavored to define current tobacco product flavoring due to participants’ indication of different flavors for each product used (see Table S4 for details).

Other covariates included age (18–34, 35–54, 55+), sex (male, female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and other race), annual household income (<$30 000, $30 000–$49 999, ≥$50 000), education (less than high school, high school graduate or GED, some college or a 2-year degree, college graduate or higher), and US Census region of residence (Northeast, Midwest, South, West).

Statistical Analysis

All the data analyses accounted for the complex survey design and were conducted using survey estimation commands in Stata V.18.22 We excluded participants with missing data on cigarette, cigar, smokeless tobacco, or e-cigarette use from analyses relevant to each product category. We estimated weighted prevalence estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Due to the small sample sizes of several tobacco use groups (eg, smokeless tobacco, cigars), we used the Stata command “kg_nchs,” which applies the Korn–Graubard standards for CIs and criteria established by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) to assess the reliability of our proportions.23 The Korn–Graubard confidence interval is a variation on the “exact” confidence interval for a binomial proportion that has been designed for use with complex survey data such as ours. For example, the NCHS uses it to assess the reliability of estimated proportions from complex surveys and helps determine whether a proportion is statistically reliable enough to be presented as an official estimate. Using the output from the “kg_nchs” command, we created three cautionary flags for interpreting these estimates: (1) when the relative Korn–Graubard CI width exceeded 130% of the proportion, (2) when the complementary proportions were greater than 130% of the proportion, and (3) when the unweighted size for the proportion was less than 30, but the Korn–Graubard CI width remained under 130% of the proportion.23

We ran unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models to examine associations between the dependent variables (receipt of any tobacco product coupon, use of any tobacco product coupon, how likely to purchase the product again after coupon use, and provision of contact information to tobacco company for discounts) and the independent variables (currently smoking cigarettes, currently smoking cigars, currently using smokeless tobacco, currently using e-cigarettes, dual/poly tobacco use, tobacco product flavor [mint/menthol, flavored, unflavored], provided contact information, age, sex, race/ethnicity, annual household income, education, and Census region of residence). Due to the exploratory nature of this analysis, we did not adjust regression results to account for multiple testing.

Results

Sample Descriptive Statistics

Table 1 and Figure 1 present weighted population percentages of coupon receipt and redemption among adults currently using cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco. Figure 1 reports figures for those who received and redeemed coupons by tobacco product. Overall, 41.7% of adults who use these products received a tobacco product coupon in the prior 12 months (ie, in 2019/2020) and, among recipients, 78.7% redeemed these coupons. Cigarette coupons were the most prevalent, with 34.3% receiving coupons for cigarettes compared to less than 10% who received coupons for cigars (4.4%), smokeless tobacco (8.8%), and e-cigarettes (6.1%). However, coupon redemption was generally high among recipients, with redemption rates of 76.8% for cigarettes, 60.3% for cigars, 63.9% for smokeless tobacco, and 37.1% for e-cigarettes.

Table 1 reports receipt and redemption of tobacco product coupons; notably, people who use one type of tobacco product receive coupons for multiple product types. The product categories in this table do not reflect people who report exclusive use. For example, the row labeled “Current Cigarette Use” includes all adults currently smoking cigarettes, with 61.6% reporting exclusive cigarette use and 38.4% reporting dual/poly use (see Table S3 for current tobacco use status by exclusivity of use). Among cigarette coupon recipients, 86.5% reported current cigarette smoking. However, people who reported current use of other products also received cigarette coupons—21.8% of cigarette coupon recipients reported current cigar smoking and 20.0% reported current e-cigarette use. Of note, only 37.3% of e-cigarette coupon recipients reported current e-cigarette use, while 86.3% reported current cigarette smoking. In general, the highest rates of coupon redemption were among those who received like kinds of coupons. For example, 91.7% of those who redeemed a cigarette coupon reported current cigarette smoking. One exception was e-cigarette coupon redemption: 82.8% of those redeeming e-cigarette coupons reported current cigarette smoking, compared to 71.5% who reported current e-cigarette use. High proportions of adults who reported dual/poly use redeemed coupons for cigars (62.0%) and e-cigarettes (70.6%).

Nearly a third of adults who use tobacco (31.6%) shared their address, e-mail, or phone number to sign up for coupons, discounts, or sweepstakes from a tobacco or e-cigarette company (data not shown). The most common way to sign up for discounts was via email or online. Fewer than 3% of adults who use tobacco liked or followed a tobacco company on social media to receive discounts (data not shown).

Regression Models

Table 2 reports the associations between select demographic/socioeconomic characteristics and coupon receipt, coupon redemption, likelihood of subsequent purchase following coupon redemption, and provision of contact information to a tobacco company for discounts (see Table S5 for full model results). Providing contact information to a tobacco company was the strongest predictor of coupon receipt (odds ratio [OR] = 5.45; 95% CI = 4.13% to 7.18%] and coupon use (OR = 1.94; 95% CI = 1.25% to 3.03%). People who reported current cigarette smoking were more likely to receive a coupon (OR = 1.87; 95% CI = 1.15% to 3.05%). People who reported current smokeless tobacco use were more likely than people who did not report using smokeless tobacco to receive a coupon (OR = 1.87; 95% CI = 1.12% to 3.13%) and use a coupon (OR = 3.77; 95% CI = 1.23% to 11.59%). People who reported current e-cigarette use (OR = 3.51; 95% CI = 1.24% to 9.93%) were more likely to purchase the product after coupon use. People who reported dual/poly use (OR = 0.17; 95% CI = 0.04% to 0.66%) were less likely to purchase the product after coupon use. When controlling for current tobacco use and provision of contact information, few demographic characteristics were significant predictors of coupon receipt, use, and subsequent purchase. Compared to those with less than a high school education, high school graduates were more likely to receive a coupon (OR = 1.63; 95% CI = 1.02% to 2.60%) but less likely to use it (OR = 0.25; 95% CI = 0.07% to 0.89%).

Given that providing contact information was the strongest predictor of coupon receipt and coupon use in models 1–3 (Table 2), we hypothesized that this predictor may be masking the effects of other demographic characteristics, such as, age, race/ethnicity, sex, and income. To explore this, we used “providing contact information” as the dependent variable in model 4 while retaining the same set of demographic covariates as in the previous models. The results from model 4 suggest that some age, race/ethnicity, income, and geographic categories are significantly associated with providing contact information to a tobacco company. Thus, it is feasible that providing contact information was masking some effects of those variables in models 1–3. Compared to models of coupon receipt, use, and likelihood of purchasing a tobacco product after coupon use, current tobacco use was not a significant predictor of providing contact information. People who currently used mint/menthol (OR = 1.74; 95% CI = 1.13% to 2.69%) and unflavored (OR = 1.77; 95% CI = 1.15% to 2.72%) tobacco were more likely to provide contact information for discounts than those who did not. Females (vs. males; OR = 1.57; 95% CI = 1.19% to 2.06%) and participants with an income ≥$50 000 (compared to an income <$30 000; OR = 1.61; 95% CI = 1.17% to 2.22%) were more likely to provide contact information. Race/ethnicity also significantly predicted providing contact information. For example, non-Hispanic Black (OR = 0.35; 95% CI = 0.23% to 0.54%) and Hispanic participants (OR = 0.39; 95% CI = 0.24% to 0.62%) were less likely than non-Hispanic White participants to provide contact information for discounts.

Table 3 reports the associations between select demographic characteristics and the dependent variables (coupon receipt, use, and likelihood of purchasing a tobacco product after coupon use) restricted to those who provided their contact information to a tobacco company (see Table S6 for full model results). In the model of coupon receipt by those who provided contact information, people who reported current cigarette smoking (OR = 2.61; 95% CI = 1.17% to 5.83%) and current smokeless tobacco use (OR = 2.59; 95% CI = 1.03% to 6.5%) were more likely to receive a coupon. After limiting the sample to those who provided contact information, no variable was statistically significant in the model of coupon use. However, in the model of likelihood of purchasing a tobacco product after coupon use among those who provided contact information, people who reported current e-cigarette use (OR = 21.51; 95% CI = 2.16% to 213.66%) were significantly more likely to make a subsequent purchase. Discussion

Considering the tobacco industry’s ongoing efforts to reach people who use tobacco and stimulate sales through discount coupons, we examined the prevalence and correlates of coupon receipt and use, the likelihood that coupon use would lead to subsequent tobacco purchases, correlates of providing contact information for discounts, and reasons for coupon use. Our findings confirm that coupon receipt is prevalent in the United States, as more than 40% of adults who use tobacco reported receiving a coupon in the past 12 months. Of the 42% receiving coupons, 79% used the coupon; 91% of those who used the product reported that subsequent purchase was likely. Our results are consistent with previous studies; in a systematic review focused on tobacco coupon receipt, redemption, and the effects of marketing activities on tobacco use, Liber and colleagues24 found tobacco coupon receipt and redemption were more prevalent among price-sensitive subpopulations, with a median prevalence of 5.4%–49.4%. Our finding that 42% of adults currently using tobacco received coupons is at the high end of this range. However, most studies in the Liber paper examined people who smoke cigarettes exclusively, and one study examined consumers of electronic vapor products (which include e-cigarettes). Liber and colleagues24 also found tobacco product coupons were more often received by people with lower educational attainment, higher tobacco use, and those who identify as sexual minorities; they found that coupon use was higher among younger age groups, women, non-Black or Indigenous people, and persons of color.24 In our nationally representative sample, race and educational attainment were not important factors in determining coupon receipt and use. Instead, we found providing contact information to tobacco companies was the most important factor regardless of race and other demographic characteristics.

Our study also found that economic concerns were the main reason for coupon use. Although the overall pattern of results by household income was largely null, the findings suggest that cost-saving behaviors may not be limited to people with lower incomes, reflecting broader patterns of frugality across income groups.

In the United States, tobacco product costs vary substantially across states due to diverse policy and taxation strategies, implemented alongside the federal taxes, designed to increase consumer prices and reduce consumption.25 For instance, state excise taxes range from $0.17 per cigarette pack in Missouri to $5.35 per pack in New York and locality excise taxes increase prices even further. Similarly, cigars may incur no excise taxes in some states or up to 95% tax of the wholesale sales price in others.25 Between 2015 and 2021, the national average cigarette pack price increased 29.5%, rising from $5.57 to $7.22.26 Additionally, in 2019, the contrast in average cigarette pack prices between the highest and lowest states was $5.21 in Missouri compared to $10.53 in New York.27 These cost variations and increases over time combined with individual financial and other circumstances, likely motivate many consumers to seek coupons and explore alternative, cheaper brands and product types.

Coupons are a tobacco industry strategy to price discriminate (thus segmenting the market) and can also reduce the impact of taxes on tobacco product purchases. Although race and other demographic characteristics were not important factors in determining coupon receipt and use in our analysis, these differences may be due to including “providing contact information” as a covariate. Liu and colleagues21 found that most adults who use cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco in the TCS Panel and the 2018 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) had similar characteristics. However, the TCS Panel had a higher proportion of adults of ages 18–25, a higher proportion of participants with a high school diploma/GED, a lower proportion of participants who had never married, and a higher proportion of married people using tobacco than the NHIS,21 which may also affect our results. Thus, coupon receipt and use may be higher in reality than previously reported.

Our study also found that adults who use e-cigarettes (whether exclusively or in combination with cigars, cigarettes, and/or smokeless tobacco) were 3.51 times more likely to repurchase the tobacco product after coupon use compared to adults who use cigarettes, cigars, or smokeless tobacco but do not use e-cigarettes. In contrast, people who reported dual/poly tobacco use of cigarettes, cigars, smokeless tobacco, and/or e-cigarettes were less likely to repurchase after coupon use. Notably, the 9.3% of adults who exclusively use e-cigarettes in our sample had previously used at least one other tobacco product (cigarettes, cigars, or smokeless tobacco) to be eligible for the panel and had quit those products by the time of this study. This context is important when interpreting findings, as our sample does not represent participants who exclusively used e-cigarettes without any prior tobacco use. Nevertheless, the results may suggest that adults who use e-cigarettes exclusively (despite past use of other products) may be particularly responsive to coupons, whereas dual/poly consumers may be less inclined to repurchase a specific product, possibly due to varied product use and potential for reduced brand loyalty. Kaplan and colleagues’28 nationally representative study on cigarette and electronic nicotine delivery system (ENDS) dependence found that exclusive ENDS use exhibited higher product dependence compared to dual use. This heightened dependence among those exclusively using ENDS could drive their greater likelihood of purchasing a tobacco product after coupon use, whereas those who exhibit dual/poly use may show less loyalty, reducing the likelihood of repurchasing a tobacco product after coupon use.

Strengths

This is the first national study to report the prevalence of coupon receipt and use to purchase cigarettes, cigars, smokeless tobacco, and e-cigarettes by a large, nationally representative sample of people who reported currently using cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco. Our findings show a relatively low prevalence of coupon receipt and use for cigars, smokeless tobacco, and e-cigarettes, but that between one-third and two-thirds of those who receive these coupons redeem them. To our knowledge, this study is also the first to report coupon receipt and use for multiple tobacco products among this group. Our findings show that many receive and redeem coupons for tobacco products they may not currently use, illustrating how manufacturers cross-market tobacco products.

Our findings also show that providing contact information to tobacco companies to receive coupons is the strongest predictor of coupon receipt and coupon use. However, in the model of coupon use limited to those who provide contact information, no correlates were statistically significant. It may be that previous studies that found associations between demographic factors and coupon receipt were actually measuring the likelihood of people providing contact information to tobacco companies for discounts. Given what the literature shows is the effect of coupon receipt and use on initiation, progression to additional tobacco use, and poorer cessation outcomes,24 our results suggest that adults currently using cigars, smokeless tobacco, and e-cigarette products who receive and use coupons may be susceptible to the same effects. A longitudinal study could better determine the causal relationship between consumer behaviors (such as sharing contact information and downloading mobile apps), the receipt and use of coupons, and any role these actions may play in tobacco product switching, initiation, and cessation.

Our findings, which indicate that coupon receipt and redemption, while low overall, is on the higher end of the prevalence range found in the literature,24 reinforce the idea that coupon receipt and redemption may be more widespread than previously understood. These findings, along with other related research, strengthen the current knowledge base for evidence-based policy among regulators at many levels for potential restrictions related to coupon receipt and redemption to promote public health. A rationale for enacting such policies is that coupons undermine public health initiatives, such as excise taxes, that are designed to increase product prices. Two states (New Jersey and New York) and numerous localities have enacted policies that prohibit the redemption of tobacco coupons and other price-reducing promotions. By limiting price reductions, the policies can maintain an increased barrier of access that discourages tobacco use among young people and reduces overall tobacco consumption in the population.

Limitations

Our study is not a longitudinal design, so we were not able to definitively test whether study participants who received and used coupons are susceptible to the same effects of coupon receipt and use as those reported in the literature. Another limitation is that e-cigarette use was not an inclusion criterion in the TCS Panel study design; as a result, the sample is not necessarily representative of the US population of adults who use e-cigarettes. Additionally, data collection was conducted during the initial wave of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, which could have influenced coupon receipt and use and affected the generalizability of our findings; however, the recall period for the study was the previous 12 months (predating the COVID-19 pandemic), minimizing this impact. Similarly, data were self-reported; thus, recall bias is possible if participants failed to remember receiving or using coupons. In such a case, our estimates of coupon receipt and use are likely underestimations.

Additionally, a sizable majority of all the coupons received are for cigarettes. Although coupons are received and used for other products, such as cigars, smokeless tobacco, and e-cigarettes, the number of survey participants who reported receiving coupons for those products was relatively low. As a result, some of the results presented in Tables 1 and 4 are limited by small sample sizes and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, logistic regression analysis can overestimate effect sizes for outcomes among small subsamples, especially outcomes that occur more frequently. However, many previous studies on coupon receipt have also employed logistic regression, and maintaining this approach facilitates comparability with prior research.3,5,7,8,16 Despite these limitations, we believe our analysis provides useful insights for regulators and tobacco control researchers.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first analysis to examine cigarette, cigar, smokeless tobacco, and e-cigarette coupon receipt and redemption among a nationally representative sample of adults currently using cigarettes, cigars, and smokeless tobacco. The results indicate coupon receipt is common, as almost half of the population received coupons, and many received coupons for multiple tobacco products. Coupon use by recipients is also common, as is the use of coupons for multiple tobacco products. Unsurprisingly, the strongest single factor determining coupon receipt or use is providing contact information to tobacco companies. This finding underscores the potential public health implications of consumers sharing personal contact information with industry. The downstream effects of this decision, we found, are associated with receipt and subsequent redemption of coupons. As prior research has suggested, coupon use is associated with increased initiation and decreased or delayed cessation in adults and young people.

This also suggests that tobacco company efforts to build consumer relationships through electronic apps and other means are largely successful, and that tobacco companies keep consumers engaged via frequent offers of discounts and coupons. By illuminating the central role of contact information in coupon distribution, this study contributes to the understanding of how tobacco marketing strategies leverage personal contact information to potentially influence tobacco use behaviors and provides valuable insights for public health professionals and policy makers considering approaches for public health.
