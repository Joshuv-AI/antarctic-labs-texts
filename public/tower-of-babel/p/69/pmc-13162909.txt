The subset of M1-paired MHC class II conformers is critical for CD4+ T cell activation
Authors: Sophia Cangialosi, Jesse L Cimino, Safiehkhatoon Moshkani, Matthew J Kryzak, Michael D Robek, James R Drake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor histocompatibility complex class II molecules present exogenous antigen-derived peptide to CD4+ T cells leading to T cell activation as well as signaling into the antigen-presenting cell. Class II is an αβ heterodimer that forms 2 distinct conformers via differential pairing of transmembrane domain GxxxG motifs (i.e. M1- and M2-paired class II). Previous studies with mouse I-Ak and human HLA-DR class II used conformer-specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) to define unique immunobiological properties of each conformer. Here, we report that AMS-32.1, a widely used anti-I-Ad mAb, is-specific for the 20% of M1-paired I-Ad class II expressed by the cell, and 34-5-3S anti-I-Ad mAb is non–conformer specific. While AMS-32.1 engagement of M1-paired I-Ad elicits B cell calcium signaling, 34-5-3S coengagement of M1 and M2-paired I-Ad blocks this response, indicating M2-paired class II inhibits M1-paired class II signaling. In vitro, AMS-32.1 blocks >90% of T cell activation by ovalbumin peptide–I-Ad complexes, even under conditions where peptide is loaded onto both conformers. In vivo, AMS-32.1 blocks accumulation of ovalbumin-specific CD4+ T cells in the spleen and lymph nodes. In a murine model of hepatitis B virus replication, in which major histocompatibility complex class II–restricted T cell–dependent humoral responses are key to antigen clearance, AMS-32.1 blocks hepatitis B surface antigen seroconversion, indicating that M1-paired class II is central to the in vivo development of CD4+ T cell–dependent antibody responses. Overall, this study reveals that AMS-32.1 is specific for M1-paired I-Ad class II and that M1-paired class II is critical for CD4+ T cell activation both in vitro and in vivo.

Introduction

The major histocompatibility complex (MHC) is a critical part of the adaptive immune system known for its role in lymphocyte activation via antigen presentation. MHC class II is responsible for presenting exogenous antigen-derived peptide to CD4+ T cells by B cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages. This allows formation of an antigen-specific immune response, leading to efficient protection. T cell receptor (TCR) engagement of MHC class II is essential for both T cell and B cell activation leading to a robust adaptive immune response. Therefore, investigating the structure and function of MHC class II is integral to understanding adaptive immune responses and preventing disease.

MHC class II is an αβ heterodimer and exists in 2 different conformations known as M1- and M2-paired class II (Fig. 1A).1–4 The class II conformers differ in the pairing of transmembrane (TM) domain dimerization motifs. The TM domain of the α chain contains 2 GxxxG (GG4) dimerization motifs, while the β chain only contains 1 GG4 motif. The β chain motif can pair with either the first or second α chain GG4 motif forming the two different conformers.

Conformer-specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are a primary tool for the study of class II structure and function. Previous work identified 11-5.2 as an mAb specific for M1-paired I-Ak and 10-3.6 as an mAb that recognizes all I-Ak class II molecules.1,2,5 For human HLA-DR, Tü36 was found to be specific for M1-paired class II, while L243 is pan-reactive.3 Using these mAbs, we have shown that M1-paired class II comprises 10% to 20% of class II on the surface of B cells while 80% to 90% of class II is M2-paired.1–3,5,6 Despite the smaller percentage of M1-paired class II, this conformer has a strong activating phenotype. M1-paired class II aggregates in lipid rafts and elicits calcium signals in murine B cells.1,2,5–8 M2-paired class II is found outside of lipid rafts and inhibits B cell calcium signaling, indicating each class II conformer plays distinctly different roles in the immune system.

While conformer-specific mAbs are a key tool for studying MHC class II conformer immunobiology, a method of differential peptide loading can also be used in B cells to discriminate conformer function. BCR-mediated antigen processing results in selective loading of peptide onto M1-paired class II.4,6 In contrast, fluid phase antigen processing, which might occur in autoimmune conditions, results in peptide loading onto both M1- and M2-paired class II.4,6 Differential peptide loading along with conformer-specific mAbs can be used to study differences in the impact of MHC class II conformers on immune responses.

Defining conformer-specific mAbs for more MHC class II alleles will provide additional models to study MHC class II conformer function in mice and humans. The mouse allele for which we currently have conformer-specific reagents, I-Ak, is not expressed by commonly used mouse models, limiting its application. This report characterizes AMS-32.1 as a conformer-specific mAb. AMS-32.1 recognizes I-Ad expressed by multiple mouse strains like BALB/c. This finding will allow investigators to identify MHC class II conformers in additional models. In addition, demonstrating similar properties of class II conformers across the various alleles strengthens the evidence of their function. Identifying more conformer-specific mAbs in other models will lead to a more comprehensive understanding of adaptive immunity. In this report we define a new conformer-specific anti-I-Ad mAb (AMS-32.1) and use it to demonstrate M1-paired class II is uniquely responsible for CD4+ T cell activation both in vitro and in vivo.

Methods

Cell lines

K46µ B cells were grown in RPMI supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 0.1% 2-ME, 1% nonessential amino acids, 1% Na-pyruvate, and 1% L-glutamine. HEK293T cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, and 1× nonessential amino acids. DO-11.10 and 3DO-54.8 T cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1× nonessential amino acids, 1× Na-pyruvate, and 1× L-glutamine.

Antibodies

Antibodies used were AMS-32.1 (BD Biosciences; 553545), AMS-32.1-FITC (BD Biosciences; 553547), AMS-32.1-btn (BD Biosciences; 553546), 34-5-3S (Southern Biotech 1901-01), 34-5-3S-PE (Invitrogen; MA5-17780), IgMb (BD Biosciences; 553435), and M5/114 (Bio X Cell; BE0108).

mAb dialysis

mAbs administered to mice or used in cell culture were dialyzed into physiological buffer using 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff Side-A-Lyzer dialysis cassettes (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 66382). Antibodies were dialyzed in 2 L of saline 3 times, then once in phosphate-buffered saline. The final concentration and volume of the mAbs was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Immunoprecipitation

K46µ B cells and BALB/c splenocytes were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% deoxycholate 1% NP-40 substitute) containing protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific; A32955). Lysate was cleared by high-speed centrifugation at 15,000 g for 10 min. MHC class II was precipitated over a period of 4 d at 4 °C with constant inversion with 5 µg of indicated antibody and 100 µL 10% Protein A-agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 20334).

Western Blot

Immunoprecipitation (IP) samples were boiled in sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis sample buffer and separated on 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels. Proteins were transferred to 0.2 µm nitrocellulose (Bio-Rad; 1620112) by semi-dry blotting. Membranes were blocked with Blotto (5% nonfat dry milk and 0.1% Triton X-100 in borate buffer pH 8.0) containing 10% horse serum (HS). Blots were then probed with 1630a antibody (1:25,000 in Blotto 10% HS)9 for 4 to 6 h then washed. Next, blots were probed with goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (Calbiochem; 401353; 1:25,000 in Blotto 10% HS). Blots were then developed with SuperSignal West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 34076). Blots were imaged with Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (V2.0.0.25). Molecular weight and densitometry were calculated with Image Lab Software (V.6.1; Bio-Rad). Plots were made on GraphPad Prism (V.10.4.1; GraphPad Software).

Isolation of I-Ad sequence

I-Ad RNA from K46µ B cells was isolated using SV Total RNA Isolation Kit (Promega; Z3101). RNA was reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) via Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche; 11483188001). Target genes were amplified via reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction with AccuPrime Taq Hi Fidelity DNA polymerase (Invitrogen; 12346-086). Sequences were cloned into pcDNA3.1+ as previously reported.5 DNA sequences were compared with known I-Ad sequences.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Mutagenesis of I-Ad sequence was done with Agilent QuickChange mutagenesis kit as previously reported.1 All sequences were confirmed by DNA sequencing.

HEK293T cell transfection

HEK293T cells were transfected with mutated I-Ad cDNAs in pcDNA3.1+ using Fu-GENE transfection reagent as previously reported.1,3 After 48 h cells were collected and stained with indicated antibodies then analyzed by flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry

Cells were labeled with indicated antibodies, washed, and then analyzed on a BD FACS Symphony A3 collecting 20,000 live cell events/sample. Data were analyzed with FlowJo (V.10.10). For mutagenesis experiments, cells were gated on total MHC class II expression and analyzed on a cell to cell basis as previously reported.2 For cross-competition experiments, cells were gated on lymphocytes by forward scatter and side scatter, then by CD19+ to isolate B cells.

Sequence comparisons

MHC class II protein sequences were taken from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Indicated α chains were aligned by ClustalW on MacVector (version 18.6). Known crystal structure of I-Ad complexed to ovalbumin (OVA) peptide was used for modeling (PBD ID: 1IAO). Residues of interest were highlighted and displayed on the NCBI iCn3D modeling website.

Calcium flux

K46µ B cells were labeled with 1 mg/mL Fluo-3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; F1242) and 1 mg/mL Fura Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific; F3021) in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution 0.1% bovine serum albumin 4 mM probenecid (Thermo Fisher Scientific; P36400), for 30 min at 37 °C as previously reported.8 Samples were added to culture tubes then rested for 60 min in the dark. mAbs were added to samples and incubated at 37 °C in a water bath 5 min prior to acquisition. Cells were acquired on a BD FACSymphony A3, and the calcium signal was traced for 5 min. A total of 15 s after the start of acquisition, streptavidin (Jackson ImmunoResearch; 016-000-113) was added. Samples were analyzed via FlowJo (V.10.10; BD Biosciences). Plots were made using GraphPad Prism (V.10.4.1).

NP-OVA

A total of 600 mg of OVA (Sigma-Aldrich; A5503) was dissolved in 12 mL borate buffer pH 8.0. A total of 48 mg of NP-e-Aminocaproyl-OSu was dissolved in 480 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide. NP-e-Aminocaproyl-Osu was added dropwise to OVA. The solution was incubated at room temperature for 1 h with constant stirring. Next it was dialyzed in borate-buffered saline 4 times. The final concentration was 3.68 mg/mL.

IL-2 ELISA

K46µ B cells and either 3DO-54.8 or DO-11.10 T cells were cocultured at a 1:2 B cell-to-T cell ratio. A total of 100 µg OVA323–339 (Sigma-Aldrich; A5503) or 10 µg NP-OVA (created in lab) were added to the cultures. Cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 15,000 g to remove debris, then added to a 96-well plate. IL-2 concentration was measured via ELISA MAX Deluxe Set Mouse IL-2 (BioLegend; 431004). Absorbance was read at 450 nm via BioTek Citation 7 imaging reader using GEN 5 microplate reader and imaging software (V.3.11). IL-2 concentration was interpolated and plots made using GraphPad Prism (V.10.4.1). IL-2 samples without blocking antibodies were consistently 10- to 1,000-fold greater than samples with blocking antibody.

In vivo ova model

Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice (strain #000651) and 6- to 12-wk-old female DO-11.10 TCR transgenic mice (strain #0003303) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Mice were housed in the Animal Resource Facility at Albany Medical College. Animal procedures were conducted according to protocols approved by the Albany Medical College Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Donor CD4+ T cells were isolated from splenocytes harvested from BALB/c and DO-11.10 mouse spleens by negative selection (Miltenyi Biotec; 130-104-453). BALB/c CD4+ T cells were labeled with CellTracker Green (Invitrogen; C2925) while DO-11.10 CD4+ T cells were labeled with CellTracker Red (Invitrogen; C34552). A total of 5 × 105 of each T cell type was injected into BALB/c recipients (day 1). A total of 100 µg anti-MHC class II mAbs (AMS-32.1 or 34-5-3S) were injected intraperitoneally on day 2. A total of 50 µg OVA323–339 dissolved in 200 µL sterile phosphate-buffered saline was injected retro-orbitally on day 3. On day 7, spleen and inguinal lymph nodes were harvested. Organs were processed and analyzed by flow cytometry. Flow cytometry plots were gated on lymphocytes, single cells, and CD4-positive cells to identify green- and red-labeled donor T cells of interest. Bar graphs were made using GraphPad Prism (V.10.4.1).

Hepatitis B mouse model

Seven-week-old male BALB/c mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (strain #000651). Mice were housed in the Animal Resource Facility at Albany Medical College. Animal procedures were conducted according to protocols approved by the Albany Medical College Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were transduced by retro-orbital injection of 4 × 1010 genome copies of adeno-associated virus (AAV) serotype 8 (SignaGen) harboring a 1.2-mer hepatitis B virus (HBV) genome (AAV-HBV) diluted in 200 µL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline. Blocking anti-MHC class II mAbs (M5/114 and AMS-32.1) and IgG2b isotype control (Bio X Cell; BE0086) were diluted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline and administered intraperitoneally twice weekly (100 µg/dose/mouse) beginning 2 d before AAV-HBV transduction, for a total of 5 doses. Serum hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and anti-HBsAg antibody were measured by ELISA according to the manufacturer’s protocol (International ImmunoDiagnostics). HBsAg protein used for standards was purchased from Fitzgerald Industries. On week 7 post-transduction, splenocytes were used for detection of HBV-specific IgM+ and IgG+ B cells using a dual ELISPOT assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Cellular Technology Limited; mIgGIgM-DCE-2M/5), as previously reported.10

Statistics

Unpaired Student’s t test, 1-way analysis of variance with multiple comparisons, and 2-way analysis of variance were performed on GraphPad Prism (V.10.4.1) and used to determine statistical significance.

Results

AMS-32.1 recognizes a subset of MHC class II molecules

The AMS-32.1 anti-I-Ad mAb was previously shown to elicit calcium signaling in B cells similar to 11-5.2 anti-I-Ak mAb,7 and 11-5.2 was subsequently found to selectively bind M1-paired I-Ak class II molecules.1,2 This suggests that AMS-32.1 might recognize M1-paired I-Ad class II molecules. We therefore sought to determine if AMS-32.1 selectively binds M1-paired class II.

The first step was to determine if AMS-32.1 recognizes a subset of I-Ad class II molecules. Sequential IP of lysate from I-Ad expressing K46µ B cells with anti-MHC class II mAbs was performed and the samples analyzed by Western blot for MHC class II β chain (Fig. 1B). Multiple mAbs were used in combination with AMS-32.1, however we focused on 34-5-3S because we found that it does not cross-compete for binding with AMS-32.1 (discussed subsequently). After 3 rounds of IP with 34-5-3S, all of the class II was removed from the cell lysate, leaving no class II to be bound by subsequently added AMS-32.1. This indicates 34-5-3S recognizes all class II molecules. After 3 rounds of IP with AMS-32.1, there is still detectable I-Ad class II in the supernatant that can be bound by 34-5-3S. This indicates AMS-32.1 recognizes a subset of I-Ad class II molecules.

Further supporting the idea that AMS-32.1 binds a subset of class II molecules, quantification of the first round of IP shows that the level of I-Ad class II in the initial 34-5-3S IP (red box) is greater than the level in the initial AMS-32.1 IP (blue box). Comparison of these initial band densities reveals that AMS-32.1 recognizes 18% of total class II recognized by 34-5-3S (Fig. 1B). This is similar to 11-5.2, which recognizes ∼20% of total I-Ak class II.2 Similar analysis with the AMS-32.1 versus M5/114 anti-MHC class II mAbs gave comparable results (Fig. S1).

These results were confirmed using lysate from BALB/c splenocytes which express I-Ad. In splenocytes, AMS-32.1 recognizes 17% of class II compared with 34-5-3S (Fig. 1C). Together, the results establish that AMS-32.1 recognizes a subset of class II, while 34-5-3S recognizes all I-Ad class II molecules (Fig. 1D).

AMS-32.1 recognizes M1-paired class II

In previous studies, the impact of TM domain GG4 motif mutations on the binding of anti-class II mAb was used to determine the conformer specificity of those reagents. In those studies, cells expressing different class II molecules were stained with a test mAb and a second conformer-insensitive mAb as a measure of overall class II expression.1–3 To apply this approach to the current analysis, we needed to determine if labeled AMS-32.1 and 34-5-3S mAbs can be used in combination to stain cells without significant cross-inhibition. K46µ B cells were stained with each mAb individually or in combination. Flow cytometry analysis of the samples shows that AMS-32.1 and 34-5-3S bind to cells at high levels in the presence or absence of each other (Fig. 2A). These results mean that the two mAbs can be used together to further define the subset of class II molecules recognized by AMS-32.1.

In mouse I-Ak and human HLA-DR, mutation of the small glycine residues of the α chain M1 GG4 motif to a larger residue such as valine results in motif inactivation and decreased binding of the conformer-specific mAbs 11-5.2 or Tü36 mAb.1–3 In order to determine the effect of TM domain GG4 motif mutations on AMS-32.1 binding, an established site-directed mutagenesis protocol was used.1 I-Ad cDNA was isolated from K46µ B cells and TM domain GG4 motifs mutated, as shown in Fig. 2B. Wild-type (WT) or mutated I-Ad was transfected into HEK293T cells, and after 48 h the cells were stained with AMS-32.1 and 34-5-3S (as a readout of total class II expression). Cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2C–E).

In the dot plots of AMS-32.1-FITC versus 34-5-3S-PE binding (Fig. 2C), cells expressing WT I-Ad class II (red dots) exhibited robust levels of binding of both mAbs. In contrast, cells expressing M1 G > V mutant I-Ad (blue dots) showed a relative decrease in AMS-32.1 binding, indicated by the left shift in AMS-32.1-FITC signal. Analysis of cells expressing M2 G > V mutant I-Ad (purple dots) shows that AMS-32.1 binding to the M2 G > V mutant is similar to that of WT class II (Fig. 2C).

In order to compare the effect of the TM domain GG4 mutations on AMS-32.1 binding across multiple experiments, the ratio of the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of AMS-32.1-FITC to 34-5-3S-PE staining was determined on a cell by cell basis (Fig. 2D, E). This ratio was compared across 4 independent experiments. Figure 2D is a representative histogram of the MFI ratios for 1 experiment. AMS-32.1 binding is clearly decreased (as indicated by the significantly lower FITC:PE ratio) with the M1 G > V mutation compared with WT class II (Fig. 2D). There is no difference in AMS-32.1 binding between WT class II and M2 G > V mutated class II. Quantification of these ratios for all 4 experiments is shown in Fig. 2E. AMS-32.1 binding to the M1 G > V mutant is significantly decreased compared with WT class II (Fig. 2E). This decrease is not seen with the M2 G > V mutation. These results are similar to those obtained with previously identified M1-paired conformer-specific mAbs.1–3 Overall, these results reveal that AMS-32.1 recognizes M1-paired I-Ad class II as AMS-32.1 binding is selectively decreased by the M1 G > V mutation, meaning that AMS-32.1 can be used as a tool to study the immunobiology of class II conformers in I-Ad.

AMS-32.1 binding parameters

As with the previously identified class II conformer-specific mAbs, the exact epitope recognized by AMS-32.1 is poorly defined. Better understanding of the epitope recognized by AMS-32.1 will help elucidate which part of the class II molecule is sensitive to differences in TM domain GG4 motif pairing and whether the mAb might be able to block TCR recognition of M1-paired class II molecules.

To further define the likely epitope of AMS-32.1, we took an approach similar to that used for the 11-5.2 and Tü36 conformer-specific anti-MHC class II mAbs and compared the amino acid sequences of AMS-32.1 reactive versus nonreactive I-A alleles.2,3 AMS-32.1 is reported to bind to I-Ad, I-Ag7 I-Af, I-Ai, and I-Av11. mAb staining of mouse splenocytes, which confirms that AMS-32.1 binds to I-Ad and I-Ag7 (Fig. 3A, B). I-Ad and I-Ag7 share the same α chain but possess different β chains.12 Therefore, AMS-32.1 mAb binding is α chain dependent. In contrast, 34-5-3S is reactive to I-Ad but not I-Ag,7 so the 34-5-3S epitope is likely on the class II β chain.13 This alternate chain specificity is consistent with the lack of cross-competition for binding between the two mAbs (Fig. 2A).

To narrow the list of amino acids involved in AMS-32.1 mAb binding, the amino acid sequences of AMS-32.1 reactive and nonreactive I-A class II α chain alleles were aligned and compared (Fig. 3C; Fig. S2). The amino acid sequences were analyzed for similarities across I-Ad, I-Ag,7 and I-Af (AMS-32.1 reactive) that are not shared with the nonreactive alleles (sequences for I-Ai and I-Av are not available in the NCBI database). Within the α chain amino acid sequences, 2 residues were found to fit this criteria, phenylalanine 53 and arginine 90 (Fig. 3C). The position of these two residues are highlighted in the sequence. We then mapped the position of these residues on the molecular structure of the ova peptide–I-Ad complex (Fig. 3C, D). Based on these analyses, we predict that the epitope recognized by AMS-32.1 is on the class II α chain in the membrane distal portion of the molecule near the region of the peptide binding groove occupied by the N-terminal end of the bound peptide. Therefore, it is likely that AMS-32.1 will block M1-paired class II recognition by the TCR.

Interestingly these results are similar to those from the analysis of the binding specificity of 11-5.2, the mAb specific for M1-paired I-Ak. The 11-5.2 epitope is also likely on the class II α chain near the same region of the peptide binding groove.2 This suggests that the region of the peptide binding groove occupied by the N-terminal end of the bound peptide is sensitive to different transmembrane domain pairing and is a possible site for functional differences between the two conformers. Also, the epitopes recognized by both these M1-paired–specific mAbs seem to involve the class II α chain, suggesting that the α chain contains 1 or more regions that exhibit conformational differences driven by changes in transmembrane domain pairing. Understanding these epitopes may lead to further knowledge on the differences in function of the class II conformers.

M2-paired class II blocks B cell calcium signaling driven by M1-paired class II

Selective engagement of M1-paired I-Ak class II elicits calcium class II driven signals within murine B cells.7,8 This signal is inhibited by coligation of M1 and M2-paired I-Ak class II, indicating that M2-paired class II can inhibit/block M1-paired class II driven calcium signaling.8 Inhibition of M1 signaling by M2 is proposed to be caused by association of M2-paired class II with ITIM-bearing molecules.8 These conclusions were drawn from calcium flux experiments using 11-5.2-btn and 10-3.6 in the I-Ak system. Having defined the conformer specificity of the AMS-32.1 and 34-5-3S anti-I-Ad mAbs, we decided to determine if this inhibitory effect of M2-paired class II on M1-paired class II signaling extends to I-Ad.

To address this question, we determined how the addition of pan-reactive 34-5-3S anti-I-Ad mAb (which will coligate M1 and M2-paired I-Ad class II molecules) affects M1-paired I-Ad signaling driven by AMS-32.1 binding. AMS-32.1-btn crosslinked with streptavidin was used to mimic TCR interaction with M1-paired class II. 34-5-3S was used in its unbiotinylated form to crosslink M1 and M2-paired class II without impacting streptavidin crosslinking of AMS-32.1-btn. Calcium flux was measured by flow cytometry as previously reported (Fig. 4A).8 The results show that AMS-32.1-btn/streptavidin elicits a strong calcium signal in K46µ B cells and that this signal is inhibited by 34-5-3S dose dependently (Fig. 4B). Because AMS-32.1 and 34-5-3S do not compete for binding (Fig. 2A), the decrease in AMS-32.1–driven signaling is not due to 34-5-3S blockade of AMS-32.1-btn binding (Fig. 3A, B). This inhibitory effect of 34-5-3S binding is not a global shutdown of B cell calcium signaling, as AMS-32.1 and 34-5-3S mAbs do not impact the ability of an anti-BCR mAb to elicit a BCR-driven calcium signal (Fig. 4C). These results with I-Ad class II demonstrate that M2-paired class II has an inhibitory effect on M1-paired class II signaling (Fig. 4D) and establish that MHC class II conformers are involved in signaling regulation, which impacts downstream immune cell function.

M1-paired class II uniquely drives T cell activation in vitro

While a considerable amount is known about how MHC class II conformers impact B cell function, less is known about how class II conformers impact T cell function. As mentioned previously, development of the adaptive immune response requires interactions between B cells and T cells in which the TCR interacts with peptide-MHC class II complexes on B cells. Based on what we surmised of the AMS-32.1 epitope (Fig. 3C), we predict that AMS-32.1 will have the ability to block TCR interaction with M1-paired class II, leaving only M2-paired class II to interact with the TCR. Thus, we have a tool that we can use to analyze the impact of M1-paired versus M2-paired class II on B cell–T cell interactions and T cell activation (cytokine production).

As discussed previously, besides the use of conformer specific mAbs, previous investigations have used differential peptide loading to analyze MHC class II conformer function. In the I-Ak system, it was demonstrated that when peptide is generated subsequent to BCR-mediated antigen uptake, it preferentially loads onto M1-paired class II (Fig. 5A).6 In contrast, if peptide is generated subsequent to fluid phase antigen uptake, peptide loads onto both M1- and M2-paired class II (Fig. 5A).4,6 This allows for the comparison of cellular interactions mediated by peptide loaded onto the two distinct conformers. While the tools needed to formally extend these findings on selective conformer peptide loading to mouse I-Ad (mAbs uniquely reactive to specific peptide-I-Ad complexes) do not exist, it is reasonable to expect that the two systems are similar.

In order to determine the roles of MHC class II conformers in B cell antigen presentation and T cell activation, IL-2 cytokine production was used as a readout of T cell activation (Fig. 5B). We used K46µ B cells (expressing an NP-specific IgM BCR and I-Ad class II molecules) and 2 T cell hybridomas specific for OVA (OVA323–339) peptide-I-Ad complexes. First, B cells were cultured with NP-ova (which is processed via the BCR and will be selectively loaded onto M1-paired class II) and either of the T cell hybridomas, with or without AMS-32.1 or 34-5-3S. Culture supernatant was analyzed for IL-2 by ELISA. In the absence of mAb, both T cell hybridomas produce robust levels of IL-2, indicating recognition of ova-I-Ad complexes on the surface of the B cell (Fig. 5C, D). As expected, addition of 34-5-3S (blocking all MHC class II) completely inhibits T cell activation (Fig. 5C, D). Moreover, consistent with the idea that BCR-mediated antigen processing results in selective peptide loading onto M1-paired class II, addition of AMS-32.1 (which only binds M1-paired class II) also completely prevents T cell activation (Fig. 5C, D). These results reveal that T cells are effectively stimulated by M1-paired ova peptide-I-Ad complexes and that both 34-5-3S and AMS-32.1 can block TCR recognition of these complexes.

We next determined the effect of both anti-I-Ad class II blocking mAbs when peptide was loaded onto both M1- and M2-paired class II via fluid phase uptake. Again, as expected, 34-5-3S inhibits T cell activation, as it would prevent both MHC class II conformers from interacting with the TCR (Fig. 5E, F). Interestingly, AMS-32.1 also inhibits T cell activation to the same degree as 34-5-3S (Fig. 5E, F). This result is somewhat unexpected, as AMS-32.1 only blocks M1-paired class II, leaving any M2-paired ova peptide-I-Ad complexes available to interact with the TCR. These results demonstrate M1-paired MHC class II (which represents 10% to 20% of surface class II molecules) is unique in its ability to activate T cells, whereas M2-paired class II (which represents 80%–90% of surface class II) appears to be unable to drive a similar level of T cell activation.

M1-paired class II uniquely drives T cell function in vivo

To define the roles of class II conformers in T cell activation in vivo, we first used an adoptive transfer approach. Naïve CD4+ T cells from DO-11.10 mice (expressing a TCR specific for OVA323–339 peptide-I-Ad complexes) were labeled with CellTracker Red, and naïve CD4+ T cells from BALB/c mice were labeled with CellTracker Green. Cells were co-injected into BALB/c recipient mice, and mice were treated 1 d post-transfer with blocking antibody (either AMS-32.1 or 34-5-3S). One day later, mice were immunized with OVA protein. Five days postimmunization, the level of labeled CD4+ T cells in the spleen and inguinal lymph nodes was determined (Fig. 6).

In the unimmunized animals, the ratio of transferred cells in the spleen and lymph nodes was the same as the input population, indicating equal seeding by the two populations in the absence of antigen activation. In the immunized (but not antibody treated) mice, there is a profound shift in the ratio of CD4+ T cells in favor of the antigen-reactive DO-11.10 T cells. Pretreatment with AMS-32.1 (and to a lesser extent with 34-5-3S) blocks this shift, indicating a critical role for M1-paired class II in the accumulation of antigen reactive CD4+ T cells to these secondary lymphoid organs.

To further study the function of MHC class II conformers in vivo, we chose an infection model in which antigen clearance is reliant on B cells interacting with CD4+ T cells to generate a robust humoral immune response. In the AAV-HBV mouse model, the HBV genome is packaged in AAV8, which allows for liver-tropic delivery of HBV into mice. This results in HBV replication in hepatocytes and secretion of HBsAg that can be measured in the blood.14 In H-2d BALB/c mice, HBsAg clearance depends on antibody-mediated control via a T cell–dependent humoral immune response.10,15 In this model, early transient inhibition of B cells or CD4+ T cells leads to decreased HBsAg-specific IgG and persistently high serum HBsAg levels.10

Previously in this model, we have shown that blocking I-Ad MHC class II with the M5/114 mAb (I-Ad reactive)16 prevents the mice from clearing viral antigen.10 The M5/114 mAb recognizes all I-Ad class II molecules (Fig. S1). To extend the in vivo analysis of AMS-32.1 to a model pathogen, we determined the effect of AMS-32.1 as a blocking mAb on the BALB/c immune response to HBsAg, repeating M5/114 mAb treatment in parallel as a control. BALB/c mice were transduced with AAV-HBV and treated with blocking anti-MHC class II mAbs (M5/114 or AMS-32.1) as indicated in the timeline (Fig. 7A). Serum was collected biweekly to measure blood HBsAg levels by ELISA, and spleens were harvested at the endpoint of the experiment to assess HBsAg-specific IgG and IgM secretion by ELISPOT. The humoral immune response was also evaluated by measuring anti-HBsAg antibody in the final serum sample by ELISA.

Treating mice with either AMS-32.1 or M5/114 equally inhibits the ability of the mice to clear HBsAg, which remained elevated in the serum of the treated animals for the duration of the experiment (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, measurement of total serum anti-HBsAg antibody at week 7 shows that mice treated with either AMS-32.1 or M5/114 both fail to mount detectable antibody levels in the blood compared with the isotype control (Fig. 7C). Hence, both anti-class II mAbs block antigen clearance and generation of an effective humoral immune response.

Production of antigen-specific IgM is a relatively T cell–independent event, whereas production of class-switched antigen-specific IgG requires CD4+ T cell help.17 To determine if the blocking mAbs have different effects on the anti-HBsAg IgM or IgG response, the level of anti-HBsAg IgM and IgG secreting cells in the spleen at week 7 was determined. Treatment with either M5/114 or AMS-32.1 inhibits HBsAg-specific IgG production equally compared with the isotype control, whereas IgM production is unchanged (Fig. 7D). These results indicate that blocking M1-paired I-Ad class II in an HBV model prevents T cell–dependent antibody production, leading to prolonged antigenemia. Moreover, cellular interactions mediated by unblocked M2-paired I-Ad class II cannot overcome the loss of activity of M1-paired class II to drive antigen clearance. Overall, these results reveal that M1-paired class II is essential for B cell–T cell help and the formation of class-switched antibody responses.

Discussion

Herein, we report that the AMS-32.1 mAb selectively recognizes the 20% of M1-paired I-Ad class II molecules expressed by B cells, whereas the 34-5-3S and M5/114 mAbs bind all expressed I-Ad class II molecules. This identifies and characterizes a third pair of MHC class II conformer-discriminating mAbs that can be used in the study of the immunobiology of these molecules (Table 1). In addition, we demonstrate that selective blockade of M1-paired I-Ad class II with AMS-32.1 completely blocks CD4 T cell activation both in vitro and in vivo, indicating a central role for M1-paired class II molecules in T cell activation.

In this study, we show that addition of AMS-32.1 blocks TCR recognition of M1-paired I-Ad class II molecules. This mAb blocks T cell activation both in vitro and in vivo, demonstrating that M1-paired class II is essential for CD4+ T cell activation. In the I-Ak system, our lab and two others have shown that 11-5.2 blockade of TCR recognition of M1-paired peptide–I-Ak complexes impedes in vitro B cell–T cell interactions.5,18,19 Results from both I-Ad and I-Ak indicate that M1-paired class II is critical for CD4+ T cell activation.

There is also a small but growing number of studies of MHC class II conformers in vivo. We demonstrate in this report that AMS-32.1 effects antigen driven CD4+ T cell accumulation in the spleen and inguinal lymph node. It is possible the cells are being retained in these organs or selectively recruited. We also report that AMS-32.1 prevents HBsAg clearance and generation of an HBsAg-specific IgG antibody response in mice. Notably, the T cell–dependent class switched IgG response was impaired, but the non–class switched IgM response remained unchanged. In the I-Ak system, one study investigated the ability of anti-I-Ak mAbs to block the generation of T cell–dependent humoral immune responses to sheep red blood cells. The authors found that both 10-3.6 (which binds all I-Ak class II) and 11-5.2 (which selectively binds M1-paired I-Ak) completely impede T cell help for the B cells equally.20 Thus, while the number of reports is limited, in two different in vivo model systems, selective blockade of M1-paired class II molecules results in prevention of the generation of an effective humoral immune response, again highlighting a key role for M1-paired MHC class II molecules.

There are multiple potential reasons why M1-paired class II is unique in its ability to drive B cell and T cell activation. These possibilities include differential potency of lipid raft-restricted class II versus non–raft class II, the effect of MHC class II signaling on B cell “potency” as an antigen-presenting cell, and differential conformer binding of T cell costimulatory molecules such as CD4 and LAG-3. Each of these possibilities is discussed subsequently.

M1-paired class II has been found to selectively associate in lipid rafts, while M2-paired class II resides outside the raft portion of the membrane.5,6 The greater potency of lipid raft–resident MHC class II molecules for T cell activation is well established in the literature.21–23 Therefore, simple clustering of M1-paired class II within rafts could be the reason for their greater ability to drive T cell activation. However, the story is likely more complex.

As detailed in this report, M1-paired class II drives calcium signaling in resting B cells, while M2-paired class II blocks this response. This signaling dynamic is likely driven by CD79 association with M1-paired class II and STING association with M2-paired class II.8,24,25 It is possible that class II signaling in B cells also impacts T cell activation when the cells interact. When B cells interact with T cells, B cell activation status has been shown to be critical for immunological synapse formation.26–28 Previous studies show that during B cell–T cell interaction, B cells rearrange their intracellular compartments, bringing their microtubule organizing center, Golgi apparatus, late endocytic compartments, and mitochondria to the area underlying the T cell interface.26,29 This may all serve to induce strong immunological synapse formation and drive T cell activation. The precise signals driving this rearrangement are unclear, but it is likely signaling via M1-paired class II plays a role, while M2-paired class II may temper this effect. Furthermore, it is known when the BCR is crosslinked, B cells release exosomes which can stimulate primed CD4+ T cells.30,31 Because calcium signaling via M1-paired class II is likely via associated CD79 (i.e. the BCR signaling subunit), M1-paired class II signaling may also induce exosome secretion, affecting T cell activation.30 Overall, M1-paired calcium signaling within the B cell may enhance immunological synapse formation or exosome secretion leading to enhanced CD4+ T cell activation.

It is also possible that T cell costimulatory molecules such as CD4 and LAG-3 interact with MHC class II conformers differently. CD4 is a costimulatory molecule that binds directly to MHC class II in the immunological synapse, an interaction critical for activation of T cell signaling, proliferation, and cytokine production.32,33 In contrast, LAG-3 is evolutionarily and structurally similar to CD4 but inhibits T cell activation.34–38 The MHC class II binding sites for CD4 and LAG-3 are located directly adjacent to the class II TM domain.36 Because MHC class II conformers differ in their TM domain pairing, it is possible that conformer structure impacts CD4 and LAG-3 binding sites, affecting the quality of B cell–T cell conjugation and downstream cytokine production. To further support this hypothesis, our lab has shown that an amino acid residue involved in the binding of the Tü36 conformer-specific anti-HLA-DR mAb is near the CD4 binding site, suggesting that the CD4 binding site may change its structure based on TM domain interactions.3

Interestingly, CD4 and LAG-3 have overlapping binding sites, with the LAG-3 binding site encapsulating the whole CD4 binding site.32,36 The literature is unclear as to whether CD4 and LAG-3 can bind simultaneously to class II.35,37,39 We propose that CD4 and LAG-3 do not simultaneously bind the same class II molecule, but rather bind different MHC class II conformers. Our hypothesis is supported by our modeling of human class II to CD4 and LAG-3 (Fig. S3), which demonstrates that CD4 and LAG-3 cannot bind class II at the same time. Because M1-paired class II activates T cells and CD4 binding is critical for TCR signaling, the most likely scenario is that CD4 preferentially interacts with M1-paired class II to drive T cell activation. In contrast, LAG-3 likely associates with M2-paired class II to inhibit T cell activation. If CD4 preferentially binds to M1-paired class II and LAG-3 preferentially binds to M2-paired class II, this could explain why some studies have detected simultaneous CD4 and LAG-3 binding to a mixed preparation of M1- and M2-paired class II molecules. Overall CD4 and LAG-3 likely do not bind concurrently to the same class II molecule. Instead, the two costimulatory molecules might interact with M1- or M2-paired class II, respectively, leading to differences in the ability of each conformer to activate CD4+ T cells.

While the role of M1-paired class II in lymphocyte activation is supported by considerable evidence (see previous), the role of M2-paired class II in lymphocyte responsiveness is less clear despite the fact it represents a significantly larger fraction of the class II on the B cell surface. Studies in nonobese diabetic (NOD) mice suggest that M2-paired class II may have a role in immune tolerance. NOD mice are an autoimmune model created to study type 1 diabetes by the introduction of an insulitis susceptibility gene.40 In a study in which NOD mice had an introduced I-Ag7 class II β chain mutation at Asp 57 (known to limit disease progression), AMS-32.1 binding (M1-paired class II) was decreased compared with 10-3.6 binding (a measure of total class II).41 This finding suggests that peptide loading onto M1-paired class II may be detrimental in autoimmune conditions because it results in overactivation of the immune system.42,43 In contrast, peptide binding to M2-paired class II may regulate the immune system, leading to decreased autoreactivity. The use of AMS-32.1 as a blocking mAb to selectively block M1-paired class II conformers could be a possible treatment for inducing immune tolerance in this model. Within the larger context of overall immunoreactivity, we offer the idea that M1-paired MHC class II represents the smaller amount of class II on the cell surface to avoid excessive immune activation, leaving the excess of M2-paired MHC class II in charge of regulating activation to prevent immune overactivation.

The results presented in this report establish that the AMS-32.1 mAb selectively binds M1-paired I-Ad MHC class II molecules, and along with the pan-reactive 34-5-3S mAb, represents a new set of tools to study the immunobiology of MHC class II conformers (Table 1). We also used the AMS-32.1 mAb to demonstrate the unique role of M1-paired I-Ad class II in the activation of CD4+ T cells, both in vitro and in vivo, further supporting the idea that the two class II conformers have distinct immunological functions.
