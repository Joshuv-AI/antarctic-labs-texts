Investigation of antioxidant, anti collagenase, anti-elastase, and anti-hyaluronidase activities of Solanum trilobatum L. for pharmaceutical development
Authors: Chanai Noysang, Monsicha Khuanekkaphan, Teerarat Pummarin, Wiphupat Buranasukhon, Em-on Chaiprateep
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The health and beauty sectors are significant drivers for the continuous growth of related product markets. Solanum trilobatum L. (S. trilobatum), locally known as Ma Waeng Krue, is a popular medicinal plant utilized in primary health care in Thailand. Renowned for their remarkable antimicrobial, antioxidant, antibacterial, and antifungal characteristics, the leaves and fruits of this plant yield abundant quantities of saponins, anthraquinones, terpenoids, and tannins. This study investigated the antioxidant activity, total phenolic content (TPC), and inhibitory effects of unripe and ripe S. trilobatum fruit extracts against collagenase, elastase, and hyaluronidase, specifically methanol and ethyl acetate extracts. The ethyl acetate extracts from unripe fruits contained the highest TPC and showed the greatest antioxidant activity as demonstrated by the 2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) and ferric reducing antioxidant power methods. In contrast, methanol extracts from ripe fruits had the highest yield and demonstrated the greatest antioxidant activity according to the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl assay. Notably, methanol extracts from unripe fruits exhibited the most potent inhibition of collagenase, elastase, and hyaluronidase compared with ethyl acetate extracts.

INTRODUCTION

The global anti-aging sector is forecasted to reach USD 140.94 billion by 2034, growing at 6.8% annually.[1] Skin aging is a complex process affected by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors. Oxidative stress is one of the major contributors. Ultraviolet radiation and environmental pollutants generate excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS). These molecules can exceed the skin’s antioxidant defenses. As a result, they cause cumulative damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Consequently, this process results in the formation of wrinkles and a reduction in skin elasticity.[2] The skin’s extracellular matrix (ECM) is a complex network comprising fibroblasts and crucial structural proteins, including collagen and elastin. Collagen, the primary protein found in the dermis, imparts tensile strength to the skin, whereas elastin, found in connective tissue, imparts flexibility to the skin. The elasticity, structural integrity, and youthful appearance of the skin are significantly dependent on proteins. Photoaging-induced ROS activate collagenase and elastase, causing collagen and elastin degradation. As a result, this process causes premature skin aging, which is visible as wrinkles, freckles, sallowness, etc. Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a glucose-based polymer in the dermal and epidermal layers, promoting skin rejuvenation, retaining moisture, and reducing wrinkles. However, aging and hyaluronidase production can cause skin aging and loss of strength and moisture.[3] Utilizing plant compounds to inhibit free radicals and enzymes such as collagenase, elastase, and hyaluronidase offers a promising approach to preventing skin aging.[4] S. trilobatum, a climbing shrub belonging to the Solanaceae family, is known for its leaves being rich in iron, fiber, calcium, carbohydrates, minerals, lipids, and phosphorus. It is recommended for the treatment of asthma, high blood sugar, and various skin conditions. Additionally, it has potential for anti-inflammatory, antifungal, and antibacterial properties.[56] The phytochemical composition of S. trilobatum was found to include alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides, phenols, phytosterols, tannins, saponins, solanine, sobatum, and solasodine, among various others.[78] Examination of the literature reveals a lack of comparative studies exploring the antioxidant activity, total phenolic content (TPC), elastase inhibition, collagenase inhibition, and hyaluronidase inhibition between unripe and ripe of S. trilobatum fruit extracts. To address this gap, we conducted a study to assess the potential of S. trilobatum fruit extract as an antioxidant and anti-aging agent.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials and chemicals

2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), butylated hydroxytoluene, Clostridium histolyticum collagenase type I, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), Folin and Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent, ferrous sulfate, L-ascorbic acid, N-succinyl-Ala-Ala-Ala-p-nitroaniline (AAAPVN), N-[3-(2-furyl)acryloyl]-Leu-Gly-Pro-Ala (FALGPA), porcine pancreas elastase type I, sodium hyaluronic, tannic acid, Trizma® base, 2,4,6-Tris(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine, P-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde, and α-tocopherol were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Analytical grade chemicals and solvents were employed in all the experiments.

Extraction

The unripe (white) and ripe (red) fruits of S. trilobatum were collected from a Thai herbal garden in Nakhon Pathom Province, Thailand. After authentication, a voucher specimen (BK085694) was placed at the Bangkok Herbarium, Thailand. The fruits were washed, air-dried, oven-dried at 50°C, and ground into powder. Extraction was carried out by maceration with methanol or ethyl acetate (1:10, w/v) with ultrasound assistance (40 kHz, 40°C, 30 min, repeated twice). After a 7-day standing period, the mixtures were filtered, and the resulting filtrates were concentrated under reduced pressure. The obtained crude extracts were then stored at −20°C. Total phenolic content

The TPC was determined by the Folin–Ciocalteu method,[9] with some modifications. An extract aliquot of 100 μL was first diluted to 3 mL. This solution was then treated with Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and Na2CO3. After incubation for 90 min, the absorbance of the solution was measured at 725 nm.

Antioxidant activity assays

2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl assay

The DPPH method[10] was employed to quantify the antioxidant activity. Briefly, a 100 μL extract aliquot was mixed with an equal volume of DPPH, followed by 30-min incubation in the dark. The results were reported as percentage inhibition and IC50 values after absorbance was measured at 517 nm.

2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) assay

The ABTS method[11] was utilized to assess the antioxidant activity. Briefly, a 20 μL extract aliquot was mixed with 2 mL of the ABTS working solution and incubated for 6 min. The results were reported as percentage inhibition and IC50 values after absorbance was measured at 734 nm.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power assay

The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) method[12] was used to evaluate the antioxidant activity. Briefly, following the addition of 20 μL of the 1 mg/mL extract to the FRAP reagent, the mixture was allowed to react for 4 min. The absorbance was then recorded at 595 nm. The results were expressed as μmol Fe²⁺ equivalents per g extract using a ferrous sulfate calibration curve.

Anti-aging enzyme inhibition assays

Anti-collagenase activity

Inhibition of collagenase (EC 3.4.24.3) was evaluated using FALGPA as the substrate.[11] The reaction mixture contained collagenase at a final concentration of 0.067 U/mL and FALGPA at 0.83 mM. Absorbance was monitored at 340 nm, and EGCG was used as the positive control.

Anti-elastase activity

Inhibition of elastase (EC 3.4.21.36) was evaluated using a modified enzymatic assay with AAAPVN substrate.[11] The final concentrations were 0.025 U/mL elastase and 0.44 mM AAAPVN. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm, and EGCG was used as the positive control.

Anti-hyaluronidase activity

Inhibition of hyaluronidase was evaluated using a modified enzymatic assay.[3] The reaction mixture contained sodium hyaluronic acid at a final concentration of 0.043 mg/mL and 0.89 mM CaCl2. Absorbance was measured at 585 nm, and tannic acid was used as the positive control.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with group differences analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test (GraphPad Prism 5.01, San Diego, CA, USA).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Extraction

Extraction yields from unripe and ripe of S. trilobatum fruits obtained using methanol and ethyl acetate varied significantly [Table 1]. The highest percentage yield was observed in the methanol extract from ripe fruits (21.68 ± 0.47%). This was followed by methanol extract from unripe fruits (19.03 ± 0.52%), ethyl acetate extract from unripe fruits (3.44 ± 0.11%), and ethyl acetate extract from ripe fruits (1.64 ± 0.08%). These findings suggest that methanol is particularly effective at extracting compounds from ripe, likely due to its broad solubility for both polar and nonpolar compounds.[13] The higher yield observed in ripe fruits may be attributed to the increased accumulation and concentration of bioactive compounds, such as various secondary metabolites, antioxidants, and phytochemicals, as the fruits mature.[14]

Total phenolic content

The TPC of ethyl acetate and methanol extracts from unripe and ripe S. trilobatum fruits varied considerably. The highest content was found in ethyl acetate extract from unripe fruits (12.74 ± 1.13 mg GAE/g crude extract), followed by ethyl acetate from ripe fruits, methanol from ripe fruits, and methanol from unripe fruits [Table 1]. These results suggest that the phenolic compound content in the extracts is influenced by the solvent type, fruit ripeness, and the specific extraction method.[15] Specifically, ethyl acetate extraction demonstrated higher efficiency in isolating phenolic compounds from unripe of S. trilobatum fruits compared to methanol extraction. Although ethyl acetate extracts contained higher levels of total phenolics, this parameter alone cannot fully explain the observed biological activities. Differences in the qualitative composition of phenolics, the presence of other polar metabolites, and the influence of fruit maturity on bioactive compounds may also play important roles.[14]

Antioxidant activity assays

2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl assay

The DPPH radical assay is widely used to assess the free radical scavenging and hydrogen-donating abilities of compounds, offering a rapid, simple, and cost-effective method for evaluating antioxidant potential. This technique involves the reduction of the stable, dark purple DPPH free radical, resulting in a color change to colorless or light yellow (DPPH-H). Numerous plant extracts have demonstrated in vitro capacity to neutralize DPPH radicals, indicating potential antioxidant activity.[16] As shown in Table 2, the DPPH assay revealed that the methanol extract from unripe of S. trilobatum fruits exhibited the lowest IC50 value (0.37 ± 0.01), demonstrating statistically significant activity (P < 0.05). In comparison, L-ascorbic acid showed a much lower IC50 value (0.02 ± 0.00). This finding indicates that the methanol extract from unripe fruits effectively inhibits DPPH radicals at a lower concentration, highlighting its strong capability in neutralizing oxidative stress.[16]

2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) assay

The ABTS assay measures the ability of antioxidants to neutralize the blue-green ABTS radical cations, which are formed through the interaction of ABTS and potassium persulfate. This highly sensitive assay is widely applicable to both hydrophilic and lipophilic antioxidants, facilitating their spectrophotometric quantification.[17] Results from the ABTS assay [Table 2] showed that the ethyl acetate extract from unripe of S. trilobatum fruits exhibited the lowest IC50 value (17.22 ± 0.19), indicating statistically significant activity (P < 0.05). In comparison, L-ascorbic acid demonstrated a significantly lower IC50 value of (0.22 ± 0.00). These findings highlight the profound antioxidative potential present in S. trilobatum fruit extracts, suggesting their ability to effectively counteract oxidative stress and neutralize harmful free radicals. The remarkable performance of the ethyl acetate extract in the ABTS assay underscores its significance as a potent source of natural antioxidants.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power assay

The FRAP method was utilized to determine the antioxidant property as a FRAP value (μmole of Fe (II)/g sample) for unripe and ripe of S. trilobatum fruit extracts [Table 2]. The ethyl acetate extract from unripe fruits demonstrated the highest FRAP value among the extracts (312.40 ± 4.16). However, this value was considerably lower than those of the standard comparative compounds: L-ascorbic acid (13,683.14 ± 33.85) and α-tocopherol (3,845.63 ± 73.78). Comparing the extracts, unripe of S. trilobatum fruits consistently yielded higher FRAP values than their ripe counterparts. One plausible explanation is that diverse stages of fruit maturity could potentially impact the constitution of bioactive compounds responsible for antioxidant activity.[18] Furthermore, considering the results from the TPC measurements, it is conceivable that unripe of S. trilobatum fruit extracts harbor increased quantities of specific antioxidants, possibly encompassing polyphenols, flavonoids, or other categories of phytochemicals.

Anti-aging enzyme inhibition assays

Anti-collagenase activity

Collagen, constituting over 30% of the body’s total protein content and primarily as Type I collagen, holds a pivotal place within the ECM, particularly in structural tissues.[19] Collagenases, specialized enzymes that specifically target and degrade collagen, assume a significant role in tissue remodeling. The repercussions of any disruption in these processes are substantial, spanning consequences from skin aging to conditions such as fibrosis and cancer.[1920] Hence, the principle of anti-collagenase activity centers on the potential of substances to hinder collagenase enzyme function. In the enzyme inhibition assay, extracts from both unripe and ripe of S. trilobatum fruits (1 mg/mL) were compared with the standard compound EGCG (0.1 mg/mL), as shown in Figure 1. It was observed that methanol extracts from both ripe and unripe fruits displayed higher collagenase inhibition ability compared to ethyl acetate extracts from unripe and ripe fruits. Nevertheless, the collagenase inhibition capacity of both ripe and unripe methanol fruit extracts remained lower than that of the standard compound EGCG.

Anti-elastase activity

Elastin, a crucial protein constituting the ECM, is essential for maintaining the flexibility and structural integrity of various tissues.[3] Its degradation, primarily mediated by elastase, a key enzyme within the protease family, leads to a decline in tissue elasticity, manifesting as visible signs of aging, including sagging skin and wrinkles, as well as impaired tissue functionality.[21] Consequently, inhibiting elastase activity is a vital strategy for protecting elastic fibers from degradation and mitigating the formation of wrinkles. In this enzyme inhibition assay, the anti-elastase activities of unripe and ripe of S. trilobatum fruit extracts (1 mg/mL) were evaluated against the standard compound EGCG (0.1 mg/mL), as detailed in Figure 2. Results indicated that methanol extracts from both ripe and unripe fruits exhibited superior elastase inhibition compared to their ethyl acetate counterparts. Despite this, the inhibitory capacity of both ripe and unripe methanol fruit extracts remained lower than that of the EGCG standard.

Anti-hyaluronidase activity

HA is a major component of the ECM, playing a crucial role in providing tissue hydration, lubrication, and overall skin health.[22] Hyaluronidase degrades HA molecules, contributing to reduced skin elasticity, dryness, and accelerated aging.[323] Therefore, inhibiting hyaluronidase can help the skin retain moisture and delay the formation of wrinkles. In the hyaluronidase inhibition assay, extracts from both unripe and ripe of S. trilobatum fruits (1 mg/mL) were compared with the standard compound tannic acid (0.1 mg/mL), as shown in Figure 3. It was observed that methanol extracts from unripe fruits displayed the highest hyaluronidase inhibition among the extracts. However, the hyaluronidase inhibition capacity of unripe methanol fruit extracts remained lower than that of the standard compound tannic acid.

In this study, methanol extracts from unripe fruits showed stronger inhibition of collagenase, elastase, and hyaluronidase than ethyl acetate extracts. As a polar solvent, methanol can extract phenolic and flavonoid compounds more efficiently, and these constituents are likely to contribute to the observed anti-aging activity. The unripe methanol extract also demonstrated superior inhibition of elastase and hyaluronidase, suggesting that fruit maturity influences the levels of bioactive compounds. Younger fruits may contain higher concentrations of metabolites that support skin protection.[918] In addition, possible synergistic interactions among multiple phytochemicals may enhance the overall inhibitory effects.[4] Nonpolar compounds extracted by ethyl acetate could also contribute in different ways, but their roles may be less pronounced compared with the polar metabolites enriched in methanol extracts.[1418] In this study, enzyme inhibition assays were performed using a single concentration (1 mg/mL). This was chosen to make a direct comparison between solvents and fruit maturity. Future studies should include dose–response assays to confirm potency and determine IC50 values.

CONCLUSIONS

The study into the antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory characteristics of methanol and ethyl acetate extracts from S. trilobatum fruits has revealed that ethyl acetate extracts exhibit the highest antioxidative potential, as demonstrated through the ABTS and FRAP assays. In contrast, methanol extracts show strong DPPH inhibitory activity. Furthermore, methanol extracts from ripe fruits exhibited strong inhibition of collagenase and elastase, while methanol extracts from unripe fruits showed superior inhibition of hyaluronidase. In summary, methanol extracts exhibited stronger enzyme inhibition than ethyl acetate extracts, supporting their potential for pharmaceutical and cosmeceutical applications.
