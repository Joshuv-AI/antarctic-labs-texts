Endovascular treatment of acute upper extremity deep vein thrombosis in Paget-Schroetter syndrome: A longitudinal analysis of three endovascular techniques
Authors: Naomi Elbaranes, Anja Ulrich, Simon Wolf, Leonie Rimann, Benedetta Madaro, Nils Kucher, Stefano Barco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOptimal procedural strategies for the management of acute upper extremity deep vein thrombosis (UEDVT) due to Paget-Schroetter syndrome are poorly studied due to its rarity. The aim of this study was to compare short-term patency, complication rates, and course after endovascular therapy of acute UEDVT due to Paget-Schroetter syndrome.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective longitudinal study, data of consecutive patients with acute Paget-Schroetter syndrome treated with interventional thrombectomy at the University Hospital Zurich with any available device: computer assisted vacuum thrombectomy (CAVT), AngioJet/Zelante catheter, or catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) was collected. We studied the need for reintervention, primary and secondary patency, and course of symptoms at 2 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months.ResultsWe included 41 patients with acute UEDVT treated between 2017 and 2025. The median age was 34 years; 61% were men, and 41% were athletes. After a median symptom duration of 4 days, they received thrombectomy with AngioJet/Zelante (61%), CAVT (17%), and CDT (22%). Sixteen patients (39%) required reintervention, mostly within 48 hours (62.5%). Primary patency was 60% (24/40) at 2 weeks, 52.6% (20/38) at 3 months, and 54.1% (20/37) at 6 months (P = .08 for between-group difference). Secondary patency was 72%, 76%, and 86%, respectively. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed higher freedom from reintervention after CAVT (P = .04). Symptom relief occurred mainly within the first 2 weeks: swelling (98% to 17%), pain (60% to 14%). Cyanosis resolved by 6 months, whereas paresthesia slightly increased (15% to 23%). Only one major and one minor bleeding event occurred.ConclusionsCatheter-directed therapies appeared to be safe and effective for UEDVT due to Paget-Schroetter syndrome. In this exploratory study, bearing the small sample size, the data suggest that single-session thrombectomy (especially CAVT) may be associated with fewer reinterventions and higher primary patency.Keywords: Catheter-directed thrombolysis, Clinical outcomes, Computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy, Paget-Schroetter syndrome, Patency, Upper extremity deep vein thrombosis

Take Home Message: Catheter-based therapies are safe and effective for acute UEDVT caused by Paget-Schroetter syndrome. Single-session thrombectomy strategies (especially CAVT) may be associated with lower reintervention rates and higher patency than standard CDT, though results remain exploratory.

Paget-Schroetter syndrome is a rare form of upper extremity deep vein thrombosis (UEDVT) caused by repetitive effort and mechanical compression of the subclavian vein, usually in the thoracic outlet between clavicle and first rib. UEDVT typically occurs among young individuals and is characterized by an annual incidence of 1 to 11 cases per 100,000 general population.1, 2, 3 Due to its rarity, evidence concerning any aspect of the acute and late management is sparse.

Anticoagulation for acute UEDVT is recommended and should be followed by physiotherapy to relief the anatomic compression.4 Yet, the latter may predispose patients to recurrent thrombosis, which has been estimated to occur in 30% of patients after discontinuation of anticoagulation.4 Endovascular therapies, such as catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) and percutaneous thrombectomy devices followed by vein angioplasty, have the potential to swiftly restore venous patency. This may prevent the development of post-thrombotic syndrome or functional impairment.5,6 In some cases, surgical decompression (eg, first-rib resection) is recommended to relieve pressure on the vein trapped between the two bones.7

The decision to opt for an invasive approach in the acute and post-acute phase is usually driven by symptom severity, estimated risk of recurrence also based on prior events, and patient preferences. However, there is no recent data focusing on novel endovascular strategies of UEDVT, particularly concerning long-term outcomes.4 In this longitudinal study, we studied patients who underwent catheter-directed lysis or thrombectomy for acute UEDVT.

Patients and methods

In this retrospective longitudinal single-center analysis, we included all consecutive patients (representing the total institutional volume during the study period) with acute UEDVT due to compressive syndromes (venous thoracic outlet syndrome [vTOS]) who underwent endovascular treatment in the acute phase at the Department of Vascular Medicine of the University Hospital Zurich between 2017 and 2025.

Patients with written informed consent were included if UEDVT was diagnosed with ultrasound sonography and confirmed with diagnostic angiography or computed tomography angiography. A vTOS was confirmed based on: (1) the lack of other local causes of thrombosis, such as catheter-related, malignancy; (2) duplex ultrasound showing local compression; or (3) confirmation of a chronic stenosis at the level of the distal subclavian vein in the typical location for vTOS with venoplasty and contrast venography after thrombectomy (Supplementary Fig 1, A and B, online only). Patients were excluded from analysis if they presented with a subclavian vein thrombosis without signs of compressive syndrome (ie, patients with DVT related to a central venous catheter, patients exclusively undergoing venoplasty, and patients who have received their primary endovascular intervention for the current episode at an outside institution).

Interventions included single-session computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy (CAVT) (7Fr and 12Fr) followed by venoplasty in case of chronic stenosis, single-session pharmacomechanical rheolytic thrombectomy with the AngioJet (6Fr) or Zelante (8Fr) catheter followed by venoplasty in case of chronic stenosis, or catheter-directed lysis alone followed on the next day by venography and bail-out thrombectomy or venoplasty. The choice between the three approaches was left to the discretion of the treating physician. The interventions were performed by an experienced team of six interventional angiologists at the Department of Vascular Medicine of the University Hospital Zurich. The AngioJet and Zelante catheter represented the standard thrombectomy catheter used between 2017 and 2021. CAVT was used in the period comprised between 2022 and 2025. Early technical success was defined as complete thrombus removal and no residual stenosis after angioplasty, confirmed via venography at the end of the procedure. Furthermore, we identified a group of patients with complete thrombus removal with residual stenosis despite venous angioplasty.

At our center, all physically active patients with acute symptomatic UEDVT and suspected Paget-Schroetter syndrome were considered for an endovascular treatment as first-line therapy to improve early patency, but also to confirm the presence of residual chronic vTOS compression after thrombectomy to guide future treatment after a 3-month course of anticoagulation (Supplementary Fig 1, online only). Our institutional strategy for first rib resection follows a stepwise approach: surgical decompression is indicated only if clinical symptoms persist or residual stenosis/rethrombosis is identified during standardized duplex surveillance. Patients were followed up in our clinic with ultrasound at 2 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months after intervention.

The primary outcome was the need for reintervention and primary patency rate, which were analyzed separately. Primary patency was defined as a composite of freedom from occlusion in the treated segment and following reintervention needed to maintain patency within 2 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months. Specifically, catheter removal with contrast venography was not considered loss of patency due to the staged nature of the procedure; however, if venoplasty was required to treat residual stenosis or thrombectomy was performed, it was documented as loss of patency. The need for reintervention encompassed any subsequent endovascular or surgical procedure required to maintain or restore patency. Secondary outcomes included secondary patency rates and the progression of symptoms over time, with subgroup analyses conducted between different catheter types. The duplex ultrasound criteria used to assess UEDVT in this study were based on the definitions published by Desjardins et al,8 which provide standardized thresholds for venous ultrasound evaluation. To ensure reproducibility and identify extrinsic compression, dynamic testing was performed using duplex ultrasound or catheter venography with the arm in both neutral and provocative positions (eg, 90° and 180° abduction/elevation). Multimodal confirmation, including contrast-enhanced computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging in selected cases, was used to evaluate the costoclavicular space and central venous patency when ultrasound was inconclusive. Post-thrombotic syndrome was defined based on the presence of at least two symptoms among these and their severity: swelling, pain, and heaviness of the arm.9 Major and minor bleeding were assessed according to the criteria of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis.10

Data were extracted from electronic health records, imaging archives, and laboratory systems. Laboratory parameters (D-dimer, fibrinogen, C-reactive protein) at diagnosis and recurrence were recorded when available. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Ethics Committee Zurich, reference number EK 2021-00,579).

The analysis was conducted with an intention-to-treat approach. Each patient was included in the primary analysis once, based on the initial endovascular treatment received. Patients who underwent multimodal therapies or sequential interventions were primarily analyzed according to the first device used. Symptom progression over time was analyzed using generalized linear mixed models with random intercepts for patients. Categorical variables regarding symptom frequency in patients with and without first rib resection were compared using Fisher’s exact test, and statistical significance was set at P < .05. Time to reintervention and primary and secondary patency were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to estimate time from event over time with log-rank test. This approach was specifically utilized to account for patients lost to follow-up, ensuring that individuals were appropriately censored at the time of their last evaluation. Patients receiving sequential treatments were included in a secondary per-session analysis specifically for the evaluation of primary and secondary patency rates, treating each session as an independent observation. This methodological choice was made to evaluate the technical success and safety profile of individual devices, which might be obscured in a per-patient longitudinal analysis. Although this approach may introduce bias by not accounting for intra-patient correlation, it was utilized to provide a real-world assessment of device-specific performance in complex, multi-step treatment scenarios. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline data. Analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0).

Results

After exclusion of 17 patients who underwent venoplasty for chronic vTOS without any evidence of acute UEDVT requiring thrombectomy, we included 41 patients in the analysis. Twenty-five patients were men, and the median age was 34 years (interquartile range [IQR], 26-45 years). The demographic and baseline characteristics are presented in Table I. The median symptom duration was 4 days (IQR, 2-11 days). A family history of venous thromboembolism was reported by two patients (4.9%), and five (12.2%) had prior thromboembolic episodes. Genetic thrombophilia was present in five patients (15.2% of tested), including Factor V Leiden rs6025 heterozygote polymorphism in three (60% of tested), protein C deficiency in one (20% of tested), and unspecified in one. Seventeen patients (41.4%) were classified as athletes.

Clinical presentation and symptoms are summarized in Table I. Arm swelling was present in almost all patients, of whom 17 (43.6%) reported severe swelling. Paresthesia was reported by six (14.6%) patients, cyanosis by 10 (25%), and pain by 24 (60%). An extension of the DVT distally from the subclavian and axillary veins was documented in all patients, up to the brachial vein in four (9.8%) and also involving the basilic vein in three (7.3%). Two patients (4.9%) had a thrombosis also involving the brachiocephalic and cephalic veins, respectively. Median thrombus length was 10.7 cm (IQR, 7.4-14.0 cm). Acute symptomatic pulmonary embolism was present in five patients (12.2%).

Index intervention was performed using CAVT in seven patients (17%), AngioJet/Zelante in 25 (61%), and CDT in nine (22%)(Table II). Complete thrombus removal and no residual stenosis after angioplasty was documented in 15 patients (36.6%). Complete thrombus removal with residual stenosis despite angioplasty was documented in 12 patients (29.2%), and incomplete thrombus removal in 14 (34.2%). Bleeding complications occurred in two patients (4.9%), of which one was major (2.4%). Notably, both events were associated with the AngioJet/Zelante device group.

Reintervention was required in 16 patients (39%): in 10 cases (24%), this was performed in the 48 hours following index revascularization. The overall primary patency rate (defined as freedom from occlusion) of 60% (24/40; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.43-0.75) at 2 weeks, 52.6% (20/38; 95% CI, 0.36-0.69) at 3 months, and 54.1% (20/37; 95% CI, 0.37-0.71) at 6 months (Table III). To align with standard vascular reporting, these patency rates are reported separately from the reintervention rate. The primary patency after CAVT was 83% at 2 weeks and 83.3% at 3 months. The primary patency for CDT was 37.5% at 2 weeks and 22.2% at 3 months. The primary patency for AngioJet/Zelante was 64% at 2 weeks and 56.5% at 3 months. The Kaplan-Meier curve in Fig 1 illustrates the time to reintervention across index endovascular treatment groups. A log-rank analysis of time free from first recurrent DVT or restenosis leading to a reintervention showed a difference across catheters (P = .04). The same results were observed if the analysis focused on any endovascular intervention (Supplementary Fig 2, online only). A Kaplan-Meier curve depicts data on primary patency after vein occlusion (Supplementary Fig 3, online only) and secondary patency (Supplementary Fig 4, online only). Primary patency was higher for CAVT and AngioJet compared with CDT, whereas secondary patency was superior for CDT and CAVT.

Symptom progression was assessed at baseline, 2 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months. The greatest improvement occurred within the first 2 weeks (Fig 2). Complete functional recovery was achieved in 77.8% at 2 weeks and 85.7% at 6 months. Cyanosis resolved in all patients by 6 months, the prevalence of pain decreased from 60% to 14.3%, and swelling decreased from 98% to 17.1% at latest follow-up. The prevalence of multiple symptoms consistent with the presence of a post-thrombotic syndrome declined from 51% at 2 weeks to 2% at 6 months (Supplementary Table I, online only). Symptom burden was compared between patients with and without complete recanalization with no clinically relevant differences between groups. Changes in the prevalence of symptoms were studied in a multivariable model taking into account time of observation (Supplementary Table II, online only) showing the greatest reduction for pain (P < .001), swelling, and cyanosis.

This graph illustrates the proportion of patients presenting with clinical symptoms at baseline, 2 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months post-intervention. Swelling and pain were the most prevalent symptoms at baseline, both showing substantial decline over time. Functional recovery remained high throughout the follow-up period, whereas cyanosis and paresthesia were less common and stable.

A total of 11 patients (31.4%) underwent surgical first rib resection during follow-up: one at 2 weeks, two at 2 months, and eight at 6 months post-intervention. The indication was most likely related to residual symptoms. Symptom resolution appeared more pronounced in this subgroup, particularly at 6 months (Supplementary Table III, online only). The rate of surgical first-rib resection was lower in the CAVT group (0%) compared with the AngioJet/Zelante group (64%; P = .0039) (Supplementary Table IV, online only).

Discussion

This observational retrospective longitudinal study provides one of the first direct comparison of different endovascular treatments and clinical outcomes for acute symptomatic UEDVT (Paget-Schroetter syndrome). Based on this single-center experience, endovascular therapies appeared feasible and safe, and patients showed a reduction in symptoms burden, which was sustained over a 6-month follow-up. Of note, the prevalence of swelling, pain, and cyanosis decreased, and the post-thrombotic syndrome was rare, about 2%, at 6 months. Such an approach may represent an option to manage acute UEDVT among younger patients who are physically active and would like to accelerate functional recovery and reduce the risk of post-thrombotic syndrome.

The goals of endovascular intervention are to relieve acute symptoms and to prevent the thrombus from becoming chronic, leading to a permanent occlusion of the subclavian vein. Preventing chronic occlusion may be considered a prerequisite for improving the success of first rib resection, which aims to eliminate the underlying anatomical compression of the subclavian vein. Various strategies combining these two interventions, performed at different timepoints after acute UEDVT, have been described.4,11,12 Our findings are consistent with this principle. Yet, in our cohort, we also observed symptom relief in patients managed without surgical decompression, suggesting that the latter may be considered only in selected cases after thrombectomy and initial 3-month course of anticoagulant therapy for acute UEDVT.13,14 Consistent with the European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines, these results reinforce the importance of multidisciplinary approach.15

According to the 2016 American College of Chest Physicians, CDT may be considered for selected patients with upper-extremity DVT, especially when thrombosis is extensive and bleeding risk is low.14,16 It must be noted that this recommendation relies on the fact that CDT had represented the main endovascular strategy for several years, and it is only in the past 10 to 15 years that novel catheter-based aspiration thrombectomy and pharmacomechanical thrombectomy devices have been established. In our exploratory analysis, single-session treatment with AngioJet/Zelante or CAVT achieved the highest patency and less frequent need for reintervention. The latter is usually needed after CDT to assess the venous patency after lysis.

A point merits further discussion. CDT is effective for thrombus resolution but does not directly address the underlying venous stenosis typical in vTOS, often necessitating further procedures to achieve durable patency, including venous angioplasty followed or not by rib resection. Consistent with this, Guzzo et al17 reported that, in patients with subacute or chronic subclavian vein thrombosis, preoperative thrombolysis and venoplasty conferred no additional benefit in patency following first rib resection and scalenectomy, underlining the limited efficacy of CDT once the thrombus has chronicized. By contrast, in acute cases, Stuck et al18 demonstrated that thrombolysis combined with venoplasty and provisional decompression can be effective in restoring venous patency and improving symptoms. In our study, the 24% early reintervention rate reflects a staged clinical strategy and underlying anatomy rather than technical failure or lack of procedural completeness, particularly with CDT, where follow-up venography often identified residual thrombus or chronic stenosis due to the underlying anatomical pathology. These secondary interventions were essential to optimize the venous outflow tract; although they often served as a bridge to surgical decompression, some patients were successfully managed with repeat endovascular therapy alone when first rib resection was not immediately indicated.

Our device-specific observations are supported by recent findings from Fuller et al,19 who compared aspiration thrombectomy using CAVT with non-aspiration-based approaches such as AngioJet/Zelante and CDT. In this study, technical success was defined as above 70% thrombus resolution. Using this definition, CAVT was associated with lower thrombolytic dosage requirements and a higher rate of single-session success, suggesting potential safety and efficiency advantages. In contrast, our study applied a more rigorous definition of early technical success, requiring complete thrombus removal and no residual stenosis after angioplasty. The bleeding events observed with AngioJet/Zelante (one major and one minor bleeding event) may be explained with the use of thrombolysis. In contrast, CDT, despite requiring prolonged thrombolytic infusion, did not result in clinically significant bleeding in our cohort as the infusion rate is usually low or very low, not exceeding alteplase 1 mg/hour. However, this may reflect conservative patient selection or stringent monitoring rather than an inherently safer profile. Overall, the low complication rate across all device groups suggests that endovascular management of acute UEDVT in vTOS can be performed safely in specialized centers. Nonetheless, given the potential for device-specific adverse events, larger multicenter studies are warranted to more accurately compare safety profiles, particularly in high-risk patients or those requiring prolonged thrombolysis.

Long-term follow-up assessment is particularly relevant in this group of patients characterized by young age and who are functionally active. In this perspective, single-session catheter-based thrombectomy may reduce in-hospital stay over CDT, as well as bleeding risk. However, as symptom resolution did not consistently parallel procedural outcomes, we call for incorporating patient-reported outcome measures into future research.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective, single-center design, small cohort size, and lack of standardized follow-up restrict generalizability. Device selection was at the physician’s discretion, introducing potential selection bias and temporal ‘era’ confounding as newer technologies were introduced sequentially. We utilized a per-session analysis to evaluate device-specific efficacy in complex treatment paths but acknowledge that treating multiple procedures from the same patient as independent observations is a statistical limitation that may introduce intra-patient correlation bias. Furthermore, the symptom-based post-thrombotic syndrome criteria are not formally validated for axillo-subclavian DVT; the observed variability in edema and pain reduction suggests potential measurement imprecision, limiting the interpretability of these clinical data. Despite these constraints, our findings provide valuable real-world insights into endovascular modalities for this rare condition. Finally, the relatively small sample size within each subgroup limits the statistical power of our comparisons, and all results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Future research should prioritize parallel-group trials including standardized treatment algorithms that clarify the optimal sequencing of endovascular and surgical interventions, alongside long-term follow-up to assess durability of patency and symptom relief.20 Studies should incorporate validated patient-reported outcome measures focusing on functional recovery.

Conclusions

In conclusion, endovascular therapy for vTOS-associated acute UEDVT appears feasible and safe, providing rapid symptom relief. Our exploratory findings suggest promising technical success and outcomes for single-session strategies (especially CAVT) compared with CDT. However, due to the retrospective design and small sample size, these results are hypothesis-generating.

Patients treated with computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy (CAVT; blue line) demonstrated the highest reintervention-free rate followed by Angiojet/Zelante (red line) and catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT; green line) (log-rank test, P = .20).
