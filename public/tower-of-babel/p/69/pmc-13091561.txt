Fueling asthma inflammation via bioenergetic metabolic reprogramming
Authors: Anny Mulya, Allison J Janocha, Michael V Novotny, Andrew Reichard, Emma Hamm, Ruoying Chen, Jacob T Mey, Laura Peterson, Lori Mavrakis, Brittany Beck, Michelle Koo, Jacqueline Sharp, Emily Pennington, Stephanie McCarroll, Sarah Micklewright, Peng Zhang, Cynthia Koziol-White, Mark Aronica, Suzy AA Comhair, John P Kirwan, John Barnard, Reynold A Panettieri, Victor Darley-Usmar, Serpil C Erzurum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic and bioenergetic abnormalities are associated with asthma, yet the mechanisms linking these disruptions to disease pathophysiology remain unclear. We hypothesized that asthma arises from altered connectivity within metabolic networks. To test this, we employed a systems-level approach integrating platelet bioenergetic profiling with plasma metabolomics to map the bioenergetic–metabolite interactome. Fasting plasma metabolites and platelet bioenergetics were analyzed in individuals with asthma (n = 40) and healthy controls (n = 18). Among 119 altered metabolites, adenosine derivatives correlated with inflammation, and lung function, but did not fully distinguish asthma from controls. Platelet bioenergetic profiling revealed less ATP-linked oxygen consumption, and greater bioenergetic reserve capacity in asthma. Studies of primary human airway smooth muscle cells identify less mitochondrial glucose use in asthma as compared with controls. Integration of platelet bioenergetics and plasma metabolites shows a restructured network in asthma with fewer connections between metabolites and bioenergetic nodes, and new asthma-specific clusters enriched in lipid metabolism. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized bioenergetic phenotype of asthma, defined by greater oxidative reserve capacity, less mitochondrial glucose utilization, and a shift of the bioenergetic - metabolic network toward lipid pathways.Keywords: Asthma, Bioenergetics, Metabolomics, Bioenergetic-metabolite network

Introduction

Disruptions in bioenergetic metabolism are now emerging as a major factor linking altered cellular function to the molecular mechanisms underlying development and progression of various diseases, particularly those with an inflammatory component [[1], [2], [3]]. Revealing these interactions has become possible with the data generated from high throughput bioenergetic assessments and metabolomics from patient populations. Asthma, characterized by chronic airway inflammation, is increasingly recognized as a disease associated with metabolic alterations [4,5]. Individuals with asthma have greater resting energy expenditure, ∼approximately 100 kcal/day higher than people without asthma [6] and report a lower dietary caloric intake [[6], [7], [8]]. Yet, paradoxically, individuals with asthma generally have a higher BMI as compared to people without asthma [[9], [10], [11]]. Alterations of arginine pathways, including those that lead to synthesis of nitric oxide (NO), are well-established as biomarkers of the severity of inflammation in asthma [[12], [13], [14]]. In addition to metabolic alterations, changes in mitochondrial number [15], morphology [16], function [[17], [18], [19]], and dynamics [20] are present in asthma pre-clinical murine models and in patients with asthma. Studies of airway epithelial cells and/or platelets in asthma suggest that changes in mitochondrial function are associated with the changes in arginine metabolism [19]. These data support the concept that bioenergetic and metabolic pathways are interconnected in asthma and potentially related to pathophysiology.

Traditional metabolomics research focuses on identifying aberrant metabolites that distinguish diseased groups from non-diseased groups. In contrast, network-based approaches integrate metabolite data with complementary functional datasets to identify clusters of interconnected molecules, placing metabolites within biologic networks that reflect coordinated pathways and functions. Over the past two decades, network-based research has highlighted that complex diseases are often driven by disruptions in network connectivity, rather than isolated failures of individual components. Recent studies show that integrating bioenergetic parameters with metabolomics data provides a comprehensive, systems-level view of metabolic and energy homeostasis, revealing how energy metabolism is intricately linked with broader metabolic pathways and cellular functions [21,22]. In healthy individuals, the bioenergetic–metabolite network displays a reproducible and tightly coordinated balanced metabolic state [21].

This study aims to investigate whether asthma is characterized by a distinct pattern of metabolic and bioenergetic system disruption. We hypothesize that individuals with asthma will exhibit a bioenergetic–metabolite network that differs significantly from that of healthy individuals, with unique connections between bioenergetic parameters and specific metabolites that define the metabolic signature of asthma. To test this, we measured platelet bioenergetic parameters and performed global plasma metabolomic profiling in individuals with asthma and healthy controls. Platelets offer advantages for bioenergetics study, as they are abundantly available, easily accessible, possess fully functional mitochondria and active glycolytic pathways. They have been reproducibly used as a surrogate to study bioenergetics in human disease settings [17,19,21,[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]], e.g. platelet bioenergetic changes mirror those seen in asthmatic airway epithelial cells [19]. Here, we also study primary human airway smooth muscle cells ex vivo to interrogate bioenergetic fuel use in asthma as compared to control cells. Finally, platelet bioenergetics were correlated to plasma metabolites to construct and compare the bioenergetic-metabolite interaction network of asthmatics to healthy controls.

Methodology

Study design and population. This cross-sectional study evaluated reprogramming of metabolites in relation to mitochondrial oxygen consumption activity in adults with asthma and healthy controls. The study design and population have been described previously [6]. This study was conducted at the Cleveland Clinic (Cleveland, OH) with approval of the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board Committee (IRB Protocol ID: 10-1049, approval date March 22nd, 2018). Complete inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in previous publication [6]. All participants provided written informed consent before initiation of study. Briefly, participants were adults (age ≥18 years) of both sexes, with BMI less than 40 kg/m2. Participants with asthma had either prior asthma diagnosis from a medical specialist or a history of a positive methacholine test and/or bronchodilator reversibility of more than 10% of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1). This report included 18 healthy controls and 40 individuals with asthma. Three participants (two healthy controls and one with asthma) from the original study were excluded in the present study: (1) technical concerns with platelet mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate profile (1 healthy male), (2) high fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO) (>30 ppb) suggesting recent viral infection (1 healthy male) and (3) the absence of metabolomics data (1 asthmatic male). Thus, the missing data were not related to asthma status nor sex and represent <5% of the study population [32].

Plasma metabolomic analysis. Nontargeted metabolomic analysis was performed on fasting plasma samples (Metabolon Inc., Durham, NC). The details of the analytical platform and data curation were described in previous publication [33].

Platelet isolation. Platelet isolation followed methodology described previously [17,31]. Isolated platelets were resuspended in 1.0 mL of Tyrode's buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 12 mM NaHCO3, 0.4 mM NaH2PO4, 5 mM HEPES, 0.1% d-glucose and 0.35% fatty acid free BSA, pH 7.4) and counted using a hemocytometer.

Platelet bioenergetic profiling analysis. Intact platelet oxygen consumption rates were monitored following the MitoStress test protocol (Agilent Seahorse Biosciences, Billerica, MA) with slight modification [17,31]. Platelets (25x106 per well, 3-5 wells per subject) were resuspended in XF assay medium. XF assay medium used for platelets was unbuffered Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Media (DMEM; at 37 °C, pH 7.35 ± 0.05) and freshly supplemented with 5.55 mM glucose (euglycemic concentration), 1 mM sodium pyruvate and 2 mM l-glutamine. Exogenous fatty acids were not added to XF assay medium. Platelets were attached to XF24 V7 PS plate (Seahorse) coated with CellTak (Corning, Corning, NY). Final seahorse XF assay medium is 500 μL per well. The oxygen concentration and pH levels were monitored in real-time at baseline and when inhibitors to target mitochondria were added. These inhibitors included a mitochondrial complex V inhibitor (oligomycin, 2.5 μM), a mitochondrial uncoupler (carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoro-methoxyphenyl hydrazone (FCCP), 0.7 μM), a complex I inhibitor (rotenone, 1 μM), and a complex III inhibitor (antimycin A, 1 μM) and were added in sequential order. Data were collected for three measurements at each step and for a duration of 3 min per measurement cycle. The oxygen consumption rate (OCR, pmoles/min/25x106 platelets) was determined by the rate at which the oxygen concentration decreases over time. Similarly, glycolysis was measured as the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR, mpH/min/25x106 platelets), which was determined by the rate at which pH level decreases (acidifies) over time. Glycolytic metabolism was reported as basal, max, stress and glycolytic reserve ECAR. Fig. S1A and S1B show representative ECAR and OCR profiles, respectively. Table S1 provides the formulas for calculating bioenergetic parameters. Two experienced Seahorse specialists reviewed all data to identify failed wells and outliers.

Primary human airway smooth muscle cells (HASMCs). Primary HASMCs were isolated from the explanted donor lungs of non-asthmatic and asthmatic individuals, as described previously [34]. Primary HASMCs were a gift from Dr. Reynold A. Panettieri (non-asthma control N = 5; asthma cells N = 5). Primary HASMCs were cultured with DMEM:F12 medium supplemented with 15 mM HEPES, 2.5 mM l-Glutamine, 10% FBS, 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin and 1 mL of Primocin (Invivo Gen, Catalog # ant-pm-1). DMEM:F12 medium contained 17 mM d-glucose and 0.5 mM sodium pyruvate. Primary HASMCs at passage 8 to 9 were used for the assessment of mitochondrial fuel dependency.

Mitochondrial fuel dependency. Primary HASMCs were plated in XFe96 culture plate at a density of 10,000 cells per well with growth medium overnight. Growth medium was replaced the following day with XF assay medium for HASMCs, DMEM medium supplemented with 17 mM d-glucose, 2.5 mM l-glutamine and 0.5 mM sodium pyruvate.

Mitochondrial fuel dependency was assessed following fuel dependency protocol (Seahorse Biosciences, Agilent). Diagram for mitochondrial fuel dependency measures is shown in Fig. S2. Mitochondrial fuel assessment was conducted using specific inhibitors to target mitochondrial oxidative glucose, glutamine and fat pathways. Mitochondrial oxidative glucose pathway was targeted by an inhibitor of mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC, protein for transferring pyruvate into mitochondria) (UK5099, 2-cyano-3-(1-phenyl-H-indol-3-yl) acryl acid, 2 μM). Pyruvate is the end metabolite of aerobic glycolysis metabolism. Mitochondrial glutamine pathway was targeted using an inhibitor of glutaminase (GLS, enzyme for the conversion of glutamine to glutamate) (BPTES, 2-phenyl-N-[5-[2-[2-[5-[(2-phenylacetyl)amino]-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl]ethylsulfanyl]ethyl]-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl]acetamide, 3 μM). Mitochondrial fat pathway was targeted using an inhibitor of carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT, enzyme to shuttle fatty acid from cytosol to mitochondria) (Etomoxir, ethyl (2R)-2-[6-(4-chlorophenoxy)hexyl]oxirane-2-carboxylate, 4 μM). Exogenous fatty acid was not added to the medium, i.e. etomoxir targeted fatty acids generated by endogenous metabolic pathways.

OCR and ECAR were monitored using the extracellular flux analyzer (XFe96, Seahorse Biosciences, Agilent) at baseline, in response to inhibition of 1 fuel pathway and in response to inhibition of the other 2 fuel pathways. Changes in OCR and ECAR were calculated as the differences in response to the inhibition of 1 fuel pathway or the other two, compared to baseline values. Mitochondria fuel dependency was calculated as the percentage of changes in OCR with 1 fuel inhibitor relative to changes in OCR with additional 2 fuel inhibitors.

Statistics and bioinformatics analysis

Global metabolomic analysis. Metabolite levels provided by Metabolon were normalized following variance stabilizing normalization (VSN) methodology. The significantly different metabolites between any two groups were generated by Heatmap function from library ComplexHeatmap in R. The principal component analysis (PCA) plots were created by autoplot function (library ggplot2) based on the first principal component (PC1) and the second principal component (PC2) of significant metabolites. The pathway enrichment analysis of altered metabolites was based on the proportion of significant metabolites relative to the total metabolites within the pathway of interest. A hypergeometric test was used to find the significantly enriched pathways (R software, PHYPER function). The p‐values were adjusted by FDR with a threshold less than 0.05. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Bioenergetic-Metabolite Interactome analysis. Integration of bioenergetics and metabolomics data was performed using xMWAS software [35]. xMWAS software is a tool for data integration and network visualization of functionally related parameters [35]. The current study aims to integrate and visualize the network of metabolites with mitochondrial oxygen consumption activity [21,36]. The inputs for xMWAS included the platelet bioenergetics (6 parameters: basal, ATP-linked, proton leak, maximal, reserve and non-mitochondrial OCRs) and plasma metabolome (1249 detected metabolites) data. The xMWAS analysis used the sparse partial least-squares (sPLS) regression method for pairwise data integration of bioenergetics and metabolomics data. The software's multilevel community detection algorithm was used to identify community clusters of bioenergetics and metabolites that relate to each other [37]. The threshold for correlation coefficient (r) and p-value were calculated by the xMWAS algorithm with the r threshold input of 0.4 and p value at < 0.05. The correlation coefficient was unbiasedly decided by the xMWAS algorithm. The outcome of r threshold was above 0.47 for control network, 0.40 for asthma network, 0.40 for asthmatic with BMI <30 kg/m2, and 0.52 for asthmatic BMI ≥30 kg/m2. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by lowering and increasing the absolute r threshold in asthmatic population. Changes in the threshold did not eliminate the observed differences between the asthma and control networks. The original xMWAS software package is from https://kuppal.shinyapps.io/xmwas/and further edited by Roman Zenka. The xMWAS software is accessed from https://github.com/romanzenka/xMWAS.

Statistical Analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless indicated otherwise. Data were checked for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance between two groups was concluded by Student's t-test for normally distributed data. For non-normally distributed data, statistical significance was determined on log-transformed data or using nonparametric Mann-Whitney test.

Results

Study population. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the healthy controls [N = 18, 11 female] and asthmatic individuals [N = 40, 31 female] have been described previously [6] [Table S2]. Briefly, individuals with asthma were similar in age but had higher body mass index (BMI) (p = 0.033) as compared with healthy controls. Airflow measured as % predicted of FEV1 was lower in asthma (p = 0.049) and FENO tended to be higher (p = 0.15). Compared to healthy people, individuals with asthma had higher white blood cell counts (WBC) (p = 0.014) and neutrophil counts (p = 0.024). All individuals with asthma were well-controlled on their current care.

Global metabolomics profile of plasma from controls and asthmatics. Nontargeted metabolomics analyses of plasma from controls and asthmatics identify a total of 1249 metabolites, which belong to 118 unique sub-pathways. Principal component analyses of metabolomics data show a partial segregation of the two groups [Fig. S3A]. Out of 1249 metabolites, 1130 are similar among controls and asthmatics but 119 have distinct profiles evident in the heatmap [Fig. 1A]. Of the 119 metabolites different among controls and asthmatics, 45 metabolites are higher (FC > 1.10, p < 0.05) and 74 metabolites are lower in asthma (FC < 0.90, p < 0.05) [Fig. 1A–Table 1]. Pathway enrichment analysis of 119 altered metabolites shows enrichment of steroid-related metabolites [Fig. S3B].

Altered metabolites in asthma.

Metabolites are arranged based on the metabolite's super pathway. Fold change, p-value and FDR value are listed. Metabolites with FDR < 0.2 are highlighted bold.

Of the 119 differentially expressed metabolites, only five show significant alterations in asthma compared to controls, with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.2. Two of these metabolites are lower in asthma, i.e., arabonate/xylonate (0.54-fold, FDR = 0.048, p < 0.001) and 5-acetylamino-6-formylamino-3-methyluracil (AFMU) (0.25-fold, FDR = 0.048, p < 0.001) [Fig. 1B]. Three metabolites are higher in asthma, i.e., 1-linolenoylglycerol (18:3) (2.56-fold, FDR = 0.068, p < 0.001), malonate (2.49-fold, FDR = 0.048, p < 0.001), and N6-methyladenosine (1.89-fold, FDR = 0.048, p < 0.001) [Fig. 1C].

Neutrophil counts are elevated in asthmatics compared to controls. To evaluate whether neutrophil counts influence the levels of plasma metabolites, we applied a multivariable regression analysis approach to include neutrophil counts, along with age and gender as covariates. Four metabolites remain altered in asthma after adjustment for neutrophil counts: arabonate/xylonate (FC = 0.382-fold, FDR = 0.126, p < 0.001), AFMU (FC = 0.123-fold, FDR = 0.126, p < 0.001), malonate (FC = 3.580-fold, FDR = 0.126, p < 0.001) and N6-methyladenosine (FC = 2.416-fold, FDR = 0.140, p < 0.001) (Table S3). The lipid 1-linoleoylglycerol (18:3) is no longer significantly different post adjustment with neutrophil count. N-acetylaspartate which is not different in unadjusted data, is found to be significantly lower in asthma as compared to controls with adjustment [FC = 0.607-fold, FDR = 0.165, p < 0.001].

To explore whether any of these different metabolites are associated with clinical features of asthma, we analyzed correlations with BMI, immune cell counts, lung functions (spirometry), airway inflammation (FENO), and airway reactivity (bronchodilator reversibility). The lipid metabolite 1-linolenoylglycerol (18:3) positively correlates with BMI (asthma: r = 0.46, p < 0.001) [Fig. 1D] and WBC (asthma: r = 0.32, p = 0.045) [Fig. 1E] in individuals with asthma. N6-methyladenosine correlates with measures of asthma severity, i.e., higher levels of N6-methyladenosine associate with lower FEV1 (%predicted) (asthma: r = −0.35, p = 0.03, Fig. 1F), with higher FENO (asthma: r = 0.35, p = 0.03, Fig. 1G), and with greater bronchodilator reversibility (r = 0.34, p = 0.037, Fig. 1H). The metabolites and their relationship to metabolic pathways and clinical features are summarized in Fig. 1I. These data support the hypothesis that specific metabolic pathways are different in asthma and associated with the pathophysiological changes in asthma.

Bioenergetic profiling of platelets from controls and asthmatics. Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in platelets are used as surrogates of glycolysis and mitochondrial bioenergetics, respectively. Briefly, in these experiments, ECAR and OCR in platelets were measured using a Seahorse flux analyzer in the presence of fuel sources including glucose, pyruvate, and glutamine. To assess mitochondrial function, oligomycin, a complex V inhibitor, is added to measure the portion of OCR used for ATP production (ATP-linked OCR) and to calculate proton leak not related to the electron transport chain. As mitochondrial ATP production is blocked, cells increase glycolysis, shown by an increase in ECAR. Maximal OCR is measured after adding FCCP, which uncouples mitochondria and drives electron flow at maximum capacity. Respiratory reserve capacity is calculated as the difference between maximal and basal respiration. [See Fig. S1A for ECAR and Fig. S1B for OCR, Table S1 for formulas].

ECAR profile is identical among controls and asthmatics [Fig. 2A]; basal, maximal, stress and glycolytic reserve ECAR are similar between the groups [Fig. 2B]. The OCR profile of asthma parallels healthy controls [Fig. 2C], but the resting basal OCR tends to be lower (p = 0.08) and ATP-linked OCR is lower in asthma as compared to controls (p = 0.04) [Fig. 2D]. Notably, basal and ATP-linked OCR values range from 23 to 144 pmol/min per 25x106 platelets, indicating substantial inter-individual variability in energy metabolism capacity as has been observed in platelets in other studies [21,22,36]. Interestingly, in response to FCCP, platelets from asthma achieve a maximal OCR equivalent to controls (p = 0.70) and thus have a greater respiratory reserve capacity (p = 0.021) [Fig. 2D]. Asthma and controls have similar proton leak (p = 0.83) and non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption (p = 0.64) [Fig. 2D].

Bivariate plots of ECAR and OCR provide a visual representation of the energy phenotypes in asthma and controls (control: r = 0.40, p = 0.10; asthma: r = 0.37, p = 0.018; Fig. 2E). For any given level of glycolysis (ECAR), platelets from individuals with asthma show lower oxygen consumption (OCR) compared to controls. As previously reported, ATP-linked OCR and maximal OCR are strongly correlated in healthy controls [21]. In both control and asthmatic groups, basal OCR is positively associated with ATP-linked OCR, and the strength of this relationship is essentially identical between the two groups (control: r = 0.94, p < 0.001; asthma: r = 0.98, p < 0.001; Fig. 2F).

Basal OCR is positively correlated with maximal OCR in control and asthma groups (control: r = 0.52, p = 0.027; asthma: r = 0.78, p < 0.001; Fig. 2G). However, in asthmatics, maximal OCR is shifted upward as compared to controls. This indicates that although asthmatic platelets exhibit less oxygen consumption for ATP production at baseline, they have the capacity to rapidly increase respiration when energy demand rises. These findings suggest that bioenergetic metabolism is regulated differently in asthma. Asthmatic platelets may be more metabolically efficient or adaptable in meeting energy needs. This bioenergetic shift may be influenced by changes in metabolite levels and/or how they interact with the mitochondrial respiratory machinery, including the regulation of electron transport chain activity and substrate availability.

Asthma-associated shift in bioenergetics-metabolites network. Metabolites were correlated with OCR-based bioenergetic parameters in control and asthmatic groups using the xMWAS integration tool [Fig. 3A and B]. In the resulting networks, circular nodes represent the six OCR bioenergetic measures, while square dots represent metabolites that show significant correlations with these measures. Edges (lines) indicate statistically significant associations between a metabolite and a bioenergetic parameter, forming a network of functional interactions. In healthy controls, the network includes 231 metabolite–bioenergetic connections, involving 132 unique metabolites that show strong correlations with OCR parameters (absolute r > 0.47, p < 0.05) [Fig. 3C]. In contrast, the asthma network contains only 140 connections, involving 77 unique metabolites with correlations above the defined threshold (absolute r > 0.40, p < 0.05) [Fig. 3D]. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by lowering and increasing the threshold for asthmatic network to absolute r of 0.32 and 0.50, respectively [Fig. S4]. As expected, lowering the r threshold to 0.32 results in more metabolites connected to OCR (426 connections and 208 unique metabolites), but the resulting network still differs substantially from control. Increasing the r threshold to 0.50 causes loss of the network and thus 0.40 threshold is used in asthma.

The systems-level view reveals a closed-loop connected network of metabolites and bioenergetics in healthy controls, similar to prior reports [21,22] but a linear disconnected network in asthma. The control network organizes into 3 clusters: cluster 1 includes basal and ATP-linked OCR, cluster 2 has non-mitochondrial OCR, and cluster 3 has maximum, proton leak and reserve capacity OCRs [Fig. 3C]. The asthma network organizes into 4 clusters: cluster 1 includes non-mitochondrial OCR, cluster 2 has basal and ATP-linked OCRs, cluster 3 has maximum OCR, and cluster 4 has reserve capacity OCR [Fig. 3D]. The ATP-linked and basal OCRs share the same community cluster, indicating a close relationship between these bioenergetic parameters whether in control or asthma. However, the maximum OCR, proton leak and reserve capacity are in separate community clusters. Proton leak OCR does not correlate with any metabolites in asthma, leading to the absence of a proton leak OCR community cluster in asthma.

All metabolites connected to bioenergetic parameters in either the control or asthma networks are unique and show distinct patterns of association with the bioenergetic nodes in each group. Table S4–S9 provide the complete list of OCR-associated metabolites in controls and asthma. Among all metabolites connected to nodal parameters, only six metabolites are present in both asthma and control networks [Fig. 3E], i.e., 126 metabolites are present only in control and 71 metabolites are present only in asthma [Fig. 3E]. Six metabolites are present in both networks, including 4-methoxyphenolsulfate, 3-methylxanthine, adenine, citramalate, octadecanedioylcarnitine (C18-DC) and X-24306 [Fig. 3E]. However, these six metabolites associate with bioenergetic parameter nodes differently in asthma as compared to control [pointed in Fig. 3C and D, Table S4–S9].

Metabolites linked to bioenergetic nodes were organized by their metabolic pathways in Table 2. These pathways were grouped into categories including carbohydrates, lipids, amino acids, nucleotides, cofactors and vitamins, xenobiotics, and other metabolic pathways. In general, there are fewer metabolites connected to bioenergetic nodes in asthma as compared to control, except for the maximum OCR node. The bioenergetic nodes are mostly connected to lipid and amino acid pathways [Table 2], with higher preference for lipids in asthma. In the asthma network, enrichment of phospholipid metabolites is found in cluster 2 (basal and ATP-linked) and cluster 3 (maximum) [Fig. 3D]. Among the metabolites associated with the ATP-linked OCR node, 72% are lipid-related in asthma, whereas in controls, the metabolites are more evenly distributed across lipids (29%), amino acids (20%), and other pathways (25%) [Fig. 3F for control and 3G for asthma, Table 2]. BMI and asthma-associated shift in bioenergetics-metabolites network. Similar to previous reports, asthmatics in our population have a higher BMI compared to healthy controls [[9], [10], [11]]. To evaluate whether BMI contributes to the lipid-heavy metabolic network in asthma, we analyzed and stratified our data based on BMI. Asthmatics were categorized into non-obese (BMI <30 kg/m2, n = 25) and obese (BMI ≥30 kg/m2, n = 15). Table S10 shows the clinical characteristics of non-obese and obese asthmatic subgroups. Non-obese asthmatics have BMI similar to controls (Control: 25.5 ± 3.9 vs. Non-obese Asthmatic: 25.2 ± 2.6, p = 0.81), while obese asthmatics have significantly higher BMI than controls or non-obese asthmatics (Obese Asthmatic: 33.2 ± 2.4, p < 0.001). Non-obese and obese asthmatics are similar in age and gender. Lung functions, percent predicted FEV1 and the ratio of FEV1 to FVC, are similar between non-obese and obese asthmatics. However, percent predicted FVC is lower in obese than non-obese asthma subgroups (p = 0.012). Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO), WBC and neutrophil counts are also similar among non-obese and obese asthmatics. Obese asthmatics have lower percent predicted FEV1 (p < 0.01), percent predicted FVC (p < 0.01), and higher WBC (p < 0.01) and neutrophil counts (p < 0.01) than healthy controls (Table S2 and S10). Higher eosinophil counts are observed in obese asthmatics compared with non-obese asthmatics (p = 0.013, Table S10).

The bioenergetic-metabolite network of non-obese asthma comprises 272 connections involving 147 unique metabolites that exhibit correlations with OCR parameters (absolute r > 0.40, p < 0.05) [Fig. S5A]. The network of obese asthma includes 220 connections, involving 135 unique metabolites (absolute r > 0.52, p < 0.05) [Fig. S5B]. Despite having a comparable BMI to controls, the non-obese asthma network differs substantially from the control network; the non-obese asthma network closely aligns with the network observed in the complete asthma cohort. Metabolites connected to bioenergetics are listed on Table S12–S16.

Among the asthma subgroups, 17 metabolites are shared across both networks, but the metabolites correlate differently with bioenergetic parameters (Fig. S5C and Table S11). Metabolites associated with ATP-linked OCR in obese asthma are much more enriched in lipids compared to the non-obese (94% vs. 43%, Fig. S5D and S5E). Sub-pathway analyses are shown in Fig. S5A and S5B. There is enrichment of diacylglycerol in cluster 1 and phosphatidylethanolamine in cluster 2 of the non-obese network, and enrichment of fatty acids in clusters 1 to 3 in obese asthma. Enrichment in long chain fatty acids in the obese asthma network may indicate a higher preference of fatty acids for ATP. These findings show that the different bioenergetic-metabolite network of asthma as compared to healthy controls is not dependent on BMI, but also that BMI in asthma influences the network pattern.

Mitochondrial fuel utilization in asthma. The bioenergetic-metabolite network showed enrichment of lipids in asthma. To directly investigate specific fuels used for energy production in asthma, we examined mitochondrial fuel utilization in primary human airway smooth muscle cells (HASMCs). This assay allows us to inspect how glucose, glutamine and fat are used by mitochondria and to directly study primary airway cells.

Fig. S2 illustrates the mitochondrial fuel dependency assay diagram. Briefly, baseline OCR and ECAR is recorded, followed by the injection of inhibitors targeting specific mitochondrial oxidative pathways and both OCR and ECAR responses are monitored. Mitochondrial oxidative glucose pathway is targeted using UK5099, an inhibitor of the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC). Mitochondrial oxidative glutamine pathway is targeted using BPTES, an inhibitor of the glutaminase (GLS). Mitochondrial oxidative fat pathway is targeted using Etomoxir, an inhibitor of carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT). In the presence of each of these inhibitors, OCR decreases, and is accompanied by an increase in ECAR, reflecting a metabolic shift from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis. The proportion of OCR reduction following the inhibition of a single fuel pathway relative to inhibition of all three pathways is a measure of fuel dependency [Table 3 and Table S17].

Discussion

This study provides further evidence for the foundational concept that asthma pathophysiology is linked to changes in metabolism and bioenergetics. Two new findings advance understanding of asthma as a metabolic disorder. First, asthmatic cells have greater mitochondrial reserve capacity, but less ability to use glucose for either oxidative metabolism or glycolysis. This pattern suggests a shift away from glucose toward alternative fuel sources. Second, the bioenergetic–metabolite network is reprogrammed in asthma. Bioenergetic parameters in asthma are predominantly connected to lipid metabolites, while bioenergetics in controls have a balanced network involving multiple metabolite classes.

Studies have shown that individuals with asthma have a unique plasma metabolome profile that distinguishes them from healthy controls [12,33,[38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45]], including higher arginine bioavailability [12], nicotinamide and ADP levels [33,40], suppression of steroid metabolites [33,40], aberrant phospholipids [41,42] and reduction-oxidation (redox) imbalance with lower total antioxidants [43]. Here, global nontargeted metabolomics show that controls and asthmatics differ in 119 metabolites, 4 of which are significant even after adjusting for neutrophil counts in this population, i.e., arabonate/xylonate, 5-acetylamino-6-formylamino-3-methyluracil (AFMU), malonate, and N6-methyladenosine. Arabonate and xylonate are oxidized products of the pentose sugars l-arabinose and d-xylose, respectively, and ultimately converted into alpha-ketoglutarate to enter the TCA cycle [[46], [47], [48]]. Lower levels of these oxidized sugars suggest that they may serve as an alternative to glucose energy sources [34]. AFMU, a metabolite of xanthine [49], is a biomarker of N-acetyltransferase enzymes (NATs), which transfer acetyl groups from acetyl-CoA to xenobiotic metabolites [50]. Genetic variants of NAT, which are associated with slow acetylation of metabolites and greater oxidative stress [51], are also associated with increased susceptibility to asthma [50,52,53]. The finding of low AFMU is consistent with the slow acetylation phenotype of asthma and validates prior studies. Malonate is higher in asthma and may be generated by oxidative decarboxylation of oxaloacetate [54,55]. Malonate, a precursor for malonyl-CoA, is important in fatty acid synthesis [56], but can also act as a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase, a key enzyme in the TCA cycle and the ETC [[57], [58], [59]]. N6-methyladenosine is associated with greater asthma severity, as shown by positive associations with FENO and bronchodilator reactivity and inverse association with lung function. The methylation of adenosine, i.e. m6A mRNA methylation, is a common RNA modification [60,61] that regulates the translation of genes, including inflammatory and immune response genes [[62], [63], [64], [65]]. Abnormal level of m6A regulators have been previously reported in asthma [[66], [67], [68], [69], [70]]. Higher levels of m6A methylation are associated with asthma development and severity via inflammatory pathways [[62], [63], [64], [65],68,70,71]. The association between m6A level in blood with asthma severity highlights its potential as a clinical biomarker and/or perhaps a new therapeutic target to be studied in the future.

The current study population consists of well-controlled asthmatics without recent exacerbations, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to more severe disease. Likewise, some of the asthmatic participants were receiving inhaled bronchodilators and/or inhaled corticosteroids, which may also limit the findings. In severe asthma or during exacerbations, increased inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune activation are expected to increase energy demands. Under these conditions, metabolism may shift away from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation toward greater reliance on glycolysis to support rapid ATP production, similar to metabolic shifts observed in other high-demand states such as cancer. However, our findings show that asthma is associated with greater mitochondrial reserve capacity compared with controls, suggesting an enhanced ability to rapidly increase mitochondrial energy production when needed. This ability to augment oxidative phosphorylation under stress may represent a metabolic physiological adaptation in asthma. Similarly, the enrichment of fatty acids observed in the bioenergetic–metabolite network may support increased energy production, as lipids yield more ATP per molecule than glucose. Future studies in severe and exacerbating asthma will be important to determine whether these metabolic features are preserved across different levels of disease severity.

Network analyses integrating bioenergetic parameters and plasma metabolites identified a previously unrecognized and extensive reorganization of energy metabolism biased toward lipids in asthma. This reconfiguration of bioenergetic–metabolic interactions is consistent with broader metabolic remodeling associated with the disease. Altered lipid metabolism linked to bioenergetic function and inflammation has previously been implicated in asthma [72]. Asthma is associated with a higher prevalence of obesity and dyslipidemia [[9], [10], [11],63]. Patients with obesity-related asthma often exhibit a more severe phenotype characterized by poorer asthma control and reduced responsiveness to standard therapies. Obesity itself is associated with chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, hormonal dysregulation, and altered metabolic pathways that may exacerbate disease severity. Notably, the network structures observed in obese and non-obese individuals with asthma were similar to each other but different from controls. Thus, although BMI influences metabolic network features and disease severity, it is unlikely to be the primary driver of the bioenergetic–metabolic network remodeling observed in asthma.

Our findings establish a framework for considering whether reversing metabolic alterations could provide therapeutic benefit in asthma. Metformin, widely used in diabetes, is a promising candidate. Clinical studies link metformin use to lower asthma incidence [67,68] and reduced risk of exacerbations [[69], [70], [71]]. It acts via AMP-activated protein kinase activation, suppressing lipogenic enzymes [73,74], promoting fatty acid oxidation [75], and improving energy balance [76]. These effects decrease circulating lipid levels, lipotoxicity, and inflammation, supporting the idea that targeting lipid metabolism may benefit asthma. Despite these promising early reports, reversing metabolic abnormalities is challenging due to the complexity and adaptability of metabolic networks. Targeting a single pathway may trigger compensatory mechanisms that limit sustained correction. Future studies of metabolic interventions in asthma are needed to evaluate clinical effects on asthma severity and exacerbations as well as restructuring of the bioenergetic-metabolites network.

Our study has notable strengths, including comprehensive data on both metabolomics and bioenergetics in healthy controls and individuals with asthma. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report an integrated rewiring of bioenergetic and metabolomic networks in an asthmatic population. However, we acknowledge limitations. While platelets are a valuable surrogate for systemic mitochondrial function, they may not fully capture bioenergetic changes occurring in whole body metabolism or other cell types or tissues. ATP content was not available in these studies, which limits complete understanding of bioenergetic efficiencies. The bioenergetic–metabolite interactome was constructed using platelet bioenergetics and plasma metabolite data. While plasma metabolites provide a snapshot of systemic metabolism, they may not reflect cell-specific metabolic changes. However, the consistency of our healthy control interactome with previously published reports [21] strengthens confidence in the asthma findings. Data was collected at a single time point under fasting conditions. Given the dynamic nature of metabolism and its sensitivity to internal and external factors, these findings may not fully generalize to other physiological states, such as postprandial metabolism. Finally, our metabolomics approach relied on global profiling and did not include targeted or extended lipidomics. As a result, we have not included some lipid mediators involved in inflammation and immune regulation, which are components in asthma pathophysiology. It is also important to note that several of the metabolites were not identified due to technical limitations, but at least some of these are environmentally derived xenobiotics, as noted in our previous studies [21,22]. In future studies, it may be possible to identify environmental toxins which contribute uniquely to the metabolic networks associated with asthmatics. The network interactions are referred to as interactomes, but a direct link between metabolites and bioenergetic hubs is not established. Finally, our cohort primarily included individuals with well-controlled asthma. Future studies that include patients with frequent exacerbations could provide greater insight into how bioenergetic remodeling contributes to asthma heterogeneity and clinical outcomes.

Overall, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized bioenergetic phenotype in asthma, characterized by increased mitochondrial reserve capacity, reduced reliance on glucose, and a uniquely rewired bioenergetic–metabolic network with a strong dependence on lipids. The unique asthma network pattern may be useful to guide future studies investigating how metabolism mechanistically affects disease severity and potentially therapeutic outcomes.
