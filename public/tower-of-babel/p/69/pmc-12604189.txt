Detection of extracellular amyloid beta aggregates by an Aducanumab-based synNotch receptor: an in vitro proof-of-concept study
Authors: Nicholas J Bergo, Suckwon Lee, Cynthia J Siebrand, Zachary Mayeri, Julie K Andersen, Chaska C Walton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSynthetic Notch (synNotch) receptors are a powerful gene regulation platform that activate transcription in response to membrane-bound ligands and extracellular matrix components, with emerging applications in cancer, autoimmunity, and regenerative medicine. Whether synNotch can be adapted to detect and respond to extracellular neurotoxic protein aggregates—such as amyloid beta (Aβ), a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease (AD)—remains unknown.MethodsTo address this, we engineered an Aβ-responsive synNotch receptor (Adu-synNotch) by fusing the single-chain variable fragment (scFv) derived from Aducanumab (Aduhelm®), an FDA-approved anti-Aβ antibody, to the extracellular domain of synNotch. This construct was expressed in NIH 3T3 cells and paired with downstream reporters CLIP-tag, secreted Metridia luciferase (MetLuc), and synthetic expression cassettes encoding chimeric human-mouse versions of the therapeutic antibodies Lecanemab (Leqembi®) and Aducanumab. Cells were exposed to Aβ(1–42) aggregates, and synNotch activation was assessed via CLIP-tag imaging, MetLuc secretion assays, and immunocytochemistry for Lecanemab and Aducanumab.ResultsNIH 3T3 cells expressing Adu-synNotch responded to extracellular Aβ aggregates with robust induction of CLIP-tag and MetLuc reporters, along with secretion of chimeric Lecanemab and Aducanumab antibodies. Activation was dose-dependent and the secreted antibodies bound to Aβ aggregates, confirming that extracellular Aβ can serve as a functional synNotch ligand.ConclusionsThese findings establish that synNotch receptors can be engineered to detect and respond to pathological extracellular protein aggregates such as Aβ. Background

Recently, the FDA has approved three immunotherapies for Alzheimer’s disease (AD)—Aducanumab (Aduhelm®), Donanemab (Kisunla™), and Lecanemab (Leqembi®)—all targeting amyloid-beta (Aβ), offering a promising means to slow cognitive and functional decline associated with AD [1–4]. Although many additional therapeutics are currently being tested in clinical trials [5], the ability to halt the disease has not been achieved.

A mostly unexplored alternative for new interventions or improving existing ones in the AD field is synthetic biology. Synthetic modular receptors present an exciting opportunity for the development of novel targeted cell-based therapies [6]. A well-known example is the chimeric antigen receptor (CAR), used in cytotoxic CD8 T-cells and helper CD4 T-cells for cancer treatment [7]. CAR T-cells (CAR-T) link extracellular cues to native immunomodulatory functions via endogenous signaling cascades. There are additional synthetic receptors that can link extracellular cues to synthetic transcriptional programs using orthogonal signaling [6]. In this case, the receptors do not activate native cell functions. Instead, they allow for custom sense-and-respond programs that can trigger the expression of artificial genes. Now taken to clinical trials (NCT06186401), a well-established transcriptional switch receptor is the synthetic Notch receptor (synNotch) [6, 8–10].

One major application of synNotch receptors is to regulate the expression of CARs to enhance specificity and efficacy in CAR-T cell therapies [10–14]. Alternatively, synNotch can be engineered to create “sense-and-respond” biological drug factories. In cancer treatment research, synNotch receptors have been used to trigger the synthesis and secretion of therapeutic agents, such as antibodies or cytokines, in response to specific antigens on target cells [9, 15]. Recently, CD4⁺ T cells expressing synNotch receptors secreting interleukin-10 (IL-10) in response to the brain extracellular matrix protein brevican (BCAN) were shown to ameliorate pathology in a mouse model of multiple sclerosis (MS) [16]. Although still unexplored, the use of synNotch for precision delivery of therapeutic peptides directly to the brain of AD patients could be a promising approach to minimize the side effects of traditional bulk drug delivery methods, such as intravenous injection.

A key factor in designing synthetic receptors is their antigen-binding domain (ABD), which determines the antigen that activates the receptor [6, 7]. In cancer applications, the ABD of synNotch receptors mainly targets antigens present on the membrane of cells [6]. In contrast, AD is characterized by extracellular protein aggregates, particularly Aβ plaques [17–19]. A novel application of synNotch receptors would be to target these extracellular Aβ aggregates, making them ideal candidates for delivering Aβ-clearing antibodies like Lecanemab. However, synNotch receptors were originally shown not to be activated by soluble monomers unless tethered to the cell membrane and only recently has evidence shown that they can be activated by extracellular matrix proteins [6, 8, 16, 20]. Whether synNotch receptors can be activated by extracellular Aβ aggregates remains unknown. Gaining insight into this would be crucial for expanding synNotch applications to AD and other diseases involving similar extracellular aggregates.

To answer this, we present tests conducted in NIH 3T3 cells using a receptor system developed in our laboratory that integrates synNotch and CAR technology for cell-based drug delivery. This system includes an Aβ-targeting synNotch receptor based on Aducanumab (Adu-synNotch), designed to regulate the expression of murine versions of Lecanemab and Aducanumab. Our results demonstrate that Adu-synNotch is activated by extracellular Aβ aggregates, leading to the synthesis and secretion of the therapeutic antibodies.

Methods

Constructs

The Sleeping Beauty transposon-transposase system was used for experiments in which Adu-synNotch regulated the expression of chAducanumab Fab-Fc and CLIP-tag [21–23]. For chAducanumab Fab-Fc experiments, there was an additional expression cassette regulated by a human PGK promoter that included an Adu-CAR [24], a tagBFP reporter protein, and a BSD antibiotic resistance gene. The Adu-CAR gene was not tested in the present study and is part of a larger system being developed by our laboratory to provide clinical AD interventions [24]. The constructs were expressed from a pT4-HB transposon plasmid. pT4/HB was a gift from Wolfgang Uckert (Addgene, Plasmid #108352; http://n2t.net/addgene:108352; RRID: Addgene_108352) [25]. Expression cassettes were codon optimized for expression in mice, synthesized, subcloned, and sequence-verified by GeneScript. For CLIP-tag experiments, the constructs were expressed from a Sleeping Beauty transposon backbone provided by VectorBuilder. Expression cassettes were codon optimized for expression in mice, synthesized, subcloned, and sequence-verified by VectorBuilder. SP1-SB100X transposase was used for transposition. SP1-SB100X was a gift from Joseph Dougherty & Rob Mitra (Addgene, Plasmid #154887; http://n2t.net/addgene:154887; RRID: Addgene_154887) [26].

Lentiviral delivery was used to transduce Adu-synNotch and chAducanumab scFv-Fc or chLecanemab scFv-Fc. The synNotch receptor and payload (chAducanumab or chLecanemab plus MetLuc) were expressed from two separate third-generation self-inactivating (SIN) lentiviral vectors based on pLV[Exp]-EGFP/Puro-EF1A >mCherry transfer vector (VectorBuilder, VB010000-9298rtf). SynNotch expression was regulated by an EF1A promoter. Downstream of the synNotch receptor, there was a murine Foxp3 gene and a SNAP reporter protein. This vector included a second expression cassette regulating the expression of an Adu-CAR [24] and an mNeonGreen reporter protein separated by a T2A sequence. The murine Foxp3 and Adu-CAR genes were not tested in the present study and are part of a larger system being developed by our laboratory to provide clinical AD interventions [24]. Vectors were codon optimized for expression in mice, synthesized, subcloned, and sequence-verified by VectorBuilder.

Cell Culture, lipofection, and transduction

NIH/3T3 mouse fibroblast adherent cells (ATCC®, CRL-1658™) were cultured in DMEM (VWR, 45000-304) supplemented with 10% FBS (VWR, 45000-734) at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Sleeping Beauty. For chAducanumab experiments, cells were transfected with 0.6 µg of transposon carrying Adu-synNotch and chAducanumab Fab-Fc along with 0.06 µg of SP1-SB100X transposase (10:1 transposon: transposase ratio) using 1 µL Lipofectamine per 1 µg DNA. Transfected clones were selected with 10 µg/mL BSD (Invivogen, Ant-bl-05) for 7 days before freezing in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 5% DMSO. For CLIP-tag experiments, cells were transfected with 2 µg of transposon carrying Adu-synNotch, SNAP-tag, and CLIP-tag along with 0.2 µg of SP1-SB100X transposase (10:1 transposon: transposase ratio) using 3 µL Lipofectamine per 1 µg DNA. Transfected cells were sorted for SNAP + markers using a BD FACS Aria II cell sorter. Lentivirus. NIH/3T3 cells were seeded in Ibidi 8-well slides using the DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and incubated overnight. Cells were co-transduced at a 1000 MOI (by physical titer) with 5 µg/mL polybrene transduction enhancer (VectorBuilder, PL0001). After 24 h, transduction media was replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. 48 h post-transduction, cells were incubated with SNAP-cell SiR 647 substrate (NEB, S9102S) and Live Dead Fixable Violet (Invitrogen, L34955) per manufacturer instructions. SNAP+/mNeonGreen + cells were sorted using a BD FACS Aria II cell sorter, expanded, and re-sorted to ensure high purity before freezing in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 5% DMSO.

Aβ treatments

Amyloid beta: Beta amyloid [1–42] aggregation kit was purchased from rPeptide (rPeptide, A-1170-2). Lyophilized Aβ peptides were reconstituted in 5mM Tris or 10 mM NaOH at a concentration of 1 mg/ml and diluted with HPLC water and Tris-buffered saline (TBS) to obtain a 100 µM stock solution. Preparations to obtain solutions enriched in oligomer and solutions enriched in fibrils were done as previously described, with some modifications [27]. To obtain solutions enriched in oligomers or fibrils, the stock solution was incubated for 3 hours at 37 °C or for 7 days at 37 °C, respectively. For Aβ treatments, cell clones were thawed per the vendor’s instructions (ATCC®, CRL-1658™) and passaged prior to Aβ treatments. Approximately 3 × 103 (CLIP-tag experiments) or 6 × 104 (chAducanumab Fab-Fc experiments) cells were plated per well into treated µ-Slide 8 Well chambers (Ibidi, 80826) and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then incubated with Aβ preparations at the specified durations and concentrations. FITC-labeled Amyloid beta: Lyophilized FITC-labeled Aβ42 peptides (rPeptide, A-1119-1) were reconstituted in 50 µL DMSO (0.5 mg in 50 µL), diluted 1:10 in Neurobasal Plus culture medium (Gibco, A3582901) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 hours. VEH consisted of 50 µL of DMSO diluted in Neurobasal Plus medium (Gibco, A3582901). For AβO-FITC treatments, cell clones were thawed per the vendor’s instructions and passaged prior to Aβ treatments. Approximately 6 × 103 sorted double-positive SNAP and mNeonGreen NIH/3T3 clones were seeded per well into treated µ-Slide 8 Well chambers and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then incubated with AβO-FITC enriched preparations at the specified concentrations for 48 h, after which supernatants were collected for MetLuc reporter assay and the cells were fixed for ICC.

Immunofluorescence and microscopy

Immunostaining and confocal imaging were performed without removing cells from µ-Slide 8 Well chambers. For experiments involving CLIP- and/or SNAP-tag labeling, cells were stained with CLIP-Cell™ 505 (NEB, S9217S) and/or SNAP-Cell® 647-SiR (NEB, S9102S) prior to fixation, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For ICC, cells were fixed for 10 min with 4% paraformaldehyde (Santa Cruz, 3052589-4) at room temperature (RT), followed by three 10-minute washes with ice-cold PBS and incubated with 10% NGS (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 005-000-121) in PBS for 30 min at RT to block unspecific antibody binding. ICC was performed to stain extracellular proteins, so no permeabilization step was included after blocking. Cells were then incubated for 1 h at RT with primary antibodies in 10% NGS in PBS. After 4 washes with PBS, cells were incubated for 1 h at RT with secondary antibodies in 10% NGS in PBS. After 4 washes with PBS, DNA labeling was performed using PBS containing 1 µg/mL 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (1:10000) (Invitrogen, D1306). After 3 additional washes with PBS, PBS was completely replaced by pure glycerol (Invitrogen, 15514-011). For live imaging, antibodies were directly added to the media. Confocal images, tile scan images, and live tile scan images were obtained with the Zeiss LSM 780 Confocal Microscope using an EC Plan-Neofluar 20X/0.50 M27 objective or the Zeiss LSM 980 Confocal Microscope with a Plan-Apochromat 20X/0.8 M27 objective. Live cell imaging was performed in a sealed chamber at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Western blot

Standard (Bio-Rad, 1610376) and 6.8 µg of amyloid beta samples with fluorescent compatible sample buffer (Invitrogen, LC2570) were loaded into the 4–12% NuPAGE Bis-Tris Gel (Invitrogen, NP0321BOX). The gel was run with NuPAGE MES SDS running buffer (Invitrogen, NP0002) at 50 V for 30 min for stacking, and 100 V for separation. After the run, PVDF was activated with methanol for 1 min and rinsed with the 1x transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM Glycine, and 20% v/v methanol). The transfer was run at 25 V for 2 h. PVDF was washed with PBS/0.1% Tween 20 (PBST) (VWR, 0777–1 L) three times for 5 min. PVDF was blocked with 10% NGS in PBST for 1 h at RT and washed with PBST. Anti-amyloid beta 6E10 in PBST was incubated for 1 h at RT and washed 3 times with PBST. Secondary antibody anti-mouse IgG1 AF488 (1:3000) (Invitrogen, A-21121) was added and incubated for 1 h at RT and washed 3 times with PBST. The membrane was imaged by ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Antibodies

Primary antibodies: anti-amyloid fibril OC antibody (1:100, ICC) (Sigma-Aldrich, AB2286), anti-β-amyloid 1–16 6E10 (1:1000, WB) (Biolegend, 8030001). Secondary antibodies: anti-rabbit IgG AF488 (1:500, ICC) (Invitrogen, A-11008), anti-mouse IgG1 AF488 (1:3000, WB) (Invitrogen, A-21121), and anti-mouse IgG2a AF546 (1:500) (Invitrogen, A-21133) for chAducanumab and chLecanemab for ICC and live imaging. Conjugated antibodies: Alexa Fluor 594 conjugated anti-β-amyloid 1–16 6E10 (1:200) (Biolegend, 803019) was used for ICC. Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated to anti-β-amyloid 1–16 6E10 (1:200) (Biolegend, 803001) with 488 Conjugation kit (1:200) (Abcam, ab236553) performed per manufacturer instructions was used for live imaging.

MetLuc assay

The MetLuc reporter assay was performed using Ready-To-Glow™ Secreted Luciferase Reporter Assay (Clontech, 631728) per the manufacturer’s instructions in a white 96-well (flat-bottom) plate (Greiner Bio-One, 655074). Luciferase activity was measured using the BioTek Cytation3 multi-mode plate reader. Luminescence readings were recorded as relative light units (RLU).

Image analysis and statistical analysis

Videos and images were generated using ZEISS ZEN Microscopy Software (RRID: SCR_013672) and IMARIS software (RRID: SCR_007370). Image processing was performed on 3D images with IMARIS. To quantify voxel (volumetric pixel) signal intensity, 3D ROIs were generated using the surface function in IMARIS, with settings applied uniformly across replicates for all treatment conditions. 3D ROIs based on SNAP were generated to obtain SNAP-tag and CLIP-tag voxel signal intensity as well as transduction metrics for AβO-FITC experiments. 3D ROIs based on FITC were generated to obtain chLecanemab scFv-Fc and AβO-FITC voxel signal intensity. Voxel signal intensities captured by the 3D ROIs were exported into IBM SPSS Statistics (RRID: SCR_002865) for statistical analysis. Assumption tests were conducted for all analyses. Box plots were used to identify outliers, normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test (p > .05), and homogeneity of variances was tested using Levene’s test (p > .05). When assumptions were met, independent t-tests or ANOVA and Tukey multiple comparisons were performed. For lack of homogeneity of variances, Welch’s t-test or Welch’s F test with Games-Howell multiple comparisons were used. Non-parametric tests were performed for lack of normality. All pairwise comparisons were two-tailed.

Results

Engineering and evaluation of Aβ-targeting synNotch

We recently demonstrated that CARs can be engineered to recognize tau aggregates and specific subspecies of Aβ aggregates [24]. Among these constructs, a CAR incorporating the scFv derived from the variable light and heavy chains (VLC and VHC) of Aducanumab effectively detected both oligomer-enriched Aβ aggregates and the pyroglutamated Aβ variant Aβp3–42. This is consistent with Aducanumab’s comparatively broad binding specificity for Aβ aggregate species, relative to other Aβ-targeting antibodies [1, 4, 28]. To explore whether synNotch receptors can be activated by Aβ, we fused the same Aducanumab-derived scFv from the previously described CAR (Adu-CAR) to a synNotch receptor, generating Adu-synNotch (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a) [24]. The synNotch design included the mouse Notch1 core and the Gal4VP64 artificial transcription factor (ATF) [8, 9]. The Gal4 sequence functions as the DNA-binding domain and VP64 serves as the transcriptional activator, as previously described. To enable identification of transfected cells, a SNAP-tag fused to a nuclear localization signal (NLS) was included in the construct (Fig. 1a). The SNAP-tag is a self-labeling protein tag derived from a modified version of the human O6-alkylguanine-DNA alkyltransferase (hAGT), which covalently binds to benzylguanine-conjugated substrates. This allows for flexible, specific, and covalent labeling of SNAP-tagged proteins using a variety of fluorescent or affinity-based reagents. Adu-synNotch and SNAP-tag were separated by a Thosea asigna virus 2A (T2A) peptide sequence with an N-terminal Furin cleavage site and a GSG linker upstream of the T2A sequence to minimize residual amino acids remaining on the C-terminus of Adu-synNotch following ribosomal skipping at the T2A site [29–31]. The synNotch system incorporates an inducible synthetic expression cassette that is transcriptionally activated by receptor engagement [6, 8, 9]. This design creates a closed loop signaling architecture, in which activation of the synNotch receptor leads exclusively to the transcription of synthetic genes without engaging endogenous signaling pathways, thereby rendering the system orthogonal to native cellular signaling. Upon ligand binding, the synNotch receptor undergoes stepwise proteolytic cleavage by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase (ADAM) and γ-secretases, resulting in the release of the membrane-tethered ATF. The Gal4 DNA-binding domain of the Gal4VP64 ATF targets four tandem upstream activating sequences (Gal4UAS) positioned upstream of a minimal cytomegalovirus (mCMV) promoter (Gal4UASmCMV), thereby initiating transcription of the inducible gene. For these experiments, the inducible transgene encoded a CLIP-tag with an N-terminal NLS (Fig. 1b). The CLIP-tag is another variant of the human hAGT enzyme engineered to covalently bind to benzylcytosine-derivatized fluorescent dyes or affinity labels.

We employed the Sleeping Beauty transposon (pT4/HB)–transposase (SB100X) system, which enables stable genomic integration [21–23, 25, 26]. The transposon plasmid carried two expression cassettes (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The first was the inducible Gal4UASmCMV promoter driving expression of nuclear-localized CLIP-tag. The second was the constitutive bicistronic expression cassette, positioned 3′ of the inducible unit, encoding both the Adu-synNotch receptor and SNAP-tag reporter. In this configuration, nuclear SNAP-tag fluorescence served as a proxy for vector integration of the inducible and constitutive expression cassettes. Accordingly, in addition to the mean CLIP-tag signal, the ratio of nuclear CLIP-tag to nuclear SNAP-tag fluorescence could be calculated to provide an integration-normalized readout of synNotch activation in response to ligand stimulation (Fig. 1c).

To evaluate the responsiveness of Adu-synNotch to Aβ aggregates, we adapted protocols previously developed in our laboratory for assessing Aβ-targeting CARs [24]. Unlike conventional in vitro CAR and synNotch assays, which typically involve co-culture systems where sender cells express a membrane-bound ligand and responder cells express the receptor of interest (e.g. synNotch or CAR), Aβ aggregates are not membrane-associated. Instead, Aβ peptides are cleaved from the membrane-bound amyloid precursor protein (APP) and subsequently aggregate in the extracellular space—a process that contributes to the formation of amyloid plaques, a pathological hallmark of AD [32]. As a result, CAR or synNotch receptors designed to target Aβ must recognize extracellular aggregates. To accommodate this distinction, we employed a modified assay in which receptor-expressing cells were first plated, followed by the addition of pre-aggregated Aβ directly into the culture medium (Fig. 1d). The goal of the present study was not to evaluate a therapeutic intervention, but rather to determine whether synNotch receptors can be activated by extracellular Aβ aggregates for future applications. As previously discussed, synNotch receptors are orthogonal signaling systems designed to operate independently of endogenous cellular pathways, and thus their function is expected to be largely cell-type-independent [8, 9]. Consequently, we selected NIH 3T3 fibroblasts for these experiments due to their high transduction efficiency, robust growth, and ease of handling—characteristics that make them well-suited for assessing receptor functionality. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) was used to enrich NIH 3T3 cell clones expressing the Adu-synNotch receptor system. NIH 3T3 cells expressing either the Adu-synNotch construct or control constructs were treated with 5 µM oligomer-enriched Aβ(1−42) aggregates (AβO) or vehicle (VEH) for 48 h (Fig. 1d). Following treatment, cells were imaged for analysis.

As expected, confocal imaging revealed that untransduced cells, identified by DAPI staining, were negative for SNAP-tag signal (Supplementary Fig. 1c). However, CLIP-tag exhibited occasional non-specific signal localized to Aβ-like aggregates and what were likely autofluorescent dead cells (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). This background signal is likely due to spectral overlap between the emission wavelengths of the benzylcytosine-derivatized fluorescent CLIP-tag substrate (532 nm) and the autofluorescence of Aβ and cellular debris. Cells expressing Adu-synNotch displayed robust SNAP-tag expression (Fig. 1e). Notably, SNAP-tag signal did not exhibit non-specific signal on Aβ-like aggregates. Consistent with the known cytotoxicity of Aβ [24, 33], a higher number of SNAP-positive cells was observed under VEH-treated conditions compared to AβO-treated cells (Fig. 1e). Among the VEH-treated group, transduced cells showed varying levels of CLIP-tag expression (white arrows; DAPI⁺/SNAP⁺/CLIP⁺), consistent with ligand-independent activation (LIA)—a known characteristic of synNotch receptors [8, 9, 34, 35]. Nevertheless, as per visual inspection, most VEH-treated transduced cells remained negative for CLIP-tag (yellow arrows; DAPI⁺/SNAP⁺/CLIP⁻). In contrast, AβO-treated cells exhibited both stronger and more frequent CLIP-tag expression relative to VEH-treated controls (Fig. 1e). This was evidenced by an increased proportion of DAPI⁺/SNAP⁺/CLIP⁺ cells (white arrows) compared to DAPI⁺/SNAP⁺/CLIP⁻ cells (yellow arrows; Fig. 1e), consistent with ligand-dependent activation (LDA) of the Adu-synNotch system in response to Aβ aggregates.

For the quantification and statistical analyses of Adu-synNotch activation, we generated three-dimensional regions of interest (ROIs) based on the SNAP channel using the 3D image processing software IMARIS. As noted, the SNAP channel did not present the background signal evidenced in the CLIP and DAPI channels in untransduced cells, therein allowing the generation of 3D ROIs to accurately measure the intensity of SNAP and CLIP signal (Supplementary Fig. 1e). First, we assessed whether there were differences in the transduced cell count (SNAP-tag positive) between treatments. The analysis confirmed there were significantly fewer transduced cells under AβO treatments relative to VEH treatments, t (4) = 5.2234, p =.0064 (Fig. 1f). Thus, despite the same number of cells being plated, fewer cells were present by the end of Aβ incubations, which is consistent with the above-mentioned Aβ-induced toxicity [24, 33]. The mean SNAP signal intensity—a proxy for average transgene copy number—was also significantly reduced in AβO-treated cells compared to VEH, Welch’s t-test(2.0321) = 5.1932, p =.034 (Fig. 1g). This potentially suggested that cells with higher transgene expression may be more susceptible to Aβ toxicity. Despite these reductions in SNAP signal and viable transduced cell numbers, CLIP-tag signal intensity—the measure of Adu-synNotch activation—was significantly elevated under AβO treatment compared to VEH, t (4) = -4.0604, p =.0153 (Fig. 1h). To further verify receptor activation, we quantified the mean CLIP-tag to mean SNAP-tag fluorescence ratio in transduced cells. This measure normalizes Adu-synNotch activation (CLIP-tag) to Adu-synNotch gene copy-number (SNAP-tag). The ratio was significantly increased under AβO treatment relative to VEH, t (4) = -6.2091, p =.0034 (Fig. 1i). Together, these results indicate that despite reduced cell viability and transgene expression (Figs. 1f, g), Adu-synNotch-expressing cells exhibited a robust response to 5 µM AβO (Figs. 1h, i). Nevertheless, as seen in the images (Fig. 1e), there was still LIA detectable in VEH-treated cells (Figs. 1h, i), once again consistent with known basal “leaky” synNotch activation [8, 9, 34, 35].

Engineering and evaluation of Aβ-targeting antibody formats under Adu-synNotch regulation

The results above indicate that CLIP-tag is synthesized in response to Adu-synNotch activation by Aβ. In a clinically relevant system, however, CLIP-tag would be replaced with a coding sequence for an amino acid-based therapeutic. Our laboratory is currently developing a receptor system for an immune cell–based therapeutic platform named smart cell delivery (SmaCD), which combines synNotch and CAR receptors for the targeted delivery of amino acid-based drugs such as Aβ-clearing antibodies. SmaCD includes the Adu-synNotch receptor tested in the CLIP-tag experiments and the above-mentioned Adu-CAR [24], and a murine Foxp3 gene (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Both the synNotch and CAR constructs incorporate the same Aducanumab-derived scFv. Although the CAR should not have an effect in NIH-3T3 cells, when expressed in T cells the Adu-CAR component serves to guide and enrich the cells co-expressing Adu-synNotch at sites of Aβ pathology. Otherwise, T cells without the CAR would have reduced capacity to enable concentrated, localized delivery of the therapeutic payload regulated by the synNotch receptor. Thus, since synNotch receptors alone cannot mediate cell localization or retention at the target, the CAR is essential for this enrichment. Murine Foxp3 is included to stabilize a regulatory T cell (Treg) phenotype, which defines the immune cell platform for SmaCD interventions. Tregs are a specialized subset of CD4⁺ T cells with immunosuppressive functions essential for maintaining immune tolerance, preventing autoimmunity, and controlling chronic inflammation [36]. Unlike cytotoxic CAR-T for cancer, CAR-Tregs are arguably the safer T-cell platform to deliver amino acid-based drugs like antibodies for neurodegenerative diseases like AD.

As noted, for the development of SmaCD we use suitable cell lines to confirm the function of the receptors [24]. Accordingly, for the next experiments the NIH 3T3 cells are transduced with the additional components of the SmaCD system (Adu-synNotch, Adu-CAR, and Foxp3), but only the function of the Adu-synNotch receptor is assessed. We have already validated the functionality of Adu-CAR in DO11.10 CD4 hybridoma cells, and the role of Foxp3 in inducing a Treg-like state in primary murine CD4 T cells is well established [24, 37, 38]. It is important to note that synNotch signaling is orthogonal to CAR signaling, and, to the best of our knowledge, NIH 3T3 cells lack the T cell receptor (TCR) signaling machinery necessary for CD3ζ-mediated CAR activation [7, 8, 10].

For the next experiments we used the same Adu-synNotch receptor as for CLIP-tag experiments but without the SNAP-tag (Fig. 2a). Several antibodies and formats were screened as therapeutic payloads to be regulated by Adu-synNotch receptors. For our initial tests, we expressed a full-length human-mouse chimeric Aducanumab (chAducanumab). Aducanumab itself is a human IgG1 monoclonal antibody isolated from cognitively normal healthy aged individuals with selectivity for AβO [4]. chAducanumab consists of a fragment antigen-binding (Fab) region formed by the variable regions of human Aducanumab fused to mouse constant chains and a fragment crystallizable (Fc) region based on mouse IgG2a [4]. To recapitulate the standard antibody architecture, the chimeric heavy and light chains were encoded on the same vector, with the heavy chain positioned 5′ of an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) and the light chain 3′ of the IRES (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 2b). To enable antibody secretion, we incorporated the secrecon peptide as the N-terminal secretory signal sequence [39]. Secrecon is a synthetic leader peptide optimized for efficient secretion of recombinant proteins in mammalian cells. In this system, detection of Aβ aggregates by the Adu-synNotch receptor is designed to trigger the synthesis and secretion of chAducanumab, which in turn should bind to Aβ aggregates (Fig. 2c).

As noted, we evaluated Adu-synNotch as a component of the SmaCD receptor system under development in our laboratory. The Adu-synNotch receptor and the inducible chAducanumab Fab-Fc cassette were integrated into the SB transposon plasmid (pT4/HB) with the addition of a third expression cassette encoding the Adu-CAR, an enhanced monomeric blue fluorescent reporter protein (tagBFP), and a Blasticidin (BSD) resistance gene (Supplementary Fig. 2c). The tagBFP is a blue fluorescent reporter, used to identify transduced cells, and BSD is an antibiotic resistance gene, used to enrich transduced BSD-resistant cell clones [40]. As indicated in the text, for some experiments we used a control plasmid lacking the Adu-synNotch system that included only the Adu-CAR, tagBFP, and BSD (Supplementary Fig. 2d). To assess Adu-synNotch activation in response to Aβ, we added mixed Aβ(1−42) aggregate preparations (Aβagg) to the plated cells (Fig. 2d). These consisted of mixed AβO 0.1 µM plus Aβ fibrils (AβF) 0.5 µM enriched preparations, obtained from incubating Aβ(1−42) for 3 h or 7 days, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2e).

We conducted time-lapse imaging experiments to monitor the production and secretion of chAducanumab in live cells. As noted, the Fc domain of chAducanumab was derived from mouse IgG2a (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 2b), enabling detection with a monoclonal antibody specific to mouse IgG2a (Fig. 2d). Importantly, no permeabilization step was performed, ensuring that only extracellular epitopes were accessible to mouse IgG2a antibody. As expected, time-lapse videos initiated 2 h after addition of the Aβagg cocktail showed no production of chAducanumab by untransduced cells (Video 1 transmitted light plus chAducanumab; Video 2 tagBFP plus chAducanumab) or NIH 3T3 cells transduced with the Adu-CAR construct lacking the Adu-synNotch system (Fig. 2e; Video 3 transmitted light plus chAducanumab; Video 4 tagBFP plus chAducanumab). In contrast, cells expressing Adu-synNotch exhibited a clear, time-dependent increase in the IgG2a signal, consistent with Aβ-dependent induction of antibody secretion (Fig. 2e; Video 5 transmitted light plus chAducanumab; Video 6 tagBFP plus chAducanumab). The IgG2a signal appeared as structures of varying sizes with the characteristic morphology of in vitro Aβ aggregates (Fig. 2e; Videos 5, 6).

To further validate chAducanumab localization on Aβ aggregates, we included both the mouse IgG2a secondary antibody and a fluorescently conjugated pan-Aβ antibody (6E10) to assess colocalization. Both antibodies were added directly to live cells. Time-lapse imaging was initiated 6 h after treatment with Aβagg and antibody addition. As expected, 6E10-labeled Aβ aggregates were observed in untransduced cells (Video 7), as well as in cells expressing Adu-CAR (Video 8) and Adu-synNotch (Video 9; Fig. 2f). TagBFP served as a marker for transduced cells. Adu-CAR–expressing cells (Video 8) displayed stronger and more abundant tagBFP signal than Adu-synNotch–expressing cells (Video 9; Fig. 2f), which is consistent with the larger size of the Adu-synNotch construct affecting transfection and or genomic integration efficiency. Occasional faint non-specific tagBFP signal was detected in untransduced cells, reflecting the autofluorescence of Aβ aggregates, as shown by colocalization between tagBFP and the Aβ antibody 6E10 (Video 7; Fig. 2f). Neither untransduced cells (Video 10) nor Adu-CAR–expressing cells (Video 11) exhibited detectable chAducanumab signal (Fig. 2f). In contrast, Adu-synNotch–expressing cells showed robust chAducanumab signal (Video 12), which colocalized with 6E10-labeled Aβ aggregates (Fig. 2f; Videos 13 and 14), confirming synNotch-mediated production and binding of chAducanumab to pathological Aβ.

We next performed immunostaining with the OC antibody to detect fibrillar Aβ aggregates and assess their colocalization with chAducanumab in cells expressing either Adu-synNotch or Adu-CAR alone, following treatment with Aβagg. OC specifically recognizes fibrillar Aβ species [41]. Two days after Aβagg treatment, a secondary anti-mouse IgG2a antibody was applied to live cells to detect secreted chAducanumab. After an additional two days, cells were fixed for immunocytochemistry (ICC) with the OC antibody. As expected, OC staining confirmed the presence of Aβ aggregates in the samples of Adu-CAR-only and Adu-synNotch–expressing cells that had been treated with Aβagg (Fig. 2g). OC labeling sometimes exhibited shell-like patterns around Aβ deposits, a phenomenon we have observed previously with multiple Aβ antibodies [42]. In contrast, IgG2a signal—indicating the presence of chAducanumab—was detected only on Aβ aggregates in Adu-synNotch experiments (Fig. 2g). Interestingly, colocalization between OC and chAducanumab was not always observed. This is consistent with the known specificities of the antibodies: OC preferentially binds fibrillar Aβ, while Aducanumab exhibits broader specificity [1, 4, 28, 41]. Additionally, because chAducanumab was being produced in situ during Aβ aggregation, and the anti-IgG2a secondary antibody was applied to live cells prior to fixation and OC staining, chAducanumab may have had earlier or enhanced access to epitopes that were later masked or structurally altered by fixation. Regardless, combined, these results demonstrate that chAducanumab expressed in the standard Fab-Fc format accumulates over time in response to Aβ-induced activation of Adu-synNotch and colocalizes with Aβ-specific antibodies such as 6E10 and OC.

A major limitation in the engineering of synthetic receptor systems for therapeutic applications is the delivery of large nucleic acid constructs into primary cells. Switch-like receptor systems such as synNotch are inherently large, and expanding their functionality with additional elements—such as CARs, reporters, or regulatory components—further increases vector size. Larger constructs significantly reduce transfection and transduction efficiencies, thereby limiting the number of successfully engineered cells available for adoptive cell transfer (ACT). This presents a critical challenge when working with primary T cells commonly used for ACT, which have limited capacity for clonal expansion. The SB system for the full-length chAducanumab Fab-Fc was advantageous to transfect the large constructs in NIH-3T3 cells that could be later expanded, but efficiencies were too low to engineer primary cells for ACT (data not shown). The chAducanumab Fab-Fc construct we initially generated is relatively large because it includes an IRES for bicistronic expression as well as the constant chains of the Fab (Fig. 2b). We therefore generated a more compact chAducanumab scFv-Fc variant (Fig. 2h; Supplementary Fig. 2f). In this design, the VL and VH domains are joined by a G4S(3) linker to form an scFv identical to that used in the Adu-synNotch receptor. This scFv is directly fused to the hinge and CH2 domain of mouse IgG2a, omitting the IRES and the complete constant regions of both the light and heavy chains thereby reducing its size. We also generated a chimeric antibody based on Lecanemab (chLecanemab; Fig. 2i; Supplementary Fig. 2g). Lecanemab is currently preferred over Aducanumab due to its improved clinical outcomes [1]. It is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody specifically developed to target Aβ protofibrils [1, 2]. We cloned the variable regions of Lecanemab into the same scFv-Fc architecture used for chAducanumab, yielding a chimeric human-mouse scFv-Fc construct fused to the mouse IgG2a backbone. Instead of a single SB plasmid for transfection, we reduced the sizes of the expression cassettes and used two self-inactivating lentiviral vectors (SIN-LV) to co-transduce the components of the SmaCD system (Supplementary Fig. 2h, i). For the detection of co-transduced cells, the vector containing Adu-synNotch included a SNAP-tag (Supplementary Fig. 2h) and the vector containing the Gal4UASmCMV regulating the Aβ-clearing antibodies contained a constitutively expressed mNeonGreen reporter (Supplementary Fig. 2i).

To assess this Adu-synNotch system we followed the same Aβ treatments as above (Fig. 2d) but used fluorescein (FITC)-labeled Aβ(1–42) (Aβ-FITC). Aβ-FITC was incubated at 37 °C for 3 h to generate oligomer-enriched preparations (AβO-FITC). These preparations, at a final concentration of 5 µM, or VEH, were added to the culture media of NIH 3T3 cells and incubated for 48 h prior to fixation for ICC. Cell nuclei were labeled with DAPI, and chAducanumab or chLecanemab were detected using the monoclonal anti-mouse IgG2a antibody. In VEH-treated cells, confocal microscopy revealed no detectable signal for Aβ-FITC, chAducanumab scFv-Fc, or chLecanemab scFv-Fc (Fig. 2j, k). In contrast, both chAducanumab scFv-Fc (Fig. 2j) and chLecanemab scFv-Fc (Fig. 2k) colocalized with Aβ-FITC aggregates following treatment with 5 µM AβO-FITC. As Aβ-FITC was added extracellularly and no permeabilization step was performed during ICC, IgG2a staining that colocalized with FITC reflects chLecanemab/chAducanumab bound to extracellular Aβ-FITC aggregates. Taken together, these results shows that the Adu-synNotch system drives the production of chAducanumab Fab-Fc, chAducanumab scFv-Fc, and chLecanemab scFv-Fc in response to Aβ stimulation.

For the chAducanumab-regulated Adu-synNotch construct, in-frame MetLuc secreted into the media differed significantly across treatment conditions, Welch’s F-test (3, 3.9052) = 245.2400, p <.0001 (Fig. 3d). Games-Howell post hoc comparisons revealed no significant difference in MetLuc relative light units (RLU) between VEH and 0.05 µM AβO-FITC treatments (p =.352), but both were significantly lower than 0.5 µM and 5 µM AβO-FITC treatment conditions (p =.005; Fig. 3d). Additionally, MetLuc at 0.5 µM AβO-FITC was significantly lower than at 5 µM (p =.006; Fig. 3d). Similarly, for the chLecanemab-regulated Adu-synNotch construct, MetLuc luminescence also varied significantly with treatment, F (3, 8) = 179.4442, p <.0001 (Fig. 3e). Tukey’s multiple comparisons revealed the pattern of differences mirrored that observed for the chAducanumab construct: VEH and 0.05 µM AβO-FITC conditions did not differ (p =.9991), but both were significantly lower than 0.5 µM and 5 µM AβO-FITC treatments (p ≤.0001; Fig. 3e). RLU values at 0.5 µM were also significantly lower than those at 5 µM (p =.0001; Fig. 3e). Thus, as expected, both Adu-synNotch receptor systems similarly responded to AβO-FITC treatments.

For confocal image analyses, nuclear SNAP was used to identify transduced cells, DAPI to label cell nuclei, FITC to detect Aβ, and a secondary anti-mouse IgG2a antibody to visualize chAducanumab and chLecanemab. As expected, control-untransduced cells did not show SNAP-positive cells nor IgG2a-positive aggregates (Fig. 3f). In contrast, chAducanumab (Fig. 3g) and chLecanemab-expressing clones (Fig. 3h) presented SNAP-positive populations. IgG2a signal in chAducanumab-expressing cells was faint, but present (Fig. 3g, see expanded view of AβO-FITC 5 µM treatments in yellow boxes). The signal for chLecanemab-expressing clones was visibly stronger (Fig. 3h, see expanded view of AβO-FITC 5 µM treatments). Stronger chLecanemab than chAducanumab signal on Aβ-FITC aggregates replicated the observations seen when using the same vectors without MetLuc (Figs. 2j, k).

To assess relative antibody production, the FITC channel was used to isolate 3D ROIs. This ensured the quantification of signal intensities for FITC, chAducanumab, and chLecanemab on Aβ aggregates. Signal values from each channel were normalized to their respective means replicate-by-replicate and the resulting values used for statistical comparisons. Although VEH treatments are shown in the bar graphs, the FITC-based algorithms used to generate ROIs in the rest of the treatment conditions did not produce ROI in VEH due to the lack of Aβ-FITC. Consequently, their values were zero and were not included in the statistical analysis.

As expected, for chAducanumab scFv-Fc–expressing cells, average-normalized total FITC signal increased significantly with treatment concentration, F (2, 6) = 26.208, p =.0011 (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The average-normalized mean IgG2a signal, reflecting mean chAducanumab levels within FITC-based ROIs, also differed significantly across treatment conditions, F (2, 6) = 29.8657, p =.0008 (Fig. 3i). Post hoc analysis showed that the signal in 5 µM AβO-FITC–treated cells was significantly higher than in both 0.05 µM (p =.0013) and 0.5 µM (p =.0013) treatment groups (Fig. 3i). However, no significant difference was observed between the 0.05 µM and 0.5 µM conditions (p =.9994; Fig. 3i). To account for differences in total FITC signal between treatment conditions, we also calculated the ratio of summed IgG2a (chAducanumab) to FITC signal, serving as a normalized metric of antibody binding relative to aggregate load. This ratio also differed significantly across treatment groups, F (2, 6) = 28.3339, p =.0009 (Fig. 3j). The pattern of statistical differences was consistent with the mean chAducanumab analysis: 5 µM AβO-FITC treatments showed significantly higher ratios compared to both 0.05 µM and 0.5 µM (p =.0015), while no difference was observed between the lower concentrations (p =.9993; Fig. 3j). Thus, as expected, the pattern of statistical differences in this ratio (Fig. 3j) mirrored that observed for the mean chAducanumab signal (Fig. 3i). Both metrics normalize total signal by FITC-positive voxels: the mean signal divides by the voxel count within each ROI, whereas the ratio divides by FITC signal intensity in the same voxels. Treatment differences were also significant for the average-normalized sum IgG2a signal, representing total chAducanumab associated with Aβ-FITC aggregates, F (2, 6) = 690.4793, p <.0001 (Fig. 3k). Consistent with the other metrics, 5 µM AβO-FITC treatments resulted in significantly higher total chAducanumab signal compared to both 0.05 µM and 0.5 µM treatments (p <.0001), with no significant difference between the latter two (p =.1578; Fig. 3k).

We additionally generated 3D ROIs based on the SNAP channel to assess the number of transduced cells and their relative copy-number integration. For chAducanumab-expressing clones, there were no statistically significant differences in the average-normalized number of SNAP-positive cells across treatment conditions, χ² (3) = 2.6923, p =.442 (Supplementary Fig. 3c). An omnibus test revealed significant differences in the mean SNAP signal per ROI, a proxy for average copy-number integration per transduced cell, across treatment conditions (F (3, 8) = 4.2611, p =.0449; Supplementary Fig. 3d). However, after performing family-wise error-corrected multiple comparisons using Tukey’s post hoc test, no differences between treatments were detected (p ≥.0512; Supplementary Fig. 3d). These findings contrast with the results observed for the CLIP-tag–expressing Adu-synNotch construct (Figs. 1f, g), potentially due to greater cytotoxicity of the AβO preparations used in those experiments compared to the AβO-FITC preparations used here.

For chLecanemab scFv-Fc–expressing cells, average-normalized total FITC signal increased significantly with treatment concentration, F (2, 6) = 2594.3563, p <.0001 (Supplementary Fig. 3e). The average-normalized mean IgG2a signal, reflecting mean chLecanemab levels within FITC-based ROIs, also differed significantly across treatment conditions, Welch’s F-test(2, 2.8069) = 124.800, p =.002 (Fig. 3l). Cells treated with 5 µM AβO-FITC showed significantly higher signal than those treated with 0.05 µM (p =.022; Fig. 3l). Interestingly, the 0.5 µM AβO-FITC condition yielded significantly higher signal than both 0.05 µM (p =.006) and 5 µM (p =.007; Fig. 3l). Likewise, the ratio of chLecanemab to FITC signal, used to control for differences in total FITC signal between conditions, also varied significantly across groups, Welch’s F-test(2, 2.8393) = 135.7400, p =.002 (Fig. 3m). The AβO-FITC 5 µM condition showed a significantly higher ratio than 0.05 µM (p =.02), while 0.5 µM was significantly higher than both (p =.005; Fig. 3m). Thus, for both the mean signal and the ratio to FITC signal, chLecanemab at the highest AβO-FITC concentration was lower than the intermediate concentration, potentially indicating that at 0.5 µM chLecanemab production is sufficient to saturate Aβ aggregates with the remaining antibody remaining soluble in the media. In contrast, despite a higher total antibody production for AβO-FITC 5 µM, the available chLecanemab may be insufficient to saturate the Aβ aggregates, resulting in reduced mean signal intensity. This is supported by higher MetLuc production in the media at AβO-FITC 5 µM than 0.5 µM (Fig. 3e). In fit, the assessment of total antibody production revealed significant differences between treatment conditions, F (2, 6) = 282.0304, p <.0001 (Fig. 3n). The 0.05 µM treatment group had significantly lower total chLecanemab signal than both 0.5 µM (p =.0457) and 5 µM (p <.0001) conditions (Fig. 3n). In contrast to the mean and ratio analyses, the 5 µM treatment yielded significantly higher total chLecanemab signal than 0.5 µM (p <.0001), indicating that more antibody was produced overall at the highest concentration (Fig. 3n). In summary, while the 0.5 µM treatment yielded the highest average chLecanemab signal per Aβ-FITC aggregate, the 5 µM condition resulted in the greatest total antibody production and binding.

As per the analysis of the number of SNAP-positive cells, for chLecanemab-expressing clones, a significant difference was observed, F (3, 8) = 4.1668, p =.0472 (Supplementary Fig. 3f), driven by a significant increase in SNAP-positive cell counts between VEH and 0.05 µM AβO-FITC treatment (p =.0373). No other pairwise differences were detected (p ≥.1491; Supplementary Fig. 3f). The observed increase in SNAP-positive cells at 0.05 µM is unlikely to account for antibody production trends, as both the 0.5 µM and 5 µM groups showed significantly higher chLecanemab output. There were also significant differences in the average-normalized mean SNAP values (F (3, 8) = 60.1779, p <.0001 Supplementary Fig. 3g), where AβO-FITC 5 µM treatments were lower than the rest of the treatment conditions (p <.0001). While this reduction in mean SNAP signal may have impacted antibody production per cell, total chLecanemab levels at 5 µM remained significantly elevated (Fig. 3n), indicating that overall output was maintained despite the lower per-cell expression.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that synNotch receptors can detect and respond to extracellular Aβ aggregates, thereby broadening the scope of synthetic receptor applications beyond membrane-bound targets and extracellular matrix proteins [6, 8–10, 16]. The engineered Adu-synNotch system triggered the expression of CLIP-tag and secretion of MetLuc as well as chAducanumab in Fab-Fc and scFv-Fc formats and chLecanemab in scFv-Fc format. The latter antibodies are chimeric human-mouse versions of the FDA-approved Aβ-clearing antibodies Aducanumab (Aduhelm) and Lecanemab (Leqembi). Thus, our work presents a synNotch receptor system potentially capable of delivering Aβ-clearing antibodies within nanometers of their amyloid targets.

Limitations and future directions

Several important limitations, along with considerations for future work, apply to these receptors and to similar cell-based, switch-like receptor systems designed as drug delivery vehicles for AD interventions.

Cell Type

All experiments in this study were performed in NIH 3T3 fibroblasts, which serve as a convenient platform for receptor testing but are not suitable for therapeutic applications in vivo. Although synNotch function is generally considered to be largely cell type–independent [8], potential therapeutic platforms such as primary T-cell subsets, microglia, or peripheral macrophages may introduce variables that are not captured in NIH 3T3 cells. While the present work provides an initial validation of Adu-synNotch receptor function, future studies should evaluate the system directly in the intended therapeutic cell types.

Antibody production and quantification

In some experiments, antibody and MetLuc secretion induced by Adu-synNotch was evaluated in cells that also expressed additional constructs (e.g., CAR), which we disclose here for full transparency. This design was intentional, as it allowed us to assess system performance within the broader gene expression context envisioned for future in vivo interventions. Nevertheless, although synNotch signaling is designed to be orthogonal [8], future studies should explicitly assess antibody production in the absence of additional receptors to rule out any potential influence.

Ligand-independent activation or LIA

In this study, we describe the presence of LIA, a phenomenon also reported by others [8, 9, 34, 35]. Further work will therefore be required to minimize off-target activation of Adu-synNotch. In addition, cell-based delivery differs fundamentally from conventional administration in ways that can be leveraged to mitigate LIA. For example, in conventional systemically delivered antibody therapies, epitopes for the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) are incorporated into the Fc domain to stabilize antibodies in circulation [43], thereby maximizing the fraction that reaches the target. In cell-based systems, the opposite strategy may be advantageous. Antibodies could be engineered to be unstable so that any off-target secretion in the blood is rapidly cleared, while local secretion within the brain remains unaffected. Thus, future work could be directed toward exploring engineering payloads such as Lecanemab with reduced systemic stability as a complementary means of improving targeting efficiency.

Alternative synthetic receptor systems

Previous work has shown that synNotch receptors can also be triggered by Myc antibodies immobilized on culture wells through recognition of a Myc-tag on the antigen-binding domain of the receptor [8], and by scFvs targeting brain extracellular matrix proteins [16]. To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first evidence that synNotch receptors can be activated by extracellular aggregates. However, the precise requirements for synNotch activation are not fully defined and may depend on factors such as the number of epitopes presented per ligand and whether the ligand is immobilized. In the context of Aβ, it is therefore possible that lower-order aggregates may fail to activate synNotch receptors.

Several alternatives exist [6], each with distinct advantages and limitations. Building on synNotch as a template, others have developed synthetic intramembrane proteolysis receptors (SNIPRs) that are capable of responding to soluble monomers [34]. The scFv used in the Adu-synNotch receptor described here could, in principle, also enable SNIPR activation by soluble Aβ monomers. However, at least in part, SNIPR function depends on endosomal internalization of the ligand [44], which in the case of Aβ would require cellular uptake of cytotoxic species that could affect the function of the therapeutic cell platform.

Another example alternative is T cells Redirected for Universal Cytokine-mediated Killing (TRUCK) receptors, also known as armored CARs or fourth-generation CARs [45–48]. TRUCKs couple a CAR to a nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT)-responsive expression cassette driven by a minimal promoter, thereby linking CAR activation to regulated payload synthesis. CAR signaling requires ligands capable of inducing receptor dimerization [49]; in principle, this means that at least two binding epitopes per soluble ligand are needed for activation. Thus, while soluble Aβ monomers may be insufficient to activate some TRUCK designs, such receptors should still recognize lower-order soluble Aβ aggregates, including dimers. A potential limitation, however, is that TRUCKs depend on endogenous NFAT signaling rather than orthogonal pathways, as is the case with synNotch and SNIPRs. This reliance on native signaling introduces a higher risk of unwanted activation by endogenous stimuli and LIA.

In principle, Adu-synNotch can be used to condition antibody delivery to the detection of higher order Aβ aggregates, such as those forming Aβ senile plaques. However, for future work targeting lower order aggregates and monomers, alternative synthetic receptor platforms may offer complementary or improved properties.

In vivo validation

The future development of our research into in vivo interventions for AD is supported by recent in vivo work from the Wendell Lim laboratory, which demonstrated that BCAN-targeting synNotch-engineered T cells could successfully deliver IL-10 in a mouse model of MS [16]. The architecture of their BCAN-targeting synNotch and that of the Adu-synNotch tested here are largely the same. Both systems employ the canonical Notch1 core together with the Gal4VP64 transcriptional activator, coupled to an inducible cassette consisting of a four-copy Gal4 upstream activation sequence (Gal4UAS) and a minimal CMV promoter. Noteworthy, in looking forward, one avenue to enhance translational relevance will be to reduce the potential immunogenicity of the hybrid yeast (Gal4) and viral (VP64) activator by adopting humanized artificial transcriptional systems described by others [34, 50].

As per the key differences between the BCAN-targeting synNotch system [16] and the Adu-synNotch described here, they lie in the antigen-binding domain and therapeutic payload. Our design employs an Aducanumab-derived scFv to detect Aβ aggregates and regulates the secretion of chimeric Lecanemab or Aducanumab antibodies. In contrast, the BCAN-targeting system utilized an scFv against the brain extracellular matrix protein BCAN to drive IL-10 delivery [16].

While the in vitro data presented here are encouraging, future studies must directly evaluate Adu-synNotch–mediated delivery of Aβ-clearing antibodies in models of Aβ pathology. As outlined below, strategies to achieve effective brain tropism may differ depending on the specific pathology being modeled. Equally important is the careful selection of the cellular platform. For instance, using helper CD4⁺ T cells to deliver Lecanemab could exacerbate neuroinflammation, whereas immunosuppressant subsets such as Tregs may represent a safer and more suitable option.

Cell tropism

Tropism to the brain depends not on the synNotch receptor itself but on the choice of cell type (e.g., Tregs, microglia), the route of administration (e.g., intravenous, intranasal, or intraventricular injection), and the use of additional receptors introduced specifically for this purpose. In T cells, for example, TCR signaling can be harnessed to promote enrichment at target sites. CARs directed against MOG have been shown to guide intranasally administered Tregs to oligodendrocytes in the brain of mouse models of MS [51]. We have recently validated CARs targeting Aβ, Aβp3–42, and tau aggregates in vitro, which can potentially also be used to target T cells in vivo [24]. Future in vivo studies in AD models will be necessary to validate different CNS homing systems to compliment Adu-synNotch.

Payload type

To the best of our knowledge, compounds that cannot be synthesized by living cells fall outside the scope of deliverable payloads for synNotch and related synthetic receptor systems. While this restriction may appear to narrow the therapeutic repertoire, synthetic systems also create opportunities to deploy agents that are otherwise limited by conventional systemic delivery—for example, therapeutics with poor stability in circulation or those unable to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Engineered cells can secrete payloads within nanometers of their targets, potentially enabling the use of systemically unstable molecules such as small interfering peptides or large biomolecules like brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) that would otherwise fail to reach the brain. Future in vivo studies employing Adu-synNotch should explore alternative payload types to broaden the scope of possible interventions.

Comparison with traditional forms of administration

In principle, therapeutic payloads delivered by engineered cells should behave similarly to the same agents delivered conventionally once comparable concentrations are achieved. Nevertheless, direct comparisons between the two approaches remain essential, both in vitro and in vivo. We anticipate a major challenge in making such comparisons is the difficulty of precisely controlling the dose produced by cell-based drug–synthesizing systems, which will vary depending on the design. For example, in the synNotch system developed by Simic and colleagues [16], the target BCAN is constitutively expressed in the extracellular matrix, leading to continuous payload secretion for as long as engineered cells persist. In such scenarios, effective dose can only be modulated indirectly, for example, by adjusting the number of adoptively transferred cells and by limiting their persistence through safety switches or suicide genes [52]. By contrast, the Adu-synNotch system described here is designed to be self-regulating: engagement with Aβ aggregates induces secretion of an Aβ-clearing antibody and, once the aggregates are cleared, receptor activation ceases thereby shutting off further on-target production. Arguably, given LIA is low enough, a self-limiting system of this type can potentially be allowed to persist indefinitely. Future work should therefore focus on benchmarking cell-based interventions against conventional dosing regimens to gain insights into therapeutic efficacy and safety, developing quantitative methods to measure payload output in vivo.

In sum, this study provides a proof-of-concept for using synNotch receptors to detect extracellular protein aggregates and drive therapeutic gene expression. With further optimization and in vivo validation, this platform could offer a powerful strategy for precision drug delivery in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD.

Conclusions

Here we describe an Aβ-targeting synNotch system that includes an Adu-synNotch receptor based on the Aβ-targeting antibody Aducanumab. We describe and test several synNotch systems by placing different genes under the control of the same Adu-synNotch receptor sequence. We show that, in response to Aβ aggregates, Adu-synNotch reproducibly initiates the transcription of diverse payloads, including human-mouse chimeric versions of FDA-approved Aβ-clearing antibodies. Expression of synNotch receptors in appropriate carrier cell systems would provide a means of direct delivery of therapeutic payloads to brain regions burdened by toxic proteinaceous aggregates, including Aβ. These results are envisioned to be extendable to other diseases characterized by proteinaceous aggregates.
