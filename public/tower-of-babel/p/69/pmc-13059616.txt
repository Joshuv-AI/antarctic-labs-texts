Inflammatory protein expression patterns in Hashimoto’s thyroiditis: a cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Vanna Žnidar, Dean Kaličanin, Zvonimir Boban, Ana Barić Žižić, Marko Vuletić, Sanda Sladić, Ivana Novak, Vesela Torlak Lovrić, Maja Cvek, Ante Punda, Vesna Boraska Perica
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe primary aim of this study is to gain insights into the immunopathogenesis of the most common autoimmune thyroid disorder, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT). A secondary aim is to identify reliable protein biomarkers that can aid in the prediction of the presence and progression of HT, thus advancing disease monitoring and therapeutic development.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 440 individuals − 267 HT patients and 173 healthy controls. HT patients were stratified based on disease severity into two subgroups: euthyroid/levothyroxine-treated (EUTHY-LT4) and hypothyroid (HYPO). Serum levels of 92 inflammation-related proteins were measured using the state-of-the-art Olink proteomics platform. Group comparisons were conducted using ANOVA and post hoc tests to identify differentially expressed proteins. The Boruta machine learning algorithm was applied to identify protein predictors of disease presence and severity.ResultsA total of 42 differentially expressed proteins were identified, revealing eight distinct expression patterns based on their direction of regulation across comparisons. IL-17C and CCL19 were the most upregulated in HT patients, and ST1A1, CASP-8, CXCL1, SIRT2, and LAP TGF-beta-1 were the most downregulated. An increased IL-17C/IL-10 ratio correlated with disease severity. Additionally, machine learning identified 22 protein markers with potential for distinguishing HT patients from controls, and 7 proteins for differentiating EUTHY-LT4 from HYPO patients.ConclusionsOur findings provide novel insights into the molecular landscape of immune-related protein changes in HT, highlighting a dynamic inflammatory response that varies with disease progression. These findings may contribute to the development of biomarkers for patient stratification, improved disease management, and targeted therapies to prevent or delay hypothyroidism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12014-026-09593-5.

Background

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) is the most prevalent autoimmune disorder globally [1]. It is characterized by chronic thyroid inflammation, lymphocytic infiltration, and progressive thyroid tissue destruction, eventually resulting in hypothyroidism [2]. HT begins with a breakdown in immune tolerance to self-antigens, particularly thyroglobulin (Tg) and thyroid peroxidase (TPO) [3], triggered by specific environmental factors in genetically predisposed individuals [4].

The immune mechanisms involved in HT have been extensively studied. Self-antigens are recognized by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in thyroid tissue and peripheral blood. APCs present these antigens to CD4+ T helper cells, which differentiate into Th cell subsets depending on the cytokines they release [5]. The Th1 subset promotes the cellular immune response, mediated by IL-12, IFN-γ, and TNF-β, which activate cytotoxic lymphocytes and macrophages, leading to thyroid follicle destruction. In contrast, increased Th2 activity, mainly mediated by IL-4, promotes the humoral immune response, stimulating B cells to produce autoantibodies. Antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity further contributes to thyroid follicular cell apoptosis. B lymphocytes constitute up to 50% of the inflammatory infiltrate in the thyroid tissue, along with CD8+ and CD4+ T lymphocytes [6], highlighting the thyroid gland’s role in antibody production in HT. Additionally, increased Th17 activity, driven by IL-17 cytokines, contributes to inflammation and exacerbates the immune reaction [7–10].

In HT, the activity of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) is weakened [11]. Tregs secrete IL-10, which regulates IL-17 activity, but in HT, an imbalance between Th17 and Treg cells has been observed. This imbalance positively correlates with higher TgAb levels, suggesting that an increased Th17/Treg ratio may reflect HT severity [7, 12]. Additionally, regulatory B cells (Bregs), which also secrete IL-10 and play an immunosuppressive role, are reduced in HT, potentially contributing to the loss of immune tolerance [13].

Despite extensive research on HT’s immune mechanisms, a comprehensive study of inflammatory proteins in HT patients and controls is lacking. To address this gap, we employed state-of-the-art technology, the Olink platform, to measure 92 inflammatory proteins and analyze the inflammatory profiles of HT patients. A strength of our study is the availability of detailed clinical and phenotypic data from the Croatian biobank of HT patients (CROHT) [14], allowing for precise patient stratification into early-stage (euthyroid) and advanced-stage (subclinical/overt hypothyroid) disease.

The goals of our research are to explore HT’s molecular mechanisms and identify inflammatory protein biomarkers of HT presence and severity, which may serve in disease detection, progression monitoring, and therapy development.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This study included serum samples and clinical data from 440 individuals: 173 controls (94.2% females) and 267 HT patients (92.9% females) from the CRO-HT biobank [14]. Recruitment and sample collection were conducted at the Outpatient Clinic for Thyroid Disorders, University Hospital Split. Nuclear medicine specialists were engaged in patient recruitment, following standardized clinical guidelines [15]. Blood samples were collected, and serum was stored at − 80 °C for later analysis. Thyroid hormone levels (TSH, FT4, FT3) and antibodies (TgAb, TPOAb) were measured using LIAISON chemiluminescence immunoassays (DiaSorin, Italy). Clinical evaluation and thyroid ultrasound were performed to assess thyroid structure and pathology. The HT diagnosis was confirmed by decreased thyroid hormone levels, elevated antibodies, and typical ultrasound findings (heterogeneous or hypoechoic thyroid tissue). Controls were selected based on normal thyroid function and negative thyroid autoantibodies, confirmed by clinical, laboratory, and ultrasound examinations.

In addition to clinical and laboratory assessments, comprehensive medical histories were collected. Structured questionnaires assessed environmental and lifestyle factors, such as diet, physical activity, smoking, and reproductive history. All participants were adult white Europeans from iodine-sufficient southern Croatia [16]. Details on biobank formation, diagnostic criteria, and phenotype collection methods are available in previous studies [17, 18].

Biomarker measurements

The Olink Target 96 Inflammation panel was used to measure 92 inflammatory proteins via proximity extension assay (PEA), as explained in detail in previous studies [19]. This technology offers high-throughput detection and quantification of specific proteins using minimal sample volumes (1–3 µL). The method relies on paired antibodies labeled with unique oligonucleotide strands, which hybridize upon binding to the target protein, creating a DNA “barcode” quantified by next-generation sequencing. Data normalization involves several steps, including using internal controls, log2 transformation of sequencing reads into NPX (Normalized Protein Expression) values, correcting for inter-plate variation using reference samples, and minimizing technical artifacts by correcting background noise or using median intensity normalization.

Identification of significantly associated proteins and their expression patterns

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2, with the “OlinkAnalyze” package. Preliminary analyses compared controls and four patient groups: euthyroid patients (TSH, 0.3–3.6 mlU/L), subclinical hypothyroid (TSH, 3.6–10 mlU/L), overt hypothyroid (TSH > 10 mlU/L), and those receiving levothyroxine (LT4) therapy. Modified ANCOVA (“Olink_anova”) and post hoc analysis (“Olink_anova_posthoc”) showed no significant differences between euthyroid and LT4-treated patients except for IL-20 (p = 0.0274), nor between subclinical and overt hypothyroid patients (Supplementary Table 1). To increase statistical power, euthyroid and LT4-treated patients were combined into the EUTHY-LT4 group, and subclinical and overt hypothyroid patients into the HYPO group for the main analysis. To evaluate if merging euthyroid and LT4-treated patients masked treatment effects on inflammatory profiles, we performed a sensitivity analysis comparing the LT4-only subgroup with controls. The analysis showed that all previously identified proteins except CX3CL1 remained statistically significant with consistent effect directions, justifying the use of the combined EUTHY-LT4 group. CX3CL1 showed a marginal loss of significance after merging (adjusted p-value increased from 0.036 to 0.058) with effect estimates remaining directionally consistent, suggesting attenuation rather than reversal of the signal.

The main analysis was finally performed using 173 controls, 150 euthyroid patients (EUTHY-LT4), and 117 hypothyroid (HYPO) patients. Protein expression differences between groups were assessed, as in preliminary analyses, using “Olink_anova” with Benjamini-Hochberg correction and “Olink_anova_posthoc” with Tukey’s test. The dependent variable was NPX values, and the independent variables included age, gender, and BMI. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Proteins relevant for the prediction of HT disease status and severity

To identify proteins predicting HT status and severity, feature selection was performed using the Boruta method [20] with 1000 iterations, a significance threshold of p = 0.001, and 500 trees in the underlying random forest to ensure model stability and stringent selection. To confirm the predictive power, random forest models were trained on both full and filtered data, with and without feature selection, with five-fold stratified cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning and performance estimation. Boruta was applied only to training data to avoid data leakage. Models were developed and tested using the “tidymodels” framework together with the “ranger”, “Boruta”, and “colino” R packages.

Identification of significantly associated proteins and their expression patterns

We identified 42 differentially expressed proteins across the three groups, 33 from “Olink_anova” analysis (Supplementary Table 3) and 9 from post-hoc analysis (Supplementary Table 4). The most significant proteins, based on an adjusted p-value threshold of 1 × 10⁻3, included: ST1A1 (ANOVA, p = 7.63 × 10− 14), CASP-8 (post hoc EUTHY-LT4 vs. Control, p = 1.15 × 10⁻⁸), IL-17C (post hoc HYPO vs. Control, p = 2.45 × 10⁻⁸), CXCL1 (post hoc EUTHY-LT4 vs. Control, p = 1.13 × 10⁻⁵), SIRT2 (post hoc EUTHY-LT4 vs. Control, p = 1.76 × 10⁻⁵), CCL19 (post hoc HYPO vs. Control, p = 2.06 × 10⁻⁵) and LAP TGF-beta-1 (post hoc EUTHY-LT4 vs. Control, p = 4.17 × 10⁻⁵). Volcano plots for pairwise comparisons are shown in Fig. S2.

We categorized 42 proteins into eight expression patterns (EP). Specifically, we identified the group comparisons in which proteins exhibited significant differences and classified them according to whether they were upregulated or downregulated. This approach enabled the systematic identification of the EP characteristic of HT in general, as well as for the early and late stages of HT. The resulting eight groups thus provide a structured overview of inflammatory biomarker alterations, which are summarized in Table 1.

An important finding was the progressive increase in proinflammatory IL-17C, accompanied by a significant rise in anti-inflammatory IL-10. To assess the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory responses, we analyzed IL-17C/IL-10 ratio (Fig. 2) by one-way ANOVA (F(2, 437) = 7.577, p = 0.0006). This ratio provides a more informative measure of immune dysregulation in autoimmune disease than individual cytokine levels alone [7, 21].

Post hoc Tukey’s showed that the IL-17C/IL-10 ratio is significantly higher in the HYPO compared to the Control (p = 0.0004), while the differences between EUTHY-LT4 and Control (p = 0.082), as well as HYPO and EUTHY-LT4 (p = 0.170), were not significant, suggesting disproportional rise of IL-10 compared to IL-17C. These findings indicate a progressive shift toward pro-inflammatory signalling with insufficient anti-inflammatory compensation.

Proteins relevant for the prediction of HT disease status and severity

The Boruta feature selection method [20] was used to identify relevant proteins by comparing the importance of original features to “shadow” features in a random forest classifier. Two analyses were conducted: the first identified proteins distinguishing controls from HT patients, and the second focused on proteins associated with HT progression in HT patients only. Twenty-two proteins were selected for distinguishing controls from HT patients, with the top 10 being ST1A1, CCL19, IL-17C, CASP-8, 4E-BP1, AXIN1, LAP TGF-beta-1, CXCL1, FGF-21, and SIRT2 (Fig. 3).

Three proteins (IL-8, IL-17C, and MCP-3) were relevant in both categories. At least one of these proteins appeared in each of the eight EPs (Table 1). Model performance, assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), showed an AUC of 0.837 ± 0.009 for distinguishing controls from HT patients using the full protein set, and 0.85 ± 0.02 with Boruta feature selection. For separating early from advanced HT, the AUC was 0.60 ± 0.03 with all proteins, and 0.57 ± 0.05 with Boruta-selected subsets. The lower AUC and larger standard errors in this scenario may be due to several factors. These include the smaller sample size, which increases variability and reduces model robustness; the inherent similarity between patient groups affected by the same disease, which limits the discriminative signal; and the relatively limited availability of strongly differentiating features associated with HT severity. Together, these factors suggest that additional biomarkers or integrative features may be needed to improve predictive performance although, the model’s performance did not significantly deteriorate when 85–90% of features were excluded, confirming the relevance of the selected proteins.

Discussion

This is the first study to analyze associations of a comprehensive panel of 92 inflammatory proteins with HT. Machine learning was applied to distinguish healthy individuals from HT patients, and to differentiate early- from late-stage disease based on inflammatory profiles. Patients were initially classified into four groups: euthyroid, LT4-treated euthyroid, subclinical hypothyroid, and overt hypothyroid. Since no significant differences were found between subclinical and overt hypothyroid patients, they were combined into one hypothyroid (HYPO) group representing more advanced disease. Similarly, LT4-treated euthyroid and untreated euthyroid patients were grouped (EUTHY-LT4), as their inflammatory profiles were largely similar, except for IL-20. This suggests that LT4 may exert anti-inflammatory effects, consistent with previous findings [22, 23].

Identification of significantly associated proteins and their expression patterns

The main comparisons between healthy individuals and the two patient groups revealed 42 dysregulated proteins across eight EPs (Table 1). Functions and potential roles of all 42 proteins and HT are detailed in the Appendix, while we discuss our top associations (p < 1 × 10⁻3) below. IL-17C and CCL19 were the most significantly upregulated proteins, while ST1A1, CASP-8, CXCL1, SIRT2, and LAP TGF-beta-1 were the most significantly downregulated ones. Although not among the proteins with the lowest p-values, we find IL-8 (upregulated) and PD-L1 (dual state) as important proteins in the HT course. Functional overview of identified inflammatory proteins

IL-17C (EP 1) is the only protein showing a statistically significant increase across all comparisons (HYPO vs. Control, HYPO vs. EUTHY-LT4, and EUTHY-LT4 vs. Control), indicating a continuous rise from disease onset through progression and suggesting it to be a key driver of inflammation in HT. IL-17C is mainly secreted by epithelial cells, and it is released in response to bacterial infections or pro-inflammatory cytokines. Through an autocrine signaling mechanism, IL-17C activates immune pathways associated with innate immunity, leading to the production of cytokines, chemokines, inflammatory mediators, and antimicrobial peptides, similar to the action of IL-17A. IL-17C also directly regulates occludin expression in colonic epithelial cells, indicating its role in maintaining mucosal barrier integrity [24]. This finding suggests a potential connection between barrier immunity and thyroid autoimmunity. While current literature suggests the involvement of IL-17A in the pathogenesis of HT [25], our findings highlight a significantly greater role for IL-17C in HT.

CCL19 (EP 5) shows a sharp increase in the early euthyroid stage and continues rising more gradually toward the hypothyroid state. CCL19 is a chemokine involved in the trafficking of immune cells, especially the migration of lymphocytes and dendritic cells to secondary lymphoid organs [26]. The increased expression of CCL19 may facilitate the accumulation of autoreactive immune cells that perpetuate the autoimmune process, further driving chronic inflammation within the thyroid.

IL-8 (EP 2) is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays an important role in recruiting and activating neutrophils at sites of inflammation [27]. It is produced by mononuclear macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, T lymphocytes, epithelial cells, and fibroblasts in response to inflammatory signals. Elevated IL-8 levels point to a significant role of neutrophils in HT, as IL-8 may enhance neutrophil recruitment to the thyroid gland, thereby contributing to tissue injury and amplifying the inflammatory response. In our study, IL-8 emerged as the strongest predictor of HT progression (Fig. 4) but also of HT presence (Fig. 3).

ST1A1 (EP 7), also known as SULT1A1, shows significant downregulation in both euthyroid and hypothyroid patients compared to controls. ST1A1 catalyzes the sulfate conjugation of a wide range of substrates, including thyroid hormones, which is important for their bioavailability and biological activity [28, 29]. Dysregulation of this pathway may have significant implications for thyroid function and overall endocrine homeostasis, as inhibition of ST1A1 has been shown to alter thyroid hormone metabolism, potentially contributing to thyroid-related disorders such as HT. It has also been demonstrated that TSH regulates ST1A1 activity in thyrocytes, exerting a suppressive effect on its expression [30]. The bold reduction in ST1A1 expression observed in all HT patients may reflect impaired sulphation and metabolism of thyroid hormones, potentially leading to disruptions in hormone activity and availability, which could compromise physiological regulation and contribute to the pathophysiology of HT.

CASP-8 (EP 7) initiates apoptosis and regulates necrosis, pyroptosis, and immune signalling [31]. Its suppression blocks apoptosis, triggering alternative cell death pathways [32]. CASP-8 deficiency is linked to immunodeficiency and autoimmune diseases, including autoimmune thyroiditis [33, 34]. CASP-8 ablation is also associated with an exacerbated autoimmune response, driven by increased macrophage-derived IL-1β secretion, which enhances the activation of antigen-specific T helper cells [35]. Furthermore, the loss of CASP-8 enzymatic activity in epithelial cells disrupts the gut-vascular barrier, leading to chronic intestinal inflammation [31, 36]. In HT patients, the observed significant downregulation of CASP-8 may promote a pro-inflammatory state, impair the apoptosis of autoreactive T and B lymphocytes thus contributing to the breakdown of central tolerance and potentially leading to gut barrier dysfunction [33, 34].

CXCL1 (EP 7) is a chemokine that acts as a potent chemoattractant for neutrophils [37] and T cells via its interaction with the CXCR3 receptor [38] aiding in immune defence and tissue repair. Beyond its role in acute inflammation, where it is usually upregulated, CXCL1 has been implicated in fibrosis and autoimmunity, where its role varies by disease, potentially enhancing or reducing progression based on the specific autoimmune condition [39–42]. For instance, in a mouse model of autoimmune myocarditis, elevated CXCL1 levels correlated with reduced disease severity and administration of CXCL1 exerted an anti-inflammatory effect by suppressing the expansion of effector T cells and inducing the production of anti-inflammatory mediators [42]. These findings suggest that the role of CXCL1 in autoimmunity may be disease-dependent, with the potential to either exacerbate or attenuate disease progression depending on the specific autoimmune condition and the local immune environment. In our study, CXCL1 is suppressed in early HT, while hypothyroid patients show slightly higher levels than euthyroid patients but remain below healthy controls. This pattern indicates a potentially unique CXCL1 immunomodulatory mechanism in thyroid autoimmunity and a unique immune microenvironment in HT, perhaps less reliant on neutrophil-driven inflammation; however, further research is needed to clarify the dynamics of CXCL1 regulation in HT.

SIRT2 (EP 8) is a NAD+-dependent deacetylase primarily involved in cell cycle regulation [43], and it exerts both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory effects depending on the specific biological and pathological conditions [44]. SIRT2 is significantly downregulated in euthyroid patients in comparison to controls in our study. Functional investigation in cell lines showed that SIRT2 knockdown leads to increased expression of cytokines [45], providing clues about a possible role in HT; however, it has not yet been directly investigated in HT.

LAP TGF-beta-1 (EP 8) is downregulated in euthyroid HT patients. It is a precursor cleaved to produce LAP and TGF-β1, which remain bound in a latent complex that may interact with the extracellular matrix [46]. TGF-β1 was already studied in HT and plays a stage-dependent role, with reduced serum levels linked to increased disease risk [47], aligning with our observation of decreased LAP TGF-beta-1 in early-stage HT.

PD-L1 (EP 3) shows a dual pattern in HT, being significantly downregulated in euthyroid patients but returning to normal in hypothyroid stages. As an immune checkpoint, PD-L1 suppresses excessive T-cell activity and maintains immune tolerance [48]. Its inhibition in cancer therapy can trigger autoimmune hypothyroidism [49, 50], supporting the idea that early PD-L1 suppression that we observe may be an important early step in the pathogenesis of HT and that it may initiate the autoimmune cascade in HT, rather than being a secondary consequence of thyroid damage. The changes in PD-L1 levels between disease stages highlight its dynamic role in immune regulation during HT progression. Further research is needed to determine if PD-L1 dysregulation drives or results from HT autoimmunity.

In summary, persistent upregulation of IL-17C, CCL19, and IL-8 may drive chronic inflammation and T-cell recruitment, while dynamic PD-L1 changes may indicate immune checkpoint dysfunction in HT onset and progression. The imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators, particularly insufficient IL-10, highlights failed regulatory control, allowing sustained autoimmunity. Altered mucosal immunity markers IL-17 and CASP-8 (also CCL25, CCL28, CDCP1, which are among 42 dysregulated proteins, explained in the Appendix) suggest the existence of a gut-thyroid axis influencing autoimmunity via barrier dysfunction. Emerging evidence supports this concept: HT patients exhibit increased intestinal permeability and elevated zonulin levels, and compositional changes in gut microbiota have been documented in both children and adults with HT, indicating that barrier disruption may contribute to disease pathogenesis. Taken together, although direct mechanistic studies in HT remain limited, these findings support the biological plausibility of a gut-thyroid axis [51–54]. Collectively, these findings suggest that early interventions targeting Th17-driven inflammation, immune checkpoint restoration, and mucosal immune modulation may offer promising strategies to prevent or delay the progression to hypothyroidism in HT. Conversely, significantly downregulated proteins ST1A1, CASP-8, and CXCL1 may indicate impaired hormone metabolism, defective immune cell clearance, and disrupted immune homeostasis. Additional regulatory proteins, SIRT2 and LAP TGF-β1, were also altered in early disease stages, suggesting enhanced inflammatory signalling and weakened immune tolerance. Together, these changes may contribute to early immune dysregulation and progression toward autoimmunity.

Proteins relevant for the prediction of HT disease status and severity

The secondary goal of our study was to identify proteins that predict HT presence and progression phases in our cohort. Using the Boruta feature selection method, which builds multiple decision trees to improve accuracy and reduce overfitting, we identified 22 proteins distinguishing controls from HT patients (top 10: ST1A1, CCL19, IL-17C, CASP-8, 4E-BP1, AXIN1, LAP TGF-beta-1, CXCL1, FGF-21, SIRT2) and 7 proteins differentiating early from advanced HT (IL-8, IL-17C, IL-10RA, IL-22RA1, CCL11, MCP-3, IL-18R1). Most predictors belong to the eight EPs (Table 1). Proteins from EPs 1–5 were more linked to disease progression, while EPs 5–8 distinguished HT from healthy states. IL-17C, IL-8, and MCP-3 were strong predictors in both classifications.

Advantages

A practical outcome of our study is its potential to improve HT detection in large population-based biobanks, especially where clinical data is limited. Given HT’s high prevalence, protein markers could help uncover hidden cases, enhancing the value of these large datasets for genetic and epidemiological studies of HT by increasing sample sizes and data resolution. However, optimal prediction is expected to emerge from combining genetic, metabolic, and proteomic risk scores. Ultimately, this approach could support better identification of at-risk groups, support longitudinal studies of disease progression, and improve the design of preventative strategies.

Limitations

As an observational, cross-sectional study, we cannot establish causality between the identified protein changes and the development or progression of HT; therefore, functional validation is needed. Also, while the protein-based predictive models show promise for use in large population studies, their performance should be validated in independent cohorts and combined with other omics markers to confirm generalizability. In terms of cohort characteristics, our study reflects the strong female predominance of HT, which limits extrapolation of findings to male patients. Sex was included as a covariate in all analyses, however, given the small number of male participants (n = 29), sex-specific effects could not be reliably assessed.

Conclusions

This study highlights that HT is characterized by a coordinated, stage-specific dysregulation of immune pathways. It brings novel insights into immunopathogenesis of HT and demonstrates that specific protein patterns can distinguish healthy individuals from those with HT, as well as patients in early stages from those in advanced stages of HT. This study offers potential for the development of targeted therapies, improvement of the stratification of HT patients by their disease progression, and creation of strategies for disease management with implications for preventing or delaying progression to hypothyroidism.
