Nutritional support in critical care: estimating resting energy expenditure in the intensive care unit when indirect calorimetry is limited
Authors: Jeong Yun Park
Published: 2025

Adequate nutritional support is a cornerstone of intensive care. It preserves immune function, lowers the risk of infection, and promotes recovery and wound healing. In contrast, malnutrition worsens outcomes by increasing susceptibility to infection and impairing respiratory performance. Overfeeding, however, may induce hyperglycemia, raise insulin demand, and prolong mechanical ventilation through excess carbon dioxide production (VCO2). Accurate assessment of caloric requirements in critically ill patients is therefore essential to ensure appropriate energy intake.

For decades, clinicians have relied on predictive equations such as weight-based formulas, the Harris–Benedict equation, and the Ireton–Jones or Penn State equations to estimate energy needs. However, their accuracy is limited; studies indicate that predictive methods may be inaccurate in up to 60% of critically ill patients [1]. This inaccuracy is further complicated by the fact that body weight in intensive care unit (ICU) patients often fails to represent true lean mass because of fluid shifts and edema.

Indirect calorimetry (IC) remains the gold standard, as it directly measures oxygen consumption (VO2) and VCO2 to calculate resting energy expenditure (REE). The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines strongly recommend its use whenever feasible [2]. Nonetheless, widespread adoption is hindered by the high cost of equipment, lack of trained personnel, and technical challenges in some ICU patients [3]. As a result, predictive equations remain widely used, particularly when IC is unavailable, and efforts continue to refine their accuracy.

In this issue of Acute and Critical Care, Romanelli et al. [4] analyzed data from 30 ICU patients and compared two predictive approaches for estimating REE: the Harris–Benedict equation and a weight-based method using VO₂ and VCO2 derived from the Fick principle. The 2019 ESPEN guidelines recommend using VO₂ and VCO2 obtained from ventilators or pulmonary artery catheters when IC is not available [2]. Romanelli et al. [4] calculated VO₂ and VCO2 using standard arterial blood gas analysis, which is a less invasive and more clinically feasible approach in the ICU environment. While this method provides a more accessible option, it also highlights the limitations of predictive tools and underscores the need for validation against IC. The study is further limited by the lack of direct IC comparison, its retrospective design, and the small sample size. Nevertheless, it provides a valuable exploratory step toward bedside REE estimation and emphasizes the need for prospective validation.

Looking ahead, critical care nutrition requires a dual strategy: expanding access to IC while refining predictive models for use when IC is not available. Importantly, precise estimation of energy requirements is not simply a technical matter but a critical determinant of survival and recovery in the critically ill.
