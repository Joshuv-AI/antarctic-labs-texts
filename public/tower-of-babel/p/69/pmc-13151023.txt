Gastric hemorrhage due to high pungency food?
Authors: Rachel J van der Pol, Anemone van den Berg, Wendy L van der Woerd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a case of a 9‐year‐old patient who presented with hematemesis after consuming crisps with a high pungency (the so‐called ghost peppers). Blood loss resulted in a significant decrease in hemoglobin and for this reason a gastroduodenoscopy was performed. This demonstrated diffuse bleeding of the gastric mucosa, in the corpus of the stomach for which hemospray was applied. Since we were not able to identify a plausible other cause, and the patient remained asymptomatic with a normal follow‐up gastroduodenoscopy, we consider it most likely that the crisps have contributed to the development of the stomach hemorrhage. In scientific literature no previous reports have described this possible relationship so far. However, in the news media, there have been several reports about morbidity and even mortality when eating high‐pungency crisps. With this case report, we hope to provide a scientific background and create awareness of the possible consequences of high‐pungency food to prevent future complications.Keywords: ghost pepper, hematemesis, hemospray

INTRODUCTION

Intake of high‐pungency food, the so‐called ghost peppers, has become increasingly popular. Food challenges with spicy crisps are posted on social media and even advertised by vloggers. Some are innocent and enjoyable but others can have hazardous side effects. This is a trend also seen with other irritating foods and spices, such as nutmeg and cinnamon. 1

CASE REPORT

A 9‐year‐old patient was referred to our tertiary center because of hematemesis and melena. Symptoms started that same day with bloody vomiting, three times in total. There was no history of foreign body ingestion. The patient did not have nausea, abdominal pain, diarrhea, nor fever. There were no known allergies, he did not use any medication. A history of prolonged bleeding was absent, no recent travels were reported. In the referring center, no bleeding focus was identified in the nasopharyngeal region. Laboratory work showed anemia with a hemoglobin of 6.1 mmol/I and an increased urea of 9.3 mmol/I. Other lab results showed normal coagulation as well as normal liver enzymes.

On presentation, the patient was alert, pain‐free but pale with a tachycardia of 110/min. Blood pressure was 100/58 mmHg, the capillary refill 2 s and the temperature 36.2°C. There were no abnormalities on physical exam, besides some blood crusts on the nares. He was admitted and an intravenous proton pump inhibitor (PPI) was started. Differential diagnosis included ulcers (possibly due to Helicobacter pylori [H. pylori]), esophagitis and/or gastritis, a Mallory Weis lesion, variceal bleeding (however unlikely with normal thrombocytes and liver enzymes), a foreign body, vasculitis or vascular malformation, a bleeding disorder (e.g., Glanzmann disease), Crohn's disease, malignancy or a bleeding focus outside of the gastrointestinal tract (e.g., pulmonary or the nasopharyngeal region).

The patient remained clinically stable, and no hematemesis nor melena was observed. However, the hemoglobin level dropped to 4.5 mmol/L in 12 h and a gastroduodenoscopy was planned. With repeated history taking and discussing the differential diagnosis, the parents stated that their child ate extremely spiced crisps in the night and morning prior to the hematemesis. At gastroduodenoscopy, the esophageal and duodenal mucosa were completely normal. However, the stomach had to be rinsed extensively to remove fresh blood covering the wall. An area with a diameter of about 2 cm was found in the corpus (see Figure 1) with blood mildly oozing. Immediately after rinsing it started bleeding again. There were no ulcerations and no signs of angiodysplasia. Since no clipable focus was identified, hemospray was applied to stop the bleeding.

Post‐procedure, the patient was given an erythrocyte transfusion, observed for another day, and then discharged. Post‐transfusion, the hemoglobin increased to 4.9 mmol/l. The hemoglobin increased with only 0.4 point after transfusion because the hemoglobin was probably even lower than 4.5 mmol/l pre‐procedure. During the admission, a pediatric hematologist was consulted to exclude an underlying bleeding or clotting disorder and coagulation tests were normal. The patient was advised to continue oral PPI therapy while awaiting fecal H. pylori antigen test. Fecal samples were assembled prior to the PPI therapy. However, at an outpatient check‐up the patient stated to have stopped PPI therapy just 2 days after discharge. Fecal H. pylori testing remained negative.

Six weeks after presentation, the patients remained asymptomatic. A follow‐up gastroduodenoscopy did not show any abnormalities: no lesions, nor bleeding were seen in the corpus of the stomach. No biopsies were taken because of the normal endoscopic aspect. Hemoglobin was 6.2 mmol/l. Because of the spontaneous clinical and macroscopic resolution, the negative H. pylori test, and no other cause for the previous stomach hemorrhage was found, we considered the exceptionally spiced snack as the most likely etiology. The fact that our young patient consumed these extremely spiced snacks has been discussed with the patient and his parents. They did not realize that these easy accessible and on social media popular chips could be potentially dangerous.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates the possible relationship between ingestion of a ghost pepper and disruption and bleeding of gastric mucosa. So far, no previous scientific reports have described this possible relationship. However, in news media there have been several reports about gastrointestinal morbidity and even mortality when eating high‐pungency crisps. In adults there have been reports about an esophageal rupture due to forceful vomiting after eating substances containing ghost peppers. 2 Also, a report about a presumed acute abdomen has been published, again after eating ghost peppers. 3 In both cases, no endoscopy was performed. Cerebrovascular vasoconstriction after eating ghost peppers has been reported as well. 4

Capsaicin (8‐methyl‐N‐vanillyl‐6‐nonenamide) is the main piquant compound of chili peppers. It can stimulate the transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) in the gastrointestinal tract and can produce pain and a burning sensation. 5 Capsaicin deters acid secretion, promotes production of alkali, mucus secretions, and particularly gastric mucosal blood flow which may even help in prevention and healing of ulcers. 6 Moreover, it is extensively studied for its numerous beneficial effects, such as its influence in metabolic, and cardiovascular disorders and even in cancer treatment, 7 , 8 yet the contrary of these beneficial effect are also debated. 9 When taken in large quantities, it may reduce gastric acid production and impair gut mucosa by stimulating gastric inflammation. In a recent study in mice, 10 it has been shown that inflammation and histopathological changes were substantial in the jejunum, ileum, and colon, and to some extent in the stomach in higher capsaicin‐dosages. Underlying mechanisms might be correlated to the decreasing levels of anti‐inflammatory cytokines (interleukin‐10) and dysregulation of gut microbiota. In our case, intake of capsacin was not chronic nor in extremely high quantities, although we were not able to retrieve the exact capsaicin concentration. However, the above‐mentioned pathophysiologic mechanism could have led to the gastric hemorrhage.

In our case, it is questionable that no biopsies were taken. By the initial endoscopy, because of the use of hemospray, there was no clear view to take biopsies. By the second endoscopy, biopsies were not taken because there were no macroscopic abnormalities. In hindsight, it would have been better to take biopsies because this could have given more insight in a possible underlying condition, or a possible relationship with the ghost pepper.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, when a child presents with hematemesis and/or melena, a detailed and repeated food history may contribute to the further work‐up and therapy. If products with high capsaicin content have been consumed, this may be the cause of gastro‐intestinal bleeding and warrants a further diagnostic work‐up, including gastroscopy. With this case report, we hope to create awareness of the risk of severe morbidity after high‐pungency food and hope to prevent more complications in the future.
