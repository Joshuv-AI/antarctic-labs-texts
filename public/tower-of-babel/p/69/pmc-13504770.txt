Impact of opioid free anesthesia on postoperative nausea and vomiting, chronic pain, and quality of recovery in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Jingchen Yang, Tianwu Liao, Jin Luo, Aixin Ma, Jincan He, Hao Li, Rongsheng Liu, Yang Yu, Jun Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpioids are frequently used in general anesthesia but may delay recovery owing to adverse effects, which include respiratory depression, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and opioid-induced hyperalgesia. Opioid-free anesthesia (OFA), based on multimodal analgesia, offers an alternative to mitigate these issues and enhance recovery in thoracic surgery. However, high-quality evidence confirming its effectiveness and safety is needed.MethodsThe Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines were followed in this study. PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library were systematically searched from inception to March 01, 2025, for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that compared OFA and opioid-based anesthesia (OBA) in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). Study titles, abstracts, and full texts were separately evaluated for eligibility by two researchers; disagreements were resolved by discussion or a third researcher. The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool was used to evaluate the risk of bias in included studies. Primary outcomes included PONV incidence and 24-h postoperative pain scores. Secondary outcomes included chronic pain, intraoperative hemodynamic events, length of stay in the recovery room, length of hospitalization, and quality of recovery (as measured by QoR-40/QoR-15).ResultsThis systematic review and meta-analysis consisted of 10 RCTs involving 1,106 patients. OFA reduced the risk of PONV (risk ratio (RR) = 0.41, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.380–0.665) and the incidence of postoperative chronic pain at 3 months (RR = 0.566, 95% CI: 0.347–0.925), compared with OBA. However, the 24-h postoperative pain scores did not differ (MD = 0.082, p = 0.305). Bradycardia incidence was lower in the OFA group, but hypotension rates did not differ. The length of stay in recovery rooms and the length of hospitalization did not differ significantly. Although 24-h QoR-40 scores favored OFA (mean difference = 2.914, 95% CI: 1.017–4.811), this improvement did not reach the clinically established threshold of 6.3 points.ConclusionWithout worsening intraoperative hemodynamic instability or extending postoperative recovery, OFA significantly reduced the incidence of PONV and chronic pain in patients undergoing VATS. These results validate OFA as a potentially safe and effective anesthetic strategy for improving postoperative recovery after thoracic surgery. Further large-scale RCTs are warranted to standardize OFA protocols and validate its long-term advantages.Trial registrationRegistration of systematic reviews: CRD42025636410.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-04028-y.Keywords: Opioid Free Anesthesia, Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery, ERAS

Introduction

Opioids exert powerful analgesic effects by activating the µ-opioid receptor (µOR), which triggers intracellular signaling through G protein pathways and β-arrestin recruitment. Opioids have been a mainstay of general anesthesia management for decades because of their capacity to maintain anesthetic balance, ensure surgical safety, improve postoperative pain, and enhance intraoperative hemodynamic stability [1, 2]. However, opioid-based anesthesia (OBA) results in clinically relevant adverse effects. Intraoperative opioid administration may induce respiratory depression, and both postoperative cognitive impairment and delayed anesthesia recovery have been associated with residual postoperative respiratory depression [3]. Furthermore, through pro-inflammatory and central sensitization processes, brief exposure to opioids may cause dose-dependent hyperalgesia, exacerbating both acute and chronic postoperative pain through pro-inflammatory mechanisms. This condition is manifested as increased pain intensity and higher postoperative opioid requirements [4, 5]. Additionally, opioid-induced adverse effects, such as nausea, vomiting, constipation, and pruritus, are still prevalent and can negatively hinder mobilization and rapid postoperative recovery [6]. Therefore, strategies that minimize intraoperative opioid exposure or opioid-free anesthesia approaches have garnered academic and clinical interest.

Perioperative techniques that reduce complications and enhance postoperative recovery have been receiving more attention owing to the development of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS). Because of its lower risk of respiratory depression and ability to promote early postoperative recovery, opioid-free anesthesia (OFA) has garnered substantial attention in thoracic surgery [7–9]. Traditional opioids have drawbacks, particularly in thoracic surgery cases, despite their effectiveness in suppressing intraoperative pain, maintaining hemodynamics, and lowering the dosage of anesthetics. First, opioid-mediated suppression of the respiratory center may raise the risk of hypoxemia in patients undergoing one-lung ventilation. Additionally, residual postoperative respiratory depression may hinder sputum clearance and raise the risk of complications, such as atelectasis and pneumonia [10, 11]. Second, respiratory depression and ventilation disorders caused by opioids may serve as risk factors for brain injury after surgery [12]. Additionally, the high incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and opioid-mediated suppression of gastrointestinal motility may lengthen hospital stays and further impede patient recovery [13]. In contrast, OFA minimizes opioid-related side effects during thoracic surgery by combining drugs with different mechanisms of action and working in tandem with regional nerve block procedures to deliver adequate analgesia. Dexmedetomidine, one of the key drugs in the OFA regimen, acts on α2 receptors in the central nervous system, allowing OFA to deliver sufficient analgesia while successfully preserving cough reflex and spontaneous breathing function in patients. Ketamine or esketamine acts antagonistically on NMDA receptors to prevent and reverse central sensitization. In addition to improving intraoperative analgesia, this is a core mechanism for preventing postoperative acute hyperalgesia and potential chronic pain. Opioid-induced hyperalgesia can be reduced by the intravenous infusion of local anesthetics, including lidocaine. Regional nerve block—the cornerstone of the OFA regimen—substantially reduces the need for general anesthetics and analgesics by achieving superior nociceptive stimulation blockade at the surgical site. Postoperatively, OFA has been associated with a lower incidence of PONV (relative risk, RR 0.5–0.7), shorter recovery period for gastrointestinal function (by approximately 8–12 h), and a possible decrease in pulmonary complications, all of which have improved alignment with the ERAS goals [14, 15].

Therefore, extensive prospective studies with larger sample sizes and more comprehensive outcome evaluation are warranted to clarify the standardized application pathways of OFA in thoracic surgery. Specifically, its long-term safety and health economic benefits are still not understood. Therefore, through a systematic review and meta-analysis, the present study aimed to compare postoperative recovery outcomes between OFA and OBA in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines were followed during the literature search and study selection [16]. On January 21, 2025, this systematic review was filed under registration number CRD42025636410 in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/).

Selection criteria

The following inclusion criteria were used by two independent researchers to identify eligible studies: (1) Study design: randomized controlled trials (RCTs); (2) Population: patients who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS); (3) Intervention: Control group: intraoperative anesthesia protocols primarily using opioids; experimental group: intraoperative anesthesia protocols that did not use opioids. Animal studies, observational studies, and trials awaiting approval were excluded.

Search strategy

From database inception up to March 01, 2025, a thorough literature search was conducted throughout major electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. The search strategy included terms and phrases associated with opioids, thoracic surgery, and RCTs, which was created in conjunction with a skilled medical librarian. No further restrictions were applied to the language or publishing status. Supplemental Table 1 provides the complete search strategy.

To identify potentially eligible studies, two researchers independently used EndNote software to screen the titles and abstracts. They retrieved full-text articles and independently screened them for eligibility. Discrepancies during the screening process were resolved through discussion, and a third researcher was consulted in cases where consensus could not be reached. Additionally, the reference lists of the included studies were manually searched backwards (snowballing) to identify more relevant studies. Using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias for the included studies [17].

Data extraction

The following data were extracted: the number of patients, type of surgery, opioid-based anesthesia regimen, non-opioid anesthesia regimen, postoperative analgesia regimen, and opioid usage. The primary outcomes included PONV and postoperative acute pain scores, and pain scores were assessed using either the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) or the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). The score that was closest to 24 h after surgery was recorded for the meta-analysis when pain scores were reported at various postoperative time points. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of chronic pain at 3 months after surgery, intraoperative hemodynamic events (particularly bradycardia and hypotension), length of stay in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), postoperative length of hospitalization, and quality of recovery measured using either the QoR-40 or QoR-15 at the time point closest to 24 h after surgery. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was determined to be 6.3 for QoR-40 and 6.0 for QoR-15 [18, 19].

Statistical analysis

Review Manager (RevMan version 5.4; Cochrane Collaboration, 2020) and STATA (StataCorp, 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18) were used for the meta-analysis. Mean differences (MDs) were used to summarize effect sizes expressed as the mean and standard deviation. Standardized mean differences were calculated for studies using other descriptive statistics, such as medians and interquartile ranges [20]. When required, mean values from RCTs with three study arms were pooled to compare the OFA and OBA groups. The choice of model was based on heterogeneity test results. First, the Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic were used to evaluate heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was used for insignificant heterogeneity (P > 0.10 and I² < 50%); otherwise, a random-effects model was used. To evaluate the robustness of results, sensitivity analyses were conducted by comparing results from both fixed-effects and random-effects models. Funnel plots were used to visually assess potential publication bias when 10 or more studies were available for an outcome. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 706 studies were identified through database search. After removing duplicates, 369 studies were deemed suitable for screening the titles and abstracts. Supplementary Materials describe the comprehensive literature search and study selection procedures. The final analysis included 10 RCTs [21–30]. A total of 1,106 patients were chosen from RCTs with sample sizes ranging from 50 to 174 participants. The selection process is illustrated in the flow diagram (Fig. 1). Table 1 summarizes the key characteristics, and the quality of the studies was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool. The risk of bias was graded into three levels, namely low, unclear, and high risk (Fig. 2A and B).

PONV

Seven RCTs evaluated the incidence of PONV, with a total of 857 patients. A fixed-effects model suggested minimal between-study heterogeneity (I² = 18.7%). OFA was related to a reduced risk of PONV than OBA, with a pooled risk ratio (RR) of 0.503 (95% CI: 0.380–0.665), as shown by the forest plot (Fig. 3). Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that after sequentially excluding separate studies, the pooled effect estimate remained constant, with RRs ranging from 0.468 to 0.587 (Supplemental Fig. 1). In summary, OFA reduced the risk of PONV in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery (RR = 0.503), with strong stability, providing robust evidence for its therapeutic use.

Postoperative pain

A fixed-effects model was used to evaluate the impact of OFA on postoperative pain scores within 24 h in patients undergoing thoracic surgery. This analysis included seven RCTs with a total of 798 patients: three studies used the VAS, whereas four used the NRS. The overall meta-analysis showed a combined MD of 0.082 (95% CI: -0.075 to 0.238, p = 0.305), indicating no significant difference in pain scores between the groups. Additionally, low heterogeneity was observed among the RCTs (I² = 0.7%, p = 0.419). Subgroup analyses were conducted in accordance with the pain assessment method to further investigate possible sources of variation. The pooled MD for the VAS subgroup (three studies) was − 0.091 (95% CI: -0.336 to 0.153, p = 0.464), suggesting that OFA did not significantly reduce pain. In contrast, the pooled MD for the NRS subgroup (four studies) was 0.201 (95% CI: -0.002 to 0.405, p = 0.052), which was close to but insignificant (Fig. 4). Neither subgroup demonstrated a significant clinical analgesic advantage of OFA. Additionally, differences between VAS and NRS outcomes may not have been robust because of an insignificant variance between subgroups (p = 0.071). Sensitivity analyses indicated stable results (Supplemental Fig. 2). Overall, OFA does not provide a clinically significant benefit in terms of lowering discomfort 24 h after thoracoscopic surgery. The OFA group demonstrated a moderate tendency toward higher NRS scores at 24 h after surgery (MD = 0.201, p = 0.052). OFA and OBA offer similar analgesic benefits during the early postoperative period, which was further supported by the lack of a significant effect in the VAS subgroup (WMD = -0.091, p = 0.464).

Forest plot of postoperative pain scores at 24 h (subgroup analysis by VAS and NRS)

Chronic pain

A total of 279 patients participated in two RCTs that used NRS scores to evaluate the incidence of mild chronic postoperative pain at 3 months. No heterogeneity was observed (I² = 0.0%). Thus, a fixed-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. With a risk ratio (RR) of 0.566 (95% CI: 0.347–0.925) (Fig. 5), the pooled results showed that patients receiving OFA had a considerably decreased risk of developing chronic postoperative pain than patients receiving OBA (p = 0.023). Sensitivity analysis demonstrated moderate robustness, as excluding one study maintained significance (RR = 0.512, P = 0.0204), whereas excluding the other did not (RR = 0.750, P = 0.5714) (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Forest plot of chronic pain incidence at 3 months postoperatively

Intraoperative hemodynamic changes

A meta-analysis of RCTs was conducted to assess the impact of OFA on intraoperative hemodynamic safety. Data on intraoperative hypotension were published from three RCTs involving 345 patients. A random-effects model was used because of the significant heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 68.0%). No significant difference was observed between the OFA and OBA groups, based on the pooled RR of 0.771 (95% CI: 0.335–1.775) (p = 0.541) (Fig. 6).With pooled RR values ranging from 0.467 to 1.204 after sequentially excluding separate studies (Supplemental Fig. 4), sensitivity analysis indicated relatively weak result stability. Additionally, three RCTs involving the same 345 patients reported the incidence of intraoperative sinus bradycardia. A fixed-effects model was applied because of no heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%). Although this difference did not achieve significance (p = 0.053), the pooled RR was 0.335 (95% CI: 0.111–1.012), indicating a trend toward a decreased incidence of bradycardia in the OFA group (Fig. 7). After excluding separate studies, the sensitivity analysis suggested that the RR ranged from 0.200 to 0.604(Supplemental Fig. 5).

Duration of stay in the recovery room

Five RCTs involving 515 patients evaluated the length of stay in the PACU. A random-effects model was used because of significant heterogeneity among the trials (I² = 87.0%, p < 0.0001). The OFA group demonstrated a mean difference in length of PACU stay of 3.262 min (95% CI: −3.568 to 10.093), compared with the OBA group, without significant difference (p = 0.349) (Fig. 8). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the pooled MD ranged from 0.609 to 5.967 min after eliminating any one RCT, with all 95% confidence intervals including zero, suggesting low robustness of the results (Supplemental Fig. 6).

Forest plot of length of stay in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU)

Length of postoperative hospitalization

Six RCTs comprising 802 patients assessed postoperative hospitalization. A random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis because of moderate heterogeneity (I² = 41.2%). Compared with the OBA group, the pooled results showed an MD of 0.09 days for the length of postoperative hospitalization in the OFA group (95% CI: -0.14 to 0.32), (p = 0.541). The corresponding forest plot illustrates these findings (Fig. 9). Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that after excluding any one RCT, the pooled MD ranged from 0.007 to 0.140 days, with all 95% confidence intervals including zero (Supplemental Fig. 7). Moreover, the direction of the results remained consistent, indicating stable results.

Forest plot of length of postoperative hospitalization

Postoperative quality of life

Four RCTs evaluated the impact of OFA on postoperative quality of life after thoracic surgery. Two of these trials, involving 324 patients, reported QoR-15 scores at 24 h after surgery. A fixed-effects model was applied because of no heterogeneity (I² of 0.0%). As seen in the forest plot (Fig. 10), the pooled analysis suggested an MD of 2.918 points (95% CI: 1.369–4.467), suggesting a significant improvement in postoperative QoR in the OFA group (p = 0.0002). Sensitivity analysis showed strong robustness, with MDs between 2.667 and 3.000 and consistent significance (P < 0.05 or near-significant) (Supplemental Fig. 8). An additional two RCTs that involved 170 patients, reported QoR-40 scores at 24 h after surgery. A fixed-effects model was used for the meta-analysis after observing moderate heterogeneity (I² = 43.2%). As seen in the forest plot (Fig. 11), the pooled MD was 2.914 points (95% CI: 1.017–4.811), demonstrating a significant difference that favored the OFA group (p = 0.0026). Sensitivity analysis confirmed stable results, with MDs between 2.300 and 5.600 and all P < 0.05, despite not reaching the MCID of 6.3 (Supplemental Fig. 9).

Discussion

In this meta-analysis, patients in the OFA group demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of PONV than patients in the OBA group. At 3 months after surgery, patients in the OFA group demonstrated a lower incidence of chronic pain than the OBA group, despite no significant differences in acute pain scores within 24 h after surgery. Regarding intraoperative hemodynamic safety, the incidence of hypotension did not differ between the OFA and OBA groups. However, patients in the OFA group demonstrated a significantly reduced incidence of sinus bradycardia. Additionally, there were no significant differences in the length of postoperative hospitalization or PACU stay after surgery. Patients in the OFA group did not demonstrate an improvement in postoperative quality of life. The QoR-15 and QoR-40 scores did not meet the MCID requirement, despite significant differences.

Patients undergoing VATS may benefit from OFA [9]. However, previous studies were retrospective in design and had small sample sizes. In contrast, the current meta-analysis included 10 RCTs, consisting of the most recent data. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first VATS-based meta-analysis that entirely focused on RCTs. Therefore, there are extensive data about the effectiveness and safety of OFA. However, extensive randomized controlled trials are warranted to further confirm its effectiveness and establish strong clinical recommendations.

PONV is a major adverse event after thoracic surgery. Although the emetic reflex may hinder airway clearance and jeopardize lung recovery, effective coughing and sputum clearance are vital for preventing atelectasis and pneumonia. Furthermore, severe vomiting may raise the risk of chest tube dislodgement and postoperative bleeding from lung lesions caused by sudden increases in the intrathoracic pressure. In this study, OFA reduced the risk of PONV by 49.7%; this impact is both statistically significant and clinically relevant.

The NRS scores showed a marginal difference between the two groups (MD = 0.201, p = 0.052), which was considerably lower than the MCID (≥ 1 point). OFA did not improve postoperative pain scores after 24 h when data from both VAS and NRS assessments were pooled (p = 0.305). However, these results should be interpreted carefully because variations in patient characteristics, study design heterogeneity, and perioperative analgesic regimens may have affected pain outcomes. In Wang et al.’s study [24], both the OFA and OBA groups received the identical PCEA regimen, which comprised 0.12% ropivacaine and 0.4 µg/mL sufentanil, along with regular oral doses of paracetamol and ibuprofen. The null findings may be attributed to high-intensity regional analgesia, which obscured any differences in acute pain outcomes between the anesthetic procedures. In contrast, Yan et al. [25] used PCEA in both groups but with distinct adjuvants: 25 mg of esketamine in the OFA PCEA and 10 mg of morphine in the OBA PCEA. Despite similar lengths of hospital stay, the OFA group experienced a higher incidence of acute pain at 24 h postoperatively (17.5% vs. 0%, p < 0.001) but a lower incidence of chronic postsurgical pain (p = 0.004). Therefore, the specific analgesic regimen used postoperatively largely influences the efficacy of OFA in postoperative analgesia. Nonetheless, OFA appears to offer distinct benefits in mitigating chronic postoperative pain, which may explain the variation in pain relief outcomes observed across studies. Future research should focus on standardized analgesic protocols and pain assessment techniques, and conduct thorough sub-population analyses to improve trial comparability and the interpretability of postoperative pain outcomes.

Reducing intraoperative opioid exposure or the use of non-opioid anesthetic procedures may help improve postoperative acute pain outcomes [31]. Mechanistically, opioids can cause central sensitization by activating µ-opioid receptors, thus increasing pain intensity and induces postoperative hyperalgesia [32]. In contrast, OFA often includes non-opioid drugs, such as dexmedetomidine, lidocaine, and ketamine, which provide equivalent analgesia through mechanisms, such as α2-receptor agonism and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonism [6, 33]. By altering nociceptive receptor sensitivity, this multimodal strategy may break the positive feedback loop that causes intraoperative opioid exposure to raise postoperative opioid needs [34]. Therefore, more carefully planned RCTs are warranted to establish standardized methods for postoperative pain evaluation and to further validate these findings. More comprehensive outcome measures, including multidimensional QoR assessments, may better reflect individual differences in pain perception and overall recovery, despite the widespread use of NRS and VAS [35, 36]. With an approximately 50% risk reduction (RR = 0.566, p = 0.023), OFA showed significant potential in lowering the incidence of chronic postoperative pain. Thus, OFA may offer long-term advantages by reducing intraoperative opioid-induced central sensitization. However, this result is based on a few RCTs; therefore, the existing database remains limited. To establish the protective effects of OFA against chronic postoperative pain in thoracic surgery and to further clarify the underlying biological mechanisms, future research should prioritize carefully planned RCTs with more rigorous designs, sufficient sample sizes, and long follow-up periods.

Owing to the lack of clinically relevant differences in postoperative pain outcomes, OFA may be chosen for thoracic surgery based on its safety profile and capacity to lower opioid-related adverse events. However, numerous studies have reported a mean intraoperative blood pressure and heart rate without specifying the incidence of hypotension or bradycardia, making them inappropriate for inclusion in the current study [21, 22, 25–27]. Despite no significant difference in the incidence of hypotension between the OFA and OBA groups, the incidence of bradycardia was lower in the OFA group. Opioids may cause sinus bradycardia through both direct negative chronotropic effects on the cardiac conduction system and indirect negative chronotropic effects mediated by increased parasympathetic activity [37, 38].

The OFA group demonstrated a significant improvement in postoperative recovery quality, as evidenced by the pooled MD for the QoR-40 scores of 3.362 points at 24 h after surgery. However, the observed improvement may not result in a clinically substantial advantage. This is because Myles et al. reported that the MCID was 6.3 points for the QoR-40 [18]. Furthermore, these results should be interpreted carefully because they are based on only RCTs with small sample sizes.

Among the included studies, Fan et al. [23] used a third-generation double-tube laryngeal mask airway (LMA) for ventilation during non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (NIVATS). NIVATS outperforms intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (IVATS) in reducing postoperative complications, promoting early mobilization, and minimizing hospital stay [39–42]. Notably, NIVATS is characterized by markedly lower intraoperative opioid usage. Therefore, the favorable prognostic outcomes of NIVATS may be mostly attributed to decreased intraoperative opioid use.

Several limitations of this meta-analysis should be acknowledged. First, all interventional regimens were collectively categorized as OFA. However, as Table 1 suggests, the specific OFA protocols varied considerably across trials, using different combinations of propofol, etomidate, ketamine (or esketamine), dexmedetomidine, and lidocaine. Additionally, several trials adopted different regional anesthetic techniques, such as epidural anesthesia and paravertebral block. These studies aimed to avoid the use of opioids during surgery. Nonetheless, the dosages, specific mechanisms, and synergistic effects of these drugs differed, which may account for the heterogeneity observed in some of our results. The clinical heterogeneity of OFA regimens, which is an inherent feature of current OFA research, necessitates future studies on standardized OFA regimens. Second, two distinct measures were used for postoperative pain assessment in the included studies; this could lead to methodological variability owing to the pooled integration of diverse evaluation tools. Subgroup analyses stratified by pain assessment methods were conducted to mitigate this bias. No significant differences were observed in postoperative pain outcomes between the VAS and NRS subgroups. This finding is in line with previous studies that suggests VAS and NRS can be used interchangeably for postoperative pain evaluation [35, 36]. OFA was shown to successfully lower the incidence of PONV and chronic postsurgical pain, although it did not significantly improve acute postoperative pain in the current analysis. Third, it was impossible to assess publication bias through funnel plots or statistical tests owing to the small number of qualifying studies for multiple outcomes. This limitation increases the likelihood that the findings may be influenced by unpublished studies or the absence of unfavorable results.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analyses demonstrated that OFA significantly reduces the risk of PONV and chronic pain in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery, without increasing intraoperative hemodynamic instability or lengthening recovery time. OFA is a safe and effective anesthetic for improving postoperative recovery. However, owing to the heterogeneity of existing studies and the small sample size, the individualized selection of OFA regimens is recommended in clinical practice, particularly for patients who are at a high risk of PONV or who prioritize long-term pain control. To assess the synergistic effects and long-term advantages of combining opioid-free anesthesia with non-intubated thoracoscopic surgery, further large-sample, multicenter, RCTs using standardized anesthesia protocols are warranted. In the future, researchers should prioritize longer follow-up periods and patient-centered outcomes to improve perioperative management techniques in thoracic surgery and to elucidate ERAS-based anesthetic pathways.
