The Disordered Proliferative Endometrium: Time for Evidence-based Clinicopathological Studies
Authors: Seetu Palo, Mishu Mangla
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The disordered proliferative stage of the endometrium (DPE) represents a diagnostically important yet conceptually underexplored entity in gynecologic pathology. Encountered in endometrial biopsies performed for abnormal uterine bleeding, especially in the peri-menopausal age group, DPE occupies an ill-defined position along the continuum between normal proliferative endometrium and endometrial hyperplasia. Despite its routine reporting in daily practice, DPE remains an understudied clinicopathological entity, and robust clinicopathological data addressing its biological behavior, clinical significance, and optimal management remain limited.

From a pathological perspective, DPE is characterized by architectural glandular irregularity and dilated glands without significant cytologic atypia, often reflecting prolonged or unopposed estrogenic stimulation [Figure 1]. However, the histological criteria used to diagnose DPE show considerable interobserver variability, and its distinction from simple hyperplasia without atypia or normal proliferative endometrium can be subtle [Table 1].[123] Recent revisions in endometrial pathology classification, particularly the transition toward the Endometrial Intraepithelial Neoplasia/Atypical Endometrial Hyperplasia framework, have positioned disordered proliferative endometrium at the benign, lower end of the proliferative spectrum.[12] With evolving classification systems emphasizing reproducibility and risk stratification, the lack of standardized, evidence-based diagnostic thresholds for DPE poses challenges for both pathologists and clinicians.

DPE: Disordered proliferative endometrium

From a clinician’s standpoint, DPE is not just an isolated histologic entity, but a reflection of systemic reproductive, endocrine and metabolic health. DPE most commonly manifests as abnormal uterine bleeding, particularly in women with anovulatory cycles. In perimenopausal women, fluctuating estrogen levels and the absence of regular ovulation lead to prolonged proliferative stimulation of the endometrium.[4] In reproductive-aged women, conditions such as polycystic ovarian syndrome, obesity, thyroid disorders, and hyperprolactinemia can produce a similar effect. Transvaginal sonography often reveals a diffusely thickened or heterogeneous endometrium, and the appearance may overlap with simple hyperplasia. Management of DPE primarily focuses on correcting the hormonal imbalance.[5] In reproductive-age women, the goal is to restore regular ovulatory cycles using cyclic progesterone or combined oral contraceptives. In perimenopausal women or those on estrogen replacement therapy, low-dose continuous progestin or the levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system effectively protects the endometrium from unopposed estrogen effects. For obese or insulin-resistant patients, lifestyle modification and management of metabolic risk factors are equally important to prevent recurrence. Follow-up plays a pivotal role in clinical management. After 3–6 months of hormonal therapy, patients should be reassessed clinically and, if indicated, by repeat TVS. Persistent or recurrent bleeding warrants a repeat endometrial biopsy.

Although DPE has traditionally been considered benign, chronic unopposed estrogen exposure over time may lead to endometrial hyperplasia or, rarely, carcinoma if left unaddressed. Huang et al. reported “focal gland crowding” in endometrial biopsies – an indeterminate histologic feature that does not fulfill the criteria for atypical endometrial hyperplasia, yet was associated with a significant risk of subsequent atypical hyperplasia (23%) and endometrial carcinoma (4%) on follow-up biopsies over a mean duration of 1.5 years.[3] These findings highlight the existence of a poorly defined subset of endometrial biopsies, particularly within the DPE spectrum, that lack cytologic atypia but nonetheless carry a measurable risk of future neoplastic transformation. Despite increasing advances in molecular endometrial pathology, these insights have not yet been systematically applied to the study of DPE. In this context, there is a clear unmet need for well-designed prospective clinicopathological studies specifically focused on DPE to conclusively determine whether DPE is entirely benign or harbors a genuine potential for progression, to delineate its histomorphological and molecular spectrum that would enhance diagnostic confidence, and to establish evidence-based management and follow-up recommendations.
