Bariatric surgery and semaglutide in a youth with juvenile Huntington disease and severe obesity: a case report

Background

Managing severe obesity in youth becomes more intricate when it intersects with chronic diseases that affect physical, cognitive, and metabolic functions [1, 2]. Conditions such as juvenile Huntington disease (JHD) present unique challenges in obesity management, necessitating a multidisciplinary approach that not only prioritizes weight reduction but also addresses the complexities of these coexisting, life-altering diseases [3–5]. JHD is marked by rapid progression, with most patients experiencing significant motor and cognitive decline within 10–20 years of symptom onset, leading to markedly reduced life expectancy [5–8]. Common complications, such as aspiration pneumonia, infections, or organ failure, often lead to mortality in the late teens or early adulthood [6, 7]. Therefore, management of obesity in JHD must be tailored to the patient’s limited lifespan, focusing on symptom management, improving quality of life, and addressing the complex interplay between neurological degeneration and metabolic health [5–8]. Adolescents with JHD face the dual burdens of a progressive neurodegenerative illness and severe obesity, which together can exacerbate challenges such as diminished quality of life, increased social stigma, and elevated psychological distress [3–5].

In addressing obesity in these patients, a comprehensive treatment plan incorporating pharmacological interventions, such as semaglutide, and surgical options such as laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, provide a comprehensive approach [2]. Semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, facilitates significant weight reduction by regulating appetite, slowing gastric emptying, and improving glucose metabolism, making it a promising treatment for youth with obesity [9–11]. Additionally, laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy can provide significant, sustainable weight loss for eligible patients, both improving physical health and reducing obesity-related comorbidities. However, the ethical challenges of providing these interventions to pediatric patients with limited life expectancy must be considered carefully, ensuring treatment strategies focus on enhancing quality of life rather than simply extending it [11].

The ethical implications of treating adolescents with chronic, progressive diseases such as JHD are profound. With time-limited prognoses, these patients may not experience long-term benefits, requiring clinicians to balance the risks and burdens of interventions against short-term goals, such as improving social and functional outcomes. A patient-centered approach that aligns with individual goals fosters a sense of agency and empowerment, allowing young patients to engage actively in their treatment plans. This case report aims to illustrate a multimodal treatment approach to severe obesity in a young patient with JHD, highlighting the integration of medical, nutritional, and psychological interventions delivered through a shared decision-making process. The goal of the treatment was not only to address obesity but also to improve the patient’s overall quality of life, by reducing further medical interventions and optimizing the remaining years of life. The intervention was carried out in close collaboration by a multidisciplinary bariatric team—including specialists in obesity medicine, nutrition, psychiatry, and ethics—to ensure a holistic approach to care. Through this case, we hope to emphasize the importance of a compassionate, collaborative, and individualized strategy for managing severe obesity in youth with progressive chronic conditions, where traditional approaches may not suffice.

Case presentation

Comprehensive obesity care—lifestyle modification and obesity pharmacotherapy

At his initial presentation to the multidisciplinary clinic, a 17-year-old Hispanic male with juvenile Huntington disease (JHD), diagnosed at age 11 years, had a body mass index (BMI) of 44 kg/m2 (190% of the 95th percentile; Fig. 1). His father was diagnosed with Huntington disease at age 39 years, and his younger brother was diagnosed with JHD at age 9 years. On physical examination at initial presentation, the patient demonstrated choreiform movements of the upper extremities, mild dysarthria, and a broad-based gait consistent with early neurocognitive and motor involvement of juvenile-onset Huntington disease, in addition to findings related to severe obesity, including acanthosis nigricans and limited exercise tolerance. Leading up to this consultation, he reported extreme food cravings and difficulties with portion control, contributing to further weight gain. He began intensive health and behavior lifestyle modification for obesity treatment, while in-depth conversations occurred with him and his mother regarding his goals of care. In addition, an ethics consult was requested to consider ethically appropriate options for obesity care given his underlying progressive condition. The ethics consultation addressed the appropriateness of surgical intervention in light of his medical condition, psychosocial circumstances, and the patient’s expressed desire to improve his quality of life. The ethics consultant facilitated an interdisciplinary meeting including members of the Ethics Committee and representatives from Neurology, Pulmonary, Cardiology, Endocrinology, Bariatric Surgery, Psychology, Palliative Care, and Nutrition. The patient’s primary motivation for pursuing bariatric surgery was to enhance his quality of life by enabling him to participate in activities he values, such as travel and social interactions, before his prognosis potentially declines further. Given his understanding of a shortened life expectancy—estimated at 3 to 6 years—the patient and his mother were eager to explore interventions that could facilitate a more fulfilling experience in his remaining years. After deliberation with the patient, family, and the collaborative clinical team, he was started on semaglutide, initially starting at a dose of 0.25 mg and titrating up to 1 mg weekly. After 3 months on semaglutide 1 mg weekly, he achieved an 7% BMI reduction, which decreased his BMI to 40 kg/m2.

Metabolic and bariatric surgery consideration

Despite significant reductions in his BMI, the patient continued to have elevated liver enzymes, blood lipid levels, and persistent obstructive sleep apnea requiring positive pressure ventilation. He was referred to the metabolic and bariatric surgery program for consideration of laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy. After completing the bariatric pathway, including medical, surgical, and psychological assessments, as well as a six-session nutrition education program, he underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy just before his 18th birthday, with no intraoperative or perioperative complications.

At 2 and 4 weeks postoperatively, he completed a validated eating behavior questionnaire, reporting a threefold reduction in hunger and a twofold increase in satiety. However, his emotional overeating and food responsiveness remained elevated, prompting the reintroduction of semaglutide, which was titrated from 0.25 mg to 1.7 mg weekly with no gastrointestinal side effects. At his 3-month postoperative visit, his BMI had decreased to 36 kg/m2 (130% of the 95th percentile), liver enzyme levels improved (alanine transaminase (ALT) 161 to 76 IU/L, Table 1), triglycerides decreased (508 to 275 mg/dL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels dropped (194 to 160 mg/dL). Additionally, he was weaned off his continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), resulting in significant improvements in sleep quality. He reported enhanced physical activity, mental clarity, and an increased desire to engage socially and travel, reflecting improvements in both his physical health and overall quality of life. At his 6-month postoperative visit, his BMI had decreased to 33 kg/m2 (a 25% BMI reduction from the start of comprehensive obesity treatment).

Discussion

The management of severe obesity in a young person with juvenile Huntington disease presents complex ethical and clinical considerations [1]. In this case, the ethics consultation was instrumental in determining whether obesity medication and bariatric surgery was appropriate for a young patient with a rapidly progressive, life-limiting neurodegenerative disease [5]. The core ethical question was whether the potential short-term benefits (improved quality of life, physical functioning, and reduced obesity-related complications) justified the risks of surgery, given the patient’s limited life expectancy and cognitive and emotional challenges [12].

Bariatric surgery, particularly laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, is generally safe in adolescents, with low complication rates [2]. In this case, anticipated rapid weight loss was expected to alleviate comorbidities such as liver dysfunction and obstructive sleep apnea, enhancing his physical well-being and overall quality of life more quickly than any other options. As this patient was transitioning from adolescence to young adulthood, a central ethical challenge was to ensure that both the patient and his mother were well informed and empowered in the shared decision-making process, and that any medical interventions aligned with the patient’s values, supporting his emerging autonomy and quality of life [3–5].

Concerns about cognitive impairments, including memory loss, depression, and hallucinations, prompted a rigorous assessment of the patient’s decisional capacity, ensuring that he understood his condition and the potential outcomes. Ultimately, the patient demonstrated adequate understanding and actively participated in discussions. The palliative care team facilitated in-depth assessment of the patient’s goals of care and his mother’s hopes and worries. The goal of surgical intervention was to address obesity-related health issues and to foster the patient’s agency by enabling him to participate in meaningful activities. The team also considered potential implications for end-of-life care, such as the impact of bariatric surgery on future nutritional support if tube feeding became necessary. It was concluded that the surgery would not preclude such care. Additionally, the patient’s do-not-resuscitate (DNR) and do-not-intubate (DNI) orders were reviewed to ensure alignment with his evolving wishes throughout the perioperative period [1, 6–9].

Beyond JHD, the management of obesity in pediatric patients with other neurodegenerative diseases, such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy, spinal muscular atrophy, and Batten disease, shares similar challenges. These conditions often involve progressive loss of mobility and muscle mass, which can mask or complicate obesity diagnosis and management. In such populations, early multidisciplinary intervention focused on nutrition, physical therapy, and symptom management is crucial. However, the use of metabolic therapies, including anti-obesity medications and bariatric surgery, remains understudied and ethically complex owing to the progressive nature of these diseases and variability in life expectancy.

This case may inform future clinical guidelines by illustrating the potential benefits of timely metabolic interventions in youth with progressive neurodegenerative disease. Clinical decision-making should consider not only disease severity and prognosis but also short-term goals related to quality of life, functionality, and patient autonomy. Integrating palliative care principles early in the obesity treatment pathway for these populations may help balance the risks and benefits of surgery, while ensuring that care aligns with the evolving values and wishes of the patient and family. This case also emphasizes the need for ongoing, long-term nutrition monitoring in late-stage neurodegenerative disease. As patients with JHD often experience cachexia and dysphagia in advanced stages, clinicians must anticipate and adapt nutritional strategies accordingly. Monitoring should focus on maintaining comfort, preventing aspiration, and supporting quality of life, while being mindful of goals of care.

As the disease progresses, follow-up strategies should become increasingly tailored to the patient’s functional status and goals. Early postoperative follow-up may prioritize weight loss and metabolic improvement, but later phases should focus on nutritional maintenance, palliative support, and symptom management. For example, as oral intake declines, the focus may shift to minimizing discomfort, ensuring hydration, and avoiding invasive interventions unless aligned with clearly defined care goals. Incorporating dietitians, speech-language pathologists, and palliative care early and consistently can support a dynamic, patient-centered approach to nutrition over time.

This case underscores the importance of a comprehensive, individualized approach to care for adolescents with complex, life-limiting conditions such as JHD [1, 9, 10]. Shared decision-making that balances clinical recommendations with the patient’s goals is critical for navigating these ethical complexities [1, 9, 11]. This case also highlights the necessity of tailoring medical care to the patient’s unique circumstances and desires, particularly in the context of a limited life expectancy and a rapidly progressive disease [3, 8, 12]. The decision to pursue bariatric surgery prioritized quality of life, autonomy, and careful monitoring of cognitive and emotional well-being. Given the patient’s limited life expectancy, surgical intervention was considered sooner than it might otherwise have been because it offered the best likelihood of achieving the patient’s goals in as short a time as possible [2]. Successfully addressing obesity in JHD requires an integrated, multifaceted strategy that considers the impact of neurological degeneration on both physical and psychological health, demonstrating the value of compassionate, patient-centered care in pediatric neurodegenerative diseases.

Limitations

While this case report illustrates a successful multimodal treatment approach to managing severe obesity in a young patient with JHD, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single case study, the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with JHD, as individual responses to treatment can vary depending on various factors, including disease stage, genetics, and psychosocial circumstances. Second, the relatively short follow-up period post-surgery limits our ability to assess the long-term sustainability of weight loss and the potential for recurrence of obesity-related complications. Additionally, the long-term impact of bariatric surgery in patients with JHD remains uncertain, especially considering that these patients tend to experience significant weight loss and emaciation toward the end of life as the disease progresses, and they may stop eating.

Conclusion

This case underscores the value of a multimodal treatment approach to severe obesity in a young patient with juvenile Huntington disease, highlighting the role of bariatric surgery, semaglutide, and health and behavior lifestyle modification in improving physical health and quality of life. By aligning treatment with the patient’s goals, and integrating input from a diverse multidisciplinary team, we can achieve significant improvements in both physical and emotional health outcomes. The ethical complexities of providing care to adolescents with complex, progressive diseases such as JHD emphasize the need for a patient-centered, compassionate approach that considers not only the clinical aspects of treatment but also the values, wishes, and autonomy of the patient. Moving forward, clinicians managing obesity in pediatric patients with neurodegenerative diseases should consider early, individualized, and ethically guided interventions. Future research should focus on outcomes of metabolic therapy and surgery in these populations, with particular attention to long-term nutritional needs, psychosocial impacts, and evolving goals of care. This case contributes to a growing body of evidence supporting the importance of patient-centered, values-aligned treatment strategies in medically complex pediatric populations.
