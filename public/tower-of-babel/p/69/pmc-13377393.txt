Street-Based Hepatitis C Treatment and Patient Experiences Among Adults Experiencing Unsheltered Homelessness
Authors: Alizée Natsoulis, Claire Nussbaum, Lauren Howard, Daphne Achilles, Korrinne Yurick, Camilo Zaks, Alexis Coulourides Kogan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat are the perspectives of patients experiencing unsheltered homelessness who are engaged in street-based hepatitis C virus (HCV) treatment?FindingsIn this qualitative study with 15 participants, adults experiencing unsheltered homelessness described common facilitators and barriers to HCV care, identified far-reaching psychosocial benefits of engaging with treatment beyond just achieving cure, and called for increased outreach and education about HCV treatment.MeaningThese findings suggest that street-based models of care are a promising approach to increasing access to HCV treatment for patients experiencing unsheltered homelessness; policy changes facilitating widespread access to HCV treatment are needed.

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is a blood-borne virus and a leading cause of chronic liver disease and liver transplant in the US.1 Approximately 1% of the general population is HCV-RNA positive, indicating current infection, which can progress to chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, or hepatocellular carcinoma.2,3 People experiencing homelessness are disproportionately affected, with prevalence estimates ranging from 10% to 53%, driven by higher rates of injection drug use, incarceration, and other risk factors.2,4,5 In Los Angeles, California, one study6 found a 27% positivity rate among adults experiencing homelessness, nearly half of whom were unaware of their infection.

People experiencing homelessness face persistent barriers to HCV care, including limited knowledge of transmission and treatment, lack of longitudinal care, and competing priorities for survival.7,8 The availability of highly effective direct-acting antivirals (DAAs), with fewer adverse effects and shorter treatment courses than previous therapies, presents an opportunity to improve treatment engagement.

Previous interventions targeting sheltered homeless individuals (ie, people experiencing homelessness who access shelters or temporary housing) have demonstrated high HCV cure rates with DAAs, measured by sustained virologic response (SVR), ranging from 82% to 97% of those engaged in treatment.9,10,11 However, linkage to care remains a challenge, and little is known about treatment success among people experiencing unsheltered homelessness (“rough sleeping” in a place not intended for human habitation).12 Research is needed on innovative models that improve treatment engagement for this particularly marginalized and often underengaged population.

In the street medicine model, full spectrum primary care is delivered directly to people experiencing unsheltered homelessness in their own environment (eg, encampments, under bridges, and near freeways), offering a promising model to reduce barriers to care.13,14 Due to recent policy changes resulting in Medicaid coverage of DAAs in some US states, street medicine teams in those states appear ideally positioned to screen for and treat HCV among individuals experiencing unsheltered homelessness.15,16 The purpose of this study was to explore the perspectives of patients who engaged in street-based treatment for HCV, assess treatment outcomes, and compare these findings to clinicians’ predictions.

Methods

We conducted one-time, in-person, semistructured interviews with patients who engaged in street-based HCV treatment through a street medicine program from June to August 2023. This qualitative study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board at the University of Southern California (USC). This study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) reporting guideline.

Setting

This study was conducted with USC Street Medicine, which has provided full-spectrum primary care to people experiencing unsheltered homelessness in Los Angeles County since 2018. All patient encounters, including physical examination, laboratory sample draws, and dispensing of medications, are conducted at the patient’s place of residence or preferred location (eg, encampment, sidewalk, or temporary shelter). Core members of the USC Street Medicine team include a prescriber, registered nurse, social worker, and community health worker. Consultative specialty care is provided as needed. In June 2022, Medi-Cal Rx, the statewide pharmacy benefits program for Californians on Medicaid, removed specialty-based and severity-based restrictions on HCV treatment.17,18 The USC Street Medicine program therefore began drawing annual HCV screening laboratory samples, including reflex testing to HCV RNA and genotype, from all adult patients and treating HCV-RNA–positive patients. Uncomplicated HCV was treated according to American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases and Infectious Diseases Society of American guidelines (ie, Simplified HCV Treatment Algorithm) and complicated HCV in consultation with USC’s Hepatology’s Project ECHO (Extension for Community Healthcare Outcomes) program.19,20

Participants and Procedures

Study eligibility criteria included adult patients (aged ≥18 years) served by USC Street Medicine who initiated street-based HCV treatment after a positive screen. We identified 24 patients who met the initial study eligibility criteria, of whom 9 were excluded for various reasons: death (n = 3), diagnosed with HCV and agreed to treatment but had not started treatment yet (n = 3), patient could no longer be located (n = 2), and stopped HCV treatment due to other diagnosis (n = 1). A total of 15 patients were eligible for the study (Figure).

A research assistant (A.N. and C.N.) accompanied the street medicine team during a regularly scheduled clinical care visit with prescreened patients to explain the research study, invite them to participate, and obtain written informed consent and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act authorization from those agreeing to participate (physical copies of the consent form were provided). Informed consent included permission to record and publish participant quotations. All 15 patients invited to participate agreed to do so and were interviewed on the spot by one of the research assistants (100% response rate). Seven of the 15 participants (47%) were interviewed after they completed treatment but before their SVR laboratory tests. Five of the fifteen (33%) were interviewed after completing treatment and receiving SVR laboratory results. One participant was interviewed 1 week before completing HCV treatment, and the 2 participants who did not fully complete treatment were interviewed after their HCV visits had concluded. Interviews followed a 4-item semistructured interview guide that elicits information about the decision to start treatment and the experience of engaging in the street-based model (see eAppendix in Supplement 1) codeveloped by all members of the study team and reviewed for face validity, clarity, and appropriateness by a USC Street Medicine team member with previous lived experience with homelessness. Interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent (all agreed to the recording) and transcribed verbatim, and transcripts were analyzed. Interviews lasted a mean (SD) of 10.25 (5.1) minutes. Participants were given $10 cash in appreciation for their time and commentary. Participant demographics, including age, gender identity, racial and ethnic identity, and housing status, were extracted after the interviews were completed from the electronic health record by a researcher (C.N.) and entered into a study dataset for analysis. Patient racial and ethnic identity data were collected to describe study participants and observe how these data compared with the overall patient census at USC Street Medicine and local point-in-time homelessness numbers. Additionally, the electronic health record was reviewed by a senior clinical member (C.Z.) of the research team to assess treatment measures and relevant HCV comorbidities: HCV treatment medication used, HCV treatment completion status, SVR results, HIV status, hepatitis B, cirrhosis, and substance use disorder. This included documenting the medication name; nominal yes, no, or other completed treatment (defined as an 8- or 12-week course of the corresponding medication); and nominal yes, no, or other achieved SVR (defined as undetected HCV viral load at blood draw at least 12 weeks after treatment).

Separately, the primary clinician responsible for each study participant’s care was asked about their level of confidence in the participant completing HCV treatment and their perception of the participant’s barriers and facilitators to treatment completion by a senior member of the study team (A.C.K.) (eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1). This qualitative feedback was provided in the office after participants had completed the interview, but clinicians were asked to reflect on their perspective at the time of the participant’s initiation of HCV treatment. Confidence ratings were assessed on a 10-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating not confident at all to 10 indicating totally confident in the participants ability to complete treatment. Data (quantitative rating and qualitative commentary) were recorded as typed notes directly into a study dataset for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Following a modified grounded theory21 approach with thematic analysis, 4 independent coders (A.N., C.N., L.H., and A.C.K.) analyzed the transcripts to identify themes derived from the data (inductive approach). The coding team independently conducted open and axial coding concurrently with data collection and iteratively met as a group to discuss transcripts in sets of 5. During group meetings the team compared open coding, determined initial codes, discussed emerging themes, and talked through disagreements. To ensure objectivity, an adjudicator (K.Y.) reviewed the initial set of themes alongside the transcripts to ensure that no salient themes were overlooked and to resolve coding discrepancies. Working independently once more, the 4 coders reapplied the themes and codes codeveloped in the meetings and identified direct quotations exemplifying each theme and subtheme. After analyzing 10 interviews, saturation was reached because no new concepts and themes were identified. The remaining 5 interviews were still analyzed and included. The coding team met again to review the themes and quotations, further pare down language, and sort and consolidate data until 100% consensus was reached. Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively and with a nonparametric Spearman correlations test (α level 2-sided P < .05) using SPSS statistical software, version 30 (SPSS Inc) from November to December 2023.

Results

In total, 15 USC street medicine patients (mean [SD] age, 48.8 [7.6] years; 12 [80%] male and 3 [20%] female; 1 African American or Black [7%], 11 Hispanic or Latinx [73%], and 3 White [20%]) participated in the study (Table 1). No diagnoses of HIV, cirrhosis, or hepatitis B were identified, although 10 participants (67%) had comorbid substance (or polysubstance) use disorder documented in the electronic health record. Thirteen participants (87%) were experiencing unsheltered homelessness, and 2 (13%) were in temporary housing at the time of HCV treatment initiation. Six participants (40%) transitioned into temporary housing after the treatment period but before being interviewed. One participant transitioned from temporary to permanent housing during the treatment period.

Participants followed 1 of 3 different treatment regimens determined by clinical considerations; 8 (53%) were receiving ledipasvir and sofosbuvir, 6 (40%) were receiving sofosbuvir and velpatasvir, and 1 (7%) was receiving lecaprevir and pibrentasvir. Of the 15 participants, 12 (80%) completed treatment. Two participants did not complete treatment, one of whom did not achieve SVR and the other of whom was lost to follow-up due to a change in the street medicine team’s contracted service area so his SVR status could not be confirmed. One participant was lost to follow-up in the middle of treatment; therefore, completion status could not be confirmed. Of the 12 participants who completed treatment, the team was unable to recheck and confirm SVR status for 2 participants because they were lost to follow-up due to contracted service area changes. The remaining 10 participants who completed treatment achieved SVR. Despite 4 participants being lost to follow-up before treatment completion or SVR laboratory testing, there were high rates of treatment completion (12 [80%]) and achievement of SVR (14 [91%]) among those retained in care.

Things That Changed

Many participants described substantial life changes that occurred shortly before or during HCV treatment. Some reported that increased housing stability facilitated treatment initiation, whereas others experienced greater housing or employment stability after starting treatment.

Completing treatment was also associated with changes in participants’ outlook on their lives, including increased optimism, self-confidence, and sense of agency. Given the challenge of adhering to daily medications for 8 to 12 weeks, successfully completing HCV treatment brought a strong sense of pride and accomplishment for participants. They reflected on being “serious” about treatment and taking medication “religiously” (participant 108).

Participants also described a deepened commitment to their health after completing treatment, including greater motivation to engage in care and a desire for longer, healthier lives. One participant said, “I’m quick to listen to what a doctor has to say now than I was before.” (participant 102).

Increased knowledge about HCV transmission and reinfection risk sometimes led to safer substance use behaviors. One participant shared that after treatment, “I don’t even touch a needle no more” (Table 2), whereas another described how he had “weaned off drugs.” (participant 108). Finally, participants reported improvements in perceived health and well-being after completing HCV treatment, citing improved energy, sleep, and stress.

Barriers to Treatment

Before engaging with the street medicine team, participants faced significant barriers to accessing HCV treatment due to being unsheltered. Many described how the lack of routine, general instability, and sleep disruptions made medication adherence difficult. Others expressed concerns about losing medications and lack of safe, private storage.

Fears about medication adverse effects, particularly for those diagnosed before the advent of DAAs, also discouraged treatment. Participants likened prior regimens to “chemo,” describing them as “devastating on the body” (Table 2). These concerns, combined with the often asymptomatic nature of chronic HCV, contributed to delayed or avoided care. One participant explained, “Hep C didn’t really bother me at all, so that’s why I had it for so long.” (participant 108).

Participants also described being previously denied treatment despite seeking it. One participant shared, “I had been wanting to do it [HCV treatment] for the last 10 years...I would tell them [other clinics or clinicians] about it [HCV diagnosis] and they wouldn’t give me the treatment for it” (Table 2). Several reported being diagnosed while incarcerated but never receiving adequate counseling or treatment. These experiences contributed to further distrust of the health care system and a perception that treatment was inaccessible.

Facilitators to Treatment

Many participants identified their support systems as crucial in facilitating their engagement with HCV treatment. Romantic partners, family members, and peers motivated them to seek care. One participant stated, “I’m glad to finally get rid of it [HCV]. And then my mom’s going to be happy, because my mom’s 87 years old. And she’s always telling me, ‘Go get help’.” (participant 109). Hearing about success stories from peers also served as a motivator; one participant was inspired by his friend: “He took the medication and he’s good now [achieved SVR]. He’s the one who told me about it, and he’s like, ‘So do it. Do it!’” (participant 109). Some participants also cited faith as a source of guidance and comfort during treatment.

The street medicine team was central to facilitating care. Participants emphasized the strength of their relationships with team members, describing the connection as deeply caring, trusting, and rooted in mutual respect and kindness. One participant stated, “I never had anybody really, really look into my life [like] that” and described their street medicine clinician as a “big sister” to them (participant 102). Consistent and frequent follow-up reduced logistical barriers. The team used strategies such as pill boxes, text reminders to take medication, and dispensing medication one week at a time. Importantly, these strategies were tailored to each patient and their unique circumstances. This person-centeredness is at the core of the street medicine model.

Advances in HCV treatment, including simpler regimens with fewer adverse effects, further facilitated engagement. Participants described these newer regimens as more tolerable, “just like taking vitamins.” (participant 111). In addition, the prospect of cure was a powerful motivator, as one participant noted, “I’m doing this [HCV treatment] to get rid of it [HCV]” (participant 109).

Communication

Participants frequently expressed their desire to share their treatment experiences and raise awareness that HCV is curable. Many were motivated by their own success and felt a responsibility to encourage others to seek treatment, including their friends. To this point, one participant declared, “I’m going to let a thousand mother---ckers know right now!” (Table 2).

Clinician Perceptions

Clinical care for each participant was led by 1 of 4 clinicians (1 physician and 3 physician assistants). On a scale of 1 to 10, where 1 meant not confident at all and 10 meant totally confident, clinicians’ confidence in the participant completing HCV treatment ranged from 4 to 9 (mean [SD], 6.5 [1.5]). High clinician confidence ratings did not consistently correspond with treatment completion and achieving SVR, just as low confidence ratings did not consistently correspond with discontinued treatment (Spearman r12 = 0.0, P > .99). Discussion

We aimed to explore the perspectives of patients experiencing unsheltered homelessness who engaged in a unique street-based model of HCV treatment to better understand how to meet the needs of this population. Our study elicited the perspectives of a mostly unsheltered homeless population, which makes up the majority of people experiencing homelessness in Los Angeles County and represents a particularly at-risk group with low health care access and utilization.22 In addition, most participants were Hispanic or Latino, addressing another gap in the literature, because evidence on HCV treatment outcomes among people who belong to minoritized racial and ethnic groups and are experiencing homelessness is limited. The barriers and facilitators identified in this study align with those described in previous qualitative literature on HCV treatment for unhoused individuals and offer important new insights on street-based models of care delivery.

Consistent with previous findings,12 participants in our study reported prolonged delays between diagnosis and treatment, underscoring persistent challenges with linkage to care in populations experiencing homelessness. As our participants described, the street medicine model addresses key obstacles by bringing care directly to patients, reducing logistical barriers, ensuring frequent follow-up, and cultivating deep, trusting patient-clinician relationships, all of which support treatment engagement among previously unengaged patient populations. Practical adherence strategies used by our team, such as pill boxes and text-based reminders, may be readily implemented by other programs serving similar populations.

Beyond achieving SVR, participants described broader downstream effects of HCV treatment, including increased confidence and sense of agency, renewed engagement in their health and well-being, and greater stability in areas, such as housing and employment. Together, these findings suggest that HCV treatment may serve as a catalyst for psychosocial benefits beyond viral cure.

Prior work8 shows that concerns about reinfection or nonadherence influence clinician perceptions of patient readiness to engage in HCV care, limiting access for patients experiencing unsheltered homelessness and patients who use drugs. The results from our clinician survey show that even street medicine clinicians, who know their patients’ health and social circumstances well, had varying confidence levels in patients’ ability to complete treatment, which did not consistently correspond with actual outcomes. The high rates of treatment completion and SVR in this study and other studies9,10,11 highlight the importance of moving beyond assumptions that certain populations are inherently difficult to reach or unlikely to adhere to treatment. By adopting a patient-centered approach, using innovative strategies to address barriers, and offering continuous encouragement, clinicians can better facilitate HCV treatment and help patients realize the wide-ranging benefits that often accompany treatment.

The participants themselves described appropriate next steps based on our findings. They emphasized the need for increased HCV outreach and education, highlighting peer support and word-of-mouth as effective motivators—an approach supported by prior studies.23,24 Seeing others successfully complete treatment appeared to drive engagement in treatment, illustrating an informal peer recruitment process at play in our patient population, which could be more formally integrated into future HCV treatment programs.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The study reflects the experiences of individuals primarily experiencing unsheltered homelessness in Los Angeles County and was conducted within a policy context that facilitated street-based HCV treatment through expanded Medicaid coverage of DAAs. As such, findings may not be transferable to other settings. As a qualitative study of a single street medicine program, our findings were not intended to be broadly generalizable. Instead, our goal was to elicit the perspectives of our patients to better serve this population. Participant selection may have introduced bias because interviews were only conducted among individuals who had already initiated treatment. These individuals may have had more positive perceptions of their health and greater motivation to engage in care than individuals who declined or never initiated therapy. Although we included perspectives from participants who were lost to follow-up or did not complete treatment, individuals who never engaged in HCV care were not represented. Future research should capture the full spectrum of patient experiences with HCV among those experiencing unsheltered homelessness, including comparing the experiences of those who engage in treatment with those who do not. Additionally, using a longitudinal study design to follow up patients from diagnosis through treatment and after treatment would provide valuable insight into barriers and facilitators of treatment and how engagement with HCV care influences perceptions of health.

Conclusions

In this qualitative study of street-based HCV treatment among adults experiencing unsheltered homelessness, we highlighted an innovative approach to care for an at-risk population with high HCV prevalence and low treatment engagement and found it was associated with high rates of treatment completion and cure, along with psychosocial benefits for patients. These findings suggest that similar street-based approaches may be valuable in other regions with large unsheltered populations and high rates of HCV. Sustainable implementation requires supportive policy environments, including Medicaid expansion and less restrictive DAA prescribing. Consistent and predictable funding streams are also critical to sustaining impact on patient health because funding cuts can disrupt continuity of care for patients who already face high levels of instability. Eliminating hepatitis C will never be achieved unless all patients, regardless of housing status, can access timely diagnosis and treatment.
