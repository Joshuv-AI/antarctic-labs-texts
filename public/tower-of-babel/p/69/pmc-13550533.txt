Assessing conventionally bred lentil (Lens culinaris Medik) cultivars for adaptation to organic cropping systems in the southeastern USA
Authors: Dil Thavarajah, Mark Dempsey, Tristan Lawrence, Emerson Shipe, Shiv Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing demand for organic plant-based protein calls for research aimed at adapting new specialty cash crops for winter production instead of leaving farmland fallow or using cover crops. Organic breeding programs for lentil (Lens culinaris Medik) are limited globally, and conventionally bred lentil cultivars are not well-adapted to organic systems. Lentil is a rich dietary source of protein and prebiotic carbohydrates, supporting human gut health. This study determined the grain yield, 100-seed weight (TSW), and nutritional quality traits of nine conventionally bred lentil cultivars grown in two USDA-certified organic on-farm locations in South Carolina (SC), USA, over 2 years. Grain yields ranged from 330 to 541 kg/ha with no significant difference between cultivars; these values are 18 to 34% of those reported for non-organic production. The low grain yields likely reflect poor adaptation of these conventionally bred cultivars to organic production conditions, compounded by mechanical harvesting limitations. Average days to flower (DF; 80 to 89 days) and days to mature (DM; 114–116 days) were both longer than reported values. Protein content ranged from 14.3 to 30.1 g/100 g, with 0.05–0.22 g/100 g of sulfur-containing amino acids (SAAs) and 85–91% protein digestibility. Total known prebiotic carbohydrates ranged from 1.9 to 10.9 g/100 g with 45–57 g/100 g total starch. Cultivars significantly varied in terms of TSW, protein, several prebiotic carbohydrates, and minerals (Ca, Mg, Zn). Lentil cultivar “Avondale” had significantly higher protein, TSW, two simple sugars, and verbascose + kestose than the other cultivars tested, which would be slightly better for organic adaptation. These results provide insights required to establish a lentil breeding pipeline under organic conditions. Germplasm selection from a large lentil diversity panel, sourced by location, would enable the development of biofortified, high-yielding lentil cultivars for organic production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-63027-8.

Introduction

Organic agriculture is the fastest-growing agriculture segment in the United States, with total sales of $9.6 billion in 2022, up 32% from 20171. The Pacific region (mainly California) leads in organic acreage and sales, accounting for ~ 39% of the total organic sales value in America.1 The global plant-based protein industry has also experienced steady growth; as a result, demand for plant-based protein has increased, driven by the need for pesticide-free plant proteins from organic legumes.

Marketing opportunities in America for organic specialty crops, including pulse crops (dry pea: Pisum sativum L., lentil: Lens culinaris Medik, and chickpea: Cicer arietinum L.), have increased, catering to ethnically diverse consumers and vegetarian communities2. Dry pea has been successfully adapted to the southeastern USA regions as an organic winter cash crop for plant-based protein production3,4. Lentil is a popular, low-cost, protein-dense, low-digestible carbohydrate and micronutrient-rich staple food crop for many populations. A 50 g serving of lentil provides 3.7–4.5 mg of iron (Fe), 2.2–2.7 mg of zinc (Zn), and 22–34 µg of selenium (Se) and is very low in phytic acid (2.5–4.4 mg/g) and other protein inhibitors5–7. Organic legume production is limited globally, and the nutritional quality of organically grown crops varies with the cropping conditions8–10. Legume-inclusive cropping systems have multiple benefits in line with organic agriculture: (1) at the food system level, lentil provides a significant amount of protein (20–25%), low-digestible carbohydrates (10–15 g/100 g), and a range of micronutrients with low phytate for both humans and animals11–13; (2) at the production system level, legumes fix atmospheric nitrogen (N) and improve soil phosphorus (P), making them more suitable for low-input cropping systems and mitigating greenhouse gas emissions as a result of no N and P fertilizer inputs14,15; and (3) at the cropping system level, legumes can be used as diversification crops in agroecosystems to break pest and disease cycles, conserve soil moisture, and improve soil health via soil microbial activity16. As a result, demand has increased for pulse crop varieties that are well-suited to organic farming systems17,18. Incorporating lentil or dry pea into their organic cropping systems will enhance crop productivity for growers and support the growing demand for nutritious food for many populations4.

Lentil is a cool-season food legume, mainly grown in Mediterranean climatic conditions. Global lentil production was 7.1 million tonnes in 2023, led by Australia (1.84 million tonnes), Canada (1.67 million tonnes), and India (1.56 million tonnes)19. American lentil production was 0.2 million tonnes, with the crop mainly grown in the midwestern regions due to its agronomic adaptability to cooler summers19. Lentils produced in the USA are typically conventionally grown, planted in May, and harvested in early fall. Organic lentil production is limited, and organic lentils on the market are typically imported at a higher price. Therefore, organic lentil represents a new winter cash crop opportunity in southern areas with mild winters, with planting in January and harvest by mid-May.

The conventionally bred lentil cultivars tested in this study were selected based on their commercial availability for testing in the organic variety trials, days to flower, days to maturity, and consumer acceptance (Table 1). The objective of this study was to determine the yield, 1000-seed weight (TSW), and nutritional quality traits of nine conventionally bred lentil cultivars grown at two USDA-certified organic on-farm locations in South Carolina (SC), USA, over 2 years.

Materials and methods

Materials

Standards, chemicals, and high-purity solvents used for prebiotic carbohydrate and mineral analysis were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA), Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA), and VWR International (Radnor, PA, USA) and were used without further purification. Distilled and deionized water (ddH2O) with a resistance of ≥ 18.2 MΩ × cm (PURELAB flex 2 system, ELGA LabWater North America, Woodridge, IL, USA) was used for sample and reagent preparation.

Experimental design

The experimental field design was a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with nine lentil cultivars with four replications at one location in 2022 and two locations in 2023 (n = 108; Table 1). Because the experiment was not implemented at both locations for both years, each location-year combination was treated as a unique environment. The lentil cultivars were obtained from Pulse USA (Bismarck, ND, USA) and the Washington State Crop Improvement Association (Pullman, WA, USA). These commercially available lentil cultivars were selected based on yield (range 1547–2753 kg/ha), days to flower (DF; range 48–55), days to mature (DM; 94), and market classes (Table 1; Supplementary Table S1).

Environments

The USDA-certified organic on-farm locations used for this study included WP Rawl and Sons (Pelion, SC, USA; 2022 and 2023) and Cherry Farm (Clemson, SC, USA; 2023 only). Pelion had a cooler winter (January to February) and less precipitation in 2022 than in 2023 (Table 2). Clemson had cooler temperatures and more precipitation than Pelion in 2023, indicating favorable weather conditions for organic lentil production (Table 2). Before sowing, two soil samples were randomly taken at a depth of 0–15 cm from each plot. The soil samples were homogenized, and three composite samples were analyzed for soil properties at the Clemson University Soil Testing Laboratory, SC, USA (Table 3). Pelion has sandy soils that are more alkaline than those at Clemson, which features clay loam (Table 3).

Field history and management

Before planting lentil, Pelion fields were managed with cover crops, including cereal rye (Secale cereale L.), sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench), and sudangrass (Sorghum bicolor var. sudanense) cover crops. Sudex (Sorghum bicolor × Sorghum bicolor var. sudanense) cover crops were planted before lentil was planted at the Clemson location. These cover crop practices are routine organic land management measures for pest and disease control, and the effects of land management with cover crops were not among the study objectives; therefore, differences between organic management systems were not evaluated. This study has only one cropping system: lentil planted in USDA-certified organic locations, preceded by warm-season cereal cover crops.

Fields were tilled using a disc harrow and then smoothed out to a level surface. All plots were then marked with a weatherproof, barcoded field tag, and the cultivar “CDC Maxim” was planted as a check or control to reduce the border effect. A cone plot planter was used to sow seed in 1.5 m × 3 m plots (4.65 m2) containing seven rows spaced 19 cm apart, with a seeding depth of 5–7 cm and a seeding rate of 130 seeds m− 2. Inoculants were applied as an in-furrow application (Premier Tech Agriculture | AGTIV Pulses Granular Mycorrhizal Inoculant with Rhizobium, Quebec, Canada) at a rate of 4.4 kg/ha. Organically certified fertilizers, pesticides, and chemicals were not used; weeds were removed by a mechanical cultivator attached to a small tractor. Irrigation was not provided, as growing season rainfall (ranging from 30 to 75 cm) is adequate for successful lentil production.

The Pelion field location was planted on February 1, 2022, and harvested on June 1, 2022. For the second year, it was planted on February 8, 2023, and harvested on July 13, 2023. The Clemson location was planted on February 22 and harvested on June 29 in 2023. At physiological maturity (110–120 days after planting), plots were harvested with a small-plot combine. Yield data were recorded for the Pelion location in 2022 and 2023; however, no yield data were recorded from the Clemson location due to a mechanical malfunction with the plot combine, resulting in the loss of more than 25% of the seeds during harvest. The scope of the lentil yield comparison was limited, as the data were collected at a single location over two years. Subsamples (100 g) of harvested air-dried seed (moisture content less than 10%) collected from each replication were hand-cleaned, finely ground using a UDY grinder, and stored at − 10 °C until analysis of nutritional quality. All data are reported on a dry weight basis (15% moisture), and all four replications per location were analyzed.

Yield and Test Weight (TSW)

Lentil grain yield (kg/ha) was calculated per plot for Pelion in 2022 and 2023. Yield data were adjusted to 15% moisture to match the standard moisture content used for nutritional data. TSW was calculated from the weight of 100 seeds, measured using a top-loading electronic balance4.

Protein, sulfur-containing amino acids (SAAs), protein digestibility, and total starch analysis

Finely ground lentil samples were used to measure protein, SAAs, protein digestibility, and total starch using a Cary 630 Fourier-transform mid-infrared (FT-MIR) spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) with attenuated total reflectance (ATR)20. Each spectrum was obtained with 100 scans, and a background scan of 200 scans was conducted every 30 min. The instrument was operated at a spectral resolution of 2 cm-1 with a zero-fill factor (ZFF) of 2 and employed a Happ–Genzel apodization. Spectral acquisition was performed with Micro Lab Personal Computer software (version B.06.5) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The ATR crystal surface was cleaned with high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade (assay > 99%) methanol (Fisher Scientific Company, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) between each sample. Data for each trait based on the acquired spectra were predicted from partial least squares (PLS) models. All chemometric analyses were performed with Micro Lab Expert software (version 1.1) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). All data are presented based on 15% moisture (dry weight basis), with values converted to total protein content by multiplying by 6.25. SAAs and protein digestibility values were only available for the two years (2022 and 2023) at the Pelion location.

Prebiotic carbohydrate analysis

Prebiotic carbohydrates were extracted using a previously described method21. Finely ground lentil dry seed samples (150 mg) were weighed into a centrifugal polypropylene tube (VWR International, Radnor, PA, USA). After 10 mL of water was added to each tube, the samples were vortex-mixed and placed in a water bath at 80 ℃ for 1 h. Tubes were then centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 13 mm × 0.45 μm nylon syringe filter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) into an HPLC vial. Carbohydrate analysis was done using a Dionex ICS-5000 + system (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a pulsed amperometric detector (PAD) with a working gold electrode and a silver-silver chloride reference electrode. Analyte separation was achieved using a Dionex CarboPac PA1 analytical column (250 mm × 4 mm) in series with a Dionex CarboPac PA1 guard column (50 mm × 4 mm). Pure standards were used to identify peaks, generate calibration curves, and monitor detector sensitivity; a lab reference sample was also used to monitor extraction consistency. Concentrations were quantified within a linear range of 0.1–500 ppm with a minimum detection limit of 0.1 ppm. Each carbohydrate’s concentration was calculated according to X = (C × V) / m, where X is the moisture-corrected analyte concentration in the sample, C is the concentration in the filtrate, V is the sample volume, and m is the mass of the sample.

Mineral analysis

Lentil seed minerals were measured using a modified acid digestion method followed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES)6. Finely powdered seed (200 g) was digested overnight in 4 mL of concentrated nitric acid (70% HNO3). The seed samples were then heated to 150 °C for 2 h, with 4 mL of 70% hydrochloric acid (HCl) added to the solution for 1 h. The digested solution was filtered through Whatman paper (20–25 μm) and diluted to 10 mL with deionized H2O. Mineral concentrations of potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), phosphorus (P), Fe, and Zn were determined by ICP-OES (ICP-6500 Duo, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Standards from a 1000 mg/L stock solution were serially diluted to produce calibration curves ranging from 0.5 to 5.0 mg/L. Ground lentil and National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) reference peach leaf (SRM 1547) samples were used as laboratory and standard references for data quality control.

Statistical analysis

The experimental design employed an RCBD, and the data were analyzed using mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA). Cultivar and environment were treated as fixed effects and tested against the variance components associated with the random terms (replicate and environment). Data from both years were combined (after testing for heterogeneity) and analyzed using a mixed model procedure (PROC MIXED) in JMP Pro 18 and SAS 9.422. Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) at P ≤ 0.05 was performed for mean separation. Pearson correlation coefficients among yield, TSW, and other traits were calculated, and ANOVA was used to determine if the effects were statistically significant. A statistical model was developed to estimate broad-sense heritability (H2), with variables and genotype treated as random effects. The model was calculated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. H2 was estimated as the proportion of variance due to genotype, and analyses were performed using JMP Pro 1822. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to identify underlying factors influencing overall cultivar performance; the phenotypic data were standardized prior to analysis. Two PCAs were conducted: one on protein traits and yield, and one on carbohydrate traits. PCAs were performed only on the data for the Pelion location due to yield loss at the Clemson location resulting from a mechanical malfunction.

Results

Analysis of variance

Cultivar was significant (P < 0.05) for yield, TSW, and most nutritional traits (Table 4). Yield, SAAs, and protein digestibility traits were only measured at the Pelion location for 2 years; cultivars were not significantly different for protein, SAAs, and protein digestibility, but environment was significant for all three traits, and the cultivar × environment interaction was significant for yield, TSW, and protein (Table 4). Environment was significant (P < 0.05) for protein concentration, with low broad-sense heritability estimates. Cultivar was significant (P < 0.05) for all sugar alcohols, with a range of values (2.2–43.7) for H2. Cultivar was significant (P < 0.05) for most simple sugars, except sucrose, and for the oligosaccharides, stachyose+raffinose and verbascose+kestose (Table 4). Lentil cultivar was not significant for total starch and nystose. Cultivar was significant at P < 0.05 for Ca, Mg, and Zn with low H2. Environment was significant for most nutritional traits, yield, and TSW; cultivar×environment significant in various cases (Table 4).

Nutritional quality

Lentil cultivars grown under organic conditions show a broad range of concentrations for protein (14.3–30.1 g/100 g), prebiotic carbohydrates (1.9–10.9 g/100 g), total starch (45–57 g/100 g), and minerals (K: 837–2067 mg/100 g; Ca: 34–178 mg/100 g; Mg: 92–171 mg/100 g; P: 439–777 mg/100 g; Fe: 5.3–15.7 mg/100 g; and Zn: 4.5–10.4 mg/100 g) (Table 5). Additionally, these organically grown lentil provides a significant portion of the recommended daily allowance (%RDA) of protein (31–65% for females and 26–54% for males), prebiotic carbohydrates (10–55%), and minerals (significant source of K, Mg, P, Fe, and Zn and a good source of Ca) from a 100 g serving on a dry weight basis, with high protein digestibility ranging from 85.2 to 91.4% (Table 5).

Correlation analysis

Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to determine the significant associations between yield and protein traits (protein, SAAs, and protein digestibility) at the Pelion location over two years (Fig. 1). Correlation analysis for total starch and prebiotic carbohydrates traits was performed for the Pelion location in 2022 and both locations in 2023 (Supplemental Table S2). A significant (P < 0.05) and positive correlation was observed between protein digestibility and yield (r = 0.81); a significant negative correlation was observed between SAAs and both yield (r = − 0.58) and protein (r = − 0.27); and a significant negative correlation was noted between protein digestibility and SAAs (r = − 0.69) (Fig. 1). However, most significant correlations were driven by differences between years, and when years were analyzed separately, the only significant association was between SAA and protein digestibility in 2023 (r = 0.75; results for by-year analyses are not shown). Total starch was significantly and negatively correlated with all prebiotic carbohydrates except xylitol and rhamnose (Supplemental Table S1).

Principal component analysis

For the protein quality traits and yield, the first two principal components explained a substantial amount of the variance: (1) component 1 (58%): SAAs, protein digestibility, and yield; and (2) component 2 (25.8%): protein (Fig. 2). The traits contributing mostly to protein digestibility, SAAs, and yield are explained by PCA component 1, and protein is explained by component 2 (Fig. 2). For prebiotic carbohydrates and total starch, the first four principal components explained a substantial amount of the variance: (1) component 1 (42.9%): sorbitol, myo-inositol, total starch, xylitol, maltose, and rhamnose; (2) component 2 (18.3%): fructose, glucose, mannitol, and ribose, (3) component 3 (12.9%): stachyose+raffinose, verbascose+kestose, sucrose, and galactinol, and (4) component 4 (7.3%): arabinose and nystose (Fig. 3). For the prebiotic carbohydrate traits, most are explained by PCA component 1, most simple sugar traits are explained by the PCA component 2, and most RFO traits are explained by the PCA component 3.

Cultivar responses

All lentil cultivars had very low yields under organic conditions, with no significant differences observed among cultivars (Table 6). The lentil cultivar “Avondale” had a significantly higher protein concentration than “CDC Maxim”; SAAs and protein digestibility were consistent between the two cultivars. For TSW, “Avondale” and “CDC Impress” had significantly higher values than “CDC Dazil”, “CDC Invincible”, and “CDC Impala”. For prebiotic carbohydrates, significant cultivar differences were observed for two sugar alcohols (myo-inositol and mannitol), four simple sugars (arabinose, rhamnose, ribose, and maltose), and verbascose+kestose (Supplemental Table 3). Among the cultivars, “Avondale” had significantly higher concentrations of mannitol, rhamnose, and verbascose+kestose than all other cultivars tested. Significant cultivar differences in minerals were only observed for Ca, Mg, and Zn (Supplemental Table 4). Lentil cultivars “CDC Impress” and “CDC Maxim” had the highest concentrations of Ca; “CDC Dazil” had the highest concentration of Mg; and “CDC Dazil” and “CDC Impress” had the highest concentrations of Zn.

Discussion

Pulse crops in organic cropping systems can provide sustainable environmental and nutritional benefits to global populations. This study was designed to provide preliminary information on the performance of conventionally bred spring lentil cultivars for organic cropping as a winter cash crop by assessing variation in seed yield and nutritional quality. While the number of cultivars and growing environments included in this study was limited, yields of conventionally bred lentil cultivars grown under organic management were lower than those grown under conventional management. In fact, yields in this study were only 18–34% that of the published yield data (Tables 1 and 6; Supplementary Table S1). Because this study did not directly compare performance of organically bred vs. conventionally bred lentils, results should be interpreted cautiously. However, results suggest that cultivar development under organic management may be needed to improve agronomic performance, and further testing is needed to confirm results. Among the lentil cultivars tested, “Avondale”, “CDC Imvincible”, “CDC Maxim”, and “CDC Proclaim” had the highest yields in this study. Yields significantly varied by environment (Pelion 2022 vs. 2023) and the interaction of cultivar × environment (P < 0.05), indicating that lentil cultivar performance is sensitive to environmental conditions, including rainfall and temperature23. Similar results were reported in a study that tested 20 conventionally bred lentil cultivars in organic cropping systems across five environments in Greece18. In their study, the conventional system produced 3–37% higher yields than organic cropping systems, primarily due to cultivar adaptability and chemical management18. A similar study reported that conventionally bred lentil cultivars yield poorly and exhibit poor disease resistance in an organic system where no agrochemicals were used, a key consideration in organic lentil breeding17. Although the small dataset and low heritability estimates constrain conclusions about the genetic potential of the cultivars tested, the current study can inform future organic lentil cultivar development by selecting the most suitable parents for organic systems that exhibit greater yield, disease resistance, and weed suppression. Longer-term, more genetic studies are needed with larger, more diverse populations to identify the genetic underpinnings of these traits and incorporate them into new cultivars, as the cultivars evaluated here showed limited adaptation to the organic conditions tested in this study23. For several major traits, heritability estimates were low, likely due to the limited number of cultivars and environments tested, and the substantial environmental influence on trait expression. Consequently, cultivar performance should be interpreted cautiously until validated across broader multi-environment trials conducted over multiple years.

Lentil is a well-researched pulse crop for biofortification and a sustainable whole-food source of plant-based protein, prebiotic carbohydrates, and minerals11,13,24–27. In the context of this study (nine cultivars evaluated at two to three environments), our results indicate that organically grown lentil is rich in protein, including SAAs, with high protein digestibility and a notable portion of the RDA (Table 5). The values obtained in this study are similar to those previously reported23,25,28. Prebiotic carbohydrates and starch are present at levels similar to those grown in conventional cropping systems11,28,29. Organically grown lentil is also a significant dietary source of K, Mg, P, Fe, and Zn, and a moderate source of Ca (Table 5). As such, results from our study suggest that organically grown lentil can provide similar nutritional benefits as conventionally grown lentil, but without the use of added fertilizers or pesticides. Our correlation analysis reveals that yield is significantly negatively correlated with SAAs and positively correlated with protein digestibility. Protein is significantly and negatively correlated with SAAs, whereas protein digestibility is not correlated with SAAs (Fig. 1). Our PCA supports these results, with protein digestibility and yield having similar loadings and accounting for the majority of the variance on PCA component 1, and with protein loading separately along PCA component 2 (Fig. 2). Globally, pulse breeding programs focus mainly on improving yield and total protein quantity rather than protein quality traits such as SAAs and digestibility23,26,28. However, traits such as protein digestibility and SAAs can indicate better quality protein and increased protein bioavailability for human health4,12,25. More in-depth quantitative genetic studies are required to understand the correlation between protein quality traits and yield, as most traits are controlled by many genes12,26.

Lentil is a rich source of prebiotic or low-digestible carbohydrates that support a healthy gut microbiome and weight management. Among the low-digestible carbohydrates, organically grown lentil has relatively high concentrations of sorbitol, sucrose, stachyose, and raffinose, with values comparable to those reported in previous studies30,31. Higher concentrations of simple sugars may contribute to the taste of pulse crops, but these sugars are essential for plant resistance to biotic stress32–35. Specifically, sugar alcohols, disaccharides, and oligosaccharides play a crucial role in plant responses to high temperatures, drought, and oxidative stress, and accumulate in seeds in response to moisture deficit36,37. Our PCA analysis indicates, generally, these sugars loaded differently onto principal components, e.g., sorbitol and sucrose loaded on component 1, stachyose+raffinose, and verbascose+kestose loaded on component 3. This suggests different genetic mechanisms control these sugars in lentil; however, detailed genetic studies have yet to be conducted24,36,38.

Organic crop breeding focuses not only on cultivar adaptability and yield but also on consistent performance across a wide range of low-input production systems. This includes traits such as nutrient and water use efficiency, large stature to compete with weeds, resistance to insect pests and disease pressure, and the ability to host beneficial soil microorganisms (e.g., rhizobacteria and mycorrhizal fungi). Consistent performance across low-input systems suggests at least some of these traits are integrated into new cultivars, regardless of whether a breeding program explicitly targets them. The organic breeding process must also comply with the organic guiding principles, such as preserving genetic diversity and protecting environmental quality. It should comply with organic regulations, including maintaining organic integrity and avoiding the use of genetically modified organisms. These cultivars should exhibit high yield stability with minimal or no chemical inputs and produce seed with high nutritional quality18,39–41. Our study, using a limited set of cultivars and growing environments, suggests that conventionally bred lentil cultivars may have limited yield adaptation potential for the organic farming system. Still, their nutritional quality is similar to that of conventionally grown lentils. For successful lentil production in organic cropping systems, both crop management and the cultivars used must be optimized based on overall crop and pest management, genetic diversity, resistance to biotic stress, and organic farming practices tailored to the value of niche crops and their markets23,40–43.

The organic lentil breeding pipeline should be dedicated to advancing breeding material under continuous organic management through all screening stages. This practice can help develop appropriate cultivars with stable yields, weed competitiveness, tolerance to abiotic stress, and resistance to diseases and insect pests. In pulse crops, traits including seed yield, protein, and mineral concentration are influenced by both additive and non-additive genetic components, while genotype × environment interactions can strongly affect trait expression across contrasting production environments45,46. The considerable variation in yield and nutritional traits among cultivars observed in this study highlights the importance of selecting appropriate parental selection lines to develop cultivars adapted to organic and low-input production systems47–54. While low-input, organically managed lentil may have lower yield potential than high-input, conventionally managed lentil, the yield of organically grown lentil could likely be improved through organic cultivar development.

Conclusions

These preliminary results suggest that lentil has potential as a winter cash crop in organic systems in the southeastern USA, although additional multi-environment testing is needed. In this study, organically grown, conventionally bred lentil has a nutritional profile similar to that of conventionally grown lentil, albeit with considerably lower yield. Organically grown lentil is a rich source of protein, SAAs, prebiotic carbohydrates, and minerals with high protein digestibility. Still, in the context of this limited study, conventionally bred lentil cultivars do not appear to be well-suited for organic systems due to low yields. Of those cultivars tested here, “Avondale” is notable for its somewhat higher yield and protein concentration; thus it may be a suitable parent for future organic lentil breeding programs. However, this result should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of growing environments and genotypes tested, as well as the low heritability estimates for these traits. Further genetic studies are required to initiate the lentil breeding pipeline with a lentil diversity panel to select short-season (90–95 days to maturity), high-yielding cultivars for organic on-farm selections.
