Patient with portal vein thrombosis and suppurative pylephlebitis presenting with multiple liver abscesses: a case report
Authors: Murat Ay, Veli Eroglu, Oguzhan Tokur, Balca Begüm Cengiz, Merve Ay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPylephlebitis (or thrombophlebitis) of the portal vein system is defined as infective suppurative thrombosis affecting the portal vein and is a rare pathology associated with high rates of mortality and morbidity due to intra-abdominal infection. This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges, complex clinical course, and successful multimodal management of suppurative pylephlebitis with multiple liver abscesses following laparoscopic appendectomy.ReportA 56-year-old White Turkish male patient presented to the emergency room with severe right lower- and middle-quadrant abdominal pain. The patient was septic and had a recent history of laparoscopic surgery for perforated appendectomy. The patient responded to meropenem and daptomycin antibiotic therapy, with significant clinical and laboratory improvement, and rivaroxaban was used as anticoagulant therapy. Thus, bacteremia should be evaluated, repeated blood cultures should be obtained, and a differential diagnosis of thrombosis should be considered. Background

Pylephlebitis (or thrombophlebitis) of the portal vein system is defined as infective suppurative thrombosis affecting the portal vein and is a rarely observed pathology that can complicate splanchnic infections and inflammation, which develop as a result of intra-abdominal infections, with severe mortality and morbidity being reported [1]. The incidence of pylephlebitis is low; thus, randomized controlled and prospective studies describing its risk factors, diagnosis, and treatment are limited. This condition is usually associated with gram-negative bacteremia, and its incidence rate is 2.7 cases per 100,000 people per year [2]. Delays in diagnosis and treatment are usually observed, thus explaining a mortality rate of 8.7% [3]. Pylephlebitis is characterized by abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, fever, asthenia, and jaundice in patients with liver involvement. Diagnosis is difficult, because the findings are non-specific [1]. In addition, there is no common consensus regarding disease management, because there are limited data and case reports documenting this condition in the literature.

Any intra-abdominal or pelvic infection with portal venous drainage may be further clinically complicated by pylephlebitis. Anatomically, the portal vein is formed by the union of the superior mesenteric vein and splenic veins. The portal vein drains most of the intra-abdominal portion of the gastrointestinal tract (except for the lower part of the rectum). In pylephlebitis, the process begins with thrombophlebitis of small vessels surrounding an infected area and spreads to the main branches of the portal vein. Diverticulitis (26.5%) and acute appendicitis (22%) are the two most common causes of pylephlebitis [3]. In addition, cholangitis/cholecystitis (3.5%) and pancreatitis (5.5%) constitute other causes of pylephlebitis. Positive blood cultures are definitive evidence of infection; however, many patients do not demonstrate positive blood cultures despite the presence of infective thrombi in the portal system [4]. In approximately 11.5% of pylephlebitis cases, the infection source cannot be identified [3]. Some studies have suggested that the hypercoagulable state constitutes the initial stage of pylephlebitis. For example, in a case series with 44 patients, the hypercoagulable state was reported to be caused by malignancy, coagulation factor deficiency, or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection [5, 6]. As the disease progresses, complications such as sepsis, metastatic abscesses, and multi-organ failure may develop, thus leading to a mortality rate of 19% [7]. This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges, complex clinical course, and successful multimodal management of suppurative pylephlebitis with multiple liver abscesses following laparoscopic appendectomy.

Case history/examination

A 56-year-old White Turkish male patient was admitted to the emergency department with severe right lower- and middle-quadrant abdominal pain. The patient was diagnosed with perforated appendicitis at an external center based on clinical examination, investigations, and imaging. The patient underwent emergency laparoscopic surgery, and newly diagnosed case of diabetes mellitus (DM) was detected during clinical follow-up. The patient was discharged after 2 days of postoperative follow-up. Ten days after discharge, the patient experienced hypotension, severe abdominal pain, and tenderness (especially in the right upper quadrant), and acute-phase elevation was detected. The patient was followed up in our clinic due to the lack of a ward or intensive care unit in the clinic, where the surgery was performed.

There was no history of smoking, and no previous surgical history was reported in his medical history file. Moreover, there was no known recent use of additional medication except for antibiotic administration during the hospital follow-up. He had no known history of rheumatological disease, vasculitis, malignancy, or thrombosis. In addition, there was no history of hereditary thrombophilia reported in the patient’s family history. The patient was diagnosed with DM 2 weeks before the follow-up and was not receiving regular treatment. In addition, there was a history of short-term intravenous ertapenem antibiotic use during postoperative follow-up, and his HbA1c level was 12.3%. The patient had no prior known diagnosis of DM, presented with newly developed and acute hyperglycemia, had a body mass index of 23 kg/m2, and was not considered to have the typical phenotype of type 2 DM. Therefore, autoantibody tests, as well as those for determining fasting insulin and C-peptide levels, were requested for the purpose of DM classification. Results showed that his serum insulin and C-peptide levels were normal, whereas his antibody status for anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase (anti-GAD), anti-insulin, and pancreatic islet cells was negative. Diabetes typing revealed that the patient was diagnosed with type 2 DM, so insulin detemir and insulin aspart were initiated. In the patient’s initial evaluation, the physical examination findings included an admission blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg, sinus tachycardia, tenderness and voluntary defense in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen, a body temperature of 38 °C, and a fasting blood glucose (FPG) concentration of 348 mg/dL. The patient complained of anorexia, which had been increasing in frequency for the past week. Bilateral radial pulses were palpated, and no petechiae or purpura was detected. Homan’s sign in the bilateral lower extremity was negative, and the abdominal examination revealed no evidence of ascites. No additional encephalopathy was detected, and an electrocardiogram of the patient at admission revealed no pathological findings except for sinus tachycardia. The patient’s Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)-2 score at admission was 4, indicating high-risk sepsis. Throughout the acute phase, the patient was managed in a general internal medicine ward with close hemodynamic monitoring rather than in an intensive care unit. The admission hypotension (90/60 mmHg) responded to intravenous fluid resuscitation alone, and vasopressor support was not required at any point during the clinical course. Blood, urine, and sputum cultures were obtained from the patient in two sets at specified intervals. The patient had a history of ertapenem and cefazolin intravenous antibiotic therapy lasting 5 days at the external center, where the surgery was performed. However, there was no clinical or laboratory evidence of a response to this treatment. Therefore, based on the results of the Infectious Disease consultation and considering the patient's recent history of abdominal surgery and hospitalization, it was decided to re-evaluate the patient's antibiotic therapy. Antibiotic therapy was re-evaluated by the Infectious Diseases Department, with a focus on suspected nosocomial infection. The patient was administered intravenous antibiotic therapy with 3 × 1 g/day meropenem and 2 × 50 mg/day tigecycline. Details of the laboratory analysis performed at admission are shown in Table 1.

At the initial evaluation stage, abdominal ultrasonography (US) was performed on the patient, who had described tenderness in the right upper quadrant. US, which was performed by an experienced operator, revealed a liver size of 20 cm and heterogeneity in the parenchyma. In addition, multiple hypoechoic, dense multiloculated cystic lesions were detected, the largest of which was 8 cm in the portal hilus. The main portal vein diameter was 14 mm, and isoechogenous thrombus material was detected in the portal vein lumen. Complete occlusion was detected when the portal vein was evaluated via color Doppler ultrasonography (RDUS). The spleen size was determined to be 16 cm, and splenomegaly was observed. Since the patient had portal vein thrombosis, a recent history of perforated appendicitis and appendectomy, and suspicion of sepsis progression, a prediagnosis of suppurative pylephlebitis was considered based on the current clinical and imaging findings. Contrast-enhanced abdominal tomography (CT) was performed for further evaluation. In addition, contrast-enhanced angiography of the renal vein and artery, upper extremity, lower extremity, and mesenteric vein and artery was performed for etiologic evaluation. There was no evidence of thrombosis at any site other than the portal vein. Abdominal CT revealed hepatomegaly (with a liver size of 20.5 cm), a geographic pattern secondary to perfusion damage in the parenchyma, thrombus materials in the portal vein branches from the confluence level in the portal vein, total occlusion of the lumen, and multifocal and multicystic lesions in the wall along the traces of the thrombus materials, the largest of which was 8 × 6 cm in the hilus. In addition, cavernous transformation was observed in the portal hilus. Fat necrosis was detected between the mesenteric fat planes up to the hepatic flexure level. Liver biopsy was performed using Interventional Radiology, as infiltrative lesions in the liver could not be ruled out in the abdominal CT results. Liver biopsy pathology revealed sinusoidal dilatation in the liver parenchyma and mild interface hepatitis in the portal area. Prophylaxis treatment with 40 mg of enoxaparin (initiated at admission) was increased to 80 mg/day (1 mg/kg/day) after portal vein thrombosis was detected. Embolectomy was considered necessary due to the risk of developing hepatic failure, as evidenced by total occlusion of the portal vein and its branches, worsening right upper quadrant pain, and newly developed grade-2 ascites. The opinions of the cardiovascular surgery (CVS) and general surgery clinics were obtained for portal vein thrombosis embolectomy. However, the patient was considered to have a high mortality risk if embolectomy was performed, so it was avoided. Echocardiography was requested from the patient (whose clinical findings were inconsistent in terms of infective endocarditis), and no vegetation was detected by the Cardiology clinic. Given the absence of a clear infectious source on initial workup and the presence of portal vein thrombosis in a relatively young patient without known risk factors, a systematic evaluation for secondary causes of thrombosis was deemed mandatory. This included screening for vasculitis, autoimmune conditions, inherited thrombophilia, myeloproliferative disorders, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, and occult malignancy—all of which are recognized etiologies of non-cirrhotic portal vein thrombosis and require exclusion before a septic/infectious etiology can be confirmed. Recurrent oral aphthae, papulopustular lesions, erythema nodosum, genital ulcers, and arthritis were not detected; however, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) B51 expression was evaluated for the etiology of portal vein thrombosis. In addition, CD55 and CD59 levels were assessed in the peripheral blood for possible paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH). In addition, the patient was evaluated for possible granulomatous polyangiitis (GPA), microscopic polyangiitis (mPA), and polyarteritis nodosa (PAN). Moreover, tests for extensive hepatitis viral serology, the anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), and ANCA subtypes, including anti-protease-3 ANCA (PR3-ANCA) and anti-myeloperoxidase–ANCA (MPO–ANCA), were ordered. To rule out possible systemic lupus erythematosus, tests evaluating 24 h urine proteinuria, the anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), antibodies against double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), and complement C3 and C4 were ordered. Finally, hereditary thrombophilia panel Janus kinase-2 (JAK2) V617F, Quantiferon, anti-mitochondrial antibody (AMA), liver kidney microsomal type-1 (anti-LKM1) antibody, and anti-smooth muscle antibody (ASMA) assays were performed to rule out possible autoimmune hepatitis in the patient, who exhibited signs of liver cirrhosis. Upper-gastrointestinal-tract (GIS) endoscopy and colonoscopy were performed to rule out possible malignancy in the patient, with no suspicion of additional malignancy based on imaging findings. No pathological findings or esophageal varices were observed during upper-GIS endoscopy. The results of the etiologic investigations and detailed analysis are given in Table 2. Warfarin was considered suitable as anticoagulant therapy, as the patient did not present with acquired bleeding disorders, including acute hepatic failure, acute stroke, active peptic ulcer, thrombocytopenia, or hypertensive urgency or emergency, or inherited bleeding disorders, such as hemophilia. Warfarin sodium was initiated in the patient, who was deemed to be unsuitable for surgery by the General Surgery and CVS clinics due to a high mortality risk. Warfarin sodium was continued with enoxaparin at the therapeutic dose until the INR was >2, after which enoxaparin was discontinued. The target therapeutic INR range was 2–3, and no growth was observed in the blood, urine, or sputum cultures collected before the start of treatment. In addition, the patient (who exhibited acute-phase reactants and a clinical response to antibiotic therapy involving 3 × 1 g/day meropenem and 2 × 50 mg/day tigecycline for 5 days) was re-evaluated upon clinical- and acute-phase reactant progression, and blood cultures were obtained and renewed intermittently in two sets. An Infectious Disease consultation regarding antibiotic therapy was also requested, and adding daptomycin antibiotic treatment was deemed appropriate, as it could cover possible pathogens, such as Enterococci, Streptococci, and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus. In addition, daptomycin antibiotic therapy was preferred over vancomycin due to the potential risk of acute kidney injury in patients with sepsis. Daptomycin was initiated at a dosage of 10 mg/kg/day because of its Gram (+) inclusive effect; moreover, tigecycline antibiotic therapy was discontinued in favor of meropenem antibiotic therapy. After clinical follow-up, the patient (whose treatment was reorganized) was referred to the Interventional Radiology clinic for drainage. Since the cystic lesions in the hilus of the patient (whose imaging was renewed using Interventional Radiology) demonstrated shrinkage, it was primarily thought that the patient had responded to medical antibiotic therapy, and reconsultation was recommended if clinical progression was observed. After 4 days of clinical follow-up, hypervolemia was observed in the patient (who had responded to intravenous resuscitation); moreover, no hypotension was observed, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) regression was observed. Furthermore, intravenous resuscitation was discontinued after grade-1 ascites was observed, which could not be detected on abdominal examination but was identified via US; in addition, bilateral 1.5 cm pleural effusion was detected on imaging, but encephalopathy was not detected. Spironolactone (100 mg/day), furosemide (40 mg/day), and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) (750 mg/day) were administered, and a fluid restriction of 1.5 L/day was applied. In addition, a 40 g/day intravenous albumin replacement was provided for 2 days, and after 2 days of follow-up, euvolemia was achieved again. Based on the opinion of the Infectious Diseases clinic, it was decided that daptomycin intravenous antibiotic therapy should be completed within 1 month. A significant clinical- and acute-phase reactant regression response was obtained 1 week after daptomycin antibiotic therapy initiation. No increase in creatine kinase (CK) activity was observed during follow-up, and no side effects due to daptomycin-induced rhabdomyolysis were observed. When the patient was evaluated according to all of the investigations, all secondary causes were excluded, and the diagnosis of suppurative pylephlebitis was confirmed. On the 20th day of daptomycin intravenous antibiotic therapy, the physician at the Interventional Radiology clinic recommended response evaluation and drainage (if necessary). The size of the largest microabscess was determined to have regressed to 4 mm, and an almost complete treatment response was observed. In addition, the millimeter-sized microabscesses had completely regressed, so drainage was once again deemed unnecessary.

Daptomycin antibiotic therapy was administered for 1 month, where the patient received tigecycline from day 1 to day 5, meropenem from day 1 to day 30, and daptomycin from day 6 to day 35. With the patient’s INR being observed within the therapeutic range with warfarin sodium treatment, a switch to rivaroxaban for portal vein thrombosis treatment was planned, and warfarin sodium was discontinued; moreover, the INR was controlled 3 days later due to its half-life of 36–48 h, and warfarin was switched to rivaroxaban, because the INR was <2.5. The patient (who demonstrated a complete treatment response in the clinical and acute phases) was then discharged. Their treatment evaluation plan involved follow-up imaging every 3 months; the patient’s C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin, and D-dimer levels, which were obtained at the 35-day follow-up, are shown in Figs. 6, 7, and 8, respectively.

The patient was re-evaluated at 1 month after discharge, and clinical SIRS findings were not observed; moreover, abdominal examination did not reveal ascites, and neurological examination did not reveal encephalopathy. Laboratory analysis revealed negative acute-phase reactants, and rivaroxaban was continued. The patient was further re-evaluated 3 months after discharge, and a control abdominal CT scan with contrast was performed, which revealed that the liver was determined to be 16 cm long, the contours were regular, and contrast filling had occurred in the portal vein up to the hilus level; however, no contrast filling was observed in the intrahepatic branches, suggesting the presence of a chronic thrombus. Infected thrombus findings in the left lobe branch of the portal vein were completely regressed, and microabscess foci in the distal portal vein branches were almost completely regressed, so rivaroxaban treatment was continued; in addition, imaging follow-ups were performed every 3 months, and outpatient clinical follow-up was continued. Discussion

The most common etiologic causes of suppurative thrombophlebitis in the porto-mesenteric axis include diverticulitis (26.5%), appendicitis (22%), chronic inflammatory bowel disease (6%), acute pancreatitis (5.5%), infectious enteritis, intestinal perforation, and gastrointestinal tract malignancies (6%) [8]. Suppurative pylephlebitis originates from infectious processes that are initiated in organs draining into the portal system. This condition spreads from peripheral veins to the portal vein and intrahepatic portal branches. Moreover, it may cause upper portal-mesenteric thrombosis, bowel ischemia, and peritonitis, and bacteremia-induced sepsis, septic shock and multiple liver abscesses may be observed during clinical progression. Escherichia coli growth in blood cultures was observed in a case report by Falkowski et al., in which sigmoid diverticulitis was complicated with thrombophlebitis in the mesenteric vein [9]. In our patient exhibiting bacteremia-induced portal vein suppurative pylephlebitis following perforated acute appendicitis, no growth was detected in his blood cultures despite the septic status detected at presentation. This culture-negative result may be attributed to the 5-day course of ertapenem and cefazolin administered at the referring center prior to transfer, which may have suppressed bacteremia without eradicating the septic thrombus. In culture-negative pylephlebitis, the diagnosis relies on clinical presentation, imaging findings, and response to empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. Pylephlebitis positivity in blood cultures has been detected at a rate of approximately 77% [7].

Diabetes presents a significant risk factor for various infection types. People with diabetes are 1.5 to 4 times more likely to get both common and serious infections, and infections are still the primary cause of death and illness [10]. Our case involved a newly diagnosed case of DM. Although the complications in our case developed after appendectomy, we believe that DM is a significant predisposing factor in the development of the current complications.

Congenital or acquired coagulation disorders may also contribute to suppurative thrombophlebitis [11]. Most inherited hypercoagulable states are associated with an increased risk of venous thromboembolism. The association between hereditary thrombophilias and the first venous thrombotic event is clearer than that with a recurrent event [12]. The causes of thrombophilia, such as antithrombin deficiency, protein C and protein S deficiency, homozygous factor V Leiden mutations, homozygous factor II mutations, heterozygous factor V Leiden mutations, and heterozygous factor II mutations, can be considered factors that cause inherited hypercoagulable states [13]. A meta-analysis of 31 published studies indicated that the presence of the homozygous MTHFR gene was associated with a very weak risk factor for thrombosis [14]. Therefore, in practice, neither polymorphism in the MTHFR gene nor serum homocysteine levels are a risk factor for venous thromboembolism. Based on the best available evidence, there is no support for the association of the MTHFR gene with significantly elevated thrombotic risk [15]. In this study, the MTHFR mutation was found to be heterozygous-positive, and plasma homocysteine levels were normal. Considering the literature and meta-analyses, hereditary thrombophilia causes were not considered in thrombosis etiology. In contrast to common etiologic causes, some case series have reported cases of suppurative thrombophlebitis after hemorrhoidal ligation, liver biopsy, and gastric band procedures [16–18]. Although suppurative thrombophlebitis is rarely encountered in patients with inflammatory bowel diseases, Scaringi et al. reported suppurative mesenteric and portal thrombophlebitis complications in patients with septic shock due to fistulized Crohn’s disease [19]. Clinical findings in patients with suppurative thrombophlebitis are not specific, and constitutional findings, such as fever, nausea, vomiting and anorexia, are usually observed [1]. Hepatic cytolysis, hepatomegaly, and cholestasis may be observed in sepsis. Moreover, leukocytosis may be observed in the early phase of the disease. In addition, serum D-dimer levels may be elevated in some cases of suppurative thrombophlebitis, although this marker is not detected in all case series. Wang et al. reported a case report of suppurative thrombophlebitis after acute appendicitis in which the D-dimer level was observed to be 14.047 ng/mL at presentation [20]. In our patient, an elevated D-dimer level was detected, which is not commonly observed in patients with suppurative thrombophlebitis after acute appendicitis. In addition, elevated serum CRP and serum procalcitonin levels are expected due to the observation of bacterial infections in patients with suppurative thrombophlebitis.

Pylephlebitis can be diagnosed with abdominal ultrasonography, and abdominal tomography with intravenous contrast is the preferred imaging method for detecting the source of intra-abdominal infection and possible thrombosis. Baril et al. reported that thrombosis in suppurative thrombophlebitis is mainly observed in the portal vein but rarely observed in the inferior mesenteric veins [6]. In a case series with 95 patients, only 44% of patients with suppurative thrombophlebitis demonstrated bacterial growth in thrombus cultures [21], while in other case series, studies on suppurative thrombophlebitis, Streptococcus viridans, Escherichia coli, Bacteroides fragilis and Streptococcus anginosus were isolated as the most common bacterial agents [21]. Although the blood cultures of our patient did not exhibit any growth, drainage was not deemed to be appropriate due to the fact that the antibiotic therapy response and thrombus culture could not be obtained from the thrombophlebitis site. Although percutaneous drainage is considered standard practice for pyogenic liver abscesses larger than 5 cm in many centers, drainage was deferred in our patient on the basis of a multidisciplinary decision. This approach was supported by progressive clinical improvement, normalization of acute-phase reactants under empirical antibiotic therapy, and serial imaging demonstrating regression of the abscesses, with the largest decreasing from 8 cm to 4 mm. The multiloculated and predominantly periportal microabscess morphology, distributed along the thrombosed portal branches, also rendered the collections less amenable to effective percutaneous drainage. The decision to pursue source control medically rather than interventionally was, therefore, individualized and continuously re-evaluated, with drainage held in reserve had clinical or radiologic deterioration occurred.

In our patient, liver biopsy played a decisive role in management beyond confirming the diagnosis. In the setting of multiple liver abscesses and portal vein thrombosis with a concurrent autoimmune and infiltrative workup, biopsy was undertaken to enable rapid diagnostic decision-making in a septic patient and, specifically, to determine whether an etiology requiring immunosuppressive therapy, such as autoimmune hepatitis, vasculitis, or an infiltrative or malignant process, was present. The histological findings, limited to sinusoidal dilatation and mild interface hepatitis without evidence of malignancy or active autoimmune hepatitis, excluded the need for immunosuppressive treatment and allowed management to be confidently directed toward the septic etiology with antimicrobial and anticoagulant therapy.

The main treatment for suppurative thrombophlebitis should be based on the elimination of the causative agent of bacteremia and the elimination of septic emboli. The most important feature in the treatment of suppurative thrombophlebitis with splanchnic or systemic involvement is the delay before antibiotic therapy initiation. Although the ideal duration of antibiotic therapy is not known, a study conducted by Plemmons et al. suggested a time frame of 4–6 weeks as the ideal duration [22]. In our case, meropenem and tigecycline were initiated as antibiotic therapy regimens at the beginning of treatment, after which daptomycin antibiotic therapy was initiated because of clinical- and acute-phase reactant progression; in addition, treatment was administered for 4 weeks, and a complete clinical- and acute-phase reactant response was obtained. From a clinical decision-making perspective, the initial choice of meropenem and tigecycline was justified by the patient’s recent hospitalization and prior ertapenem/cefazolin exposure, raising concern for nosocomial and resistant organisms. The subsequent escalation to daptomycin was evidence-based: daptomycin provides gram-positive coverage, including Enterococcus, Streptococcus, and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, and was preferred over vancomycin given the risk of acute kidney injury in sepsis. This stepwise, culture-guided empirical escalation strategy, validated by Infectious Diseases consultation, reflects current recommendations for nosocomial intra-abdominal infections. It should be noted that vancomycin remains the standard first-line agent for empirical gram-positive coverage, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, in most intra-abdominal and nosocomial infection guidelines, and that daptomycin is not a routine first choice in this context. In our patient, daptomycin was selected as an individualized decision, guided by Infectious Diseases consultation, to mitigate the risk of vancomycin-associated nephrotoxicity in the setting of sepsis and to avoid the need for serum vancomycin level monitoring while providing comparable gram-positive coverage. This choice, therefore, reflects patient-specific risk stratification rather than a general recommendation to prefer daptomycin over vancomycin.

In suppurative thrombophlebitis, small branches of the portal vein are initially thrombosed; as a result of dissemination, septic thrombophlebitis of the portal vein occurs [23]. Recommendations for anticoagulant therapy in suppurative thrombophlebitis usually vary according to the clinical presentation of the case; however, there are no routine protocol recommendations. Although some studies have investigated systemic and local thrombectomy and percutaneous thrombectomy, the efficacy of these recommendations has not been established. Allaix et al. conducted a study and suggested that 6 months of anticoagulant therapy is sufficient unless prothrombic disorders are detected [23]. The mortality rates of patients with suppurative thrombophlebitis have decreased, from 80% to 25% [1], due to the development of imaging methods, the possibility of early diagnosis, and advances in antibiotic therapy.

Currently, four cases of suppurative pylephlebitis after laparoscopic appendectomy have been reported in the literature [24–27], with three of the reported cases involving adults and the other involving a pediatric patient. Our patient presented with bacteremia-associated sepsis, and portal vein thrombosis was detected. Etiologic evaluation was performed, and low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was administered at a therapeutic dose of 1 mg/kg, followed by warfarin sodium. Our patient did not receive a liver cirrhosis diagnosis; however, when considering transient hepatic insufficiency and the increase in portal pressure, new-generation oral anticoagulant (NOAC) treatments were initiated based on the 2025 American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) guidelines on portal vein thrombosis in patients with liver cirrhosis, and a response follow-up with imaging performed every 3 months was planned [28]. In a case report of hemochromatosis complicated with portal vein thrombosis, Pannach et al. obtained a complete response to rivaroxaban treatment [29]. Moreover, Ageno et al. demonstrated the potential benefit of rivaroxaban compared to other standard anticoagulant therapies in splanchnic vein thrombosis in non-cirrhotic patients [30]. In our case, due to the presence of a thrombus formation occluding the portal vein almost completely, Warfarin was initiated over LMWH due to its presumed higher efficacy. The INR was maintained within the therapeutic range, and treatment efficacy was achieved. Treatment was switched to rivaroxaban, because it can be administered on an outpatient basis, does not require INR monitoring, and has similar treatment efficacy, based on examples in the literature. In their study on the efficacy of rivaroxaban treatment in 111 patients diagnosed with chronic non-cirrhotic portal vein thrombosis, Plessier et al. demonstrated that daily doses of rivaroxaban reduced the incidence of venous thromboembolism and did not increase major bleeding events in patients with chronic non-cirrhotic portal vein thrombosis who did not have significant risk factors for thrombosis [31]. A retrospective, single-center study evaluating the efficacy and safety of NOACs, including rivaroxaban and apixaban, compared with warfarin in the treatment of portal vein thrombosis has been published. No primary failure event (recurrent thromboembolic event) was reported in patients receiving a NOAC vs. four patients receiving warfarin, and no primary safety event (bleeding event) was reported patients receiving a NOAC vs. one patient receiving warfarin [32]. The AGA, in its 2025 guidelines on evaluating anticoagulant therapy in patients with portal vein thrombosis and developing cirrhosis, recommended that cross-sectional imaging be performed every 3 months to assess response to treatment. If thrombus resolution is achieved, anticoagulant therapy should be continued until transplantation or, in patients not undergoing transplantation, at least until the thrombus has completely regressed [28]. In our case, the thrombus had almost completely regressed at the first imaging performed after 3 months, but treatment was to be continued for at least 6 months, and NOAC therapy was discontinued after complete thrombus resolution.

With respect to anticoagulant selection, the comparative evidence between direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and conventional agents in this setting warrants explicit consideration. In non-cirrhotic splanchnic and portal vein thrombosis, Ageno et al. reported a potential benefit of rivaroxaban relative to standard anticoagulants [30], and Plessier et al. demonstrated that rivaroxaban reduced recurrent venous thromboembolism without increasing major bleeding in chronic non-cirrhotic portal vein thrombosis [31]. In a direct comparison, the retrospective study cited above reported no recurrent thromboembolic events among patients receiving a DOAC vs. four among those receiving warfarin, and no major bleeding events with DOACs versus one with warfarin [32]. While these data favor the efficacy and safety profile of DOACs, they are derived from retrospective and non-randomized cohorts rather than dedicated trials, and prospective comparative evidence against low-molecular-weight heparin and vitamin K antagonists specifically in septic pylephlebitis is lacking. Low-molecular-weight heparin and warfarin, therefore, remain well-established options, particularly in the acute, hemodynamically evolving phase, when parenteral administration, dose reliability, and reversibility are advantageous. In our case, this rationale informed the initial use of low-molecular-weight heparin and warfarin, with transition to rivaroxaban reserved for the stabilized outpatient phase, balancing the practical advantages of oral therapy, namely, outpatient administration without INR monitoring, against the limited prospective evidence in this specific septic context.

Conclusions

This case report provides several evidence-based take-home messages for clinicians. First, suppurative pylephlebitis should be considered in any patient presenting with sepsis and a recent history of intra-abdominal surgery or infection, even in the absence of positive blood cultures, as culture negativity may reflect prior antibiotic exposure rather than a non-infectious etiology. Second, early contrast-enhanced abdominal CT is essential for diagnosing portal vein thrombosis, characterizing the extent of septic thrombus, and guiding management decisions. Third, empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy should be initiated promptly, with escalation guided by clinical trajectory and Infectious Diseases consultation; in patients with prior antibiotic exposure and nosocomial risk, daptomycin offers a rational gram-positive escalation option. Fourth, anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin bridged to a direct oral anticoagulant is feasible and effective in non-cirrhotic portal vein thrombosis; rivaroxaban, in line with current AGA 2025 guidelines, represents a practical long-term option that does not require INR monitoring. Fifth, a comprehensive differential diagnostic workup is mandatory to exclude thrombophilia, autoimmune, and malignant etiologies before attributing portal vein thrombosis to a septic source. Imaging follow-up every 3 months is recommended until complete thrombus resolution is confirmed. Taken together, early diagnosis, systematic differential evaluation, prompt antibiotic escalation, and appropriate anticoagulation are the cornerstones of successful management in patients with suppurative pylephlebitis patients.
