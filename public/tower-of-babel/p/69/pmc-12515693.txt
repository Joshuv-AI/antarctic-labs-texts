High‐Dose Hormone Replacement Therapy Improved Embryo Transfer Outcomes in a Woman With 21‐Hydroxylase Deficiency Who Had Persistently High Serum Progesterone Levels: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Yuri Mizusawa, Hiroaki Shibahara, Masahide Shiotani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWomen with 21‐hydroxylase deficiency have reduced fertility because of excessive production of adrenal androgen and progesterone, which can inhibit folliculogenesis, disturb the normal gonadotropin secretion pattern and development of the endometrium, and affect endometrial receptivity. A 40‐year‐old woman with 21‐hydroxylase deficiency and persistently high progesterone levels in spite of receiving corticosteroid treatment visited our institution because she wanted to have a second child. Previously, she had difficulties with frozen–thawed embryo transfer because of a gradual increase in progesterone levels. After the reduction of progesterone levels with increased corticosteroid treatment, she succeeded in having a healthy baby after frozen–thawed embryo transfer with hormone replacement therapy. She hoped to have a second child with the same method, but increased corticosteroid treatment did not decrease her progesterone level. Frozen–thawed embryo transfer with hormone replacement therapy was attempted twice despite the insuppressible high progesterone level, and both ended as chemical pregnancies. In the third attempt, the patient was given a high dose of estradiol and progesterone medication, which led to a clinical pregnancy. Many studies have reported that women with 21‐hydroxylase deficiency conceived after suppression of serum progesterone with corticosteroid, and there are also some reports of insuppressible progesterone levels. Administering a high dose of estradiol and progesterone medication during frozen–thawed embryo transfer with hormone replacement therapy might achieve better results in women with 21‐hydroxylase deficiency who have difficulty in reducing high progesterone levels with corticosteroid therapy.Keywords: 21‐hydroxylase deficiency, endometrial receptivity, estradiol, hormone replacement therapy, progesterone

Introduction

21‐hydroxylase deficiency (21OHD) is the most common form of congenital adrenal hyperplasia. Women with 21OHD have reduced fertility because of the excessive production of progesterone, which causes inhibition of follicular growth, inadequate development of the endometrium, and lack of endometrial receptivity [1].

Previously, we reported a woman with 21OHD who initially had normal progesterone levels with corticosteroid therapy, but had repeated failures of embryo transfer (ET) because of gradually increased progesterone. After adjusting the progesterone level with increased corticosteroid therapy, the woman gave birth to a healthy baby [2]. Here, we report on this woman's attempt to have another child despite insuppressible high progesterone levels that did not respond to increased corticosteroid therapy. This woman succeeded in becoming pregnant with high doses of estradiol and progesterone medication during hormone replacement therapy (HRT), but the pregnancy resulted in early miscarriage due to 16‐trisomy.

Case History

This case report was approved by the Hanabusa Women's Clinic ethics committee (reference number 2023‐01), and written informed consent was obtained from this woman.

Two years after giving birth to her first child, a 40‐year‐old woman (height, 150 cm; weight, 46.8 kg; body mass index, 20.7 kg/m2, blood pressure, 120/70 mmHg) who had been diagnosed with 21OHD classic‐salt wasting type at age 3 years old revisited our institution because she wished to have a second child. Her menstrual cycle was irregular, and on day 3 of menstruation, her hormone levels were as follows: estradiol, 110 pg/mL; progesterone, 9.6 ng/mL; follicle‐stimulating hormone, 16.3 mIU/mL; luteinizing hormone, 5.1 mIU/mL. While trying to become pregnant with her first child, she had difficulties because of a gradual increase in progesterone levels during frozen–thawed ET (FET) treatment [2]. After reducing progesterone levels with proper corticosteroid treatment, she succeeded in having her first child using the FET.

Method (Treatment)

At the recent visit, we attempted to treat her with in vitro fertilization and FET, that is the same way that she had successfully become pregnant. Using aromatase inhibitor (Letrozole F, 2.5 mg; Fuji Pharma Co. Ltd., Toyama, Japan) tablet per day for 5 days and human menopausal gonadotrophin injection (150 IU/day; Ferring Pharmaceuticals Co. Ltd., Saint‐Prex, Switzerland) as stimulation, gonadotropin releasing hormone agonist (GnRHA) nasal spray (Buserelin nasal solution 0.15% F 600 μg; Fuji Pharma Co. Ltd., Toyama, Japan) as a trigger at 36 h before oocyte retrieval, we retrieved three oocytes, fertilized with intracytoplasmic sperm injection, and vitrified 5 day‐five‐blastocysts (four‐AA in the Gardner classification) [3].

Re high progesterone level, we consulted her endocrinologist to reduce it to at most 1.3 ng/mL in the proliferative phase because this was the level at which she became pregnant with her first child [2]. We administered GnRHA by injection (Leuprorelin acetate, 1.88 mg; Asuka Pharmaceutical Ltd.) every 4 weeks to inhibit progesterone production by the ovary. The endocrinologist increased the dexamethasone dose to 0.5 to 1.0 mg/day; however, the progesterone level did not decrease.

The endocrinologist told that her 21OHD had been controlled well enough, and that administering a higher dose of dexamethasone would increase the risk of complications such as diabetes. The laboratory data were as follows: testosterone 23.2 ng/dL (reference range: 10.8–56.9), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) 54.8 pg/mL (7.2–63.3), aldosterone 23.1 ng/dL (10.8–56.9), renin activation 3.6 ng/m/h (0.2–3.9), dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA‐S) 64 μg/dL (23–266), HbA1c 9.6% (< 6.5), and progesterone level 9.2 (0.1–1.5) ng/mL on day three of the menstruation cycle.

We explained to the patient that the high progesterone levels were unsuppressible and were coming from the adrenal gland. The increased corticosteroid hormone treatment didn't decrease the progesterone level enough, and further administration of corticosteroids would pose a risk to her health, such as the risk of developing diabetes.

Additionally, it appeared that an embryo transfer under high progesterone levels was unlikely to be successful. However, the patient had a strong desire to continue her infertility treatment and agreed to our suggestion of a high‐dose HRT treatment, which had been discussed from ethical and clinical points of view in our facilities.

To evaluate endometrial receptivity, we performed the endometrial receptivity analysis (ERA: Endometrial Receptivity Analysis, Igenomix) and endometrial biopsy for dating [4, 5].

For HRT, from days 2 to 30, we used three transdermal estradiol patches (Estraderm M; Kissei Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Nagano, Japan) every 2 days and oral estradiol (Progynova, Estradiol valerate, 2 mg; Bayer Ltd., Leverkeusen, Germany) at a dose of 2 tablets per day. On day 15, the progesterone level was 4.4 ng/mL, estradiol was 877 pg/mL, and the endometrium was 8.4 mm. From days 15 to 30, 9 vaginal progesterone suppositories (Progestan; Kocak Farma Pharmaceutical and Chemical Industry Co. Ltd., Istanbul, Turkey) and 3 oral dydrogestorne tablets (Duphaston 5 mg tablets; Mylan IRE Healthcare Ltd., Dublin, Ireland) were administered daily (Figure 1).

At 113 h after the first progesterone administration, we performed a biopsy of the endometrium using an endometrial suction curette (Pipet Curet; Fuji Medical Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Subsequent ERA showed that the endometrium was post‐receptive and indicated that ET was recommended to be 89 ± 3 h after the first administration of progesterone. Endometrial dating indicated the late secretory phase and showed prominent spiral arteries because of the beginning of pre‐decidualization of periarterial stromal cells (Figure 2).

With these results, it was suggested that the high progesterone levels from the adrenal gland might have already caused endometrial secretory changes before the administration of progesterone during the HRT cycle. Therefore, we tried to transfer the embryo in the cycle at the lowest progesterone levels, and once we found the progesterone level to be 2.3 ng/mL on the 15th day after the start of HRT, we transferred one 4‐AA blastocyst 89 h after the first progesterone administration. On day 30, the serum human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) level was 22.5 mIU/mL. Consequently, she had a miscarriage with no visible gestational sac (GS) in the uterus and was diagnosed as having a chemical miscarriage.

Next cycle, progesterone level was 3.4 on the 15th of HRT cycle; we added a 125‐mg hydroxyprogesterone caproate injection per week intramuscularly (Proge Deport intramuscular injection; Mochida Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) from day 15 to the regular method to increase the progesterone concentration for the improvement of the result [5]. Another 4‐AA blastocyst transfer was performed 87 h after the first administration of progesterone. On day 30, the serum hCG level was low (13.8 mIU/mL); she had a chemical miscarriage with no visible GS again. For the third FET, to increase the expression of estradiol and progesterone receptor as priming of endometrium, we increased oral estradiol from 2 to 4 tablets [6]. The way of progesterone administration was the same as in the second FET cycle. On day 15, the progesterone level was 3.1 ng/mL, estradiol was 721 pg/mL, and endometrium was 7.2 mm. We performed another 4‐AA blastocyst ET at 94 h after the first progesterone intake. On day 30, the hCG level was 260.6 mIU/mL, progesterone was 20.78 ng/mL, and estradiol was 1040 pg/mL. At gestational week 5, we observed a small gestational sac inside the uterus. Unfortunately, we could not detect a fetal heartbeat at 7 weeks of pregnancy, which resulted in miscarriage. Chromosomal analysis of the production of conception revealed that the reason for miscarriage was trisomy 16.

Conclusion and Result

This is the case of insuppressible high serum progesterone level in a woman with 21‐hydroxylase deficiency, and this patient had two chemical miscarriages with normal HRT FET, but high dose HRT improved embryo transfer outcome even with persistently insuppressible high serum progesterone level.

Discussion

This case report pointed out that there are cases of insuppressible progesterone in 21OHD even with increased corticosteroids, and the endometrial embryo receptivity, which is decreased because of high progesterone level, might have been improved with increased doses of estradiol and progesterone medication in HRT.

Several studies have reported that 21OHD suffers from infertility mainly due to high progesterone levels, and many conceived cases had been reported after suppression of serum progesterone with corticosteroid treatment [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, there are some reports in which the progesterone level cannot be suppressed even with increased corticosteroid though 17‐hydroxyprogesterone (17‐OHP) decreased [11] (Table 2). We suppose that our case appears to be one of the insuppressible progesterone cases. There are few reports of insuppressible progesterone in 21OHD, and the details and the differences between suppressible progesterone cases have been unknown well.

In women with 21OHD, 17‐OHP is not converted to 11‐deoxycortisol, and cortisol production is decreased; this negative feedback loop leads to increased ACTH secretion. Consequently, excessive 17‐OHP and progesterone are produced. These high progesterone levels inhibit endometrial receptivity, resulting in ET failure [1].

The primary trophic hormone for the endometrium is estrogen, which acts via the estrogen receptor (ER) during the proliferative phase to cause priming and proliferation. Progesterone antagonizes the action of estrogen and promotes glandular and stromal differentiation via the progesterone receptor (PR) [6]. Studies on HRT have shown that the sequential administration of estradiol and progesterone medication is also essential for normal endometrial maturation, adequate embryo implantation, and receptivity [12].

We hypothesized that in our case, this women's endometrial maturation and embryo implantation and receptivity were diminished because of the antagonistic effect on estradiol of continuous high progesterone. The direct effect of prematurely elevated progesterone on endometrial receptivity before hCG administration in fresh ET cycles has been analyzed by means of its gene expression profile and in clinical studies1 [13, 14]. These indicate that this hypothesis is biologically plausible and is consistent with the known accelerated maturation of the endometrium. Though the progesterone level was persistently high in our case, one could hypothesize that high progesterone during the period of estradiol priming in the endometrium had some effects on the changes required for intimal receptivity and that the mechanism was similar to that of prematurely elevated progesterone. We suggested that the endometrium provide suitable conditions for pregnancy with external high‐dose HRT in the 3rd attempt despite the high progesterone level. We assume that the pregnancy was established because increased estradiol administration for priming might have increased ER and PR expression and subsequent administration of increased progesterone resulted in the provision of suitable conditions of the endometrium, which had been antagonistically suppressed in the improvement of the endometrium, including ER and PR expression of the endometrium by high progesterone level. And this hypothesis would explain why the third FET attempt resulted in clinical pregnancy even though the previous two attempts ended as chemical pregnancies.

The limitation in our report is that the embryos were not genetically screened; therefore, we cannot rule out that the embryos in the first two FETs might have had characteristics that resulted from previous two FET cycles.

In conclusion, we suggest that in women with insuppressible progesterone in 21OHD, it may be possible to improve endometrial receptivity, which is diminished by persistent high progesterone, with administering of high doses of estradiol and progesterone in HRT. This method may be suitable for women with insuppressible progesterone in congenital adrenal hyperplasia. However, some studies have found that the administration of high progesterone doses is not always beneficial for pregnancy [15]. Consequently, careful consideration and observation are required about progesterone administration.
