Acalabrutinib Versus Venetoclax Plus Obinutuzumab in Treatment‐Naive Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia: A Real‐World Propensity Score‐Matched Study
Authors: Po‐Wei Liao, Bor‐Sheng Ko, Yu‐Chen Su, Wei‐Ting Chao, Chieh‐Lin Jerry Teng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTherapy for chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) has shifted from chemoimmunotherapy to targeted agents such as Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors and BCL‐2 inhibitors. Approved first‐line options include acalabrutinib, a second‐generation BTK inhibitor, and the fixed‐duration combination venetoclax–obinutuzumab, yet real‐world head‐to‐head data are scarce.MethodsUsing the TriNetX global federated health database, we retrospectively compared outcomes in treatment‐naïve patients with TP53‐unmutated CLL who received either regimen as initial therapy. After applying eligibility criteria and 1:1 propensity score matching, 669 matched pairs were analyzed. The primary endpoint was time to next treatment (TTNT); secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS) and adverse events.ResultsThe results showed that patients treated with venetoclax plus obinutuzumab had a significantly longer TTNT (median not reached vs. 47.7 months) and improved 4‐year OS. Across all subgroups, the venetoclax‐based regimen performed better. Cytopenias and infections were more common with venetoclax–obinutuzumab—neutropenia 54.3% versus 33.3%, thrombocytopenia 45.9% versus 31.9%, febrile neutropenia 7.1% versus 3.4%, and tumor lysis syndrome 6.3% versus 2.3%. Acalabrutinib caused fewer severe hematologic toxicities.ConclusionIn real‐world practice, venetoclax plus obinutuzumab was associated with longer TTNT and improved OS compared to acalabrutinib as first‐line therapy in patients with TP53 wild‐type CLL.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), the most prevalent leukemia in adults across Western nations, occurs at an annual rate of roughly 4.2 per 100,000 people [1]. CLL primarily affects older adults, with a median age at diagnosis of approximately 70 years, and is more prevalent in males [2]. CLL involves clonal proliferation and buildup of mature but dysfunctional CD5+ B cells within peripheral blood, bone marrow, and lymphoid organs [3]. Most patients with CLL are not symptomatic at diagnosis and do not require immediate treatment. Current guidelines recommend a watch‐and‐wait approach until clear indications for therapy arise, such as symptomatic splenomegaly or lymphadenopathy, progressive marrow failure, refractory autoimmune cytopenias, or disease‐related fever, night sweats, or weight loss [4]. For patients requiring treatment, CLL management has shifted over the past decade from chemoimmunotherapy to precision‐based approaches. Current first‐line regimens commonly include Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors and BCL‐2 inhibitors, often in combination with anti‐CD20 antibodies. These combinations offer improved effectiveness and tolerability compared to conventional strategies [5, 6, 7, 8] and are now widely used in clinical practice.

Acalabrutinib, a next‐generation selective BTK inhibitor, offers greater target precision and reduced unintended activity compared to the earlier agent ibrutinib [9]. In treatment‐naive patients with CLL, acalabrutinib significantly improves progression‐free survival (PFS) compared to chlorambucil plus obinutuzumab [10] while offering a similar bleeding risk and reduced cardiac toxicity relative to ibrutinib [11]. Venetoclax, a BCL‐2 inhibitor that induces apoptosis in CLL cells, is used as a time‐limited regimen in combination with obinutuzumab. This combination has shown superior PFS compared to chlorambucil plus obinutuzumab [12]. Moreover, over half of the treated patients remained progression‐free 6 years after randomization [13], highlighting the possibility of long‐term disease control with time‐limited therapy.

Although both regimens are effective and recommended as preferred frontline options in international guidelines [14], they differ notably in treatment duration, administration, and toxicity profiles. Acalabrutinib is administered continuously until either disease progression or adverse effects become intolerable, while venetoclax plus obinutuzumab is administered as a fixed 12‐month regimen. The continuous use of BTK inhibitors requires long‐term adherence and is associated with risks such as atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and bleeding [15]. Conversely, venetoclax‐based therapy requires close monitoring for tumor lysis syndrome during the dose ramp‐up phase [16] but may be preferred by patients seeking time‐limited treatment.

Despite robust phase 3 data supporting each regimen, direct head‐to‐head comparisons are lacking. Moreover, randomized trials often involve selected populations that may not represent real‐world patients, who commonly have more comorbidities, variable adherence, and differing clinical priorities. To address this gap, we performed a retrospective analysis using the TriNetX network database to evaluate clinical outcomes and adverse events in patients with CLL receiving either acalabrutinib or venetoclax plus obinutuzumab as first‐line therapy in a real‐world setting.

Participants and Methods

Data Sources

This retrospective cohort analysis used the TriNetX network (Cambridge, MA, USA), an international health data platform providing real‐time access to anonymized electronic medical records [17]. As of April 2025, TriNetX includes data from over 150 healthcare organizations across the United States and other countries, encompassing nearly 180 million patients. The platform complies with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and has received a waiver of informed consent from the Western Institutional Review Board, as all data are anonymized and presented in aggregate form.

The TriNetX database offers comprehensive clinical and administrative data, including demographics, diagnoses (ICD‐10‐CM codes), procedures (ICD‐10‐PCS or CPT codes), medications (Veterans Affairs National Formulary codes), laboratory results (LOINC codes), genomic data (HGVS nomenclature), and healthcare utilization metrics such as inpatient and outpatient visits, hospitalizations, and emergency department encounters. Table S1 lists all classification codes referenced in this analysis.

Study Design

The study design is depicted in Figure 1A. We used the Research Network, a subset of the TriNetX platform encompassing 106 healthcare organizations, to identify patients with a confirmed diagnosis of CLL who initiated first‐line therapy with either venetoclax plus obinutuzumab or acalabrutinib on or after January 1, 2015. Eligible patients were adults aged 18 years or older with no history of CLL‐directed therapy before the index date. Patients were excluded if they had documented TP53 mutations or lacked essential clinical data required for analysis.

(A) Study flowchart. (B) A total of 115,624 patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and without TP53 mutations were identified in the Research Network database. Among them, 30,603 patients initiated first‐line treatment. Of these, 2097 received acalabrutinib, 682 received venetoclax plus obinutuzumab, and the remainder were treated with other regimens.

The index event was defined as the initiation of treatment with venetoclax–obinutuzumab or acalabrutinib, with the index date corresponding to the first dose administration. To ensure baseline comparability, demographic and clinical characteristics were evaluated using the most recent available data from the 12‐month period preceding the index date. To minimize reverse causation, follow‐up for outcomes commenced 1 day after the index date and continued until the earliest occurrence of death, loss to follow‐up, or the data cutoff date (April 15, 2025).

Pre‐Specified Outcomes

The primary outcome was time to next treatment (TTNT), defined as the interval from the index date to the initiation of subsequent therapy or death, whichever occurred first. Patients who experienced neither event were censored at the last follow‐up date (April 15, 2025). Secondary outcomes included overall survival (OS), TTNT within predefined clinical subgroups, and the incidence of adverse events. OS was defined as the time from the index date to death from any cause; patients who remained alive were censored at the last known date alive or the end of data availability (April 15, 2025), whichever occurred first. Adverse events were identified using diagnosis codes recorded after treatment initiation. To enhance causal inference between treatment and adverse events, patients with pre‐existing diagnoses of the relevant events before follow‐up were excluded from the safety analysis. We focused on adverse events commonly reported in previous studies or considered clinically significant, including atrial fibrillation and tumor lysis syndrome. Severity was assessed according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0.

Statistical Analyses

To minimize confounding due to baseline imbalances, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching via a greedy nearest neighbor method, applying a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standard deviations of the logit‐transformed score. Variables incorporated into the model included age, sex, race, hemoglobin, platelet count, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), β2‐microglobulin, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Matching was restricted to patients with complete data for all covariates. For subgroup analyses, matching was conducted separately within each subgroup to maintain covariate balance. The balance between matched cohorts was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD < 0.1 indicating negligible imbalance.

Baseline characteristics across treatment arms were analyzed using the Pearson chi‐squared test for categorical data and the Student's t‐test for continuous measures. Kaplan–Meier curves estimated TTNT and OS, with group differences evaluated via the log‐rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from Cox proportional hazards models. Adverse event rates were descriptively reported and contrasted using risk differences. A two‐sided p‐value below 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Study Cohort and Patient Characteristics

A total of 115,624 patients with CLL and without TP53 mutations were identified in the Research Network database. Among them, 30,603 patients initiated first‐line treatment between January 1, 2015, and April 15, 2025. Of these, 2097, 682, and the remainder were treated with acalabrutinib, venetoclax plus obinutuzumab, and other regimens, respectively (Figure 1B).

Baseline characteristics of the acalabrutinib and venetoclax plus obinutuzumab groups are provided in Table 1. Patients receiving acalabrutinib were significantly older, with a higher proportion aged ≥ 75 years (40.5% vs. 20.1%, p < 0.001). Conversely, the venetoclax plus obinutuzumab group exhibited a higher baseline disease burden and a greater prevalence of renal dysfunction, as indicated by hemoglobin ≤ 11 g/dL (49.7% vs. 39.6%, p < 0.001), platelet count ≤ 100,000/μL (34.9% vs. 22.9%, p < 0.001), elevated LDH (65.0% vs. 40.6%, p < 0.001), β2‐microglobulin > 3.5 mg/L (14.8% vs. 8.1%, p < 0.001), and eGFR < 60 mL/min (50.1% vs. 42.3%, p < 0.001). After 1:1 propensity score matching, 669 matched pairs were identified. Post‐matching, baseline characteristics were well balanced, with no statistical differences observed in age, sex, race, laboratory parameters, or comorbidities (Figure S1).

Treatment Outcomes

The median follow‐up was 23.4 and 20.5 months for those receiving acalabrutinib and venetoclax plus obinutuzumab, respectively. Upon analysis, 250 (37.4%) and 148 (22.1%) patients in the acalabrutinib and venetoclax–obinutuzumab groups had started additional therapy, respectively. Moreover, median TTNT was 47.7 months with acalabrutinib, while it remained unreached for the venetoclax–obinutuzumab arm; the estimated 4‐year TTNT rates were 49.1% and 63.6%, respectively (HR 1.84; 95% CI, 1.50–2.26; p < 0.0001) (Figure 2A).

(A) The median time to the next treatment was 47.7 months in the acalabrutinib group, whereas it was not reached in the venetoclax plus obinutuzumab group. The estimated 4‐year time to next treatment (TTNT) rates were 49.1% and 63.6%, respectively (HR, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.50–2.26; p < 0.0001). (B) The median overall survival was not reached in either group. The estimated 4‐year overall survival (OS) rate was 81.7% for the acalabrutinib group and 88.4% for the venetoclax plus obinutuzumab group (HR, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.28–2.65; p = 0.0009).

The median OS was not reached in either group. The estimated 4‐year OS rate was 81.7% and 88.4% for the acalabrutinib and venetoclax plus obinutuzumab groups, respectively (HR, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.28–2.65; p = 0.0009) (Figure 2B). Subgroup analyses of TTNT consistently favored venetoclax plus obinutuzumab across all predefined categories, including treatment era, age at index, sex, race, body mass index, Rai stage, laboratory parameters, and comorbidities. Statistical differences were observed in all subgroups except among patients with atrial fibrillation, diabetes mellitus, or cerebrovascular disease (Figure 3).

Adverse Events

Common adverse events are outlined in Table 2. Cytopenias were the most frequently reported adverse events in both treatment groups. Compared to acalabrutinib, venetoclax plus obinutuzumab was associated with significantly higher rates of neutropenia (all grades: 54.3% vs. 33.3%, p < 0.001; grade 3/4: 35.2% vs. 20.2%, p < 0.001) and thrombocytopenia (all grades: 45.9% vs. 31.9%, p = 0.003; grade 3/4: 15.1% vs. 10.6%, p = 0.022). Infections such as febrile neutropenia (7.1% vs. 3.4%, p = 0.003) and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) (13.1% vs. 9.4%, p = 0.037) were more common in the venetoclax plus obinutuzumab group. Tumor lysis syndrome occurred more frequently in this group as well (6.3% vs. 2.3%, p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between groups for other adverse events.

Discussion

Here, we compared real‐world outcomes of first‐line treatment with acalabrutinib versus venetoclax plus obinutuzumab in patients with CLL without TP53 mutations using the TriNetX network database. Following propensity score matching, patients receiving venetoclax plus obinutuzumab showed significantly longer TTNT and higher 4‐year OS rates than those treated with acalabrutinib. Subgroup analyses consistently favored venetoclax plus obinutuzumab across most clinical categories. However, this regimen was associated with higher rates of cytopenias—including neutropenia and thrombocytopenia—and increased incidences of febrile neutropenia, COVID‐19 infection, and tumor lysis syndrome.

In our cohort, acalabrutinib was more frequently used as first‐line therapy (n = 2097 vs. 682), likely due to its oral administration, favorable safety profile, and lower monitoring requirements, making it a more convenient option for both physicians and patients [18]. Patients treated with venetoclax plus obinutuzumab tended to be younger and presented with more advanced disease at baseline. This likely reflects clinical decision‐making practices in which fixed‐duration regimens are preferentially offered to younger, fitter individuals who can better tolerate the associated risk of adverse events. Similar treatment patterns have been observed in other real‐world studies [19, 20].

We restricted our analysis to patients with TP53 wild‐type CLL, as continuous BTK inhibition has been associated with more durable responses in those with TP53 mutations. In previous studies with up to 6 years of follow‐up, acalabrutinib demonstrated sustained PFS with a prolonged plateau, indicating effective long‐term disease control in patients with TP53 mutations [21]. In contrast, venetoclax plus obinutuzumab was associated with an early decline in PFS within the first 2 years, showing a marked divergence from the outcomes observed in patients with TP53 wild‐type CLL [13]. A possible explanation for this difference is that CLL cells with adverse genetic features may exhibit reduced dependence on BCL‐2, resulting in lower sensitivity to BCL‐2 inhibition [22]. However, caution is warranted when interpreting cross‐study comparisons due to differences in trial methodologies and enrolled patient groups.

Our findings are consistent with prior evidence supporting the efficacy of venetoclax plus obinutuzumab in TP53 wild‐type CLL. In the GAIA–CLL13 study, this regimen achieved a 4‐year PFS rate of 82% and a TTNT rate approaching 90%, indicating durable disease control [23]. Retrospective analyses have supported these findings, showing a trend toward lower mortality (11.86% vs. 9.83%, p = 0.0732) and significantly longer TTNT (HR 2.32, p < 0.01) compared to BTK inhibitors [24, 25]. These findings reinforce the role of venetoclax plus obinutuzumab as an effective and durable first‐line treatment option for patients with TP53 wild‐type CLL.

Several factors may contribute to the inferior outcomes observed with acalabrutinib. First, treatment discontinuation is more frequent in routine clinical practice due to comorbidities, adverse events, or financial limitations. Real‐world data have reported a mean treatment duration of only 20.7 months for acalabrutinib [26], substantially shorter than the 72 months observed in clinical trials with 6‐year follow‐up [21]. In older patients, prior studies have reported even shorter treatment durations, with a median of only 4.0 months [27]. Second, resistance to BTK inhibitors can develop over time [28]. Mutations in BTK or its downstream target PLCγ2—such as R665W and S707Y—typically emerge at a median of 31.6 months after treatment initiation and are associated with disease progression [29, 30, 31, 32]. Additional non‐genetic mechanisms may contribute to resistance and support CLL cell survival, including activation of alternative pathways such as CD19–PI3K–AKT, epigenetic alterations involving TCF4 and RAC2, and dysregulation of microRNAs including miR‐494, miR‐495, and miR‐543 [33, 34].

In contrast, venetoclax plus obinutuzumab has shown favorable outcomes for several reasons. First, resistance‐associated BCL‐2 mutations have not been observed with this fixed‐duration regimen, and retreatment following this approach has continued to yield clinical benefit [35]. Second, venetoclax exerts immune‐modulating effects, including the expansion of CD8+ T‐cell subsets, upregulation of T‐cell activation genes, and promotion of pro‐inflammatory responses such as M1 macrophage polarization [36]. These immunologic effects are not observed with BTK inhibition and may contribute to the superior clinical efficacy of venetoclax‐based therapy.

Consistent with prior studies, venetoclax plus obinutuzumab was associated with increased rates of cytopenias and tumor lysis syndrome [13]. Although these toxicities occur more frequently, real‐world data indicate that the regimen is associated with low rates of treatment discontinuation due to adverse events [19, 37], suggesting that they are manageable with appropriate supportive care. In addition, the incidence of atrial fibrillation in the acalabrutinib group was 9.4%, consistent with previous reports [11]. Although this rate was higher than that in the venetoclax plus obinutuzumab group, the difference was not statistically significant. Still, atrial fibrillation is a key concern when using BTK inhibitors, especially in individuals with underlying heart‐related risk factors [38].

Infectious complications varied between treatment groups. COVID‐19 infections were more frequent in the venetoclax plus obinutuzumab group (13.1% vs. 9.4%, p = 0.037). This may be partly due to treatment‐induced cytopenias but is likely attributable to the use of anti‐CD20 monoclonal antibodies, which impair humoral immunity and reduce vaccine responses [39, 40, 41]. Previous studies have shown that combining venetoclax with anti‐CD20 antibodies increases the risk of COVID‐19 infection in patients with CLL [42, 43]. These findings highlight the need for infection prevention measures and close monitoring in patients receiving venetoclax plus obinutuzumab during the ongoing pandemic.

Our study has certain limitations. First, although patients with TP53‐mutated CLL were excluded, the TriNetX database lacks detailed cytogenetic and molecular information—such as IGHV mutation status and del(17p) assessed by FISH—precluding subgroup analyses based on established genomic risk factors and potentially influencing treatment outcome comparisons. Second, reliance on coding systems to identify diagnoses, adverse events, treatment lines, and regimens may lead to misclassification or delays in outcome reporting. Third, the database does not capture the reasons for initiating subsequent treatments, making it difficult to determine whether changes were prompted by adverse events, disease progression, or other causes. Fourth, data on medication adherence, dose intensity, and supportive care—such as granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor use—were unavailable, potentially impacting the interpretation of efficacy and toxicity. In addition, TTNT may be influenced by differences in treatment strategies (fixed‐duration versus continuous therapy), follow‐up duration, and clinical practice patterns, rather than solely reflecting treatment efficacy. Lastly, as most patients in the cohort were from the United States, the findings may not be generalizable to other healthcare systems or populations with different demographic and clinical characteristics. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable real‐world insights into treatment outcomes and adverse events in patients with CLL.

Conclusion

In real‐world practice, venetoclax plus obinutuzumab was associated with longer TTNT and improved OS compared with acalabrutinib as first‐line therapy in patients with TP53 wild‐type CLL. Although this regimen was linked to higher rates of cytopenias, infections, and tumor lysis syndrome, these adverse events were generally manageable with supportive care. Given the limitations of retrospective database analyses and potential residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Ongoing prospective trials such as CLL17 may further clarify optimal frontline therapy selection.
