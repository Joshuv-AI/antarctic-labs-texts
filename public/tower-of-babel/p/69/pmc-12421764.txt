Biodegradation of patulin in apple juice by phosphoribosyl transferase (URA5): implications for food safety
Authors: Kopano Mapheto, Oluwakamisi Festus Akinmoladun, Tiisetso Colleen Maphaisa, Patrick Berka Njobeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Patulin (PAT), a mycotoxin produced primarily by Penicillium expansum, poses significant health risks and frequently contaminates apples and apple-derived products, often exceeding permissible safety limits. This study investigated the potential of orotate phosphoribosyl transferase (URA5) to degrade PAT in apple juice under controlled conditions. PAT degradation was assessed at initial concentrations of 100 µg/L and 250 µg/L, with enzymatic treatment using 0.2 mg/mL URA5. Samples were incubated for up to 24 h, and PAT degradation was monitored at time intervals of 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 h using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). The results demonstrated a time-dependent PAT degradation, with significant reductions observed as incubation time increased. After 6 h, PAT concentrations decreased to 57.30 µg/L and 112.69 µg/L for the 100 µg/L and 250 µg/L samples, respectively. At 12 h, PAT levels in the 100 µg/L sample fell just below the permissible limit (50 µg/kg), while substantial degradation was observed in the 250 µg/L sample. By 18 h, PAT concentrations dropped further to 47.22 µg/L and 40.10 µg/L, reaching safe consumption levels. After 24 h, degradation rates reached 96.36% and 98.25%, reducing PAT levels to 30.22 µg/L and 31.48 µg/L, confirming the efficacy of URA5 in detoxifying PAT-contaminated apple juice. The findings highlight the potential application of URA5 as a biocatalyst for PAT detoxification in the fruit juice industry. Compared to existing detoxification methods, enzyme-based degradation presents a promising, environmentally friendly, cost-effective, and non-toxic alternative. Further studies should explore its feasibility in large-scale processing and its interaction with other contaminants in commercial apple juice production.

Introduction

Mycotoxins are hazardous secondary metabolites produced by various toxic fungi that are widely distributed in nature [1]. These contaminants are commonly found in food and pose serious health risks to both humans and animals. To date, over 400 mycotoxins have been identified, with their contamination levels increasing in agricultural products both during and after harvest, particularly during storage [2]. However, only a few mycotoxins are actively monitored and regulated, as certain types extensively contaminate agricultural products and exhibit high toxicity in both humans and animals [3]. According to [4], the most significant mycotoxins include fumonisins (FBs), zearalenone (ZEA), ochratoxins (OTs), aflatoxins (AFs), and trichothecenes (THs), along with patulin (PAT), which is frequently detected in fruits, particularly apples. Apple is among the most widely cultivated fruits globally, with an annual production of approximately 86 million tons [4, 5]. As the primary ingredient in apple juice, apples undergo various processing methods, making PAT contamination a major food safety concern [6].

PAT is produced mainly by Penicillium expansum, the pathogen responsible for blue mould in apples, and was initially identified alongside Penicillium griseofulvum following Alexander Fleming’s discovery of penicillin [7, 8]. Beyond causing fruit decay, P. expansum produces PAT, a carcinogenic mycotoxin that poses significant economic and public health risks to the food and beverage industry. Therefore, consumers, especially children, are particularly vulnerable to exposure to contaminated fruit juices [9, 10] and [11]. reported that exposure to PAT, acute or chronic, can induce ailments in humans. The mechanism of PAT toxicity is partly due to its inhibition of the activities of sulfhydryl group enzymes, leading to the disruption of membrane integrity, RNA and protein synthesis, as well as other cellular functions [12]. Additionally, PAT’s strong affinity for sulfhydryl groups contributes to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), playing a critical role in the apoptotic process of human leukaemia cells [13]. To protect consumers, regulatory bodies have set a maximum allowable PAT limit of 50 µg/kg in fruit juices and apple-derived products [14]. Given its widespread occurrence in agricultural products, effective mitigation strategies are crucial to reducing PAT contamination during food processing.

Various strategies have been developed to prevent, eliminate, or inactivate mycotoxins in the food supply chain. These include physical methods such as cleaning, milling, cooking, roasting, and high hydrostatic pressure, as well as chemical treatments like ammonia and hydrogen peroxide [15–18]. However, these methods, including membrane separation and food-adsorption techniques, are unsuitable for large-scale use due to their high cost and uncertain safety [17]. Other approaches, including UV radiation, pulsed light, γ-irradiation, and food-adsorption techniques, have also been explored for patulin removal [17–21]. While ozone is an effective chemical method, concerns about toxic byproducts during oxidation remain [22]. Ascorbic acid has shown potential for reducing patulin stability but requires an acidic, juice-like environment for effectiveness [23]. Given these limitations, there is a pressing need for environmentally friendly, safe, and practical mycotoxin decontamination techniques.

Biological methods, particularly enzymatic degradation, are among the most promising strategies for patulin detoxification due to their mild reaction conditions, high specificity, and production of non-toxic byproducts [24]. Several enzymes have been identified with patulin degradation potential, including lipase from porcine pancreas [25, 26], ribonucleoside diphosphate reductase (NrdA) from Enterobacter cloacae subsp. dissolven [27], intracellular enzymes from Pseudomonas aeruginosa TF-06 [28], short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase from Candida guilliermondii [29], and extracellular lipase from Ralstonia [30]. However, the strains and enzymes with the potential for PAT degradation differ by source, reaction conditions, efficiency, and degradation products [31]. The known patulin degradation products—E-ascladiol, Z-ascladiol, desoxypatulinic acid (DPA) and hydroascladiol—have been shown to exhibit negligible toxicity compared to patulin [32–35]. Despite these advancements, the patulin-degradation potential of orotate phosphoribosyl transferase (OPRTase) remains largely unexplored, presenting an opportunity for further investigation.

Orotate phosphoribosyl transferase (OPRTase) is an enzyme that plays a crucial role in nucleotide biosynthesis. OPRTase is one of ten phosphoribosyl transferases (PRTases) involved in de novo and salvage pathways for nucleotide synthesis, as well as in the biosynthesis of histidine and tryptophan [36, 37]. It catalyzes the conversion of orotic acid to orotate-5’-monophosphate (OMP), and the attendant pyrimidine intermediates and derivatives contribute to the detoxification of harmful substances. In humans, OPRTase is involved in the activation of 5-fluorouracil conversion, and mutations in this enzyme are associated with Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, orotic aciduria, and 2,8-dihydroxyadenine lithiasis [37]. In previous studies, the degradation ability of OPRTase extracted from Rhodotorula mucilaginosa on PAT increased significantly from 0.00 g/L until it achieved a maximum of 81.04% PAT degradation at 0.15 g/L OPRTase in apple juice [38].

In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, orotate phosphoribosyl transferase (OPRTase) is encoded by two genes, URA5 and URA10, with URA5 accounting for approximately 80% of OPRTase activity [39]. Due to its crucial role in pyrimidine biosynthesis, URA5 has been widely used as a selectable marker in various fungal species, including Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus nidulans [40, 41], and Colletotrichum graminicola [42]. Beyond its genetic applications, research suggests that URA5 may influence cellular repair mechanisms and oxidative stress responses, potentially mitigating the toxic effects of compounds like PAT. Given these insights, this study investigated the enzymatic degradation potential of recombinant Cryptococcus neoformans var. neoformans serotype D Orotate phosphoribosyl transferase (URA5) (strain AA 1-225) in detoxifying PAT-contaminated apple juice. By assessing its efficiency, this research explores the feasibility of URA5 as a biocatalyst for mycotoxin detoxification in fruit juice processing.

Materials and methods

Materials

The primary sources of raw material in this study were apple juice (Granor Passi, Polokwane and Cape Town, South Africa), Patulin (PAT) pure crystalline standard (Fermentek Biotechnology, Jerusalem, Israel) and Recombinant Cryptococcus neoformans var. neoformans serotype D Orotate phosphoribosyl transferase (URA5) (strain AA 1-225) (CSB- YP317769CTL biological reagent) with a purity of ≥ 90% (Inqaba Biotech Africa (pty) Ltd, Pretoria, South Africa). Ethyl acetate, sodium carbonate and acetonitrile acetic acid of chromatographic purity (Biocom Biotech Africa (pty) Ltd Johannesburg, South Africa).

Study area

The study was conducted in Langkloof, Western Cape, Cape Town. Good manufacturing and agricultural practices were followed along the food processing chain of apples to apple concentrates. Samples of apple concentrate (70 °Brix, pH 3.2 to 4.10) batches were collected along the processing stage of the product and stored aseptically at -16 °C for analysis.

Production of Apple concentrate and blended Apple juice

Fruit Preparation for processing

Upon receiving the apple fruits, the samples were first washed with ozonated water (Fig. 1a). A quality check was then conducted on all batches to identify any rotten apples or visible fungal growth. To detect mycelial contamination, an automatic cutter sliced through the middle of each fruit as it moved along the conveyor belt, ensuring that contaminated apples were separated before processing to prevent further contamination of healthy ones. Any batch where the proportion of internally rotten fruits exceeded the acceptable threshold for juice production was rejected (Fig. 1b).

Processing of Apple juice concentrate

The washed apples were crushed into a mash, which was then pumped into a mash tank before being transferred to belt press machines. As the belts pressed against each other, the juice was extracted and subsequently pumped into storage tanks. The fresh juice was then heated and cooled in preparation for pectinase enzyme treatment.

Next, the juice underwent another heating and cooling cycle before being transferred to holding tanks, where enzymes were added and left to act for 10 min. Following enzymatic treatment, the juice was pumped through an ultrafiltration unit to obtain a clear liquid. The filtered juice was transferred to separate holding tanks, where activated charcoal was added to enhance colour. To concentrate the juice, it was pumped into a taste evaporator, where water was removed until it reached a concentration of 70° Brix.

Preparation of PAT stock, standard and enzyme working solutions

A 25 mL volumetric flask containing 5 mg of pure crystalline PAT was filled with 25 mL of ethyl acetate. This flask was labelled the stock solution, securely covered with aluminium foil, and stored in a freezer at -16 °C until further use. To calibrate the PAT standard stock concentration, 250 µL of the stock solution was transferred into a 5 mL volumetric flask and evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen stream [43]. The remaining residue was then homogenized and dissolved in 1 mL of 100% ethanol. The final PAT standard solution was analyzed using LC-MS, with the average PAT retention time recorded at 9 min.

To prepare PAT working standards, 100 µL of the PAT stock solution was transferred into a 10 mL volumetric flask and evaporated to near dryness at room temperature under a nitrogen stream. The residue was immediately diluted with the acetic acid solution and thoroughly mixed. Aliquots of 50, 100, 250, and 500 µL were taken, and corresponding volumes of 0.95, 0.9, 0.75 and 0.5 mL acetic acid solution were added in 1 mL volumetric flasks. The PAT working standard solutions were stored in amber vials wrapped in aluminium foil and refrigerated at 2–5 °C. Fresh PAT working standard solutions were prepared weekly.

To create a series of standard working solutions, acetic acid was used to dilute PAT solutions to concentrations ranging from 100 to 500 µg/L, yielding final concentrations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, and 50 µg/L. Both original and diluted solutions were further used to prepare a range of PAT concentrations from 1 to 250 µg/L, which were then used to generate a calibration curve at 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 µg/L.

Preparation of Recombinant cryptococcus neoformans var. neoformans serotype D URA5 (OPRTase)

The enzyme OPRTase was supplied as a lyophilized powder with a purity exceeding 90%, as determined by SDS-PAGE analysis. The molecular weight of the recombinant Cryptococcus neoformans var. neoformans serotype D URA5 (OPRTase) was characterized using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) following purification through Immobilized Metal Affinity Chromatography (IMAC) [44]. For further confirmation, peptide sequencing was performed after excising and purifying the SDS-PAGE gel band [45]. Prior to opening the enzyme vial, it was briefly centrifuged to ensure all contents had settled at the bottom, as recommended. The enzyme was then reconstituted to a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL using 200 µL of water and 200 µL of glycerol, facilitating long-term storage at -20 °C.

The chosen concentration of 0.2 mg/mL was based on both the manufacturer’s recommended activity range (0.1–1.0 mg/mL) and concentrations used in comparable enzyme-mediated degradation studies. For instance, Tang et al. [38] utilized OPRTase within a concentration range of 0–0.15 mg/mL in their studies on PAT degradation. Similarly, Song et al. [31] employed a concentration of 0.15 mg/mL using OPRTase from Cyberlindnera fabianii, while another study [46] applied 0.6 mg/mL of a reductase from Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The limitation of the enzyme resources and these precedents informed the selection of 0.2 mg/mL as an effective and relevant concentration for the present study.

Batch degradation experiments

A PAT solution was used to spike two aliquots, resulting in final concentrations of 100 µg/L and 250 µg/L—both exceeding the South African permissible limit of 50 µg/L for apple juices and apple juice-containing beverages. As a control group, unspiked apple juice samples were analyzed. Before testing, all solutions were frozen at -20 °C and then thawed overnight at 4 °C in the absence of light.

The PAT degradation assay was conducted in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes following the method described by [38], with modifications. A 0.2 mg/mL aliquot of OPRTase was mixed with PAT-contaminated apple juice for each concentration (100 µg/L and 250 µg/L). The samples were placed in a shaker-incubator (Labcon 3081U, Labcon, South Africa) and incubated at 36 °C for 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 h at 180 rpm.

After the 24-hour degradation period, samples were centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 10 min for PAT extraction [47]. PAT was extracted using ethyl acetate, followed by a sodium carbonate solution for sample cleanup. The quality of the extracted samples was assessed by filtering the supernatant through a 0.22 μm syringe filter before LC-MS analysis to identify and quantify any remaining PAT. The degradation assay was performed in triplicate to ensure reliability and reproducibility.

Quantification of PAT on the LC-MS

PAT analysis was conducted using a Shimadzu mass spectrophotometer (LCMS-CBM-40lite) coupled with a Shimadzu UHPLC system [48]. Chromatographic separation was performed using a Kinetex 150 × 4.6 mm × 5 μm C-18 100Å analytical column with an UltraGuard cartridge (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). The oven temperature was preheated to 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of: mobile phase A: 40 mL ammonium acetate in water (10:90, v/v); mobile phase B: 40 mL ammonium acetate in methanol (95:5, v/v) and gradient flow rate: 0.40 mL/min. In negative ionization mode, the mass spectrometry parameters were set as follows: probe voltage: 4 kV; block heat (BH) temperature: 400 °C; desolvation line (DL) temperature: 250 °C; interface temperature: 350 °C; nebulization gas flux: 3 L/min; drying gas flux: 15 L/min and the collision gas: Argon. Statistical analysis

Using IBM SPSS Statistics 22 (SPSS/IBM, Chicago, Illinois), analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the PAT concentration and PAT percentage degradation data [40] at the probability threshold of p ≤ 0.05. Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) was employed to ascertain significant differences between mean values. The average of three separate computations is represented by the mean ± standard error of mean.

Results and discussion

Patulin calibration and linearity

The linearity of PAT detection was evaluated over a spiking concentration range of 50–250 µg/L. The calibration curve was generated and repeated three times on separate days to ensure consistency in the analysis. For the linearity assessment, an optimal coefficient of determination (R²) > 0.997 was established, aligning with values reported in the literature [50]. The generated calibration curve demonstrated a strong linear relationship with an R² value of 0.9919. The accuracy of PAT quantification within the linear response range was calculated, with values ranging from 93 to 109.6% for each final concentration. The LOD was determined to be 27.2 µg/L, while the LOQ was 82.5 µg/L.

Analysis of PAT and Apple standard

The mass spectra of pure PAT obtained from LC-MS analysis are presented in Figs. 2a, b, and c. When analyzed using a higher source voltage probe, two dominant fragment ions were detected at m/z 109.15 and 81.10. Additionally, deprotonated PAT was identified at m/z 153.10. According to [48], the most intense fragment at m/z 109.15 was selected for quantification, as the ratio of these two fragment ions allows for the precise identification of PAT. Chromatograms of PAT-fortified apple juice samples are also displayed, illustrating the method’s effectiveness. This analytical approach is considered highly specific, as no interference from the sample matrix was observed during PAT retention time, ensuring accurate and reliable detection.

Orotate phosphoribosyl transferase results

After purification, the presence of OPRTase was confirmed using agarose gel electrophoresis, which revealed a single protein band at the predicted size of 28.4 kDa (Fig. 3). OPRTase was identified as the key enzyme responsible for PAT degradation and was therefore selected for use in this study. The purity of the OPRTase used was confirmed to be ≥ 90% by SDS-PAGE analysis. According to [38], optimal conditions for PAT degradation in apple juice were achieved using 0.15 g/L OPRTase with an initial PAT concentration of 1 mg/L, incubated for 18 h at 25 °C. Under these conditions, the PAT degradation rate exceeded 80%.

PAT degradation

The degradation of PAT was investigated by incubating two different concentrations of PAT-spiked apple juice with URA5. The study examined PAT degradation at initial concentrations of 100 µg/L and 250 µg/L, using 0.1 mg/mL of URA5. Each sample was analyzed before incubation to establish a baseline measurement, serving as a control. The degradation process was monitored over time at 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 h, with PAT reduction assessed based on peak area and height using LC-MS.

The results for 100 µg/L PAT are presented in Figs. 4a-g, while those for 250 µg/L PAT are shown in Figs. 5a-g. The final PAT recoveries at each time interval are summarized in Table 2. After quantitative analysis of the extracted samples, the proportion of PAT degraded by URA5 was calculated. The final PAT concentration in each sample was determined using a linear calibration curve to quantify the extent of degradation.

Two different PAT concentrations (100 µg/L and 250 µg/L) were analyzed, exceeding the permissible 50 µg/L limit [52]. The initial control concentrations were measured at 96.99 µg/L and 242.66 µg/L, with corresponding peak areas of 11,000 and 34,284, respectively. As shown in Table 2, PAT recovery ranged from 15.61% at 100 µg/L to 86.95% at 250 µg/L, demonstrating a high recovery rate, particularly at the higher concentration (250 µg/L). After 3 h of incubation, PAT concentrations decreased by 15.61 µg/L in the 100 µg/L sample and 86.95 µg/L in the 250 µg/L sample. However, the final recovered concentrations were 84.36 µg/L and 163.10 µg/L, both of which remained above the permissible limit. A statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) was observed after 3 h of treatment.

After 6 h (p > 0.05), PAT concentrations were reduced to 57.30 µg/L in the 100 µg/L sample and 112.69 µg/L in the 250 µg/L sample, with the degradation rate tripling (3×), indicating that longer enzyme contact time enhances PAT degradation and recovery. At 9 h, PAT degradation reached nearly 50%, with reductions of 52.25 µg/L in the 100 µg/L sample and 96.32 µg/L in the 250 µg/L sample, and although the final concentrations remained above permissible levels, the overall PAT reduction was significant.

After 12 h of incubation with URA5, the results showed that higher initial PAT concentrations resulted in higher recovery rates, with the 100 µg/L sample yielding a final PAT concentration of 49.99 µg/L, which fell just below the permissible limit, thus indicating the safety of the product for human consumption, while the 250 µg/L sample had a final concentration of 57.92 µg/L with a PAT recovery of 192.08 µg/L, demonstrating substantial degradation even though it remained slightly above permissible levels.

At 18 h (p < 0.05), PAT concentrations further decreased to 47.22 µg/L in the 100 µg/L sample and 40.10 µg/L in the 250 µg/L sample, showing significant differences (p < 0.05), with PAT recovery rates reaching 52.78% and 81.11% and degradation rates increasing to 71.82% and 93.88%, confirming that both products were now safe for human consumption as PAT levels had fallen below the permissible threshold. The degradation activities in this study revealed an increase in the degradation potential of the enzyme with increased PAT concentration. This contradicts the study conducted by [53] on the detoxification of patulin in apple juice by enzymes, with the degradation rate decreasing with an increase in patulin concentration. Similarly, the degradation rate of patulin by Orotate phosphoribosyl-transferase from Rhodotorula mucilaginosa decreased with increasing initial patulin concentration [38]. The improved degradation ability of the enzyme with increasing initial patulin concentration in this study could be attributed to the fact the enzyme was fully exposed to the active site of patulin for degradation. According to [38], the addition of orotate phosphoribosyl transferase from Rhodotorula mucilaginosa to apple juice spiked with 1 mg/L PAT at 25 °C for 18 h achieved a PAT degradation rate of up to 80%.

After 24 h (p < 0.05), the PAT degradation rate increased sharply to 96.36% in the 100 µg/L sample and 98.25% in the 250 µg/L sample, demonstrating highly effective degradation, with PAT recovery values of 69.78 µg/L and 218.52 µg/L, resulting in final PAT concentrations of 30.22 µg/L and 31.48 µg/L, respectively. Following incubation, both treated samples exhibited an overall PAT recovery rate of up to 87.41%, further supporting their suitability for human consumption. The result of this study revealed a time-dependent improvement in the degradation ability of PAT by URA5. In a similar study [54], found that not only was the extracellular enzyme(s) of Pichia calibic able to degrade PAT, but also that the degradation ability of the enzyme greatly improved after induction with PAT for 12 h. Also [55], found that the extracellular enzymes of Burkholderia spp. XHY-12 could reduce aflatoxin B1 and B2 to 15% in 60 h. In the report of [22], PcCRG1 from Pichia caribbica successfully degraded PAT from 20 µg/mL to undetectable levels within 72 h, while [56] concluded that Kluyveromyces marxianus YG-4 yeast effectively adsorbed and degraded PAT within 72 h at 28 °C. Contrarily, the extracellular enzymes produced by M. guilliermondii did not affect PAT degradation after 24 h [57]. The mycotoxin elimination mechanisms employed by microorganisms have been attributed to the physical adsorption of mycotoxins by cell walls and subsequent degradation [58]. The present study thus showed that the cells of URA5 could absorb PAT and degrade it.

Given the enzymatic role of OPRTase in nucleotide biosynthesis, particularly its involvement in modifying pyrimidine intermediates, its interaction with patulin, although not characterized in this study, may result in the formation of distinct degradation by-products. Patulin degradation typically involves hydrolysis, ring opening, or chemical modifications targeting its lactone group and α,β-unsaturated ketone moiety [53]. Several by-products resulting from enzymatic breakdown of patulin have been well documented, including E-ascladiol, Z-ascladiol, desoxypatulinic acid, and hydroascladiol [59].

For example [60], reported the use of a short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase, in the presence of NADPH, to convert patulin into E-ascladiol. In a related study [29], evaluated the potential of manganese peroxidase to degrade patulin, identifying hydroascladiol as the major intermediate. The authors proposed that enzymatic ring opening of the hemiacetal moiety was the primary degradation mechanism.

Despite these insights, there remains a significant knowledge gap concerning the specific by-products formed during patulin degradation by OPRTase. Given OPRTase’s native function in catalyzing the formation of orotidine 5’-monophosphate (OMP) in the pyrimidine biosynthetic pathway, it is plausible, though speculative, that phosphorylated derivatives, such as patulin-5-phosphoribosyl adducts, could form during the reaction. Confirming such products would require advanced analytical techniques, including LC-MS for initial identification, NMR for structural elucidation, and subsequent toxicity assessments to evaluate the potential risks of the degradation products.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the effectiveness of orotate phosphoribosyl transferase (URA5) in breaking down PAT into less toxic products. PAT degradation was evaluated at two initial concentrations (100 µg/L and 250 µg/L) through enzymatic treatment with URA5. While significant PAT reduction was observed, results showed that after 9 h of incubation, PAT levels in both samples remained above the permissible limit, making the products unsafe for human consumption. At 12 h, only the sample fortified with 100 µg/L PAT achieved a final concentration of 49.99 µg/L, which fell just below the safety threshold. By the 24-hour mark, PAT degradation was nearly complete, with final concentrations of 30.22 µg/L (69.78% recovery) in the 100 µg/L sample and 31.48 µg/L in the 250 µg/L sample. These results confirm that URA5 effectively reduces PAT contamination over time, making it a promising biocatalyst for PAT detoxification in apple juice processing.
