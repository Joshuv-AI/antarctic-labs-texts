Understanding human metacontrol and its pathologies using deep neural networks
Authors: Kai J. Sandbrink, Laurence T. Hunt, Christopher Summerfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceComputational systems have to learn when and how they should exert control over their actions. How do agents learn to solve this “metacontrol” problem? Here, we created a task that externalizes this control (through special actions that sample more information about the world or increase efficacy). Using deep neural network models, we show that only agents equipped with brain modules that explicitly compute the likely success of their actions perform optimally, behaving very similarly to human participants. This helps us understand why mammals have evolved brain signals, in the medial prefrontal cortex, that compute the likely success or failure of their actions. Moreover, perturbing this computation leads to behaviors reminiscent of psychological disorders such as depression, anxiety, and compulsion.

Humans and other animals have evolved dedicated neural processes that help them achieve their goals, known collectively as cognitive control (1–3). Control processes help ensure that actions are realized as intended, even in the face of uncertainty, conflict, or external perturbation. For example, if playing tennis on a windy day, or when tired, control processes might be engaged to help a player hit the ball more accurately. However, the value of control depends on the action-outcome statistics of the environment (4). Intuitively, cognitive control is most helpful when the environment is neither trivially easy nor impossibly hard to control, because in the former case automatic processes may suffice, whereas in the latter control is futile. Biological agents have evolved neural mechanisms that estimate the value of deploying control processes (5), which have been collectively called “metacontrol.” However, the computational processes that make metacontrol possible, and their neural implementation, remain unclear (6, 7).

Recently, neuroscientists have rediscovered connectionist (or neural network) models as tools for explaining cognition and brain processes (8–11). Deep network models learn to master complex tasks by trial and error, as model parameters are gradually adjusted via an optimization process (12). Deep networks can account for diverse behavioral and neural signatures that occur as animals perform perceptual and cognitive tasks (13–16). Here, we used networks trained with deep reinforcement learning (deep RL) (17), an emerging computational paradigm for modeling the brain (18, 19) as a theoretical framework for studying metacontrol. We asked under what computational constraints a deep RL agent might learn to optimally “control itself,” i.e., to engage control processes when they are demanded by the environment (7, 20–23).

The engagement of cognitive control has long been linked to processes that monitor for errors. In mammals, response errors lead to heightened neural signals contiguous with action onset, that are generated in brain regions including the medial prefrontal cortex (24–28). These have been called “action prediction errors” (APEs), because they signal a mismatch between intended and realized actions, importantly without carrying reward information (29–31). APEs may offer information about how controllable actions are, and thus can help regulate the subsequent deployment of control. For example, in paradigms like the Stroop or Eriksen Flanker task, or during task switching, response errors lead to more cautious responding (32, 33) and lower subsequent error likelihoods (34). The controllability of an agent’s actions is called its efficacy, describing its ability to implement its intentions (23, 35). In deep RL agents, learning is naturally guided by reward prediction errors, but networks do not typically come equipped with means to explicitly encode whether their actions are translated into their intended consequences (i.e., to compute APEs) and thus to estimate the controllability of their actions. Here, thus, using a reward-guided learning (“bandit”) task we asked whether providing deep RL agents with an additional module that computed APEs would help it learn an optimal metacontrol policy.

We report that deep RL agents can learn an approximately optimal metacontrol policy, but—critically—only when equipped with explicit mechanisms for computing APEs. When trained to predict the likelihood of errors, deep RL agents show patterns of metacontrol that closely resemble humans recruited to perform the same bandit task. Moreover, by introducing subtle biases in the way neural networks compute APEs, we can simulate patterns of disrupted behavior characteristic of human psychological disorders, such as learned helplessness and compulsivity (36). This provides a computational framework, based in deep learning, for understanding metacontrol and its pathologies.

Results

To study metacontrol in humans and deep networks, we employed a reward learning task that involves choosing between two actions that pay out a reward with unknown probability (a bandit task). The variant we chose, known as the “observe or bet task,” is designed to disambiguate information-seeking from reward-harvesting decisions (37–39) (Fig. 1A). On each trial, participants can decide to “bet” on one of the two bandits, yielding an outcome (rewarded or unrewarded, depending on the payout probability) that is not immediately disclosed. Alternatively, they can choose to “observe” whether each of the two bandits would have paid out or not, a choice that yields information but no reward. To introduce action prediction errors, we adapted this task by introducing a probability pflip that “bet” choices would be randomly reversed from chosen to unchosen bandit after participants placed their bet (Fig. 1C). We define the “efficacy” ξ of the environment as 1-2×p(flip) so that when ξ=0 the observer has no control over the environment, whereas ξ=1 is the standard case in which actions always play out as intended. On each trial, exactly one of the two bandits paid out. The payout probability was high for one of the bandits, phigh(r), and low for the other, plowr=1-phigh(r). For the version of the task we used for the human behavioral experiments, the payouts were deterministic, with phighr=1 and plowr=0. The payout probabilities reversed with a small probability on each trial (Materials and Methods). This formulation differs from the classic setting with full efficacy but a lower payout bias ϕ because participants receive fully informative feedback on observe trials, and receive information about p(flip) on bet trials that would be unavailable otherwise. To facilitate learning, at the end of each block of 50 trials, a screen was shown depicting the overall pattern of choices and outcomes, as in prior work using a previously described method (39) (Fig. 1B).

We used this task to study whether humans and deep networks could adapt the policy to account for the efficacy of their actions. Human participants were randomly split into two groups that were first trained and then evaluated on different subsets of the discrete values ξ∈0.125k0≤k≤8,k∈N} (Materials and Methods). During test blocks, the level of efficacy ξ was not explicitly cued, and had to be inferred on the fly from the pattern of response errors (during training human participants were given overt cues to assist learning, with the background color changing based on the current level of efficacy; see Materials and Methods). We began by using agents’ tendency to “observe” as proxy for their estimate of the efficacy of their actions. Intuitively, if ξ=0 then each realized action is made entirely at random (because p(flip)=0.5) and each “observe” is wasteful, because it yields no useful information, but incurs an opportunity cost. By contrast, when ξ>0 observation helps agents learn the value of each bandit for subsequent trials. We verified this intuition by calculating the optimal policy under each level of efficacy as a partially observable Markov decision process using an approach called SARSOP (40). This optimal model revealed that in our task, it is only advisable to “observe” for efficacy levels in excess of ξ∼0.4. Observation is also unwarranted toward the end of a block, as the opportunity cost exceeds the benefit of continuing to harvest at risk. Due to the volatility, it also becomes profitable to observe in cycles that are aligned to the end of the block at higher efficacy levels (Fig. 1D).

Learning Metacontrol: Humans.

We recruited n = 182 human participants (n = 111 after exclusions, see Materials and Methods) to take part in the study, which was preregistered at https://osf.io/9ewt8 (data from a pilot study with very similar results is described in SI Appendix). Since we are interested in comparing well-trained humans with well-trained neural networks, we conducted the study across three different days, with day 1 for practice, day 2 for task 1, and day 3 for task 2. Each day contained cued training and uncued test blocks as described above. In Fig. 2A (Left) we plot human observe frequencies on test blocks (from task 1) across the entire range of values of ξ. Without being explicitly cued, participants began to observe more frequently in more controllable environments after the first trials of the episode (P < 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons according to Tukey’s HSD; see Table 1 and SI Appendix for full statistics). As shown in Fig. 2B (Left) they were also more likely to intend correct bets when they had greater efficacy (P < 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons according to Tukey’s HSD; see Table 1 and SI Appendix for full statistics). Collectively, these differences mean that humans achieved greater rewards in the high-efficacy settings (P < 0.05 for pairwise comparisons ξ = 1 vs. ξ = 0.5 and ξ = 1 vs. ξ = 0, P < 0.1 for ξ = 0.5 vs. ξ = 0 according to Tukey’s HSD; see Table 1 and SI Appendix for statistics). Indeed, combining training and test trials, we can see that in humans both the frequency of “observe” choices, and aggregate reward, vary approximately linearly with the efficacy of their actions (Fig. 2 C and D, Left). After each episode, participants were asked to indicate what level of action efficacy they believed they were operating under, using a slider; their choices indicate they were able to explicitly estimate action efficacy even on test blocks, which were not disambiguated with a cue (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Thus, humans adapted their behavior to the efficacy of their actions, similar to an ideal agent.

Learning Metacontrol: Deep Networks.

Next, we studied the conditions under which deep neural networks were able to “meta-learn” how to adjust their behavior to the efficacy of their actions (41, 42). Before comparing neural networks with human behavior, we first tested the networks’ capacity for learning and generalization on a separate version of the task (see Materials and Methods for an overview). In this version, we used probabilistic bandits, so the high-paying bandit paid out with probability p(r1) = 0.9, and the low-paying bandit with p(r2) = 0.1. Agents were trained on blocks defined by ξ∈0,13∩23,1 and evaluated on blocks with the same disjoint values of ξ. We trained deep RL agents to maximize reward using REINFORCE (43) with baselines, a machine learning method that uses deep networks to learn a mapping from observations onto optimal action probabilities (Fig. 3A). The network was equipped with a long short-term memory (LSTM) layer (44), which incorporates recurrence (activation memory) and gating in a way that partially resembles the prefrontal cortex (45). This is necessary to learn within a block [i.e., to adapt policy within the block on the basis of past actions, observations, and rewards, also known as “learning to reinforcement learn” (46, 47)]. The network was trained with backpropagation to maximize reward across several hundred training episodes.

We created three variants of the deep RL network. All variants receive as inputs the feedback received on the previous turn ot-1, if any, alongside both the action realized on the previous trial at-1, and the originally intended action a^t-1 (i.e., that which was chosen before any flip was applied). Critically, like humans, the networks do not receive access to trial-wise reward information while performing the task, although it is used retrospectively to update the weights during training after a batch of episodes. They output intended action probabilities for the current trial p(a^t), which are translated into behavior at. The standard variant has no further inputs or outputs, and thus has to infer the level of efficacy implicitly, from frequency of match or mismatch between a^ and a in its inputs. Other variants explicitly compute an APE, which is defined asδAPE,t=0, if at=a^t1, otherwise.

The ξ-input variant receives the action efficacy ξ directly, as an input signal, but is otherwise identical to the standard variant. The APE-trained network produces an additional output that estimates an error likelihood signal, i.e., the probability of an intended bet action switching, p^t(flip). This scales directly with efficacy of their actions ξ, which can be estimated using ξ^t=1-2p^t. We train the network to produce this estimate by using APEs as a teaching signal and adjusting network parameters using gradient descent with a dynamic learning rate α:p^t←p^t+α·δAPE,t-p^t.

The network then learns to predict the probability of errors in-context. This mirrors the proposed function of the medial prefrontal cortex in frameworks that emphasize the learning of error likelihoods or the expected value of control (5, 34). Relative to the other networks, the APE-trained variant has just a single additional node that encodes this prediction (Fig. 3A). To exactly parameter-match the APE-trained models, we train additional standard+ networks that have the exact same architecture as the APE-trained model, including an additional node between recurrent and feedforward layers, but do not receive the additional APE-signal.

We observed that the APE-trained network was able to solve task 1, converging just below the maximum reward level obtainable, as revealed by SARSOP. However, the standard and APE-input variants struggled, reaching a level of reward that was only slightly higher than that expected under random choice (Fig. 3B). To ensure that the standard network did not fail because it had one unit fewer than the APE-trained version, we also included a network called standard+ that was parameter-matched to APE-trained; this did not improve performance (and for brevity we do not consider it further). Thus, computing APEs seems to be important for learning a metacontrol policy.

Neither the standard nor the ξ-input network learned to respond to the efficacy of their actions. This can be seen in Fig. 3C, which plots the average frequency of “observe” choices as a function of ξ. For the standard and ξ-input networks, this relationship is flat (or mostly flat). Although the standard networks did learn to use the “observe” choices, they did so at a low, constant rate across all efficacy levels in both versions of the task, and were therefore not able to harvest much more reward when they had more efficacy. However, APE-trained networks (like humans) observe more in more controllable environments, and so obtain higher levels of reward (Fig. 3D). The proportion of “observe” choices over efficacy, and the average reward, for the APE-trained and standard networks are very similar to those obtained by SARSOP (Fig. 3 C and D), revealing that APE-trained networks (but not other variants) learn the optimal metacontrol policy. Interestingly, if we manually set the efficacy node to the ground truth label, rather than having the network learn the representation, a few but not all of the seeds learn to adapt (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), indicating that the saliency of the representations that arises through training contributes meaningfully to the adaptation.

On the “human” version of the task, the time-varying pattern of observation and reward in these networks is also strikingly similar to that seen in humans, alongside which they are plotted in Fig. 2 A–D (Center and Right). The APE-trained model’s policy therefore predicted human choices significantly better than the standard model for all but the lowest efficacy levels (estimated model frequencies of 0.9955 ± 0.0006 and 0.0045 ± 0.0006, mean ± SEM, n = 111 and exceedance probabilities of ~1 and ~0 for APE-trained and standard, respectively; see Table 1 and the SI Appendix for statistics). For this version of the task, we trained the neural networks on the entire efficacy range ξ∈[0,1], to ensure a valid comparison with humans.

Neural Representations of Control in Deep Networks.

Next, we studied how efficacy was internally represented in the APE-trained networks and other variants. We used principal component analysis to visualize how the trajectory of population responses of hidden layer units for the LSTM was affected by different levels of action efficacy, both for APE-trained and standard networks. In APE-trained networks, neural responses begin from a common point for all levels of efficacy but then separate out along a controllability axis in the first two principal components over the course of the episode, with activity under low- efficacy conditions at one end of PC1 and high- efficacy conditions at the other (Fig. 3E). However, no such segregation is observed for the standard networks, indicating that efficacy does not explain as much variance in their representations (Fig. 3F). For completeness, we trained a classifier to decode ξ from the recurrent layer (full lines) and the final fully connected (FC) linear layer (dashed lines) of the APE-trained, ξ-input and standard networks (Fig. 3G). Consistent with the neural state space analysis, decoding was much poorer in the recurrent layer of standard networks. Surprisingly, however, with sufficient training, efficacy could be decoded from the final layer of standard networks just as readily as it could from APE-trained networks, and could be decoded throughout the ξ-input network with even greater accuracy (Fig. 3G). This implies that explicitly computing APE signals is not required to encode action efficacy per se, but instead helps format information about efficacy in a way that makes it most likely to be read out to guide behavior. In line with this, when we use an encoding model to see what fraction of variance of individual neurons is explained by ξ, the APE-trained networks do indeed represent efficacy more strongly (average encoding r2 over final 25 trials: 5.25 × 10−3 ± 1.67 × 10−3, mean ± SEM for APE-trained, −5.34 × 10−5 ± 3.38 × 10−5, mean ± SEM for standard, and 1.19 × 10−4 ± 4.59 × 10−5 for ξ-input; P < 0.05 for both pairwise comparisons with APE-trained according to an independent t test, see SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Thus, by predicting when errors might occur, neural systems learn to encode action efficacy in a useful format for behavioral adaptation.

Together, these analyses imply that deep RL agents are able to learn an optimal metacontrol policy, but only when asked to explicitly predict how controllable an environment is. However, this claim is potentially limited by our reliance on the frequency of observe choices as a proxy for the agents’ estimate of efficacy. Optimal behavior on the task requires “observe” choices to be made more frequently in controllable environments, and so (for example) agents could be learning to make auxiliary (nonbet) actions whenever the world is more controllable. To rule this out, it becomes necessary to modify the task to include a condition where auxiliary actions were required when efficacy was low, rather than high. This is what we did next.

Learning to Increase Control via Self-Care.

On day 3, human participants returned for an extra session in which they performed a new test (task 2). In this task they were offered (on each trial, in addition to observe and bet options) a third alternative that increased ξ by 0.1 for the remainder of the episode (to a maximum of 1). We call this the “sleep” option, and equate it with self-care processes that increase efficacy and thus reduce response errors. For example, a tennis player will serve more accurately after a good night’s rest. In this new task, participants learned to use the sleep action early on in training, and between trial 8 to 20 they used it more frequently when ξ was low (Fig. 4 A, Left), leading to an overall downward trend in use of the sleep button and the efficacy of their actions (Fig. 4 D, Left panel; P < 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons according to Tukey’s HSD; see Table 2 and SI Appendix for statistics). This meant that just on day 2, overall human reward grew with ξ (P < 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons according to Tukey’s HSD; see Table 2 and SI Appendix for statistics). By contrast, now the number of observe responses now did not grow with level of ξ (Fig. 4 B and E, Left panels; P > 0.82 for all pairwise comparisons according to Tukey’s HSD; see Table 2 and SI Appendix for statistics). This presumably occurs because the participants sleep consistently at the start of the block, effectively rendering every condition highly controllable, and obviating the need to observe differentially with levels of ξ.

As can be seen in Fig. 4 A–F (Center), these findings were almost precisely recapitulated by the APE-trained network, including the time course of sleep choices for each level of action efficacy (Fig. 4A). By contrast, although again the standard network learned to use the auxiliary action, sleeping on average 2.5 times per episode, it did not learn to adjust its rate based on efficacy (Fig. 4 A–F, Right). These results indicate that humans can learn to take steps to increase their efficacy when it is needed and when they do not have a lot of it. The correspondence of the APE-trained models with that of the human data illustrate the importance of forming a salient and accurate representation of efficacy. In the next sections, we will investigate what happens when this representation is perturbed.

A Deep Learning Model of Learned Helplessness and Compulsivity Partially Predicts How Individuals Learn the Value of Control Based on Transdiagnostic Factors.

Many psychological disorders can be conceived of as pathologies of control (4). For example, people that underestimate the level of action efficacy may refrain from taking useful actions in the belief that they are pointless. This is called learned helplessness, and it has been proposed to be a major component of depression and anxiety disorders (48–50). By contrast, agents that overestimate the level of action efficacy may compulsively overexplore, even when doing so has no effect on outcomes. This is reminiscent of the behavioral pathologies in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), where participants may engage in stereotyped or ritualistic actions that lack apparent motive. OCD has been linked to misperception of control (51, 52).

We used our APE-trained networks to create simulations of these pathologies. The trained efficacy-readout predicts efficacy based on the position of neural activity along a single linear axis in the network’s high-dimensional recurrent latent space. At test, we added a perturbation to the hidden unit representation along this axis, systematically biasing its output to over- or underestimate action efficacy, and replaced the value of the readout node itself (Materials and Methods). This neural perturbation was successful in increasing or decreasing the number of “observe” responses (total number of observe actions across all efficacy settings: 67.84 ± 6.76, mean ± SEM, n = 10 for δξ=+1; 38.16 ± 2.94, n = 10 for δξ=0; 12.34 ± 2.24, n = 10 for δξ=-1; P < 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons according to a paired t test with Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons). Since the perturbation was introduced at inference time (test), the network continued to respond as if the recurrent layer was representing efficacy truthfully. The results are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1A, which illustrate how behavior changed across levels of efficacy when the efficacy readout was biased by different amounts for both task 1 and task 2 (similar results were obtained by giving false feedback to the networks, explicitly “gaslighting” them into believing that the world was more or less controllable than it really was, see SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In SI Appendix, we explore structure in behavioral variation as we thus perturb the networks to either over- or underestimate the level of efficacy of their actions. We observed a distinct pattern of correlation as we compared behavior for different levels of perturbations across efficacies, resulting in autocorrelated behaviors and a block-like pattern for rewards.

In the human population, we saw similar patterns of correlation that resembled those of the perturbed APE-trained networks. This is presumably because our randomly sampled human population contains a spectrum of individual differences, with some participants more prone to depressive/anxious type phenotypes, and others to show forms of compulsivity. With this idea in mind, at the end of the study we asked participants to complete a short self-report questionnaire which has successfully been used to capture these traits in healthy populations (36) (using scoring tools available at https://github.com/the-wise-lab/FACSIMILE/). The survey required participants to note how much they agreed with statements such as “I get tired for no reason” and “I get upset if things are not arranged properly.” The survey questions were chosen to be the most important for reconstructing scores on three transdiagnostic dimensions: 1) anxiety and depression (A/D), 2) compulsivity and intrusive thoughts, and 3) social withdrawal (53, 54). We preregistered our prediction that participants with higher scores on A/D would be less prone to observe in task 1, and more prone to sleep in task 2, whereas the converse would be true for those with higher scores on compulsivity. We had no predictions either way about social withdrawal.

In Fig. 5A, we show the observation probability in task 1 (left) and sleep probability in task 2 (right) predicted by a multivariate linear regression based on participant A/D and compulsivity scores. The reward-maximizing strategy is highly dependent on ξ, so we perform the analysis separately for both low efficacy levels with ξ∈{0,0.125,0.25,0.375} and high efficacy levels with ξ∈{0.625,0.75,0.875,1} since they required different behavior from participants. Contrary to our preregistered predictions, we did not see convincing evidence for a linear relationship between transdiagnostic scores and the probability of observe choices. The overall regressions were insignificant for all behavioral measures except the number of observe actions in task 2 [R2 = 0.064, F(2, 108) = 3.720, P = 0.027 for low ξ; R2 = 0.074, F(2, 108) = 4.320, P = 0.016 for high ξ; see SI Appendix for full statistics]. For task 1 observe actions, the resulting coefficient for A/D was weakly positive but not significant for either high or low efficacy levels (coefficient m = 0.592, P = 0.121, n = 111 for low ξ; m = 0.623, P = 0.100, n = 111 for high ξ). The relationship for compulsivity was weakly negative for low efficacy settings, and weakly positive for high efficacy (m = −0.339, P = 0.384, n = 111 for low ξ; m = 0.058, P = 0.881, n = 111 for high ξ). We observed that A/D and compulsivity scores were positively correlated (Pearson’s R = 0.358, P = 1.16 × 10−4), which could be an indication of a shared axis of psychopathology. Since our neural network simulations predict opposite effects of A/D and compulsivity phenotypes on estimated efficacy, we adopted an exploratory approach in which we identified factors of variance in questionnaire data using data-driven methods. We orthogonalized the space spanned by A/D and compulsivity by calculating the principal components across both factors. The first principal component (PC1), which had positive coefficients across both factors (0.729 for A/D and 0.685 for compulsivity) and explains 67.9% variance, presumably represents generic psychopathological factors shared across the whole cohort. The second principal component (PC2), which has coefficients with opposite signs for both factors (0.685 for A/D and −0.728 for compulsivity, with the sign chosen so that A/D has the positive coefficient) and explains the remaining 32.1% of variance, was a better candidate to index the transdiagnostic symptoms that differ between these phenotypes (Fig. 5B). In Fig. 5E, we plot the joint distribution of observe choices as a function of efficacy and quantile of PC2 on task 1, and in Fig. 5H for task 2.

In the center panel, we plot the predictions from the trait-simulated deep network population, sorted by quantile of perturbation. Each plot shows how a given variable (e.g., rewards in Fig. 5D) varies jointly with both efficacy (on the x-axis) for participants sorted into nonile bins by their transdiagnostic scores, as measured by PC2 (see SI Appendix, Fig. S9 for similar effects on PC1, SI Appendix, Fig. S10 for original transdiagnostic scores, and SI Appendix for full statistics). On the rightmost panel we see the predictions from an unperturbed network, which by definition has the same response for each quantile—as there is no variation by trait. The trait-simulated network predicts that several variables will depend not just on efficacy but on transdiagnostic score, or on the interaction between these variables. As can be seen, transdiagnostic scores interact strongly with behavioral performance across efficacy levels: Participants with higher PC2 score tend to observe slightly more frequently on task 1 (higher values in the lower part of the plot) and strongly more on task 2, matching the population response of the perturbed APE-trained networks (fraction remaining similarity explained by the trait-simulated networks: 0.019, 90%-CI = −0.209 to 0.180 for task 1; 0.567, 90%-CI = 0.401 to 0.610 for task 2).

Over the other panels in B–F we repeated this exercise for relevant variables, namely rewards (Fig. 5D) in task 1 and rewards (Fig. 5F), sleep (Fig. 5G), and observe (Fig. 5H) actions in task 2 (see SI Appendix, Fig. S9 for the remaining variables). PC2 was correlated significantly more strongly with behavior of the perturbed neural networks for all of these factors except number of sleep actions in task 2 (fraction remaining similarity explained: 0.662, 90%-CI = 0.557 to 0.699 for task 1 rewards; 0.232, 90%-CI = 0.194 to 0.248 for task 2 rewards; −0.399, 90%-CI = −0.665 to −0.064 for task 2 sleeps, see Fig. 5C). The relationship between transdiagnostic scores and performance in the task is further highlighted by the difference in transdiagnostic scores between participants included in the study and those who were excluded from analysis on the basis of preregistered criteria for out-of-distribution behavior on the practice day [A/D: 0.135 ± 0.084, mean ± SEM, N = 111 for included and −0.199 ± 0.107, mean ± SEM, N = 71 for excluded, t(180) = 2.437, P < 0.05; compulsivity: 0.171 s ± 0.082, mean ± SEM, N = 111 for included and −0.043 ± 0.110, mean ± SEM, N = 71 for excluded, t(180) = 1.546, P = 0.124]. Together, these results imply that our perturbed neural networks were able to partially capture human variation in a tendency to under- or overestimate the efficacy of their actions, as measured by responses to transdiagnostic questionnaires. However, the relationship between survey responses and the predicted patterns of behavior in our task is more complex than the one that we originally preregistered, and will need to be confirmed with further research.

Discussion

We studied the ability of humans and deep neural networks to learn a metacontrol policy—to adapt their behavior to the level of efficacy of their actions, operationalized as the probability that an action is converted into its intended consequences. We did not explicitly cue action efficacy in either humans or networks, who instead had to estimate this quantity on the fly from the history of actions and outcomes. For computational inspiration, we drew upon the framework of deep meta-learning, which is increasingly popular as a theory of human cognition (41, 42). Deep networks were able to meta-learn a near-optimal solution to this task, but only when explicitly trained to the likelihood of an error—and thus to infer levels of environment efficacy.

This finding provides a candidate computational explanation for why biological agents have evolved to explicitly monitor and update levels of uncertainty or conflict in the environment beyond reward-related feedback, that is, the likelihood that an error will occur, suggesting these representations could help mediate trade-offs governing exploration and learning in the brain. It also explains why APEs—a neural signal coding mismatch between intended and realized actions—are such prominent brain responses for humans and other animals (24–27, 29, 34). In a refinement of existing frameworks, we predict it may be possible to identify value-free error signals reflecting subjectively estimated degrees of efficacy (due to both internal and external factors) in the medial prefrontal cortex. Prior work has found benefits to auxiliary predictions in other domains to avoid representational collapse in deep reinforcement learning (55, 56), and has made the link to brain representations in areas such as the hippocampus (57). One of the most interesting aspects of our data was that it was not sufficient for the network simply to represent action efficacy per se. In fact, in ξ-input networks, which did not compute APEs but instead received efficacy explicitly as an input, this variable was consequently much easier to decode—and yet these networks never learned an optimal metacontrol policy. Tentatively, although the information remains decodable, the standard and ξ-input networks represent efficacy less strongly than the APE-trained ones (as evidenced by the difference in low-dimensional projections of activity in Fig. 3 E–G and the encoding analysis in SI Appendix, Fig. S2). This could lead to interference between updates, causing the policy to converge to the same behavior across different levels. In other words, there exists a deep connection between the requirement to predict how controllable the environment is, and the capacity to reformat information in a way that permits the exercise of cognitive control.

Our human behavioral experiments show that the APE-trained network learned a metacontrol policy that was very similar to that displayed by people. Figs. 2 and 4 show strikingly conserved behavioral signatures (between humans and networks) over the course of blocks with different levels of efficacy. Of note, our manipulation involved both conditions where lower levels of action efficacy required participants to overlook (“observe” in task 1) or engage with (“sleep” in task 2) auxiliary actions, and so are unlikely to be driven by generalized levels of engagement or disengagement with the task. Rather, both humans and APE-trained networks learn how to exploit available controllability (by observing) or to increase controllability (by sleeping) in the service of reward-maximization. In this paper, we use “observe” and “sleep” as externalizations of cognitive control processes that would result in, for instance, increased response time. Studying the relationship between these externalizations and internal processes represents an interesting direction of future research.

We used perturbations to create deep networks that exhibit individual differences in how they learn the value of control—being encouraged to either over- or underestimate action efficacy. We found that human participants who report transdiagnostic traits previously associated with anxiety/depression and compulsivity display patterns of behavior that are similar to neural networks that are perturbed to over- and underestimate action efficacy respectively (as derived from the sign of the coefficients on the second principal component). This is consistent with theories that cast these pathologies as disorders of control that result in behavioral abnormalities. For example, depressed patients often neglect to take the initiative—to seek information that would permit reward-maximizing behaviors, which could come from under-estimating the likelihood that these actions will translate to a positive outcome as intended. Conversely, OCD patients may overestimate the efficacy of their actions, leading them to compulsively check for the value of latent states, at the expense of reward-maximizing (51). Both perturbations thus create behavioral pathologies.

However, we note here that the correspondence between over- and underestimation of action efficacy and the symptomatology of A/D and compulsion is not precise. In particular, we found that the main component of transdiagnostic scores in our convenience sample was a generic one which jointly indexed both A/D and compulsive symptomatology. The axis spanning an A/D phenotype (indicative of underestimation of action efficacy) and a compulsive phenotype (overestimation) emerged in the second principal component, and its relationship with the predictions of our population of perturbed neural networks was close but not exact. It is possible that with a significantly larger cohort (thousands rather than ~100 participants) this result would be much cleaner. As our participants were recruited from the general population on Prolific and therefore without clinical symptoms, it is also possible that the effect would be more apparent in participants with severe disorders. In either case, prior studies have also found opposing associations, with obsessive-compulsive behavior sometimes linked to decreased activation of goal-direct systems and a reduced sense of control as in our study (54, 58), indicating that symptomology might be more complex than what is captured by the three factors. Nevertheless, we think that our work opens up avenues for studying these behaviors using computational tools based on deep neural networks.

Materials and Methods

Human Experiments.

Data collection was carried out online using the platform Prolific Academic (https://prolific.com). The experiment was collected in two batches over successive weeks following a 3-d procedure. Following preregistered exclusion criteria (https://osf.io/9ewt8), we had complete data for 111 participants. These were randomly assigned to group A or group B, determining the allocation of efficacy levels to train or test.

The experiment was conducted using JSPsych (59), and is available online (https://www.hipexperiments.co.uk/sandbox/tasks/kai/HumanObserveBetEfficacyDay1Of2).

Neural Network Simulations.

We also simulated machine learning solutions to the task. First, we approximated an upper bound on performance using SARSOP, a solver for partially observable Markov decision processes, in JuliaPOMDP (60). Then, we simulated various kinds of deep neural networks architectures on the task, using REINFORCE with baseline in PyTorch with no additional libraries.

See SI Appendix for a more detailed version of the methods.
