Procedural Utility, Reliability, and Success of Endovascular Intervention for Peripheral Arterial Disease Utilizing Transradial Access
Authors: Ahmad Khraisat, Zaid Abood, Mark W Mewissen, Mohamed Osman, Suhail Q Allaqaband, Tanvir Bajwa, M Fuad Jan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransradial access (TRA) in coronary interventions is widely recognized for its reduced complication rates and improved patient outcomes. Its application in peripheral vascular interventions (PVI) is limited.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the electronic medical records of all patients who underwent PVI via TRA between November 2021 and November 2024 at a single center. The primary outcome was procedural success.ResultsA total of 137 patients (median age, 70 years; 51% men) with 213 lesions were included; smoking (94.2%) and hypertension (93.4%) were the most common comorbidities. Chronic total occlusions were present in 36.4% of cases. Access was predominantly obtained via the left radial artery (83.9%); the right radial artery was used in 7.2% of cases. Bilateral interventions were performed in 21.8% of cases. The success rate was 98.5%, with a 2.1% rate of periprocedural complications. TRA facilitated early ambulation (30-120 minutes) and a 96.5% same-day discharge rate.ConclusionsTRA appears to be a viable alternative to femoral or brachial access for PVI, demonstrating reduced complication rates and enhanced patient outcomes and satisfaction in this series. A few tools are needed to perfect and facilitate this approach.Keywords: peripheral arterial disease, peripheral vascular intervention, transradial

Introduction

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is a progressive atherosclerotic disease affecting more than 236 million people1 and associated with 2- to 3-fold higher mortality.2 Although PAD refers to atherosclerotic stenosis in any peripheral artery, the term most commonly describes lower extremity arterial disease. Endovascular peripheral vascular interventions (PVIs) are the first-line therapy for invasive management of patients with PAD and critical limb ischemia or lifestyle-limiting claudication.3 Transfemoral access (TFA) has historically been the first choice in PVI but is associated with significant access site complications (ASCs), including retroperitoneal bleeding, hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, and thrombosis.4,5 These are even more common in patients with PAD and carry a 2.5-fold increased risk of surgical intervention.6 We previously reported that even with ultrasound guidance, the TFA ASC rate remained high at 3.5%, with 10% requiring surgery.7 Transbrachial access is an alternative when TFA is contraindicated or inappropriate, such as in patients with previous femoral surgery, a prosthetic endograft, tortuous iliac arteries, or morbid obesity. However, it carries a high risk of neurovascular ASCs, including brachial plexus injuries, brachial artery thrombosis, and upper limb ischemia, which can lead to significant morbidity.8,9

In recent years, transradial access (TRA) has become the standard of care in coronary artery diagnostic and interventional procedures owing to its lower incidence of ASCs, improved patient comfort, and improved procedural outcomes compared with traditional TFA.10,11 These benefits of TRA in coronary procedures have stoked interest in adoption of the radial route for PVI.12,13 In addition to shorter length of stay, substantial cost savings, and improved patient comfort and satisfaction, TRA is believed to enable interventions on bilateral lower extremity lesions without need of an additional access site or second procedure14,15 during PVI on fully anticoagulated patients without significant added bleeding risk.16

Despite these benefits, TRA for PVI remains underused, primarily owing to the lack of a large body of contemporary experience, perceived technical challenges, and the lack of dedicated equipment for the transradial route. Recent advancement in TRA equipment—for example, longer sheaths, wires, and catheters; longer-shaft/larger balloons; and 10-mm stents deliverable via a 6F sheath—is making PVI via TRA more attractive. This study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of TRA in one of the largest contemporary cohorts undergoing PVI via TRA. By evaluating key outcomes such as procedural success, ASC rates, and need for an additional access site, we sought to determine whether TRA can serve as a reliable, possibly preferable, alternative to traditional access routes in PVI.

Materials and methods

We reviewed patients who came to the cardiac catheterization laboratory between November 2021 and November 2024 with the intent of PVI for symptomatic PAD where the operators were committed to adopt a radial first approach for the PVI. The Advocate Health-Wake Forest University School of Medicine institutional review board approved this study and waived the requirement for patient consent. Patient demographic characteristic data, including age, sex, and comorbidities, were collected, along with procedural details such as anatomic location of arterial interventions; access site laterality; use of intraoperative ultrasound guidance for access; stent deployment; use of drug-coated balloons (DCBs), atherectomy, and contrast volume; procedure and fluoroscopy duration; complications; success; and same-day discharge. Data collection was performed via manual chart review by 3 authors (A.K., Z.A., M.O.) and cross-referenced by 2 authors (A.K., Z.A.). The Rutherford classification system was used to evaluate the severity of lower extremity claudication. Radial artery patency and suitability for access were assessed preprocedurally in all patients using the Allen and/or Barbeau tests and duplex ultrasonography. Ultrasound was used to access the left radial artery in almost all cases; the left ulnar artery was the alternate access site if the left radial artery was not deemed feasible for access (<2 mm; occluded or severely calcified). The right radial artery was rarely used. Sheath lengths were selected based on lesion location and patient height (Figure 1). Postprocedural hemostasis was achieved using a TR Band (Terumo Medical) with or without a SoftSeal-STF (Chitogen). The use of a SoftSeal in TRA has been described by our group in a randomized controlled trial.17,18 Interventions included plain-balloon angioplasty followed by DCB angioplasty, stenting (if indicated), and/or atherectomy. Postprocedural evaluations of all patients involved monitoring access site patency and identifying complications such as hematoma or hand ischemia. At 30 days, a history and physical examination were conducted to assess outcomes and complications. The primary outcome was procedural success, defined as successful intervention of the target lesion(s) without conversion to femoral access or need of an additional access site. Secondary outcomes were fluoroscopy time, total procedure duration, time to ambulation, same-day discharge, ASCs (eg, hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, and arteriovenous fistula), bleeding requiring transfusion, acute stroke, acute kidney injury (increase in creatinine by 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours), and 30-day mortality.

Suggested sheath lengths for left transradial access in peripheral arterial disease interventions. Estimated arterial bed lengths from the left radial artery in patients of <1.82 m (6 feet) are as follows (add an additional 10 cm for right radial access): subclavian (50-75 cm), renal (80-100 cm), common iliac (105-115 cm), common femoral (125-135 cm), superficial femoral (135-155 cm), popliteal (155-170 cm), and foot (200-230 cm). These measurements serve as a reference for selecting appropriate sheath lengths during peripheral arterial disease interventions with transradial artery access. SFA, superficial femoral artery.

Procedural technique

Patient positioning and access

When performing left TRA (Figure 2), the patient is typically positioned with the left arm abducted to a 90° angle on an arm board, and the operator stands caudal to the patient’s left arm. The scrub table is continuous with the arm board, and imaging monitors are directly in front of the operator, to the left of the patient’s head (Figure 3). A key challenge of TRA is extracorporeal wire management: extended wire lengths (400 and 480 cm) exceed the scrub table length, making maintenance of the sterile field tedious. Using devices with a rapid exchange platform helps mitigate this issue. Through our evolving experience, we engaged with industry partners to develop better wire options, leading to the creation of the Medtronic F18 Flex, a 0.018-inch, 400-cm wire, with improved torque control and better maneuverability. Access is obtained via ultrasound-guided puncture of the radial or ulnar artery, followed by insertion of a 10-cm Glidesheath Slender (Terumo) and administration of a radial cocktail (5000 units heparin and 5 mg verapamil). A pigtail catheter, usually of 125 cm (Cordis), is advanced over a 0.035-inch, 300-cm Rosen (Cook Medical) or Silverway wire (Asahi Intecc) for an abdominal aortogram and bilateral lower extremity angiogram with runoff. The pigtail and short sheath are then exchanged over a 0.035-inch, 300-cm SupraCore wire (Abbott) for an R2P Destination Slender guiding sheath (Terumo) or Sublime radial access guide sheath (Surmodics; typically 119 or 120 cm, respectively) to allow selective engagement and intervention of the target side (Figure 1).

Aortoiliac intervention

For aortoiliac disease, depending on patient height (Figure 1), a 105- or 119-cm, 6F R2P Destination Slender or Sublime 120-cm sheath is parked just proximal to the diseased segment. Multiple adjunctive support and crossing catheters are available, but we prefer a 0.035-inch or 0.018-inch, 150-cm NaviCross catheter (Terumo) with either a 0.035-inch or a 0.018-inch angled Glidewire Advantage guide wire (Terumo). Multiple balloons and self-expandable stents up to 10 mm are available in various shaft lengths ranging from 135 to 200 cm (Table 1).

Infrainguinal intervention

For infrainguinal disease (superficial femoral artery [SFA] and popliteal arteries), a 6F R2P Destination or Sublime 119/120-cm sheath is used for proximal SFA lesions, whereas a 149- or 150-cm sheath may be preferred for distal SFA and popliteal lesions. A 0.018-inch, 300-cm angled Glidewire Advantage guide wire, supported by a 0.018- or 0.035-inch, 200-cm Sublime microcatheter if necessary, is used to traverse the lesion. The wire is subsequently exchanged for either a 0.018-inch, 480-cm Roadrunner wire (Cook Medical) or the F18 Flex 0.018-inch, 400-cm wire. Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) is performed using a 0.035-inch, 200-cm R2P Metacross RX (Terumo); 0.018-inch, 200-cm Crosstella RX (Terumo); or 0.018-inch, 220-cm Sublime RX PTA balloon, typically followed by a 0.018-inch, 200-cm shaft IN.PACT Admiral DCB (Medtronic). If a stent is required, the 0.035-inch, 200-cm R2P Misago RX stent (Terumo) is deployed. If 0.018-inch, 480- or 400-cm wires are unavailable, we trap a 0.018-inch, 300-cm guide wire using a 3 × 12-mm RX NC coronary balloon inflated at the distal end of the sheath to facilitate over the wire (OTW) DCB and microcatheter exchanges without losing wire position. If an atherectomy is required, a 0.014-inch, 150-cm Shockwave E OTW intravascular lithotripsy balloon (Shockwave Medical) or 0.014-inch, 200-cm Diamondback 360 peripheral orbital atherectomy device (Abbott) can be used after exchanging for a 0.014-inch wire (Command; Command ES; or SpartaCore; Abbott).

Infrapopliteal intervention

Infrapopliteal arterial disease (runoff) treatment via TRA is challenging for operators; however, devices are increasingly available to facilitate this approach. We initiate treatment with a 6-F 149-cm Destination or 150-cm Sublime sheath, followed by a 200-cm Sublime microcatheter if additional support is required. A 0.018-inch angled Advantage Glidewire is then exchanged for a 0.018-inch, 400-cm F18 Flex or 0.018-inch, 480-cm Roadrunner wire when OTW balloons are necessary. The workhorse wires of choice are the 0.018-inch, 300-cm angled Glidewire Advantage and 0.014-inch, 300-cm Command ES. For more distal lesions or OTW balloons, the 0.018-inch, 400-cm F18 Flex or 0.018-inch, 480-cm Roadrunner wire is often supported by a 0.018-inch, 150-cm NaviCross or 0.035- or 0.018-inch, 200-cm Sublime microcatheter. When 400- or 480-cm wires are unavailable, we use wire trapping with a 3- × 12-mm NC coronary balloon at the tip of the long sheath to facilitate OTW balloon and catheter exchanges. Devices available for this purpose include the 0.035-inch, 200-cm R2P Metacross RX; 0.018-inch, 200-cm R2P Crosstella RX; 0.018-inch, 220-cm Sublime RX PTA; and 0.014-inch, 250-cm Sublime RX PTA, which can reach ankle vessels. For atherectomy, the 200-cm Diamondback is an option. Currently, no stents that can reach these vessels via TRA are available.

Statistical analysis

The database was created using Excel 2019 (Microsoft). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or median with IQR and compared using paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables were expressed as count (%) and compared using χ2 or Fisher exact tests. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp). A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 140 patients with the intent of PVI via TRA between November 2021 and November 2024 were identified. Three patients who had small radial/ulnar artery diameters (<2 mm) and required initial femoral access were excluded, leaving 137 patients for analysis. The median age was 70 years, 51% were males, and 36.4% had Rutherford classes 4 to 5 claudication. The majority of the patients were smokers (94.2%) and had hypertension (93.4%) (Table 2). The left radial artery was accessed in 115 patients (83.9%), the right in 10 (7.2%), and the left ulnar in 12 (8.7%). No case required secondary bailout access during the procedure.

Chronic total occlusions (CTOs) were present in 50 cases (36.4%), with 93.3% of below-knee cases involving CTOs (n = 14/15 cases). Thirty cases (21.8%) had bilateral lower extremity lesions treated in the same session. Five cases involved severe in-stent restenosis. A total of 213 lesions were treated, and of these, 93 (43.6%) were in the iliac arteries (common iliac, n = 48; external iliac, n = 45; inflow). The most common treatment site in the infrainguinal territory was the SFA (n = 70; 32.8%). The femoropopliteal (outflow) segment accounted for 104 lesions (48.8%) and the infrapopliteal (runoff) 8 (3.7%) (Table 3). Balloon angioplasty was performed in all lesions and atherectomy in 3.7% (Table 3). All SFA lesions were treated with balloon angioplasty followed by a DCB; a stent was used primarily if there was flow-limiting dissection. Atherectomy was used for heavily calcified lesions with suboptimal results after balloon angioplasty. Procedural success was achieved in 135 patients (98.5%). Two patients in whom the procedure was not completed via TRA required femoral access at a later date owing to a failure to cross a heavily calcified CTO and a reentry device that did not have a long enough shaft for left radial access during the index TRA procedure (Table 4).

Complications were observed in 3 cases (2.1%). In 1 case, the end of the R2P Destination Slender sheath severed and was entrapped in the radial artery at the end of the procedure. There were 2 cases of Sublime balloon shaft fracture in the proximal SFA: 1 was treated by jailing it with a stent, and the other required surgical removal. In the patient in whom the sheath had to be removed surgically, attempts were made, with great care, to relieve radial artery spasm; both intra-arterial verapamil and nitroglycerin were used repeatedly. The patient was heavily sedated, and local anesthesia at the site of the radial artery sheath insertion was used. Careful attempts were made to manipulate and pull the sheath further back. However, we observed that the spasm would not be relieved, and the sheath began to stretch and unravel. The access wire was lost. Given the degree of spasm, we were concerned that further attempts would injure or totally dissect the artery. At this time, vascular surgery was consulted, and an incision was made at the wrist and extended superiorly and inferiorly. An additional incision was made at the antecubital fossa. The end of the embedded sheath was noted to be just below the brachial artery bifurcation. The patient had a good ulnar signal, and the hand was viable. The radial artery was transected distally, and the sheath was removed. The patient had good ulnar signal upon completion. In the second case, after stenting (×2) a long CTO of the right SFA and after deflation of the 6 mm × 80 mm postdilation balloon, we attempted to remove the balloon from the body but met resistance before we exited the stented segment. Gentle traction was applied, but we noted that the balloon fractured from the catheter shaft and was retained within the stented segment of the right distal SFA. The balloon was still attached to the wire. We tried withdrawing the wire back into the sheath, but the fractured balloon and shaft were unable to be retrieved into the sheath. Attempts to remove the balloon with a 6F gooseneck snare were unsuccessful. When we made no progress in the case and the fractured balloon shaft was retained within the right common femoral artery, we were left with no endovascular option, and vascular surgery was consulted for right femoral cutdown, endarterectomy, and removal of the retained fractured balloon. The fractured balloon shaft was successfully removed from the body intact. Endarterectomy was needed for extensive atherosclerotic calcified plaque. Additionally, an ulnar pseudoaneurysm that required surgical repair was observed in 1 case (0.7%). No stroke, acute kidney injury, or major bleeding was observed.

The median total procedure time was 71 minutes, and the median fluoroscopy time 22.4 minutes. The median contrast volume was 142.5 mL. The median time to ambulation was 1 hour, 46 minutes, and the median time to discharge was 2 hours, 17 minutes. A total of 114 patients were scheduled outpatient cases; of these, same-day discharge was achieved in 110 patients (96.5%). At 30 days, outpatient physical examination showed radial artery patency in all cases (except the case treated with surgical removal of the embedded sheath), and no mortality was observed.

Discussion

This is one of the largest single-center experiences to evaluate the feasibility and safety of exclusive TRA for PVI (radial first). We found that TRA enabled complex and multilevel lower extremity endovascular interventions with high technical success (Central Illustration). Only 2 cases (1.4%) required conversion to femoral access at a later date owing to failure to cross the lesion. Our findings are comparable to previous, smaller reports. Sher et al14 reported a 100% technical success rate in their single-center experience of 36 patients, and Castro-Dominguez et al15 reported a 99% success rate in a multicenter cohort of 120 patients. In a slightly larger single-center experience, Sayfo et al18 reported a success rate of 98%.

Despite the high prevalence of complex lesions in our cohort (femoropopliteal and below-knee CTOs), we demonstrated a high success rate of 98.5% with no need of additional access sites. This must be understood within the context of the significant evolution in TRA equipment availability during our study period (November 2021 to November 2024). The availability of longer dedicated sheaths, extended-length wires (including the F18 Flex 0.018-inch, 400-cm wire developed through industry collaboration) and extra-long balloons and atherectomy devices was crucial to achieving these results. Earlier in the adoption of TRA for PVI, and likely at the beginning of our own program, limited equipment availability can necessitate bailout access. Centers beginning TRA programs may encounter these equipment limitations. Our results not only highlight the effectiveness of TRA as a standalone approach when appropriate tools are available but also emphasize that success is closely tied to the continued evolution and availability of purpose-built, long-shaft peripheral devices. Our results are noteworthy because previous studies have reported a need for additional access. In the TRIACCESS study, in which 180 patients were randomized into TRA, TFA, or transpedal access for SFA treatment, 30% of patients in the TRA and pedal groups required a secondary access site.19 Similarly, Castro-Dominguez et al15 reported that 25% of their cases required an additional access site despite a lower CTO rate (33.6%). The results of our study appear consistent with the recent American Heart Association Scientific Statement, which highlights that the evolving armamentarium for TRA can improve the success of complex interventions, although need for adjunctive access may remain a challenge in many centers.20

Our predominant use of left radial access (83.9% of cases) represents a significant departure from standard coronary practice, where right radial access is typically preferred for operator convenience and familiarity. For coronary interventions at our institution, we routinely use right radial access following standard practice patterns. However, the anatomical requirements for peripheral interventions favor the left radial approach despite a setup that introduces variation in standard catheterization laboratory practice.

The left radial artery provides several technical advantages for peripheral interventions, including a more direct anatomical path to the descending aorta, reduced tortuosity compared with the right radial approach, and elimination of the need to navigate the challenging aortic arch anatomy that can complicate catheter manipulation from the right side. This anatomical benefit becomes particularly important in the treatment of infrainguinal disease, during which precise catheter control and reduced manipulation are crucial for success.

However, operators and staff in the catheterization laboratory need to be aware that the setup requires patient positioning with left arm abduction at 90°, modified operator positioning caudal to the patient’s left arm, and repositioning of imaging monitors and scrub tables. Wire management becomes particularly challenging with extended wire lengths (400-480 cm) that exceed standard table configurations, requiring careful attention to sterile field maintenance. The learning curve for operators accustomed to right radial coronary procedures includes adapting to reversed ergonomics, modified hand positioning, and altered spatial relationships. For those cardiac catheterization laboratories in which the room cannot be set up for left radial intervention, the PVI can be performed via the right radial artery by abducting the right arm of the patient. The operator can then stand by the head of the patient, facing caudad, with the monitors facing the operator.

Despite these challenges, our experience suggests that the anatomical advantages of left radial access for peripheral interventions justify the modified setup. The 98.5% success rate achieved with predominantly left radial access supports this approach, although we recognize that institutional preferences may vary based on operator experience, laboratory configuration, and available equipment. Centers adopting TRA for peripheral interventions should consider both anatomical advantages and logistical challenges when determining their preferred access strategy.

Access site complications remain a major concern in patients undergoing endovascular intervention, particularly those with PAD and TFA access. We observed a low ASC rate (0.7%), with no cases of major bleeding or transfusion requirement, reinforcing the safety of TRA in this high risk population. The absence of major bleeding is especially relevant since TFA is associated with higher bleeding risk in patients with PAD. This safety profile21 aligns with the American Heart Association Scientific Statement, which emphasizes the lower risk of ASCs and reduced bleeding associated with TRA compared with those of TFA for PVI.20

Regarding cerebrovascular complications, our study reported a zero stroke rate, aligning with previous studies, including a report that demonstrated no significant difference in stroke risk between TRA and TFA for aortoiliac and femoropopliteal interventions.22 To minimize stroke risk during TRA for PVI, our center follows a procedural strategy that emphasizes repetitive reinforcement, a standard operating protocol for TRA cases, meticulous catheter preparation, and early management of radial artery spasm to avoid excessive manipulation of catheters across the vessels and promotes the use of hydrophilic catheters and sheaths to facilitate smooth navigation.

Beyond its safety profile, our study demonstrated that TRA is an efficient approach for PVI. Median fluoroscopy time was 22.2 minutes, and median contrast volume was 142.5 mL. These findings align with those of previous studies reporting that TRA had comparable fluoroscopy times with TFA interventions while offering potential advantages in the ability to treat bilateral lower extremities in a single session, shorter length of stay, same-day discharge rate, and overall patient comfort and satisfaction.20,23 Some reports also suggest that TRA may reduce contrast volume owing to the more direct catheter path and reduced need for multiple vascular manipulations.24 Minimizing contrast exposure is important given the high prevalence of chronic kidney disease in patients with PAD.

Our findings also align with existing literature regarding procedural safety. Sayfo et al18 reported a periprocedural complication rate of 3.84% and Kumar et al24 3.2% in single-center studies, higher than our observed rate of 2.1%. This reinforces the safety profile of TRA in a real-world setting.

As adoption of TRA continues to expand, additional research is warranted to refine patient selection criteria and optimize procedural techniques. The availability of longer dedicated TRA sheaths, wires, and catheters has improved the feasibility of this approach, and our study adds valuable data supporting its broader implementation.

Limitations

As a single-center study, our findings may not be generalizable to all centers, particularly those with less TRA experience. In addition, the available armamentarium for PVI is limited by the lack of covered stents with 200-cm shafts that can be delivered through a 6F TRA sheath, intravascular ultrasound, thrombectomy, lysis catheters, and directional atherectomy devices.

Conclusion

Our study is timely and adds to the limited body of evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of TRA for PVI. The high procedural success rate, low complication profile, and patient-centered benefits we observed emphasize the potential for TRA to become a preferred approach for PAD interventions. The number of available radial devices continues to grow as the industry opens itself to cooperating with and responding to operators’ interest in achieving alternatives that may be better accessed for PVI to improve patient outcomes. TRA in PVI may follow coronary experience, with TRA becoming the first-line approach for PVI as it has for percutaneous coronary interventions. Future studies should focus on larger, multicenter cohorts and randomized comparisons between TRA and TFA to further validate these findings. At the same time, emphasis on establishing training protocols and fostering strong industry partnerships could drive widespread adoption of TRA for PVI. With the right investments in education, research, and technology, we believe this procedural route will follow a similar trajectory as TRA for coronary interventions and become a cornerstone of peripheral interventions.
