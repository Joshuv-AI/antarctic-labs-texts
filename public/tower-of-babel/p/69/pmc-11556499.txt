The Relative Impact of Clinical and Investigational Factors to Predict the Outcome in Stroke Patients
Authors: Rizwana Shahid, Azra Zafar, Saima Nazish, Erum Shariff, Foziah Alshamrani, Danah Aljaafari, Nehad Mahmoud Soltan, Fahad A Alkhamis, Aishah Ibrahim Albakr, Majed Alabdali, Maher Saqqur
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective:As stroke is still considered a significant cause of mortality and morbidity, it is crucial to find the factors affecting the outcome in these patients. We aimed to interpret the various clinical and investigational parameters and establish their association with the outcome in stroke patients.Materials and Methods:This is a retrospective, cross-sectional study, conducted in the Department of Neurology between June 2019 to November 2021. The study involved the review and analysis of medical records pertaining to 264 patients, admitted with the diagnosis of stroke. Various clinical, radiological, and electroencephalographic (EEG) patterns in stroke patients were analyzed and their association with outcome was established. The association between the studied variables was performed by the logistic regression (LR) and presented as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI).Results:The study sample consisted of 264 patients. Males comprised 165 (62.5%) with the mean participant age of 57.17 ± 18.7 3 years (range: 18–94). Patients younger than 50 years had a better likelihood of a good outcome in comparison to patients older than 50. The admission location was the most significant factor in predicting the outcome (P = 0.00) in favor of inpatient department and outpatient department (OPD), in contrast to patients admitted directly to intensive care unit (ICU). Normal EEG was associated with good outcome (P = 0.04; OR, 3.3; CI, 1.01–10.88) even after adjustment of the confounders, whereas patients having marked EEG slowing had a poor outcome (P = 0.05; OR, 2.4; CI, 0.65–8.79). Among the clinical parameters, hemiparesis (P = 0.03), trauma (P = 0.01), generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTC) (P = 0.00), and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale of more than 4 were more likely associated with a poor outcome as well as the presence of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) or infarction in the cortical and cortical/subcortical locations were associated with poor outcomes. After adjustment of confounders, the factors found to have prognostic significance in favor of good outcomes were inpatients or OPD referrals and normal EEG while direct admission to ICU, marked slowing on EEG, and presence of ICH were found to be associated with poor outcome.Conclusion:Certain patterns are predictive of good or worse outcomes in stroke patients. Early identification of these factors can lead to early intervention, which in turn might help in a better outcome. The results of the study, therefore, have some prognostic significance.

INTRODUCTION

Given the fact that stroke is still a serious problem and a major cause of disability and death, more reliable techniques for determining the factors affecting the outcome in this setting are essential.[1] Being a noninvasive and easily available test, routine electroencephalographic (EEG) is frequently requested not only to diagnose epilepsy or seizure disorders but rather it also provides valuable information in identifying the potentially reversible causes of altered mentation, such as nonconvulsive seizure, metabolic or toxic encephalopathy, or status epilepticus.[234] In the past, in the absence of readily available imaging, EEG was utilized frequently in predicting or detecting stroke in carotid endarterectomy;[5] however, nowadays, due to the emergence of more sophisticated brain imaging and currently, the use of EEG in stroke centers has been limited to diagnose poststroke epilepsy or differentiating possible seizures as stroke mimic.[67] Localized cerebral lesions like a stroke can cause ipsilateral EEG slowing and alteration of local physiological electrical activity.[8] EEG findings in stroke patients have been the subject of extensive clinical research in the past,[135689] but most of the current studies on EEG and stroke utilized more sophisticated EEG monitoring. The study conducted by Bentes et al.[10] utilized quantitative EEG analysis and found its usefulness in the assessment of ischemic lesions and their prognosis. In a prospective study with routine EEG,[11] the authors found that patients with stroke in general have the characteristics of EEG patterns including focal slowing and generalized slowing which might be found even without any evidence of clinical or suspicious seizures. The findings of systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies by Bentes et al.[12] revealed 7% and 8% of the patients following stroke have ictal and interictal epileptiform discharges (EDs), respectively, and based on their results, they suggested whether EEG should be used more frequently after stroke as a biomarker for epileptic manifestations. Many studies have documented the association between poststroke seizures and increased mortality, longer hospital stay, and disability at discharge;[1011] however, the effect of the epileptiform abnormalities as the independent predictor of outcome in the setting of stroke is uncertain. Thus, we aimed to evaluate the association of various clinical, EEG, and imaging parameters in a group of stroke patients concerning the outcome parameters. The findings obtained by our study can provide some insight for future decision-making in this subgroup of patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Setting

The study was conducted in a retrospective, cross-sectional manner after approval by the Institutional Review Board (IRB-2022-01-280). It was carried out in the Department of Neurology of King Fahad university hospital, Alkhobar, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia from June 2019 to November 2021.

Study subjects

All patients above 18 years of age, who were admitted with the diagnosis of stroke, and underwent EEG recording for various reasons were included in the study. A minimum sample size of 267 patients was calculated using the Daniel 1999-based formula for finite population, assuming a population proportion of 50%, precision of 6% (0.06), and an available population of 47,300 patients and stroke prevalence rate of 43.8/100,000[13] with 95% confidence limits.

Data collection

Data were collected through the electronic medical record of the hospital. The demographic and clinical variables including age, gender, clinical presentation, level of consciousness, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) rating on the day of the EEG as well as investigational procedures, i.e., EEG patterns, and neuroimaging findings were considered the predictors of outcome.

Outcome parameters

Outcome measure was the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at discharge. Patients with mRS 0–2 were defined as having good, whereby ≥3 was poor outcome, respectively.

Electroencephalographic acquisition

Nihon Kohden Electroencephalograph Model EEG-9200K (Version 05–21) was used for standard EEG recording. For each patient, at least 20 min of recording was obtained using silver–silver chloride disk scalp electrodes placed according to the international 10–20 system, utilizing both bipolar and average referential montages, with measured impedances <5 kohm at all electrodes. Analog signal conditioning included a 35 to 70 Hz low pass filter, a 0.3 to 1 Hz high pass filter, and a 60-Hz notch filter. The digital sampling rate was 200–500 Hz. Activating techniques, including intermittent photic stimulation and hyperventilation, were applied unless medically contraindicated. EEG records were evaluated and interpreted by a consultant neurologist, who classified the observed patterns as normal and abnormal. Abnormal patterns were further classified as focal EDs, generalized EDs, slow wave background activity (theta, theta/delta, or delta activity), periodic discharges (PDs), asymmetric background, markedly attenuated cerebral activity, and burst suppression. Theta activity was defined as generalized slow background activity with a frequency of 4–7 Hz and amplitudes exceeding 40 μV without intrusions of delta (<4 Hz) or alpha (8–13 Hz) activity for <20% of recording during wakefulness. Theta/delta activity was defined as generalized slow background activity of 4–7 Hz and amplitudes of >80 μV with the intrusion of alpha activity (8–13 Hz) for <20% of recording during wakefulness, along with intermixed with delta activity (<4 Hz) in 20% to 50% of recording during drowsiness or arousal. Delta activity was defined as generalized background activity of <4 Hz and amplitudes of >80 μV with the intrusion of theta or alpha activity for <20% of recording during drowsiness or arousal.[14] PDs were defined as the repetition of a waveform with relatively uniform morphology and duration with a quantifiable inter-discharge interval between consecutive waveforms and waveform recurrence at nearly regular intervals. EDs were defined as polyspikes, with a duration of <70 ms for the spike or 70–200 ms for a sharp wave (SW) consistently followed by a slow wave in a regularly repeating and alternating pattern, and were further subcategorized as focal or generalized depending on the involvement of EEG epochs.[15] The asymmetric background was defined as asymmetry in amplitude on the referential recording of ≥50% amplitude or >1 Hz frequency asymmetry, present for the majority (>50%) of the epochs/records. The markedly attenuated cerebral activity was defined as suppression (<10 mV) of activity in the entirety of the record. Triphasic waves (TWs) (currently referred to as generalized periodic discharges with “triphasic” morphology) apply to PDs and SWs, defined as repetitive electrographic elements consisting of 3 phases, each longer than the preceding one, that is, a surface positive high-amplitude (>70 μV) wave preceded and followed by negative waves with a smaller amplitude.[13] The persistent focal slowing was defined as waves of frequency <8 Hz, which commonly appear at one or a few electrodes and persist for at least 60 s.[15] Burst suppression was defined as discontinuous EEG, with more than 50% of the record consisting of attenuation or suppression with periods of marked suppression or isoelectric intervals alternating with bursts of activity, with or without embedded epileptiform features.[16]

Neuroimaging

Analyses also included neuroimaging findings. For this purpose, either computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was utilized, whereby MRI was performed on 1.5-T MRI apparatus and included T1-, T2-weighted, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery and diffusion-weighted sequences. Each imaging was reviewed and analyzed by a consultant neuroradiologist and was categorized as normal or abnormal. Abnormalities were further classified based on the etiology as infarction, hemorrhage, or hemorrhagic infarct as well as according to lesion sites as cortical, subcortical (SC), and cortical/subcortical stroke.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York) and the results were expressed as number (percent), mean (standard deviation [SD]), or median (interquartile range) as appropriate. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and related percentages. Chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables between outcomes. To establish the statistical significance of differences between the groups in terms of age, sex, patient location, clinical presentation, baseline NIHSS, EEG patterns, and radiological parameters, Pearson’s Chi-squared test was conducted. Variables were subjected to multivariate analysis with logistic regression (LR) after univariate analysis to calculate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The study sample consisted of 264 patients with stroke whose EEG records were reviewed and subjected to further analysis. The demographic data and clinical characteristics of the patients included in the study are presented in Table 1, indicating that males comprised 165 (62.5%) with a male: female ratio of 1.66:1. The mean participant age was 57.17 ± 18.7 years (range: 18–94). One hundred and thirty-five (51.1%) patients were referred for EEG from the intensive care unit (ICU), 106 (40.2%) patients from the general ward, and 23 (8.7%) patients were referred as an outpatient. The most common presentation was altered level of consciousness (ALC) and affecting (37.5%, n = 99) followed by unilateral weakness in 31.8% (n = 84). Another frequent presentation was various types of seizures we may omit it and keepm as overall 57 (21.6%) of patients with seizures making overall 57 (21.6%) patients. The majority of the patients had evidence of stroke on first imaging (n = 240, 90.9%), while in 9% of the patients, the diagnosis of stroke was based on clinical judgment. As shown in Figure 1, generalized theta wave activity was the most frequent EEG pattern (109, 41.3%), followed by focal EDs in 67 (25.3%) while 40 (15%) EEG records were reported as normal. Upon arrival, all patients underwent CT scans as a part of the stroke protocol, and the 100 patients had subsequent MRIs to evaluate the stroke type and location. The outcome was classified as good or poor based on the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS). The outcome was considered good or favorable with mRS of 0–2, while patients with mRS of 3 and above were categorized to have a poor outcome. Among the clinical parameters, patients above the age of 50 were more likely to have a poor outcome, although the difference did not reach statistical differences. Investigational parameters included in the study to predict the outcomes were EEG and brain imaging findings. DISCUSSION

Analyses conducted as a part of this study suggest that many clinical, EEG, and radiological factors can affect the outcome in stroke patients. Understanding these factors can help in early identification, which in turn might lead to the implementation of preventive strategies to improve the outcome. The use of routine EEG is not only restricted to diagnose epilepsy, rather it has diverse indications like most of the time, it is requested to identify the potentially reversible causes of delirium, like non convulsive status or metabolic/ toxic encephalopathies.[234] The authors have previously utilized EEG in stroke patients to detect the other causes of disturbed mentation which is not explainable by stroke exclusively.[12] In our center, more than two-third of the EEG requests were for reasons other than seizures in stroke patients. The most common reason was unexplained or disproportionate ALC in 37.5%. Harmon et al.[17] have reported the same findings previously. They reported altered mental status (n = 52, 26.0%) and seizures (n = 48, 24.0%) to be the most frequent reasons for performing an EEG. Prior similar studies relating EEG features in structural or critically ill patients have reported that ALC was the reason for admission to ICU in 7% of the patients[18] while 12.5% of patients had ALC during the hospital stay, and in up to 30% of the patients, the cause of ALC was a neurological condition.[19] In our study, we could not find any disparities regarding the EEG patterns in association with stroke, as previously described by our center.[2021] These findings are not surprising, as half of our EEG recordings were requested for ICU patients, and previous authors have reported the prevalence of neuronal dysfunction on EEG in ICU patients. There is a postulation that cortical impairment is usually associated with background slowing while SC abnormalities are usually associated with slow wave activity in the delta range or TWs.[22] In addition, the abnormalities, involving both cortical and SC structures, can lead to the combination of slow wave activity along with background slowing.[22] Our results were comparable to the prior work from our department[2021] and also confirmed the association of specific EEG patterns such as focal slowing (P = 0.003), generalized theta waves (P = 0.00), generalized delta activity (P = 0.04), persistent focal slowing (P = 0.003), and asymmetrical background (P = 0.01), with abnormal brain imaging findings. The association between the EEG patterns and outcomes in stroke patients without having clinical seizures has been reported previously by utilizing the routine EEG.[11] Many other studies have previously described this association, but most of the literature that assessed the stroke prognosis with EEG was based on quantitative EEG methods or continuous EEG monitoring. Kayser-Gatchalian and Neundörfer[23] have reported an association between EEG background slowing with stroke severity, which in turn affects the outcome. Cillessen et al.[24] also reported background slowing with unfavorable stroke outcomes. Rogers et al.[25] have established the objective prediction of functional outcome using the single left prefrontal channel in 16 stroke patients and found that it has significant prognostic value in predicting the functional outcome. Lima et al.,[26] in their prospective study, which included 157 patients with acute stroke also concluded that admission NIHSS, age, and the presence of epileptic form abnormalities were significantly associated with the stroke outcome at 3 months. The study conducted by Rogers et al.[25] found that the relationship of theta value of >0.025 on EEG was correlated with clinical outcome. Our observations were more or less consistent with the prior work as mentioned. Results of the prior work by Bentes et al.[10] in their prospective study also revealed that NIHSS of more than 4 was a predictor of unfavorable outcomes in anterior circulation hemispheric stroke and was independently associated with NIHSS score (P = 0.001), EEG background activity slowing (P = 0.001), and asymmetry (P = 0.001). Watson et al.[27] have previously reported burst suppression and markedly suppressed EEG activity to be associated with more adverse clinical outcomes in critically ill and sedated patients. We could not prove this association, which might be the result of a few patients having burst suppression and asymmetrical background in our study. Wang et al.[28] have reported stroke location in the cortical area as well as its severity measured by the NIHSS stroke scale as a major factor associated with the poor prognosis. Similar observations were made in other studies.[2930] The present study findings also revealed high NIHSS and cortical and cortical/SC stroke to be associated with higher odds of poor clinical outcomes. Stroke location as a predictor of the clinical outcome of stroke has been the topic of extensive clinical research in the past. Cheng et al.’s[31] findings were related to infarct location with the stroke outcome in terms of the Modified Rankin Scale. In their study, 101 patients were enrolled prospectively with middle cerebral artery infarction, and an association between higher mRS in regions involving the corona radiate, internal capsule, and insula was established in their study. Their findings also revealed asymmetrical impact pattern in association with infarct location was found in their study. Other authors also found the results of functional MRI mapping of lesion location with stroke outcome. their results revealed that the lesion located at the junction of the corona radiata leading into the corticospinal tract were correlated with a decreases motor performance,[32] while study conducted by Munsch et al also proved the stroke location as the predictor of cognitive outcome.[33] This association was proved in the primary analysis but was not persistent after eliminating the confounders in our study.

CONCLUSION

The presence of certain clinical and investigational parameters has a relative impact on the outcome in stroke patients. As stroke is a serious neurological disorder associated with high morbidity and mortality, identification of these factors can help in early intervention. Thus, the results reported here can have some significant prognostic implications as well.

Limitations

The findings presented in this study should be interpreted subject to limitations. First, as it is a retrospective, cross-sectional one, the exact time from stroke onset to EEG request was not uniform and could not be predicted reliably through the electronic medical record and prone to the effect of confounders. EEG interpretation might be biased due to differences in interpretation and reporting of the findings due to different reviewers having reported the findings and unavailability of video EEG monitoring. Another limitation of our study is a relatively small sample size; therefore, further large-scale, prospective studies are recommended.
