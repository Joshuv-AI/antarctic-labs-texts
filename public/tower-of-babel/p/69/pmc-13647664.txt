Sensitivity and predictors of false-negative SPECT myocardial perfusion imaging in a high-burden coronary artery disease population: a retrospective analysis using revascularization as the reference standard
Authors: Lauren Wright, Jay Hamze, Tarun R Nagrani, Nikki Arnold, Raquel McGlone, Jeremiah Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery disease (CAD) places a disproportionate burden on rural Appalachian communities; Scioto County, Ohio has among the highest CAD-related hospitalization rates in the state. Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) myocardial perfusion imaging is widely used, yet its performance in high-burden populations with substantial comorbidity is poorly characterized. We evaluated the sensitivity of SPECT for predicting subsequent revascularization and identified predictors of false-negative results.MethodsRetrospective analysis of prospectively collected American College of Cardiology CathPCI registry data at a single community hospital, including all cardiac catheterizations from Q3 2013 to Q1 2018. Sensitivity was calculated using subsequent revascularization (percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting) as the primary reference standard, with angiographic ≥ 70% and ≥ 50% stenosis as secondary standards. Logistic regression with forward stepwise selection identified predictors of false-negative SPECT, with sex forced into the model as a sensitivity analysis. Reporting followed STROBE.ResultsOf 7,312 catheterizations, 3,116 (42.6%) led to revascularization. Among 3,189 patients with SPECT data, sensitivity for predicting revascularization was 82.0% (95% CI 79.8–84.0%); 84.6% (82.5–86.6%) excluding indeterminate and unavailable results; and 82.9% (81.1–84.6%) using anatomic ≥ 70% stenosis, very similar to the revascularization-based estimate. Territory-specific sensitivity ranged from 82.2% (mid-distal LAD, n = 1,026) to 91.2% (ramus, n = 91). Among negative-SPECT patients who proceeded to catheterization, 32.8% (226/688) required intervention, a proportion specific to this selected subgroup and not a population-level false-negative rate. Family history of premature CAD was independently associated with false-negative results (OR 1.73, 95% CI 1.25–2.40), unchanged when sex was forced into the model (OR 1.73) and attenuated but still significant under an anatomic outcome definition (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.05–1.84).ConclusionsSPECT sensitivity for predicting revascularization was 82.0%, consistent with contemporary benchmarks and unchanged under an anatomic reference standard. Family history of premature CAD was independently associated with false-negative SPECT, a finding that, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported in the SPECT literature, though residual referral bias cannot be excluded. Where SPECT remains the primary cardiac imaging modality, these findings support careful clinical interpretation of negative SPECT results in patients with a strong family history of CAD.Keywords: SPECT, Myocardial perfusion imaging, False negative, Coronary artery disease, Appalachia, Cardiac catheterization, Revascularization, Diagnostic sensitivity

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the United States, with chronic heart disease prevalence of 14.2% in rural areas compared to 9.9% in urban areas [1, 2]. Rural Americans are 40% more likely to develop heart disease, with a 30% higher stroke risk and a 3-year life expectancy gap compared to urban counterparts [2]. The rural-urban cardiovascular mortality gap has widened since 2010, driven by a striking rise among younger rural adults [3]. Certain regions bear a disproportionate burden: national rates of hospitalization related to CAD are 72.4 per 1,000 Medicare beneficiaries, while the state of Ohio exceeds this at 88.4 per 1,000. Scioto County, located in the Appalachian region of southern Ohio, has among the highest rates in the state at 152.0 per 1,000, approaching double the national rate [4]. Central Appalachia has a heart disease mortality rate of 249 per 100,000, nearly 1.5 times the national average, in a region in which obesity, smoking, and physical inactivity are also more prevalent than national averages [5]. This disease burden underscores the critical importance of accurate non-invasive diagnostic testing in such populations.

The gold standard for diagnosis of coronary heart disease remains invasive coronary angiography. However, given its invasive nature, non-invasive testing modalities are employed as first-line tools for detection of myocardial ischemia. Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) myocardial perfusion imaging remains the most frequently performed of these modalities in the United States [6]. The long-cited SPECT sensitivity benchmark of 88% was established by the Jaarsma et al. 2012 meta-analysis [7] and the 2003 ACC/AHA/ASNC guidelines [8]; however, both sources explicitly noted these figures were uncorrected for verification bias. The most recent comprehensive meta-analysis by Xu et al. (2021), pooling 134 SPECT studies, reported a downward-revised sensitivity of 83% (95% CI 81–85%) [9], suggesting earlier benchmarks were inflated. Despite this, false-positive and false-negative results remain a significant clinical concern, as the Xu et al. meta-analysis reported specificity of only 77% (95% CI 74–80%) [9].

The consequences of false-negative results are clinically significant. A normal pharmacologic SPECT carries an annualized hard-event rate (cardiac death or myocardial infarction) of 1.78% [10], which would linearly extrapolate to approximately 18% over 10 years, though follow-up in the source meta-analysis was 22 months. False-negative results may lead to premature discharge of patients with significant coronary disease, placing them at substantial risk. Only one prior study has specifically examined predictors of false-negative SPECT in patients with normal imaging who subsequently underwent angiography; Nakanishi et al. (2016) identified elevated pretest probability, residual sub-threshold perfusion defects, and abnormal transient ischemic dilation as predictors, but did not include family history of CAD in their predictor model [11].

Since the period of data collection for this study, the diagnostic landscape has evolved considerably. The 2021 AHA/ACC chest pain guideline elevated coronary CTA to a Class 1 recommendation for intermediate-risk patients without known CAD and expressed preference for PET over SPECT when nuclear imaging is selected [12]. Nevertheless, 2022 Medicare billing data demonstrate that SPECT remains the most commonly performed cardiac imaging modality in the United States by a factor of six, with approximately 1.34 million examinations compared to 212,000 PET and 119,000 CCTA studies [6]. Access barriers including PET radiotracer logistics, personnel and training shortages, low-volume economic viability, and the concentration of PET capacity in urban centers explain the continued predominance of SPECT in community and rural hospital settings [13]. Understanding the strengths and limitations of SPECT in these settings therefore remains directly relevant to clinical practice.

Notably, nearly all published SPECT diagnostic accuracy data originate from academic medical centers with expert readers and optimized protocols. Data on SPECT sensitivity from community and non-academic hospital settings remain limited and under-represented in the literature.

The present investigation was prompted by a recurrent clinical pattern in our institution. We had observed cases in which patients presenting with symptoms suspicious for CAD underwent non-invasive testing with negative results yet, on the basis of persistent clinical suspicion and risk-factor profile, proceeded to catheterization and were found to have significant disease. The question we sought to answer was how often this occurred in our patient population and what patient characteristics predicted it. The purpose of this study was therefore not to assess the comprehensive diagnostic accuracy of SPECT (which our design cannot do), but rather to evaluate the sensitivity of SPECT for predicting subsequent revascularization, and to identify patient and lesion characteristics independently associated with false-negative SPECT, in a community hospital serving a population with among the highest burden of CAD in the United States.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from the American College of Cardiology National Cardiovascular Data Registry (NCDR) CathPCI registry at Southern Ohio Medical Center (SOMC), a community hospital serving Scioto County in the Appalachian region of southern Ohio. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [14]. Local institutional review board approval was obtained prior to the study.

Study population

All patients who underwent cardiac catheterization at SOMC over a five-year period from Q3 2013 to Q1 2018 were included. Patients are prospectively entered into the CathPCI registry at the time of catheterization. The CathPCI registry is a national quality improvement registry that captures detailed demographic, clinical, procedural, and outcome data for cardiac catheterization procedures performed at participating institutions.

Variables and definitions

Baseline demographics included age, sex, smoking status, height, and weight. CAD presentation type (no symptoms, symptom unlikely to be ischemic, stable angina, unstable angina, non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction [NSTEMI], or ST-elevation myocardial infarction [STEMI]) and Canadian Cardiovascular Society angina class were extracted. Comorbidities recorded included use of antianginal medication, chronic lung disease, current dialysis, diabetes, dyslipidemia, family history of premature CAD, hypertension, prior cerebrovascular disease, prior heart failure, prior myocardial infarction (MI), prior peripheral artery disease, and prior percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).

Family history of premature CAD was captured by the registry per the NCDR CathPCI v4 coder definition (field 4015) as a binary Yes/No variable, defined as a documented history of CAD in a first-degree relative diagnosed at age younger than 55 years (male) or younger than 65 years (female). The registry does not separately record degree of relationship beyond first-degree or the precise age at family-member diagnosis. The variable is captured at the time of catheterization from patient-reported history; recall bias and the absence of source-document verification are therefore potential sources of misclassification.

SPECT acquisition and interpretation

SPECT myocardial perfusion imaging was performed at SOMC throughout the study period using a standardized institutional protocol that did not change during the five-year window. Imaging was acquired on GE Discovery NM 530c cameras with cadmium-zinc-telluride (CZT) solid-state detectors. Tc-99 m sestamibi was used as the radiotracer (10 mCi rest, 30 mCi stress). A one-day rest-then-stress protocol was used in the majority of patients; a two-day protocol was reserved for patients exceeding 300 lb, those with an infiltrated intravenous access, or those who left the department before stress imaging could be completed. Studies were processed on GE Xeleris with Cedars 4DM (Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, Los Angeles, CA) and included ECG-gated images. Attenuation correction was not used. Prone imaging was added at the discretion of the technologist when body habitus allowed, as a supplemental view to mitigate diaphragmatic and breast attenuation.

Pharmacologic stress was used in patients unable to perform adequate exercise, reflecting the comorbidity burden of the cohort. The pharmacologic agent used in the large majority of cases was regadenoson (Lexiscan), with adenosine, dipyridamole, and dobutamine available per protocol but used rarely by the latter portion of the study period. Exercise stress was performed in cardiovascular testing per institutional protocol with tracer injected at greater than 85% of maximum predicted heart rate. The exact patient-level split between exercise and pharmacologic stress is not captured in the CathPCI v4 export and could not be reconstructed.

Studies were interpreted by attending radiologists or cardiologists using integrated visual analysis and quantitative perfusion software (Cedars 4DM), incorporating gated wall-motion and ejection-fraction information. Patient-level semi-quantitative summed scores (SSS, SDS, SRS) were not reliably captured in the registry export and could not be analyzed as continuous predictors.

Reference standards and outcome definitions

Whether SPECT was performed as part of the diagnostic workup was recorded in the registry and used to define the study group. SPECT results were classified as positive, negative, indeterminate, or unavailable.

The primary reference standard was subsequent revascularization at catheterization, defined as PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). A true positive was defined as a positive SPECT followed by revascularization, a false negative as a negative SPECT followed by revascularization, a true negative as a negative SPECT in a patient managed medically at catheterization, and a false positive as a positive SPECT in a patient managed medically. Because our cohort was derived from the CathPCI registry, only patients who underwent catheterization were included; patients with negative SPECT who did not proceed to catheterization were not captured, and specificity and negative predictive value cannot be meaningfully derived from these data (see Limitations).

Two secondary anatomic reference standards were also examined to evaluate how sensitive the findings were to the choice of reference standard. The first defined anatomic disease as native or graft stenosis ≥ 70% in any of the major epicardial vessels evaluated (left main, proximal LAD, mid-distal LAD, circumflex, ramus, right coronary artery, or corresponding bypass grafts). The second used a less stringent ≥ 50% threshold. These anatomic reference standards are independent of revascularization decisions and address residual concerns that conflating clinical decision-making with anatomic disease severity could systematically bias the sensitivity estimate.

For the primary analysis of SPECT classification, indeterminate (n = 59) and unavailable (n = 55) results were grouped with negative results. Two additional sensitivity analyses were also performed: (a) restricting to patients with definitively positive or negative SPECT results (excluding indeterminate and unavailable categories); (b) excluding only unavailable results while retaining indeterminate as negative.

Native coronary lesion severity was categorized by vessel territory (left main, ramus, proximal left anterior descending [LAD], mid-distal LAD, circumflex, and right coronary artery [RCA]). Critical stenosis was defined as native lesion greater than 70%. Functional invasive flow measurements (fractional flow reserve and instantaneous wave-free ratio) were not separately captured in the CathPCI v4 data export and could not be incorporated into the reference standard.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were reported as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range as appropriate. Sensitivity for predicting subsequent revascularization was calculated overall and stratified by critical lesion location. Wilson 95% confidence intervals were computed for all proportions.

A logistic regression model was used to compute univariate and multivariable odds ratios for a false-negative outcome based on available comorbidities and lesion location. Variables for the multivariable model were selected using forward stepwise selection based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). To address whether sex (a well-recognized determinant of SPECT performance through breast attenuation, smaller chamber size, and microvascular disease distribution) was inappropriately omitted by the stepwise algorithm, a sensitivity analysis was performed in which sex was forced into the final multivariable model and the family-history odds ratio compared before and after this adjustment. As a separate robustness check, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation was also performed. To address residual concern that referral bias might inflate the family-history association in the revascularization-based outcome, the multivariable model was additionally re-estimated using anatomic ≥ 70% stenosis (in any major epicardial vessel) rather than revascularization as the criterion for the false-negative outcome.

The multivariable model was fit by complete-case analysis. The analytic subgroup for the regression comprised the patients with a true-positive or false-negative SPECT result (those who underwent revascularization), and all covariates retained by the selection procedure were available for every patient in this subgroup; the model therefore included 1,257 complete observations (226 false-negative and 1,031 true-positive) with no observations excluded for missing data.

Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC, or C-statistic). Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Time intervals between admission and procedure were summarized as median and interquartile range. Time from SPECT to catheterization could not be computed because SPECT performance date is not separately captured in the CathPCI v4 export. The proportion of patients presenting with each CAD presentation category (no symptoms, stable angina, unstable angina, NSTEMI, STEMI) was tabulated and a subgroup analysis was performed to verify that sensitivity was consistent in patients without an acute coronary syndrome (defined narrowly as STEMI or NSTEMI).

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) with the glmnet, pROC, ResourceSelection, and binom packages.

Results

Study population

A total of 7,312 cardiac catheterizations were performed during the study period (Fig. 1). Overall, 3,116 (42.6%) required intervention. Baseline demographics are presented in Table 1. Patients requiring intervention were older (median age 65 vs. 63, p < 0.001), more commonly male (63.1% vs. 49.2%, p < 0.001), and more likely to present with some form of MI rather than angina (p < 0.001). Among comorbidities, patients requiring intervention had higher rates of diabetes (48.9% vs. 40.0%, p < 0.001), dyslipidemia (76.9% vs. 70.7%, p < 0.001), prior MI (26.0% vs. 19.0%, p < 0.001), and prior PCI (47.6% vs. 33.0%, p < 0.001).

Stress imaging modality

Among all catheterization patients, 3,476 (47.5%) had stress imaging performed as part of their workup. SPECT was the dominant modality, performed in 3,189 patients (~ 92% of those with stress imaging). Stress echocardiography (n = 43), cardiac CT angiography (n = 21), cardiac magnetic resonance stress imaging (n = 12), and exercise stress without imaging (n = 42) were each used in fewer than 1.3% of stress-tested patients, in some cases in addition to SPECT. Of patients with SPECT, 2,501 had positive results, 574 had negative results, 59 had indeterminate results, and 55 had unavailable results; the remaining 287 had stress imaging recorded but no SPECT result captured, comprising those who underwent one of the alternative modalities above and others for whom the specific modality or result was not recorded in the registry export. Baseline characteristics restricted to this SPECT cohort (the 3,189 patients who form the analytic population for all subsequent analyses), stratified by intervention status, are provided in Supplementary Table S1 and were broadly similar to the full catheterized cohort shown in Table 1. Time intervals and procedural urgency

Among patients with both arrival and procedure dates recorded, the median time from admission to catheterization was 0 days (interquartile range 0–2 days), reflecting the acute presentation and rapid inpatient catheterization that characterized most of the cohort. By recorded procedural status, catheterization was elective in 1,961 (56.4%) and urgent in 1,303 (37.5%) of the stress-imaging cohort, with the remainder emergent (n = 14), salvage (n = 2), or unspecified (n = 196). The predominance of elective procedural-status coding alongside short admission-to-catheterization intervals reflects that many catheterizations, although prompted by an acute presentation, were scheduled within the index admission rather than performed emergently. Symptom-onset to arrival time, when recorded, had a median of 0 days (IQR 0–1 day), consistent with the unstable-angina and acute-coronary-syndrome predominance of the cohort. SPECT performance date is not separately captured in this CathPCI export and therefore SPECT-to-catheterization interval could not be computed; SPECT was typically performed in the outpatient setting in the days to weeks preceding admission for catheterization in elective cases, and in the inpatient setting in the hours preceding catheterization in urgent cases, per institutional practice.

Sensitivity for predicting subsequent revascularization (primary reference standard)

The overall 2 × 2 classification table is presented in Table 2. Among 3,189 patients with SPECT data (including indeterminate and unavailable results grouped as negative), sensitivity for predicting subsequent revascularization was 82.0% (95% CI 79.8–84.0%). Notably, among the selected subset of patients with negative SPECT who nevertheless proceeded to catheterization, nearly one in three (32.8%, 226/688) were found to require intervention. This proportion reflects the high pretest probability driving continued workup in patients referred onward despite a negative SPECT and should not be interpreted as a population-level false-negative rate. Specificity and negative predictive value could not be meaningfully calculated from the revascularization-based reference standard due to verification bias inherent in the cohort design (see Limitations).

Sensitivity increased monotonically with anatomic disease burden. Among patients with critical stenosis in a single epicardial territory, sensitivity was 77.7% (95% CI 74.5–80.6%); in two-territory disease, 83.4% (95% CI 79.8–86.5%); in three-territory disease, 89.5% (95% CI 86.3–91.9%); and in four-territory (left main plus three-vessel) disease, 91.2% (95% CI 82.1–95.9%). This pattern is the opposite of what would be expected if balanced ischemia were the dominant mechanism of false-negative SPECT in this cohort, and instead suggests that single-territory (single-vessel) disease accounts for most missed findings.

Stenosis burden and the decision to catheterize in negative-SPECT patients

Because the cohort by design includes only patients who proceeded to catheterization, the 688 patients with a negative SPECT who were nonetheless catheterized warrant separate description. Anatomic disease burden in this group varied widely: 296 (43.0%) had ≥ 70% stenosis and 431 (62.6%) had ≥ 50% stenosis in at least one major epicardial vessel. Of the 462 negative-SPECT patients who were not revascularized (true negatives, two-thirds of the negative-SPECT group), most had no high-grade disease: 256 (55.4%) had less than 50% stenosis and only 75 (16.2%) had ≥ 70% stenosis. In these patients the negative SPECT was therefore largely concordant with the absence of revascularization-warranting anatomic disease, so a negative SPECT followed by no revascularization should be read as an appropriate outcome rather than as a missed diagnosis. Conversely, among the 226 false-negative patients, 221 (97.8%) had ≥ 70% stenosis and 225 (99.6%) had ≥ 50% stenosis, confirming that these represented genuine high-grade disease missed by SPECT rather than borderline angiographic findings. The decision to proceed to catheterization in negative-SPECT patients likely reflected the acute and symptomatic character of the broader cohort (the stress-imaging cohort comprised 86.9% broad acute coronary presentations, with a median admission-to-catheterization interval of 0 days) more than the SPECT result itself. Revascularization decisions at catheterization were operator-determined on the basis of angiographic stenosis severity and clinical context; no protocolized FFR/iFR threshold was applied, and the registry export does not capture functional flow measurements (FFR/iFR) that might have further informed these decisions.

Anatomic reference-standard sensitivity analyses (secondary)

The secondary analyses using an anatomic reference standard yielded sensitivity estimates very similar to the revascularization-based primary analysis. Using ≥ 70% stenosis in any major epicardial vessel (native or graft) as the reference, sensitivity was 82.9% (95% CI 81.1–84.6%; TP = 1,439, FN = 296). Using a less stringent ≥ 50% threshold, sensitivity was 80.8% (95% CI 79.1–82.3%). Of the 1,795 patients with anatomic ≥ 70% stenosis in the broader stress-imaging cohort (which includes patients without an interpretable SPECT result, and so exceeds the 1,735 anatomic-positive patients in the SPECT cohort used for the sensitivity estimate above), 1,275 (71.0%) underwent revascularization at catheterization and 520 (29.0%) were managed medically, suggesting that medical management of significant anatomic disease occurs in a meaningful minority but does not materially alter the sensitivity estimate (Table 4).

Robustness of SPECT result categorization

Three handling strategies for indeterminate and unavailable SPECT results yielded sensitivity estimates within a 3-percentage-point range. Restricting to patients with definitive positive or negative SPECT results (excluding indeterminate, n = 59, and unavailable, n = 55), sensitivity was 84.6% (95% CI 82.5–86.6%). Retaining indeterminate as negative but excluding unavailable yielded 83.1% (95% CI 81.0–85.1%). Counting indeterminate as positive and excluding unavailable yielded 84.9% (95% CI 82.8–86.8%). The conclusions of the analysis are robust to these handling choices.

Subgroup analysis by clinical presentation

The cohort was predominantly comprised of patients with an acute coronary presentation: strict acute coronary syndromes (STEMI or NSTEMI) accounted for 12.2% of the stress-imaging cohort, and broad acute coronary presentations (including unstable angina) accounted for 86.9%. Sensitivity for predicting revascularization was 83.9% (95% CI 78.1–88.4%) in the strict-ACS subgroup, 82.0% (95% CI 79.7–84.1%) in the broad-ACS subgroup, 81.7% (95% CI 79.2–83.9%) in the non-strict-ACS subgroup, and 82.6% (95% CI 72.0–89.8%) in the small non-broad-ACS subgroup. Sensitivity was therefore very similar across clinical presentation categories (range 81.7%–83.9%).

Predictors of false-negative SPECT

A comparison of patients with false-negative versus true-positive SPECT results is presented in Table 5. False-negative patients were more commonly female (45.6% vs. 35.6%, p = 0.006) and more commonly had a family history of CAD (34.5% vs. 24.1%, p = 0.002). False negatives were less likely in patients with critical circumflex or RCA lesions (p < 0.001) and those with prior MI (p = 0.017). Unstable angina was the predominant presentation in both groups (81.0% of false negatives vs. 78.8% of true positives), reflecting the overall population character of this high-burden cohort.

An important distinction must be drawn between the predictors in this model. The critical-lesion-location terms are post-catheterization angiographic findings: they describe which anatomic patterns of disease are more or less readily detected by SPECT (for example, circumflex and RCA lesions being more reliably detected), and they are known only after catheterization. They are therefore not pre-test variables and cannot be used to guide the initial decision to catheterize. Family history of premature CAD, by contrast, is a pre-test variable known at the time of presentation, which is what makes its independent association with false-negative SPECT potentially actionable for clinical interpretation. The lesion-location terms should accordingly be read as characterizing the anatomic correlates of SPECT detection within this cohort rather than as a clinical prediction rule.

Sex modeling sensitivity analysis

On univariate analysis, male sex was associated with a lower likelihood of false-negative SPECT (OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.49–0.88, p = 0.005), consistent with the well-described impact of breast attenuation and smaller cardiac chamber size on SPECT performance in women [15]. However, sex was not retained by forward stepwise selection in the final multivariable model. To verify that this exclusion did not bias the family-history association, we re-fit the multivariable model with sex forced in. The adjusted family-history odds ratio was essentially unchanged (OR 1.73, 95% CI 1.25–2.39, p < 0.001), and the adjusted sex coefficient was no longer significant (OR for male sex 0.87, 95% CI 0.60–1.25, p = 0.44). Height (which is strongly correlated with sex and was already retained in the stepwise model) appears to have absorbed the residual variance previously attributed to sex on univariate analysis.

Anatomic reference-standard re-analysis of family-history association

To further address concern that referral bias might inflate the family-history association under a revascularization-based outcome, we re-estimated the multivariable model with the false-negative outcome redefined as a negative SPECT in a patient with anatomic ≥ 70% stenosis (regardless of subsequent revascularization decision). The family-history odds ratio in this re-defined model was 1.39 (95% CI 1.05–1.84, p = 0.022), attenuated in magnitude relative to the primary analysis but remaining statistically significant. This pattern is consistent with the family-history association reflecting a possible biological contribution in addition to a partial referral-bias effect.

Model performance

The multivariable model demonstrated modest discrimination with an AUC of 0.678 (95% CI 0.642–0.715). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated adequate calibration (chi-squared = 7.0, df = 8, p = 0.54).

LASSO robustness check

LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was performed as a robustness check. At the most parsimonious regularization (lambda.1se), only critical circumflex stenosis and critical RCA stenosis were retained. At lambda.min, the retained variables included family history of CAD, prior MI, circumflex stenosis, RCA stenosis, left main stenosis, current dialysis, dyslipidemia, sex, height, and ramus stenosis, broadly consistent with the forward selection model. The convergence of both methods on circumflex and RCA stenosis as the strongest predictors, and the inclusion of family history at lambda.min, supports the stability of the primary model findings.

Discussion

This study evaluated the sensitivity of SPECT myocardial perfusion imaging, using subsequent revascularization as the reference standard, in a population with one of the highest burdens of CAD in the United States. In 7,312 cardiac catheterizations performed at a single community hospital in Appalachian Ohio over five years, SPECT demonstrated 82% sensitivity for predicting subsequent revascularization, and family history of CAD was independently associated with false-negative results. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to report SPECT sensitivity for predicting revascularization from a community hospital setting and among the first to identify family history of CAD as an independent predictor of false-negative SPECT.

SPECT sensitivity in context

The observed sensitivity of 82.0% (95% CI 79.8–84.0%) for predicting revascularization is numerically close to the most recent meta-analytic benchmark of 83% (95% CI 81–85%) reported by Xu et al. in their pooling of 134 SPECT studies [9]. The estimate was very similar when an anatomic reference standard (≥ 70% stenosis in any major epicardial vessel) was substituted, yielding 82.9% (95% CI 81.1–84.6%), and when the analysis was restricted to patients with definitive positive or negative SPECT results (84.6%). The similarity of these estimates across alternative definitions suggests that the primary finding is reasonably robust within this catheterization-referred cohort, while not eliminating the broader limitations imposed by verification bias. Direct comparison with meta-analytic benchmarks nonetheless requires caution: meta-analytic estimates typically use angiographic disease definitions rather than revascularization decisions, and they pool studies that differ in verification-bias correction. Our 82% sensitivity represents a meaningful downward revision from the long-cited 88% benchmark established by Jaarsma et al. [7] and the 2003 ACC/AHA/ASNC guidelines [8], both of which acknowledged that their figures were uncorrected for verification bias. Cecil et al. demonstrated the magnitude of that bias: in their series of 2,688 SPECT studies, uncorrected sensitivity was 98%, but after Begg-Greenes correction, sensitivity dropped to 82% ± 6% [16]. Our finding of 82% in a verification-biased cohort using a revascularization-based reference standard is therefore broadly consistent with contemporary evidence and may reflect the real-world sensitivity of SPECT in community practice. Because our cohort is by design comprised entirely of patients who underwent catheterization, Begg-Greenes correction cannot be directly applied at the cohort level; at the source-population level (all patients undergoing SPECT at our institution, including those who did not subsequently proceed to catheterization) the unmeasured verification rate could substantially modify the population-level sensitivity estimate and we present the cohort-level estimate accordingly.

This finding is notable given the significant comorbidity burden in our population, including high rates of diabetes (48.9% among those requiring intervention), chronic lung disease (32.8%), and hypertension (89.9%). That SPECT sensitivity is comparable in this high-risk, high-prevalence population is reassuring, though the absolute number of missed diagnoses carries greater clinical consequence when disease prevalence is high. Among the selected subset of 688 patients with negative SPECT who nevertheless proceeded to catheterization, 226 (32.8%) required intervention. This proportion pertains only to the highly selected subgroup who continued workup despite a negative SPECT and does not represent a population-level false-negative rate; it nonetheless underscores the need for clinical vigilance when interpreting negative SPECT in high-burden populations. Stress imaging preceded catheterization in 47.5% of patients at SOMC (SPECT in approximately 92% of these). Although not directly comparable, the appropriate-use analysis of Doukky et al. reported that 54.5% of SPECT studies met appropriate-use criteria [17]; that figure measures test appropriateness rather than pre-catheterization utilization, but both observations reflect the prominent and variable role of SPECT in contemporary cardiac evaluation. Both American and European guidelines recommend tailoring the choice of non-invasive test to the clinical likelihood of obstructive disease, with coronary CT angiography favoured at low-to-moderate clinical likelihood, functional imaging (including SPECT) recommended at moderate-to-high clinical likelihood, and invasive coronary angiography reserved for very-high clinical likelihood [12, 18]. In a population such as ours, where most patients fall into the moderate-to-high clinical-likelihood band by virtue of comorbidity burden and CAD prevalence, SPECT remains a guideline-supported initial choice, and the 32.8% finding underscores the importance of preserved clinical judgment when SPECT results do not align with the pre-test estimate.

Family history as a predictor of false-negative SPECT

The finding that family history of premature CAD was independently associated with false-negative SPECT results (OR 1.73, 95% CI 1.25–2.40) represents the most clinically noteworthy finding of this study. To our knowledge, this association has not been previously reported in the SPECT literature. Nakanishi et al. examined predictors of high-risk CAD in patients with normal SPECT but did not include family history in their predictor model [11]. We propose three biologically plausible mechanisms that may, individually or in combination, contribute to this observation.

First, family history is associated with more extensive multivessel disease. Hindieh et al. demonstrated that among patients with premature acute coronary syndromes, those with family history had significantly more multivessel disease (49.7% vs. 37.9%, p < 0.01) and were more likely to have three-vessel disease (OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.29–3.95) [19]. Because SPECT relies on relative perfusion differences between myocardial territories, balanced multivessel disease can theoretically produce globally reduced but homogeneous perfusion that appears “normal,” a phenomenon illustrated by Aarnoudse et al. in a case report using invasive coronary pressure measurement [20]. However, the observation in our cohort that SPECT sensitivity increased rather than decreased with the number of diseased territories (from 77.7% in single-territory disease to 89.5–91.2% in three- and four-territory disease) argues against balanced ischemia being the dominant mechanism in this population; most false-negative results in our cohort appear to arise from single-territory (single-vessel) disease rather than from globally balanced multivessel ischemia.

Second, family history is associated with a diffuse, predominantly noncalcified plaque phenotype. In the GeneSTAR study, Kral et al. found that 45.5% of asymptomatic individuals from families with early-onset CAD had coronary plaque on CTA, with the majority being noncalcified and distributed across multiple territories, with noncalcified plaque accounting for more than 75% of total plaque in men and more than 80% in women under age 55 [21]. Sunman et al. similarly found that family history independently predicted noncalcified plaque (OR 3.32, 95% CI 1.74–6.34) [22]. This diffuse plaque pattern may impair coronary flow reserve without producing focal high-grade stenosis, reducing the likelihood of a regional perfusion defect on SPECT.

Third, family history has been associated with coronary microvascular dysfunction. Schächinger et al. demonstrated that family history independently predicted impaired endothelium-dependent coronary blood flow regulation (p = 0.008 on multivariate analysis), even in patients with angiographically normal or minimally diseased vessels [23]. Although microvascular dysfunction itself would not be expected to produce angiographic lesions sufficient to drive revascularization at catheterization, it could coexist with epicardial disease and contribute to the homogeneous perfusion patterns that obscure focal regional defects on SPECT. We present this mechanism cautiously and as a hypothesis-generating consideration rather than a demonstrated cause in our cohort.

Finally, we must acknowledge that a portion of the family-history association may reflect referral bias: clinicians at our institution may have applied a lower threshold to proceed to catheterization in SPECT-negative patients with a strong family history, contributing to a higher false-negative count in this subgroup independent of any biological mechanism. To partially address this, we re-estimated the multivariable model with an anatomic ≥ 70% stenosis outcome that is independent of revascularization decisions; the family-history odds ratio in that sensitivity analysis (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.05–1.84, p = 0.022) was attenuated relative to the primary estimate but remained statistically significant, consistent with the association reflecting a possible biological contribution in addition to referral bias. The CathPCI v4 registry captures family history of premature CAD as a single binary variable and does not record the precise degree of relationship or age at family-member diagnosis, limiting the mechanistic specificity of our interpretation; future work should incorporate granular family-history characterization.

Notwithstanding these caveats, family history remained significant in our model after controlling for sex, lesion location, prior myocardial infarction, and other comorbidities, suggesting it captures a risk dimension not accounted for by traditional clinical variables. Pending prospective validation in independent cohorts, this finding should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and may justify heightened clinical caution when SPECT results are negative in patients with a strong family history of premature CAD.

Sensitivity by coronary territory

Sensitivity for predicting subsequent revascularization varied by the location of the critical coronary lesion. Sensitivity was highest for left main and ramus lesions (~ 91%) and lowest for mid-distal LAD lesions (82.2%, 95% CI 79.7–84.4%). In the multivariable model, critical circumflex (OR 0.60) and RCA (OR 0.54) lesions were protective against false-negative results, suggesting that perfusion defects in these territories are more readily detected by SPECT. This is consistent with the typical perfusion distribution patterns of these territories and has been reported in prior studies of SPECT diagnostic performance [9].

The lower sensitivity in mid-distal LAD lesions may reflect the smaller territory at risk or the challenge of distinguishing apical thinning from true perfusion defects in this region. These findings reinforce the importance of correlating SPECT results with the clinical probability of disease in specific coronary territories.

Technical advances in SPECT and implications for false-negative reduction

Several recent technical advances may directly mitigate the false-negative pattern observed in our cohort. Dynamic SPECT imaging with absolute myocardial blood flow quantitation adds information beyond relative-perfusion analysis, with the potential to capture both microvascular dysfunction and balanced multivessel disease that conventional SPECT can miss [24]. Cadmium-zinc-telluride (CZT) detectors offer higher count sensitivity and faster acquisition than conventional dual-detector systems, enabling these dynamic flow protocols [24]. Routine attenuation correction (CT-based or transmission-source) reduces breast and diaphragmatic artifacts that disproportionately affect women and patients with high BMI. The absence of routine attenuation correction in our institutional protocol during the study period is consistent with this mechanism, and could potentially contribute to the false-negative pattern observed in our cohort. Artificial intelligence-assisted interpretation has achieved AUC of 0.83 compared with 0.71 for expert readers in multicenter validation [25]. Together, these advances suggest that a substantial fraction of our observed false-negative cases (particularly those attributable to balanced multivessel ischemia, attenuation artifact, or operator-dependent interpretation) may be tractable with contemporary SPECT technology, even before patients are routed to alternative modalities such as PET or coronary CT angiography.

Evolving diagnostic landscape and changing SPECT utilization

The data for this study were collected between 2013 and 2018, prior to the publication of the 2021 AHA/ACC chest pain guideline that elevated coronary CT angiography (CCTA) to a Class 1 recommendation for intermediate-risk patients without known CAD and expressed preference for PET over SPECT when nuclear imaging is selected [12]. The DISCHARGE trial (2022) demonstrated similar major adverse cardiovascular event rates with a CT-first strategy in stable chest pain and fewer procedural complications [26]. CT-derived fractional flow reserve has shown overall per-vessel diagnostic accuracy of 82.2% in meta-analysis (43 studies, 7,291 vessels) [27].

National SPECT utilization is anecdotally reported to have declined in recent years, with academic and large-volume centers shifting toward CCTA and PET. Whether the false-negative profile we describe contributes to this trend cannot be answered from our data; any such decline is more likely driven by guideline updates, technology adoption, and reimbursement than by accumulated clinician dissatisfaction with diagnostic performance. Nevertheless, 2022 Medicare data confirm that SPECT remains the dominant cardiac imaging modality nationally, with volumes exceeding CCTA by a factor of eleven and PET by a factor of six [6]. PET radiotracer logistics, personnel and training shortages, low-volume economic viability, and concentration of PET capacity in urban centers present significant barriers to adoption of advanced modalities in community and rural settings [13]. Our findings therefore remain directly relevant to the majority of clinical settings where SPECT continues to serve as the primary non-invasive cardiac imaging tool. AI-enhanced SPECT interpretation may offer a pathway to improve disease detection at community hospitals without requiring capital investment in new imaging equipment.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results.

First, this study is subject to verification bias (also termed workup bias), a well-described limitation of diagnostic accuracy studies in cardiac imaging [16, 28, 29]. Because our cohort was derived from the CathPCI registry, only patients who proceeded to cardiac catheterization were included. Patients with negative SPECT who were managed conservatively and never underwent catheterization are not captured. Miller et al. demonstrated the magnitude of this bias at Mayo Clinic, where only 13% of SPECT patients underwent angiography within 3 months, and apparent sensitivity of 98% fell to approximately 67% after adjustment [30]. Our design precludes calculation of specificity, negative predictive value, or the true false-negative rate in the broader SPECT population. Paradoxically, however, the unusually high catheterization referral rate in this high-burden population may attenuate the magnitude of verification bias compared to lower-risk cohorts.

Second, indeterminate and unavailable SPECT results (n = 114) were grouped with negative results in the primary analysis. While our sensitivity analysis excluding these patients showed improved sensitivity (84.6% vs. 82.0%), this methodological choice may have modestly overestimated the false-negative proportion.

Third, the multivariable model was constructed using forward stepwise selection based on AIC, a method known to be potentially unstable. However, LASSO regression as a robustness check produced broadly concordant results, with the core predictors (circumflex stenosis, RCA stenosis) retained even at the most parsimonious regularization and family history retained at lambda.min.

Fourth, the model demonstrated modest discrimination (AUC 0.678), indicating that the identified predictors explain only a portion of the variance in false-negative outcomes. This likely reflects the multifactorial nature of SPECT interpretation and the influence of unmeasured patient-level variables such as the specific stress modality applied to each patient, body habitus, breast and diaphragmatic attenuation, and interpreter experience.

Fifth, the positive catheterization outcome was primarily defined as requiring revascularization (PCI or CABG) rather than by angiographic stenosis severity alone. This revascularization-based reference standard conflates clinical decision-making with anatomic disease severity and may not capture all patients with significant coronary disease who were managed medically. In our cohort, 520 of 1,795 patients (29.0%) with anatomic ≥ 70% stenosis in the stress-imaging subset were managed medically rather than revascularized; this is the magnitude of the discrepancy between the two reference standards. We addressed this concern by performing secondary analyses using anatomic ≥ 70% and ≥ 50% stenosis as alternative reference standards; both yielded sensitivity estimates (82.9% and 80.8%, respectively) very similar to the revascularization-based primary analysis (82.0%). Functional invasive flow assessment (FFR/iFR) was not separately captured in the CathPCI v4 export available to us and could not be incorporated. Comparisons between our sensitivity estimate and meta-analytic benchmarks derived from angiographic disease definitions should therefore be interpreted with the reference-standard distinction in mind, but the similarity of revascularization-based and anatomic-based estimates suggests the bias introduced by our primary definition is modest in this cohort.

Sixth, family history of premature CAD was captured as a single binary variable per the NCDR CathPCI v4 coder definition (first-degree relative diagnosed at age < 55 years for men or < 65 years for women). The registry does not record degree of relationship within the first-degree band, exact age at family-member diagnosis, or whether more than one family member was affected. The variable is captured from patient-reported history and is therefore subject to recall bias and the absence of source-document verification. Mechanistic interpretations that depend on the precise pattern of family history (for example, dominantly inherited versus polygenic forms of premature CAD) cannot be supported by this dataset and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Seventh, the CathPCI v4 export records the SPECT result only as a categorical interpretation (positive, negative, indeterminate, or unavailable) and does not capture patient-level technical fields or quantitative perfusion data, including semi-quantitative summed scores (SSS, SDS, SRS), the grade or extent of the perfusion abnormality, the specific stress modality applied to each patient, supine-only versus supine-plus-prone acquisition, or attenuation-correction status. This registry-imposed dichotomy has two consequences for interpretation. First, we could not examine grade-dependent performance: a mildly abnormal positive study, for which revascularization is often not indicated, is counted identically to a high-grade defect. For this reason, and because the cohort contains only catheterized patients, we make no claim regarding specificity, positive predictive value, or false-positive behavior, none of which can be validly estimated from these data. Second, because the patient-level technical and quantitative variables that would distinguish the candidate mechanisms are unavailable, any mechanistic explanation for individual false-negative results (balanced multivessel ischemia, attenuation artifact, or microvascular dysfunction) remains necessarily speculative and hypothesis-generating. The absence of routine attenuation correction in our institutional protocol may contribute to the false-negative pattern observed and is consistent with the well-described sex- and habitus-related performance limitations of non-attenuation-corrected SPECT, but this cannot be confirmed at the patient level in the present data.

Eighth, this is a single-center, retrospective study, and findings may not generalize to other populations or practice settings. However, the large sample size and the unique characteristics of this high-burden population provide data from a clinical context that is systematically underrepresented in the diagnostic accuracy literature. In particular, because the cohort was predominantly acute in presentation and catheterized with minimal delay (median admission-to-catheterization interval of 0 days, with broad acute coronary presentations in 86.9% of the stress-imaging subset), these results may not generalize to stable outpatient populations undergoing SPECT as a first-line test for chronic chest pain. In such lower-prevalence, less-acute settings the pre-test probability of disease, and the proportion of negative-SPECT patients who proceed to catheterization, would both differ substantially; the sensitivity and false-negative proportions reported here should therefore not be extrapolated to that context.

Finally, the data were collected between 2013 and 2018. Practice patterns, SPECT technology (notably the diffusion of CZT cameras and dynamic flow quantitation), and clinical guidelines have evolved since the study period [12]. These findings should be interpreted as reflecting SPECT sensitivity during this historical period, though the continued dominance of SPECT in community practice [6] supports their ongoing relevance.

Conclusions

In a large retrospective cohort from one of the highest-burden CAD populations in the United States, SPECT myocardial perfusion imaging demonstrated 82.0% (95% CI 79.8–84.0%) sensitivity for predicting subsequent revascularization. The estimate was very similar when an anatomic ≥ 70% reference standard was substituted (82.9%, 95% CI 81.1–84.6%), supporting the robustness of the primary analysis to reference-standard choice. Family history of premature CAD was independently associated with false-negative results, a finding that, to our knowledge, has not previously been reported in the SPECT literature; the association remained present though attenuated (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.05–1.84) when an anatomic outcome definition was substituted, consistent with a possible biological contribution in addition to referral bias. Sensitivity varied by coronary territory, with lowest sensitivity observed in mid-distal LAD lesions, and increased monotonically with anatomic disease burden, arguing against balanced multivessel ischemia as the dominant mechanism of missed disease in this cohort. In high-prevalence populations where SPECT remains the primary advanced cardiac imaging modality, these findings support careful clinical interpretation of negative SPECT results in patients with a strong family history of premature CAD. Prospective validation in independent cohorts is needed before firm clinical recommendations can be made.
