Invasive electrical nerve stimulation for post-stroke motor rehabilitation
Authors: Xianze Li, Tao Xue, Zixiao Li, Jianguo Zhang, Ting Gao
Published: 2024

To the editor: Stroke is the foremost cause of death and disability worldwide.[1] A stroke event can cause severe motor sequelae, including motor dyscoordination, hemiparesis, and hemiplegia. Although rehabilitation training is an essential part of post-stroke treatment, rehabilitation training alone may not significantly improve motor function in patients with chronic stroke. Recently, numerous preclinical studies and animal experiments have preliminarily validated the safety and efficacy of electrical nerve stimulation technology for chronic post-stroke motor rehabilitation, which awaits large-scale clinical application. Motor cortex stimulation (MCS), vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), deep brain stimulation (DBS), and spinal cord stimulation (SCS) are invasive electrical neurostimulation techniques for patients with chronic stroke [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C135]. We reviewed the progress of research into the mechanism, efficacy, targets, and loops of these techniques.

MCS in post-stroke rehabilitation: MCS, a neuromodulation technique involving the implantation of epidural electrodes into the ipsilesional motor cortex, has received significant research attention owing to its applicability to the recovery of motor function after stroke. The underlying assumption is that subthreshold stimulation could facilitate the reorganization of residual cortical functions and improve residual motor function outcomes. Through a preliminary study involving 10 chronic stroke patients, Brown et al provided initial evidence that MCS was safe and effective for treating post-stroke upper limb motor impairments. Huang et al, who studied 24 patients, further showed that MCS, in combination with rehabilitation therapy, effectively enhanced upper limb control and improved Fugl–Meyer Assessment Upper Extremity Scale (FMA-UE) scores in post-stroke patients. While animal studies and phases I and II trials were initially very successful, a subsequent large multicenter controlled trial did not demonstrate any significant benefits of this therapy with respect to motor function or quality of life.[2] The integrity of the descending pathway is crucial to the prediction of MCS efficacy. Patients with spared descending pathways, which are demonstrated by inducible movements via preoperative transcranial magnetic stimulation or intraoperative cortical stimulation, showed greater improvements than those receiving rehabilitation alone in phase III trials.

VNS in post-stroke rehabilitation: VNS, a neuromodulation technique approved for refractory focal epilepsies, stimulates the vagus nerve through electrodes attached to its middle cervical trunk, which in turn is connected to a pulse generator, under the clavicle or in the anterior axillary folds. By extending to the nucleus tractus solitarius and nucleus basalis, the afferent fibers of the vagus nerve activate noradrenergic and cholinergic systems, thus playing a vital role in motor functions. Improvements in motor function are the results of modulatory feedback in neural circuits that shape future responses based on previous neural activity. Pairing brief VNS pulses with sensory or motor experiences can strengthen specific neural circuits through training, thereby responding to significant activation of adrenergic and cholinergic neurons. Cortical plasticity, which is characteristic of pairing training, is enhanced by this process. Ultimately, input-specific functional reorganization of cortical neurons is enabled. Given a pulse-time-dependent plasticity time-learning window, VNS-mediated inter-synaptic neuromodulation may promote long-term synaptic changes in motor neurons, indicating a mechanism for integrating VNS therapy with rehabilitation. In a previous study, the VNS–exercise combination, in contrast to exercise alone, significantly improved forelimb motor recovery in stroke rats and increased motor cortex synaptic connectivity by 200%. Notably, VNS, combined with upper limb training, improved upper limb motor function but not lower limb function, signifying that VNS induces specific plasticity in the corticospinal network, which is central to VNS treatment success.

The application potential of VNS for chronic post-stroke motor rehabilitation was first demonstrated by Dawson et al[3], who discovered that VNS with rehabilitation significantly improved FMA-UE scores in stroke patients compared with rehabilitation alone. In a subsequent study, a total of 106 ischemic stroke patients treated with VNS showed a mean FMA-UE score increase of 5.0 points, compared with 2.4 points in the control group. The VNS parameters—0.8 mA, 100 μs, 30 Hz, and 0.5 s—optimize neuroplasticity, thus avoiding neurotransmitter supersaturation, with the intensity reduced to a tolerable level if 0.8 mA is uncomfortable for patients. By avoiding neurotransmitter supersaturation and receptor desensitization, this moderate intensity ensures optimal neuroplasticity and patient comfort. A meta-analysis involving 274 patients in seven studies revealed that VNS significantly improves upper extremity motor function after a stroke event. Despite its approval for post-stroke upper extremity motor impairments, VNS has potential for use in treating lower extremity motor deficits.

DBS in post-stroke rehabilitation: DBS, which is a therapeutic technique involving stereotactic implantation of electrodes into the brain, enables long-term electrical stimulation of selected brain targets. DBS delivers an electrical stimulation that is both reversible and programmable, with the ability to adjust parameters.

Although motor function impairment is the most common post-stroke sequela, there is limited research on DBS in post-stroke sequela cases. Crossed cerebellar diaschisis, a phenomenon that inspired the development of DBS in the dentate nucleus (DN) of the cerebellum, is characterized by a decrease in cerebellar function on the contralateral side of the brain due to damage to the cerebral cortex as a result of stroke or other causes, including decreases in excitability, blood flow, metabolism, and other biomarkers. This pathway is mediated by the corticopontine nuclei–cerebellar cortex pathway. Concurrently, damage to the cerebellum can lead to hypofunction of the contralateral cerebral cortex, which is mediated by the DN-thalamocortical (DTC) pathway. The reciprocal pathway between the cortex and the cerebellum, highlighting a significant connection, signifies that DN-DBS provides a promising direction for post-stroke motor rehabilitation research.

Baker et al[4] showed that, in rats with severe ischemic strokes, 20 Hz electrical stimulation of the lateral cerebellar nucleus (LCN), a homolog of the human DN, improved locomotor skills. By increasing pericortical excitability, β-band stimulation of DTC loops showed potential for post-stroke recovery. Moreover, Zhang et al revealed that optogenetic activation of the dentate area promoted post-stroke brain recovery. Shah et al showed that repeated optogenetic stimulations of the LCN resulted in significant and lasting motor function improvements. In their studies, Baker et al[4] indicated that DN stimulation, which required minimal electricity, also involved all pathway areas, whereas thalamus stimulation, which required multiple electrodes, could disrupt rhythms. The cerebellar–cortical pathway is highly correlated with post-stroke motor function recovery. Other mechanisms may include increased synaptogenesis, dendritic arborization, glutamatergic neurogenesis, and decreased GABAergic neurogenesis.

In an open-label, non-randomized phase I trial, 12 patients with moderate-to-severe upper extremity impairment received DN-DBS and physical rehabilitation. This led to a median improvement of 7 points on the FMA-UE assessment, with significant gains in distal limb motor function averaging 15 points. These functional increases were linked to cortical reorganization, confirmed by increased ipsilesional cortex metabolism.[4] Although no serious adverse events related to the DBS device were reported in this trial, there were 21 treatment-related adverse events, highlighting the need for careful monitoring and management of potential risks.

SCS in post-stroke rehabilitation: The exploration of SCS in chronic pain treatment also highlights its therapeutic benefits for patients with motor function impairments due to a spinal cord injury or stroke. In lower limb paralysis, it partially restores voluntary motor ability by promoting neuroplasticity, upregulating neurotrophic factors, modulating neuroinflammatory responses, and reducing secondary injuries.

The rehabilitation of patients with motor impairments after chronic stroke is similar to that of patients with a spinal cord injury. The injury is generally considered to occur in the corticospinal tract (CST). The interruption of these descending pathways abolishes the sources of modulation and excitation required to enable functional states of spinal circuits. As the majority of CST injuries are incomplete, enhancing the residual CST is crucial to restoring voluntary motor control. The mechanism of SCS consists of modulating the excitability of relatively intact injured spinal circuits to increase the responsiveness of the remaining CST neurons. SCS acts on the primary sensory afferent neurons in the dorsal roots of the spinal cord, including large-diameter myelinated fibers related to proprioceptive and cutaneous feedback circuits, and these nerve fibers form synaptic connections with motor neurons via spinal interneurons. To modulate the excitability of spinal sensory-motor circuits and restore the spinal cord’s ability to control movement, it constitutes the motor circuitry controlling the muscles. Therefore, SCS enables sensory information to act as a source of motor control.

Powell et al[5], who implanted subdural electrodes into the C3-T1 nerve roots of two chronic stroke patients with impaired motor function, noted immediate and significant improvements in upper extremity strength. After continuous stimulation for 29 days, both patients showed considerable improvements in strength, dexterity, and functional movements. These improvements, persisting even after the cessation of stimulation, provide a promising basis for incorporating SCS into post-stroke rehabilitation strategies.

Current issues/future directions: Current experimental designs for stroke rehabilitation are limited in many respects. MCS exhibited no significant difference in primary outcomes due to variability in stroke lesions and CST damage, implying that future studies should better quantify CST integrity. Consistent with effective rehabilitation studies, VNS showed modest therapeutic effects with a 5-point FMA-UE improvement. DBS displayed higher therapeutic effects but lacked a blind design, and this limited the ability of DBS to be superior to rehabilitation alone. Furthermore, DBS showed a lack of reliable clinical references for parameter adjustments. SCS studies involve a small number of participants and are of short durations, hindering definitive conclusions.

Future research should investigate, preferably through imaging analysis, the relationship between CST damage and treatment effectiveness, and select suitable candidates for MCS surgery. To ensure a better comparison with rehabilitation alone, phase II DN-DBS trials should be conducted using a crossover design with a blind setup. Larger-scale VNS studies and extensive research are imperative to verify the safety and efficacy of DBS and SCS.

In conclusion, MCS, VNS, DBS, and SCS have all demonstrated potentials for application to post-stroke motor function rehabilitation. In summary, the various invasive electrical stimulation techniques have preliminarily demonstrated their efficacy and safety; however, further research is necessary to determine the optimal stimulation targets and patient selection for the best outcomes.
