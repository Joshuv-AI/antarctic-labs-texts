Identification and validation of cell senescence genes in recurrent spontaneous abortion via multiple bioinformatics algorithms
Authors: Yiyun Wei, Zhuolin Zhou, Changqiang Wei, Jinlian Cheng, Shanshan Lin, Zhiwei Zhu, Lihong Pang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Recurrent spontaneous abortion (RSA) represents a significant challenge in reproductive obstetrics, affecting approximately 5% of couples globally. Despite various treatments, the effectiveness of these interventions remains highly contentious. Emerging evidence suggests that cellular senescence plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases, potentially implicating its involvement in RSA as well. This study integrated two RNA sequencing datasets and employed Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) alongside five machine learning algorithms (XGBoost, Boruta, LASSO, SVM-RFE, and Random Forest) to identify cellular senescence genes linked to RSA. Gene expression was validated at both the transcriptome and protein levels using qPCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence techniques. An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) model was implemented to evaluate their diagnostic value. Additionally, functional enrichment and single-cell RNA sequencing analyses were conducted to investigate the biological functions of these genes. Three cellular senescence genes—TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B—were identified and found to be upregulated in RSA patients. Functional enrichment analysis revealed these genes’ involvement in the MAPK signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction, and cell-cell communication. Single-cell RNA sequencing demonstrated the distribution of these genes across various cell types, underscoring their significance in RSA. Furthermore, drug sensitivity analysis identified potential small molecule therapeutics for RSA. Cellular senescence may play a central role in the pathology of RSA, with TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B emerging as potential diagnostic biomarkers. This research enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying RSA and lays the groundwork for new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Nonetheless, further experimental studies are required to elucidate the specific roles and mechanisms of these genes in RSA, with the ultimate goal of achieving precise prevention and personalized treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19251-9.

Introduction

Recurrent spontaneous abortion is defined as the occurrence of two or more consecutive spontaneous pregnancy losses, typically before the 24th week of gestation1. Despite advancements in medical technology that have brought hope to many couples, RSA remains a complex and not fully understood issue, causing significant psychological and physiological distress for both partners. The causes of RSA are varied, with chromosomal abnormalities recognized as a primary contributing factor. In addition, uterine anatomical abnormalities, such as a uterine septum or fibroids, are closely associated with RSA2. Moreover, disruption of normal immune tolerance mechanisms—leading to an aggressive maternal immune response against the fetus, can also result in recurrent pregnancy loss3. Environmental factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, and infections, along with endocrine disorders, particularly luteal phase insufficiency and polycystic ovary syndrome, are also widely recognized as important causes of RSA4,5. However, in many cases, the underlying cause remains unexplained, highlighting the need for further investigation into novel pathogenic mechanisms.

The placenta, particularly the villous trophoblast tissue, plays a central role in early pregnancy.The villi invade maternal uterine blood vessels to establish a nutrient exchange system between the mother and fetus6. These villous structures are responsible for secreting various hormones and immunomodulatory factors essential for maintaining pregnancy and supporting healthy fetal development7. Impaired villous function is increasingly implicated in RSA, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying villous dysfunction remain poorly understood.

Cellular senescence-a state of irreversible growth arrest triggered by cellular stress or damage-is a crucial regulator of tissue homeostasis and disease progression8. Key genes involved in cellular senescence, including p53, p21, Rb, and Sp1, regulate the cell cycle, DNA repair, and responses to cellular stress. Increasing evidence suggests that cellular senescence plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of various diseases9,10. Emerging evidence suggests that premature senescence in placental or villous trophoblast cells may impair their proliferative and invasive functions, thereby contributing to pregnancy complications including RSA11–14. In particular, the senescence of villous cells has been increasingly recognized as a crucial factor in RSA12, with genes associated with cellular senescence being linked to the progression of RSA13,14. For instance, the overexpression of p53 has been shown to reduce the proliferation, migration, and invasion of human trophoblast cells (HTR-8/SVneo) while increasing apoptosis, thereby promoting RSA13. Nevertheless, research investigating the specific relationship between cellular senescence and RSA remains limited.

Despite these findings, the relationship between cellular senescence and RSA remains underexplored, and the specific senescence-related genes involved in RSA pathogenesis have not been comprehensively identified. By integrating multi-omics data and applying complex algorithms, researchers can more precisely identify disease-related molecular markers, thereby providing a solid foundation for early diagnosis and personalized treatment15,16. Machine learning, with its superior pattern recognition and data processing capabilities, is particularly advantageous for screening characteristic genes in disease expression profiles. These algorithms are capable of managing high-dimensional datasets, a critical requirement for analyzing gene expression profiles that contain extensive genetic information17–19. In this study, we aim to explore the role of cellular senescence in RSA by analyzing transcriptomic data from villous tissues using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and multiple machine learning models. Our goal is to identify senescence-associated genes involved in RSA and provide potential targets for early diagnosis and personalized therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Data collection and integration

The GSE12195020 dataset was sourced from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi) and is based on the GPL11154 platform. This dataset includes villous tissue samples from three patients with RSA and three control patients who underwent induced abortions. Additionally, we utilized sequencing data from our research group (GSE237734)21, which contains villous samples from five RSA patients and five patients with induced abortion. To ensure consistency across datasets, we performed batch correction and integrated the two datasets using the “SVA” package in R software (Version 4.4.1, URL: https://cloud.r-project.org/) for further analysis. The GSE214607 dataset includes meconium and chorionic villus tissue from five normal pregnancies and three RSA cases22. For this study, we specifically analyzed eight chorionic villus samples. Furthermore, we obtained 503 Cellular Senescence Genes (CSGs) from the CSGene database (https://bioinfo-minzhao.org/csgene/index.html) (Supplementary Table 1). Clinical sample collection

To validate the RNA sequencing data, villous tissue samples were collected from 20 RSA patients and 20 patients who elected to terminate their pregnancies for non-medical reasons at the gynecological outpatient surgery room of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University. The diagnostic criteria for RSA were defined as the occurrence of two or more consecutive spontaneous abortions with the same partner. This study complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (No: 2023-K057-01). Eligible women were those with confirmed pregnancies via ultrasound and a gestational age of ≤ 12 weeks. Exclusion criteria included women with signs of infection, uterine abnormalities, endocrine disorders, systemic diseases, villous karyotype abnormalities, or unhealthy lifestyles. All eligible patients provided written informed consent before surgery. The study design is depicted in Fig. 1, and the baseline characteristics of the two patient groups are summarized in Table 1.

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis

We analyzed the differential expression of mRNA using the “limma” package in R software23. The thresholds for identifying differentially expressed mRNAs (DEGs) were set at p < 0.05 and |logFC| ≥ 0.3. To explore the potential regulatory mechanisms of cellular senescence-related DEGs (CS-DEGs) in RSA, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses for both upregulated and downregulated genes using the “ClusterProfiler” package24. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed, referencing the gene set “c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt” from the MSigDB database25. An adjusted p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

WGCNA and hub module identification

We conducted Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) using the “WGCNA” package in R software26 to construct a scale-free co-expression network associated with clinical traits. All genes from the merged datasets were used as the input dataset, and outliers were removed using the “goodSamplesGenes” function. A soft threshold (softPower) of 13 was chosen to construct a weighted adjacency matrix, which was then transformed into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM). The dissimilarity of the TOM matrix (dissTOM) was calculated using the dynamic tree cut method with a minimum module size of 100, clustering genes into different modules and calculating module eigengenes (ME). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to associate clinical information with the modules, identifying the modules most relevant to RSA.

Screening cellular senescence genes in RSA using machine learning

To accurately identify hub genes associated with RSA, we employed five machine learning algorithms: XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting), Boruta algorithm, LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator), SVM-RFE (Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination), and Random Forest, to screen cellular senescence-related feature genes in RSA. Each algorithm has unique strengths, and their combined use enhances the accuracy and efficiency of gene screening.

XGBoost, implemented using the “xgboost” package, is an efficient gradient boosting decision tree algorithm that enables the identification of genes with a significant impact on prediction results. The objective function was set to “multi: softprob” to provide probability outputs for multiclass classification, and the number of classes (num_class) was determined based on the actual sample groups. The evaluation metric was set to “mlogloss,” which is suitable for assessing multiclass model performance. The number of boosting rounds (nrounds) was set to 1000 after multiple comparisons to balance sufficient training and the prevention of overfitting. Boruta Algorithm, implemented using the “Boruta” package, is a feature selection method based on Random Forest that enables the identification of genes significantly influencing classification results. During feature selection, Boruta iteratively eliminates original features that perform worse than randomly generated shadow features, which helps the algorithm effectively manage high-dimensional data and reduce noise or redundancy. The maximum number of iterations (maxRuns) was set to 1000 to ensure stable and reliable feature selection27. LASSO regression analysis was performed using the “glmnet” package, which applies an L1 regularization term to achieve feature selection and a sparse model structure. The parameter alpha was set to 1, and 10-fold cross-validation (nfolds = 10) was used to evaluate model performance and determine the optimal regularization parameter, with the measurement metric set to deviance (type.measure = ‘deviance’)28. SVM-RFE was performed using the “caret” package as a recursive feature elimination algorithm based on support vector machines. The analysis applied a radial kernel (“svmRadial”) and selected feature subset sizes ranging from 2 to 40. Model performance and optimal feature subset selection were evaluated using cross-validation as specified in the rfeControl settings (method = “cv”)29. Random Forest analysis was performed using the “randomForest” package, which constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates their results for classification. The number of trees (ntree) was set to 2500 to ensure model stability and reliable estimation of feature importance. Genes were ranked based on the MeanDecreaseGini score30.

Validation of diagnostic value of hub genes

First, the diagnostic performance of the hub genes was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves. Subsequently, artificial neural networks (ANNs) were constructed using the “neuralnet” and “neuralnettools” packages31 to further explore their diagnostic potential. ANNs, which simulate neural connections to process information, represent a robust method for evaluating diagnostic utility.

The analysis began with the standardization the gene expression data. Then, a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model was designed, consisting of an input layer, several hidden layers, and an output layer. The model was trained using the backpropagation algorithm, with optimal hyperparameters selected through cross-validation. Following training, model weights and activation values were analyzed to compute the importance score of each gene, reflecting its diagnostic relevance. Finally, the diagnostic performance of the ANN model was evaluated using ROC curves. This approach leverages the advanced learning capabilities of ANNs, enabling a more precise evaluation of the diagnostic significance of hub genes in disease contexts32.

Single-cell RNA analysis

To further explore the intercellular distribution of hub genes, we analyzed the single-cell GSE214607 data using the " Seurat” package in R software33. First, data from multiple decidual samples were read in batches and combined into a Seurat object.

Filtering criteria were applied to retain genes expressed in at least three cells and with at least 250 gene expressions. Cells containing over 25% mitochondrial genes or more than 3% ribosomal genes were excluded to ensure data quality. The sample data were subsequently merged, and batch effects were mitigated using the “Harmony” package. The data were normalized with the LogNormalize method, and the top 2000 most variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA), where the top 20 principal components were selected for further analysis. Additional dimensionality reduction and visualization were conducted using the UMAP technique. Automatic cell type annotation was performed with the “SingleR” package34. Finally, the results of the analysis were visualized using “ggplot2” and other complementary tools to ensure comprehensive and clear data representation.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from villous tissue using Trizol (Takara, Japan), and cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR reactions were conducted with the SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). β-actin served as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Each experiment included at least three technical replicates to ensure accuracy. Western blotting (WB)

Villous tissue was lysed with RIPA buffer (Solarbio, China) and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method, and proteins were denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein samples were loaded onto an SDS-PAGE gel and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked in a 5% non-fat milk solution at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (GAPDH, Servicebio, China, 1/2000; SRSF3, Zenbio, China, 1/700; TBX2, Proteintech, 1/500; TNRC6B, Aviva, 1/500). After incubation with primary antibody, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Some preliminary experiments were conducted using a laser scanner (Thermo Fisher, USA). Target proteins were visualized using the LI-COR Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, USA), which was primarily used for image acquisition. Band densitometry analysis was performed using ImageJ software (Version 1.54f, Java 1.8.0_202 [64-bit], URL: https://imagej.net/ij/).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Villous samples were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 3 μm-thick slices. The sections were deparaffinized using xylene, rehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and subjected to high-temperature antigen retrieval in a sodium citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with a peroxidase inhibitor, and the sections were covered with 3% BSA solution for 1 h to block non-specific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (SRSF3, Zenbio, China, 1/100; TBX2, Proteintech, 1/100; TNRC6B, Aviva, 1/100). Following primary antibody incubation, the sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies. Finally, DAPI solution (Solarbio, China) was applied to the samples for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescent imaging was performed using a confocal microscope to visualize and analyze the stained sections.

Drug sensitivity analysis

The Connectivity Map (CMap, https://clue.io) database enables exploration of the correlation between gene expression profiles and small molecule drugs35, supporting the investigation of functional relationships between genes, compounds, and diseases. To identify potential therapeutic agents, we uploaded a list of differentially expressed cellular senescence genes associated with RSA to the database and screened for drugs highly related to the disease. Compounds with negative scores (norm_cs < -1.7) were considered as potential therapeutic candidates. The threshold of norm_cs < -1.7 was selected based on Clue.io’s statistical guidelines (|norm_cs| >1.7 corresponds to FDR-adjusted p < 0.05), indicating high-confidence inverse associations between drug profiles and our senescence signature. The molecular structures of these compounds were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Pan-cancer analysis

The pan-cancer dataset, encompassing 34 types of cancer, was downloaded from the UCSC database (https://genome.ucsc.edu/)36. Expression data for the TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B genes were extracted from each sample and log2-transformed to generate the final expression profiles. The Wilcoxon test was applied to calculate the expression differences of these genes between normal and tumor samples. High-quality TCGA prognostic datasets were obtained from a previously published study in Cell37 and supplemented with TARGET follow-up data from the UCSC Cancer Browser. Samples with follow-up times of less than 30 days were excluded, resulting in expression and follow-up prognosis data for 44 types of cancer, which were used for prognosis analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data processing, analysis, and visualization were conducted using R (version 4.3.3) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.3). Data visualization of data was predominantly achieved through the “ggplot2”, “ggpubr”, and “enrichplot” packages. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Results of differential expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis

Under the screening criteria, 926 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 516 upregulated and 410 downregulated genes (Fig. 2A,B). Among these, there were 31 differentially expressed cellular senescence genes. KEGG analysis revealed significantly enrichment in several pathways in RSA compared to normal controls. These included the MAPK signaling pathway, breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus infection, and other infection-related pathways (Fig. 2C).

Module detection was performed using hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cut functions (Fig. 3A,C). Based on the analysis of scale independence and mean connectivity values, the soft threshold power was set to 13, resulting in identification of 14 distinct modules, each represented by a unique color (Fig. 3B). Among these, the green-yellow and brown modules exhibited statistically significant p-values, and the genes within these modules were selected for further study(Fig. 3D). Scatter plots were used to illustrate the correlation between module membership and gene significance in these two modules (Fig. 3E,F). Additionally, 16 intersecting genes identified by WGCNA are presented in Fig. 3G and are considered to be strongly associated with RSA.

Results of hub gene screening using machine learning algorithms

Machine learning algorithms were employed to further identify hub genes associated with RSA from the previously obtained genes. Using the XGBoost and Boruta algorithms, 6 and 7 feature genes were identified, respectively (Fig. 4A,B). LASSO regression analysis, following cross-validation, selected three key genes (Fig. 4C,D). In the SVM-RFE process, reducing the number of features to 4 minimized the classifier error, leading to the selection of 4 core genes (Fig. 4E,F). Additionally, the Random Forest algorithm, which integrates multiple decision trees, identified 7 genes with importance scores greater than 0.5 (Fig. 4G,H). By integrating the results from all these algorithms, we identified 3 cellular senescence feature genes strongly associated with RSA: TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B (Fig. 4I).

Diagnostic performance of hub genes

To evaluate the diagnostic value of the three feature genes identified through machine learning, ROC curves was constructed for these RSA feature genes. The AUC values for TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B were 0.984, 0.906, and 0.965, respectively (Fig. 5A–C), indicating strong diagnostic performance. Furthermore, these three genes were found to be highly expressed in RSA samples compared to controls (Fig. 5D).

Intercellular distribution of hub genes

After filtering and quality control, a total of 46,580 cells were obtained for analysis. Setting the resolution to 0.5, 23 clusters were identified (Fig. 6A). The distribution of TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B across these clusters is shown in Fig. 6B, with TBX2 notably expressed only in cluster 19. Using well-defined marker gene sets, we annotated 10 major cell subpopulations: macrophages, epithelial cells, NK cells, monocytes, erythrocytes, fibroblasts, CD8 + Tem cells, endothelial cells, adipocytes, and naive B cells. The infiltration proportions of these major cell types varied between tissue types, suggesting that the progression of RSA might be related to changes in cell proportions (Fig. 6C,D). Figure 6E illustrates the distribution of the three hub genes. TBX2 expression is significantly lower than that of the other two genes and is exclusively expressed in fibroblasts, indicating its potential as a specific marker for fibroblasts. In contrast, SRSF3 and TNRC6B are broadly expressed across the majority of cell subpopulations (Fig. 6F).

Expression of hub genes in clinical samples

The relative expression levels of the hub genes TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B were analyzed at both the mRNA and protein levels in villous tissue. qPCR results indicated that the relative mRNA expression levels of all three hub genes were significantly higher in RSA villous tissue compared to normal tissue (Fig. 7A–C). Western blot (Fig. 7D–F, supplementary file) and immunofluorescence (Fig. 8A–C) analyses further confirmed that the protein expression levels of TNRC6B, SRSF3, and TBX2 were consistent with the qPCR results, showing high expression in RSA samples. Notably, the overall expression level of TBX2 was lower compared to SRSF3 and TNRC6B, which is consistent with the findings from the single-cell analysis.

Pan-cancer analysis

The Pan-Cancer Analysis revealed that the expression patterns and prognostic associations of TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B vary widely across different cancer types, highlighting their tissue-specific roles in tumorigenesis and disease progression (Fig. 10, Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). TBX2 exhibits dual roles, being significantly upregulated in 8 cancer types (e.g., GBM, LGG) and downregulated in 20 others (e.g., BRCA, LUAD). High TBX2 expression is associated with poor prognosis in cancers such as GBMLGG, LGG, and KIRP, while paradoxically, low TBX2 expression in UVM also indicates poor prognosis. SRSF3 significantly upregulated in 20 cancers (e.g., BRCA, LUAD) and downregulated in 7 (e.g., KIRP, KIRC). High SRSF3 expression correlates with poor outcomes in cancers like GBMLGG and LIHC, whereas its low expression predicts poor prognosis in cancers such as COADREAD and KIRC. TNRC6B demonstrates significant upregulation in 13 cancers (e.g., ESCA, LAML) and downregulation in 17 others (e.g., BRCA, KIRC), with high expression linked to poor prognosis in LAML and low expression associated with poor outcomes in cancers like GBMLGG and KIRC.

Discussion

RSA presents a significant challenge in modern reproductive obstetrics, affecting approximately 5% of couples attempting to conceive. It is a severe reproductive disorder that requires diagnostic evaluation, therapeutic intervention, and risk assessment for recurrence38. Even when pregnancies are successful, RSA patients face higher risks of complications such as preterm birth, small for gestational age infants, and perinatal mortality. Additionally, RSA patients exhibit higher cesarean section rates, with 9.9% of neonates requiring special care due to preterm birth complications39. Despite extensive global efforts, nearly 50% of RSA cases remain idiopathic40.

Current RSA treatments target presumed risk factors, including combined heparin and aspirin therapy for antiphospholipid syndrome, progesterone in early pregnancy, immunotherapy with intravenous immunoglobulin, and other emerging treatments. However, the efficacy of these treatments remains controversial1,41. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of RSA is crucial for developing more effective and targeted therapies.

Recent studies emphasize the critical role of cellular senescence in pregnancy. The senescence of endometrial stromal cells may impair embryo invasion and reduce endometrial receptivity42,43. During decidualization, embryonic stem cells secrete a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), and anti-aging agents have been shown to promote this process44. Zhao et al. identified eight cellular senescence genes associated with repeated implantation failure, suggesting these genes could serve as potential diagnostic markers or therapeutic targets11. However, research on cellular senescence in RSA villi remains limited, restricting our understanding of its role in RSA.

Exploring cellular senescence in RSA holds promise for uncovering the mechanisms underlying the condition and could pave the way for innovative therapeutic approaches, addressing an urgent need in reproductive medicine. Advances in computer science and bioinformatics have significantly improved our understanding of diseases, which is essential for both early detection and effective treatment45,46. High-throughput technologies such as gene microarray chips, RNA sequencing, and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have enabled rapid and accurate early detection and targeted treatment22,47. Nevertheless, integrating machine learning with WGCNA to explore hub genes from mRNA and scRNA expression data remains underutilized.

Currently, RSA diagnosis is retrospective, based on multiple miscarriages, limiting the precision of prevention and treatment strategies. This study introduces a novel approach to RSA by identifying diagnostic biomarkers and exploring the underlying molecular mechanisms. By using advanced bioinformatics and integrating machine learning with WGCNA, we offer insights that could enhance the precision of RSA diagnosis and lead to targeted therapies.

To enhance sample size and ensure reliability, we integrated multiple datasets, identifying 516 upregulated and 410 downregulated genes. Using WGCNA and five advanced machine learning algorithms (XGBoost, Boruta, LASSO, SVM-RFE, and Random Forest), we identified three hub genes (TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B). These genes were upregulated in RSA, and their expression was validated at the transcriptome and protein levels using PCR, WB, and IF. Their clinical diagnostic value was assessed using an ANN model, indicating their potential to effectively distinguish between RSA and normal tissues. Compared to traditional RSA biomarkers, such as antiphospholipid antibodies, which are primarily associated with antiphospholipid syndrome-related RSA, the three identified genes (TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B) may serve as broader biomarkers applicable to idiopathic RSA. The ANN model constructed using these genes exhibited high diagnostic accuracy, suggesting potential superiority over conventional biomarkers in distinguishing RSA cases from controls. However, large-scale validation studies are needed to confirm its clinical utility. Additionally, the feasibility of detecting TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B in clinical settings requires further investigation. While their expression was validated at both the transcriptomic and protein levels in villous tissues using qPCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence, future studies should assess their detectability in more accessible clinical samples, such as maternal serum or plasma, to enable non-invasive diagnostics. Advanced proteomic techniques, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and mass spectrometry, could be employed to quantify their circulating protein levels and further evaluate their potential as clinically viable RSA biomarkers.

Functional enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed cellular senescence genes are primarily involved in the MAPK signaling pathway, which plays critical roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and stress response48. This pathway is associated with various diseases, including cancer, inflammatory diseases, and neurodegenerative diseases49–51. During cellular senescence, the MAPK pathway may regulate the cell cycle and stress responses in RSA. Previous studies have underscored the significance of the MAPK pathway in trophoblast function, which is essential for proper placental development and successful pregnancy. In RSA, senescence-induced alterations in the MAPK pathway may contribute to the failure of trophoblasts to invade and remodel maternal spiral arteries, a crucial process for establishing a healthy pregnancy.

Additionally, GO term analysis provides deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying RSA. For instance, the BP term “cellular senescence” is particularly relevant, as trophoblast senescence has been implicated in impaired placental function. Excessive senescence in villous trophoblast cells can reduce their capacity for invasion and proliferation, ultimately contributing to placental insufficiency and pregnancy loss.

These findings align with previous studies suggesting that trophoblasts in RSA patients exhibit defects in migration and invasion. Additionally, the MF term “protein serine/threonine kinase activity” emphasizes the regulatory role of kinases in the MAPK signaling pathway, which directly influences trophoblast apoptosis and differentiation—critical processes for successful implantation. The CC term “focal adhesion” highlights the significance of cell-matrix interactions in trophoblast anchoring and placental development. Disruptions in focal adhesion can hinder trophoblast attachment to the uterine wall, a vital step for successful implantation. These observations are consistent with RSA, where inadequate trophoblast invasion into the maternal decidua and failure to remodel the vasculature are associated with impaired ECM interactions. Consequently, senescence-related disruptions in ECM-receptor interactions and focal adhesion may compromise trophoblast function, leading to impaired placental development in RSA.

GSEA further identified enrichment in terms such as dilated cardiomyopathy, which may reflect senescence-induced cellular decline affecting vascular and myocardial functions, potentially contributing to the cardiovascular abnormalities observed in some RSA patients. The “ECM-receptor interaction” and “focal adhesion” pathways are essential for tissue homeostasis and repair; senescence may disrupt these mechanisms, impairing endometrial and placental function, as well as intercellular communication and vascular function, which could hinder embryonic development.

Although the role of p53/p21 in RSA has been extensively documented in prior studies, our findings suggest that senescence-related alterations in the MAPK pathway and ECM interactions provide additional mechanistic insights into RSA pathogenesis. While overexpression of p53 has been shown to impair trophoblast proliferation, migration, and invasion, promoting RSA, our study proposes that cellular senescence in RSA involves more complex signaling cascades, particularly the MAPK pathway, which regulates critical processes such as cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and the ECM pathway, which is vital for tissue remodeling. These findings offer a more integrated understanding of how senescence-associated pathways may contribute to the RSA phenotype, complementing and extending existing research focused on p53/p21.

We hypothesize that cellular senescence induces cell cycle arrest and increased apoptosis, which may contribute to endometrial dysfunction and disrupt embryo implantation and maintenance. Senescence-driven alterations in intercellular communication and ECM structure could lead to abnormal uterine and placental development, ultimately affecting embryonic growth. Additionally, impaired ECM remodeling and trophoblast invasion due to senescence may explain several clinical features of RSA, including recurrent implantation failure and pregnancy loss.

scRNA-seq technology, widely applied in biomedical research, enables high-throughput gene expression measurement at the single-cell level, revealing cellular heterogeneity and gene regulatory networks52. In fields like cancer research, developmental biology, and immunology, single-cell data analysis has provided unprecedented insights53,54.

Analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing data of villous tissue, we identified major cell types such as immune cells, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells, and detailed the distribution of hub genes within the tissue.TBX2 was found to be exclusively expressed in fibroblasts, whereas SRSF3 and TNRC6B were expressed in most cell types.

TBX2 plays a crucial role in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and development55 and is implicated in both embryonic development and disease progression56,57. Moreover, TBX2’s overexpression has been linked to abnormal cellular senescence functions58. In the placenta, fibroblasts are essential for tissue formation and repair, and TBX2-mediated inhibition of senescence may contribute to maintaining cellular vitality. However, excessive inhibition could lead to uncontrolled proliferation and aberrant extracellular matrix remodeling, potentially impairing placental function and increasing the risk of miscarriage. As a potential therapeutic target, TBX2 could be modulated using small-molecule inhibitors or RNA-based therapeutics to restore the balance between proliferation and senescence in fibroblasts.Precise regulation of TBX2 expression may help prevent pathological fibrosis and excessive extracellular matrix deposition in the placenta, thereby reducing pregnancy complications associated with placental insufficiency.

SRSF3, a member of the serine/arginine-rich (SR) family of pre-mRNA splicing factors, plays a critical role in RNA splicing, transport, and translation and is closely associated with tumorigenesis and disease progression59. SRSF3 maintains cancer-specific energy metabolism and regulates splicing, promoting angiogenesis in colorectal cancer and inhibiting gastric cancer cell proliferation60,61. In RSA villous tissue, elevated SRSF3 expression may serve as a stress response to pregnancy-related pressures, supporting placental function. However, its dysregulation could result in aberrant cell proliferation and functional impairment of the placenta. Given SRSF3’s involvement in RNA splicing, targeted splicing modulators or antisense oligonucleotides could be explored to selectively regulate its activity in placental cells. This approach may help correct abnormal splicing patterns associated with pregnancy disorders, potentially mitigating risks related to placental overproliferation or dysfunction.

TNRC6B, a key component of the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), regulates gene expression through miRNA-mediated silencing62. It influences cell proliferation and migration and exhibits tumor-suppressive effects in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma63. Its high expression in RSA might regulate gene expression, cell proliferation, and differentiation in response to pregnancy pressure, but dysregulation of TNRC6B could impair villous tissue function, increasing the risk of miscarriage. Given TNRC6B’s role in post-transcriptional gene regulation, miRNA mimics or inhibitors targeting its downstream pathways could be explored as potential therapeutic strategies. Restoring TNRC6B-mediated gene silencing may help prevent aberrant gene expression changes contributing to placental dysfunction, offering a novel approach for managing recurrent pregnancy loss.

To understand the broader biological significance of these genes, we conducted a pan-cancer analysis, confirming their importance across various diseases and supporting further research into these genes as therapeutic targets. The analysis indicated abnormal expression patterns in multiple cancers, suggesting multiple regulatory roles in tumorigenesis and development. These findings provide valuable insights and clues for further research into the roles of these genes in RSA.

Additionally, a drug sensitivity analysis using the CMap database identified potential therapeutic interventions, offering promising new directions for the development of RSA treatment.

By integrating bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation, we provide a novel perspective on the molecular mechanisms underlying RSA. Although the machine learning algorithms used are conventional, their combined application to identify senescence-related genes in villous tissue is innovative in this specific context. Our findings offer a foundation for future functional studies and underscore the value of computational approaches in advancing understanding of reproductive disorders. While we employed robust and widely accepted bioinformatics methods and validated key gene expression at both mRNA and protein levels, the analysis remains largely exploratory. We acknowledge the limited methodological novelty and depth of biological validation. Future research should incorporate more advanced modeling approaches, in vivo functional studies, and larger, ethnically diverse cohorts to elucidate the mechanistic roles of these genes in placental development and RSA pathogenesis, ultimately advancing prevention and therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This study is the first to analyze the potential mechanisms of RSA from a new perspective by integrating advanced bioinformatics with machine learning strategies. Our findings suggest that cellular senescence may be a potential pathological mechanism underlying RSA. Furthermore, we identified three cellular senescence-related genes—TBX2, SRSF3, and TNRC6B—that hold promise as new diagnostic biomarkers for RSA.
