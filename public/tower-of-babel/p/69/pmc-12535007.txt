Predictive value analysis of diaphragmatic ultrasound evaluation for mechanical ventilation outcomes in patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Leilei Qu, Wenping Zhao, Wei Zhang, Jiping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbations often necessitate mechanical ventilation. Weaning from ventilation poses challenges, especially due to diaphragmatic dysfunction. We evaluated the predictive value of diaphragmatic ultrasound for mechanical ventilation outcomes in COPD patients during acute exacerbations.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 200 patients experiencing acute COPD exacerbations between June 2020 and September 2023. We identified these patients using the ICD-10 codes for COPD (J44.x) and acute exacerbations from our electronic medical record system.Patients underwent diaphragmatic ultrasound assessments and were then grouped based on weaning success or failure. The study investigated parameters such as the E-T index, diaphragmatic displacement (DD), and diaphragmatic thickening fraction (DTF), correlating them with weaning outcomes. Correlation and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were employed to ascertain predictive values.ResultsThe E-T index, DD, and DTF were significantly higher in the success group, indicating these parameters as predictors of successful weaning. The E-T index (rho = -0.254, P < 0.001), DD (rho = -0.269, P < 0.001), and DTF (rho = -0.201, P = 0.004) negatively correlated with weaning failure. Conversely, the diaphragmatic Rapid Shallow Breathing Index (D-RSBI) positively correlated with failure (rho = 0.179, P = 0.011). Higher LUS scores predicted weaning failure (P = 0.029). Combined analysis of ultrasound indicators demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.905, highlighting the model’s predictive utility.Additional correlations showed that higher PaCO₂ before weaning was linked with lower DD, suggesting an association between impaired diaphragmatic mechanics and elevated CO₂ levels.ConclusionDiaphragmatic ultrasound was a valuable tool for assessing readiness for mechanical ventilation weaning in COPD exacerbations, enhancing the precision of clinical decisions.Our findings also suggest that a threshold E-T index of ∼ 1.915 and DTF of ∼ 42.475% may have clinical relevance in identifying successful weaners.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03567-3.Keywords: Predictive value, Diaphragmatic ultrasound evaluation, Mechanical ventilation outcomes, Acute exacerbation, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was a prevalent and progressive respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation and typically associated with chronic inflammatory response in the airways and lungs [1, 2]. Acute exacerbations of COPD were significant events marked by a sudden worsening of respiratory symptoms beyond the normal day-to-day variations, often necessitating hospitalization and intensive management, including the potential requirement for mechanical ventilation [3]. Despite advances in therapeutic interventions, weaning patients with COPD from mechanical ventilation remains challenging, often complicated by both intrinsic respiratory pathology and the side effects of prolonged ventilator use [4, 5]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for reliable predictors to enhance clinical decision-making regarding ventilator withdrawal [6].

Recent attention has focused on the role of diaphragmatic function in the management of mechanically ventilated patients, particularly in the context of weaning [7]. The diaphragm operates as a primary respiratory muscle, and its dysfunction was increasingly recognized as a key contributor to weaning failure, particularly in patients with COPD [8]. Mechanical ventilation, despite its life-saving functionality, poses the risk of diaphragmatic atrophy due to disuse, potentially leading to ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction [9, 10]. This condition exacerbates the difficulty of transitioning patients back to spontaneous breathing, highlighting the critical importance of assessing diaphragmatic function to optimize outcomes [11].

Diaphragmatic ultrasound offers a non-invasive, real-time imaging technique to evaluate diaphragmatic function [12]. It allows for the assessment of various parameters, including diaphragmatic excursion (DE), thickening fraction, and displacement, which collectively provide insights into diaphragmatic strength and endurance [13]. Despite the potential benefits, the integration of diaphragmatic ultrasound into clinical pathways for weaning assessment remains underexplored, necessitating further empirical investigation to validate its utility and optimize its application [14].

The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) strategy underscores the need for a comprehensive approach to managing COPD exacerbations, incorporating both systemic evaluation and targeted intervention [15]. This multifaceted management framework aligns with the ethos of precision medicine, wherein individualized assessment and tailored therapeutic strategies hold the potential to enhance clinical outcomes [16]. The utilization of diaphragmatic ultrasound within this paradigm offers a pragmatic approach to refine weaning protocols based on objective, quantifiable data rather than relying solely on clinical judgment or empirical weaning trials [17]. Our retrospective study thus aimed to elucidate the predictive value of diaphragmatic ultrasound evaluation, using standardized search criteria (ICD-10 codes for COPD exacerbations) and a robust data extraction process, to determine mechanical ventilation outcomes among patients experiencing acute COPD exacerbations.

Materials and methods

Case selection

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 200 patients treated at our hospital from June 2021 to June 2024. We employed ICD-10 codes J44.x (COPD) and J44.0/J44.1 (acute exacerbation) to identify eligible patients.Patient demographic information was extracted from the medical record system, encompassing participants’ demographic characteristics, baseline disease-related features, clinical data, routine blood tests, blood gas indicators, and diaphragmatic ultrasound measurements.

For sample size estimation, we used preliminary data from an internal pilot sample (n = 40) to estimate an effect size (Cohen’s d ∼ 0.5) in terms of differences in diaphragmatic ultrasound indicators between successful and failed weaning groups. We then calculated that at least 180 patients would be required (power 0.80, alpha 0.05). We ultimately included 200 patients.

This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of our institution. Informed consent was waived because the study was retrospective and utilized de-identified patient data, ensuring no risk or impact on patient care. This waiver was granted in compliance with the regulatory and ethical guidelines governing retrospective research. A flow diagram illustrating patient selection is provided in Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Material.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Clinical diagnosis of COPD based on GOLD strategy [18] and experiencing an acute exacerbation. Participants were required to be 18 years of age or older. All participants underwent diaphragmatic ultrasound evaluation and received mechanical ventilation treatment. Additionally, clear documentation of adequate cough reflex and absence of excessive purulent airway secretions in the nursing/physician notes, and stable cardiovascular status, with no or minimal use of vasoactive drugs.

Exclusion criteria

Contraindications to ventilator treatment.Agitation or uncooperativeness during ultrasound evaluation.Use of muscle relaxants within 48 h prior to the study.Severe hemodynamic instability, tracheotomy, concurrent pneumothorax, or large pleural effusion.History of diaphragmatic paralysis, including ultrasound findings of unilateral diaphragmatic paralysis or paradoxical diaphragmatic movement.Severe organ dysfunction (heart, lungs, kidney), mental illness, malignant tumors, or drug allergies.

Grouping criteria, assessment and treatment approach

In this study, 200 patients with acute exacerbation of COPD who were evaluated using diaphragmatic ultrasound were divided into two groups: the success group, comprising 156 patients, and the failure group, consisting of 44 patients. These categorizations were made based on the success of weaning from mechanical ventilation. According to the Official ERS/ATS Clinical Practice Guidelines [19], the criteria for successful weaning included: (1) no need for mechanical ventilation within 48 h post-weaning, and (2) no re-intubation within 48 h after performing a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) and subsequent removal of the endotracheal tube. Conversely, failure criteria for extubation included: (1) re-intubation or initiation of non-invasive ventilation within 48 h of extubation, (2) an unsuccessful SBT, or (3) patient death within 48 h of weaning.

All patients received a standardized treatment protocol for COPD exacerbation, which included bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and antibiotic therapy (where indicated).

The median time interval between diagnosis of acute exacerbation and the initial ultrasound assessment was approximately 24 h (IQR 20–28 h). The diaphragmatic ultrasound evaluation procedure was as follows: patients were positioned supine, and two experienced physicians (each with ≥ 5 years of training in critical care ultrasound) performed all the ultrasound examinations for the 200 patients. They used a color Doppler ultrasound device (GE LOGIQ e, USA) set to a frequency of 7–12 MHz. We assessed interrater reliability (Cohen’s kappa = 0.86) on a subset of 30 patients for parameters such as diaphragmatic excursion and thickness.

Prior to withdrawal from mechanical ventilation in COPD patients, an SBT was conducted in accordance with the Clinical Application Guidelines for Mechanical Ventilation (2006), ensuring the following conditions were met: (1) Control of the underlying cause for mechanical ventilation; (2) hemodynamic stability, requiring no or only low doses of vasoactive drugs; (3) clear consciousness with adequate coughing and spontaneous breathing capability; and (4) blood gas analysis indicating PaO2 ≥ 70 mmHg, SaO2 ≥ 90%, pH ≥ 7.25, and a positive end-expiratory pressure of 4–8 cmH2O.

Record of the patient’s baseline disease status

The baseline disease status of patients upon admission was collected and analyzed using the medical record system. The health status of the two patient groups was assessed using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, which comprises three components: the acute physiology score, age score, and chronic health score. The total score ranges from 0 to 60 points, with lower scores indicating better health conditions. The reliability of this scoring system was indicated by a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.76 [20].

Monitoring of clinical data of patients

A vital sign monitor (Shenzhen Mindray Biomedical Electronics Co., Ltd., model: VS8) was employed to continuously monitor the vital signs of the two patient groups. Recorded parameters included heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) before mechanical ventilation, as well as HR, RR, and central venous pressure (CVP) before and after weaning. Typically, a normal HR ranges from 60 to 100 beats per minute at rest. The normal range for MAP in adults was 70–105 mmHg, calculated using the formula: MAP = (systolic pressure + 2 × diastolic pressure) / 3. It can also be expressed as: MAP = diastolic pressure + 1/3 pulse pressure difference. Normal arterial blood SpO₂ was approximately 98%, while venous blood SpO₂ was around 75%. The standard range for CVP was 50–120 mmH₂O.

Detection of routine blood test

In the morning, 3 ml of blood was collected from each patient’s vein while fasting and placed in a vacuum blood collection tube. The serum was separated by centrifugation using a low-temperature, high-speed centrifuge (Hunan Xiangxi Scientific Instrument Factory, model: TLD12A) at 4000 rpm for 10 min. The collected serum was used for testing, and the remainder was stored in a freezer at -80 °C for future use. A fully automatic blood cell analyzer (Shenzhen Mindray Biomedical Electronics Co., Ltd., model: Mindray BC6800) and a fully automatic coagulation analyzer (Wofen Medical Equipment Trading Beijing Co., Ltd., model: ACLTOP750) were utilized to assess routine blood parameters. These included white blood cell count, procalcitonin (PCT), hemoglobin, albumin, blood creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen.

Detection of blood gas indicators

Three milliliters of arterial blood were collected from each patient group prior to mechanical ventilation, as well as before and after weaning. Using a blood gas analyzer (Suzhou Ledumet Medical Technology Co., Ltd., model: ABL9), we assessed the patients’ blood gas indicators. Prior to mechanical ventilation, we recorded pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO₂), partial pressure of oxygen (PaO₂), and the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio to evaluate oxygen exchange capacity. Before and after weaning from mechanical ventilation, we documented the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂), along with the pH, PaCO₂, and PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio. These pre- and post-weaning values were compared to analyze their correlation.

Diaphragmatic ultrasound indicators and LUS

During the ultrasound evaluation, two sets of measurements were recorded for exhalation time (Ti), DE, end-inspiratory diaphragm thickness (DTei), end-expiratory diaphragm thickness (DTee), and diaphragm displacement (DD). Additionally, the E-T index and the fraction of diaphragmatic thickening fraction (DTF) were calculated.

The DE detection method involved displaying DE using M-mode ultrasound, performing three measurements, and calculating the average. DE was determined by subtracting the distance from the end-expiratory diaphragm to the baseline from the distance from the end-inspiratory diaphragm to the baseline. The E-T index was calculated as DE multiplied by Ti.

For DTF measurement, a linear array probe was placed perpendicular to the chest wall at the 8th intercostal space. The thickness of the diaphragm was measured at the end of exhalation and the end of expiration using B-mode and M-mode imaging. DTF was calculated with the formula: DTF = ((DTei - DTee)/DTee) x 100%, based on continuous measurements to obtain the average value. In normal individuals, the thickening index (DTF) ranges from 28 to 96%, whereas in those with diaphragmatic paralysis, it ranges from − 35 to 5%.

For both patient groups, tidal volume (VT) and spontaneous breathing rate (RR) were monitored using a ventilator. The rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI) and the diaphragmatic Rapid Shallow Breathing Index (D-RSBI) were calculated as RSBI = RR/VT and D-RSBI = RR/DD, respectively.

The Lung Ultrasound Score (LUS) was determined as follows: A lung sliding sign with A lines or fewer than two individual B lines indicated a normal ventilation zone (N) and was scored as 0 points. Multiple typical B-lines signifying moderate reduced lung ventilation (B1 line) scored 1 point. Numerous fused B-lines indicating severe reduction in lung ventilation (B2 line) scored 2 points. Lung consolidation areas, characterized by tissue images with typical bronchial inflation signs, were scored as 3 points (C). The maximum possible score was 36 points, with higher scores correlating with increased severity of the disease.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 29.0. Categorical data were represented as [n (%)] and analyzed using Fisher’s Exact Test if cell counts were < 5, otherwise chi-square was used. Continuous variables were tested for normality using Shapiro-Wilk and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. We performed Pearson’s correlation for continuous variables and Spearman’s correlation for categorical or non-normally distributed variables.

Results

Demographic and basic data

In the study examining the predictive value of diaphragmatic ultrasound evaluation for mechanical ventilation outcomes in patients experiencing acute exacerbations of COPD, demographic characteristics were analyzed between those who successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation (success group, n = 156) and those who did not (failed group, n = 44) (Table 1). The mean age was slightly higher in the failed group (54.24 ± 4.63 years) compared to the success group (53.25 ± 4.65 years), but this difference was not statistically significant (t = 1.253, P = 0.212). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in body mass index (BMI) between the two groups (P = 0.838). Gender distribution and smoking history showed comparable patterns, with no significant differences (P > 0.05). Key cardiac parameters, including left and right ventricular diameters, were also similar between the groups (P > 0.05). Additionally, the presence of comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and chronic liver disease did not significantly differ between the groups (P > 0.05). The presence of sepsis was similar between groups (success group: 5.13% vs. failed group: 6.82%, P = 0.666). Other demographic factors including employment status, marital status, educational level, and the prevalence of combined pulmonary arterial hypertension were similar between groups, with none of these variables demonstrating statistical significance (P > 0.05) (Table S1).These findings suggest that demographic characteristics did not differ significantly between patients who succeeded or failed in mechanical ventilation weaning, indicating that other clinical or ultrasound parameters might be more pivotal in predicting outcomes.

Baseline disease-related features

Patients in the failed group had a longer length of hospital stay (10.63 ± 2.92 days) compared to the success group (9.03 ± 1.96 days), with this difference reaching statistical significance (t = 3.420, P = 0.001) (Table 2). Additionally, the failed group required a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation (67.95 ± 6.17 h) than the success group (71.1 ± 6.36 h), which was statistically significant (t = 2.919, P = 0.004). The APACHE II score, indicating severity of illness, was also significantly higher in the failed group (21.63 ± 4.87) compared to the success group (18.62 ± 5.84, t = 3.115, P = 0.002). In contrast, there were no significant differences in the duration of COPD (P = 0.077) or the highest body temperature recorded within 24 h before the spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) (P = 0.171) between the groups. These results highlight that longer hospital stays, shorter mechanical ventilation durations, and higher APACHE II scores were associated with mechanical ventilation weaning failure.

The mean white blood cell count was similar in both the success group (10.25 ± 2.65 × 10^9/L) and the failed group (10.43 ± 2.33 × 10^9/L) (t = 0.420, P = 0.675) (Table 4). Blood gas indicators

The pH levels exhibited a significant difference, with the failed group having a higher mean pH (7.51 ± 1.12) compared to the success group (7.03 ± 1.41), indicating a statistically significant difference (t = 2.072, P = 0.040) (Table 5). Additionally, arterial carbon dioxide pressure (PaCO2) was significantly higher in the failed group (64.93 ± 5.36 mmHg) than in the success group (62.83 ± 5.32 mmHg, t = 2.310, P = 0.022). These findings suggest that higher pH and PaCO2 levels were associated with failed weaning from mechanical ventilation, while PaO2 and PaO2/FiO2 ratios do not significantly differentiate between outcomes.

Diaphragmatic ultrasound indicators

The E-T index, reflecting the efficiency of diaphragmatic movement, was significantly higher in the success group (2.15 ± 0.27) compared to the failed group (1.97 ± 0.36), demonstrating a t-value of 3.127 and a P-value of 0.003 (Fig. 1). However, DE and inspiratory time (Ti) did not significantly differ between the two groups. DE was 19.67 ± 3.26 mm in the success group and 18.94 ± 2.76 mm in the failed group (t = 1.335, P = 0.177). Ti was 1.29 ± 0.27 s in the success group and 1.23 ± 0.57 s in the failed group (t = 0.625, P = 0.535). These findings suggest that a higher E-T index were associated with successful weaning from mechanical ventilation, while DE and inspiratory time does not significantly impact weaning outcomes.

Comparison of diaphragmatic ultrasound indicators between the two groups

DTei, DTee, DTF and DD

Diaphragmatic displacement (DD) was notably greater in the success group (1.38 ± 0.36 cm) compared to the failed group (1.05 ± 0.64 cm), achieving statistical significance (t = 3.291, P = 0.002) (Fig. 2). Additionally, the DTF was significantly higher in the success group (37 ± 19%) relative to the failed group (29 ± 8%), with a highly significant t-value of 4.118 and P-value of < 0.001. Conversely, DTF at end-inspiration (DTei) and end-expiration (DTee) did not show significant differences; DTei was 0.36 ± 0.16 cm in the success group and 0.31 ± 0.19 cm in the failed group (t = 1.517, P = 0.131), while DTee was 0.26 ± 0.08 cm in the success group and 0.24 ± 0.07 cm in the failed group (t = 1.459, P = 0.146). These findings indicate that higher DD and thickening fraction values were associated with successful weaning, while DTei and DTee do not significantly impact outcomes.

The RSBI did not show significant differences in the success group (46.83 ± 14.36 time/min·L) compared to the failed group (50.74 ± 17.47 time/min·L) (t = 1.518, P = 0.131) (Table 6). However, the D-RSBI was significantly lower in the success group (1.34 ± 0.25 time/min·mm) than in the failed group (1.58 ± 0.57 time/min·mm), achieving statistical significance (t = 2.673, P = 0.010). These findings suggest that lower D-RSBI values were predictive of successful weaning from mechanical ventilation in patients with acute exacerbation of COPD.

Patients’ indicator (after weaning)

The HR was significantly higher in the failed group (105.72 ± 17.48 bpm) compared to the success group (97.74 ± 9.86 bpm) (t = 2.901, P = 0.005), indicating greater cardiovascular stress in the failed group (Table 8). RR was also notably higher in the failed group (21.74 ± 5.81 bpm) compared to the success group (19.56 ± 2.78 bpm), achieving significance (t = 2.409, P = 0.02). Partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2) was elevated in the failed group (65.77 ± 13.64 mmHg) versus the success group (59.35 ± 11.49 mmHg), with a significant difference (t = 3.136, P = 0.002). The P/F ratio, a measure of respiratory efficiency, was significantly lower in the failed group (197.32 ± 53.89 mmHg) compared to the success group (224.58 ± 55.89 mmHg, t = 2.879, P = 0.004). Additionally, the LUS was higher in the failed group (9.94 ± 4.39) than the success group (8.09 ± 3.2), with statistical significance (t = 2.601, P = 0.012). In contrast, the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), blood pH, and CVP did not differ significantly between the groups (P > 0.05). These findings emphasize that HR, RR, PaCO2, P/F ratio, and LUS were valuable indicators of weaning outcomes in patients with acute exacerbation of COPD.

ROC

In this study, the predictive value of diaphragmatic ultrasound indicators for mechanical ventilation outcomes in patients with acute exacerbation of COPD was evaluated using various statistical metrics. The E-T index showed a best threshold of 1.915 with a sensitivity of 0.523 and a specificity of 0.808, resulting in an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.677 and a Youden index of 0.331, while achieving an F1 score of 0.22. The DD presented a best threshold of 0.955 with a sensitivity of 0.5 and a notably high specificity of 0.897, resulting in an AUC of 0.687, a Youden index of 0.397, and an F1 score of 0.214. The DTF had a best threshold of 42.475, demonstrating a high sensitivity of 0.955 but a lower specificity of 0.436, with an AUC of 0.64, a Youden index of 0.391, and a F1 score of 0.035. Conversely, the D-RSBI indicated a best threshold of 1.805 with a lower sensitivity of 0.364, but it achieved the highest specificity of 0.994 and an F1 score of 0.525, yielding an AUC of 0.625 and a Youden index of 0.358. These findings highlight the advantages and limitations of each ultrasound parameter, and further construction of joint models has the potential to become reliable and specific predictors (Table 10).

Discussion

The current study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the predictive value of diaphragmatic ultrasound in determining mechanical ventilation outcomes for patients experiencing acute exacerbations of COPD. The study’s primary finding—that higher E-T index, DD, and DTF were significantly associated with successful weaning from mechanical ventilation—highlights the role of diaphragm function as an integral factor in respiratory recovery. The E-T index, a product of DE and inspiratory time, provides insight into both the amplitude and velocity of diaphragmatic contractions, which were indicative of muscle strength and endurance [21]. The negative correlation between the E-T index and weaning failure suggests that stronger and more efficient diaphragmatic movements were critical for the patient’s independent breathing capability post-weaning. The DD and DTF findings align with this interpretation, emphasizing the importance of both diaphragm mobility and muscle thickening in achieving effective ventilation and preventing respiratory failure.

In contrast, the D-RSBI exhibited a positive correlation with weaning failure. The D-RSBI reflects the respiratory efficiency by accounting for the ratio of RR to DE. Higher values indicate less efficient breathing patterns, often characterized by shallower and faster breaths, which were hallmarks of respiratory distress and fatigue [22, 23]. This aligns with clinical observations of weaning failure, where patients were unable to sustain adequate spontaneous breathing due to diaphragmatic weakness or fatigue.

Another noteworthy finding was the role of the LUS in predictive weaning outcomes. A higher LUS in the failed group suggests poorer pulmonary aeration and lung compliance, which complicate the weaning process. Lung ultrasound provides valuable noninvasive insights into pulmonary conditions, and the significant difference in LUS between groups indicates that both diaphragm and lung function should be considered for comprehensive weaning readiness assessments.

Biophysiological mechanisms underpinning these results suggest that diaphragm muscle atrophy and dysfunction—common in prolonged mechanical ventilation—hinder successful weaning. Mechanical unloading during ventilation leads to disuse atrophy, characterized by reduced muscle fiber size and contractile protein degradation [24]. This decrease in muscular integrity impairs the diaphragm’s ability to generate sufficient negative intrathoracic pressure for effective spontaneous breathing [25]. The significant diaphragm parameters observed in patients who successfully weaned imply that preserving muscular function or mitigating atrophy through early intervention or targeted therapies could enhance weaning outcomes.

The correlation analysis underscoring diaphragmatic ultrasound indicators gives credence to integrating these metrics into existing weaning protocols. By identifying functional limitations early, clinicians can tailor interventions, such as respiratory muscle training or adjusted ventilatory support, to correct deficits and optimize readiness for extubation [26]. This proactive approach could decrease the duration of mechanical ventilation and improve patient prognoses [27].

Prior research has documented the significance of diaphragmatic thickness fraction and displacement in predicting weaning outcomes, often reporting cut-offs around 30–36% for DTF [28, 29]. Our best threshold of ∼ 42.475% was slightly higher, potentially reflecting differences in COPD severity or measurement techniques. Similarly, the E-T index aligns with other studies emphasizing diaphragmatic excursion’s utility in identifying patients likely to maintain spontaneous breathing [30]. The slight variation in threshold underscores the importance of clinical judgment and local population differences.Our findings suggest that an E-T index near 1.915 and a DTF near 42.475% may be informative. These cut-offs could help clinicians better stratify COPD patients prior to extubation, prompting more targeted interventions (e.g., inspiratory muscle training) if indices fall below the threshold. Moreover, the combined model (AUC 0.905) highlights the multifactorial nature of respiratory failure, suggesting that diaphragmatic ultrasound metrics be evaluated in tandem with standard parameters such as gas exchange and lung ultrasound.

Limitations

Our study employed a convenience sampling of patients admitted to a single tertiary hospital, potentially limiting generalizability.Though only two senior physicians performed the ultrasound (ensuring consistency), it still reflects a relatively controlled scenario.We provided interrater reliability measurements (kappa = 0.86) for 30 cases, but future investigations might broaden operator diversity to evaluate reproducibility. The retrospective design may introduce selection biases and does not establish causation. A prospective or randomized approach would be more definitive.

Conclusion

In conclusion, diaphragmatic ultrasound offers valuable insights into patient preparedness for mechanical ventilation liberation, serving as both a predictive metric and a guide for therapeutic interventions. By focusing on diaphragmatic function, alongside traditional respiratory parameters, clinicians can enhance the precision of weaning strategies and potentially improve patient outcomes during acute COPD exacerbations. Future research should focus on integrating these findings into clinical pathways, optimizing non-invasive diagnostic tools, and exploring interventions targeting diaphragm preservation to improve weaning success rates in this vulnerable patient group.
