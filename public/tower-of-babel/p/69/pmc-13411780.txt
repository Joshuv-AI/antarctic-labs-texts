Removing the smoke screen of SMAX1 and SMXL2's role in seedling growth
Authors: Vanessica Jawahir
Published: 2026

Optimal timing of growth, life-stage transitions, and physiological processes is crucial for plant survival and fecundity. Plants constantly monitor their environment; the circadian clock anticipates the diurnal environmental changes, enabling optimal timing of molecular and physiological processes (Greenham and McClung 2015). Hypocotyl elongation is gated by the circadian clock restricting elongation to specific times of day, depending on day length and temperature cues (Niwa et al. 2009; Nusinow et al. 2011). In short days, hypocotyl elongation peaks at the end of night, whereas, in long days, hypocotyl elongation peaks during the end of the day. This time-of day dependent growth is governed by a complex signaling network starting with light and temperature perception.

PHYTOCHROME B (PHYB), the red/far-red receptor, perceives light and temperature. The active form of PHYB interacts with PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs), a group of bHLH transcription factors, and sequesters them away, preventing them from activating auxin biosynthesis genes. When PHYB is in its inactive form, it can no longer interact with the PIFs. This allows the PIFs to activate auxin biosynthesis and signaling genes, enabling hypocotyl elongation. This process is complemented by the core clock protein, GIGANTEA (GI), which functions to prevent premature elongation after dusk by interacting with the PIFs (Nohales et al. 2019). GI levels decline throughout the night, allowing the PIFs to accumulate and induce elongation.

A new study by Suyeon Kim and colleagues (Kim et al. 2026) describes how key karrikin signaling components contribute to circadian gating of hypocotyl growth. Karrikins (KARs) are chemicals found in smoke produced from burning plants and are a potent signal for seeds to germinate, allowing plants to rapidly reestablish after fire. In canonical KAR signaling, KARRIKIN-INSENSITIVE2 (KAI2) perceives the signal, which degrades SUPPRESSOR OF MAX 2 1 (SMAX1) and SMAX1-LIKE2 (SMXL2), enabling germination while inhibiting hypocotyl elongation.

The knockout mutant smax1-3 has reduced hypocotyl elongation compared to the wild type in both long-day and short-day conditions without KAR treatment. This indicates that SMAX1 also has a role in hypocotyl elongation in the absence of active KAR signaling. Hypocotyl elongation is further suppressed in the smax1-3 smxl2-1 double mutant, with this phenotype being more pronounced under short-day conditions. The authors found that YUCCA8 and IAA29 gene expression is not induced at the end of night in smax1-3 smxl2-1 in short days, demonstrating that the signal to induce auxin production needed for rhythmic growth is disrupted.

Yeast two-hybrid and in planta co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays demonstrated that SMAX1 and SMXL2 directly interact with GI. The gi smax1 smxl2 triple mutant suppressed the long hypocotyl phenotype of gi indicating that SMAX1 and SMXL2 are necessary for GI-mediated hypocotyl elongation. PIF4 levels are reduced in smax1-3 smxl2-1 but elevated in gi plants, consistent with the hypocotyl lengths of these mutants. Co-IP assays demonstrated that SMAX1 and PIF4 interactions were reduced in the presence of GI, implying that GI and SMAX1 compete to interact with PIF4. The GI-SMAX1 complex is more abundant early in the night, and the SMAX1-PIF4 complex is more abundant in the late night, indicating time-of-day specific complex formation. Together, these data place SMAX1 and SMXL2 at the center of a GI–PIF4 timing switch that gates rhythmic hypocotyl growth, raising the question of whether it is engaged when plants encounter warm temperatures.

Recent research has found that SMAX1 and SMXL2 have roles beyond the KAR signaling pathway (Park et al. 2022; Chang et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2023), suggesting the potential involvement of GI, SMAX1, and PIF4 in thermomorphogenesis, the morphological and developmental responses to warm temperature. Therefore, the authors sought to clarify the role of the SMAX1 protein complexes in warm environments. Co-IP experiments found that GI-SMAX1 and GI-SMXL2 complexes were more abundant at 28 °C than at 23 °C in the night, limiting thermomorphogenesis. Without GI, SMAX1 and SMXL2 are able to stabilize PIF4, leading to the excessive hypocotyl elongation seen in gi at 28 °C.

The results support a model in which GI interacts with SMAX1 and SMXL2 at the early night phase in short-day conditions (Fig. 1). GI's association with SMAX1 and SMXL2 sequester SMAX1 and SMXL2 away from PIF4, leading to PIF4 destabilization, thereby preventing auxin signaling and hypocotyl elongation during the day. During the late night, GI proteins decline, and SMAX1 and SMXL2 are available to associate with PIF4, which allows PIF4 to activate expression of its target genes leading to hypocotyl elongation.

Model of GI's interaction with SMAX1 and SMXL2 to integrate perception of environmental signals to influence hypocotyl elongation. In short days, GI interacts with SMAX1 and SMXL2 early in the night, which sequester SMAX1 and SMXL2 away from PIF4. In the late night, GI levels decline, and SMAX1 and SMXL2 can stabilize PIF4, allowing for activation of PIF4-induced auxin biosynthesis genes. Warm temperatures increase the formation of GI/SMAX1 and GI/SMXL2 complexes which regulate thermomorphogenesis to prevent excessive hypocotyl elongation. Reprinted from Kim et al. (2026).

Kim et al. unravel additional roles of SMAX1 and SMXL2 by demonstrating that these 2 proteins directly interact with GI to influence rhythmic hypocotyl growth and thermomorphogenesis. The findings that canonical KAR signaling components are also important for circadian gating of hypocotyl elongation highlight the complexity of signaling that regulates plant responses to the environment. Plants rely on a multi-layer regulatory network that ensures genes are expressed at specific times of day, proteins accumulate, are modified, and interact with appropriate binding parters at appropriate times, and that signaling pathways are shut down when necessary. Identifying protein interaction hubs may unlock the ability to manipulate and optimize plant responses to the changing and unpredictable environment.
